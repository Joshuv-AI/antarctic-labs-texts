Profiles and Prognostic Value of a Non-Invasive Liver Fibrosis Marker in Patients With Acute and Chronic Coronary Syndrome
Authors: Wataru Saitoh, Takenobu Shimada, Daiju Fukuda, Atsushi Shibata, Asahiro Ito, Kenichiro Otsuka, Hiroshi Okamura, Tetsuya Matoba, Takahide Kohro, Yusuke Oba, Hisaki Makimoto, Tomoyuki Kabutoya, Yasushi Imai, Kazuomi Kario, Arihiro Kiyosue, Satoshi Kodera, Yoshiko Mizuno, Kotaro Nochioka, Masaharu Nakayama, Takamasa Iwai, Yoshihiro Miyamoto, Masanobu Ishii, Taishi Nakamura, Kenichi Tsujita, Hisahiko Sato, Naoyuki Akashi, Hideo Fujita, Ryozo Nagai, on behalf of the CLIDAS Research Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Fibrosis-4 (Fib-4) index, a non-invasive liver fibrosis marker, is useful for assessing cardiovascular risk; however, its role and profiles in patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) and chronic coronary syndrome (CCS) remain unclear.Methods and ResultsWe analyzed 5,150 patients (ACS: 2,274; CCS: 2,876) from the Clinical Deep Data Accumulation System-Percutaneous Coronary Intervention (CLIDAS-PCI) database. The Fib-4 index was calculated 1–3 months post-PCI to avoid acute-phase transaminase elevation. Patients were stratified into low (<1.3), intermediate (1.3–2.67), and high (>2.67) Fib-4 categories. Longitudinal changes were assessed every 6 months for 2 years. The primary outcome was 3-point major adverse cardiovascular events (3P-MACE): cardiovascular death, non-fatal myocardial infarction, and stroke. Compared with CCS, ACS patients had fewer comorbidities and more intensive medical therapy. The Fib-4 index was lower in ACS than in CCS (1.83 vs. 1.93; P<0.001), and this difference persisted throughout follow up. Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated clear risk stratification across Fib-4 categories in both ACS and CCS. A higher Fib-4 index was independently associated with increased 3P-MACE risk after adjustment for C-reactive protein levels and coronary syndrome type (adjusted hazard ratio 1.15; 95% confidence interval 1.12–1.19; P<0.001).ConclusionsThe Fib-4 index efficiently stratifies cardiovascular risk in ACS and CCS, independent of systemic inflammation. ACS patients consistently exhibited lower Fib-4 values than CCS patients.

Central Figure

Liver fibrosis is often caused by metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and other chronic liver disorders. It is closely associated with systemic metabolic abnormalities, such as insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and obesity.1–3 Moreover, MASLD is not merely a cluster of comorbidities but also promotes the infiltration of proinflammatory cytokines and induces endothelial dysfunction, contributing to the development of atherosclerosis and plaque instability.4,5 Accordingly, liver fibrosis has increasingly been recognized as a manifestation of systemic atherosclerosis.6

Although liver biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosing liver fibrosis, the Fibrosis-4 (Fib-4) index is a widely used, non-invasive, safe, and cost-effective surrogate marker.7 The Fib-4 index is calculated based on age, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) level, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) level, and platelet count. Initially validated in patients with liver diseases such as MASLD, the Fib-4 index has shown prognostic value in patients with chronic coronary syndrome (CCS).8,9 However, its utility in acute coronary syndrome (ACS) is still unclear, partly due to acute increases in AST and ALT levels caused by myocardial injury or hepatic hypoperfusion. Therefore, the significance of this index as a pure marker of arteriosclerosis in ACS remains uncertain. Furthermore, the differences in the profile of this index between patients with ACS and CCS have not yet been fully elucidated.

In recent years, the drug repositioning of antidiabetic agents, such as sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists, has been found to suppress the progression of liver fibrosis.10,11 These agents reduce body weight and visceral fat, improve inflammation and oxidative stress, and have been widely recognized for their cardiovascular protective effects.12,13 These therapeutic effects are potentially linked to underlying metabolic mechanisms; therefore, understanding the Fib-4 index profile, including its baseline and longitudinal trends, might support future therapeutic strategies.

This study aimed to evaluate the baseline levels, temporal changes, and prognostic significance of the Fib-4 index in patients with ACS and CCS who underwent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) using a large, multicenter database of detailed laboratory and outcome data.

Methods

Data Source

This study was conducted retrospectively using the Clinical Deep Data Accumulation System (CLIDAS)-PCI database, which was previously referred to as CLIDAS.14–16 It started as part of the Japan Ischemic heart disease Multimodal Prospective data Acquisition for preCision Treatment (J-IMPACT) project launched in 2015.17 The database is a retrospective all-comer registry enrolling patients with ACS or CCS who underwent PCI at 7 tertiary facilities in Japan (6 university hospitals and 1 national cardiovascular center) between April 2013 and March 2019, totaling 9,690 cases. Distinctively, this infrastructure can extract comprehensive clinical information directly from the hospital information system (HIS) of each participating institution. Briefly, the Standardized Structured Medical Information eXchange version 2 (SS-MIX2) standard storage collected essential patient data, such as prescriptions and laboratory data, whereas SS-MIX2 extended storage collected the results of echocardiography, electrocardiography, and cardiac catheterization in accordance with the standard export data format.18 However, non-standardized information on detailed patients’ backgrounds and follow-up data were manually collected by researchers and data managers at each facility. All data were anonymized before being incorporating into the central server.14,15,17,18

Study Design

This study aimed to assess the detailed profiles and prognostic relevance of the Fib-4 index in patients with ACS and CCS who underwent PCI. To avoid confounding effects from acute myocardial injury, a 1-month blanking period was implemented following the index PCI. The Fib-4 index was calculated during the initial period, which was defined as 1–3 months following PCI. Patients without available laboratory data during the initial period or who died within 1 month following PCI were excluded from the analysis. The study flowchart is shown in Figure 1. Baseline characteristics and long-term clinical outcomes stratified by the initial Fib-4 index categories were examined in patients with ACS and CCS. In addition, serial measurements of the Fib-4 index were obtained at 6-month intervals, including 3–9, 9–15, 15–21, and 21–27 months post-PCI (periods 1–4, respectively; Supplementary Figure). Similarly, C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were evaluated at baseline, initial, and each subsequent period to assess longitudinal inflammatory changes.

This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki for investigation in humans and was conducted with the approval of the Jichi Medical University Bioethics Committee for Medical Research (24-164). The requirement for written informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective design of the study, and an opt-out approach was used to inform participants. The trial is registered with the University Hospital Medical Information Network Clinical Trial Registry (UMIN000057338).

Data Definitions and Risk Stratification Using the Fib-4 Index

Baseline laboratory data, except for AST and ALT levels and platelet count, were defined as the average of values measured from 2 months pre-PCI to 1 month post-PCI. Platelet count and AST, ALT, and CRP levels were obtained during the initial period and were subsequently measured at periods 1–4. The Fib-4 index was calculated using the following formula: Fib-4 index = age (years) × (AST [U/L] / (ALT [U/L]1/2 × platelet count [109/L])). The Fib-4 index was calculated using the average AST and ALT levels and platelet count obtained during each period. Furthermore, the patients were categorized into risk groups according to the initial Fib-4 index: low, <1.3; intermediate, 1.3–2.67; and high, >2.67.19 The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Japanese equation encompassing the serum creatinine level, age, and sex as follows: eGFR (mL/min/1.73 m2) = 194 × Cr−1.094 × age−0.287 (the equation was further multiplied by 0.739 for women).20 Chronic kidney disease was defined as eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2.21 Peripheral artery disease was defined as a history of non-cardiac (aortic or peripheral vascular) vascular disease. The number of diseased vessels was defined as the number of coronary arteries exhibiting severe stenosis (≥75%) in major epicardial coronary segments of the right coronary, left anterior descending, and left circumflex arteries, including their branches. The diseased left main trunk, defined as ≥75% stenosis, was counted separately. Multivessel coronary artery disease was defined as the involvement of more than 1 vessel.15

Outcome Measures

The primary endpoint was the incidence of 3-point major adverse cardiovascular events (3P-MACE). 3P-MACE were defined as the composite of cardiovascular death, non-fatal myocardial infarction, and non-fatal stroke. The secondary endpoints were the longitudinal Fib-4 index and CRP values obtained at each time point (periods 1–4), and the incidence of cardiovascular death, non-fatal myocardial infarction, and non-fatal stroke, which were components of the primary endpoint.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as medians (interquartile ranges [IQRs]) and compared using Wilcoxon’s rank-sum test. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A Sankey diagram was used to visualize the extent of transitions among Fib-4 index categories. For multiple comparisons, crude values were interpreted using the Bonferroni method.

Cumulative incidence was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. A landmark analysis was performed from 1-month post-PCI, corresponding to the start of the initial period, as patients who died within the first month were excluded. Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess the relative risk of the high Fib-4 index category compared with the low and intermediate categories, and the results are reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The HRs were also evaluated with a 1-unit increase in the Fib-4 index, treating it as a continuous variable. Furthermore, to determine whether the Fib-4 index reflects risk beyond that attributable to atherosclerotic inflammation, HRs were adjusted by the CRP level during the initial period and clinical presentation (ACS or CCS).

Subgroup analyses were conducted, and interactions between subgroups and the high Fib-4 index category were tested using the likelihood ratio tests comparing models. Subgroup variables were selected before data analysis, based on clinical considerations where differential treatment effects were plausible.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Study Population

Among the 9,690 patients registered in the CLIDAS-PCI database, 5,150 patients (2,274 with ACS and 2,876 with CCS) were included in the analysis after excluding those who died within 1 month after PCI and those with incomplete laboratory data for Fib-4 index calculation. The median follow-up periods were 1,072 (IQR 593–1,711) days and 1,094 (IQR 560–1,674) days for patients with ACS and CCS, respectively.

Baseline characteristics were significantly different between patients with ACS and those with CCS (Table 1). Compared with patients with CCS, those with ACS were younger and exhibited a lower prevalence of comorbidities, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease, dialysis, peripheral artery disease, malignancy, and atrial fibrillation. Patients with ACS also exhibited less frequent history of prior PCI, coronary bypass grafting, myocardial infarction, hospitalization for heart failure, and stroke compared with those with CCS. No significant difference was found in the sex distribution between the 2 groups. Patients with ACS were more frequently presented with multivessel disease and reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). Furthermore, patients with ACS more frequently received prasugrel, β-blockers, renin–angiotensin system (RAS) inhibitors, and statins compared with those with CCS. However, the use of diuretics was comparable between the groups. Profiles and Serial Changes of the Fib-4 Index and CRP Levels

The Fib-4 index at the initial period was significantly lower in patients with ACS than in those with CCS (1.83 vs. 1.93; P<0.001). The distribution of Fib-4 index categories also varied, as patients with ACS showed less advanced categories (low: 25% vs. 20%; intermediate: 51% vs. 54%; high: 24% vs. 26%; Table 1). The baseline characteristics of the patients according to Fib-4 index categories are shown in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. Among patients with ACS and CCS, those in higher Fib-4 index categories tended to be older, more frequently female, and had a lower body mass index (BMI). These patients also exhibited higher rates of comorbidities such as hypertension, chronic kidney disease, dialysis, malignancy, and atrial fibrillation, and more frequently experienced multivessel diseases and reduced LVEF. Conversely, dyslipidemia was less prevalent, and the use of RAS inhibitors and prasugrel was less frequent, in patients in higher Fib-4 index categories.

Throughout the follow-up period, the Fib-4 index remained consistently lower in patients with ACS across all time points (Figure 2). According to the Sankey diagram, the transitions between Fib-4 categories were infrequent (Figure 3), indicating overall longitudinal stability of Fib-4 index categories. Discussion

The key findings of this study were as follows: (1) the Fib-4 index stratified the subsequent risk of 3P-MACE in patients with ACS and CCS; (2) the transitions between Fib-4 categories were infrequent over time; (3) the Fib-4 index was consistently lower in patients with ACS than in those with CCS for up to 2 years following PCI, with a similar trend observed in CRP levels; and (4) a higher Fib-4 index was significantly associated with a high risk of 3P-MACE even after adjusting for CRP levels and type of coronary syndrome. These findings suggested that the Fib-4 index may not only reflect the magnitude of inflammation but also serve as a surrogate marker of cumulative atherosclerotic burden. This study, which involved processing a large amount of laboratory data from actual clinical practice, was only possible because of the infrastructure that can extract information directly from the HIS.

Our findings underscore the prognostic significance of the Fib-4 index in patients with ACS and CCS undergoing PCI. Although previous studies have demonstrated the prognostic relevance of the Fib-4 index in patients with CCS,8,9 its utility in those with ACS remains less well established. Transient increases in transaminase levels caused by acute myocardial injury render the evaluation and interpretation of Fib-4 in ACS challenging. In this study, this limitation was overcome by implementing a 1-month blanking period post-PCI to allow the stabilization of liver enzyme levels.

In chronic liver diseases such as MASLD, cardiometabolic comorbidities – including type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and obesity – are closely associated with the progression of liver fibrosis and have been recognized as systemic manifestations of atherosclerosis, contributing to poor clinical outcomes.1,2 Moreover, in MASLD, the association with cardiovascular disease extends beyond a mere clustering of comorbid conditions. Proinflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α and interleukin-6 have been shown to contribute to the progression of endothelial dysfunction and plaque instability, thereby promoting the development of atherosclerosis.4 In the present study, the Fib-4 index was adopted as a non-invasive tool to assess the degree of liver fibrosis. This approach was consistent with present fatty liver disease guidelines,22 which recommend the Fib-4 index for the initial risk assessment of advanced liver fibrosis. Given the established link between liver fibrosis and cardiovascular risk, the use of the Fib-4 index for cardiovascular risk stratification appears reasonable and clinically relevant.

In the present study, compared with the patients with CCS, those with ACS were generally younger and exhibited a lower prevalence of comorbidities, such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease, peripheral artery disease, and atrial fibrillation. These findings are consistent with previous reports and might reflect the differing clinical profiles of ACS and CCS.16,23 Baseline CRP levels were significantly higher in patients with ACS, potentially reflecting acute myocardial injury at presentation. However, from the initial period onward, CRP levels were consistently lower in patients with ACS than in those with CCS. Similarly, the patients with ACS showed a consistently lower Fib-4 index than those with CCS. These trends indicated that patients with CCS experience a more advanced stage of atherosclerosis, likely driven by a higher burden of cardiometabolic comorbidities, whereas patients with ACS receive more aggressive cardioprotective therapies, including statins and RAS inhibitors, which exert potent anti-inflammatory effects.24 Although both CRP and the Fib-4 index demonstrated comparable directional trends between ACS and CCS, it is important to acknowledge that these markers may reflect different pathophysiological dimensions. CRP primarily reflects the magnitude of systemic inflammation at a given time point and is markedly influenced by acute-phase reactions and pharmacological interventions. In contrast, atherosclerosis is the result of a long-standing accumulation of metabolic dysfunction and chronic low-grade inflammation.25 In this context, the Fib-4 index may reflect the cumulative burden of atherosclerosis rather than transient inflammatory activity. This conceptual distinction may explain why the Fib-4 index remained independently associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes even after adjustment for CRP levels.

When patients were stratified according to Fib-4 index categories, those with higher Fib-4 index values were older and exhibited a greater prevalence of comorbidities such as hypertension and chronic kidney disease, but showed a lower dyslipidemia rate and lower BMI. This finding indicates that advanced metabolic dysfunction does not necessarily coincide with obesity in real-world patients with coronary artery disease. In particular, older individuals or patients with long-standing diabetes might exhibit normal or low BMI because of chronic inflammation, sarcopenia, and malnutrition.26–28 These findings highlight the limitations of using BMI alone to assess metabolic status in real-world settings.29 Furthermore, the lower prescription rate of RAS inhibitors and prasugrel might reflect concerns regarding the increased risk of renal impairment and bleeding complications in older patients. Given that age is incorporated into the formula for calculating the Fib-4 index, the index increases with aging independent of actual fibrosis. Therefore, the prognostic stratification ability of the Fib-4 index might be partly attributable to its inclusion of age; however, alternative risk models might be required to more accurately assess true fibrosis, which remains a future challenge.

This study demonstrated that the Fib-4 index, a non-invasive marker of liver fibrosis, is useful for prognostic stratification in patients with ACS and CCS. It may provide clinically meaningful information for cardiovascular risk stratification in routine practice. A higher Fib-4 index identifies a subgroup of patients with an increased burden of systemic metabolic dysfunction and atherosclerosis, who may benefit from more intensive secondary prevention strategies, including tighter control of cardiovascular risk factors and closer clinical follow up after PCI.30–32 However, this study did not evaluate the therapeutic effects of specific medications. Recent studies have suggested that certain antidiabetic drugs10,11 may exert beneficial effects on liver fibrosis via drug repositioning. In the future, stratification based on the Fib-4 index might help identify patients who could benefit from such therapies. More studies are warranted to determine whether pharmacological interventions should be considered for patients with a high Fib-4 index or to evaluate how the index changes in response to treatment.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, our study was a retrospective study based on data from a non-randomized observational registry, and therefore selection bias cannot be completely excluded. In addition, a 1-month blanking period after PCI was implemented to minimize the influence of acute myocardial injury on liver enzyme levels; as a result, patients who died during this period were excluded, which may have led to the underrepresentation of high-risk patients. Second, the differences in the practice patterns and timing of laboratory measurements across participating centers may have influenced our results. Third, although the Fib-4 index was used as a surrogate marker for liver fibrosis, its concordance with the actual severity of liver fibrosis could not be confirmed because no direct assessments, such as imaging or liver biopsy, were conducted. Fourth, atherosclerosis-related comorbidities, such as hypertension, chronic kidney disease, or prior cardiovascular events, were not included in the multivariable models. This approach was based on our conceptual framework that the Fib-4 index reflects an integrative phenotype of cumulative cardiometabolic and atherosclerotic burden rather than an isolated biological factor; therefore, adjustment for multiple components of this burden may result in overadjustment and obscure the prognostic information captured by the Fib-4 index. Fifth, although a 1-month blanking period was implemented to minimize the effects of acute elevations in liver enzyme levels in ACS, residual effects from myocardial injury and other acute-phase reactions might have influenced the Fib-4 index. Sixth, unmeasured factors, such as alcohol consumption, viral hepatitis status, and other underlying liver conditions, might have affected both the Fib-4 index and clinical outcome. Last, the proportion of patients receiving SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists was low, as the study period (2013–2019) corresponded to the early phase of clinical adoption. Accordingly, interventional analyses were not conducted. This study focused on evaluating the utility and profiles of the Fib-4 index in a setting with minimal pharmaceutical intervention.

Conclusions

A higher Fib-4 index was significantly associated with an increased risk of long-term adverse cardiovascular events in patients with ACS and CCS, independent of systemic inflammation, and patients with ACS exhibited lower Fib-4 index values than those with CCS throughout the follow-up period. These results support that the Fib-4 index may not only reflect the degree of atherosclerotic inflammation but also serve as a surrogate marker of cumulative atherosclerotic burden. Future interventional analyses are warranted regarding whether the Fib-4 index can serve as a support for therapeutic strategies.
