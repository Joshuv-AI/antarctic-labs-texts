Machine Learning and Artificial Intelligence in Nutrition Research: Analytical Methods, Applications, and Key Considerations
Authors: Nicole L Southey, Ruoqing Zhu, Hannah D Holscher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Nutrition research is increasingly using artificial intelligence and machine learning to address analytical challenges posed by high-dimensional data and to enable personalized recommendations and health predictions. Objective: This review provides an overview of machine learning techniques and their application in nutrition research. Methods: The article is structured according to the steps of a typical analysis pipeline. First, we outline data quality control, preprocessing, and classical statistical tests for detecting group differences, assessing covariate associations, and prescreening input features. Next, dimension reduction and visualization methods such as principal component analysis, t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding, and uniform manifold approximation and projection are presented to simplify high-dimensional data and reveal nutrition indicators. Supervised learning approaches that support classification and outcome prediction are then reviewed, followed by unsupervised learning methods for clustering unlabeled observations. Integrative tools combining approaches such as canonical correlation analysis and supervised multiblock methods are discussed for their suitability in multiomics and multimodal studies. A comparison of commonly used supervised approaches is presented, including random forest, gradient boosting regression, penalized regression methods, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator, support vector machines, and k-nearest neighbors. Deep learning techniques, including convolutional neural networks, recurrent neural networks, long short-term memory models, natural language processing, and large language models, are highlighted for analyzing unstructured, sequential, and text-based data. To ensure the reproducibility and generalizability of findings, we discuss strategies for model validation, including cross-validation, external replication, and permutation testing. We also discuss practical considerations for implementing advanced analytical approaches in nutrition research, such as interpretability, sample size constraints, and overfitting, to guide responsible implementation. Results: A range of manuscripts were reviewed to provide vignettes exemplifying the use of artificial intelligence and machine learning in nutrition research, highlighting key methodological approaches and representative applications across diverse data types. Conclusions: Collectively, this review provides a framework for understanding and thoughtfully applying machine learning approaches to nutrition research.

Introduction

Nutrition research increasingly relies on diverse and complex datasets, including dietary assessments, clinical measures, and multiomic analyses. As these datasets grow in scale and dimensionality, advanced analytical approaches are needed to extract meaningful patterns and advance nutrition research. Artificial intelligence (AI) technologies, particularly machine learning and deep learning, provide powerful tools for addressing these challenges. AI encompasses computational methods that emulate human-like learning and decision-making, whereas machine learning is a discipline within AI that develops algorithms to learn from input data, identify patterns, and accurately predict outcomes [1,2]. Deep learning, a specialized subset of machine learning approaches, uses multilayer artificial neural networks to learn hierarchical representations from large, complex datasets.

Machine learning approaches are expanding the scope of nutrition research. However, thoughtful integration is essential. In nutrition science, machine learning should be viewed as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating framework that complements, rather than replaces, confirmatory statistical analyses. These approaches are designed to detect complex multivariate patterns that can guide subsequent experimental design and mechanistic testing. When appropriately applied and interpreted, machine learning methods enable the integration and analysis of complex nutrition datasets and support more precise hypothesis generation, personalized recommendations, and health predictions. With the increasing accessibility of these tools, identifying the methods best suited to specific research objectives, data structures, and study designs is becoming increasingly important [1,2].

This review summarizes major machine learning approaches and their application in nutrition research. We aim to provide both a conceptual and practical guide to understanding these approaches, the types of questions they are best suited to address, and their limitations. Ultimately, the review is written to provide nutrition researchers with a practical framework for selecting and evaluating machine learning approaches.

Overview

In this review, we present a practical analytical workflow for nutrition researchers by organizing machine learning approaches according to the sequential steps of a typical data analysis pipeline and illustrating each step with relevant case studies. For each method category, we describe the rationale, provide a brief methodological overview, and highlight ≥1 application to nutrition research. We begin with foundational steps common to nearly all nutrition datasets: data quality control and preprocessing (Preprocessing section) and classical statistical testing to compare groups or assess associations (Classical Group Comparison, Association Tests, and Prescreening section), which can be used to prescreen features and identify informative input features for subsequent analyses [4]. We next introduce feature engineering [37] and feature selection strategies (Feature Engineering section), which transform raw nutritional, clinical, or omics variables into representations suitable for machine learning models. We then discuss dimensionality reduction and visualization methods (Dimension Reduction and Pattern Visualization section), including principal component analysis (PCA) [4], t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) [13], and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) [26]. These approaches help reveal structure in high-dimensional datasets, such as microbiome and epigenomic profiles.

Building on these steps, we review supervised learning approaches for outcome prediction (Supervised Learning Approaches section) and unsupervised methods that identify latent sample structure without predefined outcome labels (Unsupervised Clustering section). Examples of integrative analyses include combining K-means clustering with canonical correlation analysis (CCA) [6], as well as supervised multiblock tools such as DIABLO (Data Integration Analysis for Biomarker discovery using Latent Components) [11]. Several supervised learning approaches are reviewed: tree-based methods (e.g., random forest [17,18]), classification algorithms [e.g., k-nearest neighbors (k-NN) [4] and gradient boosting regression [20]], penalized regression [e.g., least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) [4], elastic net (EN) [9], and ridge regression], and support vector machines (SVMs) [4]. As each supervised learning approach has context-specific strengths and limitations, we review multiple approaches to support informed method selection in nutrition research.

We also discuss validation strategies, which are essential for evaluating model performance, preventing overfitting, and ensuring generalizability (Machine Learning Performance, Validation, and Evaluation section). Next, we provide an overview of causal inference frameworks, an approach that allows for extension beyond associative modeling (Causal Inference section). Given the frequent need to integrate different data types in nutrition research, we then review data integration techniques for multiomics and multimodal research (Advanced Machine Learning Approaches for Complex Multimodal Research section). Advanced deep learning and language-based approaches (Advanced Machine Learning Methods and AI Tools section), including convolutional neural networks (CNNs) [38], recurrent neural networks (RNNs) [4], and long short-term memory (LSTM) models [30], as well as natural language processing (NLP) [32,33] and large language models (LLMs) [36], are also presented in the context of their emerging applications in nutrition science.

The review concludes with an outlook on emerging methods, data resources, and ethical considerations (Conclusion section). Lastly, we provide a summary of key considerations for the appropriate use and interpretation of advanced analytic approaches, including the types of questions addressed by different techniques and their respective strengths and limitations (Table 2). In practice, the choice of analytical approach should be guided by the scientific question, data structure, and sample size rather than by algorithmic novelty. Many nutrition studies are characterized by modest sample sizes relative to feature dimensionality. In these settings, simpler or regularized machine learning methods may outperform more complex deep learning approaches. Conversely, large and heterogeneous datasets may support more flexible models. Throughout this review, we emphasize that machine learning should be applied as a decision framework to select the method that best addresses the biological question while preserving interpretability, reproducibility, and generalizability. Careful preprocessing, validation, and transparent reporting are as important as model choice itself.

Characteristics of nutrition research data

Nutrition studies often collect biological samples (e.g., stool, blood) along with participant information from questionnaires and assign dietary treatments. The expected input for statistical or machine learning software requires that data be organized into accepted input formats, typically as matrices in which rows represent individual participants and columns represent sample features. For example, 16S rRNA or metagenomic sequencing produces raw microbial reads that are processed into operational taxonomic unit counts or functional profiles that are organized in columns, whereas the analysis of food records generates columns of nutrient intake estimates [39,40].

Nutrition studies typically produce high-dimensional datasets, where each feature (e.g., a microbial taxon or nutrient intake value) represents 1 dimension of the data. Moreover, the number of features can greatly exceed the number of participants in the study, increasing the risk of model overfitting. In addition, data collection may span multiple years or locations, thereby increasing the likelihood that measurements may have batch effects (e.g., differences across sequencing runs, laboratory protocols, or interviewer questionnaires) [41]. The prolonged timeline or multifactorial characteristics of studies may also increase the rate of missing measurements and associated data sparsity, outliers, confounding artifacts, and complex correlations between variables. If left unaddressed, these challenges can obscure true biological signals, leading machine learning models to capture noise rather than meaningful patterns [42]. To mitigate these risks, preprocessing steps are a critical first step in the analytic pipeline. These steps form the foundation for reliable and interpretable statistical and machine learning analyses [43].

Preprocessing

Before any machine learning model is applied, data must be cleaned and standardized to enable effective analysis. Preprocessing encompasses quality control and associated cleaning, standardization, or normalization of raw data. Protocols for handling missing observations and outliers need to be specified, and the potential impact on subsequent analysis considered. Variables that have a high proportion of missing data can be removed or imputed using standardized methods [43]. For example, in a dietary cohort study, participants with missing nutrient intake values were excluded from analysis [21]. More advanced approaches, such as multiple imputations or machine learning–based imputation, are also available and can help reduce bias compared with simple methods. However, excluding participants with missing values may reduce statistical power or introduce systematic bias, so the choice of approach requires careful consideration.

The preprocessing step also includes an assessment of the alignment between the expected and observed distributions of features, including the detection of extreme observations that may affect the results. Solutions for handling extreme observations often include filtering or data transformation. For continuous biomarker data, such as blood glucose concentration, an extreme value may indicate a technical error that is appropriate for removal [44]. In microbiome research, samples with extreme community structures (i.e., very high or low taxon representation) can be filtered based on the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity distance metric [8,41]. However, some extreme measurements may represent true biological variation and should remain in the dataset. As such, nonparametric methods that do not rely on distributional assumptions can provide an alternative to outright exclusion, while maintaining sample size and avoiding unnecessary reductions in statistical power.

High-throughput technologies generate measurements across hundreds and thousands of features that differ in magnitude and scale. For example, some metabolites are detected at low concentrations, whereas others have high concentrations across groups. Likewise, the concentration of some metabolites may be consistent within a group, whereas others may be more variable. This range of distributional behaviors can influence the results in subsequent analytical steps that simultaneously analyze all the metabolites. Normalization techniques are used to adjust for variable scales and compositional effects [45]. For example, the centered log-ratio transformation is often used in microbiota studies to correct compositional biases in taxonomic abundance data [8,41]. Likewise, normalization can be applied to scale numerical features such as weight, energy intake, and nutrient content [46]. Z-score normalization is commonly applied to nutritional risk scores, anthropometric, and biochemical data before multidimensional reduction and machine learning approaches. A nutritional risk study using facial images showed improved model performance after Z-score normalization [47]. In a nutrition recommendation study, researchers applied min–max normalization to standardize feature ranges to [0, 1] across all participants and product profiles before training deep learning approaches [27].

Preprocessing also includes consideration for batch effects. Batch effects represent systematic technical variation from factors such as study site, sequencing run, background diet, or experimental timing. These effects are particularly relevant in multicohort studies and can obscure true biological signals if not addressed. For example, in a 16S rRNA gene sequencing analysis aggregating 5 controlled feeding trials [almond (n = 18), avocado (n = 163), broccoli (n = 18), walnut (n = 18), and whole grains (n = 68); total n = 285 participants; 340 observations] [16], microbial profiles initially reflected study-specific background variation. To isolate food-specific microbial signals, PCA was applied to control-group samples to characterize the background structure. Treatment samples were then projected into the control principal component space, and principal components capturing systematic background variation were identified using the elbow method and removed using singular value decomposition. Visualization before and after correction demonstrated reduced study-driven clustering and improved separation by dietary exposure. Similar considerations were addressed in a subsequent shotgun metagenomics analysis of the same trials [48], where log-fold change normalization and within-subject comparisons were used to mitigate interstudy variation prior to training random forest classifiers. These preprocessing steps reduced the risk that models would classify study participation rather than food consumption.

In summary, preprocessing is a critical step in preparing data for subsequent analysis [43]. For additional considerations and suggestions specific to diet–microbiome data preprocessing and integration, see the review by Choi et al. [49]. Without careful preprocessing and data harmonization, downstream machine learning approaches may capture artifacts of measurement or scaling rather than meaningful biological signals.

Classical Group Comparison, Association Tests, and Prescreening

A common step after the data are cleaned and normalized (i.e., preprocessed) is to use statistical tests to identify which variables show meaningful differences or associations. The process of identifying the most relevant features for future integration is referred to as “prescreening.” Classical statistical methods, such as t-tests and analysis of variance to compare features between groups and regression models to detect covariation between continuous features, can be used when distributional assumptions are met. Regression models may also be applied to detect covariation between continuous features. When normality assumptions are violated, nonparametric alternatives such as the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (2 groups) or the Kruskal–Wallis test (>2 groups) can be applied [41]. These statistical methods test the null hypothesis of no differences between groups or no association between variables. The results highlight the features with the strongest signals, allowing subsequent analyses to focus on the most informative variables while reducing computational burden. The prescreening step is particularly important in nutrition research, where high-throughput technologies often generate datasets containing far more features than independent samples or participants [50].

An example of statistical prescreening in a high-dimensional nutrition dataset is provided by a shotgun metagenomics analysis of controlled feeding trials [48]. Across samples, >6000 Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes ortholog (KO) features were initially detected. Within each food intervention study, the treatment and control groups were compared using Student’s t-tests applied to log-fold-change-normalized KO counts. To account for multiple testing, false discovery rate correction was applied, and features meeting an adjusted threshold of q < 0.20 were retained. This filtering step substantially reduced the feature space prior to machine learning, yielding 54 significant KOs in the almond intervention, 732 in walnut, and 2474 in broccoli. These retained features were then used as inputs for random forest classification. This example demonstrates how classical statistical testing can be integrated into machine learning workflows to focus on biologically relevant signals and reduce dimensionality.

Although classical parametric and nonparametric approaches are useful for identifying informative features, multiple testing adjustment of resulting P values is recommended when applied to the analysis of hundreds to thousands of features in high-dimensional datasets. Methods such as false discovery rate control (e.g., Benjamini–Hochberg), family-wise error rate control (e.g., Bonferroni correction), or permutation-based procedures can be applied to reduce false positives [51]. Features with 0 variance should be removed because they contribute no information. Similarly, features with low variability may contribute little signal relative to noise and can be considered for removal depending on study aims. The balance between type I and type II errors also applies to prescreening, and theoretically grounded procedures such as false discovery rate control are generally preferred over arbitrary alpha thresholds. However, in settings where no features meet strict corrected significance criteria, P values may be used to rank variables by relative strength of association rather than applying a rigid cutoff. In this context, ranking serves as a prioritization strategy to retain potentially informative features for subsequent modeling while acknowledging the exploratory nature of the analysis.

Regarding collinearity, rather than applying an arbitrary correlation threshold to exclude variables, a stepwise or regularized regression approach that evaluates features jointly within the predictive modeling framework may provide more robust selection criteria.

A common prescreening tool when testing the association between continuous features is correlation analysis. When data are normally distributed, the Pearson correlation coefficient can be used to measure the strength of linear relationships between continuous variables. The nonparametric Spearman rank correlation detects monotonic trends, and the test is more robust to skew or nonnormal data [41]. For example, in the ATTICA study of dietary patterns and nutrient profiles, the Pearson correlation was used to identify associations between nutrient intake and cardiometabolic risk scores, which were then selected for further analysis [21]. These classical methods are valuable for identifying simple associations, but they may overlook nonlinear or interaction effects that can be captured by machine learning approaches. As such, prescreening should be considered one component of a broader analytical strategy.

Feature Engineering

Feature engineering is a type of data preprocessing performed before applying machine learning approaches. Conceptually, feature engineering transforms raw data into new variables that better reflect biologically or clinically meaningful constructs. For example, height and weight can be combined to calculate BMI (in kg/m2), transforming 2 measurements into a single biologically interpretable variable. Similarly, nutrient variables derived from diet questionnaires or records can be summarized into a composite healthy eating index score.

In nutrition research, feature engineering is frequently an important step rather than merely an optional enhancement. Most nutrition studies are modest in sample size and grounded in strong domain knowledge, and many commonly used machine learning models cannot automatically derive biologically meaningful constructs from raw variables. For example, linear and penalized regression models will not inherently combine height and weight to infer BMI, nor will they recognize which nutrients should be grouped based on shared nutritional or physiological effects. Without explicit feature construction informed by domain expertise, important relationships may remain obscured, limiting both predictive performance and interpretability.

Nutritional studies often generate datasets with many related variables, and meaningful analysis may require transforming the raw data into new biologically or numerically informed features. Such transformations can reveal associations that are not apparent when individual variables are considered in isolation. For example, instead of using weight measured at 2 or more timepoints, engineered features such as percentage weight change, rate of change over time, or trajectory slope may better capture meaningful longitudinal patterns relevant to metabolic risk. Feature engineering can also improve prediction accuracy by grouping participants into biologically or clinically meaningful categories, such as BMI categories (e.g., overweight or obese). An obesity prediction study provides an illustration of large-scale feature engineering: rather than using the raw health system data from ∼53,000 children and 36,000 mothers in a New York safety-net system, the investigators constructed ∼19,000 predictor variables through activities such as calculating growth trajectories and collapsing diagnosis codes into standardized disease groupings [52].

Beyond constructing composite or longitudinal variables, feature engineering can also incorporate known structural relationships within datasets. Hierarchical feature engineering approaches have emerged to leverage the structured nature of dietary and microbiome data, which are inherently organized into known hierarchies such as food groupings (e.g., food category, nutrient) or taxonomic classifications (e.g., phyla, genus) [49]. Rather than treating foods or taxa as independent variables, these methods incorporate parent–child relationships to improve interpretability and reduce dimensionality. For example, DietDiveR enables the construction of food trees from 24-h dietary recall data while preserving the hierarchical relationships among food and nutrient data within the USDA’s Food and Nutrient Database for Dietary Studies [53]. Building on this framework, Taxonomic Hierarchical Feature Engineering applies machine learning–based hierarchical feature engineering to collapse food or taxonomic trees to the most informative nodes, selecting features that retain predictive power while enhancing interpretability [54]. These hierarchical features can then be carried forward into downstream machine learning models. Applications of this approach in nutrition research have demonstrated improved explainability by enabling the identification of specific foods driving outcomes, rather than relying solely on aggregated dietary scores [[55], [56], [57]].

In summary, feature engineering can serve as a bridge between domain knowledge and machine learning implementation. By transforming raw variables into biologically meaningful constructs, feature engineering may enhance interpretability, reduce dimensionality, and improve predictive performance in downstream modeling. In nutrition research, where sample sizes are often modest and variables are biologically interrelated, thoughtful feature engineering grounded in nutrition science can facilitate the development of models that are more interpretable and aligned with physiological relationships.

Dimension Reduction and Pattern Visualization

Nutrition datasets often contain hundreds to thousands of correlated variables, making it difficult to interpret relationships directly. Dimensionality reduction techniques address this challenge by summarizing complex datasets into fewer representative patterns, similar to how dietary pattern analysis captures overall eating behaviors using nutrient and food data. For example, dimensionality reduction methods may group correlated plant-based foods, such as fruits, vegetables, legumes, whole grains, nuts, and seeds, into 1 overarching dietary pattern, whereas animal-derived foods, such as beef, pork, poultry, and fish, cluster into a separate pattern, reflecting underlying eating behaviors rather than isolated food items. In addition to complementing prescreening, dimensionality reduction techniques facilitate visualization of the data structure (e.g., correlated or confounding features) and can reduce overfitting when models are trained on high-dimensional inputs.

A dimension reduction approach commonly used in nutrition research is the PCA. PCA identifies uncorrelated linear combinations of variables, known as principal components, and the top components capture the highest percentage of the original variance in the data [4]. For example, PCA is frequently applied to reduce the dimensionality of microbiome-derived feature sets [16]. Factor analysis is an alternative dimension reduction technique that identifies latent variables responsible for correlations among input features, whereas PCA develops components that explain the maximum total variance in the dataset.

Visualization tools such as UMAP and t-SNE allow the projection of high-dimensional data into 2 or 3 dimensions for exploratory inspection and result validation [26]. A valuable feature of UMAP is the preservation of local neighborhoods of closely related samples at lower dimension representations, and it often provides better preservation of global structure across all the samples, highlighting biologically relevant groupings [14].

Complementing the advantages of UMAP, t-SNE is particularly effective at maintaining local neighborhoods and separating proximal clusters, at the cost of lower reliability in preserving the global distances between clusters [13]. Although UMAP and t-SNE approaches facilitate the visualization of sample clusters (e.g., by dietary intake or microbiome composition), caution should be exercised to prevent overinterpreting these visualizations. Apparent clusters may be more influenced by algorithmic properties or parameter settings than by true biological structure. Therefore, multidimensional reduction and associated visualization approaches should be treated as exploratory tools rather than definitive evidence.

A particular challenge of some features typical in nutrition studies, such as microbiome profiling, is that the taxonomic abundances are compositional in nature. Specifically, the proportions of all the operational taxonomic units within a sample sum to one. Therefore, an increase in the proportion of one component results in a decrease in the proportion of others, even if the absolute levels did not change between groups. This numerical constraint can distort standard dimensionality reduction methods like PCA and the resulting principal components. A common strategy to address this issue is to apply the centered log-ratio transformation to the operational taxonomic unit percentages or proportions, which converts each component into a log ratio relative to the geometric mean of all components [8,58]. Some subsequent unsupervised approaches to uncover sample clusters or supervised approaches to predict sample classifications incorporate data normalization or standardization approaches to address the nature of features with distributional restrictions [43].

Dimension reduction and many machine learning techniques generate indices or combinations of the original features to extract maximum information from the observations. This strategy generates patterns highly associated with the outcomes of interest and optimizes the predictive capability of the resulting indices (e.g., principal components or factors). Patterns often explain substantial variation in the data and may be associated with outcomes of interest (e.g., microbiome composition may vary substantially between healthy individuals and those with inflammatory bowel disease during a flare). However, this strategy does not ensure interpretability. In many nutrition contexts, particularly those informing clinical or policy decisions, prioritizing interpretability is often the priority [59]. For example, a review of AI applications in nutrition and dietetics for image-based dietary assessment reported that limited interpretability has constrained clinical integration of certain decision-support tools [60]. Explainable machine learning models have been proposed as a solution to achieve high predictive performance while offering interpretable estimates. This approach was successfully applied to predict malnutrition in children with congenital heart disease, providing clinicians with insights into identifying high-risk patients [3].

Supervised Learning Approaches

Once data are prepared, predictive modeling becomes possible. After preprocessing, prescreening, and dimensionality reduction, the next analytical step is often outcome prediction. Supervised learning approaches can be used to predict health and nutrition-related outcomes by learning patterns from labeled data. In nutrition studies, this means using features such as dietary intake records and clinical biomarkers to predict outcomes, such as disease status [11]. The following sections review supervised learning algorithms commonly applied in nutrition research, whereas technical and implementation details are described elsewhere [61].

k-Nearest neighbors

The k-NN algorithm is a nonparametric supervised classification method that assigns an unlabeled observation to the class of the majority of its closest labeled neighbors. Conceptually, k-NN is a similarity-based approach: individuals with comparable dietary or biomarker profiles are grouped together and often share similar health outcomes, such as cardiovascular disease risk. The k-NN approach does not assume a specific functional form or model to integrate the input features. It can classify samples into multiple categories. The k-NN algorithm performs well when the data exhibit clear groupings, such as dietary patterns (e.g., Western compared with Mediterranean Diet Pattern). This method is computationally efficient when the number of features is small (e.g., <50–100 predictors), but the computational cost and distance instability increases as the number of features increases. k-NN model performance (i.e., accuracy) depends heavily on the choice of the neighborhood size “k.” The k parameter is typically tuned using cross-validation by testing multiple k values and selecting the value that performs well across subsets [62]. Performance of the k-NN approach also depends on the method used to compute the distance between neighboring observations. Inverse distance weighting is commonly applied so that closer, more similar observations contribute more strongly to classification decisions. As a result, normalization of variables to a common scale is a common practice. Like other distance-based approaches to classify observations, k-NN is sensitive to noninformative, variable, or noisy features, which can distort the geometry of the feature space by artificially pulling observations closer together or farther apart in the feature space. A nutrient with large variability across foods, for example, can disproportionately influence distance calculations, making otherwise similar foods appear dissimilar and reduce prediction accuracy [63].

The k-NN approach was applied in the ATTICA study, which classified participants into 3 cardiometabolic health risk groups based on dietary and nutrient-pattern questionnaires [21]. Factor analysis (varimax rotation) was first used to derive underlying dietary patterns, which were normalized and used as input features for k-NN classification. The analytic sample included >2000 participants, which enabled 10-fold cross-validation to evaluate model performance. Given the 3-class outcome, random classification would be expected to yield an accuracy of ∼33%. The mean cross-validated accuracy of the k-NN model was 40% with food-derived patterns and 37% with nutrient-derived patterns. For the food-pattern model, sensitivity for correctly identifying individuals in the highest cardiometabolic health category was 39%, whereas specificity (correctly identifying those not in that category) was 82%. The nutrient-pattern model yielded similar specificity (79%) but slightly lower overall accuracy. Although predictive performance was modest, the k-NN models exceeded chance classification and outperformed regression-based classification (16%–22%), suggesting that nonparametric methods captured multivariate similarity structures in the dietary data not well represented by linear models.

Penalized regression methods: ridge, LASSO, and EN

Nutrition research datasets frequently contain more features than participants, and many predictors are highly correlated, such as nutrient intake variables. When the number of samples is small relative to the number of predictors, standard regression models can become overparameterized. Multicollinearity among predictors may further lead to unstable coefficient estimates and poor model performance [64]. Penalized regression methods address these structural challenges by adding regularization terms that restrict model complexity [65]. Conceptually, these approaches prioritize the most informative variables while shrinking the influence of weak or redundant predictors. This is particularly useful in nutrition datasets containing many related measurements, such as detailed nutrient intake profiles [e.g., total calories, total fat (g), percentage calories from fat, saturated fat (g), and percentage calories from saturated fat, etc.]. Penalized regression shrinks the coefficients of weakly associated variables toward 0 while retaining those most strongly linked to the outcomes of interest. For models predicting continuous outcomes, performance is typically evaluated using metrics such as the Pearson correlation coefficient (R), mean absolute error, and mean squared error, which quantify the difference between the observed and predicted values [66].

Ridge regression applies an L2 penalty (the sum of squared regression coefficients), shrinking the coefficients toward 0 without eliminating them, thereby stabilizing predictions when many correlated predictors are present. Ridge regression is most appropriate when most predictors carry some signal or association with the outcome, as in dietary pattern analyses [22]. In contrast, the LASSO uses an L1 penalty that can shrink some coefficients ≤0, effectively performing variable selection [61]. In nutrition studies, LASSO is often used to identify predictive features from large panels of dietary components or biomarkers [7]. EN combines both L1 and L2 penalties, balancing the stability of the ridge with the feature selection capabilities of LASSO, and is particularly well-suited for nutrition biomarker discovery and health risk prediction based on multiomics profiles [9].

An EN approach was used to study the relationship between dairy consumption and type 2 diabetes risk [22]. From a panel of 385 plasma metabolites, the EN method selected 38 features associated with dietary intake patterns and achieved a predictive accuracy of 37%, demonstrating that penalized regression can uncover biologically meaningful predictors in high-dimensional nutrition data [22].

Tree-based approaches

Tree-based ensemble algorithms are well-suited to identify nonlinear relationships and interactions that are common in high-dimensional nutrition datasets [67]. Conceptually, these methods repeatedly divide participants into subgroups based on combinations of factors (e.g., dietary intake, health biomarkers, lifestyle factors) to improve the prediction of outcomes such as risk of developing breast cancer. Common tree-based approaches, including random forest and boosting methods, are particularly effective for modeling complex relationships in nutrient and omics data [68]. By aggregating multiple decision trees, these models can capture interactions among nutrients or microbes that are difficult to model using traditional regression approaches.

The random forest approach comprises a collection of decision trees in which samples are iteratively split into subsets based on feature information [18]. The weighted combination of the decision tree outputs produces an interpretable variable importance score that serves as a ranked list of influential features [17]. Random forest is well-suited for sample classification tasks involving high-dimensional, sparse data, such as nutrient prediction from trees that use microbiome profiles.

For example, a multiclass random forest model was applied to 16S rRNA gene sequencing data from 5 feeding trials (n = 285 participants; 340 paired baseline-post observations) to predict consumption of specific foods (almonds, avocados, broccoli, walnuts, and whole grain oats and barley) [16]. Before model training, bacteria feature selection was performed using marginal screening, and preprocessing steps, including batch effect correction as described in the Preprocessing section. The random forest model was trained using 2000 trees, and performance was estimated using out-of-bag validation. The model achieved an overall classification accuracy of 70% across 6 foods (avocado, almond, walnuts, oats, barley, and broccoli), which increased to 77% after collapsing the 2 grain categories (barley and oats) into 1 grain category, and to 85% when restricting the analysis to 4 foods (broccoli was removed from the model because of low predictive accuracy). Validation analyses confirmed that classification performance reflected food-specific microbial signatures rather than study-specific variation.

Beyond standard random forest classification, extensions of tree-based methods have been developed to estimate heterogeneous treatment effects for multivariate outcomes. For example, Multiple Outcome Treatment Effect Forests (MOTEF) extend the random forest framework to jointly model pre–post changes across multiple correlated biomarkers in randomized feeding trials. Rather than predicting a single outcome, MOTEF recursively partitions the preintervention covariate space using oblique splits informed by multivariate canonical correlation structure, allowing estimation of individualized treatment effects across several endpoints simultaneously. The method was applied to nutrition intervention studies examining almond (n = 68) and avocado (n = 108) consumption, demonstrating that multivariate random forest approaches can capture heterogeneity in diet-induced effects on inflammatory markers and cardiometabolic biomarkers while accounting for correlated microbiota features aggregated into 3 microbial phenotypes [69]. Tree-based models are also well-suited to incorporating structured, domain-informed features derived from biological knowledge (see Data Integration section for an example using genome-scale metabolic modeling) [70].

Among the advanced decision tree-based approaches, gradient boosting regression builds decision trees sequentially enabling each new tree to reduce the errors from the previous tree's outcome prediction. The gradient boosting regression approach can be used to improve outcome prediction accuracy in the presence of complex feature interactions such as the combined effects of microbiome, diet, and lifestyle [20]. These attributes make gradient boosting regression well-suited for prediction in nutrition research with highly variable features, such as personalized responses to a range of foods. This approach was successfully applied to postprandial glycemic responses (PPGRs) and was accurately predicted using gradient boosting regression from a dataset including clinical, lifestyle, nutritional, and microbiome values from 800 participants [19]. The tree-based approach achieved a Pearson correlation of 0.68 in cross-validation and 0.70 in an independent validation cohort for predicting PPGRs and was subsequently used to design personalized dietary recommendations. Gradient boosting regression was used to design personalized “good” and “bad” diets. In the predictor-based arm, 10 of 12 participants (83%) achieved significantly lower PPGRs on the algorithm-designed diet compared with the “bad” diet, whereas in the expert (dietitian-guided) arm, 8 of 14 participants (57%) achieved significantly lower PPGRs. Although the sample size was modest, these findings demonstrate the potential of gradient boosting models to capture interindividual variability in glycemic response [19].

Support vector machines for high-dimensional classification

SVMs are supervised learning algorithms used for prediction and classification. Conceptually, SVM separates groups by identifying a boundary (hyperplane) that maximizes the distance between the closest samples from different classes. For example, in nutrition research, SVMs can be used to separate individuals based on biomarker profiles associated with phenotypes such as metabolically healthy compared with unhealthy obesity. The SVM algorithm seeks to maximize the margin between classes while minimizing misclassification, thereby reducing sensitivity to highly variable (noisy) or correlated predictors [71].

Kernel functions are a central component of SVM models. They determine how similarity between samples is calculated and whether the algorithm assumes linear or nonlinear relationships between variables. A linear kernel treats the data as separable using straight boundaries, whereas nonlinear kernels transform the data so that patterns that overlap in the original feature space become separable in a higher-dimensional space. The choice of kernel is therefore a modeling decision that reflects assumptions about the structure of the data. In nutrition research, nonlinear kernels are often used because dietary and biological relationships are rarely purely linear. This capability makes SVM particularly useful for classifying high-dimensional datasets, such as metabolomic profiles, microbiome data, or nutrient variables [4].

In a study of neonatal jaundice involving 196 neonates (98 with neonatal jaundice and 98 healthy controls), multiomics gut microbiome and bile acid metabolite profiles were analyzed to identify predictive biomarkers [8]. After feature selection using LASSO to reduce dimensionality, the selected bacterial and bile acid features were used as inputs to train an SVM classifier. The SVM model achieved strong discriminatory performance, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.91 in the discovery cohort and 0.99 in the validation cohort. Although these results demonstrate that SVMs can effectively handle high-dimensional, nonlinear multiomics data to provide strong discrimination within this particular study cohort, independent validation in larger, more heterogeneous populations is needed to confirm generalizability.

Modeling multiple outcomes: linear methods and PLS-based approaches

Among the supervised classification methods, linear discriminant analysis projects high-dimensional input features (e.g., metabolomic concentrations) into a lower-dimensional space that maximizes separation between group centroids. Conversely, partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) extends partial least squares regression to classification by identifying latent variables that describe the covariance between predictors and outcome classes (e.g., diets or health groups). Conceptually, PLS-DA relates large sets of correlated nutrition variables (e.g., nutrient values obtained from diet history questionnaires) to health outcomes by constructing underlying components that capture shared variation between diet and phenotype. The PLS-DA algorithm is particularly useful when analyzing high-dimensional datasets with relatively few samples (e.g., n < 100), especially when the number of predictors exceeds the number of observations (P > n) and strong multicollinearity is present. By focusing on a small number of latent variables, PLS-DA reduces model complexity and helps limit overfitting [72].

The software DIABLO uses PLS-DA for supervised classification and is designed for integrating multiomics datasets [11]. This approach was applied in a small, exploratory in vitro study investigating the effects of apple-derived dietary fibers on pooled samples from lean (n = 3) and obese (n = 3) individuals. Microbial communities, metagenomic (KO) and metabolomic datasets were first shown to exhibit significant interomic concordance (Procrustes analysis, P = 0.001). DIABLO was then applied to identify a minimal set of coordinated microbial functions and metabolites that discriminated among apple, apple pectin, cellulose, and control conditions. The first 2 DIABLO components were highly discriminative for apple and apple pectin treatments, respectively. This study demonstrates that supervised discriminant analysis can be applied to combine information from different omics techniques to develop diet classifiers and uncover interactions between dietary, microbiome, and metabolomic signals [10].

Unsupervised Clustering

Although supervised learning focuses on prediction, unsupervised clustering methods aim to group samples into discrete categories based on shared patterns in the data, without requiring predefined labels. Unlike dimensionality reduction techniques, which generate low-dimensional embeddings to summarize variation, clustering methods explicitly assign samples to groups, facilitating the interpretation of subgroup-specific characteristics. In nutrition research, clustering approaches can be used to partition individuals, foods, or biological samples into subgroups that share similar nutrient profiles, microbiome compositions, or metabolomic signatures. These approaches explore natural structure in complex datasets. Allowing the model to reveal data patterns independent of labels can support novel discoveries into factors underpinning group similarities. Unsupervised clustering methods often integrate diverse data types, such as nutrient composition, microbiome profiles, or metabolite concentrations, to uncover clusters with similar characteristics.

One common strategy combines CCA with clustering algorithms such as K-means. CCA identifies linear combinations of features that are maximally correlated across datasets, enabling integration of multimodal data [6]. These correlated components can then be used as input for the nonhierarchical K-means clustering, which assigns samples to the nearest cluster centroid [4]. Dimensionality reduction methods such as UMAP may subsequently be used to visualize the relative position of clusters in lower-dimensional space [14]. In nutrition research, this combined CCA and K-means clustering framework is well-suited for identifying relationships among distinct data types and shared biological profiles [5].

The combination of CCA and K-means clustering was applied to study immune dynamics of early postpartum human milk samples (n = 128,016 milk-derived immune cells) [5]. The analysis integrated single-cell RNA sequencing transcriptome data from milk-derived immune cells to identify samples with shared profiles and immune changes during the first 2 wk after birth. First, CCA was used to identify correlations among gene expression and cytokine profiles, followed by K-means clustering of the samples into distinct immune phenotypes, and UMAP visualization to track gene expression trajectories over time. Compared with linear modeling approaches, this unsupervised integration enabled the detection of coordinated multivariate immune changes across data modalities, improving the characterization of postpartum immune dynamics [5].

Machine Learning Performance, Validation, and Evaluation

Regardless of the modeling strategy, performance evaluation is essential. Model evaluation and validation can be intuitively understood as safeguards that determine whether a machine learning model has learned meaningful biological patterns from the dataset rather than noise. They also assess whether model performance is likely to generalize beyond the data used for training. Supervised learning studies rely on quantitative metrics to summarize the quality of predictions. For classification outcomes, these performance metrics include accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC, which reflect correct predictions, false positives and negatives, and discrimination performance [12].

Performance metrics can be calculated on both training and validation datasets. Validation is essential to ensure the reliability, interpretability, and generalizability of the prediction functions [66]. The primary validating strategies used in machine learning studies include cross-validation and external validation. Cross-validation uses subsets of the original dataset, where a portion of the data is temporarily withheld during model training and then used to estimate performance. In contrast, external validation evaluates model performance in a completely independent dataset collected separately from the original training cohort [73].

Regarding practical implementation, performance assessment depends on the dataset size. In nutrition research, “large” datasets typically refer to cohorts with more than several thousand observations, where sufficient data are available to support separate training, validation, and testing subsets [74]. In such cases, 60%–80% of the data are commonly used for model training, 10%–20% for hyperparameter tuning, and the remaining 10%–20% for independent performance evaluation [59]. In contrast, smaller studies, such as controlled feeding studies (often n < 100), may yield unstable performance estimates when simple training–testing splits are used. Instability in this context refers to substantial variability in model performance depending on how the data are partitioned. For these smaller datasets, repeated resampling procedures or k-fold cross-validation are recommended to obtain more reliable estimates of generalizability. Because appropriate thresholds depend on model complexity, feature dimensionality, and expected signal strength, detailed guidance for sample size planning and model evaluation should be consulted in methodological references specific to the analytic approach [74].

Cross-validation approaches apply to both classical statistical and supervised machine learning models. The validation strategy should be guided by dataset size, data structure, and model complexity to reduce the risk of overfitting. In smaller nutrition studies (e.g., n < 100), simple training–testing splits may yield unstable performance estimates, whereas moderate to larger cohorts (e.g., n > 500) can more reliably support separate training, validation, and testing partitions. Because appropriate thresholds depend on predictor dimensionality, model flexibility, and outcome balance, more detailed methodological guidance on validation strategy and sample size planning is provided in [75].

K-fold cross-validation splits the dataset into k subsets and validates the predictor trained on k–1 datasets on the remaining folds [4]. For example, a 10-fold cross-validation partitions the data into 10 equally sized subsets, trains the classifier on 9 subsets, and applies the classifier to the remaining subset, repeating the process 10 times [23]. Robust k-fold cross-validation requires stratification or grouping to ensure that each subset includes adequate representation of the levels of important study factors such as gender or age [74]. In leave-one-out cross-validation, the classifier is trained on all samples or datapoints except one, and validated on the excluded sample or datapoint; this process is repeated across the dataset [4]. Alternatively, out-of-bag validation is commonly reported in bootstrap resampling analyses and uses the samples not used by the bootstrap aggregates to test the output functions [16]. All these cross-validation approaches rely on partitions of the original dataset and therefore provide internal estimates of model performance rather than true external validation. For high-dimensional datasets such as those collected in multiomics and microbiome studies, additional confirmatory steps beyond cross-validation are often needed to minimize the potential effect of noise not removed by the normalization step on the outputs [50]. For these types of studies, permutation testing can help assess the statistical contribution of the selected feature by comparing the performance of the predictor with that of functions trained on randomly relabeled outcomes [8]. Procrustes analysis can be used to compare datasets from predicted with observed microbial patterns to confirm that they have a similar population structure [10]. These tools are helpful in settings where it is important to ensure that selected features are biologically relevant.

Although cross-validation strategies help assess the performance of classical statistical and supervised machine learning approaches, the ideal way to confirm the predictive equations is external validation using an independent cohort. External validation is important because measurements depend on the specific population and representation analyzed, such as dietary patterns and associated microbiome patterns [76]. An external cohort also helps test real-world variability in participant characteristics (e.g., across geographical regions), sample handling (e.g., microbiome sequencing across different platforms), and data-generation protocols. Validation of predictive functions and results in a separate cohort strengthens the assumption that the conclusions are not significantly affected by noise or a unique training population characteristic and conveys the generalizability of the findings [19].

In summary, studies must include thorough validation efforts to maximize the likelihood that classical statistical or supervised machine learning approaches provide an adequate description of the data and that the results are reproducible and generalizable.

Causal Inference

Unlike predictive approaches that focus on associations, causal inference addresses counterfactual questions: what would have happened if an individual had followed a different dietary intervention? This counterfactual perspective is central to nutrition research, where randomized trials are often difficult to conduct, and much evidence comes from observational data. The key metric of interest is the average treatment effect or conditional average treatment effect, which represents the expected difference in outcomes if all participants (in a population or a similar subgroup) had followed one diet compared with another. Many approaches can be used to estimate the average treatment effect. One common method is inverse probability weighting, which reweights the contribution of individuals to the outputs by the probability of receiving the target treatment. The goal of this technique is to mimic the statistical behavior of a randomized trial [77,78]. Causal inference methods are well established within statistics, econometrics, epidemiology, biomedical research, and health services research, and their relevance to nutrition science is increasing as large observational cohorts and complex intervention studies become more common. Although randomized controlled trials remain the reference standard approach, practical, ethical, and logistical constraints can limit their feasibility, thereby motivating the use of principled causal inference approaches to approximate intervention effects from observational data.

An example of machine learning–augmented causal inference is a nationwide study evaluating a cooking and nutrition education intervention among 3248 medical trainees across 20 medical schools [79]. Because the intervention was implemented as a multisite prospective cohort rather than a randomized trial, baseline differences across participants and institutions required adjustment. Machine learning was used to estimate propensity scores that flexibly accounted for participant characteristics and baseline behaviors, which were then incorporated into a multilevel causal modeling framework to estimate intervention effects on nutrition knowledge, counseling efficacy, and dietary behaviors. Using this approach, improvements in nutrition knowledge, counseling confidence, and diet quality were interpreted as causal effects rather than as simple pre–post observational associations. Similarly, causal survival forests extend tree-based methods to time-to-event outcomes, allowing investigation of heterogeneity in health risk trajectories associated with specific dietary exposures. In a longitudinal cohort study examining diet and disease incidence, this approach was used to model time-to-event outcomes while estimating individualized exposure effects. Rather than reporting a single mean hazard ratio, the method identified subgroups in whom the dietary exposure was associated with stronger or weaker risk trajectories, demonstrating how tree-based causal methods can reveal heterogeneity that may be obscured in traditional Cox proportional hazards models [80]. Together, these approaches illustrate how machine learning–augmented causal inference can extend nutrition research beyond associative analyses toward more interpretable estimates of intervention effects, particularly as data availability and methodological adoption continue to expand.

Advanced Machine Learning Approaches for Complex Multimodal Research

Data integration enables researchers to gain additional insight into underlying biological patterns by combining datasets from different platforms or cohorts into a unified analytical framework. This strategy enables meaningful connections across independent studies and facilitates the discovery of complex biomarkers that model the interaction between diet and host physiology. Data integration, such as multiomics studies, can offer insights in scenarios when no single data type (e.g., genetic or microbiome data) can offer adequate explanations on its own [81]. For example, Sarker et al. [70] augmented metabolomic features with reaction- and subsystem-level features derived from a genome-scale metabolic model and then applied random forest classifiers (1000 trees, leave-one-out cross-validation) to predict dietary intake across 6 feeding trials. After preprocessing, 109 metabolites with <20% missingness were retained, of which 75 overlapped with the Human Genome-Scale Metabolic model and were used to construct reaction-level features reflecting estimated reaction activity. Random forest classifiers (1000 trees, leave-one-out cross-validation with grid search hyperparameter tuning) were then trained to classify treatment compared with control groups within each feeding study. Compared with metabolite-only models, reaction-level features improved classification accuracy substantially for barley (0.82 compared with 0.54), broccoli (0.80 compared with 0.43), and oats (0.79 compared with 0.45), with more modest gains for avocado (0.70 compared with 0.61) and similar performance for almond and walnut. These findings illustrate how random forest models can flexibly integrate structured biochemical knowledge to improve predictive performance in small nutrition cohorts [70]. Similarly, integration of microbiome composition data with metabolomic profiles has been used to enable personalized prediction of metabolite responses to dietary interventions. By jointly modeling microbial and metabolic signals, these approaches enhance the ability to characterize diet–microbiome interactions and improve predictive accuracy in complex nutrition studies [26].

Advanced Machine Learning Methods and AI Tools

The previous approaches rely on engineering features, whereas deep learning can reduce the need for manual feature engineering. Deep learning methods and language-based models are increasingly used in nutrition research to analyze unstructured, longitudinal, and text-based data [2]. These techniques can improve estimation and prediction from traditional machine learning approaches by training on large datasets that include high-order interactions [38]. These advancements enable analysis of heterogeneous data types that were previously difficult to handle, such as images, dietary records, and time-series health measurements [82]. Moreover, these approaches perform well on prediction tasks that use spatial information data.

Deep learning techniques learn hierarchical feature representations from multimodal and complex data through multiple computational layers. In these architectures, representations learned in earlier layers are progressively transformed and refined in subsequent layers, enabling the abstraction of increasingly complex patterns [38]. For example, a deep learning framework Metabolite response predictor using coupled multilayer perceptrons was used to predict metabolite responses to dietary interventions using baseline microbiome and metabolomic data. Across 6 human dietary intervention studies (n = 18 to 132 participants per study), McMLP consistently outperformed traditional machine learning approaches such as random forest and gradient boosting in predicting postintervention metabolite concentrations. In synthetic benchmarking using data generated from a microbial consumer-resource model, with 50 training samples, McMLP achieved a mean Spearman correlation (ρ) of 0.595 when baseline metabolomic profiles were included as inputs, compared with substantially lower values for random forest and gradient boosting, and increased the fraction of well-predicted metabolites (ρ > 0.5) to 0.815. Notably, the 2-step architecture demonstrated strong performance even in studies with modest sample sizes (n = 20–50), suggesting that the architectural design and incorporation of biological structure may help to mitigate overfitting in certain settings. In addition to predictive accuracy, the model enabled biological interpretation through sensitivity analyses that identified plausible food–microbe–metabolite relationships [15]. This work illustrates how deep learning approaches can extend beyond classification tasks toward mechanistically informed regression models in precision nutrition. Although deep learning models are often thought to require very large datasets, performance in smaller cohorts may be achievable when model architectures are carefully constrained, signal-to-noise ratios are strong, or prior biological structure is incorporated into the modeling framework. However, independent replication in larger and more heterogeneous populations remains necessary to confirm generalizability.

Below, we provide an overview of frequently used deep learning approaches, including CNNs, RNNs, LSTM networks, and NLP.

Convolutional neural networks

CNNs can be intuitively understood as models that learn to detect spatial or structural patterns in complex data, such as images or ordered biological features, by scanning local regions and combining them into higher-level representations. In the context of nutrition research, CNNs are most commonly applied to food images, where the model learns to recognize visual features associated with specific foods or portion sizes. Early layers extract simple features (e.g., edges, textures, or basic shapes), whereas deeper layers integrate these signals into more complex representations linked to dietary intake or metabolic outcomes. Conceptually, CNNs operate hierarchically: first, they detect basic components and then combine them into higher-level patterns that distinguish nutritional phenotypes.

Artificial neural networks are a type of machine learning algorithm represented by interconnected nodes organized into layers. The first layer corresponds to the original feature or input data, and the nodes transform the data that serve as input to the next layer of nodes, finally arriving at the nodes corresponding to the class or group outcomes. Conceptually, artificial neural networks resemble biological neural systems, where signals pass through interconnected units that adapt their connection strengths based on experience. During the training phase, the nodes pass the processed information forward and backward, compute the errors, and adjust the processing weights as the network recognizes patterns and improves the classification of the samples into the observed groups [24]. CNNs are better suited to analyzing multidimensional, complex nutritional datasets because they use convolutional layers with local connections, whereas artificial neural networks connect to all neurons in 1 layer to all neurons in the next. Localized parameter sharing and connections improve the capability of CNNs to detect group-specific patterns, such as rare microbiome or metabolomic profiles specific to a few samples [4]. CNNs can be used in nutritional research to identify relationships between microbial taxa that can serve as biomarkers of diseases or impute dietary intake [23].

A review of CNNs applied to food quality and safety demonstrates how these networks have been used for food quality inspection, safety (e.g., assessment of chemical or microbial risks), traceability, grading, and classification [83]. The authors summarize multiple applications in which CNN-based feature extraction was integrated with spectroscopy sensors and imaging systems to improve food quality assessment. When time is of the essence, such as in preventing or minimizing health hazards, CNN approaches support real-time monitoring of food quality and the supply chain through the integration of smart sensors and food industry system classification [83].

CNNs are also well-suited to automatically identify foods and infer portions from video or still images within the context of AI-assisted dietary assessment tools [25]. Data collected from smartphone-based wearable camera dietary assessment apps can record food intake events and apply image analysis to identify consumed items. These machine learning approaches reduce the time required for research staff to manually code dietary data, thereby improving scalability and enabling more frequent, real-time dietary monitoring in large populations. In microbiome research, hierarchical clustering has been used to organize microbial taxa based on phylogenetic relationships, yielding spatial maps that serve as input images for CNN training [4]. When subsequent convolution operations were applied to the taxonomic maps, the approach enabled the detection of microbial disease. The framework was extended using a graph convolutional network, resulting in the gMic software that incorporated phylogenetic structures into a graphical representation. Across multiple benchmark datasets, iMic achieved an mean AUC improvement of 0.122 compared with baseline models and outperformed prior CNN-based approaches such as PopPhy by an average of 0.134 AUC units (P < 0.001). For example, in the cirrhosis dataset, iMic achieved an AUC of 0.924 compared with 0.536 for PopPhy-CNN2. [23]. This application demonstrates CNNs’ strong potential in microbiome-driven nutrition research.

Long short-term memory models

RNNs are a class of artificial neural networks that retain information from previous inputs through recurrent connections. This feature makes RNNs well-suited for analyzing sequential data, as the retained information enables the model to capture strong temporal dependencies. LSTMs are a subtype of RNN specifically designed to capture long-term dependencies in sequential data, such as metabolomics or microbiome associations and cause-and-effect relationships. The LSTM has 3 information gates: input, forget, and output, which regulate how information is stored, updated, and transmitted across time steps [38]. In longitudinal nutrition studies, these models can learn how earlier exposures or biomarker levels influence subsequent responses (e.g., how baseline inflammatory markers modify postprandial glucose and insulin trajectories). By maintaining a memory of prior time points, LSTMs can model delayed or cumulative effects that may not be detectable using static or cross-sectional approaches.

The LSTM approach has been applied for multiclass obesity prediction using a Saudi-specific dataset of 2975 individual records and 20 demographic, behavioral, and lifestyle features [28]. In this study, participants were classified into 6 BMI-defined categories (underweight, normal weight, overweight, and obesity classes I, II, and III). The bidirectional LSTM, which captures temporal dependencies, achieved 96% classification accuracy, with 96% macrorecall and 95% macro F1-score, outperforming standard RNN and traditional deep neural network architectures. Another example of LSTM is its application to keyword trend prediction in obesity research [29]. In this study, 8830 news articles from 52 South Korean media outlets were analyzed to determine whether the articles were published before or after the COVID-19 pandemic using obesity-related keywords. A binary classification framework was implemented (0 = prepandemic, 1 = postpandemic), and both RNN and LSTM models achieved high predictive performance. The RNN achieved 98% accuracy, whereas the LSTM achieved 97% accuracy, indicating that temporal keyword patterns were highly discriminative of pandemic-era shifts in obesity discourse.

Natural language processing

NLP methods transform unstructured text, such as food diaries, product labels, or clinical notes, into structured representations that can be analyzed quantitatively, which enables large-scale comparison, classification, and discovery from text-based data. Modern NLP techniques convert text into numerical embeddings that capture semantic meaning, allowing machine learning models to analyze relationships between words, nutrients, and health outcomes [33].

Pretrained models such as Bidirectional Encoder Representation from Transformers (BERT) generate contextual embeddings that preserve the meaning of text based on surrounding words [32]. These embeddings can then be used as inputs for downstream machine learning tasks such as classification, scoring, or prediction [84]. NLP is emerging as a powerful tool in large-scale food categorization, nutrient profiling, and literature mining.

In a demonstration of the potential of NLP in nutrition studies, a pretrained BERT model was integrated into a traditional machine learning pipeline to automate food category classification and nutrition quality scores using food label databases (n = 17,448) [12]. Text from product labels was transformed into semantic embeddings, which were then used as inputs to supervised machine learning models. Specifically, the eXtreme Gradient Boost algorithm, an ensemble method based on gradient-boosted decision trees, was trained on BERT-derived embeddings to perform classification and score prediction. This approach achieved 98% classification accuracy and an R2 of 0.87 for predicting nutrition quality scores, outperforming EN (89% accuracy) and k-NN (91% accuracy), with other comparator models ranging from 85% to 92% accuracy. The study implemented external validation to confirm the generalizability of the machine learning framework, and t-SNE was used to visualize clusters of food items based on label information [12]. This study illustrates the use of NLP to enable large-scale analysis of diet information from unstructured data.

NLP has also been applied to extract connections between diet, microbiome, and health from the scientific literature [31]. NLP models such as BioMedBERT and PL-Marker were trained on a dataset including 165 peer-reviewed publications about dietary exposure, microbial responses, and health outcomes. The NLP algorithm identified 4200 structured relationships between nutrients, food, microbial taxa, and physiological measures of health. The NLP application achieved F1 scores of 0.80 for entity recognition and 0.45 for end-to-end relation extraction across the variables analyzed [31]. The study demonstrates that literature-driven NLP tools can identify structured relationships between dietary exposures and microbial responses but also notes that the complexity of biomedical text reduces performance in end-to-end relation extraction, underscoring ongoing methodological challenges. Altogether, NLP applications to nutrition research enable integration of dietary data and scientific text, automating the discovery of relationships between food and health [33].

Large language models

LLMs extend traditional NLP techniques by enabling advanced interpretation and generation of natural language [33]. These models have the potential to support nutrition research tasks such as automated dietary assessment and personalized nutrition guidance. Models such as Generative Pre-trained Transformer 4 (GPT-4) are trained on large text corpora and can respond to nutrition or clinical queries, summarize research findings, and assist with information synthesis [36]. Notably, these models can perform new tasks with little or no additional task-specific training, often referred to as “few-shot” or “zero-shot” learning, meaning that they can generalize to novel prompts without being retrained for each specific application. Like other transformer-based architectures, GPT models analyze text by modeling relationships between words and contextual patterns. However, large commercial LLMs rely on extensive training datasets and computational infrastructure to generate predictions or recommendations derived from broad sources of knowledge [85]. As a result, these systems represent a powerful but resource-intensive extension of earlier NLP approaches.

Demonstrating the potential of GPT technology, a modified deep generative model was developed by integrating ChatGPT-like language capabilities with a variational autoencoder that generated personalized weekly meal plans [85]. The application generated nutrition recommendations aligned with guidelines from the European Food Safety Authority, and performance was evaluated based on adherence to macronutrient and micronutrient targets rather than clinical outcomes. Another study demonstrated that GPT-4’s capability to identify food items and estimate portion size from wearable camera images was similar to that of dietitians [34]. In addition, the application was able to adapt and recognize culturally specific food when guided by language. A different study evaluating responses to frequently asked nutrition questions reported that ChatGPT-generated answers performed comparably to, and in some cases received higher scores than, dietitian responses for scientific correctness and actionability under blinded expert evaluation criteria [35]. However, it is important to note that this comparison was limited to short, standardized written responses (100–300 words) addressing general nutrition topics and did not involve personalized counseling, clinical complexity, follow-up dialog, or therapeutic interaction. The previous reports indicate that although LLMs are not a substitute for the care offered by dietitians, these applications can have a role as an accessible tool to support public health and may be useful tools that can be used under the supervision of a subject matter expert.

Summary

The present review summarizes several machine learning approaches and their applications in nutrition research. Beyond defining these methods, we highlight case studies that demonstrate their use in personalized nutrition, dietary assessment, microbiome and metabolomic analysis, outcome prediction and classification, and biomarker discovery. The analytical workflow outlined in this review spans data preprocessing, feature prescreening, feature engineering, dimensionality reduction, visualization, unsupervised and supervised machine learning, and emerging deep learning approaches. Together, these methods illustrate how machine learning approaches can integrate diverse data types, uncover complex patterns across study designs, and support the development of population and personalized-level nutrition and health recommendations [2].

Future directions of machine learning applications to nutrition studies

Building on the methods and applications reviewed above, several areas remain critical for advancing the responsible and effective use of machine learning in nutrition research. Traditional machine learning approaches are now established components of the nutrition research toolkit and have generated valuable population-level insights into nutrient patterns, microbiome composition, and metabolomic variation. However, further progress is needed to translate these advances into reliable, individualized nutrition recommendations.

Advancement of personalized nutrition depends on 2 complementary developments: refinement of machine learning models capable of capturing interindividual variability, and expansion of comprehensive, high-quality datasets to support model training and validation. Emerging deep learning approaches aim to improve characterization of variation in microbiome composition, metabolic markers, and behavioral factors to predict personalized food responses. Although studies increasingly demonstrate accurate prediction of outcomes, such as glycemic responses, high predictive accuracy does not inherently establish causal validity or clinical effectiveness. Models derived from observational or retrospective datasets require independent prospective validation, and careful distinction between predictive modeling and causal inference remains essential for responsible translation into practice.

Parallel to methodological development, large-scale data infrastructure initiatives are expanding the foundation for precision nutrition research. The NIH All of Us Research program has enrolled >800,000 participants and continues to collect genomic, clinical, survey, and wearable device data across a diverse U.S. population [86]. However, it is important to note that not all data types are available for the full cohort. For example, detailed dietary assessment data are currently available for a smaller subset of participants (∼10,000 in initial survey modules). As the All of Us Research Program is integrated with the Nutrition for Precision Health initiative, it aims to leverage these data to develop and validate AI algorithms for predicting individualized dietary responses. The breadth and depth of such a dataset will be critical for improving model robustness and generalizability.

At the translational level, digital twin frameworks have emerged as a strategy to integrate longitudinal nutrition, behavioral, and physiological data into dynamic, individualized models. Specifically, digital twins represent virtual counterparts of individuals that simulate responses to dietary and lifestyle interventions. In a randomized clinical trial conducted at the Cleveland Clinic (n = 150), an AI-enabled digital twin-based intervention in adults with type 2 diabetes was associated with greater reductions in hemoglobin A1c and body weight than usual care at 12 mo [87]. Although further independent validation is needed, this example illustrates how integrative machine learning systems may extend predictive modeling toward actionable, personalized intervention strategies.

Ethical and responsible development of machine learning tools for personalized nutrition

As machine learning tools become increasingly embedded in nutrition research and personalized nutrition products, ethical, regulatory, and equity-focused considerations must evolve in parallel. Ethical and regulatory oversight should remain central to the application of machine learning approaches to personalized nutrition research. The collection, management, and analysis of personal data, such as genetic or lifestyle information, should adhere to the strictest standards developed by federal agencies and disciplinary associations. In addition, the data and analytical approaches used in nutrition studies should follow the Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable principles to enhance data transparency and reproducibility. Equally important is the evaluation of regulatory compliance, data security, and privacy protections at each step of data collection, analysis, and implementation within the personalized nutrition system [88].

Applicable to all analyses, and therefore relevant to nutrition studies that use machine learning approaches, is the possibility of recording a biased response. Biased results can be expected from machine learning approaches trained on data with nonrandom missing observations or samples that are not representative of a diverse population. Recommendations based on biased output may further disadvantage underrepresented groups. The analysis of diverse or representative datasets, the use of data sampling methods such as upsampling or downsampling, and transparent and complete reporting of dataset limitations assist in bringing awareness to potential biases [89]. Also, regulatory frameworks should evolve alongside technological advances to ensure accurate, equitable, and representative health recommendations [88].

Adherence to established guidelines and, when available, best practices in nutrition study design, data preprocessing, machine learning model development, evaluation, and validation strengthens the rigor and reproducibility of findings [2,[90], [91], [92], [93]].

Together, continued development of machine learning methods, the expansion of high-quality nutrition datasets, and careful attention to ethical and equity considerations will help to shape the next generation of nutrition science. Machine learning approaches offer powerful tools to integrate diverse biological, behavioral, and environmental data, uncover novel diet-health relationships, and support more precise and actionable nutrition recommendations. However, their impact will depend on rigorous study design, transparent reporting, external validation, and responsible implementation. With coordinated advances across methodology, data infrastructure, and regulatory guidance, machine learning has the potential to meaningfully accelerate discovery, strengthen causal inference, and improve the translation of nutrition research into practice.

Practical considerations for study design, model selection, and data governance

Many nutrition studies are characterized by modest sample sizes relative to feature dimensionality. These scenarios increase the risk of model overfitting to the training cohort and limit the generalizability of the predictive models. Reliable deep learning model performance typically requires large, heterogeneous datasets encompassing diverse participant groups. In contrast, classical machine learning approaches may be more appropriate for smaller studies.

Strategies such as meta-analysis, decentralized machine learning (federated learning), or pooling data across related studies can increase the effective sample size. However, these approaches require careful normalization, harmonization, and correction for batch effects when integrating omics or imaging data generated using distinct protocols [94]. They also necessitate rigorous study designs that prespecify model development and validation procedures aligned with the intended prediction task and data structure, thereby minimizing bias, optimizing data utility, and improving reproducibility [95].

Data safety is also a priority in research. For example, clinical nutrition research may involve sensitive dietary, clinical, and genomic data collected from different centers [86]. Approaches such as federated learning and decentralized data storage enable collaborative modeling without sharing raw individual-level data, whereas differential privacy provides formal protections against reidentification by introducing controlled noise into analyses [96,97]. Together, these strategies support privacy protection, regulatory compliance, and secure multisite research.

Data modality strongly influences method selection. Omics, anthropometric, and behavioral variables are commonly analyzed using supervised or unsupervised machine learning, whereas CNNs are primarily applied to food images and videos, and recurrent or temporal models to longitudinal intake records, wearable-derived measurements, or time-dependent metabolic profiles. Prediction-focused models often prioritize accuracy for outcomes such as obesity risk or dietary response, whereas explainability-focused approaches emphasize interpretable biomarkers or drivers of nutritional phenotypes. Across all approaches, careful attention to measurement error [98], population heterogeneity [99], and potential distribution shifts [100] between training and deployment cohorts is essential. Ultimately, rigorous model training, calibration, and independent validation aligned with study size, design, and data structure are critical to minimize bias and ensure generalizability in prospective nutrition datasets.

As nutrition research continues to generate increasingly complex and multidimensional data, thoughtful integration of machine learning approaches grounded in methodological rigor, ethical responsibility, and domain expertise will be essential to ensure that analytical advances translate into meaningful improvements in population and personalized nutrition.
