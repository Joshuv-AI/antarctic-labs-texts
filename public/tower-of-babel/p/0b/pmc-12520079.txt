Interaction with amoeba drives virulence-associated phenotypes in the Candida haemulonii complex
Authors: Artid Amsri, Kritsada Pruksaphon, Patcharin Thammasit, Wasinee Poonsawat, Joshua D Nosanchuk, Sirida Youngchim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPredation by amoebae can induce adaptive traits in microorganisms, enhancing their resilience and pathogenic potential. This study investigated whether interaction with Acanthamoeba castellanii modulates the virulence of environmental fungi Candida haemulonii complex species. Our findings reveal that adaptation to an amoebic host induces significant phenotypic changes in C. duobushaemulonii isolated from a beetle’s gut, leading to four distinct phenotypes: smooth-pink, rough-pink, intense-pink, and hyphae-white. These variants exhibited increased aggregation and filamentation. Amoeba-adapted isolates produced over 15-fold more biofilm biomass and more than 2.2-fold greater extracellular matrix than non-adapted isolates. Transcriptional analysis revealed upregulation of virulence-associated genes, including dimorphism regulators WOR1 ( > 2.4-fold), CPH1 (4.8-fold), and HGC1 ( > 1.9-fold) as well as biofilm-related genes ALS1 ( > 3.6-fold), BCR1 ( > 14.5-fold), and EGF1 ( > 1.6-fold), demonstrating amoeba-induced enhancement of pathogenic traits. Furthermore, adaptation enhanced fungal tolerance to environmental stressors and resulted in more than a 1.3-fold increase in the secretion of hydrolytic enzymes, including esterase, phospholipase, and secreted aspartyl proteinase. Pathogenicity assays using Galleria mellonella revealed significantly increased virulence of amoeba-adapted isolates, with a reduction in the lethal dose (LD50) by 71.5–97.1% compared to non-adapted controls (NA: 2.52 ± 0.22 × 106 CFU/mL; P10:HW: 7.17 ± 2.14 × 104 CFU/mL; P10:RP: 1.25 ± 0.15 × 105 CFU/mL). These findings suggest that environmental interaction with amoebae can promote the emergence of virulence-associated phenotypes in C. duobushaemulonii, providing a model for understanding how environmental pressures may facilitate fungal pathogenesis.KEYWORDS: Candida haemulonii, soil amoeba, virulence factor, antifungal susceptibility, Galleria mellonella

Introduction

Microevolution of fungal virulence is a key research area, offering critical insights into the mechanisms driving pathogenicity and their potential subsequent impact on human health. Various virulence traits in fungi and yeast are regulated in response to diverse environmental pressures. These adaptations allow organisms to optimize nutrient acquisition, evade predation, establish replication niches in diverse ecological settings, and ultimately facilitate the invasion of host organisms [1]. Notably, most opportunistic fungal pathogens are not transmitted directly between humans but persist in environmental reservoirs between host infections. Thus, it is unlikely that their virulence traits evolved solely within human hosts. Instead, these traits likely emerged in response to environmental challenges, such as interactions with microbial predators, which influenced their ability to survive, adapt, and eventually infect mammalian hosts [2].

Candida species are the most frequent fungal pathogens associated with invasive infections in immunocompromised individuals, leading to significant morbidity and mortality. Although C. albicans is the most common species, non-albicans Candida (NAC) species, including C. tropicalis, C. krusei, C. parapsilosis, C. glabrata, C. auris, C. lusitaniae, and the C. haemulonii complex (CHC), are increasingly prevalent [3]. In the past few decades, the rates of clinical isolation of CHC species have risen in several countries, including Malaysia, China, Brazil, and the United States, with concerning outbreaks reported in China [4]. Furthermore, CHC species easily adapt to environmental changes and frequently demonstrate in vitro resistance to several antifungal agents, such as amphotericin B, azoles, and echinocandins [5]. The emergence of CHC species as causative agents of candidiasis has drawn significant attention, prompting investigations into their pathogenesis and interactions with the host.

The virulence factors associated with CHC species include the production of hydrolytic enzymes, surface adherence, and the ability to persist in hospital environments, highlighting their potential for horizontal transmission. Outbreaks involving these species have raised concerns about their ecological and physiological similarities to other notable pathogens like C. auris [6,7]. The ecological adaptability of CHC species, including their interactions with environmental organisms, may contribute to their virulence and capacity to cause infection [8].

Recent investigations into fungal ecology and evolution have highlighted the importance of environmental interactions in influencing microbial virulence. Of particular interest is the relationship between fungi and protozoa, such as amoebae, which are ubiquitous in diverse ecological niches. Amoebae, known for their predatory behaviour toward microorganisms, have been shown to exert selective pressure on various bacterial and fungal species, potentially driving the evolution of virulence traits. The predation of fungi by amoebae is considered a significant selective pressure that may serve as a “training ground” for environmental fungi [9–11]. It has been hypothesized that many virulence factors found in pathogenic fungi, including those implicated in human diseases, initially evolved as adaptive responses to predation by amoebae. Studies have demonstrated that interaction between Cryptococcus neoformans and Acanthamoeba castellanii induces significant alterations in virulence-related traits of C. neoformans. These changes, including an increased capsule size, enhanced melanin production, and the release of enzymes such as urease and phospholipase, play a crucial role in the yeast’s resistance to amoebal predation [12]. Notably, Dictyostelium discoideum was unable to kill C. neoformans upon phagocytosis, highlighting the development of effective evasion mechanisms by Cryptococcus [13].

Similarly, the interaction of Cryptococcus gattii with amoebae leads to phenotypic changes in cryptococcal cells, including increased polysaccharide secretion, heightened tolerance to oxidative stress, and elevated mortality rates in infected mice [14]. Other fungal species, such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae, C. parapsilosis, and C. albicans, also exhibited enhanced virulence traits following interactions with amoebae [15–17]. These traits include improved thermotolerance, filamentation, phenotypic switching, and resistance to oxidative stress. Of particular concern is the ability of amoebae to internalize and promote the proliferation of yeast cells, especially multidrug-resistant species like C. auris, which further aggravates their potential for causing severe infections [18].

This research investigates whether interactions between saprotrophic CHC species and free-living amoebae could potentially increase fungal virulence. We hypothesize that Candida isolates exposed to amoebae in the environment may develop adaptations that enhance their resistance to amoebic engulfment and improve their survival strategies. These modifications could potentially help the yeast evade human immune defenses, possibly resulting in increased pathogenicity and serious infections. To prove that we also used Galleria mellonella larvae as an infection model. G. mellonella is well-suited for studying fungal pathogenesis due to its innate immune system, which shares similarities with mammalian innate immunity [19]. Investigating how environmental predators like amoebae influence the virulence of CHC species may offer valuable insights into the ecological drivers of fungal pathogenicity and support future strategies for infection prevention and control.

Materials and methods

Organisms and culture conditions

A. castellanii strain 30234 was maintained under previously established conditions in PYG broth at 28°C in the dark. Routine culture and experimental procedures followed standard protocols, with cells grown as adherent cultures and harvested accordingly [17,20–22].

Environmental isolates of the C. haemulonii complex were cultured following protocols provided by the Thailand Bioresource Research Center (TBRC), BIOTECH Culture Collection (BCC), located in Pathum Thani, Thailand. These isolates, sourced from various natural habitats across Thailand, are detailed in Table 1. The culture conditions of C. haemulonii complex were followed by the protocol described by [17]. Strains were initially grown on Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) at 28°C for 48 hours, then transferred to Yeast Extract Peptone Dextrose (YPD) agar under the same conditions. Before experiments, yeast cells were harvested, washed with 1×PBS, and counted using a hemocytometer.

G. mellonella larvae were sourced from a breeding farm in Sakaew, Thailand. They were maintained on an artificial diet in an incubator at 30°C [23]. For each experiment, ten larvae, weighing approximately 300–350 mg with clear, uniform color, without dark spots or grayish marks, were selected, randomly allocated to control and treatment groups. The study was carried out in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Experimental co-adaptation of C. haemulonii species complex with A. castellanii

The experimental co-adaptation between two organisms was performed according to [24] with minor modifications. Three biological replicates of five isolates were grown on YPD agar for 2 days and washed with sterile phosphate buffer saline (1×PBS, pH 7.2), adjusted to 1 × 108 cells/mL and 100 μL of diluted yeast cells (1 × 106 cells/mL) were used to inoculate three independent cultures of A. castellanii (1 × 105 cells/mL) in 1 mL of PBS in 24-well plate and incubated at 28°C, for 30 days. Every three days, the amoebae in three replicate wells were lysed with 0.5% Triton X-100 and pulling the suspension using a 27-gauge needle at least five times. The released yeast cells were transferred to a new plate of amoebae. As controls, three biological replicates of yeast were maintained in PBS without amoebae, and these were also subjected to 0.5% Triton X-100 treatment every three days. After that, samples were collected after 3 days (1st passage), 15 days (5th passage), and 30 days (10th passage) of interaction.

Ten-fold diluted samples were plated on fresh YPD medium supplemented with 5 μg/mL phloxine B (Tokyo Chemical Industry, Tokyo, Japan) and incubated at 28°C [25]. Colony color and morphological characteristics were observed over a period of 7 days, after which different colonies were selected for further analysis. Yeast cells grown in the presence of A. castellanii were classified as the exposed population, whereas those grown in the absence of A. castellanii were defined as the non-exposed population. The individual colonies isolated from these populations were defined as adapted and non-adapted isolates, respectively.

Quantification of C. haemulonii species complex viability

To assess the survival of C. haemulonii (both non-adapted and adapted isolates) following phagocytosis by A. castellanii, amoebae were suspended in PBS at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/mL, and 1 mL of this suspension was added to each well of a 12-well plate. C. haemulonii was introduced into the A. castellanii cultures at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10 (yeast:amoeba = 10:1) and incubated at 28°C for 24 hours. Following incubation, non-phagocytosed yeast cells were removed by washing with PBS. The washed solution was collected for indirect quantification of yeast cells. To detach the adherent amoebae, the plates were placed on ice for 10 minutes. Amoeba cells were subsequently lysed by pulling the suspension using a 27-gauge needle. After that, yeast suspension was performed through serial 10-fold dilutions. Yeast cell viability was determined by plating the dilutions onto SDA and incubating at 28°C for 24 hours. Viability was quantified by counting colony-forming units (CFU)/mL, providing a direct measure of yeast survival.

Morphological switching assays

To investigate morphological switching, isolated colonies from each passage (amoebae-exposed population) and the control group were cultured in YPD broth, shaking at 150 rpm at either 28 or 37°C for 2 days. After incubation, yeast cells were harvested by centrifugation at 5,000 rpm for 15 minutes and subsequently washed three times with PBS. The cell concentration was then adjusted to 1 × 103 cells/mL. A 100 μL of this diluted yeast suspension, containing approximately 1 × 102 cells, was spread onto YPD agar plates supplemented with 5 μg/mL phloxine B (obtained from Tokyo Chemical Industry, Tokyo, Japan). These plates were then incubated at either 28 or 37°C for 7 days [25,26]. Afterward, colony and cellular morphologies were recorded. The frequency of morphological switching was quantified by determining the proportion of colonies displaying an alternative phenotype relative to the total number of colonies. The data were expressed as a percentage of switching frequency, along with the corresponding standard deviation.

Aggregation kinetic assay

To evaluate cell aggregation in yeast exposed to amoebae versus unexposed yeast. Isolated yeast cells from each passage and controls were cultured in YPD broth. The cultures were incubated at 28°C for 48 hours with continuous agitation at 150 rpm. The yeast cells were then collected via centrifugation at 5,000 rpm for 15 minutes and washed with PBS. The aggregation assay was performed following the method described in [27], with minor modifications. Yeast suspensions, containing 106 cells/mL in 1× PBS, were prepared and vortexed for 30 seconds. Then, a 100 μL aliquot of yeast suspension (1 × 105 cells) was transferred by pipetting into each well of a 96-well plate. Biofilm formation assay

Biomass quantification

To evaluate biofilm formation in amoeba-adapted isolates compared to a non-adapted isolate, we employed a biofilm formation assay following the protocol outlined by [17,29]. C. haemulonii isolates were cultured in YPD for 48 hours, resuspended in RPMI 1640 (5 × 104 cells/mL), and incubated in a 96-well plate at 37°C for 24 hours. After washing with 1×PBS, biofilms were fixed with 99% (v/v) methanol, stained with 0.05% crystal violet for 30 minutes, and washed again. Biofilm biomass was quantified by solubilizing the stain with 33% (v/v) acetic acid, followed by absorbance measurement at 595 nm using a microplate reader.

Metabolic activity measurement

To assess the metabolic activity of biofilms, a colorimetric assay was performed to detect the reduction of 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT; Life Technologies™, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Oregon, USA) into an insoluble purple formazan product. Briefly, the attached biofilm was washed and subsequently added 50 μL of MTT solution (1 mg/mL). The plate was then incubated at 37°C for 4 hours in the dark. After incubation, the MTT solution was carefully removed. A 100 μL of 99.5% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to each well to dissolve the formazan product. The plate was further incubated at 37°C for 30 minutes in the dark. Optical density was then measured at 570 nm.

Extracellular matrix detection

The biofilm extracellular matrix (ECM) was quantified using methods previously described by [30,31]. In brief, biofilm-ECM formation was generated and non-fixed biofilm was further stained with 200 μL of 0.1% Safranin solution at room temperature for 5 minutes. The wells were then washed once with 1×PBS, and the bound dye was dissolved using 200 μL of 30% acetic acid. A 100 μL sample from each well was transferred to a new 96-well plate, and absorbance was measured at OD 530 nm using a microplate reader.

Tolerance to stressors to environmental factors

Yeast cell suspensions were subjected to serial dilutions, and 3 μL aliquots from each dilution (105 −102 cells/mL) were inoculated onto YPD agar containing varying concentrations of environmental stressors. To assess the halotolerance of adapted C. haemulonii isolates, cultures were grown on YPD agar supplemented with 0.5 M and 1 M NaCl. Similarly, to evaluate tolerance to elevated iron levels, isolates were cultivated on YPD agar containing 100 μM and 1,000 μM ferric ammonium citrate. In addition, yeast cell suspensions were plated on YPD agar adjusted to acidic (pH 3.0) and alkaline (pH 10.0) conditions. The stressor-containing plates were incubated at 28°C for 2 days, and isolates that exhibited growth post-incubation were categorized as halotolerant, iron-tolerant, acid-tolerant, or alkaline-tolerant, respectively. For the assessment of heat stress, additional plates were incubated at 37°C and 42°C for 2 days. All plates were subsequently photographed for documentation [32,33].

Assessment of virulence factors

Hemolytic activity

Isolates were tested for hemolysin production. An inoculum containing approximately 1 × 108 cells/mL was prepared from the adapted and non-adapted isolates. A 10 μL of each yeast suspension (1 × 106 cells) was inoculated onto fresh sheep blood agar plates. The plates were then incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2 for 2 days. Hemolytic activity was then determined by observing the plates under transmitted light, where positive activity was indicated by the formation of a clear halo surrounding the colony. The hemolytic activity (Hz) was quantified by calculating the ratio of the colony diameter to the diameter of the hemolysis zone. Based on the Hz score, the hemolytic activity was classified into four categories, following the method previously described [34].

Secreted aspartyl protease activity

Secreted aspartyl proteases (SAPs) activity was evaluated by the Yeast carbon base-Bovine serum albumin (YCB-BSA) method [35]. Both adapted and non-adapted isolates were cultured overnight in YPD broth at 28°C. Yeast cells were then harvested and washed three times with PBS. Afterward, the cell density was adjusted to 1 × 108 cells/mL. A 10 μL of yeast suspension, containing 1 × 106 cells, was spotted onto YCB-BSA agar, containing yeast carbon base (23.4 g/L) and bovine serum albumin (20 g/L), incubated at 28 °C for 5 days. The plates were subsequently stained with amido black staining solution to visualize the precipitation zones (Pz), which were measured to determine SAPs activity.

Phospholipase activity

Phospholipase activity was investigated following the method described by [32]. Briefly, 10 μL of 1 × 106 yeast cell suspension was inoculated onto SDA supplemented with 58.4 g/L NaCl, 5.5 g/L CaCl₂, and 10% egg yolk emulsion. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 5 days. After incubation, the colony diameter and the diameter of the colony-plus-precipitation zone were measured. Phospholipase activity (Pz) was determined by calculating the ratio of the colony diameter to the total precipitation zone.

Esterase activity

Esterase activity was performed by the method described by [36,37]. Briefly, 10 μL of yeast suspension from both adapted and non-adapted isolates, containing 1 × 106 cells, was dropped onto the medium containing 10 g/L peptone, 5 g/L NaCl, 0.1 g/L CaCl2, 5 mL (v/v) Tween 80, 15 g/L agar, with pH adjusted to 6.5. The plates were incubated at 37°C for 5 days. Esterase activity was determined by measuring the precipitation zone surrounding the colonies.

Quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR)

To investigate gene expression related to virulence between adapted and non-adapted isolates, each isolate was cultured on YPD agar for 2 days and washed with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (1×PBS, pH 7.2). Yeast suspensions were adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 108 cells/mL, and 100 μL of this suspension (1 × 106 cells/mL) was inoculated into 1 mL of RPMI-1640 medium in 24-well plate, followed by incubation at 37°C for 24 hours. Yeast cells were then harvested by centrifugation at 4,500 rpm for 10 minutes. Total RNA was extracted using NucleoZOL (Macherey-Nagel, Germany), and cell disruption was performed with 0.1 mm glass beads in a bead beater (Biospec, Bartlesville, OK, USA). RNA isolation was completed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. One microgram of total RNA was converted into cDNA using Maxime RT PreMix (LiliF Diagnostics, Korea). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using the SensiFAST™ SYBR Lo-ROX Kit (Bioline Ltd, UK) on a 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Primer sequences for selected genes are provided in Supplementary Table S1. The qRT-PCR conditions were an initial denaturation at 95°C for 60 seconds, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 60 seconds and 60°C for 60 seconds. Gene expression levels were analyzed using the 2− ΔΔCt method, with actin as the reference gene. All measurements were conducted in triplicate across three independent experiments.

Galleria mellonella pathogenesis model and histopathological findings

G. mellonella killing assays

To investigate the in vivo pathogenicity between adapted and non-adapted isolates, a G. mellonella killing assay was performed. Larvae, weighing between 300 and 350 mg, were utilized for all experiments. A sample size was determined using an online tool (https://clincalc.com/stats/samplesize.aspx) for a chi-squared test comparing two proportions (control survival = 80%, treatment survival = 20%, α = 0.05, power = 0.8), resulting in 10 larvae per group. To assess comparative virulence, both non-adapted (NA) and adapted isolates (P1, P5, P10; SP, RP, IP, and HW) were tested, along with PBS and methanol as control groups. G. mellonella larvae (10 per group, with three independent replicates; total of 270 larvae) were inoculated with 20 μL of yeast suspension containing 2 × 106 cells. Each treatment was administered via hemocoel injection through a left proleg of larvae. After inoculation, larvae were incubated in petri dishes at 37°C. Survival rates were monitored at 24-hour intervals over a 7-day period. Larval death was determined by the absence of movement in response to tactile stimulation with a sterile pipette tip. The larvae were also inoculated with five inoculum concentrations (2 × 106 to 2 × 102 cells/20 μL) to determine the lethal dose (LD50). Moreover, to quantify the fungal burden, we extracted hemolymph from larvae at 0- and 48-hours post-infection. Each larva was chilled on ice for 10 minutes. Hemolymph was then subsequently collected and serially diluted in 1×PBS. The fungal load per larva was quantified by enumerating CFUs on SDA following incubation at 37°C for 48 hours.

Preparation of histopathological sections from G. mellonella larvae

For histopathology procedures, each group of C. haemulonii-infected G. mellonella larvae was preserved in 10% (v/v) buffered PBS-formaldehyde [38]. The preserved larvae were divided into three transverse segments using a sterile razor blade (Gillette, MA, USA), dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin using a Leica TP1020 Semi-enclosed Benchtop Tissue Processor at 60°C under vacuum. This process was based on a method modified from [39]. Tissue blocks were sliced into serial sections measuring 3–5 μm in thickness, depending on the sample quality, using a Thermo Scientific Microm HM 325 microtome. The sections were then stained with standard hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) using a Thermo Scientific Gemini AS automation. Finally, the stained slides were visualized with an Olympus BX43microscope.

Statistical analysis

The data were subjected to statistical analysis by using a range of tests, including the two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test, ordinary one-way ANOVA in conjunction with Turkey’s multiple comparison test, and two-way ANOVA employing Sidak’s multiple comparisons test, chi-square test, and Survival analysis (log-rank) test, depending on the specific experimental context. A significance threshold of p < 0.05, p < 0.001, and p < 0.0001 was adopted for all statistical analyses. The data analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (version 9.0) and IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29).

Results

Intracellular survival and competitive growth of amoeba-adapted isolates

We investigated the co-adaptation of CHC environmental isolates to A. castellanii, a free-living amoeba. We examined five CHC isolates, as detailed in Supplementary Table S2: two C. haemulonii isolates (MH from mushroom and WH from hot spring water) and three C. duobushaemulonii isolates (BG from beetle’s gut, and ES1 and ES2 from estuarine water). The experimental approach involved co-culturing these organisms and is detailed in Figure 1(A-B). To analyze yeast characteristics, we isolated individual colonies from both the co-incubation (adapted) and control (non-adapted) samples at three points: day 3 (1st passage), day 15 (5th passage), and day 30 (10th passage). Both adapted and non-adapted isolates from each of the five strains were cultured on YPD agar plates. Within 48 hours, distinct colonies composed of morphologically similar yeast cells were observed on the agar surface (Supplementary Figure S1).

To assess differences in intracellular survival, we compared the percentage survival of adapted isolates to their non-adapted isolates following amoebic engulfment. The result found that the adapted isolates WH, ES1, and ES2 showed minimal changes in their intracellular survival capacity over the course of the experiment. In contrast, 30-day adapted isolates of BG and MH demonstrated significantly higher survival percentages compared to their non-adapted strains. Moreover, many yeast cells of BG were observed within amoebae, resulting in host cell lysis (Figure 1(C-D) and Supplementary Figure S2). These findings suggest that the adapted yeast cells might either be phagocytosed more frequently or be capable of proliferating within the amoebic host. To test the enhanced growth potential of the adapted isolates, we monitored the growth kinetics of all isolates over a 72-hour period. The finding revealed that adapted isolates from BG and MH exhibited accelerated growth rates compared to their respective control groups (Supplementary Figure S3). In addition, we found a unique characteristic in some adapted isolates, showing cellular aggregation. This aggregative phenotype was revealed in adapted isolates of BG and MH, particularly in the 10th passage of BG (Figure 1(E-F) and Supplementary Figure S4). However, this feature was less pronounced in its non-adapted isolate and absent in other adapted isolates from different strains. These results demonstrated that not all adapted CHC isolates responded uniformly to amoebic presence. These findings suggest that prolonged exposure to amoebae may differentially influence the survival, proliferation, and aggregative properties in some CHC strains, with the greatest alterations occurring in the isolate of C. duobushaemulonii from the beetle’s gut.

Selection of altered phenotypes of amoeba-adapted isolates

To assess phenotypic changes, the five strains were subjected to serial passage and subsequently plated on YPD agar supplemented with phloxine B. This vital dye enhances visualization of colony morphology by selectively accumulating in either stressed or damaged cells, thereby enabling the differentiation of distinct morphotypes based on pigmentation patterns. After 5 days of incubation at 28°C, the colonies were observed. The MH, WH, ES1, and ES2 displayed a smooth white appearance. In contrast, the adapted isolates in the 10th passage of C. duobushaemulonii BG exhibited five colony morphologies: smooth-pink (SP), intense-pink (IP), smooth-white producing hyphae (HW), and rough-pink (RP) colonies (Figure 2(A-B)). Microscopic analysis revealed that the smooth white colonies across all isolates comprised spherical and budding yeast cells (Figure 2(C)). The SP colonies contained a mixture of normal yeast cells and cellular aggregates. IP colonies consisted of budding yeast, cell clusters, and some elongated cells. HW colonies featured both yeast cells and filamentous forms, while the RP colonies were characterized predominantly by aggregated filamentous forms. Therefore, the adapted and non-adapted BG isolates were selected for subsequent investigations.

BG-adapted yeast cells demonstrated reversible phenotypic switching between smooth-white, SP, and RP colony morphologies. When grown on YPD medium containing phloxine B at temperatures from 28°C to 37°C, the transition frequencies from smooth-white to SP were: P1 (94.47 ± 2.72%), P5 (91.70 ± 0.33%), and non-adapted isolates (95.92 ± 1.83%). SP and IP P10 colonies shifted to RP at rates of 53.16 ± 2.45% and 68.23 ± 2.89%, respectively (Figure 2(D,F)). Additionally, HW cells switched to SP at a frequency of 52.36 ± 5.75%. However, no transitions were seen in the SP colonies. Upon a temperature change from 37°C to 28°C, most colonies from P1, P5, and the non-adapted group reverted to white-smooth, while SP colonies of P10 reverted to smooth-white at a rate of 27.77 ± 13.71%. Similarly, RP colonies reverted to SP at a frequency of 31.11 ± 4.21%. HW colonies, however, demonstrated a reduced frequency of transitioning to SP (20.53 ± 6.71%) (Figure 2(E,F)). Interestingly, we observed an abnormality in the filamentous form, as it absorbed the propidium iodide stain, which was not observed in other forms (Figure 2G). Therefore, the adapted yeast cells exhibited temperature-dependent, reversible transitions among smooth-white, SP, and RP colony morphologies, with varying frequencies across different passage isolates (P1, P5, and P10) and non-adapted strains, suggesting the development of complex phenotypic plasticity in response to repeated amoebic exposure.

Influence of amoebae on adapted Candida biofilm formation

We assessed three key biofilm characteristics following 48 hours of BG strain incubation on polystyrene surfaces. The parameters evaluated were total biomass, quantified through crystal violet staining of methanol-fixed cells; cellular viability, measured by MTT reduction assay; and extracellular matrix (ECM) production, determined by safranin absorption in unfixed yeast cells. Our results revealed that adapted isolates of BG in C. duobushaemulonii (P10; SP, RP, IP, and HW) produced significantly greater biofilm levels than the non-adapted isolate (Figure 3(A)). These P10 isolates also demonstrate enhanced metabolic activity within their biofilm structures (Figure 3(B)). Notably, the P10 IP and HW isolates developed distinctive interconnected networks of yeast cells and showed significantly increased ECM production (Figure 3(C)). These findings suggest that co-cultivation with amoebae generated selective pressures that promoted increased biofilm-forming capacity in C. duobushaemulonii.

Genes related to morphological changes and biofilm formation are induced by amoeba interaction

To further investigate the impact of amoeba interaction with BG isolates on genes regulating morphological changes and biofilm formation, we analyzed the transcriptional expression levels of key regulatory genes using qRT-PCR. Notably, C. duobushaemulonii homologs of the transcription factor WOR1 (CXQ87_004532) were significantly upregulated in the adapted isolates P10:RP and P10:HW compared to the non-adapted isolate (Figure 4(A)). Similarly, transcription factor CPH1 (CXQ87_000890) and the cyclin gene HGC1 (CXQ87_003669) were predominantly expressed in P10:RP and P10:HW (Figure 4(B,C)). In contrast, their expression levels were markedly reduced in other isolates with minimal morphological changes. WOR1 is a white-opaque regulator protein essential for the switch between two heritable states, white and opaque, in C. albicans and C. auris [40,41]. HGC1, a hypha-specific G1 cyclin-related protein, is critical for hyphal morphogenesis in C. albicans and Candida spp [25,42]. CPH1, a transcription factor, governs the developmental switch between yeast and mycelial forms [25,43]. Furthermore, homologue genes associated with adhesion (ALS1; CXQ87_001667) and biofilm formation (BCR1; CXQ87_002954 and EGF1; CXQ87_000637) were upregulated in most adapted isolates compared to the non-adapted isolate (Figure 4(D-F)). These results are consistent with the theory that amoeba interactions induce morphological changes and biofilm formation through the regulation of these genes.

Influence of amoebae on the adapted isolate's ability to resist environmental stressors

We evaluated the impact of amoebae co-cultivation on the tolerance of C. duobushaemulonii BG isolates to environmental stressors. BG yeasts were exposed to various conditions, including high salinity, elevated iron levels, extreme pH, and thermal stress (42°C). Growth was assessed by plating serial dilutions (106 to 103 cells/mL) under these stress conditions. In the salinity tolerance experiments, the P1, P5, and P10 of HW isolates exhibited growth patterns similar to non-adapted strains when exposed to 0.5 M and 1 M NaCl concentrations. However, the P10 SP, RP, and IP isolates showed limited growth at 106 cells/mL on media supplemented with 0.5 M NaCl, and their growth was completely suppressed at 1 M NaCl (Figure 5A). This finding indicates that some adapted populations displayed decreased tolerance to high salt concentrations, possibly due to impairments in osmotic-regulatory mechanisms or cell membrane function. Among the adapted strains, only P10:HW developed enhanced osmotic tolerance, showing more growth under high-salt conditions. Furthermore, adaptation led to enhanced iron resistance in the isolates, especially for the P10 SP, RP, IP, and HW isolates (Figure 5B). This increased tolerance to elevated iron levels suggests that these adapted strains may have developed improved defenses against iron-induced oxidative stress. Interestingly, two adapted isolates, P10 RP and HW, were able to grow in both acidic (pH 3.0) and alkaline (pH 10.0) conditions (Figure 5(C)). However, none of the isolates could tolerate thermal stress at 42°C (Figure 5(D)). These results demonstrate the substantial influence of prolonged interactions with A. castellanii on the stress tolerance of BG C. duobushaemulonii. Adaptation to amoeba exposure influenced their resistance to salinity, iron toxicity, and extreme pH, revealing dynamic phenotypic plasticity in response to environmental pressures.

Amoeba interaction influences the production of extracellular hydrolytic enzymes in the C. duobushaemulonii BG isolate

Extracellular hydrolytic enzymes play a crucial role in fungal pathogenicity by facilitating host adhesion and invasion. We assessed the esterase activity of each BG isolate using Tween 80 opacity test medium. As shown in Figure 6(A), the highest esterase activity was observed in the P10:HW isolate (Ez value = 0.5422 ± 0.0239; 45.77%), followed by P10:IP (Ez value = 0.7147 ± 0.0118; 28.52%), P10:RP (Ez value = 0.7842 ± 0.0432; 21.58%), and P10:SP (Ez value = 0.78157 ± 0.0577; 21.84%). The P1 and P5 isolates showed no detectable esterase activity and were similar to untreated BG cells. For secreted aspartyl protease activity, only P10:HW showed significant production (Pz value = 0.4629 ± 0.0299; 53.70%), while P1 and P5 were similar to untreated cells. Remarkably, the SP, RP, and IP of P10 isolates lost the ability to secrete this enzyme (Figure 6(B)). Although all isolates demonstrated hemolytic activity on sheep blood agar, the P1, P10:SP, and P10:HW produced lower amounts than the non-adapted isolate (Figure 6(C)). Phospholipase production, assessed using egg yolk agar, was present in the P10 groups with the greatest production in P10:HW (Pz value = 0.5476 ± 0.0668; 45.23%), followed by P10:SP (Pz value = 0.8104 ± 0.0658; 18.95%), P10:RP (Pz value = 0.8323 ± 0.0720; 16.76%), and P10:IP (Pz value = 0.8785 ± 0.0614;12.14%) (Figure 6D). Non-adapted cells and P1 and P5 adapted cells did not produce detectable amounts of phospholipase. These results indicate that prolonged adaptation to amoebae enhances enzymatic activities in C. duobushaemulonii, which may contribute to its virulence potential.

Amoeba-adapted C. duobushaemulonii BG isolates exhibit enhanced pathogenicity in G. mellonella larvae

We investigated the pathogenicity of amoeba-adapted and non-adapted C. duobushaemulonii BG isolates in G mellonella. Larvae were inoculated with varying concentrations (104 to 108 cells/mL) of each isolate and monitored at 37°C for seven days. At 24 hours post-infection, survival rates decreased in a dose-dependent manner, with the highest mortality observed in larvae inoculated at 108 cells/mL. Notably, larvae infected with P10:HW and P10:RP at this highest concentration exhibited complete mortality within four and five days, respectively. Although the non-adapted isolate and other amoeba-adapted isolates also reduced larval survival, they did not cause complete mortality, even at the highest dose (Figures 7A-C, and Supplementary Figure S5). To further investigate the impact of amoeba adaptation, we selected the highest infection dose (2 × 106 cells/larva) to compare survival rates between adapted and non-adapted isolates. The results showed that P10:HW and P10:RP exhibited significantly higher virulence than the non-adapted isolate, with survival rates of 0/30 and 2/30 larvae at day 7, respectively (Figure 7D). LD50 analysis further confirmed the increased virulence of the amoeba-adapted isolates. P10:HW was the most virulent strain, with an LD50 of 7.17 ± 2.14 × 104 CFU/mL at day 7, followed by P10:RP with an LD50 of 1.25 ± 0.15 × 105 CFU/mL (Table 2). These findings demonstrate that amoeba-adapted C. duobushaemulonii BG isolates exhibited enhanced virulence in G. mellonella, with effects that were both time- and dose-dependent. To investigate whether larval mortality correlated with C. duobushaemulonii proliferation in infected G. mellonella, larvae were inoculated with 1 × 106 CFUs of amoeba-adapted or non-adapted isolates, and fungal burdens in hemolymph were quantified after 48 hours. Results indicated that the CFUs of isolates P1 and P5 were comparable to those of the non-adapted isolate. Notably, the HW, RP, IP, and SP isolates from passage 10th showed significantly higher CFUs than the non-adapted isolate, reaching 2.59 ± 0.23, 1.68 ± 0.25, 1.11 ± 0.03, and 0.98 ± 0.12 × 105 CFUs, respectively (Figure 8). Moreover, a common characteristic observed in the adapted isolates was their extensive dissemination and proliferation within the fat body tissue (Figure 9). For isolates P1 and P5, localized melanized nodules were sparsely present in the fat body similar to the non-adapted isolate. In contrast, larvae infected with P10 isolates exhibited a markedly elevated level of invasion, with large numbers of melanized nodules distributed throughout the tissues, including the fat, of infected larvae.

To investigate the increased virulence and fungal burden associated with the P10 isolate, we analyzed H&E-stained tissue sections from larvae infected with either non-adapted or adapted isolates. In larvae infected with the non-adapted isolates, low-magnification observations (40×, Supplementary Figure S6) revealed sparse melanized nodules, confined to specific regions. At higher magnification (400×, Figure 10), these nodules displayed granuloma-like structures characterized by densely packed hemocytes encased in melanin deposits. These structures indicate a robust immune response in the accumulated area may help to prevent the spreading foreign microorganisms [44]. These findings suggest that the immune cells of G. mellonella effectively mitigate infections caused by non-adapted isolates. This observation is further supported by fungal burden assessments, which showed a significant reduction in CFU counts. In contrast, larvae infected with the adapted isolates, particularly P10, exhibited elongated hyphae-like structures in HW and aggregative in IP and SP as well as a mixed population in RP during infection (Figure 10). These adapted isolates appeared to rapidly induce severe tissue damage, as evidenced by fungal burden assessments, which correlated with widespread melanized nodules throughout the tissue (Supplementary Figure S6). Notably, granuloma-like structures were rarely observed in larvae infected with the adapted isolates, which might depend on a failure of G. mellonella to control the infection. Additionally, hemocytes migration toward melanized nodules were observed, reflecting an intensified inflammatory response (Supplementary Figure S7). Discussion

Free-living amoebae are ubiquitous unicellular protozoa found across diverse global environments. These organisms function as micro-predators through phagocytosis, sharing functional similarities with innate immune cells. Fungivorous amoebae have been described for several decades, although specific interactions and effects on fungal populations remain understudied. There are myriad environmental niches where saprophytic fungi and yeasts face persistent protozoal predation and nutrient competition. Thus, selective pressures have likely driven the evolution of defensive strategies against engulfment and intracellular processing. Our studies reveal that prolonged amoebic interactions trigger adaptive traits in members of the CHC, particularly in C. duobushaemulonii isolates from the beetle’s gut. Similar to C. albicans, exposure to amoebae induces morphological changes, such as increased filamentation, together with genetic variations that enhance survival and stress tolerance. Prior work shows that amoeba predation selects for traits that boost virulence, particularly the capacity to invade human lung epithelial cells, which a process mediated by mutations in genes encoding transcriptional repressors such as TUP1 and SSN6 [17]. In parallel, C. neoformans demonstrates adaptation mechanisms, notably melanization, a virulence factor controlled by specific genetic loci that confers resistance to amoeba predation without necessarily enhancing virulence in mammalian hosts [12,45]. Moreover, amoebae interactions trigger phenotypic changes, including enhanced polysaccharide secretion and oxidative stress tolerance, which are crucial for cryptococcal infection progression [14]. In our current work, we demonstrate that amoebae interactions affect the adaptations of environmental CHC species, enhancing survival capacity, environmental stress tolerance, and virulence potential.

Under intense predation pressure from amoebae, C. duobushaemulonii BG and C. haemulonii MH can survive within amoebae. Similarly, previous studies show that C. auris persists and multiplies within the amoeba species Vermamoeba (Hartmannella) vermiformis and A. castellanii [18]. Other Candida species, such as C. parapsilosis and C. glabrata, can proliferate in Hartmannella vermiformis [46], demonstrating their evolved ability to withstand predatory pressures from these protists. Our findings reveal that survival within the amoebae is associated with specific morphological characteristics. Notably, BG and MH isolates exhibit aggregation of yeast cells within 2 hours, while the feature was not observed in other isolates. Similarly, a previous study reported that CHC species can form aggregates, with cells remaining viable within these structures [27]. In fact, this aggregation may result from anomalies in cell division, leading to impaired separation of daughter cells following budding [47]. Thus, the formation of aggregates in the CHC could be a protective strategy against predators. Recent in vitro studies further support this notion that when C. auris aggregates were co-cultured with macrophages, the macrophage cells were unable to effectively recognize or eliminate these aggregates, likely due to their unique morphology [48]. Even though aggregative isolates of C. auris exhibited reduced virulence in the G. mellonella larvae model compared to non-aggregative isolates, they showed increased biofilm production, which is important for virulence [47]. Our findings similarly suggest that the BG and MH isolates, with their aggregative morphology, may achieve higher survival rates in amoebae, highlighting a potential role for this phenotype in immune evasion.

In our investigation of the C. duobushaemulonii BG strain’s response to A. castellanii predation, we observed significant morphological plasticity on YPD medium supplemented with phloxine B, particularly apparent in P10 isolates that were passed 10 times over 30 days. The colonies displayed distinct white-pink colorations and smooth-rough textural variations, encompassing aggregative, elongated aggregative, filamentous, and yeast-like morphotypes. Our findings revealed that genes associated with yeast-to-filament transitions, WOR1, CPH1, and HGC1, were predominantly upregulated in P10:RP and P10:HW isolates. This suggests that amoeba interaction enhances the ability of C. duobushaemulonii to undergo phenotypic switching from white to opaque and filamentous forms. These genes are key transcriptional regulators controlling phenotypic switching in Candida species such as C. albicans [49], C. auris [41], C. tropicalis [50], and C. dubliniensis [51]. In C. albicans, the reversible and heritable switch between white and opaque phenotypes is a well-characterized virulence trait, contributing to sexual reproduction and the ability to colonize diverse host niches. These observations are similar to recent findings demonstrating phenotypic switching in clinical isolates of the CHC under environmental stress [25]. Thus, it indicates that A. castellanii predation can induce saprophytic C. duobushaemulonii to undergo morphological switching, potentially enhancing their phenotypic flexibility.

Different morphological states are known to stimulate distinct amoebic killing responses [52], implying that certain morphological types may confer resistance to amoebic predation. This aligns with previous studies suggesting that these morphological alterations serve as fungal defense mechanisms against predation. For instance, the yeast-to-filament transition in the well-studied C. albicans demonstrates a phenotypic switching mechanism associated with mating, hydrolytic enzymes, and increased virulence. This transition also supports Candida in evading immune cell phagocytosis and adapting to the human skin environment. Additionally, morphological switching in C. lusitaniae has been strongly associated with antifungal resistance and filamentation [53,54]. These findings indicate that amoeba-driven phenotypic switching might contribute to antifungal resistance in C. duobushaemulonii as well. Moreover, C. albicans forms elongated hyphae and pseudohyphae when exposed to predation by D. discoideum and A. castellanii [17,55]. These two structures are crucial for resisting phagocytosis by both amoebae and macrophages [2]. In C. auris, pink and white morphologies have differential capacities for hyphal growth and aggregation [41,56,57]. Similarly, C. duobushaemulonii P10 isolates displayed filamentous and aggregative forms, which are associated with enhanced biofilm biomass and extracellular matrix formation. This observation supports previous findings that filamentous and aggregative forms in C. auris, C. albicans, and C. tropicalis promote biofilm formation, adherence, and altered lipid metabolism [17,58–60]. The significance of these morphological adaptations extends to pathogenicity, as biofilm formation represents a critical virulence determinant and contributes to antimicrobial resistance. Additionally, the complex structure of Candida biofilms, characterized by cellular networks embedded within an ECM composed of proteins, polysaccharides, lipids, nucleic acids, minerals, and water, provides protective barriers against host immune responses and antimicrobial agents [61,62]. This structural advantage not only enhances survival but may also contribute to pathogenic potential. Furthermore, studies using G. mellonella larvae and Caenorhabditis elegans models have demonstrated that strains of C. haemulonii, C. haemulonii var. vulnera, and C. duobushaemulonii with strong biofilm-forming abilities exhibit an increased potential for pathogenicity [31,63].

Co-culturing C. duobushaemulonii with A. castellanii results in phenotypic switching and increased biofilm production. Our findings also indicate that amoeba could enhance yeast survival under environmental stressors. Notably, C. duobushaemulonii isolates that have been passaged 10 times over 30 days show increased survival in high-iron conditions. However, high iron concentrations can be toxic to most organisms due to iron’s role in catalyzing Fenton and Haber-Weiss reactions, which produce reactive oxygen species (ROS). These effects can damage essential biomolecules and ultimately lead to cell death [64,65]. To regulate intracellular ROS levels, C. albicans and other Candida species have developed several detoxification mechanisms, including superoxide dismutase, catalase, and the glutathione/thioredoxin system [66]. Recently, alternative oxidase (AOX) has been identified in C. albicans and certain other fungi, helping maintain mitochondrial function in high-iron conditions [67]. Given the protein homology among Candida species, it is probable that C. duobushaemulonii adapted isolates might utilize similar enzymes to reduce intracellular iron and the level of labile iron pool (LIP) to manage cellular ROS levels.

Additionally, RP and HW isolates of C. duobushaemulonii were observed to tolerate both acidic (pH 3.0) and alkaline (pH 10.0) conditions, demonstrating the importance of pH adaptability for yeast survival. Environmental pH is a critical factor influencing cellular function, and the ability to adapt to pH changes is essential for survival. For example, S. cerevisiae can grow across a broad pH range, though it proliferates more effectively in acidic conditions. This adaptability is supported by cytosolic pH homeostasis, which is maintained through active proton extrusion via the plasma membrane H+-ATPase. Moreover, vacuolar pH regulation plays a crucial role in acidic tolerance by influencing mitochondrial-vacuolar signaling, protein metabolism, amino acid transport, and Fe/Cu metabolism [68–70].

In C. glabrata, low pH tolerance is associated with the regulation of acid stress response genes and alterations in lipid composition [71]. However, pH shifts can trigger morphological changes in C. albicans from nonpathogenic yeast form to a pathogenic hyphal form, a response associated with shifts from neutral to alkaline pH [72]. Moreover, the calcineurin pathway is crucial for high pH adaptation in yeast, involving calcium uptake that activates calcineurin, which then regulates gene expression to promote survival under alkaline conditions [72,73]. Additionally, the Rim101 pathway, initially characterized in S. cerevisiae and analogous to PacC in Aspergillus nidulans, mediates alkaline adaptation through membrane depolarization, proteolytic activation, and gene repression, also contributing to tolerance against weak acid stress [72,74–76]. This pathway, in coordination with the Slt2 MAP kinase, contributes to cell wall remodeling adaptation [77]. Furthermore, transient inhibition of the PKA pathway in response to alkalinization reduces cAMP levels, leading to nuclear localization of Msn2 stress-responsive transcription factor that facilitates adaptation to high pH [72,78].

Moreover, our finding reveals that some adapted isolates of C. duobushaemulonii, particularly P10:HW, exhibit halotolerance, enabling growth in YPD supplemented with 0.5 to 1 M NaCl. This adaptation might be involved with their ability to respond to osmotic and cationic stresses through a specific mechanism, which is the high-osmolarity glycerol (HOG) pathway [79]. In pathogenic fungi, the HOG1 stress-activated MAP kinase which works with PBS2 and SSK2 kinases for responding to various stresses [80]. Also, ion homeostasis can be maintained by transporters such as ENA1, NHX1, and TRK1 [81,82], which regulate ionic balance and protect the cell against cationic stress. Additionally, the CZF1 transcription factor also contributes to cell wall integrity and further enhances salt tolerance [83]. Hence, these findings suggest that C. duobushaemulonii isolates adapted through prolonged amoeba exposure acquire enhanced resistance to environmental stresses through mechanisms likely involving conserved stress-response pathways. However, this study focuses on phenotypic outcomes and does not elucidate the molecular mechanisms responsible for these changes. Further investigation is needed to confirm the involvement of specific stress-response pathways, including gene expression and protein activity changes. Future studies should incorporate transcriptomic profiling to identify differentially expressed genes and utilize gene knockout approaches to verify the roles of key regulatory pathways by comparing adapted strains with their parental counterparts.

Based on the pathogenicity of C. duobushaemulonii, P10 HW and RP amoeba-adapted isolates efficiently killed G. mellonella larvae, indicating enhanced virulence in this invertebrate model. This damaging ability may in part be associated with the increased production of extracellular hydrolytic enzymes, which are important virulence factors in Candida [84]. These enzymes play a vital role in host tissue invasion and damage by injuring tissues and facilitating the spread of infection [85]. Our findings demonstrate that prolonged interaction with amoebae significantly enhances enzymatic activities in some C. duobushaemulonii isolates. Specifically, increased production of esterase, secreted aspartyl proteinase (SAP), and phospholipase was observed. Each of these enzymes has a distinct role in fungal pathogenicity [86,87]. The interactions with amoeba may simulate environmental pressures that enhance fungal virulence, potentially bridging environmental adaptation and clinical pathogenicity. A notable limitation of this study is on the exclusive use of the G. mellonella model for virulence assessment. While this model provides valuable preliminary insights, confirmation of the enhanced virulence potential of amoeba-adapted C. duobushaemulonii isolates in a mammalian host model is essential for fully understanding their clinical relevance. Future studies employing murine infection models are necessary to validate these findings and further explore the translational implications of amoebic adaptation.

Moreover, the environmental adaptability of free-living amoeba and saprotrophic Candida species poses substantial challenges to human, animal, and environmental health, especially as climate change and human expansion continue. The survival strategies of both predators and fungi, including host and antifungal drug resistance acquisition, highlight the critical need for a one health approach to combat global health threats. Addressing antimicrobial resistance issues, enhancing water and sanitation infrastructure, and protecting ecosystems are essential. Aligning these efforts with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (UN-SDGs) offers a pathway to strengthen global health systems, tackling both the root causes and consequences of microbial adaptation in a rapidly changing world [88].

Conclusion

This study focuses on the adaptive response of CHC isolates derived from various environments to prolonged interactions with soil amoeba. Our findings reveal that amoeba-adapted isolates exhibit significant alterations in survival, morphology, biofilm formation, enzyme activity, and virulence, suggesting that amoebae act as a selective pressure facilitating fungal adaptation in this complex of species. The adaptations were greatest in a C. duobushaemulonii strain isolated from a beetle’s gut. This isolate exhibited the phenotypic plasticity and increased biofilm formation, with corresponding upregulation of regulatory genes such as WOR1, CPH1, HGC1, BCR1, and EGF1. Furthermore, adaptations led to increased production of extracellular hydrolytic enzymes, improved resistance to environmental stressors, and heightened virulence in G. mellonella larvae. These alterations observed in CHC-adapted isolates demonstrate the potential role of amoebae as environmental reservoir hosts that drive yeast evolution. The proposed hypothetical model of this study illustrates these interactions, providing a crucial concept for further investigation (Figure 11). Thus, these adaptations may enhance CHC survival and pathogenicity in diverse environments, raising further concerns about their clinical implications.

Proposed hypothetical model of amoeba-Candida duobushaemulonii interaction and induced yeast adaptation. This proposed hypothetical model is a modification of previously described concepts [17]. The stages of C. duobushaemulonii BG isolate engagement and entry into an amoeba and subsequent interactions are as follows: (i) the yeast is ingested via phagocytosis, primarily triggered by mannose-binding receptors; (ii) the formation of an early phagosome occurs, followed by its fusion with lysosomes to create the phagolysosome , where the yeast is typically destroyed through acidic conditions and oxidative stress; (iii) yeast cells that cannot escape the phagolysomal degradation are broken down; (iv) yeast cells that evade the phagosome-lysosome fusion or resist the antimicrobial conditions within the phagosome survive lysosomal killing, indicating an adaptive response and subsequent proliferation; (v) adaptation results in increased virulence, marked by phenotypic switching, enhanced tolerance to environmental stress, production of hydrolytic enzymes, increased adhesion, biofilm formation, and extracellular matrix development; (vi) the adaptation also triggers a stronger inflammatory response, leading to tissue damage. In conclusion, amoebae induce changes in the virulence factors and pathogenicity of C. duobushaemulonii isolated from the environment. Some icons in this model were sourced from BioRender.
