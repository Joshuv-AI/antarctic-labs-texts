Enhancing patient stratification methods for medication review during acute admissions
Authors: Louise Westberg Strejby Christensen, Helle Gybel Juul‐Larsen, Line Jee Hartmann Rasmussen, Esben Iversen, Kim Peder Dalhoff, Ove Andersen, Morten Baltzer Houlind
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMedication review in the hospital is an effective tool for identifying inappropriate prescribing, but not every patient can receive one due to resource constraints. Thus, it is important to identify patients who stand to benefit most from medication review. Patients are typically identified for medication review based on number of medications and age, but this may not be the most efficient strategy for identifying patients at high risk of future adverse clinical outcomes. In this exploratory study of 27 873 acutely admitted medical patients (≥18 years) at Copenhagen University Hospital Hvidovre, Denmark, we investigated whether other clinical factors could improve the prediction of adverse clinical outcomes and, therefore, be incorporated into patient stratification for medication review. The outcomes of interest were acute readmission or mortality within 90 days post‐discharge. Medication risk score (MERIS), renal function and anticholinergic cognitive burden did not improve prediction of adverse clinical outcomes compared to number of medications and age. However, addition of a frailty index based on routine biomarkers (FI‐OutRef) or plasma soluble urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (suPAR) did improve prediction of outcomes. Use of these practical proxies of disease burden may improve the identification of acutely hospitalized patients who would benefit most from a medication review.Keywords: adverse clinical outcomes, disease burden, medication burden, prediction, risk stratification

INTRODUCTION

Medication‐related admissions (MRAs) account for up to 15% of all acute admissions, with nearly 3% of affected patients dying. 1 Medication‐related adverse clinical outcomes are not clearly defined but are often related to various aspects of inappropriate prescribing, including incorrect doses, omitted medications and inappropriate medication selections. 2 , 3 Importantly, it is estimated that 33.6% of MRAs are preventable. 1

A systematic review found that medication review is considered one of the most effective strategies for addressing inappropriate prescribing during hospitalization. 4 However, the effectiveness of medication review for reducing future adverse clinical outcomes remains unclear. Only one study has demonstrated a significant effect of medication review on reducing MRAs among acutely admitted older medical patients, 3 whereas two studies reported significant reductions in all‐cause readmissions but not MRAs. 5 , 6 A recent Cochrane review reported that medication review likely reduces all‐cause readmissions and MRAs but not mortality. 7 These inconsistent findings regarding the effectiveness of medication review for reducing MRAs may in part reflect the lack of a standardized definition and method for identifying MRAs.

In acute care settings where time and resources are limited, it is also not feasible to perform medication review for all patients. Therefore, it is important to identify patients who are most likely to benefit from medication review, but there is no consensus on how to do this in acute care settings such as the emergency department. The inconsistent effectiveness on adverse clinical outcomes observed in prior studies may partly reflect this lack of targeted implementation. Existing patient stratification methods for medication review are commonly based on number of medications and age. In Denmark, common criteria for initiating a medication review include the use of five or more medications, age ≥65 years or a combination of these factors. 2 , 5 However, these simple factors may not sufficiently capture the complexity or clinical risk associated with inappropriate prescribing. An alternative stratification tool is the medication risk score (MERIS), an algorithm developed to identify acutely admitted patients at high risk of prescribing errors that incorporates number of medications, potential harm and interactions of specific medications and renal function. 8

There lacks strong evidence regarding the effectiveness of either of these patient stratification methods for predicting future adverse clinical outcomes. Given the high prevalence and preventability of MRAs, the Danish Health Authority has recommended further development of existing patient stratification methods. 9 While many clinical factors could potentially improve patient stratification for medication review, this study focused on estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), anticholinergic cognitive burden (ACB) scale and two markers of disease burden: “frailty index out of reference range” (FI‐OutRef) and plasma soluble urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (suPAR). Given that approximately half of all medications are renally excreted, eGFR is a critical parameter for guiding appropriate prescribing. 10 Medications with anticholinergic activity are prevalent among older hospitalized patients and are associated with increased risk of readmission, 11 and the ACB scale in particular has been shown to have strong association with mortality. 12 The complex interplay between medication use, chronic disease burden and adverse clinical outcomes has recently been highlighted. 13 However, current tools for measuring chronic disease burden such as the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) have limited utility in acute care settings due to underreporting of diagnoses. 14 FI‐OutRef, a marker of organ dysfunction based on routine biomarkers, 15 and plasma suPAR, a biomarker of systemic chronic inflammation, 16 are both strongly associated with adverse clinical outcomes. 17 , 18 suPAR is further associated with CCI, 19 while FI‐OutRef correlates with suPAR, 20 suggesting that both may offer a viable alternative to CCI as markers of disease burden in acute care settings.

This study aimed to investigate the ability of existing patient stratification methods for medication review to predict the risk of hospital readmission and mortality following acute admissions. Additionally, we sought to determine whether incorporating alternative clinical factors in the prediction models, such as eGFR, ACB, FI‐OutRef or suPAR, could improve the identification of patients who would benefit most from medication review based on these adverse clinical outcomes.

METHODS

Study design

This retrospective, exploratory, single‐centre, observational, register‐based cohort study included acutely admitted medical patients at the emergency department of Copenhagen University Hospital Hvidovre, Denmark, between November 2013 and March 2017.

Study population

During the study period, all acutely admitted medical patients had standard blood tests (including plasma suPAR) analysed at admission. The study population was defined as the first admission for each unique patient satisfying study criteria (hereafter: index admission). Exclusion criteria were <18 years old, missing ≥8 standard biomarkers or death during index admission.

Ethical approvals

The study was approved by the Danish Patient Safety Authority (ref. no.: 3–3013‐2655/1) and the Danish Data Protection Agency (HVH‐2014‐018, 02767; Privacy no.: P2019–694) and adheres to STROBE reporting guidelines. 21

Data

Demographic data were obtained from the Danish Civil Registration System, 22 while hospital admission, diagnoses and discharge information were obtained from the Danish National Patient Registry. 14 Diagnosis codes from the past 10 years, based on the International Classification of Diseases (10th revision), were used to calculate CCI. 23 Prescriptions redeemed within 4 months prior to admission were obtained from the Danish National Prescription Registry 24 and classified using the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification system. 25 Standard blood tests were collected at admission and analysed for 17 routine biomarkers (C‐reactive protein, leukocytes, neutrophils, haemoglobin, mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration, mean corpuscular volume, thrombocytes, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, sodium, potassium, albumin, alanine transaminase, alkaline, phosphatase, lactate dehydrogenase and international normalized ratio) plus suPAR at the Department of Clinical Biochemistry. 26

Existing and alternative clinical factors for patient stratification in medication review

The number of medications and age were extracted from national health registers, as described above. MERIS was calculated as described by Saedder et al., yielding a maximum score of 36.95. 8 The algorithm classifies number of medications (0–5, 6–11, ≥12) and eGFR (<30, 30–59, ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2) into three levels, while potential harm and interactions of specific medications are classified as low, medium or high risk. eGFR was calculated using the 2009 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation based on serum creatinine levels. 27 Patients were categorized by MERIS based on cohort distribution and thresholds established in prior studies (≤4, 5–14, ≥15). 8 , 28 , 29 , 30 The ACB scale (updated version) was used to assess anticholinergic burden, with medications assigned 1 to 3 points based on their anticholinergic activity. 31 , 32 FI‐OutRef was calculated from the number of 17 routine biomarkers outside reference range and was adjusted proportionally to maintain standardization if any biomarkers were missing. 20 Plasma suPAR was analysed using the suPARnostic Auto Flex ELISA (Virogates A/S, Birkerød, Denmark), according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Outcomes

Outcomes were acute (non‐elective) readmission or mortality within 90 days of discharge from index admission. Readmission and mortality represent pragmatic proxies for MRAs that may be more reliable. A 90‐day time frame is commonly employed in risk stratification studies, 18 providing sufficient time to capture delayed medication‐related consequences and transitional care effects, while being short enough to maintain attribution to the index admission. We chose this time frame to facilitate comparison across studies.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics are reported as number (%) for categorical variables and as median with interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables. All clinical factors were analysed as continuous variables. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated for 90‐day readmission and mortality for a base model (number of medications and age), an alternative model with MERIS and alternative models with number of medications and age combined with one of eGFR, ACB score, FI‐OutRef or suPAR. Discriminatory ability for each model was assessed using area under the curve (AUC), which reflects the model's ability to correctly distinguish between patients with and without the outcome. An AUC of 0.5 indicates no discrimination (e.g., random chance), while an AUC of 1 indicates perfect discrimination. We categorized discriminatory ability as follows: poor (AUC: 0.5–0.7), moderate (0.7–0.8), good (0.8–0.9) and excellent (0.9–1.0). 33 DeLong's test was used to evaluate statistical differences of each alternative model compared to the base model. A P‐value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

Study cohort

The initial study population included 29 285 acutely admitted medical patients with standard blood tests, including suPAR, analysed at admission during the study period. Of these, 1412 patients were excluded due to age <18 years, missing ≥8 standard biomarkers or death during index admission, resulting in a final study population of 27 873 patients. Of these, 7090 (25.4%) were readmitted, 389 (1.4%) died without readmission and 889 (3.2%) died following readmission. The study cohort was 52.5% female and had a median age of 59.3 years. Patients with higher MERIS were generally older with longer length of stay, higher number of medications, higher ACB score, higher CCI, lower eGFR, higher FI‐OutRef and higher suPAR levels (Table 1). Among the most frequently prescribed medications were antithrombotic agents and beta‐blockers, with increasing rates in patients with higher MERIS (Table 2).

Prediction of 90‐day readmission

The discriminatory ability of each model for predicting 90‐day readmission is presented in Figure 1A. The base model (number of medications and age) had an AUC [95% CI] of 0.658 [0.651–0.665] for predicting 90‐day readmission. Compared to the base model, MERIS (0.654 [0.647–0.661], P = 0.4) or the addition of eGFR (0.661 [0.654–0.668], P = 0.6) did not significantly improve discriminatory ability. In contrast, the addition of ACB score (0.660 [0.653–0.667]), FI‐OutRef (0.695 [0.688–0.702]) or suPAR (0.679 [0.672–0.686]) (all P < 0.001) led to statistically significant improvements in discriminatory ability, with the largest increase in AUC observed for FI‐OutRef.

Prediction of 90‐day mortality

The discriminatory ability of each model for predicting 90‐day mortality is presented in Figure 1B. The base model (number of medications and age) had an AUC [95% CI] of 0.831 [0.821–0.840] for predicting 90‐day mortality. Compared to the base model, MERIS (0.770 [0.758–0.782], P < 0.001) demonstrated significantly worse discriminatory ability and the addition of eGFR (0.832 [0.822–0.841], P = 0.9) did not significantly improve discriminatory ability. In contrast, the addition of ACB score (0.833 [0.824–0.843]), FI‐OutRef (0.881 [0.874–0.889]) or suPAR (0.861 [0.852–0.870]) (all P < 0.001) led to statistically significant improvements in discriminatory ability, with the largest increase in AUC observed for FI‐OutRef.

DISCUSSION

Main findings and conclusions

In this cohort of over 27 000 acutely admitted medical patients, number of medications and age had poor discriminatory ability for predicting 90‐day readmission but good discriminatory ability for predicting 90‐day mortality. In comparison, MERIS had worse discriminatory ability for both outcomes, suggesting that MERIS does not provide additional predictive value beyond number of medications and age. Similarly, addition of eGFR did not significantly improve discriminatory ability compared to number of medications and age, whereas addition of ACB score, FI‐OutRef or suPAR significantly improved discriminatory ability. The largest improvement in discriminatory ability for both outcomes was observed with FI‐OutRef; however, it did not result in change in category of discriminatory ability. Nonetheless, the model achieved close to moderate and excellent discriminatory ability for predicting readmission and mortality, respectively. In summary, a prediction model combining number of medications, age and FI‐OutRef was the most effective in distinguishing between patients who did and did not experience 90‐day readmission or mortality, suggesting that incorporating biomarker‐based proxies of disease burden could help identify acutely admitted medical patients who would benefit most from a medication review.

Existing patient stratification methods for medication review

Few studies have evaluated the standalone discriminatory ability of number of medications, with one large cohort study reporting poor discriminatory ability (AUC [95% CI]: 0.579 [0.560–0.598]) for predicting 30‐day readmission based on the number of discharge medications 34 – lower than observed for our 90‐day outcome. The same study also found that adding comorbidities and biomarkers in a combined risk score improved predictive value, supporting the need for more comprehensive risk models.

MERIS was originally developed to identify patients at high risk of prescribing errors by incorporating number of medications, potential harm and interactions of specific medications and renal function. 8 Høj et al. found that a MERIS threshold of ≥15 had moderate discriminatory ability for detecting inappropriate prescribing (AUC [95% CI]: 0.70 [0.65–0.74]), 30 but its utility for predicting adverse clinical outcomes has not previously been investigated. Our findings show that MERIS does not improve discriminatory ability over number of medications and age, at least for predicting 90‐day readmission and mortality. Despite its intended purpose for reducing inappropriate prescribing, MERIS‐based interventions have not been shown to improve outcomes such as prescribing errors, quality of life, health care utilization or mortality. 29 Importantly, patients categorized as low risk by MERIS may still carry substantial risk of adverse clinical outcomes. In our cohort, for example, patients with a MERIS of 5–14 had a median of 7 active prescriptions. Similarly, another study reported a mean of 5.2 medications in patients with MERIS <14, 28 suggesting that MERIS may not sufficiently account for polypharmacy and its associated risks. 35 This could help explain the worse discriminatory ability observed for MERIS compared to number of medications and age.

Further examination of patient characteristics showed that 60% of patients with MERIS ≥15 used ≥12 medications, while only 20% had an eGFR of <30 mL/min/1.73 m2. These findings, consistent with Høj et al., indicate that MERIS is largely driven by the number and types of medications, whereas renal function contributes relatively little to the overall score. In other words, while MERIS does include eGFR, its relative weight within the algorithm – and its contribution for predicting these outcomes – appears limited. Regardless of the relative contribution of eGFR in MERIS, we found that addition of eGFR to number of medications and age did not significantly improve discriminatory ability for adverse clinical outcomes. This finding is consistent with previous literature. For example, one study investigating mortality among acutely admitted older medical patients found that eGFR had less predictive value compared to number of medications or comorbidities. 36 Similarly, another study among patients admitted with acute heart failure found that eGFR had poor discriminatory ability for predicting readmission (AUC: ~ 0.50) and poor to moderate discriminatory ability for predicting mortality (AUC: 0.55–0.70), depending on which eGFR equation was used. 37 One potential explanation for the low impact of eGFR is the inaccuracy of creatinine‐based eGFR among hospitalized patients – particularly for older hospitalized patients, who are characterized by high rates of frailty and malnutrition that may lead to overestimating true renal function by more than 30%. 38 , 39 , 40 This drawback of creatinine‐based eGFR limits its value for predicting adverse clinical outcomes among acutely admitted medical patients.

While MERIS presents a theoretically appealing strategy for identifying inappropriate prescribing, our findings suggest that it offers no practical advantage over simpler factors such as number of medications and age for predicting 90‐day readmission or mortality.

Future directions in patient stratification

Despite widespread implementation, medication review interventions have generally failed to consistently reduce readmission and mortality. 7 The medication review interventions that have shown benefit tend to be complex, involving follow‐up, coordinated transitional care or patient/provider education. 5 , 6 , 41 These approaches are resource‐intensive and impractical in acute care settings, underscoring the need for quick but effective methods for identifying patients at highest risk of adverse clinical outcomes who stand to benefit most from medication review.

Both MERIS and ACB scale account for medications associated with adverse clinical outcomes, but our findings suggest that these tools offer limited or no additional predictive value beyond number of medications and age, at least in this patient population. This may partly be due to how they define “high‐risk” medications, which include medications such as antithrombotics, beta‐blockers, diuretics and sedatives. Since these medications are all common among acutely admitted medical patients, there is likely a substantial overlap between MERIS, ACB score and number of medications in our patient population. Therefore, it is unsurprising that they did not yield substantially different discriminatory ability. Another challenge with tools such as MERIS and ACB scale that depend on the pharmacological properties of individual medications is that they require clinical judgement, which is challenging to incorporate in acute care.

Our findings instead highlight the potential value of simple markers of disease burden, such as FI‐OutRef and suPAR. FI‐OutRef can be automatically calculated from routine admission biomarkers, making it a strong candidate for rapid risk stratification in acute care settings. Machine learning algorithms have shown that even a small subset of biomarkers used in FI‐OutRef can achieve strong predictive ability for short‐term mortality. 26 Similarly, suPAR has shown strong discriminatory ability for short‐term mortality (AUC [95% CI]: 0.92 [0.90–0.94]) and has been proposed as a tool for risk stratification in acute admissions, 42 though limited availability of suPAR assays may restrict its utility. We found that the addition of either FI‐OutRef or suPAR significantly improved discriminatory ability compared to number of medications and age, achieving close to moderate and excellent discriminatory ability for predicting readmission and mortality, respectively. One explanation for this finding is that these markers reflect a degree of clinical complexity that is not captured by number of medications and age. Incorporating these markers of disease burden into rapid risk stratification algorithms could help identify patients who would benefit most from medication review, thereby supporting more efficient resource allocation in acute care settings. Future research should explore which biomarkers best complement number of medications and age for predicting a wider range of adverse clinical outcomes – particularly ones caused by prescribing errors – and validate these findings across different clinical settings.

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of this study was the use of national health registers in a large and representative patient population. The study also has important limitations. First, this was a single‐centre study focusing on acutely admitted medical patients in Denmark, which may limit generalizability to other countries and clinical settings, including non‐acute (elective) admissions. The emergency department reflects a clinical setting where targeted medication reviews could have a substantial impact on patient outcomes, but future studies should investigate the most efficient way to identify patients for medication review in other clinical settings. Second, while our ultimate goal is to reduce MRAs, this outcome is difficult to define and cannot be easily determined using national registry data. Instead, we used all‐cause readmission and mortality as proxies for MRAs under the assumption that some proportion of these will be related to inappropriate prescribing. Future studies should look to validate these findings with outcomes more specific to medication use.
