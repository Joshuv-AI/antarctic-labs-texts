Identifying knowledge gap and adherence barriers for diabetic retinopathy screening: Findings from primary healthcare clinics in Pakistan
Authors: Asra Qureshi, Maham Fatima, Shirin Jalaluddin, Karim Damji, Haroon Tayyab, Unab I. Khan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTContext:Diabetes is a leading cause of mortality globally, with a growing prevalence in low- and middle-income countries. The increasing burden of diabetes has led to a surge in diabetic retinopathy (DR), a major cause of preventable blindness, emphasizing the need to address poor screening practices.Aim:This study aims to assess the frequency of DR screening, patients’ perceived knowledge, and barriers to adherence at two primary health care (PHC) clinics in Pakistan.Settings and Design:This descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted at two PHCs.Materials and Methods:Data were collected using a validated electronic questionnaire on REDCap®, covering sociodemographics, diabetes profile, DR screening knowledge, frequency, and adherence barriers.Statistical Analysis:Descriptive analysis summarized participant characteristics, and site-specific comparisons used the Chi-square test to explore differences in clinical profiles, DR knowledge, treatment, prevention, and adherence using STATA/SE v18.0.Results:Of 321 participants (n = 165 PHC-1 and n = 155 PHC-2), PHC-2 patients demonstrated higher knowledge of DR as a diabetes complication (76% vs 54%; P < 0.01), with its asymptomatic nature (74% vs 40%; P < 0.001). Additionally, 59% knew regular eye exams could prevent DR progression, with PHC-2 patients more informed than PHC-1 (73% vs 46%; P < 0.001). Across PHCs, 37% underwent routine eye exams every 6 months, although 70% preferred annual screening at their diabetes care facility.Conclusion:Despite good DR knowledge, participants did not adhere to yearly screenings. Integrating screenings into PHCs by equipping them with the necessary tools can enable timely detection and referral, address barriers, and reduce the DR burden on secondary and tertiary ophthalmology facilities.

Introduction

Diabetes is one of the top ten leading causes of mortality globally.[1] Around 536.6 million adults have been diagnosed with diabetes, of which more than 80% reside in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). In LMICs, a higher prevalence is found in people living in urban as compared to rural areas.[2] In Pakistan, the national prevalence of diabetes is 31% and is expected to exceed 34% by 2045.[2] Approximately USD 2.6 billion are spent on diabetes-related healthcare annually.[3] However, the economic impact extends beyond healthcare costs as diabetes-related loss of productivity is estimated to cost the country 3 billion USD annually.[4]

The growing burden of diabetes is associated with a parallel surge in the prevalence of diabetic retinopathy (DR), and more than 50% of patients with diabetes are expected to develop DR.[5] DR, a microvascular complication of diabetes, affects approximately 103 million adults globally.[6] Patients often remain asymptomatic during the early stages of retinopathy as the initial damage is typically mild and may persist unnoticed for years. Consequently, it is common for the condition to be diagnosed only at more advanced stages.[78]

DR is a major cause of preventable blindness among adults in the working-age population worldwide.[6] Optimal glycemic control, regular screening for retinal disease, and awareness of DR are crucial factors in mitigating disease progression.[910] A recent study found the prevalence of DR in 28% of Pakistan’s population.[11]

Lack of awareness of DR screening is another reason for the high prevalence. A study across 25 primary care centers in Pakistan reported that 77% of people with diabetes had never undergone an eye examination, highlighting a critical gap in prevention and early detection.[12] Notable barriers to screening included lack of knowledge about DR screening, inadequate health literacy, lack of time, and financial constraints.[13] Moreover, adhering to lifelong DR screening and prolonged treatment is exhausting for many, even when cost is not a barrier.[14] At the healthcare provider level, challenges included a shortage of skilled healthcare professionals, an inefficient referral system, limited availability of DR screening equipment, and overburdened treatment facilities.[13]

Primary care professionals (PCPs) play a crucial role in DR screening as some of these barriers can be alleviated if routine eye examinations for DR are being performed at primary health care (PHC) facilities.[15] Given that 90% of diabetic patients are being treated at PHCs,[16] offering DR screening in these settings enhances accessibility, practicality, and educational opportunities, reinforcing a person-centered approach to diabetes management.[17] To address the growing concern of DR, PHCs must adopt innovative strategies to meet the needs of diabetic patients. Research has shown that interventions aimed at improving access to care are associated with higher adherence to DR screening when these services are made available within PHCs.[181920]

The World Health Organization also emphasizes integrating primary eye care into PHC[21] through timely screening, prevention, and treatment of vision-threatening conditions as well as robust referral systems to ophthalmologists if ocular diseases are detected.[22] Different methods of DR screening include handheld cameras,[2324252627] teleophthalmology,[282930] fundoscopy, artificial intelligence,[28313233] and mobile screening units. These remote screening approaches have been effective in improving DR screening compliance, primarily within PHCs.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the frequency of DR screening in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus, patients’ perceived knowledge, and barriers to adherence to DR screening in two PHCs within a private healthcare system in Karachi, Pakistan. The findings will provide valuable insights into the factors influencing DR screening in a diverse urban population and evaluate the need for on-site screening equipment, facilitating PCP-led initiatives to integrate DR screening within PHCs.

Materials and Methods

A descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted at two primary care sites located in different geographic locations of a private healthcare system. PHC-1 is housed at the tertiary care academic medical center,[34] whereas PHC-2 is in a community setting. Both facilities provide subsidized care for patients. PHC-1 has easier access to referrals within the academic medical center, whereas patients from PHC-2 are referred to the AMC or other ophthalmologists within the community.

Participants included adult patients with a medical history of type 2 diabetes mellitus who visited either site during the study period. Sample size calculations were made using reported estimates of knowledge of DR screening among patients with diabetes, 73%[35] ±5% at α =0.05 and 80% power. The minimum required sample was 321 individuals. OpenEpi® v. 3.01 was used for sample computation.[36]

The institutional ethics review committee approved the study (ID# 2024-9723-28591). Patients with diabetes were approached and completed the written informed consent process in the language they understood (English or Urdu). Participants were provided with a signed copy of the consent for their records.

The study questionnaire was adapted from Dervan et al. and Alwazae et al.[3738] The questionnaire was electronically designed on REDCap® and comprised four main sections: sociodemographic details, diabetes profile, assessment of knowledge related to DR screening, and barriers toward adherence to screening. Participants responded to the questions in a private space. Confidentiality was ensured by using unique study identifiers; deidentified data were used for analysis.

We performed descriptive analysis to report on the sociodemographic characteristics of participants. Site-specific comparisons were made using the Chi-square test of independence to examine the differences in the clinical profile of participants, knowledge about DR screening, treatment, prevention, and their adherence to DR screening. Analysis was performed using Stata Now 18.5, SE—Standard Edition, Statistics and Data Science. Copyright 1985-2023 StataCorp LLC StataCorp, 4905 Lakeway Drive, College Station, Texas 77845 USA.

The majority of participants across PHCs had attained either college-level education or higher, accounting for 48% of the sample. Importantly, 59% of all participants knew that regular eye examination can prevent the progression of DR, with a higher percentage of PHC-2 patients being knowledgeable compared to PHC-1 (73% vs 46%; P < 0.001). Across PHCs, only 37% of participants had routine eye examinations every 6 months, while 10% never had their eyes screened. Interestingly, only 26% of patients visiting PHC-1 were using ophthalmology services within the AMC, and 66% were seeing ophthalmologists outside the health system.

Of note, 70% of participants reported a preference for having annual eye screening at the PHC where they received diabetes care.

Discussion

This is a PHC-based, cross-sectional study assessing perceived knowledge, screening frequency, and barriers to the screening of DR. Our findings demonstrate that 51% of patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus had ocular complications including DR, cataracts, and glaucoma. Overall, participants were knowledgeable about DR as a complication of type 2 diabetes mellitus, 57% were aware of its asymptomatic nature, and 59% understood that regular screening can help prevent its progression. While some of the existing literature also revealed that the majority of patients with diabetes were aware of associated ocular diseases,[3940] some studies also report contrasting findings.[4142] This discrepancy in knowledge may stem from differences in the sources of DR information as a significant percentage of our study participants (65%) reported receiving this knowledge from their doctors. Notably, studies reporting patients’ higher levels of DR knowledge often identify doctors as their primary source of information.[3940] Therefore, DR screening and education if implemented at PHCs might lead to increased awareness and reduction in DR burden.

Aligned with our results, 50% of the participants in a study by Saikumar et al. agreed that timely DR screening is needed, and it prevents progressive damage to eyes.[43] In addition, we found consistent results with studies that have explored the association between increased awareness of DR and level of education, establishing a positive correlation between them.[394044]

Our results revealed that PHC-2 participants overall demonstrated a greater level of knowledge than those from PHC-1. This difference may be attributed to better disease awareness associated with the longer disease duration as observed among PHC-2 participants as 49% of PHC-2 participants had diabetes for greater than 10 years compared to 36% of PHC-1 participants. Similar results have been reported in other studies where participants with a longer duration of type 2 diabetes mellitus were more aware of associated eye complications.[4546]

Participants from both PHCs identified multiple barriers that hindered their screening practices. These included cost, waiting times, lack of information, fear of results, and some who felt that screening was ineffective. The cost of screening has been identified as the predominant barrier in several studies.[3841]

A large majority (70%) of participants in our study expressed their preference for integrated care at the PHCs. This indicates the need for a patient-centered approach, emphasizing the importance of training PCPs and equipping PHC facilities with the necessary tools to enhance DR screening and improve patient outcomes. Existing literature supports the notion that such strategies have improved DR screening rates and adherence.

One such initiative was a 5-month quality improvement project conducted in a primary care setting in Whitsett, North Carolina, to improve DR screening rates and patient awareness using mobile screening units. Involving 100 adults with type 2 diabetes, the project led to a 15% increase in screening rates compared to 3 months before implementation, demonstrating its effectiveness. Moreover, patients appreciated the convenience, efficiency, and familiarity of screening at their PCP’s clinic, enhancing their engagement. PCPs and staff found the intervention manageable, with no added workload. As screening rates improved and patient feedback remained positive, PCPs showed growing interest in future screenings, reflecting a shift toward proactive care and closing gaps in DR management.[24]

Supporting these findings, a 5-year multicenter randomized controlled trial in the United Kingdom found that patients who received digital DR screening at a primary care clinic were more likely to undergo DR screening than those required to visit an ophthalmologist for an additional appointment.[47] Similarly, an automated DR screening system was implemented in a PHC clinic serving a low-income metropolitan population. It significantly improved adherence to follow-up eye care recommendations and reduced referrals for low-risk patients.[34]

Despite efforts to enhance patient care, a DR screening program initiated at PHCs in Aracaju, Brazil, was prematurely terminated. The program offered DR screening and free specialized treatment for high-risk DR cases. However, inconsistent patient attendance, low adherence, and organizational inefficiencies at PHCs contributed to its discontinuation.[48] To evaluate the long-term impact of subsidized DR screening programs, a longitudinal study is needed to determine whether access to DR screening at PHC facilities in LMICs improves patient compliance and facilitates early detection.

One of the key strengths of this study is the selection of participants from two different PHCs resulting in a diverse perspective of patients based on their socioeconomic status and knowledge. Regardless, our participants were from an urban metropolis, with access to health care giving them a better understanding of diabetes and its complications.

Conclusion

In conclusion, while our participants had a good level of knowledge regarding DR, the majority did not adhere to the recommended yearly screening. Our study highlights the need to train PCPs and equip PHC facilities with tools to ensure ease of access to low-cost and accurate screening. PHCs serve as the first point of contact for patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and thus can play a vital role in enhancing early detection while alleviating the burden on secondary and tertiary ophthalmology facilities. Additionally, this strategy could effectively address barriers such as cost and long waiting times of going to specialized clinics solely for an eye exam. Moving forward, incorporating artificial intelligence-assisted tools and teleophthalmology at PHC facilities could further reduce these barriers, creating promising opportunities to enhance accessibility, affordability, and accuracy in eye care.
