Mood, technical performance, and physiological responses after 4 vs 4 small-sided soccer games of different durations: A pre-post study
Authors: Zouhaier Farhani, Hatem Ghouili, Samia Boukari, Halil Ibrahim Ceylan, Amel Dridi, Nizar Souissi, Wissem Dhahbi, Anissa Bouassida, Nikolaos Mavritsakis, Raul Ioan Muntean, Ismail Dergaa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Small-sided games (SSGs) are widely used in soccer training to simultaneously develop technical, tactical, and physical abilities. Session structure, particularly duration and distribution (continuous vs intermittent), represents a key programming variable that may influence players’ mood, technical performance, and physiological responses. However, the specific effects of different SSG formats remain insufficiently clarified. This study aimed to examine how varying bout configurations (1 × 12 min, 2 × 6 min, and 3 × 4 min) during 4 vs 4 SSGs affect mood states, technical–tactical performance, and physiological responses in male soccer players. Twenty male semiprofessional soccer players (age: 20.7 ± 0.7 years) completed 3 SSG formats performed on separate occasions: 1 × 12 min, 2 × 6 min, and 3 × 4 min. Mood was assessed pre- and post-session using the Profile of Mood States questionnaire. Physiological responses included percentage of maximal heart rate (%HRmax), blood lactate concentration ([La]), and rating of perceived exertion (RPE). Technical–tactical variables were analyzed through systematic video-based performance assessment. The 12-minute continuous SSGs significantly improved mood, evidenced by reductions in depression, confusion, tension, anger, and total mood disturbance, although fatigue increased post-session. In contrast, the 2 × 6 min format led to increases in depression and anger, while the 3 × 4 min format showed no significant mood changes. Technical–tactical performance was superior during the 12-minute condition, with higher passing accuracy, greater tackle success, and fewer turnovers compared to intermittent formats. Physiologically, the continuous 12-minute SSGs elicited higher %HRmax and rating of perceived exertion values than both intermittent conditions, as well as slightly higher blood lactate concentrations compared to the 3 × 4 min format. A continuous 12-minute 4 vs 4 SSGs appears to provide the most effective integrated training stimulus, enhancing mood regulation, technical–tactical performance, and physiological load. These findings suggest that uninterrupted SSG formats may better simulate match demands and support optimal psychophysiological adaptations in semiprofessional soccer players.

1. Introduction

Soccer is the world’s most popular sport, with an estimated 265 million players globally, and it demands the complex integration of technical skills, tactical awareness, and exceptional physical conditioning for competitive success.[1,2] The physiological profile of elite soccer players reflects the sport’s intermittent high-intensity nature, with match analysis revealing players cover 10 to 13 kilometers per match at varying intensities, perform 150 to 250 brief intense actions, including sprints, accelerations, decelerations, changes of direction, and physical duels, interspersed with lower-intensity activities.[3,4] This activity pattern requires well-developed aerobic capacity to sustain repeated efforts over a 90-minute match, combined with anaerobic power for explosive actions, making soccer one of the most physiologically demanding team sports.[5,6] Contemporary soccer training methodology has evolved toward sport-specific approaches that replicate the multifaceted demands encountered during competition, moving beyond traditional isolated physical conditioning toward integrated training modalities.[7,8] Within this evolution, small-sided games have emerged as a cornerstone training method, defined as modified soccer games played on reduced pitch dimensions with fewer players than regular matches, often incorporating tactical constraints and rule modifications to emphasize specific technical or tactical objectives.[5,9] The appeal of small-sided games (SSGs) derives from their capacity to simultaneously develop multiple performance components, including aerobic and anaerobic fitness, technical skills under pressure, tactical decision-making, and psychological resilience, while maintaining high ecological validity and player enjoyment compared to decontextualized physical conditioning.[10]

The manipulation of SSG variables, including pitch dimensions, player numbers, task constraints, and bout duration structures, enables coaches to modulate training intensity and specificity to particular physiological adaptations and skill-development objectives.[11–13] Extensive research has characterized how pitch area per player influences running demands and heart rate responses, how player numbers affect ball contacts and tactical complexity, and how rule modifications direct technical-tactical behaviors.[14] Along with heart rate monitoring, the rating of perceived exertion (RPE) offers insight into athletes’ subjective perceptions of internal load and psychophysiological stress during training sessions. It has been validated as an accurate measure of internal training load in soccer athletes.[15] Recent studies in soccer training indicate that RPE remains a reliable measure of exercise intensity across various small-sided game formats and competitive levels, even when accounting for physiological load indicators.[16] Additionally, blood lactate concentration ([La]) is a recognized indicator of anaerobic metabolic contribution and exercise intensity. Recent studies on small-sided games in female players have utilized both RPE and lactate measurements to distinguish physiological responses across different game formats.[17] However, the bout duration structure, specifically whether total SSGs volume is delivered as continuous exercise or fragmented into intermittent bouts with passive or active recovery intervals, represents an under-investigated variable with potentially significant implications for training outcomes.[18] Bout duration manipulation directly influences the metabolic pathways recruited, the accumulation of physiological fatigue, and the psychological experience of training sessions.[19] Continuous, prolonged bouts may favor aerobic energy system development and technical skill execution under accumulated fatigue, while intermittent, shorter bouts with recovery intervals may allow higher work intensities but may potentially compromise skill practice under realistic match-like fatigue conditions.[20,21] Beyond physiological and technical considerations, the psychological responses to training, particularly mood state fluctuations, warrant systematic investigation given their established associations with training adaptation, injury susceptibility, overtraining syndrome development, and player adherence to training programs.[22,23]

Mood state assessment through validated instruments such as the Profile of Mood States provides quantifiable evaluation of affective responses to training interventions, with research demonstrating that excessive training loads or inappropriate training structures can induce mood disturbances, including increased tension, depression, anger, fatigue, and confusion, alongside decreased vigor, collectively manifesting as elevated total mood disturbance scores.[24–27] Previous investigations comparing SSGs with traditional high-intensity interval training have reported that SSGs may elicit more favorable mood responses, potentially attributable to their game-based nature, social interaction components, and enhancement of intrinsic motivation.[28] However, limited research has examined whether the temporal structure of SSGs delivery, specifically continuous versus intermittent bout durations, differentially influences psychological responses despite the considerable volume of literature addressing physiological and technical responses to SSGs format variations.[29] This knowledge gap represents a significant limitation for evidence-based training prescription, as coaches require a comprehensive understanding of how bout duration structures influence the full spectrum of training outcomes, including psychological well-being, technical-tactical performance quality, and physiological strain, to optimize training design and player development.[30,31] Furthermore, the interaction among psychological state, technical performance, and physiological demands remains incompletely characterized in the SSGs literature, despite theoretical frameworks suggesting that mood-state alterations may influence cognitive function, decision-making quality, motor skill execution, and perception of exertion during training activities.[32,33]

Based on the identified research gaps regarding bout duration effects on integrated psychological, technical-tactical, and physiological responses in soccer SSGs, our study aimed to comprehensively investigate the differential effects of 3 bout duration structures (continuous 12-minute, intermittent 2 × 6-minutes, intermittent 3 × 4 minutes) during 4vs4 format SSGs on mood state profiles assessed through Profile of Mood States (POMS) including total mood disturbance and individual subscale scores for tension, depression, anger, vigor, fatigue, and confusion, technical-tactical performance parameters including percentages of successful passes, successful tackles, successful duels, and ball loss occurrences, and physiological responses encompassing continuous heart rate monitoring with peak heart rate percentage calculations, post-exercise blood lactate concentrations, and ratings of perceived exertion assessments.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Ethical approval

The research protocol received approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Higher Institute of Sport and Physical Education of Kef, University of Jendouba, Tunisia (approval reference: Code PH-018-2023), and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki for research involving human participants. All participants provided written informed consent following a comprehensive explanation of the study procedures, potential risks, and their right to withdraw from participation at any time without consequence. The study was not registered as a clinical trial as it involved sports performance assessment in healthy athletic populations rather than therapeutic interventions.

2.2. Sample size calculation

The analysis of variance: n = (Zα + Zβ)2 × σ2 × (1 + (k-1) × ρ)/ (k × δ2), where Zα represents the critical value for type I error (1.96 for α = 0.05), Zβ represents the crucial value for type II error (0.84 for β = 0.20, corresponding to 80% statistical power), σ2 represents the estimated within-subject variance, k represents the number of repeated measurements (k = 3 conditions), ρ represents the correlation between repeated measurements (estimated at 0.6 based on previous SSGs research[34,35]), and δ represents the minimum detectable effect size. Based on pilot data and previous investigations examining the effects of SSG bout duration on heart rate responses,[36,37] we anticipated medium-to-large effect sizes (partial η2 ≥ 0.14) for the primary outcome measures. Power analysis conducted with G*Power 3.1 indicated that a sample size of n = 18 participants would provide 80% statistical power to detect significant differences with α = 0.05 for a repeated-measures ANOVA with 3 within-subject conditions and an assumed medium effect size (f = 0.25). To account for potential participant attrition and equipment failure, we recruited n = 20 participants, consistent with sample sizes used in comparable SSGs research on bout-duration manipulations.[38–40]

2.3. Population

Twenty male soccer players were recruited from a competitive regional-level team participating in the Tunisian third-division championship. Inclusion criteria specified: chronological age between 18 to 23 years, minimum 5 years of formal soccer training experience, current participation in systematic soccer training consisting of at least 4 sessions per week plus competitive match participation (establish a minimum of 1 match per week and 4 matches each month), absence of musculoskeletal injuries within 6 months before data collection, no history of cardiovascular, metabolic, or neurological disorders, no current medication use that could influence physiological or psychological responses to exercise, and willingness to adhere to pre-testing standardization procedures including dietary and activity restrictions. Exclusion criteria included: incomplete participation in all 3 experimental conditions, reported illness or injury during the study period, failure to comply with pre-testing standardization requirements, and equipment malfunction preventing complete data collection. Participant characteristics included: age 20.7 ± 0.7 years, height 179.5 ± 6.1 cm, body mass 67.2 ± 4.9 kg, body fat percentage 10.7 ± 0.7%, and soccer training experience 6.9 ± 1.2 years.

2.4. Experimental design

This study employed a repeated-measures within-subjects experimental design, in which all participants completed 3 bout-duration conditions (4 vs 4 SSGs) in a randomized, counterbalanced order to control for learning and order effects. The experimental period was conducted during the mid-competitive season, spanning 3 weeks, with each SSGs condition separated by at least 72 hours to ensure adequate recovery and minimize residual fatigue effects.[41] Participants maintained their regular training schedule throughout the study period, with experimental sessions replacing one of their scheduled weekly training sessions. On match days (Sundays), participants competed in their regularly scheduled league matches, with Monday designated for recovery through low-intensity regenerative activities. Experimental sessions were conducted at consistent times of day (between 14:00 and 16:00 hours) to minimize circadian rhythm influences on physiological and psychological responses.[42,43] They were performed on the same synthetic grass pitch to control environmental conditions. Each experimental session commenced with a standardized 15-minute warm-up protocol consisting of progressive intensity running (5 minutes at self-selected easy pace progressing to moderate intensity), dynamic stretching exercises for major lower body muscle groups (5 minutes), and soccer-specific movement patterns including changes of direction, acceleration, and deceleration actions (5 minutes). Following a warm-up and 5-minute passive recovery, participants completed the designated SSGs bout-duration condition with continuous heart-rate monitoring throughout the bout. Blood samples for lactate analysis were obtained approximately 3 minutes following completion of the final bout of each condition, RPE assessments were collected immediately upon cessation of the final bout, and POMS questionnaires were administered 15 minutes pre-exercise and immediately post-exercise for each condition. Technical tactical performance in all SSGs conditions was video-recorded using high-definition cameras and subsequently analyzed through notational performance analysis by a trained sports scientist.

Pre-testing standardization procedures included: 48-hour abstention from strenuous physical activity outside of regular training, 24-hour avoidance of alcohol consumption, 12-hour overnight fasting followed by consumption of a standardized breakfast consisting of 2 g carbohydrate per kg body mass, 0.3 g protein per kg body mass, and 0.2 g fat per kg body mass consumed 3 hours before testing, maintenance of adequate hydration status confirmed via urine specific gravity measurement < 1.020, 12-hour abstention from caffeine and other ergogenic substances, and minimum 8 hours sleep duration on the night preceding each experimental session. Participants were instructed to maintain consistent dietary patterns throughout the study period and to report any deviations from standardization requirements, symptoms of illness, or unusual fatigue to research personnel.

2.5. Used tests

2.5.1. Anthropometric measurements

Body mass was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg using a calibrated electronic scale (9179 SV3R; Salter, United Kingdom) with participants wearing minimal clothing and no footwear. Standing height was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm using a stadiometer (IP0955; Invicta Plastics Limited, Leicester, England) with participants standing erect, heels together, and head positioned in the Frankfort plane. Four skinfold thickness measurements (biceps, triceps, subscapular, suprailiac) were obtained in triplicate from the right side of the body using Harpenden skinfold calipers (Baty International, West Sussex, England) by a single experienced technician, with the median value used for subsequent calculations. Body density was calculated using the equation: D = 1.1765–0.0744 × (log10 ΣS), where ΣS represents the sum of the 4 skinfold measurements.[44] Body fat percentage was subsequently derived from body density using the Siri equation: Body fat (%) = (4.95/D - 4.50) × 100.[45]

2.5.2. Yo-Yo Intermittent Recovery Test Level 1

Peak heart rate values for subsequent calculation of the percentage peak heart rate during SSGs were determined using the Yo-Yo Intermittent Recovery Test Level 1 (YYIRT1), administered on the same artificial grass training surface used for the experimental SSG sessions.[46] The YYIRT1 protocol required participants to perform repeated 20-meter shuttle runs at progressively increasing speeds dictated by audio signals from a portable sound system, with 10-second active recovery periods consisting of 2 × 5-meter walks around cones positioned 5 meters from the start/finish line between each 2 × 20-meter shuttle run. The test commenced at 10 km/h with speed increments of 0.5 km/h occurring every 40 meters (equivalent to 2 shuttle runs), continuing until participants could no longer maintain the required pace for 2 consecutive shuttles or voluntarily terminated the test due to volitional exhaustion. Total distance covered in meters was recorded as the performance measure, and peak heart rate was recorded using Polar Team Sport System heart rate monitors (Polar Electro Oy, Kempele, Finland) with the highest 5-second average value during the test designated as peak heart rate for subsequent calculations.[47] The YYIRT1 has demonstrated high test-retest reliability (coefficient of variation 4.2–4.9%) and validity for assessing soccer-specific endurance capacity.[48,49]

2.5.3. Small-sided games

The 4vs4 SSGs format was implemented on a synthetic grass pitch with dimensions of 25 × 32 meters, providing a relative pitch area of 100 m2 per player (total pitch area of 800 m2 divided by 8 outfield players), which previous research has identified as appropriate for eliciting physiological intensities corresponding to 85% to 90% of maximal heart rate.[50–52] Regulation-sized goalposts measuring 3 meters wide × 2 meters high were positioned at each end of the pitch, with goalkeepers (excluded from analysis) defending each goal to maintain tactical realism and goal-scoring opportunities. Three bout duration conditions were implemented in randomized order using computer-generated random sequence: one continuous bout of 12 minutes (1 × 12 min) without recovery interval, 2 bouts of 6 minutes (2 × 6 min) separated by 2-minute passive recovery, and 3 bouts of 4 minutes (3 × 4 min) with 2-minute passive recovery intervals between each bout. The 2-minute passive recovery duration between intermittent bouts was selected based on previous SSGs research demonstrating that this duration allows partial but incomplete physiological recovery, maintaining the training stimulus while permitting multiple high-intensity bouts.[53,54] Players were organized into 4 teams of 4 outfield players each, with team composition determined by the coaching staff based on technical-tactical skill-level assessments (evaluated using a 5-point Likert scale from 1 = poor to 5 = excellent) to ensure balanced teams and competitive matches.[55] Team assignments were held constant across all 3 bout-duration conditions to control for inter-individual and inter-team variability. Standard soccer rules applied, except that offside was not enforced, and the coach provided continuous verbal encouragement throughout each SSGs condition. Multiple spare balls were positioned around the perimeter of the pitch to minimize delays when balls exited the playing area. Water was provided ad libitum during recovery periods between intermittent bouts.

2.5.4. Profile of mood states

Mood state was assessed using the French-Canadian translation of the abbreviated 37-item POMS questionnaire.[56,57] The abbreviated POMS consists of 37 adjectives rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 4 (significantly) in response to the stem question “How are you feeling right now?” The instrument evaluates 6 mood dimensions: tension-anxiety (6 items, e.g., “tense,” “anxious”), depression-dejection (8 items, e.g., “unhappy,” “sad”), anger-hostility (7 items, e.g., “angry,” “annoyed”), vigor-activity (5 items, e.g., “lively,” “energetic”), fatigue-inertia (5 items, e.g., “worn out,” “fatigued”), and confusion-bewilderment (5 items, e.g., “confused,” “uncertain”). Subscale scores are calculated by summing the item responses within each dimension, with higher scores indicating greater intensity of that particular mood state. A total mood disturbance (TMD) score is derived by summing the 5 negative mood subscales (tension, depression, anger, fatigue, confusion), subtracting the positive vigor subscale, and adding a constant of 100 to eliminate negative values: TMD = (Tension + Depression + Anger + Fatigue + Confusion) - Vigor + 100.[58] The POMS has demonstrated excellent internal consistency reliability (Cronbach α = 0.84–0.95 for individual subscales) and sensitivity to detect mood changes in response to exercise interventions in athletic populations.[59,60] Participants completed the POMS individually under standardized conditions 15 minutes before commencing each SSG’s condition and again immediately after completing the final bout, with an administration time of approximately 3–5 minutes per assessment. Although specific validation in a Tunisian athletic population has not been formally established, the participants were Arabic- and French-speaking and regularly exposed to French-language academic materials, supporting the appropriateness of this version for the present sample.

2.5.5. Heart rate measurement

Heart rate was continuously monitored at 5-second intervals across all SSGs conditions using the Polar Team Sport System (Polar Electro Oy, Kempele, Finland), which consists of chest-strap transmitters with integrated electrodes and a portable receiver unit. Data were downloaded to a computer after each session and processed using Polar Precision Performance Software version 4.0 (Polar Electro Oy, Kempele, Finland) to extract peak heart rate values for each SSGs condition and to calculate the time-averaged mean heart rate across the entire bout duration. The percentage of peak heart rate (%HRpeak) was calculated by dividing the peak heart rate recorded during each SSG condition by the individual peak heart rate obtained during the YYIRT1, multiplied by 100: %HRpeak = (HRgame/ HRpeak_YYIRT1) × 100.[61] The Polar system has been validated against electrocardiography with high accuracy (mean bias < 3 beats/min) for heart rate assessment during intermittent exercise protocols typical of team sports.[62,63]

2.5.6. Blood lactate concentration

Capillary blood samples for lactate analysis were obtained from the fingertip of the index finger 3 minutes after the end of the last bout of each SSGs, as this timing corresponds to the typical peak in post-exercise blood lactate concentration following intermittent high-intensity exercise.[64,65] The sampling site was cleaned with an alcohol swab, allowed to dry, and punctured using a sterile disposable lancet. The first drop of blood was discarded, and a 5-μL sample from the second blood drop was immediately analyzed using a portable lactate analyzer (Lactate Pro, Arkray, Kyoto, Japan). The Lactate Pro device has been validated against laboratory-based enzymatic methods, demonstrating high agreement (r = 0.99) and acceptable measurement error (coefficient of variation 3.0%) for blood lactate concentrations in the range 0.5 to 25.0 mmol/L.[66,67] The device was calibrated in accordance with the manufacturer’s specifications before each testing session.

2.5.7. Rating of perceived exertion

Subjective perception of exertion was assessed immediately following completion of the final bout of each SSGs condition using the category-ratio 10-point (CR–10) Borg scale, which ranges from 0 (nothing at all) to 10 (extremely strong/maximal) with verbal anchors at specific numerical values.[68] Participants were familiarized with the RPE scale during preliminary testing sessions, with specific instructions to provide a rating reflecting their overall whole-body perception of effort during the SSGs condition they had just completed, rather than isolated sensations of leg fatigue or breathlessness. The standardized question “How was your training session? How did you feel during the exercise?” was asked of each participant to elicit their RPE rating.[69] The CR–10 RPE scale has demonstrated validity for quantifying internal training load in soccer players, with strong correlations (r = 0.65–0.85) between session RPE and objective markers of exercise intensity, including heart rate and blood lactate responses.[70,71]

2.5.8. Technical-tactical performance analysis

All SSGs conditions were video-recorded from an elevated position, providing complete visibility of the entire pitch, using high-definition camcorders (Canon HF G40, Canon Inc., Tokyo, Japan) positioned at midfield, approximately 5 meters beyond the touchline and 3 meters above ground level. Video footage was subsequently analyzed by a single trained sports scientist specialized in soccer performance analysis with > 5 years of experience in notational analysis. To establish intra-observer reliability, 20% of randomly selected matches were analyzed twice with a minimum 7-day interval between analyses, yielding intraclass correlation coefficients of 0.92 to 0.96 for all technical-tactical variables analyzed.[72] Four technical-tactical performance parameters were quantified as percentages of total actions performed: successful passes, defined as any pass reaching the intended teammate without interception by an opponent, expressed as percentage of total passes attempted; successful tackles, defined as legal dispossession of an opponent in possession of the ball resulting in regaining possession, expressed as percentage of total tackle attempts; successful duels, defined as one-on-one physical contests for ball possession in which the player won possession, expressed as percentage of total duels contested; and ball losses, defined as loss of possession through opponent interception, unsuccessful pass, or poor ball control, expressed as percentage of total ball possessions. These variables have been validated in previous soccer research as reliable indicators of the quality of technical-tactical performance under varying task constraints.[73–75]

2.6. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 28.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation unless otherwise specified. The assumption of normality for all dependent variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, which confirmed that all variables met the normality assumption (P > .05). Sphericity was evaluated for repeated-measures analyses using Mauchly test, with the Greenhouse-Geisser correction applied when sphericity was violated (ε < 0.75). For mood state variables, a 2-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with 2 within-subjects factors [bout duration (1 × 12 min, 2 × 6 min, 3 × 4 min) × time (pre-exercise, post-exercise)] was conducted to examine main effects and interactions. For technical-tactical performance variables and physiological response variables, a 1-way repeated-measures ANOVA with one within-subjects factor (bout duration: 1 × 12 min, 2 × 6 min, 3 × 4 min) was conducted. When significant main effects or interaction effects were detected, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni correction to control the family-wise type I error rate. Effect sizes for ANOVA were calculated as partial eta-squared (ηp2), with interpretation thresholds of 0.01 < ηp2 < 0.06 indicating small effect, 0.06 ≤ ηp2 ≤ 0.14 indicating medium effect, and ηp2 > 0.14 indicating large effect.[76] For post hoc pairwise comparisons, effect sizes were calculated as Cohen d with Hedges’ correction for small sample bias, reported as unbiased Cohen d (dunb), with interpretation thresholds of dunb < 0.50 indicating small effect, 0.50 ≤ dunb ≤ 0.79 indicating moderate effect, and dunb ≥ 0.80 indicating large effect.[77] The 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated for all pairwise comparisons. Given the conservative nature of the Bonferroni correction and the substantial number of comparisons conducted, the interpretation emphasized effect sizes and confidence intervals alongside statistical significance to avoid dichotomous thinking about P-values and minimize type I error.[78,79] Statistical significance threshold was set at P ≤ .05 for all analyses.

3.2. Technical-tactical performance

Table 3 presents the technical-tactical performance parameters and physiological responses across the 3 bout-duration conditions, including mean ± standard deviation values and superscripts indicating significant differences between conditions. One-way repeated measures ANOVA revealed statistically significant differences between bout duration conditions for percentage of successful passes (F₍2,30₎ = 33.1, P = .001, ηp2 = 0.68), percentage of successful tackles (F₍2,30₎ = 3.75, P = .03, ηp2 = 0.2), percentage of successful duels (F₍2,30₎ = 4.11, P = .02, ηp2 = 0.21), and percentage of ball loss (F₍2,30₎ = 28.42, P < .001, ηp2 = 0.65). Bonferroni-corrected post hoc pairwise comparisons demonstrated that the 12-minute continuous bout condition produced significantly higher percentage of successful passes (60.1 ± 1.2%) compared to the 2 × 6-minute condition (57.2 ± 0.9%, mean difference = 2.91%, 95% CI [1.98–3.85], P < .001, Cohen d = 2.57, large effect) and the 3 × 4 minute condition (56.5 ± 1.9%, mean difference = 3.54%, 95% CI [2.18–4.91], P < .001, Cohen d = 2.14, large effect). Similarly, the percentage of successful tackles was significantly greater during the 12-minute condition (78.5 ± 9.8%) compared to the 2 × 6 minute condition (69.7 ± 10.4 %, mean difference = 8.80%, 95% CI [1.76–15.85], P = .05, Cohen d = 0.83, large effect) and the 3 × 4 minute condition (69.4 ± 10.3 %, mean difference = 9.08%, 95% CI [1.81–16.36], P = .05, Cohen d = 0.86, large effect). The percentage of ball loss was significantly lower during the 12–minute condition (18.1 ± 0.9%) compared to the 2 × 6 minute condition (20.5 ± 1.3 %, mean difference = 2.44%, 95% CI [1.61–3.26], P < .001, Cohen d = 2.14, large effect) and the 3 × 4 minute condition (20.2 ± 1.0 %, mean difference = 2.13%, 95% CI [1.45–2.80], P < .001, Cohen d = 2.11, large effect). No statistically significant differences were detected between the 2 × 6-minute and 3 × 4-minute conditions for any technical-tactical performance variable. The percentage of successful duels showed no significant differences across bout duration conditions, despite a significant main effect, suggesting that, although duels varied across conditions, post hoc comparisons did not reach adjusted significance thresholds after Bonferroni correction.

3.3. Physiological responses

Significant differences between bout duration conditions were observed for all physiological response variables: percentage of peak heart rate (F₍2,30₎ = 14.35, P = .0001, ηp2 = 0.48), peak heart rate achieved during the SSGs (F₍2,30₎ = 14.16, P = .0001, ηp2 = 0.48), blood lactate concentration (F₍2,30₎ = 4.43, P = .02, ηp2 = 0.22), and rating of perceived exertion (F₍2,30₎ = 11.43, P = .0001, ηp2 = 0.43), as shown in Table 3. post hoc pairwise comparisons revealed that the 12-minute continuous bout elicited significantly higher percentage of peak heart rate (89.1 ± 2.7%) compared to the 2 × 6-minute condition (86.7 ± 1.7 %, mean difference = 2.47%, 95% CI [1.23–3.71], P = .001, Cohen d = 1.02, large effect) and the 3 × 4 minute condition (86.4 ± 2.2 %, mean difference = 2.72%, 95% CI [1.22–4.22], P = .002, Cohen d = 1.04, large effect). Similarly, peak heart rate during the SSGs was significantly higher in the 12-minute condition (166.7 ± 4.9 beats/min) than in either intermittent condition (2 × 6 min: 162.1 ± 2.0 beats/min; 3 × 4 min: 161.6 ± 1.8 beats/min). Blood lactate concentration differences reached statistical significance only between the 2 × 6-minute condition (6.4 ± 0.2 mmol/L) and the 3 × 4-minute condition (6.9 ± 0.3 mmol/L, mean difference = 0.54 mmol/L, 95% CI [0.27–0.81], P = .0001), with the 12-minute condition (7.0 ± 1.1 mmol/L) not differing significantly from either intermittent condition following Bonferroni correction. Rating of perceived exertion demonstrated a reversed pattern, with the 3 × 4 minute condition (RPE = 4.2 ± 1.2) eliciting significantly lower perceived exertion compared to the 12-minute condition (RPE = 5.1 ± 0.9, mean difference = 0.88, 95% CI [0.03–1.72], P = .04, Cohen d = 0.81, large effect) and significantly higher perceived exertion compared to the 2 × 6-minute condition (RPE = 3.6 ± 0.6, mean difference = 1.50, 95% CI [0.65–2.35], P = .001, Cohen d = 1.91, large effect).

4. Discussion

The principal findings of this investigation demonstrate that the bout-duration structure in 4 vs 4 small-sided soccer games differentially affects mood-state profiles, technical-tactical performance, and physiological responses, with a continuous 12-minute bout producing the most favorable outcomes across all 3 domains. Specifically, the 12-minute continuous format elicited significant reductions in negative mood states, including depression, confusion, tension, anger, and total mood disturbance, alongside increased physical fatigue sensation, superior technical-tactical execution characterized by higher successful pass and tackle percentages with lower ball loss rates, and appropriate physiological training stimuli reflected by cardiovascular responses approaching 90% of peak heart rate and elevated blood lactate concentrations. These findings provide novel evidence on the integrated psychological, technical, and physiological responses to SSG bout-duration manipulations, with practical implications for evidence-based training prescriptions in soccer.

4.1. Mood state responses to bout duration variations

The 12-minute continuous bout duration elicited significant improvements in mood state characterized by large effect size reductions in depression (Cohen d = 2.0), confusion (Cohen d = 2.12), tension (Cohen d = 2.25), anger (Cohen d = 2.93), and total mood disturbance (Cohen d = 6.75) from pre-exercise to post-exercise assessments, representing clinically meaningful psychological benefits of this training format. These mood improvements occurred despite a large simultaneous increase in fatigue sensation (Cohen d = 2.12), suggesting that participants experienced positive affective responses and reduced psychological distress even while perceiving greater physical tiredness, which may reflect a dissociation between beneficial mood enhancement from exercise and the natural accumulation of fatigue from sustained physical exertion.[80,81] Previous research examining mood responses to soccer training has yielded mixed findings, with some studies reporting mood improvements following SSG sessions.[81,82] In contrast, others have documented mood disturbances, particularly when SSGs formats involve excessive intensity or inadequate recovery.[83] The current results are partly consistent with the research by Selmi et al, which found that 4 × 4-min 4vs4 small-sided games with 3-min passive recovery yielded more stable mood profiles than high-intensity interval training (15-second sprints at 110% MAS with 15-second recovery breaks). Nonetheless, variations in bout structure and task design between their protocol and the current study must be taken into account when interpreting the consistency of results. The more positive emotional reactions can be linked to the inherently motivating, game-like quality of SSGs, which includes social interaction and strategic involvement.[84] The mechanism underlying mood improvements with continuous 12-minute bouts may relate to the sustained but moderate cardiovascular intensity (89% peak heart rate) that remains below the ventilatory threshold, typically associated with deterioration in affective response,[85,86] combined with the psychological satisfaction derived from prolonged successful technical-tactical engagement without interruption. Research examining exercise intensity-affect relationships has established that continuous moderate-intensity exercise produces favorable affective responses, while high-intensity interval exercise with work-rest oscillations may induce more negative affect during work intervals,[87,88] potentially explaining why the intermittent 2 × 6-minute and 3 × 4-minute conditions in the present study failed to produce comparable mood benefits and even demonstrated some mood deterioration in the 2 × 6-minute format. This suggests that, for mood-state optimization in soccer training, coaches should prioritize continuous, longer-duration SSG bouts that maintain cardiovascular intensity in the moderate-to-vigorous range, without extreme peak intensities or frequent work-rest transitions that may psychologically fragment the training experience and reduce enjoyment.

4.2. Technical-tactical performance quality across bout durations

The continuous 12-minute bout condition produced significantly superior technical-tactical performance compared to both intermittent conditions, evidenced by 2.9% to 3.5% higher successful pass percentages, 8.8% to 9.1% higher successful tackle percentages, and 2.1% to 2.4% lower ball loss rates, all with large effect sizes (Cohen d = 0.83–2.57). These performance advantages represent practically meaningful differences in technical execution quality, as passing accuracy differentials of 2% to 4% at elite competition levels can substantially influence match outcomes and team performance.[89,90] The mechanisms explaining enhanced technical performance during continuous, more prolonged bouts likely involve multiple interacting factors, including improved cognitive focus without the psychological disruption of frequent recovery intervals, the progressive development of game rhythm and tactical coordination that require sustained interaction time to establish, and, paradoxically, potentially. This accumulated moderate fatigue may enhance neuromuscular efficiency through improved motor unit recruitment patterns and movement economy,[91,92] Research by Gréhaigne has demonstrated that 4 vs 4 SSG formats provide players with substantially more ball contacts (5 times more than regulation 11 vs 11 matches and 50% more than 6 vs 6 formats) and more frequent dueling opportunities (3 times more than 11 vs 11 and 2 times more than 6 vs 6), creating optimal conditions for technical skill execution practice under realistic pressure.[93] The present findings suggest that continuous, sustained exposure in this technically demanding format enhances skill consolidation and tactical problem-solving compared with intermittent, shorter exposures interrupted by recovery periods, which may disrupt the progressive tactical organization and technical rhythm that players establish during gameplay. Investigations comparing continuous versus intermittent training structures in soccer have reported mixed findings. Some studies suggest that intermittent formats support higher-intensity maintenance.[94] In contrast, others demonstrate that continuous formats better replicate match demands and promote the execution of technical skills under accumulated fatigue representative of late-match scenarios.[95] The reductions in ball-loss percentage observed during 12-minute continuous play, compared with intermittent formats, may reflect improved decision-making quality and reduced technical errors, attributable to sustained attentional focus and the development of tactical understanding that require time to establish among teammates. These findings suggest that coaches’ SSG-based training programs, with technical-tactical development objectives, should prioritize longer, continuous bouts to maximize the number of high-quality skill repetitions and tactical problem-solving opportunities in realistic game contexts.

4.3. Physiological responses and training load implications

The 12-minute continuous bout elicited significantly higher cardiovascular demands as evidenced by peak heart rates reaching 89.1% of maximum compared to 86.4% to 86.7% in the intermittent conditions (Cohen d = 1.02–1.04, large effects), indicating that despite the absence of recovery intervals, continuous play sustained higher cardiovascular intensity throughout the entire bout duration. Heart rate responses approaching 90% of maximal are consistent with training intensities recommended for developing aerobic capacity in team-sport athletes and replicate the cardiovascular demands typically experienced during competitive soccer matches.[96–98] Blood lactate concentrations following the 12-minute bout (7.0 ± 1.1 mmol/L) were elevated into a range indicative of a substantial contribution from the glycolytic energy system. However, the lack of significant differences from intermittent conditions (6.4–6.9 mmol/L) following conservative Bonferroni correction suggests that all 3 bout structures activated anaerobic metabolic pathways to similar degrees.[99,100] Research examining lactate responses to SSGs has reported wide variability (3–12 mmol/L) across pitch dimensions, player numbers, bout duration, and work-rest ratios, with values in the 6–8 mmol/L range typical of 4 vs 4 formats on 100 m2 per-player pitch areas.[101–103] The rating of perceived exertion pattern demonstrated an interesting finding: the 3 × 4-minute condition was perceived as most demanding (RPE 4.2) despite lower objective cardiovascular intensity, while the 2 × 6-minute condition was perceived as least demanding (RPE 3.6), and the 12-minute continuous condition was intermediate (RPE 5.1). This dissociation between objective physiological intensity and subjective exertion perception may reflect the psychological impact of bout structure, with frequent recovery intervals in the 3 × 4-minute format potentially creating perception of greater intermittent strain despite lower sustained cardiovascular load, while the 2 × 6- minute format provided sufficient work duration to establish rhythm but with recovery intervals that reduced perceived effort.[104,105] Foster et al have established that session RPE, calculated as the product of exercise duration and RPE rating, provides a valid quantification of training load in team sports,[68,106] and the present findings suggest that continuous, more prolonged bouts may offer an optimal balance of objective physiological stimulus with appropriate perceived effort for athlete tolerance and training adherence. From a training periodization perspective, these physiological findings indicate that 12-minute continuous 4 vs 4 SSGs provide appropriate training loads for aerobic capacity maintenance and development during in-season training phases, with cardiovascular intensities sufficient to stimulate adaptation without excessive accumulated fatigue or recovery demands that might interfere with technical-tactical development or competitive match performance.[107]

4.4. Integrated implications for training design

The integrated findings across the domains of mood state, technical-tactical performance, and physiological response provide convergent evidence that continuous 12-minute bouts optimize multiple training outcomes simultaneously in 4 vs 4 SSGs formats. The combination of favorable mood responses, superior technical execution, and appropriate physiological training stimuli suggests that this bout structure achieves an optimal balance among psychological engagement, skill-development opportunities, and physical conditioning demands. Coaches designing SSG-based training sessions must navigate inherent trade-offs between various training objectives, and the present findings indicate that prioritizing longer continuous bouts may serve multiple goals concurrently rather than requiring separate training sessions for psychological, technical, and physical development.[108–110] The mechanisms underlying these favorable outcomes likely involve sustained tactical engagement and progressive problem-solving afforded by uninterrupted play, which maintain cognitive engagement and intrinsic motivation while providing repetitive technical-skill practice under conditions of progressive fatigue representative of competitive-match scenarios. Research examining training enjoyment and motivation in team sports has consistently demonstrated that game-based training formats produce higher enjoyment ratings compared to drill-based or conditioning-focused training,[111,112] and the present mood state findings extend this literature by demonstrating that the temporal structure within game-based training, specifically continuous versus intermittent bout durations, further influences psychological responses. The practical application of these findings suggests that coaches should structure SSG sessions with emphasis on longer continuous bout durations when training objectives include mood state optimization, technical skill consolidation under realistic pressure and fatigue, and maintenance of appropriate cardiovascular training loads, while intermittent shorter bouts with recovery intervals might be reserved for specific situations requiring higher peak power outputs or when managing player fatigue levels in congested competition schedules.

4.5. Limitations

Several methodological limitations warrant acknowledgment when interpreting these findings. First, the participant sample consisted exclusively of male regional-level soccer players aged 18 to 23 years with specific training backgrounds and competitive experiences, which limits generalizability to female players, younger age groups, elite professional players, or recreational participants who may demonstrate different psychological, technical, or physiological responses to SSG bout duration manipulations. Second, the study examined only 3 specific bout-duration structures (1 × 12 min, 2 × 6 min, 3 × 4 min) with fixed 2-minute passive recovery intervals between intermittent bouts; alternative bout-duration combinations, varied recovery durations, or active recovery protocols might yield different outcomes that remain unexplored. Third, technical-tactical performance analysis relied on video-based notational analysis by a single trained observer. Despite acceptable intra-observer reliability (ICC 0.92–0.96), the absence of inter-observer reliability assessment and the inherent subjective elements in action classification (e.g., determining tackle success) introduce potential measurement error. Fourth, the study design did not include standardized pre-exercise nutritional provision immediately before experimental sessions beyond the 3-hour pre-session standardized breakfast protocol; variations in recent nutritional intake could have influenced physiological responses and subjective ratings despite attempts to standardize dietary intake. Fifth, mood state assessments were conducted only immediately before and after exercise, with no extended follow-up to determine the duration of post-exercise mood changes or potential delayed psychological effects, thereby limiting understanding of the temporal dynamics of mood responses. Sixth, environmental conditions, including temperature, humidity, and surface conditions, were not precisely measured or experimentally controlled, despite all sessions being conducted on the same pitch at consistent times of day; subtle environmental variations could have influenced physiological responses and perceived exertion. Seventh, the study did not include objective measures of player movement patterns such as GPS-derived total distance, high-intensity running distance, or number of accelerations/decelerations, which would have provided additional context for interpreting the technical-tactical and physiological findings in relation to external load characteristics. Eighth, the relatively small sample size (n = 18) provides adequate statistical power to detect the observed large effect sizes. Still, it may be insufficient to identify smaller effects or to conduct more sophisticated analyses, such as examining individual response heterogeneity or subgroup analyses by player characteristics. These limitations should be addressed in future research that examines SSGs bout-duration effects across diverse populations, investigates additional variations in bout structure, employs multiple objective performance measures, and examines longer-term psychological and performance adaptations to training programs emphasizing different bout-duration structures.

5. Conclusions

This investigation provides comprehensive evidence that the bout-duration structure in 4 vs 4 small-sided soccer games is a critical programming variable that influences integrated psychological, technical-tactical, and physiological training outcomes. The continuous 12-minute bout format demonstrated the most favorable response profile across all examined domains, producing significant improvements in mood state balance characterized by large-magnitude reductions in depression, confusion, tension, anger, and total mood disturbance, superior technical-tactical execution quality evidenced by 2.9 to 3.5 percentage point improvements in passing accuracy, 8.8 to 9.1 percentage point improvements in tackle success rates, and 2.1 to 2.4 percentage point reductions in ball loss frequencies, and physiologically appropriate training stimuli reflected by cardiovascular intensities reaching 89% of peak heart rate with elevated blood lactate concentrations indicative of substantial aerobic-glycolytic energy system activation. These findings challenge the assumption that intermittent-bout structures with recovery intervals universally optimize training quality, instead demonstrating that continuous, longer-duration bouts may better balance the competing demands of psychological engagement, technical skill execution quality, and physiological training load for this specific SSG format and player population.

The practical implications for soccer coaches and training staff are substantial and multifaceted. When designing SSG-based training sessions with primary objectives of maintaining positive mood states and psychological well-being during intensive training periods, enhancing technical skill execution quality under realistic match-like pressure and progressive fatigue accumulation, developing tactical problem-solving capacity through sustained coordinated play opportunities, and providing physiologically appropriate aerobic capacity maintenance or development stimuli, the evidence supports prioritizing continuous bout durations in the 10–15 minute range rather than fragmenting equivalent total training volumes into multiple shorter intermittent bouts. This training prescription recommendation is particularly relevant during congested competitive periods when efficient multifaceted training sessions are required, during pre-season preparation phases when simultaneous technical and physical capacity development is prioritized, and when working with players who demonstrate signs of training-related mood disturbances that might benefit from the psychological advantages of continuous game-based training formats. Conversely, intermittent, shorter-bout structures may retain utility for specific training situations, including sessions targeting very high-intensity repeated-sprint ability with incomplete recovery, managing individual player return-from-injury training-load progressions in which continuous exposure requires gradual progression, and situations in which tactical instruction intervals between bouts serve specific pedagogical objectives.

From a public health and sports medicine perspective, the mood state findings are particularly significant given the established associations between training-induced mood disturbances, the development of overtraining syndrome, elevated injury risk, and athlete burnout and dropout.[26,113,114] The demonstration that SSG bout duration structures can be manipulated to optimize mood responses while maintaining training efficacy provides coaches with an evidence-based tool for simultaneously pursuing performance development and athlete mental health objectives, a consideration increasingly recognized as essential in contemporary athlete care models.[115,116] The superior technical-tactical performance quality observed during continuous longer bouts suggests that this bout structure may accelerate skill acquisition and tactical development through enhanced quality repetitions and problem-solving opportunities, potentially reducing the total training volume required to achieve technical competency milestones, which has implications for long-term athlete development models emphasizing training efficiency and injury prevention through appropriate load management.[117,118]

Future research should extend these findings by examining whether chronic training programs that emphasize continuous versus intermittent SSG bout structures produce differential long-term adaptations in aerobic capacity, technical skill proficiency, psychological resilience, and competitive performance. An investigation of optimal bout duration ranges across different SSG formats (3 vs 3, 5 vs 5, etc), player populations (elite versus recreational, male vs female, various age groups), and training phases (pre-season, in-season, off-season) would provide comprehensive evidence for a contextualized training prescription. Additional research examining the dose-response relationships between SSG bout duration and mood-state changes, the temporal dynamics of post-exercise mood recovery, and individual-difference factors predicting psychological responsiveness to variations in bout duration would enhance personalized training design capabilities.[119] Furthermore, integrating advanced movement analysis technologies, including GPS and accelerometry data, with psychological and technical performance assessments would provide a deeper mechanistic understanding of how bout duration influences integrated training responses by affecting movement patterns, physical load distribution, and their consequent impacts on technical execution capacity and psychological experiences. Investigating active versus passive recovery protocols between intermittent bouts, the effects of varying recovery durations, and the interaction between bout duration and other SSG variables (pitch dimensions, player numbers, tactical constraints) would provide comprehensive evidence for optimized SSG training design across the full spectrum of programming variables. Ultimately, the goal of this research trajectory should be to develop evidence-based training prescription algorithms that integrate psychological well-being, technical-tactical development, and physiological adaptation objectives to optimize athlete development while minimizing the risk of injury and psychological distress associated with inappropriate training design.
