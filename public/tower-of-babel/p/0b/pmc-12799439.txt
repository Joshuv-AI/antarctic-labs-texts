From rare procedure to institutional capability: a proposal to embed systematic training and adoption of emergency front of neck access
Authors: Caoimhe C. Duffy, Gary A. Bass, Lewis J. Kaplan, Christopher P. Bonafide, Ellen P. O'Sullivan, Paul A. Baker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryEmergency front-of-neck access (eFONA) usage and performance vary across clinical settings despite its essential role in airway rescue. Therefore, delay or suboptimal execution occurs when eFONA is required for life-saving rescue. This observation may stem from fragmented instruction, lack of systems-level reinforcement, limited opportunities for practice, and rapid skill decay. In contrast, cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) training is embedded through structured education, credentialing, and institutional support to reduce performance variability and improve cardiac arrest outcomes. Such an approach is also supported by national professional organisations and does not solely rely on local measures. The success of the national CPR approach provides one of several complementary approaches to enhance eFONA training and performance. Reframing eFONA as a high-acuity, low-frequency intervention requiring systematic and interdisciplinary training is one pathway to improve clinician preparedness and performance. Implementation science frameworks help identify multilevel barriers and guide strategy selection for adoption and sustainment of eFONA and its component best practices. Task training, simulation-based scenario management, and virtual or augmented reality iterative assessments could dovetail with regular training coupled with expert or machine learning augmented intelligence (AI) evaluations. We propose a structured model for embedding eFONA training into clinical governance processes, credentialing pathways, and interdisciplinary education. We advocate for cross-specialty guideline convergence, standardised simulation platforms, development of reporting databases, and exploration of emerging technological adjuncts. Embedding eFONA as a core institutional capability rather than an isolated procedural skill offers a comprehensive path to improving patient safety and outcome during airway emergencies.

Emergency front-of-neck access (eFONA) represents the final, life-saving step in airway rescue when all other methods to oxygenate or ventilate a patient have failed. Although eFONA is a rare undertaking occurring in approximately one in 10 000–50 000 patients in the operating room and <1% of emergency department or prehospital encounters, delayed or improper execution carries a disproportionately high risk of complications including death.1,2 Despite the dissemination of evidence-informed expert consensus guidelines such as that from the Difficult Airway Society (DAS), significant variability persists in how eFONA is taught, practised, credentialed, and evaluated across specialties and clinical environments.1,3, 4, 5, 6

Despite technical and conceptual refinements over time, resistance to eFONA utilisation has historically stemmed from fear of complications, lack of exposure, and cognitive hesitancy.7 Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) education enjoys widespread simulation-based training, universal certification, implementation at scale, and regular reassessment built into clinical workflows. In contrast, eFONA training is often episodic, inconsistently addressed within and across parent specialty training programmes, and rarely practised with sufficient frequency to ensure competency at the time of crisis.

Accordingly, we propose an evidence-aligned approach to enhance eFONA training and performance: eFONA should be reframed and operationalised in a fashion similar to that of CPR, as a high-acuity, low-frequency skill requiring structured, routine training, with the expectation of universal proficiency across clinicians who may need to perform this life-saving intervention. Recognising that such an approach requires foundational change, we advocate for the integration of implementation science frameworks to guide the design of effective eFONA education and its sustainable dissemination across institutions. By locating eFONA training within the broader context of behavioural priming, simulation fidelity, systems accountability, and cross-specialty standardisation, such an approach can improve preparedness, reduce performance hesitation, and ultimately enhance patient safety during airway emergencies.

Current emergency front-of-neck access landscape: a procedure without standard nomenclature or a training pathway

The eFONA technique selection is highly variable across clinical settings.7 The umbrella term ‘eFONA’ remains generic and may obscure critical technique-specific nuances, risking confusion in high-stakes scenarios, particularly for novice rescuers. To that end, our recent historical review classified eFONA methods into five discrete categories (needle cricothyrotomy, scalpel-only cricothyrotomy, scalpel-bougie-tube cricothyrotomy, open surgical tracheostomy, and hybrid techniques) to enhance terminological precision and guide standardisation across training pathways.7 The scalpel-bougie-tube technique has emerged as a consistently effective approach for eFONA performance and is therefore endorsed by multiple airway societies and supported by simulation and clinical studies.8, 9, 10, 11, 12 Zhou and colleagues13 demonstrated that simulation-based training in bougie-assisted cricothyrotomy significantly improved procedural performance among board-certified anaesthesiologists, reinforcing the technique’s effectiveness in structured educational settings. Nevertheless, real-world adoption of the scalpel-bougie-tube technique remains inconsistent, likely reflecting its low frequency of clinical performance and reliance on episodic training. The DAS explicitly advocates for the scalpel-bougie-tube technique as the preferred method in their educational resources.14 This discrepancy between training endorsement and operational deployment highlights the ongoing need for a standardised approach that is systematic and informs interdisciplinary training and credentialing pathways.

Across disciplines, eFONA training and technique reflect philosophies shaped by environment, resource availability, and specialty-specific priorities. In military medicine, for example, a specialised tool like the CricKey is preferred based on simplicity and speed in austere settings.15 Recommended prehospital surgical approaches are often undermined by limited training, procedural discomfort, and minimal exposure to situations requiring eFONA performance.16 Within hospital settings, particularly in operating rooms and ICUs, the expectation is that the most senior available clinician will perform eFONA.17 Although this potentially helps ensure technical proficiency, the most senior clinician may be only poorly skilled at eFONA. Such a finding may occur in teaching and community facilities, and is highly likely to occur in critical access ones. Therefore, hesitancy to perform an uncommon and anxiety-provoking procedure, perhaps especially in children, impedes timely rescue even if eFONA is successfully performed.

Further complicating the landscape is both a lack of consensus on technique nomenclature and persistence of the generic label ‘eFONA,’ which fails to convey intended procedural steps and may delay decisive action, especially by less experienced team members. Further complicating the landscape of eFONA approach is a lack of approach or terminology consensus across major professional organisations. Although DAS and ASA support open surgical approaches and embrace similar terminology, others demonstrate variability that precludes standardisation. The Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma (EAST) and the National Association of EMS Physicians (NAEMSP) each use different terminology to putatively describe the same element but also vary with regard to technique preference.5,18 This inconsistency affects not only how clinicians are trained but also how airway emergencies are documented, evaluated, and reviewed post hoc. A fragmented guideline, best practice, or recommendation ecosystem contributes to uncertainty when comparing data across studies, challenges critical review of procedure performance within a system setting, and inhibits cohesive systems planning. Taken together, these elements set the foundation for a call for standardisation across educational, training, credentialing, and inquiry platforms.

Compounding these issues is the absence of standardised and universal embraced training approaches or credentialing requirements for eFONA. In contrast, CPR training is reinforced through Basic (BLS) and Advanced Life Support certification, with regular reassessments built into clinical workflows regardless of setting. Moreover, the specifics of CPR training are articulated by a multiprofessional medical professional organisation and accepted by private, federal, and international facilities as the standard to which all clinicians must hew with rare exception. In contrast, eFONA training is often episodic, specialty-driven, and tied to isolated events such as workshops or courses only some of which utilise simulation (most, e.g. Advanced Trauma Life Support or ATLS, use static task trainers). Unsurprisingly, when studied in an anaesthesiologist, skill decay is rapid with proficiency degradation occurring as early as 3 months post-training.19 There is no parallel study of surgeons in any discipline, and it is uncertain how different clinical practices influence skill maintenance outside of those who routinely engage in airway surgery where usual practice likely reinforces eFONA-relevant skills.

eFONA remains a procedure with several unique training pathways that are specialty dependent. Worse, some specialties are bereft of consistent education and practice and may be guided by divergent policies that are tied to specific institutions or settings. These gaps may substantially compromise patient safety as eFONA remains the airway rescue technique of last resort. An approach that is anticipated to successfully address such gaps is the articulation of a cohesive, cross-specialty consensus framework designed to standardise training, normalise preparedness, and support procedural and cognitive competency to abrogate rescue hesitancy for patients crippled by the ‘cannot intubate, cannot oxygenate’ (CICO) scenario.

Skill deficits are highlighted by closed claims data that demonstrate eFONA is often performed after an excessive delay and leads to a maladaptive outcome.20 These data further underscore the notion that eFONA should be added to the list of skills that benefit from regularly scheduled maintenance of certification (MOC) programmes.19 The implementation of hospital- or healthcare system-based eFONA training could be reasonably promoted by specifically identified clinicians (Airway Leads) to enhance patient safety and function within a national professional organisation space (national Airway Lead Network).21 Although no such approach currently exists for eFONA, other procedures that cross specialties benefit from standardisation. Such an approach is currently utilised within the United States Veterans Health Administration system for full-time employees who must regularly (every 3 months) maintain certification for the procedural aspects of CPR using a task trainer to assess the depth and cadence of chest compressions.22

Lessons from cardiopulmonary resuscitation: a blueprint for emergency front-of-neck access

CPR’s evolution from feared intervention to universal reflex response offers a replicable template. Similar to eFONA, CPR is a high-acuity, low-frequency intervention that was once met with hesitation, fear of doing harm, and a lack of coordinated training. Over decades, CPR has been transformed through deliberate investment in standardised and widely disseminated education, simulation-based training, psychological desensitisation, and mandated structured credentialing. Indeed, organisations such as The Joint Commission in the USA require CPR training and certification with acute care facilities.

Relatedly, clinicians are required to undergo BLS and, depending on scope of practice, Advanced Cardiac Life Support (ACLS) training at regular intervals. These courses incorporate low-dose, high-frequency simulation, team-based drills, and cognitive frameworks to ensure that clinicians can decisively act during a crisis and deploy life-saving skills as a matter of course. CPR training succeeds not through frequency of use but through consistent reinforcement and institutional support. For example, the North Carolina Regional Approach to Cardiovascular Emergencies (RACE-CARS) initiative in 2010 exemplifies how coordinated systems interventions across public training, emergency medical service readiness, and hospital protocolisation can improve care delivery and patient outcomes. RACE-CARS deployed a multi-pronged implementation science-informed strategy including public education, bystander chest compression–only CPR, team-based first responder training, and structured in-hospital response that collectively drove meaningful improvements in cardiac arrest outcomes.23 The trajectory of CPR adoption suggests that even rare, high-acuity interventions can become embedded in the clinical repertoire when supported by carefully structured system-wide programmes.8

eFONA preparedness requires comparable normalisation. Clinicians must be ‘primed’ for action, a paradigm that requires accessible equipment, rehearsed decision-making to cement procedure initiation, and the elimination of psychological barriers through repeated exposure in simulated environments.5 Both CPR and eFONA are time-critical interventions once associated with undertraining and stigma.7,24,25 Each demands rehearsed psychomotor and cognitive integration. Routine simulation, interdisciplinary drills, and structured recredentialing would optimally underpin transiting eFONA to a successful and consistently practised intervention such as CPR. Credentialing pathways should link simulation performance to clinical privileges, ensuring institutional and individual clinician accountability. eFONA proficiency should be explicitly embedded in clinical governance processes including, but not limited to, annual competency reviews, hospital privileging requirements, and continuous professional development portfolios. By reframing eFONA as a low-frequency, high-acuity procedure much like CPR that requires routine, interdisciplinary, and structured training, we can prepare clinicians not just to recognise the need for eFONA, but to execute it confidently and efficiently when the need arises. This shift will require not only educational reform, but also systems-level support, simulation-based credentialing, and implementation science to bridge the gap between evidence and real-world readiness.

Training and simulation gaps

Unlike other procedural skills, eFONA cannot be taught in the operating room as a planned procedure. Relatedly, didactic teaching unaccompanied by technical skills acquisition appears inadequate to support safety, and reinforcement of paired cognitive and technical skills is essential for durability.26 Although high-fidelity cadaveric and animal model training currently offer superior anatomic authenticity and psychomotor fidelity, they are limited by specimen availability, ethical and financial constraints, and the need for scheduled lab attendance. Thus, simulation approaches that rely on manikins for skill development serve as the mainstay for procedural task training.27,28 As anaesthesiologist eFONA proficiency appears to decay within 3 months, retraining must occur more frequently than the conventional annual cycle used for other procedural competencies.19 Standardised simulation and objective performance metrics should underpin recredentialing efforts.

Developing a uniform approach to eFONA simulation-based education and training requires collaboration across multiple venues including, but not limited to, academic institutions, medical professional organisations, and simulation platform developers. These collaborations may address deficiencies in current task trainers such as static anatomy and a fixed level of difficulty that principally focuses on the sequential procedural aspects of eFONA and fails to address cognitive or leadership aspects. A high-fidelity simulator should also incorporate emotional, psychological, cognitive, and physical stressors that typify clinical scenarios where eFONA provides airway rescue. The impact of working with unfamiliar individuals and in an environment that is not resource replete may also support eFONA training that is useful for the operator and the crisis leader.

A dynamic training environment such as the one outlined above may be realised using immersive virtual reality (VR) coupled with an AI-driven personalised learning environment. VR simulation enables repeatable, immersive practice with lower per-user resource requirements and flexible, on-demand access. Scenarios may adapt to learner experience and performance along the course of the scenario. An immersive environment may also incorporate elements of human factors analysis such as interdisciplinary interaction or environmental noise into the scenario environment and yield outcome metrics that may guide scenario revision, training approaches, and metrics.

Applying implementation science

Regular practice and psychological desensitisation are necessary to improve the performance of life-saving procedures in a time-sensitive context. To embed eFONA readiness in clinical systems, we must move beyond educational design and instead apply structured implementation strategies. The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR 2.0) offers a partitioned lens to identify contextual determinants such as leadership engagement, resource availability, institutional culture, and regulatory norms that influence the adoption and durability of new practices.29 Mapping known barriers to these domains enables targeted implementation strategies that address both behavioural and structural contributors to failure. One widely used approach is termed implementation mapping, a systematic method for translating identified barriers into evidence-informed, context-specific strategies.27 For instance, minimal engagement owing to perceived irrelevance may reflect a poor match between innovation values and the prevailing institutional culture. Through implementation mapping, this insight would guide the selection or adaptation of strategies such as tailored messaging, stakeholder engagement, or value-affirmation interventions, to enhance cultural alignment and increased perceived value among users.28

Recognising the organisational context into which eFONA training must be embedded, the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) framework offers a taxonomy of implementation strategies to translate evidence into practice.30 Relevant strategies include audit and feedback, educational outreach, use of opinion leaders, and integration into clinical workflows (Fig. 1). For eFONA training, these approaches may involve structured debriefs after simulated or real events, expansion of formal airway management committees, or aligning simulation with credentialing requirements. Successful implementation depends on local adaptation, regular re-evaluation, and time-driven reinforcement. Institutions should prioritise stakeholder engagement as a key initial step that is followed by qualitative inquiry and co-design approaches to adapt national recommendations to the local context. This dual-track model of top-down guidance with bottom-up adaptation fosters sustainability and local ownership. Databases and medical professional organisations

Many airway databases such as the Danish Anaesthesia Database (DAD),31 and the Department of Defence Trauma Registry exclusively focus on acute outcomes in a binary fashion (i.e. live vs dead), and therefore present only a narrow view of the emergency airway management landscape. Such a limited focus impedes devising appropriate tools to address the process that led to both successful and maladaptive outcomes for limited frequency emergency events such as eFONA. eFONA occurs so infrequently that it was not a prospective RCT to evaluate outcomes rendering other data sources essential to advance education and practice.4 Sources may include case reports, systematic reviews, and closed claims analyses, all of which represent potentially biased samples of patients, events, and outcomes. Additional insights may be drawn from crowdsourcing initiatives such as WebAIRS, an Australian and New Zealand voluntary anonymous web-based anaesthesia incident reporting system, and ‘The Airway App’, an international application for submitting detailed case information.22,23 Such systems are hampered by limited awareness, voluntary participation, and the absence of a denominator. Instead, a national database that requires reporting and can capture long-term outcomes is anticipated to identify relevant elements to inform practice. The DAS Airway Leads Committee is currently developing a UK-based eFONA database to meet this need. Another approach is database harmonisation that would preserve existing databases, but craft a dual-output path (native database and new database) that relies on a reconciled data dictionary that uses globally embraceable terminology.

Recognising that eFONA can be performed via multiple approaches and anatomical sites, crafting a recommendation for a single method to use, teach, and assess remains elusive.20,21 The Vortex approach favoured by the Australian and New Zealand College of Anaesthetists does not advocate for a particular method. Similarly, the ASA,8 the EAST, and the NAEMSP16 are hesitant to favour one method. Increasing numbers of organisations including DAS, the Canadian Airway Focus Group (CAFG), Spanish Society of Anaesthesiology, and neonatal and infant guidelines offer a defined approach for eFONA using a scalpel-bougie-tube technique.9,32, 33, 34 Medical professional organisation could ideally lead the process of standardising eFONA terminology, reporting criteria, and the development of standardised credentialing pathways.

Initiatives such as the Project for Universal Management of Airways (PUMA) involve a multidisciplinary working group primarily comprising emergency medicine and anaesthesiology specialists. PUMA’s overarching goal is to establish global consensus guidelines addressing airway management using standardised terminology.35 Nevertheless, the PUMA working group notably lacks representation from military medicine clinician and trauma surgical specialties, a key absence in that these two groups collectively perform a significant number of eFONA procedures.36,37 This highlights the difficulties of devising a unified lexicon across diverse medical specialties. Ensuring equitable representation in the development of such lexicons is crucial to avoid dominance by a single specialty, and will thereby enhance global guideline acceptance, application, and reinforcement.

Emerging approaches and future directions

There are approaches that may aid those with uncertain or atrophied skills when eFONA needs to be performed. Although inferior to routine skill maintenance, these approaches may mitigate training gaps in emergencies. One approach known as tele-mentoring links the proceduralist with a skilled operator in real time using a digital platform and a video link. The remote expert can view what the proceduralist sees and two-way audio communication is similarly enabled; this approach has been successfully used for one eFONA procedure.38,39 The SARS-CoV-2 pandemic launched an explosion of such approaches which have been used to provide remote humanitarian aid in Ukraine.40 Another developing approach is autonomous augmented reality/VR that will provide prompts for a proceduralist using the patient as the backdrop with each subsequent step overlaid within a viewing screen.41 These platforms include smartphones, tablets, portable workstations on wheels, or an ocular port akin to what is used in some military helicopter applications and helmet-based Heads-Up-Displays (HUDS). Conclusions

eFONA must transition from a rarely practised skill to a routinely rehearsed capability for failure-resilient airway management. It should be deliberately taught and rigorous evaluated along a vertical curriculum that begins in medical school and extends through a clinician’s professional life. As with CPR, effective eFONA performance requires cognitive priming, time-sensitive coordination, and institutional support systems to facilitate action at the point of need. Embedding eFONA preparedness is essential to avoid preventable deaths, mitigate legal exposure, and strengthen institutional airway safety. Implementation science frameworks, and particularly the use of implementation mapping, offer a practical structure to guide the transition from awareness to adoption and sustainment.

Training bodies, simulation consortia, and medical professional societies must work together to unify training, reporting, and credentialing processes. Institutional leaders should prioritise eFONA preparedness as a core component of airway safety. This transformation will require new tools, coupled with a durable commitment to systems integration and interdisciplinary collaboration.
