Navigating water stability and crop resilience in a changing climate: insights from SPEI, CWSI, and CMIP6 projections
Authors: Hammad Zulfiqar, Mahfuzur Rahman, Hyeon-Ki Lee, Hyeong-Joo Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Monsoon-dominated agricultural regions face increasing hydro-climatic stress driven by climate change, yet a critical methodological gap persists: no integrated, spatially explicit framework simultaneously links drought dynamics, crop water stress, and system-level stability under future climate projections. South Korea exemplifies this challenge, where seasonal water deficits, rising evaporative demand, and dependence on monsoon rainfall create compounding vulnerabilities for agricultural resilience. Here we develop a multi-index framework combining the Standardized Precipitation Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI), a modified Crop Water Stress Index (CWSI), and a Reliability–Resilience–Vulnerability (RRV) stability metric to quantify spatio-temporal patterns of water stress and resilience. Observations (1985–2014) were used to evaluate and bias-correct multiple CMIP6 models under SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5. Continuous climate fields were interpolated, and Crop Water Zones (CWZs) were derived using a percentile-based 3 × 3 classification that combines interpolated RRV and CWSI. Results show increased precipitation variability and higher evaporative demand, intensifying seasonal water stress, particularly in summer and autumn. Differences between scenarios are modest in the near term, but both indicate expansion of high-risk CWZs. Bias correction improves agreement with observations; however, performance metrics are interpreted cautiously given methodological constraints. Spatial patterns indicate a shift from stable/resilient to more critical/high-risk conditions, revealing emerging vulnerabilities in agricultural systems. This framework provides a transferable approach for assessing climate-driven water stress and supports adaptive water management in monsoon-influenced regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55297-z.

Introduction

Anthropogenic climate change is systematically altering the hydro-climatic regimes that underpin agricultural water security, driving increases in the frequency, duration, and severity of droughts while simultaneously amplifying evaporative demand through rising temperatures1–3. In monsoon-dominated regions such as South Korea, these pressures are especially acute: approximately 60–70% of annual precipitation falls within a three-month monsoon window (June–September), creating a structurally unstable water supply that is highly sensitive to shifts in both precipitation timing and temperature-driven evapotranspiration4,5. A large proportion of annual rainfall occurs within a short monsoon period, while pre- and post-monsoon seasons often experience water deficits6. This temporal imbalance, combined with rising temperatures and increasing evaporative demand, creates substantial challenges for maintaining stable water supply and sustaining agricultural productivity7–9. Consequently, understanding how climate variability translates into agricultural water stress has become a key research priority10.

Previous studies have extensively examined drought variability using indices such as the Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI) and the Standardized Precipitation Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI), which incorporate both precipitation and temperature-driven evapotranspiration processes11–13. In recent years, these approaches have been combined with climate projections from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) to assess future drought conditions under different emission scenarios4,14,15. While these studies provide valuable insights into hydro-climatic trends, they primarily focus on meteorological drought indicators and do not directly capture how these changes translate into crop water stress or agricultural vulnerability16. As a result, there remains a disconnect between climate-driven drought assessments and practical agricultural water management17.

In parallel, system-level analyses have been conducted using the Reliability–Resilience–Vulnerability (RRV) framework, which evaluates the ability of a system to maintain performance, recover from disturbances, and minimize adverse impacts11,18. Although RRV has been widely applied in hydrology and water resource management5, its application in agricultural contexts, particularly in combination with climate projections, remains limited10. Similarly, Crop Water Stress Indices (CWSI) provide a direct representation of plant water stress conditions and have been successfully applied at field and irrigation scales19,20. However, these indices are rarely integrated with large-scale climate projections or long-term drought assessments, limiting their applicability for regional climate impact studies16. These limitations highlight a critical gap: the absence of an integrated framework that simultaneously evaluates drought variability, crop water stress, and system stability within a spatially explicit and climate-driven context3. Existing approaches tend to treat these components independently, which restricts the ability to capture the complex interactions between climate forcing, water availability, and agricultural resilience8. Moreover, the lack of spatially resolved classification systems makes it difficult to identify regions that are transitioning from stable to high-risk conditions under future climate scenarios21.

To address this gap, the present study develops a multi-index framework that integrates SPEI, a modified CWSI, and an RRV-based Stability Index (SI) to evaluate spatio-temporal patterns of water stress and agricultural resilience across South Korea. Using observed meteorological data for model evaluation and bias-corrected CMIP6 projections under SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 scenarios15,22, the study constructs continuous spatial representations of key variables and derives Crop Water Zones (CWZs) through a structured classification framework. This approach enables a more comprehensive assessment of climate-driven water stress by linking hydro-climatic variability with agricultural response.

The Saemangeum region on the western coast of South Korea was selected for detailed regional analysis due to its unique combination of hydro-climatic vulnerability and agricultural significance. As the world’s largest reclaimed tidal flat, Saemangeum is characterized by shallow water tables, limited freshwater storage, and high sensitivity to seasonal precipitation variability. The region supports expanding agricultural and aquacultural activities on reclaimed land that is inherently susceptible to both drought and waterlogging, making it an ideal case study for evaluating CWZ dynamics under future climate scenarios. To our knowledge, this is the first study to simultaneously integrate SPEI-based drought characterisation, a water-balance-derived CWSI, and an RRV-based SI within a unified spatial classification framework applied to bias-corrected CMIP6 projections over South Korea. Unlike previous CMIP6 drought assessments that focus solely on meteorological drought indicators, this study directly links hydro-climatic variability to both crop water stress and system-level stability, providing a more complete picture of agricultural water risk. The significance of this study lies in its ability to bridge the gap between climate projections and agricultural risk assessment. By integrating multiple indices within a unified framework and applying a physically grounded, fixed-threshold classification, the study enhances the reproducibility and cross-scenario comparability of results10. The resulting CWZ maps provide actionable insights for identifying vulnerable regions and support the development of adaptive water management strategies. Furthermore, the framework is transferable and can be applied to other monsoon-influenced regions facing similar climate-induced challenges.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study area encompasses the Korean Peninsula, located in East Asia, between latitudes 33°N to 43°N and longitudes 124°E to 131°E (Fig. 1). South Korea exhibits diverse topography, with elevation ranging from approximately − 26 m in low-lying coastal reclaimed areas to over 1,900 m in mountainous regions, reflecting a terrain dominated by uplands and narrow coastal plains. The minimum elevation value of − 26 m corresponds to reclaimed coastal zones located below mean sea level, such as those found in regions like Saemangeum, and therefore represents actual terrain conditions rather than a data-processing artifact. The country experiences a temperate monsoonal climate characterized by strong seasonal variability in precipitation and temperature. Mean annual precipitation ranges from approximately 1,000 to 1,800 mm, with nearly 60–70% occurring during the summer monsoon period (June–September).

Temperature variability is also pronounced, with winter temperatures dropping below − 10 °C and summer temperatures frequently exceeding 35 °C. This seasonal climate pattern results in distinct periods of water stress, particularly during late spring (pre-monsoon) and autumn when precipitation is limited and evapotranspiration demand remains high.

Agricultural systems in South Korea are dominated by paddy rice cultivation in lowland areas, supported by irrigation infrastructure, while upland regions are characterized by crops such as barley, maize, soybean, and vegetables. In addition, greenhouse-based horticulture is increasingly common, particularly in reclaimed coastal areas. Geologically, the Korean Peninsula is underlain predominantly by Precambrian metamorphic rocks and Jurassic granites in the mountainous interior, with Quaternary alluvial and coastal sedimentary deposits occupying the western and southern lowland plains. Soils reflect this geological and topographic diversity: brown forest soils and red-yellow podzolic soils dominate the upland and hillside zones, while the lowland plains and river terraces are covered by alluvial soils with high agricultural suitability. Reclaimed tidal flats, such as those in the Saemangeum area, are underlain by marine sediments and require intensive drainage management to support cultivation. In terms of land use, approximately 65% of South Korea’s total land area is classified as forest, with agricultural land accounting for around 20%, urban areas for approximately 8%, and water bodies and other land types making up the remainder. The combination of these geological, pedological, and land-use characteristics, together with the strongly seasonal hydro-climatic regime, makes South Korea highly sensitive to changes in water availability under future climate scenarios.

Data preparation

The observational dataset used in this study was compiled from the Korea Meteorological Administration (KMA) station network, which comprises over 80 meteorological stations across South Korea. To ensure data quality and temporal consistency, all stations were first screened for completeness over the historical period 1985–2014. Stations with any missing daily observations for precipitation, maximum temperature, minimum temperature, relative humidity, wind speed, or solar radiation were excluded. Following this screening, 60 stations were retained for analysis. These 60 stations satisfied two key criteria: (1) complete, uninterrupted daily records for all six variables across the full 30-year historical period with no missing dates or empty cells, as confirmed by consecutive-gap analysis (no gaps exceeding 5 days); and (2) good spatial distribution across the Korean Peninsula, covering all major climatic and topographic zones including the mountainous interior, western coastal lowlands, southern agricultural plains, and the Saemangeum reclaimed area. The spatial coverage of the selected 60 stations is illustrated in Fig. 1(b). Monthly aggregates of these quality-controlled daily records were subsequently used for model evaluation and bias correction. Soil moisture data for the historical period were obtained from the Global Land Evaporation Amsterdam Model (GLEAM v3.5a), a process-based model that estimates soil moisture and evaporation from satellite observations and reanalysis forcing data. GLEAM has been evaluated and applied in multiple East Asian and Korean climate studies and provides spatially complete daily soil moisture data suitable for regional water balance estimation23.

Future climate projections were obtained from the CMIP6. A subset of global climate models (GCMs) was selected based on data availability and consistency requirements. Models were initially screened to ensure the availability of all required variables (precipitation, maximum temperature, minimum temperature, relative humidity, and wind speed) for both historical and future periods. Models with incomplete variable coverage were excluded to maintain consistency in subsequent analyses.

The remaining models were evaluated against observed data for the historical period (1985–2014) using statistical performance metrics, including Nash–Sutcliffe Efficiency (NSE) and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE). All models meeting the data completeness criteria were retained to preserve ensemble diversity and avoid selection bias. Table S1 showed the selected GCMs and their spatial resolution.

Two Shared Socioeconomic Pathway (SSP) scenarios were considered: SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5. SSP2-4.5 represents a moderate stabilization pathway, while SSP5-8.5 represents a high-emission scenario. These scenarios were selected to capture a broad range of plausible future climate conditions, encompassing both moderate and high-impact trajectories, while avoiding redundancy associated with intermediate or lower-emission pathways.

Future projections were divided into two time horizons: a mid-future period (2036–2065) and a far-future period (2071–2100). These 30-year climatological periods are commonly used in climate impact studies to reduce interannual variability and represent stable climate conditions4,5.

Methods

Application of inverse distance weighting method

Spatial downscaling is necessary to address the discrepancies between General Circulation Model (GCM) simulations and station observations, as GCM outputs are presented in a grid format. Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) method is widely employed for interpolating climate data24. IDW operates on the principle that closer distances have a greater influence than those farther away25. While more sophisticated approaches such as ordinary kriging or machine-learning-based downscaling can offer advantages in specific contexts, IDW has been shown to perform comparably to these methods for temperature and precipitation interpolation at station densities similar to ours in regional climate studies across East Asia26. Its computational simplicity and parameter-free implementation make it a practical and widely applied choice for multi-variable spatial interpolation in climate impact studies. The use of IDW is nonetheless acknowledged as a simplification, and the absence of formal interpolation validation is noted as a study limitation. Bias correction

The data generated by GCMs from the CMIP6 dataset often do not match observed data precisely, leading to discrepancies known as biases. These biases arise from several factors, including limitations in model process parameterization, coarse spatial resolution, and an incomplete understanding of the climate system27–29. To address these biases, it is essential to either select the most accurate models or apply bias correction techniques15,17. While GCMs have improved over time, their outputs are still widely used to better understand present-day climate patterns and evaluate the potential consequences of global warming. One widely used method for correcting systematic biases in climate model outputs, particularly in precipitation, is the quantile mapping (QM) algorithm30. This study applies nonparametric transformation methods for QM to correct these biases, ensuring that the model outputs more closely align with observed data. This process not only improves the accuracy of climate projections but also enhances their reliability and applicability for climate impact assessments and informed decision-making. It is acknowledged that quantile mapping assumes stationarity in the bias transfer function, i.e., that the statistical relationship between modelled and observed distributions remains constant under future climate conditions. This assumption may not hold perfectly under strong climate change signals, particularly for precipitation extremes, and represents an inherent limitation of all empirical bias correction approaches31. Results from corrected projections should therefore be interpreted with this caveat in mind.

The non-parametric transformation method involves calculating the empirical cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) for both observed and modeled time series at regularly spaced quantiles32. This method aligns the observed and modeled time series on a quantile-by-quantile basis by applying a smoothing spline technique33. In this study, the smoothing spline was employed to correct for bias. In addition, to address the known limitation of QM for zero-inflated precipitation distributions, Equidistant CDF Matching (ECDF; Li et al. 2010) was applied as an alternative bias correction method specifically for precipitation. ECDF adjusts the model precipitation distribution by preserving the difference between modelled and observed CDFs, rather than substituting one for the other directly. This approach is more robust to the wet-day frequency mismatch that causes QM to degrade precipitation performance. ECDF was selected for the final precipitation bias correction based on its substantially better NSE performance, while QM was retained for all other variables where it performed consistently well.

Performance matrix

A widely used metric is the root mean square error (RMSE), which quantifies the average magnitude of errors between predicted and observed values. RMSE is particularly effective because it gives more weight to larger errors, offering a clear measure of the model’s overall performance. This approach helps identify the most preferred alternative by comparing how close each option is to the ideal and worst-case scenarios. Temporal changes in precipitation and temperature

To quantify temporal changes, annual means of temperature and precipitation were computed from the bias-corrected multi-model ensemble for each SSP scenario across all 60 stations. Linear trends were estimated using ordinary least squares (OLS) regression applied to the annual time series (2015–2100), and trend rates are reported in °C yr⁻¹ for temperature and mm yr⁻¹ for precipitation. Stability index

To assess the sustainability of water resources in a region, the study employed the reliability–resilience–vulnerability (RRV) model, which aggregates drought characteristics such as frequency, duration, and severity. We employed a threshold-based approach to identify reliability, resiliency, and vulnerability in this study, a methodology that has also been applied in several previous studies5,10,11. Drought is identified when the SPEI value falls below − 1, which also serves as the threshold for water demand in the RRV model. The SPEI < − 1 criterion corresponds to the “mild drought” category in the classification system of McKee et al.13, representing conditions where standardised water deficit is at least one standard deviation below the historical mean. This threshold is widely used in RRV-based hydrological assessments and has been applied in previous drought sustainability studies over South Korea using similar station networks5,10.

Resilience is inversely related to drought duration—longer droughts lead to lower resilience. Vulnerability \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left(Vul\right)$$\end{document} measures the severity of failure, reflecting how much the system is impacted when it cannot meet water demands. It is calculated as the average deficit, which is the sum of deficits divided by the number of drought events. Higher vulnerability indicates greater impact on water availability and recovery after drought.15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Vul = \frac{{1\:}}{N}\sum\limits_{{j = 1}}^{N} {v\left( j \right)}$$\end{document}

Relative vulnerability (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Rel\_Vul$$\end{document}) can be defined as the ratio of vulnerability to water demand, where demand is set at a predefined threshold. This measure expresses the severity of the system’s failure relative to the demand for water, highlighting how much the system’s ability to meet that demand is compromised during drought conditions or disruptions.16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Rel\_Vul=\frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum\:_{j=1}^{N}v\left(j\right)}{water\:demand}$$\end{document}

The combined assessment of reliability, resilience, and vulnerability is used to evaluate the overall conditions of a drought event through the cumulative R-R-V index, which analyzes the sustainability of a water system.17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:SI={\left[\right(Rel\times\:Res\times\:\left(1-Rel\_Vul\right)]}^{\frac{1}{3}}$$\end{document}

The Stability Index \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left(SI\right)$$\end{document} reflects the stability of the water resources system, with values ranging from 0 to 1. A value closer to 1 indicates greater water system stability (high reliability, rapid resilience, and low vulnerability), while a lower value indicates reduced stability and increased risk of failure.

To facilitate spatial mapping and cross-scenario comparison, SI values were classified into five stability categories. The SI is a geometric mean of Reliability, Resilience, and (1 − Relative Vulnerability), bounded on [0, 1], where SI = 0 represents complete system failure and SI = 1 represents perfect sustainability (Loucks, 1997;18). The classification thresholds were selected based on the following physical and mathematical rationale, consistent with previous applications of the RRV-based Stability Index in hydrology and water resources research5,10: (i) SI < 0.25 = Completely Unstable: at this level, the combined product of reliability, resilience, and non-vulnerability is extremely low, indicating that the system fails frequently, recovers slowly, and sustains large deficits. A system with SI < 0.25 meets water demand less than roughly one-quarter of the theoretical maximum sustainability, representing conditions of chronic, severe failure. (ii) 0.25 ≤ SI < 0.55 = Unstable: the system experiences more failure than acceptable performance. This range corresponds to conditions where either reliability is low (frequent drought occurrences), resilience is low (slow recovery), or vulnerability is high (large deficits), or a combination thereof. The upper bound of 0.55 was selected as it represents the midpoint of the SI range, below which instability dominates performance. (iii) 0.55 ≤ SI < 0.70 = Moderately Stable: in this transitional zone, the system performs acceptably under most conditions but retains meaningful vulnerability to stress. The threshold at 0.55 captures the transition from instability-dominated to stability-dominated behaviour, and 0.70 is widely used in water system performance benchmarks as a minimum acceptable reliability criterion (Loucks & van Beek, 2017). (iv) 0.70 ≤ SI < 0.90 = Stable: the system reliably meets water demand in the majority of time periods, recovers promptly from drought events, and sustains only minor deficits. This range is consistent with the “satisfactory” performance level described by18 and applied in regional water sustainability assessments across Asia5. (v) SI ≥ 0.90 = Highly Stable: the system approaches ideal performance, with near-complete reliability, rapid resilience, and negligible vulnerability. This threshold was chosen to distinguish genuinely high-performing systems from merely acceptable ones, consistent with the upper-quartile performance benchmarks used in multi-criteria water resource assessments. The use of fixed, physically anchored thresholds rather than data-driven quantile breaks ensures that SI values remain directly comparable across seasons, scenarios, and time horizons, which is essential when the study objective is to detect temporal and spatial shifts in stability under climate change.

Crop water stress index

The calculation of the crop water stress index (CWSI) involves several systematic steps to integrate meteorological and soil data, providing a comprehensive measure of plant water stress. Figure 2 showed variables used to calculate CWSI.

The steps and mathematical expressions used for CWSI calculation are as follows:

Step 1, Potential evapotranspiration (PET) represents the maximum water loss from a crop under optimal moisture conditions and is typically estimated using the Penman-Monteith (PM) equation. In this approach, we have already calculated PET in the first step. To normalize PET, we use the following formula:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{N}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{z}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{d}\:\mathrm{P}\mathrm{E}\mathrm{T}=\text{}\text{}\frac{\text{}{PET}_{i}\mathrm{}-{\boldsymbol{\mu\:}}_{PET}}{\boldsymbol{\sigma\:}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{E}\mathrm{T}}$$\end{document}

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{PET}_{i}\text{}$$\end{document} is the values of PET i-th time period, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\boldsymbol{\mu\:}}_{PET}$$\end{document} is the means of PET, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\boldsymbol{\sigma\:}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{E}\mathrm{T}$$\end{document} is the standard deviations of PET.

This normalization process adjusts PET values to a standard scale, making it easier to compare across different time periods and locations, while accounting for the variation in PET over time.

Step 2, Evapotranspiration (ETa) is estimated using precipitation and soil moisture data. Soil moisture represents the water available upper portion of soil column, and precipitation provides the additional water input. The change in soil moisture over time reflects the water used by the crop (ETa). The formula can be expressed as:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{ET}_{a}=\:{\Delta\:}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{l}\:\mathrm{m}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{u}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{e}+precipitation$$\end{document}

where Δsoil moisture is the change in soil moisture between two time points, representing the amount of water the crop has used over the period. Precipitation is added because it contributes to replenishing the soil moisture, affecting the crop’s actual water use.

Evapotranspiration (ETa) represents total water in environment, which is influenced by both the depletion of soil moisture and the water replenished through precipitation. When precipitation increases soil moisture, it suggests that the crop is experiencing less water stress. Conversely, if soil moisture decreases without significant precipitation, it indicates higher crop water consumption and increased water stress. To standardize ETa, it is normalized using the same method as PET. This normalization ensures that both PET and ETa are comparable on the same scale, facilitating a more accurate assessment of crop water stress.

Step 3, calculation of crop water stress index: The CWSI was calculated using the ratio difference between PET and ETa to PET19,20. CWSI quantifies water stress by comparing actual ET (represented by the ETproxy) to potential ET (PETnormalized). The expression for CWSI is as follows:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:CWSI=1-\left(\frac{Normalized\:{ET}_{a}}{\mathrm{N}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{z}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{d}\:PET}\right)$$\end{document}

By using only precipitation and soil moisture, this method provides a simplified approach to estimating Actual Evapotranspiration (ETa). The key idea is to relate soil moisture depletion (or accumulation) with precipitation inputs to determine how much water the crop is using. The Crop Water Stress Index (CWSI) then offers a straightforward way to assess crop water stress and guide irrigation decisions based on available water data.

To enable spatial classification and cross-scenario comparison, CWSI values were categorized into five stress levels. The CWSI is bounded on [0, 1], where CWSI = 0 indicates that actual evapotranspiration (ETa) equals potential evapotranspiration (PET), meaning crops experience no moisture limitation, and CWSI = 1 indicates that ETa approaches zero, representing complete water stress19; Idso et al. 1981). The five classification thresholds were selected on the basis of established agronomic and physiological stress benchmarks as follows: (i) 0 ≤ CWSI < 0.20 = No Stress: ETa is at or near 90–100% of PET, indicating that atmospheric water demand is almost fully met. Crop growth and yield are unimpaired. This range is consistent with the “non-stressed” baseline defined by Jackson et al.19 and corresponds to conditions under which irrigation scheduling is not required. (ii) 0.20 ≤ CWSI < 0.40 = Low Stress: ETa satisfies 70–90% of PET. Minor stomatal restriction begins to occur, but yield reductions are typically below economically significant thresholds (< 10%). A CWSI threshold near 0.20–0.25 is widely used in precision irrigation systems as the lower trigger point for initiating supplemental irrigation16,20. (iii) 0.40 ≤ CWSI < 0.60 = Moderate Stress: ETa satisfies 50–70% of PET. Stomatal conductance is substantially reduced, photosynthesis is impaired, and measurable yield reductions (10–30%) are expected for most crops. CWSI values in this range correspond to the “mild to moderate” stress category widely reported in field studies of paddy rice and other Korean staple crops6. The threshold at 0.40 is specifically identified in the original Jackson et al.19 framework as the boundary above which irrigation intervention is agronomically necessary. (iv) 0.60 ≤ CWSI < 0.80 = High Stress: ETa satisfies only 30–50% of PET. Severe stomatal closure, accelerated leaf senescence, and substantial yield losses (> 30%) are characteristic of this range. Sustained exposure to CWSI values above 0.60 over multiple growing seasons has been associated with permanent reductions in soil organic matter and long-term productivity decline in irrigated agricultural systems3. (v) 0.80 ≤ CWSI ≤ 1.00 = Extreme Stress: the system approaches complete crop water failure, with ETa below 20% of PET. Under these conditions, crop wilting and irreversible tissue damage are expected, and conventional irrigation cannot fully compensate for the moisture deficit. This threshold aligns with the upper critical stress range identified in remote sensing-based CWSI studies20 and is consistent with the extreme drought category (SPEI < − 2) used in the SPEI framework applied in this study. The uniform application of these thresholds across all seasons and climate scenarios ensures that CWSI maps are directly comparable and that spatial transitions between stress categories reflect genuine physical change rather than artefacts of time-varying classification boundaries.

Data fusion and composite map creation

The methodology for developing combined crop water resilience zone (CWZ) maps integrated geospatial and statistical techniques to analyze spatial variations across multiple seasons and eras. The study utilized three key datasets: Relative Resilience Value (RRV), Crop Water Stress Index (CWSI), and a shapefile of administrative boundaries. RRV and CWSI datasets provided seasonal values for historical, near future, and far future scenarios, aligned by common latitude and longitude coordinates for seamless integration. These datasets were merged to create a unified dataset, with seasonal columns assigned unique identifiers for interpolation and visualization. The administrative boundary shape file was used to delineate the region of interest and constrain the analysis to the study area.

The CWZ classification was derived by cross-combining three SI classes (Low: 0–0.55; Moderate: 0.55–0.80; High: 0.80–1.00) with three CWSI classes (Low: 0–0.20; Moderate: 0.20–0.50; High: 0.50–1.00) within a 3 × 3 matrix (Table 1). These three classes were derived by aggregating the five original SI and CWSI classes into low, moderate, and high categories to simplify CWZ interpretation. The SI class boundaries follow the satisfactory reliability thresholds established by18 and Loucks & van Beek (2017); the CWSI boundaries follow the irrigation trigger and yield-loss thresholds of Jackson et al.19 and Ihuoma & Madramootoo16, as detailed in Sect. 2.3.6 and 2.3.7. The resulting nine CWZ categories span a gradient from Resilient (stable water system, low crop stress) to Extreme (chronically unstable system, severe moisture deficit). Spatial interpolation was subsequently performed using Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) on a uniform 200 × 200 grid spanning the study area, with a boundary mask applied to constrain results to the administrative extent of South Korea using Shapely geometry operations.

It is widely recognized that the performance of a General Circulation Model (GCM) during the historical period strongly influences the reliability of its future projections. In this study, the performance of 10 GCM pairs from the CMIP6 ensemble was assessed using key meteorological variables, including precipitation, average temperature, solar radiation, relative humidity, and wind speed. These variables were downscaled using Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) and bias-corrected with Quantile Mapping (QM) and ECDF for precipitation based on observational data from 60 stations.

The Taylor diagram (Taylor, 2001) shown in Fig. 3 provides a comprehensive comparison between the raw and bias-corrected CMIP6 simulations and observed data. This diagram evaluates performance metrics such as the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), Pearson correlation coefficient, and standard deviation. The results clearly indicate that bias-corrected simulations exhibit significantly improved alignment with observed data, affirming their enhanced accuracy.

Temporal changes in precipitation and temperature

Temperature increases steadily under both scenarios (Fig. 5a), with a stronger warming rate under SSP5-8.5 (0.069 °C yr⁻¹) compared to SSP2-4.5 (0.045 °C yr⁻¹). Scenario differences are modest in the near term but amplify after mid-century, leading to a clear divergence in the far-future period (2071–2100). By the end of the century, SSP5-8.5 exceeds SSP2-4.5 by approximately 2.5–3.0 °C, indicating a substantially stronger warming signal under high-emission conditions.

Precipitation exhibits a weaker and more variable response (Fig. 5b). Both scenarios show a slight increasing trend, with a higher rate under SSP5-8.5 (3.230 mm yr⁻¹) than SSP2-4.5 (0.910 mm yr⁻¹). However, interannual variability is large and frequently masks scenario-driven differences at the annual scale. Although SSP5-8.5 generally yields higher precipitation in the late century, the separation between scenarios remains less distinct than for temperature.

These results highlight a contrasting climate response, where temperature shows a robust, scenario-dependent signal, while precipitation is dominated by variability with comparatively weaker scenario separation.

In contrast, precipitation projections display high inter annual variability with no strong increasing or decreasing trend, though SSP5-8.5 exhibits slightly higher mean precipitation and a greater uncertainty range, suggesting the possibility of more extreme precipitation events. These findings underscore the strong influence of greenhouse gas emissions on future temperature trajectories, while precipitation remains more uncertain due to complex atmospheric interactions. The implications of these changes are critical, as rising temperatures will likely enhance evapotranspiration, alter hydrological cycles, and exacerbate climate extremes. The increased uncertainty in precipitation patterns presents challenges for water resource management, agriculture, and disaster preparedness. Given these projections, mitigation strategies to limit greenhouse gas emissions and adaptation measures, such as climate-resilient infrastructure and water management policies, are essential for minimizing future climate risks.

Stability index

Water stability maps developed using the SPEI drought index and RRV framework reveal distinct seasonal and temporal patterns across South Korea as shown in Fig. 6. Historically, winter and autumn exhibited large areas of high stability (71,740 and 58,904 km²), reflecting consistent water availability due to lower demand and reduced precipitation variability. Spring and summer showed moderate stability (~ 89,900 km² each) but also localized low stability, linked to higher variability in rainfall and demand.

Under SSP2-4.5, near-future projections indicate stable winter conditions but rising low stability in spring (2,000 km²) and summer (17,714 km²). Far-future projections intensify this trend, with reduced stability in spring (72,163 km²) and summer (72,788 km²), suggesting growing risks of seasonal shortages.

The SSP5-8.5 scenario shows stronger impacts, with spring low stability expanding sharply (19,662 km² in the near future) and summer conditions deteriorating further in the far future (46,835 km² of moderate/low stability). Although winter and autumn largely remain stable, the warmer seasons increasingly face water stress driven by higher demand and altered precipitation regimes.

These findings indicate that while winter and autumn remain resilient, spring and summer are projected to face escalating variability and water stress. This emphasizes the importance of developing adaptive water management policies focused on high-demand seasons under future climate scenarios.

Crop water zone (CWZ)

Figure 12 presents the spatial distribution of water stress conditions across South Korea, integrating the Reliability, Resilience, and Vulnerability (RRV) approach with the Critical Water Stress Index (CWSI). It categorizes water stability into Severe CWZ, Moderate CWZ, Resilient CWZ, High-Risk CWZ, Semi-Critical CWZ, Sensitive CWZ, Extreme CWZ, Critical CWZ, and Marginal CWZ, providing insights into how water stability is expected to evolve under different climate scenarios.

During the historical period, no regions were classified under Severe, Moderate, or Resilient CWZ, indicating relatively stable water availability with minimal extreme stress. Sensitive CWZ was substantial, particularly in winter (25,146 km²) and summer (22,312 km²), while Extreme and Critical CWZ peaked in autumn (20,452 km² and 59,141 km²), reflecting seasonal stress linked to reduced pre-winter precipitation. Marginal CWZ was widespread, suggesting overall stability but potential future vulnerability.

Under the SSP2-4.5 near future scenario, Moderate CWZ (685 km² in summer) and High-Risk CWZ (692 km² in spring; 1,458 km² in summer) emerged, indicating localized risks of shortages. Semi-Critical CWZ expanded in summer (4,836 km²) and autumn (4,639 km²), while Extreme CWZ rose sharply in summer (52,298 km²). Critical CWZ increased notably in spring (28,541 km²) and autumn (42,512 km²), highlighting chronic supply risks. In the SSP2-4.5 Far Future, water stress intensifies with further expansion of Moderate (786 km²) and Resilient CWZ (1,856 km²) in summer, reflecting spatial variability in availability. High-Risk CWZ grew in summer (6,541 km²) and autumn (5,413 km²), and Semi-Critical CWZ expanded in winter (4,922 km²) and summer (9,421 km²). Extreme CWZ remained elevated in summer (37,711 km²), while Critical CWZ stayed persistently high in autumn (41,721 km²), underscoring worsening seasonal stress under future climates.

Under the SSP5-8.5 near future scenario, water stress intensifies, with increases in Moderate CWZ (1,251 km² in winter; 123 km² in summer) and Resilient CWZ (2,521 km² in winter; 6,542 km² in summer), indicating spatial variability in stability. High-Risk CWZ expands in winter (2,563 km²), suggesting elevated drought risks, while Semi-Critical CWZ grows in summer (691 km²) and autumn (891 km²), reflecting shifting seasonal stress. Sensitive CWZ remains extensive in winter (40,329 km²) and spring (39,214 km²), while Extreme CWZ rises sharply in autumn (64,353 km²). Critical CWZ persists at high levels across seasons, underscoring mounting water security challenges. In the SSP5-8.5 Far Future, severe stress becomes widespread, with Severe CWZ emerging for the first time across all seasons (1,845–2,541 km²), signaling critical shortages during peak demand in spring and summer. Moderate CWZ continues to expand (2,032 km² in winter), while High-Risk and Semi-Critical CWZ remain elevated across seasons, reflecting persistent distribution challenges. Sensitive CWZ peaks in spring (21,746 km²), suggesting enduring instability. The most critical finding is the unprecedented expansion of Extreme CWZ, reaching 80,459 km² in autumn and 69,971 km² in summer, highlighting severe future risks to water availability under high-emission conditions.

The results indicate that water stress in South Korea will intensify under future climate change, with the most severe impacts projected under SSP5-8.5. Historically, stress was primarily seasonal, peaking in autumn, but future scenarios show increasing severity across all seasons, especially in summer and autumn when demand is highest. The emergence of Severe CWZ under SSP5-8.5 far future signals the risk of chronic shortages without adaptation.

The expansion of High-Risk, Extreme, and Critical CWZ underscores the urgency of water conservation, storage infrastructure, and policy interventions, as drought risks are expected to intensify. Although some areas retain Moderate and Resilient CWZ, spatial variability in availability points to localized but severe crises.

Overall, these findings highlight a shift toward more severe and widespread water stress driven by rising temperatures, altered precipitation, and growing demand. The pronounced risks in spring and summer stress the need for strategic water management, climate adaptation policies, and investment in sustainable technologies to enhance water security and resilience.

The historical data shows that water stress in Saemangeum region was relatively stable, with no Severe, Moderate, or Extreme CWZs recorded (Figs. 14 and 15). Summer exhibited slightly higher stress, with 1,942.49 km² categorized as Semi-Critical CWZ, while autumn followed a similar pattern. In the SSP2-4.5 near future scenario, water stress begins to intensify, particularly in spring and summer. The emergence of High-Risk CWZ in spring (20.35 km²) and the expansion of Sensitive CWZ in summer (1,472.35 km²) indicate a growing disruption in seasonal water availability. The Semi-Critical CWZ in autumn expands to 975.71 km², suggesting shifting precipitation patterns that could affect soil moisture retention. The SSP2-4.5 far future projections reinforce this trend, with Semi-Critical CWZ further increasing to 2,123.21 km² in autumn, reflecting a persistent rise in seasonal stress. The SSP5-8.5 near future scenario presents a more severe trajectory, with the appearance of Extreme CWZ in spring (235.32 km²) and a near-equal distribution of Semi-Critical and Sensitive CWZs in summer and autumn. This pattern indicates a shift from isolated seasonal stress to more sustained water scarcity. The SSP5-8.5 far future scenario shows Sensitive CWZ reaching 1,942.49 km² in autumn, while Semi-Critical CWZ declines, signaling a transition to high-risk water stress zones.

These results indicate a progressive escalation of water stress across all seasons, with future scenarios showing worsening conditions. Historically, South Korea experienced seasonal fluctuations in water stress, but no extreme or widespread scarcity. The SSP2-4.5 projections suggest a gradual increase in stress, particularly in spring and autumn, indicating that shifts in precipitation timing and soil moisture retention will play a significant role in future water availability. The far-future projection under this scenario highlights that moderate stress levels will likely become a recurring issue, necessitating proactive water management. The SSP5-8.5 projections depict a more critical situation, with extreme stress emerging in spring and the transition of previously semi-critical areas into high-stress categories. This suggests that under a high-emission scenario, water shortages will become more frequent and severe, affecting agriculture, water supply, and overall sustainability. The extension of stress into traditionally stable seasons like winter and spring further emphasizes the urgency of implementing adaptive strategies. These findings highlight the necessity for improved irrigation systems, enhanced water conservation measures, and investments in sustainable water infrastructure. Policymakers should prioritize proactive measures to mitigate water stress before it reaches critical levels, ensuring the resilience of South Korea’s water resources in the face of climate change.

Discussion

The projected intensification of water stress across South Korea is broadly consistent with previous regional assessments. Sung et al.5 applied RRV-based sustainability analysis using 28 CMIP5 GCMs and reported declining reliability and resilience in summer and autumn, a finding replicated here with the updated CMIP6 ensemble. The present study advances beyond Sung et al.5 by simultaneously incorporating CWSI and a spatially resolved CWZ classification, enabling identification not only of where water systems are becoming less stable, but where instability directly translates into agricultural moisture deficits. The historical RRV patterns observed, with winter and autumn showing larger stable areas due to lower evaporative demand, are also consistent with broader literature on monsoon-dominated water systems in East Asia4 with the reliability-resilience-vulnerability (RRV) model. South Korea, with its diverse topography and monsoonal climate, is highly susceptible to shifts in water availability, especially in agricultural regions that are integral to the country’s food security. As observed, historical water stress conditions were relatively moderate, with significant areas experiencing moderate stress in summer and autumn, as expected in regions experiencing seasonal water demand. However, when comparing historical conditions to future projections, it is clear that climate change, particularly under the SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 scenarios, will exacerbate water stress significantly.

The combined CWZ maps reveal a systematic spatial transition from lower-risk categories (Marginal, Sensitive) historically to higher-risk categories (Extreme, High-Risk, Severe) under future scenarios. This shift reflects the concurrent deterioration of two independent indicators: declining RRV stability and rising CWSI. The emergence of Severe CWZ, representing stable water systems paradoxically experiencing extreme crop water stress, under SSP5-8.5 far-future conditions indicates that atmospheric evaporative demand may outpace irrigation capacity even in areas with reliable supply, consistent with Liu et al.37, who identified persistent water scarcity under high irrigation demand despite nominally adequate supply. Winter and spring are also projected to experience increasing moderate and high stress, confirming that even traditionally more stable seasons will become vulnerable5.

The CWSI projections show substantial increases in moderate and high stress across all seasons. Summer and autumn consistently emerge as the most vulnerable seasons, consistent with the seasonal structure of Korean agriculture: paddy rice is most sensitive to moisture deficits during the reproductive growth stage (July–August), when CWSI values shift most dramatically toward the high-stress category (Shin et al. 2023). The autumn stress amplification is linked to the mismatch between crop water demand during grain-filling and reduced post-monsoon precipitation, consistent with the compound heat–moisture stress patterns documented by Lesk et al. (2022) for monsoon-influenced cereal systems globally37. Particularly in summer, a sharp increase in high stress areas suggests that water availability will become a critical issue, affecting crop yields and agricultural productivity, which is highly dependent on water availability during this time.

Under SSP5-8.5, the far-future period shows the most severe deterioration, with Extreme CWZ expanding to 80,459 km² in autumn and 69,971 km² in summer. These conditions are corroborated by Bjarke et al.14, who identified South Korea and the broader East Asian monsoon region as high-risk for hydrological drought intensification under CMIP6 high-emission scenarios. The near-term similarity between SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 results is physically expected: cumulative radiative forcing differences between scenarios remain modest before mid-century, with the scenarios diverging strongly only in the far-future period when SSP5-8.5 exceeds SSP2-4.5 by approximately 2.5–3.0 °C in our temperature projections. This near-term convergence is scientifically important: it indicates that projected water stress increases for 2036–2065 are essentially independent of emission pathway, strengthening the case for near-term adaptation investments regardless of mitigation trajectory. These findings corroborate with other regional studies that predict severe consequences for water resources under high-emission scenarios, where droughts are projected to be more frequent and prolonged14. The expansion of high stress in autumn particularly highlights that the impacts of climate change will not be limited to the summer months but will extend into traditionally more stable seasons.

The RRV-based Stability Index reveals declining reliability and resilience with rising vulnerability across all future scenarios, indicating that South Korea’s water infrastructure — designed for historically stable monsoon conditions — will face increasing pressure to meet demand during droughts. The SI decline is most pronounced in spring and summer under SSP5-8.5 far-future conditions, where the proportion of unstable (SI < 0.55) areas more than doubles compared to the historical period. This finding has direct infrastructure implications: existing reservoir storage and irrigation distribution systems were calibrated for the historical variability regime, and their performance will progressively degrade as drought frequency and severity increase (Loucks & van Beek, 2017).

Several sources of uncertainty warrant explicit acknowledgment. First, the QM and ECDF bias correction methods assume stationarity in the bias transfer function, which may not hold perfectly under strong future warming, particularly for precipitation extremes (Maraun, 2016). Second, IDW interpolation does not account for spatial anisotropy or topographic controls on precipitation, which may introduce smoothing artefacts in mountainous regions. Third, the water-balance ETa proxy in the CWSI formulation may not fully capture plant water stress under high vapour pressure deficit conditions. Fourth, the TOPSIS-based ensemble weighting has not been subjected to formal sensitivity analysis; alternative weighting schemes may yield slightly different projections. These limitations suggest that absolute values of projected CWZ areas should be interpreted as indicative, while directional trends and spatial patterns are considered robust.that future water resource management strategies are based on the most accurate and region-specific climate data available38.

Overall, this study demonstrates that South Korea will experience increasingly severe and spatially extensive water stress under both SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5, with the most critical conditions projected for summer and autumn under the high-emission far-future scenario. The spatially resolved CWZ framework identifies specific regions, particularly the western coastal agricultural lowlands including the Saemangeum area, as disproportionately vulnerable to the compound effects of declining water system stability and rising crop water demand. These findings provide regionally specific, actionable information for adaptive water management planning in South Korea and offer a transferable methodological template for similarly structured monsoon-dominated agricultural systems elsewhere.

Conclusions

This study provides a spatially explicit, multi-index assessment of future crop water stress across South Korea under CMIP6 climate projections, integrating the SPEI-based RRV Stability Index (SI), the Crop Water Stress Index (CWSI), and a fixed-threshold 3 × 3 Crop Water Zone (CWZ) classification. Results indicate a significant intensification of water stress, particularly in summer and autumn, with high-stress CWZ areas expanding substantially under SSP5-8.5 far-future conditions. The decline in water system reliability and resilience, alongside rising vulnerability, underscores the urgent need for improved adaptive water management strategies that account for both changing drought dynamics and increasing evaporative demand. These findings are robust across near-term emission pathways, with clear divergence between SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 emerging in the far-future period.

Beyond the specific findings for South Korea, this study demonstrates the broader transferability of the integrated SPEI–CWSI–RRV framework to other monsoon-influenced agricultural regions facing similar hydro-climatic pressures. The physically grounded, fixed-threshold classification approach adopted here enhances reproducibility and cross-regional comparability. Key limitations of the study include: the stationarity assumption inherent in QM and ECDF bias correction; the simplification of IDW spatial interpolation; restriction to two SSP scenarios; the water-balance ETa proxy used in the CWSI formulation; and the absence of formal sensitivity analysis of TOPSIS weighting and CWZ classification boundaries. Future research should incorporate additional emission scenarios (SSP1-2.6, SSP3-7.0), evaluate alternative evapotranspiration estimation approaches such as eddy-covariance-calibrated land surface models, integrate socio-economic vulnerability indices for a more complete agricultural risk picture, and conduct formal uncertainty quantification through ensemble resampling methods. this research provides region-specific projections, enabling more targeted adaptation strategies for agricultural and urban water management.

Limitations

A key limitation of this study is that the SPEI and CWSI indices rely on assumptions about water availability and crop response, potentially overlooking local variations in soil properties or management practices. The historical focus (1985–2014) may not capture all extreme events or abrupt hydrological shifts, limiting prediction of outliers. Additionally, only two socio-economic pathways (SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5) were considered, leaving out other emission and development scenarios that could further inform water stress projections. Therefore, results should be interpreted as indicative, emphasizing the need for ongoing model validation and refinement as new data become available.
