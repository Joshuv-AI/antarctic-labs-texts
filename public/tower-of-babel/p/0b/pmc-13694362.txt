Enhancing thrombolytic therapy in Japan-dwelling Japanese using clinical ultrasound with microbubble contrast agent
Authors: Takako Shirakawa, Tsutomu Matsuura, Norio Sekine, Hiroyuki Takahashi, Hirotaka James Okano, Takao Ohki, Kentaro Kasa, Yoshitaka Mine, Tomoyuki Ohta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo determine in vitro the efficacy and safety of thrombolysis in blood from Japanese subjects using standard diagnostic ultrasound (US) with or without microbubbles.MethodsBlood from six healthy Japan-dwelling Japanese (mean age 22.3 years), who have relatively low susceptibility to thrombosis because of heredity and dietary habits, was left at room temperature for 24 or 72 h. Each clot-containing well was submerged in a 150-mL heparin-physiologic saline bath (5 units heparin/mL) and rotated at 180 rpm. The 30-min US treatment conditions were: (1) no US or microbubbles; (2) grayscale US (4.5 MHz); (3) color Doppler; (4) grayscale US with addition of 4-μL perflubutane microbubbles (Sonazoid; GE Healthcare Pharma Corporation, Tokyo Japan) to the bath every 5 min; and (5) color Doppler with microbubble addition as above. The Aplio i800 (Canon Medical Systems, Tochigi Japan) US system was used with the i8CX1 transducer.ResultsAmong six subjects with clots, the number of 24-h clots that changed to hypoechoic under each condition was as follows: (1) 0, (2) 3, (3) 5, (4) 6, and (5) 5. For 72-h clots, the number of changes was: (1) 0, (2) 1, (3) 3, (4) 6, and (5) 3. In Masson’s trichrome-stained specimens, US treatment generated less fibrin within the light-blue amorphous element than in the control. No rupture of red blood cells was observed.ConclusionWe confirmed that standard clinical diagnostic US, used for grayscale or color Doppler imaging, enhanced thrombolysis. Thrombolytic efficacy was greater with combination of Sonazoid and US than with US alone.

Introduction

Intravascular thrombosis in the form of myocardial infarction, cerebral infarction, or venous thrombosis is a common disease. There have been in vitro studies of thrombolysis with ultrasound (US) since the 1970s, and papers from the 1980s indicated that small gas bubbles enhance this process [1–3]. Currently, clinical trials are underway to evaluate the efficacy of US therapy combined with microbubbles and thrombolytic agents in the treatment of myocardial infarction, cerebral infarction, deep vein thrombosis, and pulmonary embolism. The US delivery methods include transcutaneous, transcranial, and catheter-directed approaches [4–8].

The mechanisms underlying US-enhanced thrombolysis are radiation force and acoustic cavitation, which is more likely to occur at lower frequencies. Microbubbles further facilitate cavitation [3, 9–11]. Previous studies have used low-frequency US ranging from 300 kHz (research use) to 2 MHz (clinical diagnostic use). In contrast, standard clinical diagnostic US systems commonly used in Japan operate at higher frequencies. Perflubutane (Sonazoid®; GE Healthcare Pharma Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) is widely used in Japan for diagnostic purposes as a US microbubble contrast agent for contrast harmonic imaging. Here, we investigated the feasibility of achieving thrombolysis using standard clinical diagnostic US, typically used for abdominal grayscale or color Doppler imaging, without the use of contrast harmonic imaging, both with and without microbubble administration.

Blood clots were prepared from Japan-dwelling Japanese individuals, a population known to have a lower risk of thrombosis due to genetic [12–14] and dietary factors. East Asians have been reported to exhibit lower platelet–fibrin clot strength compared to White peoples [14] and the traditional Japanese diet is associated with reduced cardiovascular mortality [15].

This in vitro study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of thrombolysis under high-frequency US conditions, which are generally less favorable for clot dissolution, using a standard clinical diagnostic US system. The evaluation was conducted using grayscale or color Doppler US, modalities typically employed in abdominal imaging, without the use of contrast harmonic imaging, and with or without the administration of microbubbles, in blood samples collected from Japan-dwelling Japanese individuals.

Materials and methods

This prospective in vitro study was conducted with approval from our institution’s Human Ethics Review Committee (approval number 21024). Written informed consent was obtained from all volunteers.

Subjects

Blood samples were obtained from six healthy Japanese male volunteers residing in Japan (mean age ± standard deviation: 22.3 ± 0.47 years, range: 22–23 years). All participants self-identified as ethnically Japanese, excluding individuals of mixed heritage (e.g., half- or quarter-Japanese). All participants had a normal body mass index and blood pressure, and none had anemia, hematologic disorders, hemophilia, or hyperlipidemia. None was receiving any pharmacological treatment at the time of blood collection.

Thrombus formation and experimental setup

To prepare 24- and 72-h thrombi, whole blood was allowed to clot at room temperature in five 15-mL test tubes for each time point. Only the clotted red blood cell portion was used; serum and buffy coat were excluded. Rectangular plates with six 3-mL wells were fabricated from plaster using a 3-dimensional printer and coated with a waterproof layer. One plate was used per clot under each experimental condition. Given five experimental conditions for both 24- and 72-h clots and six subjects, a total of 60 plates were prepared.

Each clot was placed in one well of a plate, while the remaining five wells were filled with stainless steel weights to prevent flotation. The plates were submerged in a 150-mL bath of heparin-physiologic saline (5 units/mL), prepared using heparin sodium injection (Mochida Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) and physiological saline (Otsuka Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The heparin bath was placed inside a slightly larger water bath and set on a hotplate to maintain the temperature between 30 and 36 °C. A magnetic stirrer bar (10 mm × 5 mm) was dropped into the heparin-physiologic saline bath and spun at 180 rpm (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Experimental setup. Therapeutic ultrasound

US was administered using the Aplio i800 system (Canon Medical Systems, Tochigi, Japan), which has been approved by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare of Japan for clinical diagnostic use. The scanning parameters are summarized in Table 1. The study used a general standard US diagnostic device. Cavitation occurs more readily at low frequencies and high acoustic pressures [9, 11]. US treatment conditions require a low frequency. Using the abdominal preset, the scan depth was set to 30 mm, and grayscale imaging was performed with a 4.5-MHz convex transducer. The mechanical index (MI) was automatically displayed as 1.3. With the addition of color Doppler to grayscale US, the MI increased to 1.6, as automatically displayed. During treatment, the convex transducer was held with a clamp stand and adjusted to scan the cross-section passing through the long axis (12 mm) of the cylindrical well (Fig. 1). Evaluation of changes in ultrasound images before and after treatment

To evaluate changes in thrombus morphology between pre- and post-treatment conditions using high-resolution US, we employed a high-frequency linear transducer. The linear scan was adjusted to visualize the long axis of the well (12 mm). To minimize misalignment with the treatment section, we performed an end-to-end sweep of the cylindrical well and selected the image showing the largest cross-section. As a precaution, an additional end-to-end sweep was performed in the orthogonal plane.

Treatment efficacy was assessed by two authors (T.S. and N.S.), who independently evaluated changes in high-frequency linear transducer images obtained before and after treatment. Hypoechoic changes were classified based on the extent of the affected area: changes involving ≤ 20% of the area were defined as no change ( −), those between 20 and 40% as positive (○), and those exceeding 40% as strongly positive (◎). If a part of the thrombus was defective, the corresponding region was classified as hypoechoic change (Fig. 2). The intensity of hypoechoic changes was visually assessed, and only changes that were clearly distinguishable were considered valid. To avoid subjective bias, minor changes that were difficult to judge were regarded as no change. In cases of disagreement, a consensus was reached through discussion. Histopathological evaluation

Following fixation with 10% neutral buffered formalin, Masson’s trichrome staining was performed on clot specimens obtained from three subjects. The stained sections were examined under both low- and high-power light microscopy by two authors (H.T. and T.S.), one of whom is a pathologist and the other a radiologist specializing in ultrasonography. In this specimen, fibrin was identified as pink linear structures. It was considered absent when fibrin was visually estimated to constitute less than approximately 20% of the fluid component.

Results

The findings are summarized in Tables 2 and 3 and Figs. 2 and 3. In both 24- and 72-h clots treated with US, analytical grayscale US imaging revealed hypoechoic changes. Among six subjects with clots, the number of 24-h clots changing to hypoechoic or strongly hypoechoic was zero under Condition 1, three under Condition 2, five under Condition 3, six under Condition 4, and five under Condition 5. The number of 72-h clots changing was zero under Condition 1, one under Condition 2, three under Condition 3, six under Condition 4, and three under Condition 5. The extent of hypoechoic areas was more pronounced in the group treated with Sonazoid in combination with US, compared to the group treated with US alone. Furthermore, 24-h clots exhibited more marked hypoechoic change than 72-h clots. Discussion

Enhanced thrombolysis was observed in blood samples from Japanese individuals residing in Japan when exposed to standard clinical US systems commonly used for abdominal diagnosis, which operate at a higher frequency than the low-frequency US typically employed in previous studies on US-accelerated thrombolysis. Acoustic thrombolysis occurs through the combined effects of radiation force and acoustic cavitation, which disrupt the fibrin network structure. Radiation force can induce fluid motion or acoustic streaming, thereby enhancing the mixing of thrombolytic agents [3, 9, 10]. Microbubbles enhance cavitation and improve thrombolytic efficacy [9–11]. And acoustic cavitation is more likely to occur at lower frequencies [9, 11]. Inertial cavitation, induced by high acoustic pressure, occurs when microbubbles collapse, generating liquid jets that strike the thrombus, facilitate the penetration of fibrinolytic enzymes into it, and cause mechanical damage. Stable cavitation, induced by low acoustic pressure, promotes strong fluid mixing through microstreaming, thereby facilitating the distribution of fibrinolytic enzymes and the removal of fibrin degradation products from the thrombus [9, 10].

The region within a thrombus undergoing thrombolysis appears hypoechoic on grayscale US [16]. Conversely, when the fibrin content of a thrombus increases, it becomes hyperechoic [17]. Numerous hypoechoic changes were observed in the US treatment group, with pronounced hypoechoic changes occurring more frequently in the Sonazoid administration group. In contrast, no changes were seen in controls.

In the absence of Sonazoid, hypoechoic changes were observed more frequently during color Doppler US (Condition 3) than during grayscale US (Condition 2). In color Doppler mode, pulse waves are added to grayscale imaging and operate at a lower frequency (2.5 MHz) than in grayscale mode (4.5 MHz). Low-frequency waves are more prone to inducing cavitation [9, 11]. Furthermore, grayscale mode uses a shorter pulse duration to increase axial resolution, whereas color Doppler mode uses a longer pulse duration to enhance sensitivity. Consequently, color Doppler mode applies a greater number of cycles within each pulse wave. Furthermore, Doppler signals are detected by transmitting multiple pulses along each scan line. Therefore, the addition of color Doppler US to grayscale US results in a higher MI. The combination of lower-frequency US and repeated wave emissions in color Doppler mode may have enhanced thrombolysis.

Unexpectedly, with Sonazoid, a greater number of hypoechoic changes were observed during grayscale US (Condition 4) than during color Doppler US (Condition 5). Sonazoid enhances thrombolysis by inducing cavitation at the thrombus surface [9, 10]. In our study, a 5-mm gap of heparin-saline was present between the transducer and the upper edge of the thrombus, and MI was set significantly higher than that typically used in diagnostic contrast imaging modes. Therefore, Sonazoid was rapidly destroyed before it could induce thrombolysis. This effect was prominent owing to the characteristics of color Doppler mode. In the next study, to effectively enhance thrombolysis with Sonazoid, it is essential to determine an appropriately low MI.

Masson’s trichrome staining revealed more light-blue amorphous areas and reduced fibrin content in US-treated clots compared to controls. A substantial amount of fibrin persisted in the control group. Fibrinolysis was observed in the US treatment group. Under all conditions, there was no rupture of red blood cells and no substantial evidence of cellular destruction. The extent of the hypoechoic areas observed on analytical US was greater than that of the light-blue amorphous regions in the histological specimens. The reason for this was shrinkage during specimen preparation, particularly during formalin fixation and dehydration. The fibrinolysis area was presumed to have undergone more substantial shrinkage. In our specimens, fibrinolysis within the light-blue amorphous elements was observed exclusively in areas surrounded by dense thrombus, while fibrinolysis on the thrombus surface could not be included in the specimens. We assumed that most of the anticoagulant factors present in plasma were consumed during the 24-h incubation at room temperature. A small amount of residual anticoagulant factors within the thrombus gaps acted on the fibrin network and induced fibrinolysis.

In our in vitro study, US exposure enhanced thrombolysis and the effect was further amplified when combined with microbubbles. These findings suggest potential clinical applicability. Thrombolysis is expected to occur to a greater extent in vivo than in vitro as physiologically present anticoagulant factors and plasminogen in plasma are continuously supplied through blood circulation. Sonazoid is intended for intravascular administration and remains intravascular, minimizing US effects on surrounding tissues.

The safety of acoustic thrombolysis is discussed below. Conditions 2 (grayscale US) and 3 (color Doppler US) without Sonazoid used MI values within the range specified for diagnostic US systems approved by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare of Japan, thereby ensuring safety. In Conditions 4 (grayscale US with Sonazoid) and 5 (color Doppler with Sonazoid), MI was significantly higher than that used for diagnostic contrast mode and has not been approved for safety. Doelare et al. [4] conducted a phase II trial of US-accelerated thrombolysis using microbubbles in 20 patients with peripheral arterial occlusions. Under scan conditions with an MI of 1.08 and a frequency of 1.8 MHz, transdermal US was applied for 1 h, and no serious adverse events were reported. This condition of acoustic thrombolysis therapy was safe at the MI value lower than that used in our in vitro experiments. Before initiating our clinical trial, it is necessary to determine the optimal low MI value to enhance safety and ensure that Sonazoid exerts its thrombolysis-enhancing effect.

At this stage of our in vitro study, as no red blood cell rupture was observed, it can be concluded that even at high MI values within the range of clinical US systems, Sonazoid poses no risk of hemolysis.

The clinical protocol we propose is outlined below. The primary target disease is limb venous thrombosis. Even when the lesion is located near the body surface, a convex transducer with an abdominal preset is employed to provide low-frequency US. The US focus is adjusted to the thrombus. Due to the typical physique of East Asians, US using an abdominal convex transducer is expected to provide sufficient access to deep extremity veins. Limb venous thrombosis is mainly treated with oral administration of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs). The time to maximum plasma concentration (Tmax) after oral intake depends on the specific drug, typically ranging from 1 to 3 h. US treatment is applied at the estimated Tmax. On the first day, Sonazoid is administered intravenously. One vial contains 16 μL of perflubutane microbubble, which is dissolved in 2 mL of diluent and injected intravenously in 0.5-mL increments, up to a maximum of one vial. From the second day onward, US irradiation is performed in color Doppler mode without Sonazoid. Our results indicate that the use of color Doppler mode is recommended.

During diagnostic US for limb venous thrombosis, the transducer should be applied gently to the skin surface to avoid dislodging the thrombus from the vessel wall, which could result in pulmonary thrombosis. Similarly, during acoustic thrombolysis treatment, the transducer should be gently placed on the skin.

It is not necessary to continue acoustic treatment until the thrombus is completely dissolved and disappears. If cracks form in the thrombus or its surface becomes irregular, the contact area with anticoagulants will increase, which is expected to enhance the thrombolytic effect. Under our protocol, since acoustic thrombolysis utilizes a diagnostic US system, the acoustic treatment should be performed under continuous real-time monitoring of thrombus morphology to ensure procedural safety. The procedure may be discontinued once the relevant findings are observed. Furthermore, acoustic thrombolysis should be terminated before a large thrombus detaches from the vessel wall to prevent pulmonary embolism.

Acoustic thrombolysis tends to occur more readily on the surface of the thrombus than between the thrombus and the vessel wall [9]. However, in the early stages of clinical trials, it remains uncertain whether the thrombus could detach from the vessel wall; therefore, patients with a right-to-left cardiac shunt were excluded.

This in vitro study demonstrated the efficacy of thrombolysis under high-frequency US conditions, which are generally less favorable for clot dissolution, using a standard clinical diagnostic US system. Previous studies involving White populations have shown that lower-frequency US facilitates thrombolysis. One possible contributing factor is the use of clots derived from Japan-dwelling Japanese. East Asians have a lower risk of atherothrombotic events than White populations but a higher susceptibility to severe bleeding complications, such as intracerebral hemorrhage, during thrombolysis therapy. Consequently, it is necessary to reduce the dosage of antithrombotic agents from the standard levels established for White populations [12–14]. Prasugrel (an antiplatelet agent) achieved comparable efficacy and safety, preventing serious bleeding complications, in East Asian patients with myocardial infarction, in whom the treatment was administered at only 37.5% of the standard dose established for other ethnic populations [13]. Furthermore, the traditional Japanese diet has been associated with reduced cardiovascular mortality [15]. US-enhanced thrombolysis may represent a promising strategy to reduce systemic exposure to thrombolytic agents, particularly in East Asian populations.

In recent studies on US-mediated thrombolysis, tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) has been encapsulated in nanocarriers such as lipid-based, polymeric, inorganic, or biologically derived vesicles. Following intravenous injection, US is applied to the thrombus site to induce cavitation of the nanocarriers, thereby facilitating the localized release of tPA [18, 19]. It will likely take several years before this can be applied clinically.

Despite being reported as effective in both preclinical and clinical trials involving White patients, it has not yet been established as a standard therapy. However, given the genetic predisposition of East Asians, the influence of the traditional Japanese diet, the use of standard clinical diagnostic US, the availability of already widely used US contrast agents, and a noninvasive approach involving transcutaneous US irradiation that requires no specialized training, this method has the potential to have therapeutic efficacy and be readily adopted in Japan.

Limitations

In living individuals, circulating plasma contains sufficient levels of plasmin, which contributes to fibrinolytic activity within the vascular system. In our in vitro experiments, fresh plasma was not utilized. If fresh plasma were to be used, it would have required collection at 24 and 72 h for thrombolysis assays under five conditions per time point. In this study, a standard clinical diagnostic US system equipped with a convex transducer, commonly used for abdominal examinations, was employed. Due to the relatively large contact surface of the transducer, the bath container for submerging the thrombus was designed with a wide upper opening. In Japan, whole blood donation is limited to 400 mL per session, and additional collection beyond this volume is not permitted within a short interval, even from healthy individuals.

At a high MI, the microbubbles were destroyed before cavitation could occur. The optimal low MI for efficient Sonazoid-enhanced thrombolysis remains unidentified. This in vitro study does not confirm the clinical safety of the method. East Asians’ unique thrombolytic profile limits extrapolation from animal studies or clinical trials in other populations.

Furthermore, all thrombi used in this study were obtained from Japan-dwelling Japanese individuals. Therefore, the results may not be generalizable to other ethnic populations or geographic regions.

Conclusion

This in vitro study demonstrated that standard clinical ultrasound, using grayscale or color Doppler modes commonly applied in abdominal diagnosis, with color Doppler being more effective than grayscale, can enhance thrombolysis in thrombi obtained from Japan-dwelling Japanese individuals. The addition of Sonazoid further amplified this effect. Both diagnostic US and Sonazoid are already widely used in clinical diagnosis in Japan. As such, this approach holds strong potential for rapid clinical implementation.
