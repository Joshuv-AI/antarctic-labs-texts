Relationship between esophageal and central venous pressure variation during respiratory activity: the best choice among four different methods to measure the amplitude of central venous pressure changes
Authors: Giorgia Ogliari, Letizia Mazzuca Mari, Serena Crisci, Giulia Pradissitto, Federica Fusina, Filippo Albani, Alessandro Morandi, Michele Bertelli, Giuseppe Natalini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Changes in lung and pleural pressures during mechanical ventilation are transmitted to intrathoracic structures, including the venous system. As a result, respiratory swings in central venous pressure (CVP) may reflect variations in pleural pressure, typically estimated using esophageal pressure (Pes). However, the point on the CVP waveform used for measurement may influence this relationship. Our aim is to find the best association between ∆Pes and each ∆CVP considering different points in the CVP waveform to calculate ∆CVP. In this prospective study, we analysed CVP and Pes waveforms in 22 mechanically ventilated patients. For each cardiac cycle, three CVP values were identified: the minimum (CVPmin), the maximum (CVPmax), and the average (CVPavg). From these, four types of respiratory CVP variation (∆CVP) were calculated per breath: ∆CVPmin = max-min of CVPmin values; ∆CVPmax = max-min of CVPmax values; ∆CVPavg = max-min of CVPavg values; ∆CVPtot = absolute difference between the highest CVPmax and the lowest CVPmin within each respiratory cycle. The primary objective was to assess the association between ∆Pes and each ∆CVP variant using linear mixed-effects models. A total of 2,286 breaths (median 104 per patient) was analysed. All ∆CVP measures showed a statistically significant association with ∆Pes. The strongest correlation was observed for ∆CVPtot, with a marginal R² of 0.50 and a conditional R² of 0.78. The association further improved when using the median of multiple consecutive breaths. Specifically, the marginal R² increased from 0.60 (median of 3 breaths) to 0.70 (median of 23 breaths). Among the different measurement strategies, ∆CVPtot—defined as the difference between the highest and lowest CVP values within a respiratory cycle—showed the strongest association with ∆Pes. Averaging over at least three consecutive breaths further enhanced this relationship. These data are useful for understanding how ∆CVP can be used to estimate ∆Pes at the bedside.

Background

Esophageal pressure (Pes), used as a surrogate for pleural pressure (Ppl), is a valuable tool in clinical settings to assess respiratory mechanics and inspiratory effort. It is particularly useful for understanding the pathophysiology of respiratory failure, guiding personalized weaning, and titrating pressure support [1]. The most common technique for measuring Pes requires the placement of an air-filled esophageal balloon catheter connected either to an accessory port of a mechanical ventilator or to a pressure transducer on a multiparameter monitor. Correct positioning of the catheter must be confirmed by an occlusion test [2] or by a chest radiograph. Although the utility of esophageal pressure monitoring is well described in literature, it is often considered a research tool and seldom used in clinical practice [3]. Changes in both lung and pleural pressure - induced by both spontaneous breathing and positive-pressure ventilation- are transmitted to other intrathoracic structures including the vascular system [4, 5]. In critical care patients’ central venous pressure (CVP) is commonly monitored through a central venous catheter placed in the superior vena cava. Due to its high compliance and anatomical proximity to the esophagus, the superior vena cava is significantly influenced by intrathoracic pressure changes. This may explain the observed similarity between respiratory swings in CVP and variations in Pes [6]. Based on this assumption, respiratory variations of CVP (∆CVP) could be used at bedside as surrogate of variations of Pes (∆Pes). This approach is attractive because central venous lines are more commonly used in intensive care units than esophageal balloon catheters.

Despite this potential, it remains unclear how to best measure CVP variations during the respiratory cycle. The CVP waveform is a complex signal that has well-defined components [7]: the “a” wave is the first positive wave determined by the atrial contraction; the second positive wave is the “c” wave, produced by buckling of tricuspid valves often hard to see. The other positive wave is the “v” wave produced by filling of the atrium during the ventricular systole. Then, there are two negative waves that respectively are the “x” and the “y”: the first one is produced by the atrial relaxation and the downward movement of the tricuspid valve in the systole and the second one is the result of the rapid ventricular filling in early diastole. Previous studies [9–17] have used different points on the CVP waveform to measure ∆CVP, but it remains unclear whether these methods yield comparable results, or which best reflects ∆Pes.

The aim of our study was to compare the association between changes of Pes (∆Pes) and ∆CVP when CVP is measured on different points on the CVP waveform.

Methods

Patients

This prospective study was conducted between August 2023 and June 2024 in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) of Fondazione Poliambulanza Hospital (Brescia, Italy). The protocol was approved by the local Ethics Committee (Comitato Etico della Provincia di Brescia; NP 5327). Written informed consent was obtained from the patient or, if the patient was not competent, from their next of kin.

Patients were included if they met all the following criteria: age > 18 years, tracheal intubation or tracheostomy, and the presence of both an esophageal balloon catheter and a central venous catheter already in place for clinical purposes. Pregnancy was the only exclusion criterion.

Protocol and measurements

Spirometry and hemodynamic data were recorded for 5 min during either spontaneous breathing or mechanical ventilation.

Patients were mechanically ventilated with Servo-U ventilator (Getinge AB, Göteborg, Sweden) and cardiovascular monitoring was performed by Draeger CV2300 system (Draeger, Drägerwerk AG & Co, Lübeck, Germany). Airway pressure, airflow, esophageal pressure (Pes), central venous pressure (CVP) and electrocardiogram waveforms were simultaneously recorded using Servo Tracker (Maquet, Solna, Sweden) and DataGrabber V0.07.111 (Draeger, Drägerwerk AG & Co, Lübeck, Germany) at a sampling rate of 100 Hz. All signals were converted and imported into R (RStudio 2024.09.0) for offline waveforms analysis.

Esophageal pressure was measured using an esophageal balloon catheter (Marquat Gbm, Boissy-St-Léger Cedex, France) connected to the auxiliary pressure port of the mechanical ventilator. The esophageal balloon was inserted 40 cm from the nostril and inflated with 1 ml of air. Proper estimation of pleural pressure variation (∆Ppl) was confirmed by an occlusion test [2] accepting a ratio between ∆Paw/∆Pes ratio between 0.8 and 1.2. CVP was measured through a central venous catheter with the distal tip positioned at the junction of the superior cava vein and the right atrium, as confirmed by chest X radiography.

For each cardiac cycle - defined as the interval between two consecutive R waves (or Q waves, when R waves could not be identified)— three CVP values were identified:

Measurement obtained in each respiratory cycle

Respiratory cycles were identified by the beginning of inspiratory flow. Respiratory cycles which included less than two complete cardiac cycles were excluded from the analysis. Breaths showing artifacts in esophageal pressure were also excluded. Briefly, the median value of the highest esophageal pressure at end expiration (Pes, ee) within each respiratory cycle was calculated for each recording. Breaths whose Pes, ee was greater than 2 cmH₂O or more than 20% of the median value of Pes, ee were automatically excluded.

Change in Pes (ΔPes) during a respiratory cycle was calculated as the difference between the maximum and the minimum Pes value.

Outcome

The primary outcome was to identify the strongest association between ΔPes and ΔCVP by comparing the relationships between ΔPes and ΔCVPavg, ΔPes and ΔCVPmax, ΔPes and ΔCVPmin and ΔPes and ΔCVPtot.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as frequency (percentage), mean (standard deviation), or median (interquartile range), as appropriate. Comparisons were made using non-parametric tests.

The association between ΔPes and ΔCVPavg, ΔCVPmax, ΔCVPmin and ΔCVPtot was assessed using linear mixed models with ΔPes as the dependent variable, ΔCVP as a fixed effect, and patients as a random effect. The four models were compared using Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC) and marginal R2. Agreement between ΔCVPtot and ΔPes was assessed using Bland–Altman analysis. Concordance was further evaluated using the concordance correlation coefficient. Additionally, we analysed the relationship between ΔCVP and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) and between ΔCVP and dynamic lung compliance (CLdyn). Dynamic transpulmonary pressure (PLdyn) was calculated as the difference between airway pressure (Paw) and esophageal pressure (Pes), and dynamic lung compliance was calculated as the ratio of tidal volume to dynamic transpulmonary pressure (CLdyn = VT / PLdyn).The primary analysis was performed using a “breath-by-breath” approach, in which each pair of ΔPes and its corresponding ΔCVP was analysed.

Subsequently, we investigated whether using the median value of multiple consecutive breaths could improve the association between ΔPes and ΔCVP. For this purpose, the best-performing model was reassessed using the median values of ∆Pes and the corresponding ∆CVP, calculated over progressively increasing numbers of consecutive breaths (ranging from 2 to 30).

A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed with R (R Core Team, 2022. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Discussion

Our results demonstrate that the association between changes in esophageal and central venous pressure induced by the respiratory cycle depends on the method to measure ΔCVP. Although ventilatory-associated variations in CVP can be measured using different landmarks on the CVP waveform, the best relationship between ΔPes and ΔCVP was obtained by measuring the swing between the highest to the lowest value of CVP during a respiratory cycle (ΔCVPtot).

The amplitude of CVP variation associated with respiratory rhythm has been assessed with different methods in previous studies. One study used an approach similar to our ΔCVPmax [9], while two studies measured ΔCVP similarly to ΔCVPmin [10, 11] and one study used a method comparable to ΔCVPavg [12]. Other studies adopted the base of the “c” waves as the value of CVP in each cardiac cycle [13, 14], a method yielding results similar to ΔCVPavg (see Fig. 1). Instead, three studies did not clearly describe their method making comparisons difficult [15–17]. In these studies, the strength of the relationship between ΔPes and ΔCVP expressed by the R2 value ranges from 0.14 to 0.81. This could be due mainly to the heterogeneity in ΔCVP measurement method. For this reason, previous studies cannot be directly compared with each other or with our findings. Future research in this field should adopt standardized approaches to measuring ∆CVP. Among the tested methods, ΔCVPtot appears preferable not only because it yields the strongest relationship with ΔPes, but also because it is the simplest and most reproducible bedside method. Unlike other approaches, it does not require identification of CVP values in each cardiac cycle; only the highest and lowest values during the respiratory cycle are needed.

In our study, conditional R2 values, which account for both fixed and random effects, ranged from 0.61 to 0.78, depending on the method used to measure ∆CVP. Although ΔCVPtot demonstrated a modest mean bias relative to ΔPes, the wide limits of agreement observed in the Bland–Altman analysis suggest that this method currently lacks sufficient accuracy for clinical decision. Future studies will explore whether different approaches may reduce variability and improve agreement; for example, our group is now working on a calibrated method a “calibrated method” to estimate Pes from CVP using a coefficient equal to the ratio ΔPaw to ΔCVP obtained through an occlusion test.Considering only fixed effect, the relationship between ΔPes and ΔCVPtot showed a marginal R2 of 0.5. We are confident that this relationship is reliable even in the clinical field, because this value was obtained analysing a very wide range of ΔPes, from 2 to 61 cmH2O. This was obtained by recruiting both patients with optimal inspiratory support and patients at the end of a spontaneous breathing trial, both successful and failed. Furthermore, the relationship improved when ΔPes and ΔCVP were analysed not breaths-by-breaths but as the median of multiple consecutive breaths. Using the median of just three breaths increased the marginal R² from 0.5 to 0.6; with six breaths, it reached 0.65. Although an R² of 0.7 was achievable using 23 breaths (approximately one minute of data), this may be more feasible for offline rather than bedside analysis. These findings support the use of median values over 3–6 breaths to enhance clinical reliability.

Interindividual variability in the ΔPes and ΔCVP relationship (Fig. 2) may reflect differences in pleural pressure transmission to the intravascular compartment. Factors such as right atrial compliance, transmural pressure, chest wall and lung compliance, and baseline pleural and transpulmonary pressures can all influence this relationship [6]. Two pediatric studies attempted to calibrate ∆CVP and ΔPes on ΔPpl. They proposed to calculate a corrected ΔCVP value obtained by multiplying the actual ΔCVP by the ratio ΔPaw/ΔCVP obtained during an inspiration or chest compression with end-expiratory airway occlusion [9, 11]. However, these analyses used suboptimal methods for measuring ΔCVP (ΔCVP with ΔCVPmax or ΔCVPmin).

An important consideration when interpreting our results is the heterogeneity of our study population. Many patients were not admitted with a primary respiratory diagnosis, which introduces both a limitation and a strength. On one hand, we cannot draw conclusions regarding specific pulmonary or cardiovascular conditions. On the other hand, this increases the generalizability of our findings to the broader ICU population. Future research should examine patients with more homogeneous respiratory pathologies to assess whether disease severity affects pleural-to-intravascular pressure transmission.

This study has several limitations that must be acknowledged. Firstly, the single-center design and the relatively small sample limit the generalizability. Secondly, in our study, we did not systematically screen patients for severe tricuspid regurgitation or right heart failure; therefore, the potential impact of these conditions on our results cannot be precisely quantified, and we acknowledge this as a limitation of the study. Nevertheless, among the assessed methods for ΔCVP measurement, we believe that the approach showing the strongest association with ΔPes variations (ΔCVPtot) is theoretically less sensitive to CVP waveform alterations related to tricuspid regurgitation or right-sided heart failure. This is because ΔCVPtot relies exclusively on the maximal and minimal CVP values within the respiratory cycle and does not depend on predefined CVP waveform landmarks, which are more susceptible to distortion in these conditions.

Additionally, the study only included patients undergoing esophageal pressure monitoring—a practice more common in our centre for patients with ARDS or prolonged weaning. This introduces potential selection bias.

Further multicentre studies involving broader patient populations are needed to validate and extend our findings.

Conclusion

Our study confirms a significant association between ∆Pes and ∆CVP during the respiratory cycle. Among various CVP measurement methods, the difference between the highest and lowest CVP values within each respiratory cycle (∆CVPtot) showed the strongest and most consistent correlation with ∆Pes. Furthermore, using the median of at least three consecutive breaths significantly improves this relationship, supporting the clinical utility of ∆CVP as a surrogate for pleural pressure variation at the bedside.
