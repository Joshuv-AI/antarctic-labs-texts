Myocardial protection during cardiac surgery: Scientific principles and practical recommendations
Authors: Pedro J del Nido
Published: 2026

Pedro J. del Nido, MD

Central Message

Effective myocardial protection depends less on allegiance to a specific formula than on reliable arrest, hypothermia, buffering, and redosing tailored to myocardial substrate and ischemic duration.

Myocardial protection remains one of the most important determinants of outcome in cardiac surgery. The problem is deceptively simple: the heart must be rendered still and ischemic to permit repair, yet it must recover promptly and completely when coronary flow is restored. Despite decades of investigation, no single cardioplegic strategy has emerged as universally superior, and variation in practice remains wide. That variation is understandable. Myocardial protection is not a single intervention but a coordinated strategy involving electromechanical arrest, hypothermia, buffering, substrate management, route of delivery, and judgment about when protection is beginning to fail. Institutional review board approval and patient consent were not required for this work.

My interest in developing a different cardioplegic solution arose in the early 1990s from a practical concern: formulations developed largely for adult hearts were being applied to infants and neonates with little physiologic justification. The immature myocardium differs from the adult heart in metabolism, calcium handling, and tolerance of ischemia. Our goal was not to create a proprietary formula but to design a protective strategy grounded in cellular biology rather than convention. The formulation that emerged, now known as del Nido cardioplegia, was built on a few governing ideas: rapidly arrest the heart, reducing energy consumption, limitng intracellular sodium and calcium accumulation, maintaining buffering capacity, and preserving the conditions for metabolic recovery after reperfusion.1,2

After more than 30 years of clinical use, the most important lesson is not that one formulation is always best, it is that the surgeon should understand why a strategy works, because only then can it be adapted intelligently when the standard approach is insufficient.

What Injures the Heart During Surgical Ischemia

Normal myocardial contractile function depends on tightly regulated transmembrane ionic gradients: a sodium gradient of approximately 10:1 (extracellular to intracellular, ∼140 mM vs ∼14 mM) and a calcium gradient ranging from approximately 1000:1 at peak systole to 10,000-20,000:1 at diastolic rest (∼2 mM extracellular vs ∼1 μM systolic and ∼100 nM diastolic intracellular free Ca2+).3 These gradients are maintained by continuous ATP-dependent membrane transport and are essential to both excitation-contraction coupling and relaxation.

During ischemia, ATP production falls quickly. The Na+/K+-ATPase can no longer maintain the normal transmembrane sodium gradient, intracellular sodium increases, and calcium accumulates through exchanger-mediated mechanisms and impaired sequestration. Calcium overload is central to irreversible injury. It promotes contracture, disrupts mitochondrial function, activates destructive enzymes, and impairs recovery even when coronary flow is restored.4

At the same time, anaerobic glycolysis from endogenous sources such as glycogen becomes the major remaining source of ATP. This can be beneficial, particularly in the immature heart, but only if intracellular acidosis is buffered. Hydrogen ion accumulation rapidly inhibits glycolytic flux, making buffering capacity a primary rather than secondary feature of any cardioplegic strategy intended for prolonged ischemia. Our earlier experimental work emphasized this point, and it remains underappreciated in clinical discussions of myocardial protection.

These mechanisms are not expressed equally in all hearts. Hypertrophied ventricles have reduced capillary density relative to muscle mass and are particularly vulnerable to subendocardial ischemia. Chronically ischemic hearts may enter cross-clamp with depleted reserve. Senescent myocardium demonstrates impaired calcium handling and reduced mitochondrial reserve. Cyanotic and previously operated hearts may present additional problems of edema, collateral runoff, and uneven distribution of cardioplegia. For that reason, a rigid protocol is less useful than a principled strategy.

Aims of Myocardial Protection

From the pathophysiology above, 4 protective imperatives emerge: reduce oxygen demand; prevent intracellular sodium and calcium accumulation; prevent mitochondrial swelling and disruption; and maintain high-energy phosphate stores.5,6 Every component of a rational cardioplegia strategy should address 1 or more of these objectives.

Hypothermia accomplishes more toward these aims than any other single intervention. The Q10 effect means that cooling to below 20 °C reduces myocardial oxygen consumption by more than 90% compared with normothermia. It slows the rate of ionic gradient dissipation, preserves ATP stores, and exerts its own independent cardioplegic effect at very low temperatures. This is not an adjunct to cardioplegia—it is the cornerstone of protection. Cold hearts tolerate ischemia far better than warm ones, regardless of which pharmacological agent is used.

Cardiac arrest eliminates the energy cost of contraction and greatly slows ionic pump activity. Hyperkalemia, the most commonly used arresting strategy, depolarizes the myocyte membrane and achieves diastolic arrest. However, depolarized arrest permits continued sodium and calcium entry through exchange mechanisms. The addition of sodium channel blockers such as lidocaine counteracts these effects by maintaining the cell membrane in a more polarized state, limiting sodium influx and secondarily preventing the sodium-driven calcium overload that characterizes purely hyperkalemic arrest.7,8

The del Nido Formulation: Rationale for Each Component

The crystalloid component is mixed with autologous blood in a 4:1 ratio (crystalloid:blood), yielding a delivery hematocrit of approximately 6% to 8%.1 The base solution is Plasma-Lyte A, an electrolyte solution approximating extracellular fluid composition (sodium 140 mEq/L, potassium 5 mEq/L, magnesium 3 mEq/L, pH 7.4). Of note, Ringer's solution can be substituted for Plasma-Lyte A with expected equal effectiveness.9 The key pharmacologic components and broader principles of this protective strategy are summarized in Table 1.

Potassium chloride (final concentration approximately 24 mEq/L) provides the primary depolarizing arrest. The chosen concentration achieves diastolic arrest within seconds while limiting the more severe metabolic consequences of higher potassium concentrations.

Lidocaine (final concentration approximately 100 mg/L) is the defining component. As a sodium channel blocker, it reduces sodium entry during depolarized arrest, limiting the sodium-calcium exchange-mediated calcium overload that is the principal mechanism of ischemic cell death.7,8 A 2009 study by O'Brien and colleagues10 demonstrated that del Nido cardioplegia resulted in significantly lower intracellular calcium accumulation and lower postoperative troponin T levels compared with standard strategies, providing direct mechanistic validation of this approach.

Magnesium sulfate acts as a natural calcium channel antagonist, competing with calcium at sarcolemmal binding sites and further limiting calcium entry while stabilizing membrane excitability.5

Sodium bicarbonate addresses acidosis generated by anaerobic glycolysis, maintaining a favorable intracellular pH that allows glycolytic ATP production to continue. Red blood cells in the 20% blood component provide an additional and physiologically powerful buffering mechanism through erythrocyte carbonic anhydrase—which we believe represents the primary benefit of the blood component, not oxygen delivery, which is minimal at this dilution.1,11

Mannitol serves as a hyperosmolar agent limiting interstitial edema and as a free radical scavenger counteracting the oxidative burst of reperfusion injury.

Notably absent from this formulation is exogenous calcium. The trace calcium contributed by the 20% blood component is sufficient to prevent the calcium paradox while avoiding the compounding injury from supraphysiologic calcium during arrest. Also absent is exogenous glucose, as it is co-transported with sodium into ischemic cardiac myocytes, potentially contributing to ischemic damage.1

Single-Dose Strategy: Rationale and Limits

The standard dose is 20 to 30 mL/kg delivered at 8 to 12 °C over 1 to 2 minutes via antegrade aortic root infusion, providing protection extending to approximately 60 minutes of ischemia in most patients.1,12 The rationale for single-dose administration is grounded in the biology of the coronary microcirculation. Repeated cardioplegia infusions cause iterative endothelial injury through mechanical shear forces, cold stress, and hyperkalemia; inflammatory cell adhesion is activated; and coronary microvascular function progressively deteriorates.11 In the neonatal and infant heart, where the coronary capillary bed is exquisitely sensitive, this cumulative injury impairs reperfusion well beyond the operative period. Single-dose cardioplegia minimizes these insults and preserves the microvasculature that ultimately determines postoperative ventricular recovery. The rate of delivery is equally important: rapid administration with resultant supraphysiologic perfusion pressure damages endothelial cells and capillary beds. For infants, a delivery pressure at the aortic root of 30 to 40 mm Hg is appropriate; for older children 40 to 50 mm Hg is ideal; and 50 to 60 mm Hg is ideal in adults.1

The benefit of single-dose administration, however, must be balanced against the risk of inadequate protection beyond 60 minutes. A supplemental dose of 10 mL/kg should be administered at 60 minutes whenever the crossclamp is expected to extend substantially further, or earlier in high-risk substrates. The decision to redose should be driven by clinical signs: electrical activity on the electrocardiogram, myocardial warming detected by topical temperature monitoring, or ventricular distension. The heart reliably signals when protection is waning; recognizing these signs in time is a fundamental surgical skill.

Expanding Application to Adult Cardiac Surgery

The cellular mechanisms targeted by this formulation—sodium and calcium overload during depolarized arrest, mitochondrial injury from progressive calcium accumulation and acidosis, and reperfusion-associated oxidative stress—are universal pathophysiologic processes, not unique to the immature heart. In pediatric practice, the appeal of a single-dose strategy is not simply convenience. It reduces interruption of the operation, avoids repeated manipulations during delicate reconstruction, and may be especially useful in the immature heart, where repeated ischemia-reperfusion cycles and repeated exposure of the microvasculature to cold cardioplegia may be undesirable.

Over the past decade, adult cardiac surgical programs have adopted del Nido cardioplegia with increasing enthusiasm. Multiple observational studies and propensity-matched analyses have demonstrated safety and efficacy in coronary artery bypass grafting and valve surgery.13,14 The principal reported advantage is the simplicity of single-dose administration, particularly advantageous during minimally invasive procedures where re-dosing is technically cumbersome.

The landmark prospective randomized trial by Ad and colleagues,15 published in 2018, provided the first level I evidence that del Nido solution achieves at least comparable if not superior clinical outcomes to standard blood cardioplegia in adult patients undergoing routine cardiac operations. Nonetheless, unreflective adoption in any setting is inadvisable. For adult patients with severely depressed ventricular function, acute coronary occlusion, or severe hypertrophy, thoughtful modification of dosing strategy is required. The goal in every case is protection tailored to the patient, not protocol adherence for its own sake.

Practical Recommendations

1.Prioritize hypothermia. A delivery temperature of 8 to 12 °C is the most important single determinant of protection. No pharmacologic additive compensates for inadequate cooling. Topical cooling of the epicardium should also be used in all but the briefest procedures. Maintaining myocardial hypothermia is most challenging when the heart is surrounded by normothermic tissue, such as in endoscopic or minimally invasive procedures. Here topical cooling, if practical, is an effective way to maintain protection; alternatively, global hypothermia, as commonly used in pediatric cardiac surgery, or early redosing of cardioplegia should be considered.

When homogeneous distribution cannot be assumed—in patients with multivessel coronary disease, prior bypass grafts, or after aortotomy—retrograde delivery via the coronary sinus (maintaining infusion pressure below 30 mm Hg), infusion through completed grafts, or direct coronary ostial cannulation ensures adequate perfusion of vulnerable segments.16 Combining antegrade and retrograde delivery is frequently the most reliable strategy for complex cases.

5.Recognize the high-risk patient. Uniform application of any protocol regardless of patient substrate is a conceptual error. When a procedure is expected to exceed 90 to 120 minutes, a multidose approach planned from the outset is more prudent than reactive redosing. Staged repair, when anatomy permits, is always preferable to extending a single ischemic interval beyond physiologic tolerance.

Conclusions

Myocardial protection is not a solved problem, but its governing principles are well established. The heart tolerates surgical ischemia best when it is rapidly arrested, kept cold, buffered against acidosis, protected from sodium- and calcium-mediated injury, and reassessed before protection begins to fail. del Nido cardioplegia embodies these principles in a clinically practical formulation—single-dose, blood-based, and modified hyperpolarizing—designed to achieve rapid arrest, maintain it with minimal metabolic cost, and enable complete functional recovery. It was developed for the immature heart from these principles and has proved useful far beyond that setting. The most important lesson from more than 30 years of clinical application is this: the surgeon who understands why a protection strategy works—not merely how to execute it—is best equipped to modify it intelligently when the standard approach is insufficient.
