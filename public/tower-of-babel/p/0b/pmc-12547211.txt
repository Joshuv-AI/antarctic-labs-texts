Chlorogenic acid reverses growth inhibition by suppressing the hypothalamic COX-2 signaling pathway in broilers under LPS-induced immune stress
Authors: Dongying Bai, Wenrui Zhen, Kexin Liu, Xueqing Xiao, Haiqiu Tan, Xiaodi Hu, Xiqiang Ma, Xiaolin Xie, Yi Zhang, Fangshen Guo, Koichi Ito, Bingkun Zhang, Cai Zhang, Yanbo Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOur previous research found that when broilers were subjected to LPS-induced immunological stimulation, the Cyclooxygenase-2(COX-2)/Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2)/EP4 pathway was activated in the hypothalamus, leading to decreased production performance. In this study, we investigated the effects of chlorogenic acid (CGA) on the COX-2-related signaling pathway in the hypothalamus of LPS-stressed broilers to explore the mechanism of CGA’s improvement in the growth of broilers under immune stress conditions. A total of 336 one-day-old Arbor Acres broilers were randomly assigned to four treatment groups with six replicates of 14 broilers each. The treatment groups were: (i) Saline: broilers injected i.p. with saline and fed with basal diet; (ii) LPS: broilers injected with LPS and fed with basal diet; (iii) 0.1 % CGA: broilers injected with saline and fed basal diet plus 0.1 % CGA; and (iv) 0.1 % CGA+LPS: broilers injected with LPS and fed basal diet plus 0.1 % CGA. Broilers in the four treatment groups were intraperitoneally injected with the same concentration of LPS or saline from 14 days of age for three consecutive days. The lower average daily feed intake (ADFI) and average daily gain (ADG), as well as the increased feed conversion ratio (FCR) in LPS-challenged birds, were alleviated by CGA supplementation (P < 0.05). Dietary CGA mitigated the elevated serum corticosterone (CORT) and TNF-α level (P < 0.05), and the decreased serum IGF-1 concentration (P < 0.05). Further validation by RT-qPCR showed that dietary CGA significantly downregulated the LPS-induced mRNA expression of TLR1B, MyD88, SOCS3, CD40 and STAT1 in the Toll-like receptor signaling pathway (P < 0.05), reducing subsequent COX-2 expression and up-regulating the gene expression of NPY and GHRH (P < 0.05). The results demonstrated that CGA could alleviate LPS-induced immune stress in broilers and that the mechanism was related to inhibition of the hypothalamic COX-2 pathway.Keywords: lipopolysaccharide, chlorogenic acid, hypothalamus, Cyclooxygenase-2, chickens

Introduction

Immunological stress occurs when broilers develop bacterial or viral infections during production (Li et al., 2018). This stress can lead to adverse behavioral and metabolic changes resulting in growth inhibition. Previous studies have shown that stress can cause intestinal inflammation and disrupt the body’s redox balance, thereby affecting production performance (Tan, et al., 2023; Zhong, et al., 2024).Therefore, immune stress is considered to be a major cause of the failure to reach optimal feed utilization, ideal growth performance, and meat quality, resulting in substantial losses to poultry producers (He, et al., 2022; Zhu, et al., 2022). Enhancing antistress capacity in poultry via feed supplementation with Chinese herbal medicine can reduce inflammation and improve appetite for feed (Basiouni, et al., 2023; Liu, et al., 2024; Yu, et al., 2023). Natural plant (Chinese herbal medicine) extracts are known for their multicomponent, no known bacterial resistance, and low toxicity(Eldin, et al., 2023; Pan and Kong, 2018).

Chlorogenic acid (CGA) is a polyphenol compound found in diverse plants such as honeysuckle, Eucommia ulmoides and coffee (Clifford, et al., 2006; Nguyen, et al., 2024; Tan et al., 2023). Reports have shown that CGA is beneficial because of its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and antibacterial properties, but recent research has centered on its effects in mitigating inflammatory bowel disease and strengthening the intestinal barrier function (Bagdas, et al., 2020; Goya, et al., 2024; Ji, et al., 2013; Kim, et al., 2012; Lee, et al., 2012; Park, et al., 2015; Xu, et al., 2010). Few studies have investigated CGA’s positive effects on broiler performance, especially in chickens under stress from bacterial infection. The mechanism of CGA action is not well understood, and the complex interactions in multicomponent plant extracts has made comparisons among various types of animal and interpretations difficult.

In a previous study, we found that LPS-induced immune stress adversely affected broiler chicken growth through the COX-2/PGE2/EP4 pathway (Liu, et al., 2023) and its influence on immunity, inflammation, and appetite. The COX-2/PGE2 pathway activation promotes the release of inflammatory cytokines from microglia through interaction with the EP4 receptor. The PGE2 binding to EP4 can also upregulate neuropeptide Y (NPY) which enhances appetite and growth hormone releasing hormone (GHRH), which reduces the level of insulin-like growth factor-1 in the serum of broiler chickens under stress (Liu et al., 2023). However, whether CGA can improve the body's inflammatory response, appetite, and secretion of GHRH by interfering with COX-2 signaling, thereby alleviating immune stress and enhancing production performance is not known with certainty. In this study, we determined the growth performance, serum factors such as CORT, IGF-1 and TNF-α concentration of broilers to investigate whether chlorogenic acid has the effects of promoting growth and improving immune function of immune stressed broilers. In addition, we detected the changes in the transcriptome and expression of genes related to the COX-2 signaling pathway of the hypothalamus in broilers to elucidate the immunomodulatory properties of dietary CGA and its mechanism of growth regulation in immune stressed broilers.

Material and methods

Ethics approval

The animal protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Henan University of Science and Technology (DWFL36891-2023), and experimental procedures followed IACUC guidelines.

Experimental design, diets, and management

A total of 336 one-day-old male broilers in good health and with similar body weight (37 ± 0.5 g) purchased from a commercial broiler hatchery (Henan Quanda Poultry Breeding Co.) were randomly allocated into four groups with six replicates per group and fourteen broilers per replicate. Broiler followed the AA broiler management guide. The four groups were: (1) saline, (2) LPS, (3) 0.1 % CGA, and (4) 0.1 % CGA+LPS. The birds were fed the standard diet met the requirements of the National Research Council 1994, and the composition of the feed was the same as the previous experiment (Tan et al., 2023). The basic diet was purchased from Fanda Feed Co. (Luoyang, China). Broilers in 0.1 % CGA and 0.1 % CGA+LPS groups received the standard feed supplemented with 0.1 % CGA. CGA (98 %) was obtained from Changsha Shanghe Biotechnology Co. and mixed with the standard diet. Broilers in the LPS and 0.1 % CGA+LPS groups were injected intraperitoneally with LPS solution prepared with saline every morning according to the body weight of each chicken, whereas animals in other groups were injected with saline only. The LPS used for challenge in LPS and 0.1 % CGA+LPS groups were derived from Escherichia coli (Sigma-Aldrich, U.S.).LPS solution prepared with saline every morning according to the body weight of each chicken. At 14 days of age, the broilers in groups 2 and 4 were intraperitoneally injected with LPS solution (0.5 mg/kg·BW). The control and 0.1 % CGA, groups 1 and 3, received i.p. injections of the same volume of saline. The injections were given on three consecutive days. Lighting at 23L:1D was provided during the 17-day experimental period, and temperature was maintained at 32-34°C during the initial three days, then lowered 2-3°C/wk to a final 24°C. Relative Humidity (RH) was controlled at 60-65 %. Feed and water were available ad libitum. Broilers were raised in 3-layer ladder cages (80cm* 60cm* 60 cm) and cages were placed in the terrace of a mechanically ventilated room under controlled temperature, humidity, and lighting conditions.

Growth performance

At 14, 15 and 17 days of age, one bird from each cage was weighed and total feed consumed was determined for calculating average daily gain, average daily feed intake and feed conversion ratio.

Hypothalamic sample collection

At 2, 4, 24 and 72 hours after the first LPS injection, one chicken with BW closer to the average weight of the replicate was selected and euthanized by cervical dislocation. After decapitation, the skull was carefully cut open by ophthalmic surgery to expose the intact brain. The whole brain was removed with the blunt tip of a scalpel, blotted with filter paper, wrapped in aluminum foil, the hypothalamus was isolated by placing the whole brain on crushed ice, and the resected hypothalamus was placed into a ribonuclease-free centrifuge tube, quickly frozen in liquid N2, and stored at −80°C for later transcriptome sequencing.

Serum concentration of CORT, TNF-α, and IGF-1

Blood was allowed to clot, and serum was separated by centrifugation at 4°C, 3,000 × g for 20 min and stored at −20°C for later assay. Contents of CORT, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) were measured by ELISA kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Inst., Nanjing, Jinagsu, PRC) according to manufacturer’s protocol.

Sequence analysis

Two hours after the first injection, four hypothalami were selected at random, one from each group, and analyzed by transcriptome sequencing (Nanjing Parsono Technology Co., Ltd., China). Total RNA was isolated from hypothalamic samples using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen Inc., CA, U.S.). Total RNA was purified with SuperScript II reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen, USA) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina Hiseq platform. Raw data were filtered and quality controlled, and filtered clean data were screened against the chicken reference genome; HISAT2 was used for alignment efficiency and to map sequence information on the reference genome. Significantly differentially expressed genes were identified using DESeq2 with criteria, [Log2(Fold Change)] ≥ 1 and P < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment were performed with Rscript. Fisher’s exact test followed by a Bonferroni multiple test were used to calculate the percentage of false positives. The sequence data were deposited in the NCBI SRA database (No. PRJNA940969).

RT-qPCR verification

Total RNA was isolated from the hypothalamus with TRIzol reagent and purity was evaluated by 260/280. A 1st-strand cDNA synthesis kit (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) was used for reverse transcription. The target genes and primers are given in Table 1. Real-time quantitative PCR was conducted using an RT-qPCR kit (Toyobo) in a Roche LightCycle (Shanghai, China). The amplification conditions included a hold stage for one cycle of 95°C for 5 min, a PCR stage with 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 30 s. Data were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCT method.

Statistical analyses

For data processing, we used SPSS ver. 21.0 for Windows (SPSS Inc., Chicago, US) with using one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s test for multiple comparisons. P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Production performance

As shown in Figure 1, CGA alleviated the inhibition of the ADFI in stressed broilers at 14-15d and15-17d, and reached a significant level at 15-17d (P < 0.05) (Figure 1B). CGA also significantly increased the ADG in stressed broilers at 14-15d (P < 0.05) (Figure 1A). The CGA improvement in FCR mainly occurred at 14-15d, and at that growth stage, the FCR in stressed broilers was significantly increased, but the CGA supplement significantly decreased the FCR (P < 0.05) (Figure 1C).

Effect of CGA on production performance of stressed broilers. Values are expressed as mean ± SEM (n = 6). Bars with different letters differed significantly (P < 0.05).

Serum parameters

As shown in Figure 2, relative to the control group, serum corticosterone (CORT) concentration at 17d and TNF-α concentration at 15d in stressed broilers were increased (P < 0.05), whereas serum IGF-1 concentration at 2 h after the first injection was decreased (P < 0.05). Adding 0.1 % CGA to the diet reduced the expression of CORT and TNF-α and restored the IGF-1 level in the serum (P < 0.05) (Figure 2A–C).

Effect of CGA on the serum indices of stressed broilers. Serum levels of (A) CORT, (B) IGF-1 and (C) TNF-α in broilers. Values are expressed as mean ± SEM (n = 6). Bars with different letters differed significantly (P < 0.05).

Hypothalamic transcriptome

The transcriptome results showed a total of 141 genes identified in the 0.1 % CGA + LPS group with of 43 genes upregulated and 98 genes downregulated compared with the non-supplemented LPS group (Figure 3A). Differentially-expressed genes were subjected to cluster analysis, and it can be seen from the heatmap that the gene expression levels of pairs of samples in the group tended to be the same, indicating that the biological repeatability was good (Figure 3B). Nine significantly enriched KEGG pathways were identified in the 0.1 % CGA + LPS group versus the LPS group: influenza A, Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, NOD-like receptor signaling, ECM-receptor interaction, phagosome formation, retinol metabolism, cytosolic DNA-sensing, and necroptosis. Dietary CGA significantly inhibited the TLR signaling pathway, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and NOD-like receptor signaling induced by LPS (Figure 3C). Eight differentially expressed genes related to growth and immunity were selected for validation, and the results are shown in Figure 4. Addition of CGA to a final concentration of 0.1 % significantly downregulated the expression of the toll-like Receptor 1B (TLR1B), myeloid differentiation primary response gene 88 (MYD88), CD40, signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1), suppressor of cytokine signaling 3 (SOCS3), and prostaglandin synthase 2 (PTGS2) (P < 0.05) genes and significantly upregulated the expression of the NPY and GHRH (P < 0.05) genes in the hypothalamus (Figure 4A–H).

Discussion

Our measurement of the production performance of broilers at different growth stages revealed that CGA can significantly alleviate the decline in production performance of broilers caused by immune stress. Insulin-like growth factor 1, which is at the terminus of the growth axis composed of the hypothalamus and the pituitary glands, is an important regulator of metabolism, body growth and development (Junnila, et al., 2013). In this study, we found that the immune stress significantly decreased the serum IGF-1 concentration in broilers, and CGA could reversed this trend. The changes in IGF-1 and the changes in production performance are consistent.

The serum CORT level is an accurate marker of stress in poultry (Scanes, 2016) and in our experiments, we found higher CORT concentrations in immune stressed birds. As a key pro-inflammatory cytokine, tumor necrosis factor α activates a cytokine cascade in the presence of an infection, involving the synthesis and release of interleukins and secondary inflammatory mediators (Akdis, et al., 2016). In this study, the serum concentration of TNF-α was significantly increased in LPS injected broilers, which is consistent with the data of Wang (Wang, et al., 2016), confirming that LPS treatment induces immune stress in broilers. Dietary CGA supplementation can reduce the LPS-induced increase in serum CORT and TNF-α in broilers, thus supporting our hypothesis that CGA could alleviate LPS-induced immune stress.

To further understand the protective effects of CGA on broiler growth, a transcriptome analysis was performed to reveal the differentially expressed genes in the hypothalamus. Based on KEGG enrichment profiling, the Toll-like receptor signaling pathway, cytokine and cytokine receptor interaction, the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway and the MAPK signaling pathway were found to participate in growth inhibition induced by immune stress. CGA’s mitigating effects in immune stressed broilers included downregulation of the Toll-like receptor signaling pathway, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and NOD-like receptor signaling pathway.

The TLR and MAPK signaling pathways are closely related to the COX-2 pathway (Xia, et al., 2015; Zhao et al., 2019). Activated NF-κB and p38/MAPK can upregulate the expression of inflammatory mediators and enzymes associated with inflammation, resulting in increased COX-2 expression. Our results showed that COX-2 synthesis occurred in synchrony with the activity of the microglial surface activator CD40 in the hypothalamus of LPS-injected broilers. Therefore, COX-2-induced upregulation of inflammatory mediators could involve microglial signaling pathways. We hypothesized that when broilers are injected with LPS, it binds to TLR1 on microglia to activate Toll-like receptor signaling pathway. When TLR1 is activated, it self-dimerizes and binds to MYD88. Our previous research reported that LPS administration activated the MAPK-NF-κB pathway though the TLR1 receptor and MyD88, and increased the secretion of COX-2 in the hypothalamus of stressed chickens (Liu et al., 2023). NF-κB enters the nucleus and binds to specific DNA response elements, thereby promoting gene transcription. At the same time, MAPK promotes the transcription of inflammatory factors by regulating the downstream STAT1 factor, resulting in elevated release of COX-2 and downstream synthesis of PGE2 and EP4. PGE2 binds to the EP4 receptor on microglia and triggers a positive feedback loop that amplifies COX-2 activity, contributing to the overall inflammatory response to LPS (Liu et al., 2023).

The immune system and neuroendocrine system are closely interconnected. LPS-induced inflammatory cytokines can target microglia in the hypothalamus and induce the synthesis of high levels of COX-2 and the expression of PGE2 (Liu et al., 2023). Subsequently, PGE2 regulates GHRH expression through EP4 receptor binding on GHRH neurons, which inhibits growth of stressed broilers (Liu et al., 2023). In addition, PGE2 can also inhibit the appetite and consequently the growth of stressed broilers by binding to EP4 receptors on NPY neurons (Liu et al., 2023).

Previous investigators have provided evidence that CGA’s mechanism of action specifically involves COX-2. Using the RAW264.7 model, it was found that CGA effectively reduced the expression of COX-2 protein (Gu, et al., 2023). Our results showed that adding CGA to the diet reduced the expression of COX-2 in the hypothalamus. In addition, the level of CD40, an activation marker for microglia, also decreased. The transcriptome gene validation results showed that CGA significantly downregulated the gene expression of TLR1B, MYD88, STAT1, and SOCS3 in the MAPK-NF-κB pathway and had a significant inhibitory influence on transcription of the COX-2 gene. In addition, we found that adding CGA to the diet enhanced the levels of GHRH and NPY in the hypothalamus of stressed broilers. This is consistent with the results of our previous research (Liu et al., 2023) reported that broilers pretreated with celecoxib, a specific COX-2 inhibitor, showed higher expression of GHRH and NPY genes in the hypothalamus. These observations indicate that CGA can alleviate the growth inhibition in immune-stressed broiler chickens by inhibiting COX-2-related signaling pathways.

Conclusion

In conclusion, 0.1 % CGA supplementation can reduce the expression of TLR1B, MYD88, SOCS3, CD40, STAT1 and COX-2 in the hypothalamus of immune stressed broilers. This action inhibits the activation of COX-2 associated signaling pathway and elevates expression of NPY and GHRH, thus alleviating the growth inhibition in broilers caused by immune stress (Figure 5).
