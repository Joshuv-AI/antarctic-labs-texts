Ivabradine in a Neonate With Severe Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy
Authors: Yaa S. Karikari, Genevieve Baxter, Hugo R. Martinez, Kaitlin Ryan, Karine Guerrier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) affects approximately 20% of patients with Noonan syndrome and contributes significantly to mortality, particularly in neonates with severe HCM and left ventricular outflow tract obstruction, where controlling heart rate (HR) and maintaining systemic vascular resistance can be challenging.Case SummaryA full-term neonate with an Raf-1 proto-oncogene, serine/threonine kinase mutation and severe HCM developed diastolic heart failure requiring high-dose esmolol for rate control and phenylephrine for systemic vascular resistance support. After inadequate HR response and associated vasodilation due to esmolol, off-label ivabradine was started at 20 days of life, achieving HR control within 48 hours and allowing weaning of vasoactive agents and respiratory support.DiscussionStandard therapies—β-blockers, nondihydropyridine calcium channel blockers, and sodium-channel blockers—can worsen left ventricular outflow tract obstruction. Ivabradine selectively inhibits the I(f) current via hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide–gated channel 4 in sinoatrial tissue, providing HR reduction without compromising contractility or hemodynamics.Take-Home MessageIvabradine provides durable HR control without negative inotropy or hypotension, supporting its use in symptomatic pediatric HCM.

History of Presentation

Our patient was delivered at 38 weeks' gestation and transferred to the cardiac intensive care unit at 8 hours of life after a postnatal echocardiogram showed hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) with left ventricular (LV) outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO). At birth, he received positive pressure ventilation for low saturations and was transferred to the cardiac intensive care unit on high flow nasal cannula with saturations in the high 90s. His initial cardiac examination was pertinent for a grade IV/VI systolic ejection murmur and a heart rate (HR) of 130 to 140 beats/min. Physical examination findings were consistent with Noonan syndrome (NS) with low-set ears, broad forehead, and hypertelorism.Take-Home Messages•Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with congestive heart failure in patients less than 1 year of age carries a high rate of morbidity and mortality.•Our case suggests that ivabradine can be effective in patients with severe hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with left ventricular outflow tract obstruction and signs of congestive heart failure, resulting in effective heart rate control without hemodynamic compromise.

Past Medical History

Prenatal concerns included moderate to severe LV hypertrophy and moderate mitral valve regurgitation, pericardial effusion, and polyhydramnios.

Differential Diagnosis

Biventricular hypertrophy in a newborn can present in infants of diabetic mothers and inborn errors of metabolism such as Pompe disease.

Investigations

His initial echocardiogram showed moderate to severe biventricular hypertrophy (Figures 1 and 2) with an LV septum diastolic dimension of 6.3 mm (Z-score 2.9) and moderate systolic anterior motion of the mitral valve. There was hyperdynamic LV systolic function and moderate LVOTO with a peak gradient (PG) of 47 mm Hg and a mean gradient of 27 mm Hg. The right ventricle had mild hypertrophy, normal systolic performance, and moderate right ventricular outflow tract obstruction with moderate pulmonary valve stenosis, with a PG of 37 mm Hg. There was dysplastic cardiac pan-valvular disease with redundant thickened valve leaflets. Management

On day of life (DOL) 2, he became tachycardic with worsening respiratory distress and respiratory acidosis requiring escalation of respiratory support to noninvasive mechanical ventilation by full face mask, initiation of esmolol for HR control, and vasopressin for maintenance of systemic vascular resistance (SVR). Our goals for management in the setting of diastolic dysfunction and LVOTO were appropriate HR control and decreased hypercontractility of the LV while maintaining SVR and judicious use of diuretics. Avoidance of intubation was critical due to the hemodynamic effects of sedatives and afterload reduction, worsening LVOTO.

He was started on esmolol at 50 μg/kg/min and titrated to effect with a goal HR of 110 to 140 beats/min. Dexmedetomidine was initiated to decrease agitation with full face mask and for additional HR control. He responded to these interventions with improvement in HR, systolic blood pressure, and respiratory status, which led to weaning off vasopressin and respiratory support from full face mask to a RAM cannula. He did not tolerate enteral feeds, as demonstrated by recurrent abdominal distension. Propranolol was trialed to wean off of esmolol but resulted in hypotension and increasing respiratory distress. During his second week of life, he developed symptoms of congestive heart failure (HF) with worsening sinus tachycardia and intermittent atrial and ventricular tachyarrhythmia, worsening respiratory distress with pulmonary edema on chest x-ray, mixed respiratory and metabolic acidosis, and lactic acidemia requiring escalation of respiratory support back to full face mask on high noninvasive mechanical ventilation settings. Esmolol was eventually maximized at 600 μg/kg/min, and vasopressin was reinitiated for hypotension and later with subsequent transition to phenylephrine due to hyponatremia with a maximum phenylephrine dose of 0.9 μg/kg/min. The echocardiogram continued to demonstrate LVOTO with PG in the 80s (Figure 3) associated with an elevated BNP level initially at 1,796.7 pg/mL, which increased to 3,106.9 pg/mL. Because of failure of traditional pharmacologic therapy, discussion was held with the family regarding the potential utility of ivabradine. After discussion, the family agreed to off-label use of ivabradine. Ivabradine was initiated on DOL 20 at an initial dose of 0.02 mg/kg (0.1 mg) every 12 hours, which increased to 0.04 mg/kg (0.2 mg) after 2 doses. The HR was effectively controlled within 48 hours on ivabradine. The average HR on esmolol ranged between 140 and 150 beats/min and decreased to between 110 and 130 beats/min with ivabradine (Figure 4), allowing weaning off from high-dose esmolol and phenylephrine. In addition, there was a decrease in the LVOT gradient with ivabradine (Table 1). As he was found to carry a pathogenic variant in the Raf-1 proto-oncogene, serine/threonine kinase (RAF-1) c.770C>T (p.S257L) consistent with NS, trametinib was added to his medical management. He had recurrence of atrial re-entrant tachyarrhythmia that required transient reinitiation of esmolol and then transition to propranolol in combination with ivabradine. He tolerated the addition of propranolol without hemodynamic compromise. Outcome and Follow-Up

He was discharged home at 2 months of life without invasive intervention, stable on enteral medication (propranolol, ivabradine, and trametinib), on room air, and taking full feeds by mouth. His BNP level at 7 weeks was down to 121.4 pg/mL. He has since demonstrated continued HR control on ivabradine without propranolol with no recurrence of atrial or ventricular tachyarrhythmias. He was listed status 1A exception for heart transplant on DOL 40. Because of clinical improvement, he was made status 1B fourteen days later and is now off the transplant list.

Discussion

HCM presenting in patients younger than 1 year of age carries a high rate of morbidity and mortality compared with those diagnosed after 1 year of age.1,2 Among RASopathies, NS is relatively common, with a prevalence of approximately 1:1,000 to 1:2,500 live births.3 Clinical characteristics frequently include dysmorphic facial features, short stature, and congenital heart disease. HCM is present in approximately 20% of patients with NS. RAF-1-related RASopathy with the c.770C>T variant is associated with severe cardiac phenotype and a 92% lifetime probability of developing HCM.4

The presence of HCM in patients with RAF-1 mutations is associated with early diagnosis and mortality; NS is the leading cause of infantile HCM (2), with reported mortality ranging between 9% and 24%.1,3,5 Early age at presentation and the presence of congestive HF are significant risk factors for mortality. Data from the Pediatric Cardiomyopathy Registry demonstrate that patients diagnosed before 6 months of age with congestive HF had a 1-year survival of only 31%. The mean shortening fraction for patients with NS was nearly 6 standard deviations above the mean, indicating that LV diastolic dysfunction—rather than systolic impairment—is the primary etiology of HF, leading to pulmonary edema and low cardiac output.1,2

Management strategies for obstructive HCM focus on HR control to prolong diastolic filling time, reduce LVOTO through decreased inotropy, and maintain SVR to minimize the gradient across the LV outflow tract. Achieving effective HR control remains challenging in the pediatric population, particularly in neonates with severe HCM and LVOTO. β-Blockers are customary first-line therapy for HR control in HCM;6 however, in neonates, they may be associated with hemodynamic instability. Nondihydropyridine calcium-channel blockers, such as verapamil, and sodium-channel blockers, such as disopyramide, have also been used but are associated with undesirable side effects, including vasodilation, which may worsen LVOTO, and are often contraindicated in early infancy.

Ivabradine represents a potential alternative approach to HR control in this population. Ivabradine selectively inhibits the funny I(f) current in cardiac pacemaker cells by acting on the hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide–gated channel 4 in sinoatrial tissue.7 Through its specific action on I(f) and hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide–gated channel 4, ivabradine provides effective HR reduction with a favorable hemodynamic profile and without significant effects on myocardial contractility.

In this neonate with severe obstructive HCM and diastolic HF, ivabradine initiation was temporally associated with rapid HR reduction and de-escalation of vasoactive/respiratory support. Ivabradine has previously demonstrated efficacy in the treatment of automatic tachyarrhythmias, including ectopic atrial tachycardia and congenital junctional ectopic tachycardia.8,9 In the pediatric population, its use has also been described in patients with dilated cardiomyopathy and HF, both with and without tachycardia, resulting in significant improvement in LV systolic function.10 By selectively targeting the inward funny current in sinoatrial tissue, ivabradine offers durable HR control without negative inotropic effects or hypotension, supporting its use as adjunctive therapy in select neonates with severe obstructive HCM who are unable to tolerate conventional pharmacologic agents.

Conclusions

Our case suggests that ivabradine can be effective in patients with severe HCM with LVOTO and signs of congestive HF, resulting in effective HR control without hemodynamic compromise.
