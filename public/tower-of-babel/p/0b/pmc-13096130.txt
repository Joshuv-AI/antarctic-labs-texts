Research on key technologies for privacy-preserving, regulatorily compliant, and cross-chain interoperability in heterogeneous blockchain systems
Authors: Zepeng Chen, Hui Liu, Lin Zhang, Bingrong Dai, Youqun Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the development of blockchain technology, the issue of “value isolation, " caused by ecological heterogeneity, is becoming increasingly important. Interaction between blockchains has proven to be an important foundation for building the Internet of Value. However, existing solutions struggle to simultaneously meet user demands for transaction confidentiality and regulatory compliance and often suffer from risks of centralization, loss of private data, and regulatory inefficiency. To address these issues, this paper proposes a new paradigm and universal framework for ensuring confidentiality and cross-chain compatibility in heterogeneous blockchains in compliance with regulatory requirements. There are three main contributions of this work: First, the distributed threshold network (DTN) has been designed as a reliable foundation that reduces reliance on a single authority through threshold signature technology, thereby increasing system security and reliability. Second, multi-party computation (MPC) is innovatively integrated into the inter-network process and form the privacy-preserving verification protocol (PPVP), which allows nodes to verify the authenticity of transactions without openly revealing data, ensuring confidentiality by default. Third, a key-shared supervision protocol (KSSP) is being developed that makes regulatory bodies participants in the MPC process. This allows for proper evaluation of suspicious transactions without decryption, fundamentally combining confidentiality and regulatory requirements. Theoretical analysis shows that the proposed system is resistant to existential forgery attacks under the random oracle model, while its basic protocols provide universal security (UC) and computational accuracy. Experimental validation consisted of implementing a prototype system and comparing it with existing solutions. The results show that the proposed solution maintains high performance (processing capacity > 1250 TPS, latency < 400 ms) while significantly outperforming existing approaches in terms of decentralization, confidentiality, and regulatory capabilities. This opens up a real technical path to creating a secure, reliable, and next-generation compliant blockchain Internet.Keywords: Blockchain cross-chain, Privacy protection, Regulatory compliance, Secure multi-party computation, Threshold signature, Formal verificationSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In October 2008, with the publication of “Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System”1, a revolutionary decentralized cryptocurrency was born, heralding the dawn of the blockchain era. Since its inception, blockchain technology has evolved through the Bitcoin-dominated Blockchain 1.0 and the smart-contract-focused Blockchain 2.0. Driven by deep technological innovation, it has now transitioned into the Blockchain 3.0 era, represented by the Web3 vision2. Today, blockchain serves as a new generation of network infrastructure, converging with IoT, AI, and cloud computing to support diverse applications in finance, governance, and evidence storage. However, the proliferation of these diverse underlying platforms has led to a fragmented landscape where multiple heterogeneous architectures coexist3.

Significant differences in consensus mechanisms, cryptographic primitives, and state models among public and consortium chains have created severe “value silos”4,5. While cross-chain interoperability has emerged as the critical solution, existing mainstream approaches face deep-seated theoretical and architectural bottlenecks6. Relay chain models (e.g., Cosmos IBC), while architecturally clean, suffer from high maintenance costs for light clients and scalability issues7. Hash Time-Locked Contracts (HTLC) ensure atomicity but are constrained by their lack of Turing-completeness, supporting only simple asset swaps without general state synchronization8. In recent years, Notary schemes based on addressing privacy and security issues in blockchain interoperability, MPC and Threshold Signature Schemes (TSS) have gained traction for their flexibility. However, from a theoretical perspective, these schemes currently struggle with the high communication complexity inherent in multi-round interactive protocols9. As the number of signing nodes increases to ensure decentralization, the computational overhead for polynomial interpolation and the network latency for signature aggregation grow exponentially, creating a strict trade-off between security (decentralization degree) and performance (throughput)10.

Beyond these endogenous technical limitations regarding performance and interoperability, the cross-chain domain faces an even more intractable exogenous challenge: the paradox between privacy and regulation. Conventional multi-signature notary mechanisms, while efficient, often require plaintext data sharing, completely sacrificing user privacy11. Conversely, fully anonymous protocols facilitate illicit activities, drawing scrutiny from global regulators.

Concurrently, the global regulatory landscape is undergoing profound transformation. Mandates like the FATF’s “Travel Rule” now require virtual asset service providers to share transaction details, directly conflicting with blockchain’s native anonymity. Historically, zero-knowledge protocols have successfully resolved similar “verification vs. secrecy” paradoxes in high-stakes scenarios, such as nuclear warhead verification12. However, existing cross-chain solutions fail to adapt such mechanisms effectively, rendering them inadequate for compliance. Therefore, overcoming the limitations of current approaches to achieve the organic integration of “trustlessness,” “privacy,” and “regulation” has become the most critical challenge in blockchain interoperability, determining its potential for large-scale industrial application.

To address these challenges, this paper focuses on the protocol layer architecture and cryptographic implementation for regulated cross-chain interoperability. Specifically, we delineate the scope of this work as follows: We propose a comprehensive architectural framework and validate it through a prototype system. The work is distributed with approximately 70% dedicated to theoretical modeling—including formal security definitions and protocol logic—and 30% focused on system implementation and extensive empirical evaluation in realistic network environments. It is important to note that this paper does not focus on optimizing the consensus algorithms of the underlying host chains, nor does it provide a legal analysis of specific jurisdictional frameworks; rather, it provides technical primitives to support such frameworks.

Consequently, the main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:

(1) System-Level Paradigm: We propose a novel three-tier architecture integrating a Distributed Threshold Network (DTN), a Privacy-Preserving Verification Protocol (PPVP), and a Key-Shared Supervision Protocol (KSSP). Its innovation lies in being the first to integrate the three dimensions of trustlessness, privacy protection, and compliance supervision at the system level, providing a comprehensive systemic solution to address challenges in this domain.

(2) Methodological Innovation: We propose and implement key technologies encompassing novel methods and approaches. Methodologically, we innovatively integrate MPC throughout the entire cross-chain transaction verification process, designing the PPVP protocol to enable verification execution on ciphertext or fragmented data, fundamentally eliminating privacy leaks. In terms of implementation, the KSSP protocol transforms regulators into participants in MPC computations. This allows them to make compliance judgments based on algorithms rather than plaintext data, achieving “algorithmic oversight” and “functional regulation” instead of “data-centric regulation. " This approach perfectly balances the technical tension between privacy and regulatory compliance.

(3) Rigorous Validation: We validated the effectiveness and advancement of the proposed solution through formal security proofs and comprehensive experimental evaluations. This work breaks through the current technical bottleneck in cross-chain technology where privacy and regulation are difficult to achieve simultaneously, providing an original technical pathway for building next-generation trusted internet infrastructure.

Related work

Current research on blockchain interoperability primarily revolves around three dimensions: cross-chain mechanisms, privacy-preserving technologies, and regulatable privacy solutions.

Cross-chain interoperation

Existing cross-chain solutions are generally categorized into external, local, and native verification schemes. Regarding external verification schemes: To address security authentication in IoT environments and cross-chain communication issues, Shao et al.13(2021) proposed an identity-based encryption mechanism for IoT blockchain communication. However, the proxy node election process in this scheme is complex, and its reliance on a cross-chain notary mechanism retains inherent centralization characteristics; Lv et al.14(2024) introduced a Multi-User Proxy Re-Signature (MU-PRS) algorithm to facilitate cross-chain conversion of multiple user signatures, significantly improving authentication efficiency, yet the issue of centralization remains unresolved. Regarding local verification schemes: Dai et al.15(2021) improved the HTLC protocol by extending it to multi-hop cross-chain transactions, thereby enhancing transaction success rates. However, this approach suffers from prolonged lock times and vulnerability to wormhole attacks; Wang et al.16(2024) proposed Anonymous Enhanced Multi-Hop Locks (AEMHL), combining Linkable Ring Adaptor Signatures with an anonymous multi-hop payment channel network architecture to support Monero. Nevertheless, this scheme is computationally complex and functionally limited. Regarding native verification schemes: Bai et al.17(2023) proposed a secure cross-chain architecture based on identity-based public key algorithms, utilizing relay chains for identity authentication; however, both security and performance require further improvement; Wu et al.18(2025) designed “BridgeGuard”, a tool that models cross-chain transactions from a graph perspective and employs a two-stage detection framework (global and local graph mining) to identify attack patterns. Despite its efficacy, the adaptation cost of this scheme is relatively high. Existing cross-chain technologies have made significant strides in performance and universality, external verification schemes face centralization risks, local verification schemes are functionally restricted, and native verification schemes incur high adaptation costs. Crucially, all these approaches lack in-depth consideration for privacy protection and regulatory compliance.

Blockchain privacy technology

Research in this domain focuses on Zero-Knowledge Proofs (ZKPs), Homomorphic Encryption (HE), and Mixing technologies. Regarding zero-knowledge proof: Deng et al.19(2024) and Hu et al.20(2025) proposed efficient ZKP schemes to enhance system security and applicability. However, verification time increases linearly with the number of ZKPs, imposing a significant burden on transaction generation and privacy computation, particularly in high-throughput cross-chain environments. Regarding homomorphic encryption: Silva Vanin et al.21(2023) proposed a cross-chain transaction scheme based on Fully Homomorphic Encryption (FHE), utilizing Distributed Hash Table (DHT) technology to store and distribute data and metadata separately, thereby protecting transaction details; Cao et al.22(2025) utilized homomorphic encryption to enable computation on encrypted data, ensuring the correctness of cross-chain transactions while maintaining confidentiality. However, these schemes involve large-scale computations and are inherently unfriendly to regulation. Regarding mixing technologies: Mao et al.23(2024) developed a novel mixing protocol that enhances anonymity through improved ring signature schemes, effectively preventing probing attacks, but it remains unregulatable; Yang et al.24(2024) proposed a new cross-chain privacy protocol based on the Groth16 algorithm and mixing technology, utilizing combinatorial generation functions to map virtual external addresses. Despite these innovations, the risk of Man-in-the-Middle (MitM) attacks persists. In conclusion, while these privacy-preserving technologies offer strong anonymity, they generally suffer from being “regulatory-unfriendly.” They struggle to address emerging attack vectors and fail to meet Anti-Money Laundering (AML) and Counter-Terrorism Financing (CFT) requirements.

Privacy-preserving regulatory technology

Regulatable privacy has emerged as a research hotspot, primarily focusing on Threshold Signatures and MPC. Regarding threshold signature schemes (TSS): Davis et al.25(2024) integrated an enhanced MTAS algorithm derived from Schnorr signatures, which automatically adjusts flexible thresholds based on current conditions to meet varying security and efficiency requirements; Xie et al.26(2024) proposed TAPS-PR, an accountable threshold signature protocol based on EdDSA with privacy and proactive refresh capabilities, improving efficiency and reducing communication costs compared to ATS-PR. Regarding MPC for privacy-regulation balance: Yin et al.27(2021) proposed the Bool Network, a platform employing MPC to collaboratively create accounts on supported blockchains and using TSS to jointly sign cross-chain transactions; however, potential security risks remain; Zheng et al.28(2024) combined MPC with Federated Learning to mitigate data security and privacy issues, ensuring efficient and secure data processing. While these technologies have progressed in single-chain scenarios, their application in cross-chain environments remains insufficient and lacks systematic solutions.

Through a systematic analysis of existing literature, several limitations in current blockchain cross-chain research are identified:

(1) Technical Fragmentation: Existing research often isolates cross-chain interoperability, privacy, and regulation. Cross-chain studies frequently neglect privacy; privacy research overlooks regulatory needs; and regulatory schemes are often confined to single-chain scenarios29. (2) Trust Assumption Limitations: Most solutions rely on strong trust assumptions, such as trusting a specific set of validators or assuming that regulatory bodies will not abuse their power. (3) Performance Bottlenecks: Significant performance degradation occurs when privacy and regulatory functions are introduced, creating a bottleneck in system throughput. (4) Insufficient Heterogeneity Support: Existing schemes are often designed for specific platforms, lacking general support for truly heterogeneous blockchain environments30. Therefore, this paper aims to fill these gaps by proposing a unified framework capable of simultaneously addressing cross-chain interoperability, privacy protection, and regulatory compliance, providing technical support for building the next-generation trusted internet infrastructure.

Principles and models of MPC

MPC enables multiple parties to each hold private data and jointly compute a function , while ensuring that after computation, each party receives only the output y and cannot access any other party’s original input information. In this paper, MPC is pivotal for PPVP, enabling regulatory compliance checks on encrypted transaction data.

The security models for MPC are primarily categorized as follows32:

Semi-Honest Model: Assumes adversaries follow the protocol execution flow but attempt to infer other parties’ private information through intermediate computation results (e.g., an “honest-but-curious” regulator analyzing traffic patterns).

Malicious Model: The adversary may arbitrarily deviate from the protocol, such as sending falsified shares or aborting the computation to disrupt the network (e.g., a corrupted validator attempting to forge compliance proofs).

While Boolean secret sharing is efficient for logical operations (e.g., XOR, AND), this paper adopts Arithmetic Secret Sharing Methods33, because the Arithmetic Secret Sharing Algorithm is computationally more efficient than the Boolean secret sharing algorithm and incurs lower communication overhead. Blockchain cross-chain technology principles

Atomic swap

Atomic Swap technology is based on HTLC, ensuring that two cross-chain transactions either succeed simultaneously or fail simultaneously. Its core mechanism relies on a secret r and its hash value . Transactions are protected by a time lock 34.

(1) User A locks assets on Chain A, claiming that anyone who provides a preimage (satisfying ) may claim the assets. This lock expires after time .

(2) Upon observing the lock on Chain B, User B locks assets using the same hash h, with the lock expiring after a shorter duration ( < ).

(3) User A claims the assets on Chain B, consumes the locked output on Chain B, thereby revealing .

(4) User B uses the disclosed r to claim the assets locked by User A on Chain A.

If either party abandons the process midway, both can reclaim their assets after the timeout. This protocol guarantees transaction atomicity.

Relay chain model

The Relay Chain model employs an independent, typically distributed blockchain (the Relay Chain) to validate and forward the state and events of other blockchains (Parallel Chains). Its core technologies are light clients and Merkle proofs35.

Each chain maintains a light client for other chains. Light clients do not store the full state but only block headers. When verifying an Event occurring on the source chain ,validators on the relay chain will:

(1) Retrieve the block header Header of the block containing Event and the Merkle path proof for that event.

(2) Validate the consensus legitimacy of the Header (e.g., Proof-Of-Work).

(3) Use to verify that Event ‘s hash is indeed included in the Merkle root of the Header.

Only after successful verification will the event be recognized as valid by the relay chain, triggering corresponding operations on the target chain .

Notary schemes

In a notary scheme, users in different blockchains communicate with each other through a trusted third-party, all messages and assets need to be transferred to the notary and then forwarded by them. Apparently, the trusted third-party is the most vital role of this method, and according to the signature algorithm applied by the notary, this approach can be divided into three categories: single-signature notary, multi-signature notary and distributed-signature notary. The notary schemes are flexible and easy to implement, but with the risk of centralization at the same time.

Trusted computing and formal verification

Formal verification tools

Formal verification employs mathematical modeling and logical reasoning to rigorously prove a protocol’s security properties.

(1) ProVerif: An automated tool based on the Dolev-Yao model, designed to verify the secrecy and authentication properties of protocols. It translates protocol execution and attack models into a series of derivation rules, determining whether specified security properties hold under all possible execution paths. For example, it can verify if a session key in a key-exchange protocol remains secret under active attacks37.

(2) Tamarin: A more powerful interactive proof tool supporting more complex security properties (such as forward secrecy and fairness) and stateful protocols. Based on symbolic models and rewriting logic, it allows users to manually guide the proof process, handling complex theorems that ProVerif cannot prove automatically38.

Defining security models and threat models

To rigorously analyze security, security models and adversary capabilities must be formally defined.

The threat model typically adopts the Dolev-Yao model, where the adversary controls the network and can eavesdrop, intercept, tamper with, inject, or replay any message. The adversary can corrupt protocol participants to obtain their internal state. This paper assumes the adversary is computationally bounded and adaptive (able to choose targets for compromise during protocol execution).

Security properties include:

(1) Confidentiality: The adversary cannot obtain any information about non-public secrets within the protocol (e.g. private keys, transaction details).

(2) Authentication: Ensures messages originate from the claimed sender and remain unaltered.

(3) Non-repudiation: The sender cannot later deny having sent a message.

(4) Fairness: At protocol conclusion, either all honest parties receive the expected output, or none do.

Summary and relevance to system design

The concepts introduced in this section form the building blocks of our “Privacy-Preserving, Regulatable” architecture: TSS allows the DTN to function as a decentralized trust anchor; MPC (Arithmetic Sharing) powers the PPVP and KSSP, enabling “Algorithmic Oversight”—calculating compliance results on encrypted data without exposing raw user privacy; Formal Verification provides the mathematical guarantee that our protocols remain secure against the defined threat models.

System model, design goals and threat model

Design goals and principles

This paper focuses on constructing a heterogeneous blockchain interoperability system39 that safeguards user privacy while meeting regulatory compliance requirements. The design adheres to the following core principles:

(1) Trustlessness: Relying on no single trusted central authority, establishing security foundations through cryptographic mechanisms (e.g. TSS) and decentralized consensus mechanisms.

(2) Privacy with Quantifiable Security: Utilizing MPC, the system protects transaction confidentiality (sender, recipient, amount). We target a security level where the probability of privacy leakage is negligible () under standard cryptographic assumptions.

(3) Regulatory Auditability with Boundary Constraints: Provides technical interfaces for regulators to conduct controlled reviews of suspected illegal transactions without infringing on ordinary users’ privacy.

(4) Heterogeneous Compatibility: The system supports integration with diverse architectures. Specifically, it addresses the adaptation challenges between Account-based Public Chains (e.g., Ethereum, using ECDSA/secp256k1) and Certificate-based Consortium Chains (e.g., Hyperledger Fabric, using MSP certificates). Universal adapters abstract these differences in cryptographic curves and finality gadgets.

(5) High Performance: The system aims for low-latency interoperability. The target computational overhead for MPC verification is set to be within the sub-second range (< 500ms) per transaction, ensuring that the cross-chain throughput scales linearly with the underlying chain’s capacity.

Security model, threat assumptions, and objectives

To conduct rigorous security analysis and proof for the framework, its security model and threat assumptions must be formally defined.

System assumptions

We assume the following properties hold for the underlying infrastructure:

Cryptographic Hardness: The Discrete Logarithm Problem (DLP) and computational Diffie-Hellman (DH) problem are hard in the chosen groups.

Underlying Chain Security: The connected blockchains (Source/Target) are secure. We assume the cost to revert a finalized block on these chains (e.g., via 51% attack) exceeds the value of the cross-chain assets.

Honest Majority in DTN: The fraction of corrupted DTN nodes satisfies (for BFT consistency) or specific threshold for privacy.

Threat model

We consider an Active Adversary with the following capabilities:

DTN Corruption: can statically or adaptively corrupt up to nodes in the DTN network to learn internal states or disrupt consensus.

Network Attacks: controls the communication channels (Dolev-Yao model), capable of replaying, delaying, or reordering messages.

Chain-Level Attacks: may attempt to fork the underlying blockchain to double-spend assets. The system defends against this by waiting for probabilistic finality (e.g., 6 confirmations on Bitcoin) before processing events.

Regulatory Abuse: malicious regulator may attempt to decrypt transactions below the threshold . The protocol restricts this by requiring collaboration from honest DTN nodes for any decryption.

Design of DTN

DTN acts as the root of trust. Its security relies on a robust Distributed Key Generation (DKG) protocol and a BLS Threshold Signature Scheme.

We explicitly adopted the BLS scheme over traditional ECDSA-based threshold alternatives due to its support for non-interactive signature aggregation. In a cross-chain context, this feature allows partial signatures to be aggregated into a single, constant-size signature off-chain. Consequently, the target chain smart contract only needs to verify one aggregate signature using the pairing check, significantly reducing the payload size and gas consumption (as demonstrated in Sect. 7.4.4).

Core smart contract logic algorithm design

The previously proposed DTN, PPVP, and KSSP form the core cryptographic protocol layer of the cross-chain system. However, these protocols must ultimately be executed and anchored within the trustless environment of blockchain43. Consequently, the cryptographic protocols defined earlier must be transformed into self-executing, tamper-resistant smart contract code that requires no third-party trust.

Security proof

Security analysis primarily addresses two adversary behavior models:

Semi-Honest Adversary Model: Assumes the adversary faithfully follows the protocol’s execution flow but retains all intermediate computation results and attempts to infer other participants’ private inputs by analyzing this information. This model applies to privacy analysis, i.e., how much information the adversary can steal while complying with the protocol.

Malicious Adversary Model: The adversary may arbitrarily deviate from protocol specifications, performing active attacks such as sending erroneous messages or refusing to respond. This model analyzes robustness and authenticity—whether the system maintains correct operation under active attacks.

Limitations and implementation security

To provide a comprehensive security perspective, we acknowledge specific boundaries of our model:

Limitations of the random oracle model (ROM).

Limitations of the random oracle model (ROM).

Our proofs rely on the ROM. It is theoretically established that certain schemes secure in ROM may be insecure when instantiated with concrete hash functions (e.g., SHA-256) due to correlation intractability. However, for practical engineering, ROM provides a sufficient heuristic. A stricter proof in the Standard Model would require less efficient primitives (e.g., Dual-System Encryption), which is a trade-off for the high performance required in cross-chain systems.

(2)Implementation security & Side-channel attacks:

Implementation security & Side-channel attacks:

Formal proofs do not cover physical implementation leakage. Practical deployment must address:

Timing Attacks: The modular exponentiations in TSS must use constant-time algorithms (e.g., Montgomery Ladder) to prevent private key leakage via execution time analysis.

Memory Attacks: Sensitive memory regions holding key shares should be guarded against cold-boot attacks and swapped-to-disk exposure (e.g., using mlock).

Conclusions and outlook

Key research findings

This paper addresses the critical “trilemma” in heterogeneous blockchain interoperability—simultaneously achieving trustlessness, privacy protection, and regulatory compliance. By proposing a novel “Privacy-Preserving, Regulatable” paradigm, we have successfully designed and validated the DTN-MPC framework. The core contributions and findings are summarized as follows:

(1) A Novel Systemic Paradigm: We constructed a three-tier architecture integrating DTN, PPVP, and KSSP. Unlike traditional layered approaches, this design achieves an organic unification of privacy and regulation at the protocol level, providing a comprehensive solution for next-generation trusted internet infrastructure.

(2) Deep Cryptographic Integration: Moving beyond simple component stacking, we achieved a deep fusion of TSS and MPC. The PPVP enables “default privacy” by verifying transactions on ciphertext, while the KSSP introduces “Algorithmic Oversight,” transforming regulators into computational participants. This mechanism ensures compliance without exposing plaintext data, effectively resolving the inherent conflict between anonymity and accountability.

(3) Proven Feasibility and Superiority: Rigorous formal analysis confirms the system’s security (EUF-CMA and UC-secure). Empirical evaluation on a 30-node network demonstrates a throughput of 1,310 TPS with an average latency of 352 ms. Compared to traditional Multi-Signature Notary schemes, our solution increases throughput by 63.8% while reducing on-chain gas costs by 59.5%. These results validate that high-privacy and regulatable interoperability is achievable with practical performance overhead.

Limitations of the research

Despite the promising results, we acknowledge specific limitations in the current research and potential barriers to industrial deployment:

Performance Bottlenecks: Performance analysis reveals that MPC computation is the primary latency source, accounting for 68% (approx. 240ms) of the total transaction time. In scenarios with high-frequency trading or extreme concurrency, this computational overhead remains a significant constraint compared to non-privacy-preserving bridges (e.g., Cosmos IBC).

Scalability Constraints: While simulation data suggests stability up to 100 nodes, the communication complexity of the BFT consensus and DKG setup (especially during the Resharing phase for membership changes) may face challenges in ultra-large-scale WAN environments (e.g., > 1000 nodes).

Industrial Deployment Hurdles: Practical adoption faces “cold start” challenges. Migrating existing assets to this new protocol requires complex mapping of account models (e.g., EVM to Fabric MSP). Furthermore, the lack of standardized regulatory interfaces in current banking systems hinders immediate integration with the proposed KSSP module.

Future research prospects

To advance this technology from prototype to industrial standard, future work will focus on three strategic directions:

(1) Algorithmic Optimization and Hardware Acceleration.

Protocol Efficiency: We aim to transition from generic MPC circuits to optimized, application-specific protocols (e.g., using Silent OT or Function-Dependent Preprocessing) to reduce communication rounds.

Hardware Integration: Investigating the use of Trusted Execution Environments (TEE, e.g., Intel SGX) and FPGA acceleration cards to offload intensive modular exponentiations, aiming to reduce MPC latency to the sub-millisecond range.

(2) Engineering and Standardization.

Standard Formulation: Research will focus on developing privacy-preserving and regulatory frameworks supporting universal cross-chain smart contract interoperability, defining unified interfaces for regulatory signaling and asset locking.

Robustness Enhancement: Future iterations will introduce formal verification for smart contracts and develop automated tools for detecting “collusion patterns” among DTN nodes, ensuring industrial-grade security.

(3) Decentralized Governance and Regulatory Frameworks.

Dynamic Governance: Research will extend to Decentralized Autonomous Organization (DAO) models for regulatory committees, exploring voting-based mechanisms for dynamic updating of blacklists/whitelists.

Legal-Tech Alignment: We will explore “Legal Prose to Code” compilers to automatically translate evolving AML/CFT laws into executable MPC circuits, minimizing the friction between legal compliance and technical execution.
