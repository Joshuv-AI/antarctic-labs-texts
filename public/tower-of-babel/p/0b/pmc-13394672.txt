NRF2/xCT‐Mediated Antioxidant Adaptation Attenuates Apoptosis Induced by ASCT2 Inhibition in Prostate Cancer
Authors: Jiaxing Wei, Yasutaka Yamada, Takaaki Tamura, Masahiro Sugiura, Xue Zhao, Meng Ni, Adilan Aierken, Tomokazu Sazuka, Yusuke Imamura, Shinichi Sakamoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGlutamine supports biosynthesis and redox homeostasis in cancer cells. The glutamine transporter ASCT2 is highly expressed in prostate cancer and is associated with higher Gleason grade. Although ASCT2 inhibition induces reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and apoptosis, cancer cells may activate antioxidant adaptive mechanisms that limit therapeutic efficacy. Here, we identified an NRF2/xCT‐dependent redox‐adaptive response following ASCT2 inhibition in prostate cancer. Mechanistically, ASCT2 inhibition induced ROS accumulation, promoted nuclear translocation of NRF2, and upregulated xCT together with other NRF2‐associated antioxidant genes. Functional redox analysis showed that ASCT2 inhibition reduced glutathione‐dependent redox capacity, whereas combined ASCT2 and xCT inhibition further increased GSSG accumulation and markedly decreased GSH levels and the GSH/GSSG ratio. These fin\dings were further validated in C4‐2 cells as an additional prostate cancer model. Cell death discrimination assays showed that combined ASCT2 and xCT inhibition induced lipid peroxidation that was partially rescued by ferrostatin‐1, whereas z‐VAD produced a stronger rescue effect and Annexin V/PI staining confirmed prominent apoptotic cell death. In castrated 22Rv1 xenograft models, combined treatment with V‐9302 and erastin produced the strongest inhibition of tumor growth without significant body weight loss during treatment. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that NRF2/xCT‐mediated residual glutathione redox buffering functions as an adaptive survival mechanism following ASCT2 inhibition and provide a mechanistic rationale for combined targeting of ASCT2 and xCT to overcome redox‐adaptive resistance in prostate cancer.

Introduction

Prostate cancer is one of the most common malignancies in men and remains a leading cause of cancer‐related mortality worldwide [1, 2, 3]. Although localized prostate cancer can often be effectively managed by surgery or radiotherapy, a substantial proportion of patients eventually progress to advanced disease states, including castration‐resistant prostate cancer (CRPC), which represents a major therapeutic challenge [4, 5, 6, 7]. Androgen restriction therapy offers considerable initial clinical advantages; nonetheless, disease recurrence is prevalent, suggesting that tumor cells acquire the ability to survive and proliferate through mechanisms that are at least partially independent of androgen receptor signaling [8, 9, 10]. At present, durable curative therapies for advanced prostate cancer are still limited, underscoring an urgent need to identify new therapeutic strategies.

Metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of tumor growth and facilitates treatment tolerance and cellular plasticity [11, 12]. Glutamine is an important nutrient for cancer cells, providing carbon and nitrogen for macromolecular biosynthesis, supporting tricarboxylic acid cycle activity, and maintaining redox homeostasis [13, 14]. The glutamine transporter ASCT2 mediates cellular glutamine uptake and is closely linked to the activation of anabolic signaling pathways, including mTORC1 and ERK [15, 16]. ASCT2 is frequently overexpressed in multiple solid tumors and is associated with aggressive behavior and unfavorable clinical outcomes [17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22].

In prostate cancer, ASCT2 expression is significantly higher in tumor tissues than in paired benign tissues, and its expression is re‐upregulated during disease progression in castration‐resistant models, suggesting a persistent reliance on glutamine metabolism in advanced disease settings [23, 24, 25, 26]. Furthermore, our group and others have documented that numerous amino acid transporters are significantly expressed in urological malignancies, including prostate, kidney, and bladder tumors, and that their expression is associated with unfavorable clinical outcomes [24, 27, 28, 29]. In addition to these, transporters such as ASCT2, LAT1 (SLC7A5), and xCT (SLC7A11) are often increased in many solid tumors. These transporters are thought to promote tumor growth, maintain redox balance, and contribute to therapeutic resistance [24, 30, 31, 32].

Targeting amino acid transporters has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy in cancer, considering their essential role in supporting tumor metabolism and growth [33, 34, 35]. Pharmacological inhibition of transporters such as ASCT2 or xCT has shown anticancer efficacy in preclinical models, which suggests the feasibility of disrupting nutrient acquisition pathways in cancer [32, 33, 34, 35, 36]. Nevertheless, cancer cells exhibit substantial metabolic plasticity, allowing adaptive responses to nutrient stress and therapeutic pressure [11, 37]. Disruption of a primary metabolic pathway might initiate compensatory rewiring of related transport or metabolic networks to maintain cellular viability [38, 39]. Functional connections among amino acid transporters have been documented in several tumor settings, raising the possibility that inhibition of one transporter may elicit adaptive responses involving alternative transport systems [35, 38, 39]. However, how prostate cancer cells adapt to metabolic stress following ASCT2 inhibition, and whether such adaptive responses contribute to therapeutic limitation, remain incompletely defined.

In the present study, we demonstrate that ASCT2 inhibition induces intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and activates NRF2 signaling, leading to adaptive upregulation of the cystine transporter xCT. Because xCT imports cystine in exchange for intracellular glutamate, and glutamine‐derived glutamate contributes to both cystine exchange and glutathione synthesis, ASCT2 inhibition may create a redox vulnerability in which xCT induction represents an adaptive but metabolically constrained antioxidant response. We further show that combined inhibition of ASCT2 and xCT disrupts glutathione redox homeostasis, enhances ROS accumulation and lipid peroxidation, and promotes predominantly apoptotic cell death. These findings identify NRF2/xCT‐mediated residual glutathione redox buffering as an adaptive survival mechanism that limits the antitumor efficacy of ASCT2 inhibition and provide a mechanistic rationale for combined targeting of ASCT2 and xCT in prostate cancer.

Materials and Methods

Patient Tumor Specimens

A total of 95 prostate cancer specimens were obtained from patients who underwent radical prostatectomy at Chiba University Hospital between 2006 and 2015. Histopathologic diagnosis and Gleason grading were independently confirmed by experienced pathologists from Chiba University in accordance with World Health Organization criteria. Clinical and pathological staging was determined according to the Union for International Cancer Control TNM classification system.

All procedures involving human participants were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and national research committees and with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chiba University (IRB No. 1282), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Detailed clinicopathological characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1.

Reagents and Antibodies

Key reagents, inhibitors, siRNAs, antibodies, and assay kits used in this study are summarized in Table S3.

Cell Culture and Transfection

LNCaP and PC‐3 (Cell Resource Center for Biomedical Research, Tohoku University, Sendai, Japan), and DU145 (RIKEN Cell Bank, Tsukuba, Japan) cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. C4‐2, 22Rv1 (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA), and LNCaP95 cells were maintained in phenol red‐free RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% charcoal‐stripped FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin [40]. LNCaP95 is an AR‐V7‐positive CRPC derivative of LNCaP. Cells were cultured at 37°C with 5% CO2, authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling, and confirmed mycoplasma‐free. siRNA transfection was performed using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX according to the manufacturer's instructions and as described previously [41].

Knockdown by Small Hairpin RNA

Stable ASCT2 knockdown was generated using small hairpin RNA as previously described [40]. Target sequences and control information are listed in Table S1. Puromycin‐selected cells were validated by immunoblotting before subsequent experiments (Figure S2C).

Glutamine Consumption Assay

Extracellular glutamine levels were measured using a colorimetric assay kit (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Culture supernatants from treated or transfected 22Rv1 cells were collected at indicated time points. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured, and glutamine concentrations were calculated from a standard curve.

Leucine Uptake Assay

Amino acid transport was quantified by measuring [14C]‐L‐leucine uptake. Cells were preincubated in HBSS and exposed to uptake buffer containing labeled leucine for 1 min at 37°C. Uptake was stopped with ice‐cold washes. Radioactivity was quantified by scintillation counting and normalized to total protein. Nonspecific uptake was determined using excess unlabeled leucine.

Quantitative Real‐Time PCR

Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). cDNA was synthesized from 0.5 μg RNA using reverse transcription reagents (Promega). Quantitative real‐time PCR (qRT‐PCR) was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix on an ABI 7300 system. GAPDH served as an internal control and relative expression was calculated using the comparative Ct method. Primer sequences are provided in Table S2.

NAC Co‐Incubation

NAC was added at a final concentration of 5 mM at the beginning of each treatment and maintained throughout the indicated incubation period. Cells were therefore exposed to NAC for the same duration as the corresponding experimental condition.

Nuclear and Cytosolic Fractionation

Cytosolic and nuclear protein fractions were prepared using the NE‐PER Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Western Blot

Total protein was quantified using a BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS‐PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, followed by appropriate secondary antibodies. Signals were detected using a chemiluminescence imaging system (Fujifilm LAS‐4000 mini). Densitometric quantification of Western blot bands was performed using ImageJ. Target protein levels were normalized to GAPDH for whole‐cell lysates or cytosolic fractions and to Histone H3 for nuclear fractions, as indicated in each figure. Quantified values are presented as mean ± SEM, and statistical analysis was performed as described below.

Cell Viability Assay

To assess the effects of inhibitors or siRNA on cell growth, 22Rv1 cells were seeded in 96‐well plates (5000 cells/well). After 24 h, cells were treated with the indicated concentrations of inhibitors or 0.1% DMSO. For siRNA experiments, cells were transfected under the same conditions. Cell viability was measured daily using CCK‐8 (Dojindo). IC50 values were calculated with GraphPad Prism 10. Experiments were performed in triplicate and repeated at least three times independently.

Cell Migration and Invasion Assays

Cell migration assays were performed using Corning Falcon cell culture inserts, and invasion assays were conducted using Corning BioCoat Matrigel invasion chambers (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturers' instructions. A total of 5 × 104 cells were seeded into the upper chambers in 500 μL of serum‐free RPMI 1640 medium, while 750 μL of RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% CSS was added to the lower chambers as a chemoattractant. After incubation at 37°C for 72 h, migrated or invaded cells were stained using the Diff Quik kit (Funakoshi, Tokyo, Japan) and counted in three randomly selected microscopic fields per insert using ImageJ software. Data represent the average values from three independent experiments.

Xenograft Models

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Chiba University (Approval No. 7‐33) and conducted in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. Six‐week‐old male NOD/SCID mice were subcutaneously injected with 22Rv1 cells (1 × 106) mixed 1:1 with Matrigel. When tumors reached 50–80 mm3, bilateral orchiectomy was performed. Mice (n = 5 per group) received vehicle, V‐9302 (50 mg/kg), erastin (20 mg/kg), or the combination via daily intraperitoneal injection. Doses were selected based on prior reports [42, 43]. Tumor volume (length × width2 × 0.5) and body weight were monitored. At endpoint, tumors were harvested for immunoblotting and immunohistochemistry. For shRNA xenografts, shASCT2 or control cells were implanted and treated similarly, with orchiectomy and endpoint collection performed as indicated.

RNA Sequencing Analysis

RNA sequencing and subsequent data analysis were performed as previously described [44]. Briefly, 22Rv1 cells were transfected with siRNAs and incubated for 48 h prior to total RNA extraction and sequencing.

Flow Cytometric Analysis

Cell cycle distribution was analyzed using a Sony HS800 flow cytometer as reported [40]. 22Rv1 cells were treated with V‐9302 (5 μM) or DMSO for 72 h, or transfected with siASCT2 for 72 h prior to analysis.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical staining was performed according to previously described protocols [33]. Paraffin‐embedded tumor tissue sections (4 μm thick) were incubated with primary antibodies against ASCT2 (CST #8057, D7C12, 1:100), Ki67 (8D5, 1:1000), CDK1 (E1Z6R, 1:100), cleaved caspase‐3 (Asp175, 1:100), and xCT (Abcam ab307601, 1:25). Staining intensity was scored as 3 (strong), 2 (moderate), 1 (weak), or 0 (negative). The immunohistochemistry (IHC) score was calculated as 3 × S + 2 × M + 1 × W, where S, M, and W represent the fractions (0%–100%) of tumor area showing strong, moderate, or weak staining, respectively. Two independent investigators who were blinded to clinical information evaluated all specimens.

NRF2 Overexpression Assay

Human NRF2 (NFE2L2) overexpression plasmid pRP[Exp]‐Puro‐CMV>hNFE2L2 (NM_006164.5) and the corresponding empty vector (pRP[Exp]‐Puro‐CMV>ORF Stuffer) were purchased from VectorBuilder (Chicago, IL, USA). Plasmids (> 1 μg/μL) were transiently transfected into 22Rv1 cells at ~70% confluence using Lipofectamine 3000 according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, 2.5 μg plasmid DNA per well (6‐well plates) was diluted in Opti‐MEM, mixed with Lipofectamine, and added to cells. After 8 h, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. Cells were harvested 48 h after transfection for protein and functional assays. NRF2 overexpression was confirmed by Western blot.

Intracellular ROS Assay

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using 2′,7′‐dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH‐DA). Cells were seeded in 6‐well plates and cultured for 48 h, followed by drug treatment or siRNA transfection for 24 h. The medium was replaced with serum‐free medium prior to staining. For positive control, cells were treated with hydrogen peroxide (100 μM) for 2 h. Cells were incubated with DCFH‐DA (10 μM) at 37°C for 30 min in the dark, washed three times with serum‐free medium, and imaged under identical exposure settings using a fluorescence microscope. Fluorescence intensity was quantified with ImageJ.

Glutathione and GSSG Measurement

Intracellular total glutathione and GSSG levels were measured using a glutathione quantification kit according to the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, cells were treated with control, V‐9302, erastin, or the combination for the indicated duration, washed with PBS, and lysed under deproteinizing conditions. Total glutathione and GSSG concentrations were calculated from standard curves. Reduced GSH was calculated as total glutathione −2 × GSSG, and the GSH/GSSG ratio was used as an index of intracellular redox status.

C11‐BODIPY Lipid Peroxidation Assay

Lipid peroxidation was assessed using C11‐BODIPY 581/591. After treatment, cells were incubated with C11‐BODIPY at 37°C in the dark, washed, counterstained with Hoechst 33342, and imaged under identical exposure settings using a fluorescence microscope. Oxidized C11‐BODIPY fluorescence was quantified using ImageJ.

Ferrostatin‐1 and z‐VAD Rescue Assays

To discriminate ferroptosis‐related and apoptosis‐related cell death, cells were treated with ferrostatin‐1 or z‐VAD in the presence or absence of V‐9302 and erastin. Cell viability was measured using CCK‐8, and rescue effects were compared across treatment groups.

Annexin V/PI Staining

Apoptotic cells were detected using Annexin V‐FITC and propidium iodide staining according to the manufacturer's instructions. After treatment, cells were stained, counterstained with Hoechst 33342 where indicated, and imaged by fluorescence microscopy. Annexin V‐positive cells were quantified from randomly selected fields using ImageJ.

Analysis of Clinical Prostate Cancer Datasets

Publicly available prostate cancer datasets were analyzed using cBioPortal and the UCSC Xena platform. Neuroendocrine prostate cancer (NEPC) data were obtained from the cBioPortal study (nepc_wcm_2016 dataset). Gene expression data for prostate adenocarcinoma and normal prostate tissues were retrieved from TCGA‐PRAD and GTEx prostate cohorts through the UCSC Xena browser. All datasets were downloaded and processed according to the default settings of each platform.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10, Microsoft Excel, SPSS, and R software. Differences between two groups were analyzed using two‐tailed t‐test, paired t‐test, or unpaired t‐test as appropriate. One‐way or two‐way analysis of variance was applied for comparisons among multiple groups.

Associations between clinicopathological variables, IHC score, and recurrence‐free survival (RFS) were assessed using univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were compared using the log‐rank test. A p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

ASCT2 Is Upregulated in Prostate Cancer and Associated With Poor Prognosis

To establish the clinical relevance of ASCT2, ASCT2 expression was examined in prostatectomy specimens by IHC. ASCT2 IHC scores were significantly higher in tumor tissues than in matched benign glands (paired t‐test, p < 0.0001, Figure 1A,B). Representative staining patterns across Gleason scores were consistently observed in an expanded cohort (Figure S1A,B). Analysis of public datasets further confirmed significantly increased ASCT2 expression in prostate cancer compared with normal prostate tissues (unpaired t‐test, p < 0.0001, Figure 1C). ASCT2 expression was also significantly higher in NEPC than in CRPC (unpaired t‐test, p = 0.0006, Figure 1D). Using the median ASCT2 IHC score as the cutoff, high ASCT2 expression was associated with significantly shorter RFS (log‐rank test, p = 0.00047, Figure 1E). In multivariable Cox regression analysis, high ASCT2 expression was independently correlated with earlier recurrence (HR = 5.665, p = 0.026, Table 2). Collectively, these results show that ASCT2 is upregulated in prostate cancer and correlates with adverse clinicopathologic outcomes.

ASCT2 Supports Growth, Motility, and Amino Acid Uptake in 22Rv1 Cells

Based on the clinical results, we next evaluated the functional requirement of ASCT2. Among a panel of prostate cancer cell lines, ASCT2 expression was highest in the 22Rv1 cell line (Figure 2A). In 22Rv1 cells, knocking down ASCT2 significantly suppressed cell proliferation over time (Figure 2B) and markedly reduced migration and invasion (Figure 2C,D). Simultaneously, ASCT2 depletion significantly decreased glutamine consumption and [14C]‐leucine uptake (Figure 2E,F), showing impaired amino acid transport capacity. Because ASCT2‐mediated glutamine uptake can support LAT1‐dependent amino acid exchange, [14C]‐leucine uptake was used as a functional readout of coordinated amino acid transporter activity downstream of ASCT2‐dependent glutamine transport. The efficiency of ASCT2 silencing and siRNA titration experiments is shown in (Figure S2A,B). Collectively, these results show that ASCT2 is necessary for proliferation, motility, and amino acid uptake in 22Rv1 prostate cancer cells, providing a mechanistic basis for subsequent investigation of ASCT2‐mediated nutrient transport in cell cycle regulation and stress‐adaptive signaling.

ASCT2 Silencing Disrupts Cell Cycle Regulatory Signaling and Induces NRF2‐Associated Antioxidant Gene Expression

To delineate the molecular ramifications of ASCT2 depletion, transcriptomic profiling was performed in 22Rv1 cells 48 h after siASCT2 transfection. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis showed that pathways associated with the DNA damage response, p53 signaling, cell cycle control, apoptosis, and oxidative stress were far more common than expected (Figure 3A). Based on the enrichment of cell cycle associated pathways, we next examined the expression of representative genes involved in cell cycle control by qRT‐PCR (Figure 3B), and validated the corresponding protein alterations by Western blot analysis (Figure 3C). Densitometric quantification confirmed reduced phosphorylation of AKT and MAPK, decreased c‐Myc and CDK1 expression, and increased p‐p53/p21‐associated signaling after ASCT2 knockdown (Figure 3C, Figure S3). To assess the functional impact of these signaling changes, cell cycle distribution was analyzed by flow cytometry (Figure 3D,E), while additional cell cycle associated gene expression data are shown in (Figure S4B). In addition to these cell cycle changes, we next investigated whether ASCT2 depletion induced an adaptive response to metabolic stress. Heatmap analysis of significantly upregulated and downregulated transcripts from the RNA‐seq dataset revealed that the cystine transporter xCT was among the most strongly induced genes (Figure 3F). To determine whether xCT induction occurred as part of a broader NRF2‐associated antioxidant response, we further analyzed oxidative stress‐related genes in the RNA‐seq dataset. Volcano plot analysis showed upregulation of several NRF2‐associated antioxidant genes, including SLC7A11, NFE2L2, HMOX1, and NQO1 (Figure 3G). To determine whether this induction reflected a generalized compensatory response among amino acid transporters or a selective adaptive event, we compared expression changes of major transporters, including LAT1, LAT3 (SLC43A1), and xCT. Importantly, xCT expression was selectively and significantly upregulated following ASCT2 knockdown, whereas changes in LAT1 and LAT3 did not reach statistical significance (Figure 3H).

Consistent with a selective transporter response, ASCT2 depletion did not appreciably increase LAT1 protein levels, whereas LAT1 silencing induced ASCT2 expression (Figure S4A).

Considering the recognized function of xCT in cystine transport and cellular redox equilibrium, these results led us to investigate whether ASCT2 inhibition modifies intracellular redox status. GO analysis of the siASCT2 transcriptome revealed pathways linked to oxidative stress (Figure 3A), corroborating this notion. These findings suggest that oxidative stress‐responsive signaling may contribute to xCT induction following ASCT2 depletion.

Together, these data demonstrate that ASCT2 depletion disrupts cell cycle regulatory signaling while selectively inducing xCT expression, indicating a targeted redox‐adaptive response rather than a global compensatory upregulation of alternative amino acid transporters.

Pharmacologic Inhibition of ASCT2 Recapitulates the Effects of Genetic ASCT2 Silencing in 22Rv1 Cells

The ASCT2 inhibitor V‐9302 decreased the viability of 22Rv1 cells in a concentration‐dependent manner (Figure 4A) and significantly suppressed cell proliferation over time (Figure 4B). Consistent with genetic silencing, V‐9302 treatment decreased glutamine consumption and [14C]‐leucine uptake (Figure 4C,D). Flow cytometric analysis revealed a reduced proportion of cells in the G2/M phase following V‐9302 treatment (Figure 4E,F). At the molecular level, Western blot analysis and densitometric quantification showed that V‐9302 treatment reduced p‐MAPK, p‐AKT, c‐Myc, and Cyclin D1 expression and increased p53, p‐p53, and p21 expression, consistent with the effects of ASCT2 knockdown (Figure 4G, Figure S5). In parallel, increased inhibitory phosphorylation of CDK1 (Tyr15) and decreased cyclin B1 and cyclin B2 levels were observed (Figure S4C). In addition, V‐9302 significantly inhibited cell migration and invasion (Figure S2D,E). In summary, these results demonstrate that pharmacologic ASCT2 inhibition recapitulates the effects of genetic ASCT2 silencing on amino acid transport, cell growth, and related signaling pathways in 22Rv1 prostate cancer cells.

ASCT2 Inhibition Induces xCT Expression, and xCT Supports Malignant Phenotypes in Prostate Cancer Cells

To determine whether ASCT2 inhibition alters xCT expression, we examined xCT levels after genetic and pharmacologic perturbation of ASCT2. ASCT2 knockdown increased xCT mRNA and protein expression in 22Rv1 cells, and similar xCT induction was observed in C4‐2 cells (Figure 5A). Pharmacologic inhibition of ASCT2 with V‐9302 also induced xCT expression in a dose‐dependent manner in both 22Rv1 and C4‐2 cells (Figure 5B). In contrast, xCT knockdown did not markedly alter ASCT2 expression in either cell line, indicating that xCT acts downstream of ASCT2 inhibition rather than reciprocally regulating ASCT2 expression (Figure 5C). The original full Western blot images corresponding to Figure 5A–C are shown in Figure S6. Functionally, xCT knockdown suppressed proliferation, migration, and invasion in 22Rv1 cells, and erastin treatment similarly reduced cell growth in a dose‐dependent manner (Figure 5D–H).

ASCT2 Inhibition Activates a ROS‐Dependent NRF2/xCT Antioxidant Response in Prostate Cancer Cells

Since oxidative stress signatures were enriched in the siASCT2 transcriptome and xCT was identified as a prominent upregulated NRF2‐associated redox gene (Figure 3A,F,G), we next tested whether ASCT2 inhibition increases ROS accumulation and activates canonical NRF2‐dependent antioxidant signaling. ASCT2 inhibition increased intracellular ROS accumulation in both 22Rv1 and C4‐2 cells, as shown by DCFH‐DA fluorescence after V‐9302 treatment or ASCT2 knockdown (Figure 6A). To determine whether oxidative stress was sufficient to induce xCT expression, cells were treated with H2O2. H2O2 reduced cell viability in a dose‐dependent manner and increased xCT expression, whereas NAC attenuated H2O2‐induced xCT upregulation in both 22Rv1 and C4‐2 cells (Figure 6B–D). Because NRF2 is a central regulator of antioxidant gene expression, we next examined NRF2 nuclear accumulation. H2O2 increased nuclear NRF2 expression, whereas NAC attenuated this response in 22Rv1 cells (Figure 6E,F). Similarly, NAC reduced ASCT2 knockdown‐induced and V‐9302‐induced NRF2 nuclear accumulation (Figure 6G–J). Consistent with this NRF2‐dependent response, NAC also attenuated ASCT2 knockdown‐induced and V‐9302‐induced xCT expression (Figure 6K–N). Finally, siRNA‐mediated NRF2 knockdown reduced xCT expression in both 22Rv1 and C4‐2 cells, whereas NRF2 overexpression increased nuclear NRF2 accumulation and xCT expression in both cell lines (Figure 6O–T). The original full Western blot images corresponding to Figure 6C–S are shown in Figure S7. Collectively, these data demonstrate that ASCT2 inhibition activates a ROS‐dependent NRF2/xCT antioxidant response in prostate cancer cells.

Combined Inhibition of ASCT2 and xCT Disrupts Glutathione Redox Homeostasis and Enhances ROS Accumulation in Prostate Cancer Cells

To determine whether dual inhibition of ASCT2 and xCT produces greater antitumor effects and redox stress, we evaluated cell growth and intracellular ROS levels in 22Rv1 cells. Pharmacologic inhibition with V‐9302 or erastin alone moderately suppressed cell proliferation, whereas combined treatment resulted in significantly greater growth inhibition (Figure 7A). Consistently, genetic silencing of ASCT2 or xCT alone reduced proliferation, while combined knockdown produced the strongest inhibitory effect (Figure 7B). Fluorescence imaging revealed increased intracellular ROS levels following either single treatment, with a more pronounced accumulation in the combination groups, comparable to hydrogen peroxide exposure (Figure 7C). Quantitative analysis confirmed that combined pharmacologic treatment significantly elevated ROS levels compared with either agent alone (Figure 7D). Similar results were observed after combined genetic silencing of ASCT2 and xCT (Figure 7E). Because xCT imports cystine in exchange for intracellular glutamate, we next examined whether xCT induction after ASCT2 inhibition was associated with functional maintenance of glutathione‐dependent redox buffering. In 22Rv1 cells, V‐9302 treatment reduced GSH levels and the GSH/GSSG ratio, consistent with impaired glutamine‐dependent redox capacity, whereas erastin reduced GSH levels and increased GSSG accumulation (Figure 7F–H). Notably, combined V‐9302 and erastin treatment produced the most pronounced increase in GSSG and the greatest reduction in GSH levels and the GSH/GSSG ratio (Figure 7F–H). Similar changes were observed in C4‐2 cells, supporting the generalizability of this redox response in an additional prostate cancer model (Figure 7I–K). These findings indicate that xCT induction after ASCT2 inhibition represents an adaptive response that partially preserves residual glutathione redox buffering, whereas simultaneous xCT blockade disrupts this compensation.

Combined Inhibition of ASCT2 and xCT Induces Lipid Peroxidation but Predominantly Promotes Apoptotic Cell Death

Because xCT inhibition is closely associated with ferroptosis, we next evaluated lipid peroxidation using C11‐BODIPY 581/591. Combined V‐9302 and erastin treatment markedly increased oxidized C11‐BODIPY fluorescence in 22Rv1 and C4‐2 cells, and this increase was suppressed by ferrostatin‐1 (Figure 8A–F). These results indicate that ferroptosis‐related lipid peroxidation is induced by dual ASCT2/xCT inhibition. However, ferrostatin‐1 only partially restored cell viability, whereas z‐VAD produced a stronger rescue effect (Figure 8C,F). Consistently, Annexin V/PI staining showed a marked increase in Annexin V‐positive apoptotic cells after combined treatment in both 22Rv1 and C4‐2 cells (Figure 8G–J). At the molecular level, combined V‐9302 and erastin treatment markedly increased cleavage of caspase‐9, caspase‐3, and PARP compared with single‐agent treatment (Figure 8K–N, Figure S8A). Consistently, simultaneous knockdown of ASCT2 and xCT enhanced activation of these apoptotic markers compared with individual knockdown (Figure 8O–R, Figure S8B). Collectively, these data suggest that ferroptosis‐related lipid peroxidation contributes to the cellular response, but apoptosis represents the predominant cell death mechanism under combined ASCT2 and xCT inhibition.

Combined Inhibition of ASCT2 and xCT Exerts Superior Antitumor Effects Compared With Single‐Agent Treatment in Castrated Xenograft Models

To assess the therapeutic significance of concurrent ASCT2 and xCT inhibition in vivo, we developed castrated 22Rv1 xenograft models and randomized mice into four treatment groups, including control, V‐9302, erastin, and the combination (Figure 9A). V‐9302 or erastin alone suppressed tumor growth compared with control, and the strongest growth inhibition was observed with the combined treatment (Figure 9B–D). Endpoint tumor weights were lowest in the combination group, indicating enhanced antitumor activity compared with each individual treatment (Figure 9C). Tumor growth curves showed that combined treatment led to sustained suppression of tumor growth throughout the treatment period (Figure 9D). Body weights were comparable among all groups, indicating that the combination treatment did not cause significant body weight loss during the treatment period (Figure 9E). In line with the in vitro observations, immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated increased xCT expression in tumors from V‐9302–treated mice compared with controls (Figure 9F). This increase was also detected by Western blot analysis of tumor lysates (Figure 9G,H, Figure S8C), indicating that xCT upregulation represents an adaptive response to ASCT2 inhibition in vivo. Collectively, these results demonstrate that combined inhibition of ASCT2 and xCT exerts superior antitumor activity compared with single‐agent treatment in castrated xenografts, without significant body weight loss under the conditions tested, and that xCT induction serves as a pharmacodynamic marker of ASCT2 blockade in vivo. To further confirm on‐target ASCT2 dependency under castrated conditions, shRNA‐mediated ASCT2 silencing significantly suppressed 22Rv1 xenograft growth and reduced Ki67 and CDK1 staining while increasing cleaved caspase‐3 expression (Figure S8D–I).

Discussion

Our study demonstrates ASCT2 as a critical metabolic regulator in prostate cancer and identifies a redox adaptive resistance program that limits the antitumor efficacy of ASCT2 inhibition. We show that ASCT2 is overexpressed in prostate cancer and is linked to early recurrence on its own, which supports its use as a clinical marker of aggressive disease. Functional analyses further confirm that ASCT2 is important to sustain proliferation, migration, invasion, and amino acid uptake in prostate cancer cells. These results are consistent with the established role of glutamine metabolism in supporting anabolic growth and cellular homeostasis in advanced malignancies [26, 45, 46].

The major insight of this study is that ASCT2 inhibition disrupts intracellular redox homeostasis [47, 48]. Glutamine serves as a major carbon and nitrogen source for glutathione synthesis and mitochondrial metabolism [49, 50], and interference with glutamine transport therefore predisposes tumor cells to oxidative stress [51]. In line with this metabolic framework, ASCT2 blockade resulted in marked accumulation of ROS and activation of NRF2 signaling. NRF2 is a major regulator of antioxidant defense and is canonically activated in response to oxidative stress [52, 53].

In our study, ASCT2 inhibition promoted NRF2 nuclear accumulation, providing direct evidence of NRF2 pathway activation in prostate cancer cells. NAC attenuated ASCT2 inhibition‐induced NRF2 nuclear accumulation and xCT upregulation, supporting ROS accumulation as an upstream trigger of the NRF2/xCT response. These findings indicate that ASCT2 inhibition elicits a ROS‐dependent antioxidant program predominantly mediated by NRF2 signaling.

Among NRF2‐associated antioxidant genes, xCT was consistently elevated after ASCT2 suppression under various experimental conditions in the present study, including genetic and pharmacologic perturbation as well as oxidative stress modulation. xCT promotes cystine uptake essential for glutathione production and constitutes a pivotal element of cellular redox buffering [31, 54]. The selective induction of xCT, in contrast to the steady expression of other amino acid transporters like LAT1, suggests that ASCT2 inhibition triggers a targeted redox‐oriented adaptive response rather than a generalized compensatory upregulation of alternative transport systems.

It is important to note that an increase in the transcription of xCT does not always mean that the flow of cystine/glutamate exchange will also increase when there is less glutamine available. The observed elevation of xCT is likely a sign of an NRF2‐mediated stress response mechanism designed to maintain redox equilibrium, even when intracellular glutamate supply may be constrained by ASCT2 inhibition or partially compensated through alternative metabolic sources.

Although p53 has been reported to repress SLC7A11 transcription and regulate cystine metabolism under specific stress contexts, our data suggest that xCT induction following ASCT2 inhibition is predominantly governed by ROS‐dependent NRF2 activation [55, 56]. This supports the context‐dependent regulation of xCT, in which oxidative stress‐responsive NRF2 signaling can override or counterbalance p53‐mediated repression. This functional link between glutamine metabolism and cystine‐dependent antioxidant defense provides a mechanistic basis for metabolic adaptation in prostate cancer cells.

In parallel with redox remodeling, ASCT2 inhibition disrupted cell cycle regulatory signaling, as evidenced by p53 and p21 induction, inhibitory phosphorylation of CDK1, suppression of cyclin B1 and B2, and a reduction in the G2/M population. These findings are consistent with impaired G2/M transition rather than accumulation at the G2/M phase, linking metabolic stress to coordinated control of cell cycle progression.

Activation of the NRF2/xCT axis promotes cellular survival by supporting redox homeostasis under metabolic stress conditions [57, 58, 59, 60]. Our glutathione measurements further clarify this mechanism. ASCT2 inhibition alone reduced GSH levels and the GSH/GSSG ratio, indicating that impaired glutamine uptake weakens glutathione‐dependent reducing capacity. Nevertheless, xCT induction likely represents an adaptive attempt to preserve residual cystine acquisition and antioxidant buffering under metabolic stress. When xCT was simultaneously inhibited, GSSG accumulation increased further and the GSH/GSSG ratio declined most prominently, demonstrating disruption of this residual redox compensation. Thus, xCT does not appear to completely restore glutathione homeostasis after ASCT2 inhibition. Rather, it provides a partial survival buffer that becomes critical when ASCT2‐mediated glutamine supply is limited.

Because erastin is widely used as a ferroptosis‐inducing compound through xCT inhibition, it was important to determine whether ferroptosis contributed to the effects of combined ASCT2 and xCT inhibition. Our C11‐BODIPY and ferrostatin‐1 rescue experiments indicate that lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis‐related processes are induced by the combination treatment. However, ferrostatin‐1 only partially rescued cell viability, whereas z‐VAD produced a stronger protective effect. In addition, Annexin V/PI staining, together with caspase and PARP cleavage, demonstrated robust apoptotic activation. These findings support a mixed oxidative cell death response in which ferroptosis‐related lipid peroxidation occurs, but apoptosis appears to be the predominant mechanism of cell death.

In castrated xenograft models, simultaneous inhibition of ASCT2 and xCT produced the greatest suppression of tumor growth without significant body weight loss during the treatment period. Induction of xCT in tumors after ASCT2 suppression functioned as an in vivo indicator of metabolic stress, supporting its potential as a pharmacodynamic marker. In line with this adaptive model, the examination of independent clinical datasets further validated the increased expression of xCT in prostate cancer tissues (Figure S1B–E). However, body weight monitoring alone does not fully establish systemic safety. Comprehensive toxicity assessment, including serum chemistry, complete blood counts, and histological examination of major organs, will be required in future studies to define the tolerability of this combination treatment more rigorously.

One limitation of this study is that direct measurements of intracellular glutamate, cysteine, and cystine uptake were not performed. Therefore, although GSH/GSSG measurements and rescue experiments support the functional involvement of glutathione‐dependent redox buffering, further metabolite profiling and cystine flux analyses will be required to define the transport‐level mechanism in greater detail.

In summary, our data support a model in which ASCT2 represents a primary metabolic dependency, whereas xCT functions as an inducible adaptive response activated through ROS‐dependent NRF2 signaling following ASCT2 inhibition. This response partially preserves residual glutathione redox buffering under glutamine‐limited conditions. Combined targeting of ASCT2 and xCT disrupts this compensatory redox mechanism, leading to GSH depletion, GSSG accumulation, lipid peroxidation, and predominantly apoptotic cell death in prostate cancer cells.
