Clinical factors associated with mortality in ebola virus disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Brandon Kayembe, Beni Matangi, Placide Matondo, Axel Okolo, Patrick Mbo, Dieudonné Tawaba, Amede Kinuka, Ben Bepouka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEbola virus disease (EVD) remains a major global public health threat because of its high case fatality rate and recurrent outbreaks, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa. Despite advances in supportive care and targeted therapies, mortality remains substantial and varies widely across epidemics and healthcare settings. A robust quantitative synthesis is therefore needed to estimate overall mortality and identify clinical determinants associated with death among patients with EVD.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. PubMed, Google Scholar, and Index Medicus Africa were searched from inception to November 10, 2025. Observational studies reporting mortality among patients with EVD were included. Study quality was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal checklist. A random-effects meta-analysis using restricted maximum likelihood estimation was performed to calculate pooled mortality and odds ratios (ORs) for factors associated with death. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, and subgroup analyses were conducted by epidemic period, region, sample size, and age group. Reported case fatality proportions ranged from 34% to 79%. The pooled mortality was 54% (95% CI: 45–63), with very high heterogeneity (I² = 98.2%). Mortality was higher during the West African epidemic (2014–2016) than during more recent Central African outbreaks (2018–2020). Pediatric mortality appeared lower than adult mortality, although confidence intervals overlapped. Hemorrhagic manifestations were strongly associated with death, whereas age was not independently associated with mortality in pooled analyses. No significant publication bias was detected.ConclusionsEVD remains associated with high mortality, although improvements in outcomes are evident in more recent outbreaks. Background

Ebola virus disease (EVD) is a severe viral zoonosis caused by viruses of the genus Ebolavirus. Its high case fatality rate and capacity for human-to-human transmission have made it a major global public health threat since its first description in 1976 [1, 2]. Despite recent advances in clinical management and therapeutic tools, EVD remains associated with substantial mortality, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, where most outbreaks occur [3].

The case fatality rate of EVD varies considerably across epidemic episodes, viral species involved, and levels of available care, generally ranging from 25% to 90% [4–6]. The 2013–2016 West African epidemic the largest ever documented enabled an unprecedented accumulation of clinical data, demonstrating that mortality was neither uniform nor random [7, 8]. Observational studies have identified several factors potentially associated with death, including advanced age, high viral load at admission (low Ct value), the presence of severe gastrointestinal symptoms, hemorrhage, and multiorgan involvement [9–12].

However, the existing literature has important limitations. Available studies are heterogeneous with respect to diagnostic criteria, definitions of mortality, therapeutic strategies, and the quality of supportive care [13, 14]. In addition, results are often derived from single-center cohorts with limited sample sizes, substantial loss to follow-up, and insufficient adjustment for confounding factors [15]. This heterogeneity compromises the extrapolation of findings and limits the use of available data to guide clinical decision-making and resource prioritization during outbreaks.

Despite the emergence of clinical trials evaluating targeted treatments such as monoclonal antibodies, mortality remains high, underscoring the critical importance of early clinical determinants and the quality of supportive care. A rigorous quantitative synthesis of existing data is therefore needed to provide an updated estimate of EVD mortality and to identify clinical determinants independently associated with death, while accounting for substantial between-study heterogeneity. This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to address this gap and provide evidence to inform clinical management and outbreak preparedness in resource-limited settings.

Methods

This study is a systematic review and quantitative meta-analysis conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. A study protocol was developed and registered in PROSPERO (CRD42025644047) [16].

Eligibility criteria

The PICOS framework was used to determine study eligibility.

Population (P): Patients or participants with EVD confirmed by laboratory testing (primarily RT-PCR), or diagnosed according to clearly reported WHO or national outbreak case definitions. Studies including both suspected and confirmed cases were eligible only when confirmed cases represented most participants or when data for confirmed cases were separately extractable. Studies including only suspected cases without clear diagnostic confirmation were excluded.

Intervention/Exposure (I): Exposure corresponded to clinical, biological, and demographic determinants.

Comparator (C): Not applicable.

Outcome (O): The primary outcome was all-cause mortality during the acute EVD episode (including in-hospital mortality, mortality during Ebola Treatment Unit admission, or mortality during the reported study follow-up period). When multiple mortality time points were reported, the longest available follow-up during the acute illness period was preferentially extracted. Prespecified determinants of interest included age, sex, hemorrhagic manifestations, diarrhea, vomiting, dehydration, neurological symptoms, viral load indicators (including cycle threshold values), and other clinical or laboratory severity markers consistently reported across studies.

Study design (S): Observational studies, analytical cross-sectional studies, and mixed-methods studies.

Studies not relevant to the research question were excluded, including conference abstracts, meta-analyses, and case reports, as well as studies that did not clearly report mortality or provide sufficient data to calculate it.

Information sources

The following electronic databases were systematically searched: PubMed (MEDLINE interface), Index Medicus Africa, and Google Scholar. PubMed was used as the primary interface to access MEDLINE. Google Scholar was used as a complementary search tool to identify additional potentially relevant studies and grey literature. No restrictions were applied regarding language or publication status. Searches were conducted from database inception to November 10, 2025.

Search strategy

A comprehensive and exhaustive search was conducted across the selected electronic databases to identify studies reporting mortality and its associated factors among patients with Ebola virus disease. The search strategy was developed using a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords related to Ebola virus disease and mortality outcomes. Reference lists of all included studies were also manually screened to identify additional relevant publications.

Search strategy (detailed)

The full electronic search strategy used for PubMed was as follows:

(“Ebola” OR “Ebola virus disease” OR “Ebolavirus” OR “EVD”) AND (“mortality” OR “case fatality” OR “death” OR “survival” OR “outcome” OR “prognosis”) AND (“risk factor” OR “predictor” OR “determinant” OR “clinical factor” OR “viral load” OR “cycle threshold”). Equivalent strategies were adapted for Google Scholar and Index Medicus Africa (See the Supplementary Appendix S1).

Study selection and data extraction

All retrieved records’ abstracts and titles were checked by two separate reviewers. After that, the full texts of research that might qualify were evaluated for inclusion. Conflicts were settled by agreement or by talking to a third reviewer. A PRISMA 2020 flow diagram is used to illustrate the research selection procedure.

Data items

Data extracted from included studies comprised: author, year of publication, country, study period, study design, sample size, patient age, sex distribution, reported mortality, and clinical factors associated with death. When both adjusted and unadjusted estimates were reported, adjusted estimates were preferentially extracted. However, because adjusted estimates were inconsistently reported and adjustment models varied across studies, pooled analyses were primarily based on unadjusted estimates.

Handling of missing data

When relevant data were missing or incompletely reported, studies were included in the qualitative synthesis but excluded from specific quantitative analyses requiring unavailable information. No data imputation was performed.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed independently and in duplicate by two reviewers using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist. Each study was evaluated across the relevant methodological domains according to study design, with response options of “yes,” “no,” “unclear,” and “not applicable.” In the absence of a predefined threshold, studies were classified as having low risk of bias if they scored ≥ 70%, moderate risk if they scored 50–69%, and high risk if they scored < 50%. Discrepancies in domain-level ratings or overall judgments were resolved through discussion and consensus; when necessary, a third reviewer was consulted to reach a final decision. The assessment considered study design, participant selection, measurement of variables, completeness of outcome data, and control of potential confounding factors.

Statistical analysis

Extracted data were analyzed using STATA version 18. The pooled mortality proportion was estimated using a random-effects meta-analysis model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation. Heterogeneity between studies was assessed using Cochran’s Q test, the I² statistic, and between-study variance (τ²), with statistical significance defined as p < 0.10 for the Q test and I² > 50% indicating substantial heterogeneity. A Galbraith radial plot was also generated as a graphical method to explore heterogeneity and identify potential outlier or influential studies. Pooled estimates were reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Prediction intervals were calculated to estimate the expected range of mortality across different epidemic contexts.

Results are presented using forest plots. Subgroup analyses were conducted as exploratory analyses to describe potential sources of variability across studies. Formal statistical tests for subgroup interaction were not performed due to the limited number of included studies. Subgroup analyses were conducted according to age, sample size, and study period. Funnel plots were used to visually analyze publication bias, and Egger’s test was used to statistically quantify it.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were prespecified to assess the robustness of pooled estimates by excluding studies at higher risk of bias and by evaluating the influence of large studies on the pooled mortality estimate.

Certainty of evidence

The overall certainty of evidence for the main outcomes was assessed using the GRADE approach. Certainty assessments were conducted independently and in duplicate by two reviewers across the domains of risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus, with consultation of a third reviewer when necessary.

Results

A total of 11,092 records were identified through the three databases searched. After removal of 140 duplicate records, 10,952 titles and abstracts were screened. Following the screening process, 48 full-text reports were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 37 articles were excluded for predefined reasons, and 11 studies were ultimately included in the systematic review and meta-analysis. Details are presented in Fig. 1.

Characteristics of the included studies

Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the 11 studies included in the systematic review and meta-analysis. The studies were published between 2015 and 2025 and covered epidemic periods ranging from 2013 to 2020, including both the West African outbreak (2014–2016) and more recent outbreaks in Central Africa. Most studies were conducted in West Africa, primarily in Guinea, Sierra Leone, and Liberia, while two studies were conducted in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Sample sizes varied substantially, ranging from 61 to 14,163 patients. The reported mean or median age of participants ranged from 3 to 39 years, reflecting the inclusion of both pediatric and adult populations. The proportion of male participants ranged from approximately 30% to 64%, although sex distribution was not reported in two studies. Most included studies were observational in design, including retrospective and prospective cohorts, while one study was a randomized controlled trial whose data were included as a secondary cohort analysis.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias assessment of the included studies using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist is presented in Supplementary Table S1. Overall, the methodological quality of the included studies was moderate. Among the eleven studies included in the meta-analysis, one randomized controlled trial was classified as having a low risk of bias, while the remaining ten observational studies were judged to have a moderate risk of bias. Selection bias was considered moderate in most studies because the majority were retrospective or hospital-based cohorts, often conducted in single or limited numbers of Ebola Treatment Units, which may have reduced the representativeness of the study populations. Information bias was also rated as moderate in most studies due to the retrospective nature of data collection and the presence of missing or incomplete clinical records. Control of confounding varied across studies. Three studies used multivariable regression models or advanced statistical adjustment methods and were therefore considered to have adequate control of confounding. In the remaining studies, adjustment for potential confounders was limited, mainly due to incomplete data or restricted availability of clinical and laboratory variables. Overall, except for one randomized controlled trial that showed a low risk of bias, all included studies were observational and were therefore classified as having a moderate overall risk of bias. These methodological limitations were taken into account when interpreting the results of the meta-analysis.

Overall mortality associated with EVD

The 11 included studies reported case-fatality proportions ranging from 34% to 79%. The random-effects meta-analysis (REML) estimated an overall mortality of 54.0% (95% CI: 45.2–62.7), with very high heterogeneity (I² = 98.2%, p < 0.001). The 95% prediction interval ranged from 20.3% to 87.6%, indicating substantial variability in mortality across different settings. The pooled case-fatality proportion was 54% across the included hospitalized patients (Fig. 2). Sensitivity analyses excluding studies at high risk of bias did not materially change the pooled mortality estimate, which ranged from 52% to 56% and was consistent with the primary analysis.

Case-fatality proportion associated with Ebola virus disease based on a random-effects (REML) meta-analysis

Assessment of heterogeneity (Galbraith plot)

The Galbraith plot showed that all included studies were distributed within the 95% confidence limits, with no clear extreme outliers. Most studies were clustered around the regression line, indicating that heterogeneity was not driven by a single highly influential study. Overall, the plot did not identify any major anomalous study likely to disproportionately influence the pooled estimate (Fig. 3).

Galbraith plot assessing heterogeneity among studies included in the meta-analysis. Each point represents an individual study, with the standardized effect size plotted against the inverse of its standard error. Studies falling outside the confidence limits may contribute disproportionately to between-study heterogeneity. This graphical approach helps identify potential outlier studies that may influence the pooled estimate

Subgroup analyses

These subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity across outbreak contexts.

By region and epidemic period

Mortality during the West Africa epidemic (2014–2016) was 56% (95% CI: 46–66; I² = 98.6%). In contrast, the Central Africa epidemics (2018–2020) showed lower and more homogeneous mortality, estimated at 43% (95% CI: 40–47; I² = 0%) (Supplementary Figures S1 and S2).

By sample size

Small studies (< 200 participants) showed a pooled case-fatality proportion of 54% (95% CI: 43–66; I² = 89.1%). Intermediate-size studies (200–999 participants) reported a comparable pooled mortality of 59% (95% CI: 43–74; I² = 98.5%), with substantial between-study heterogeneity. In the subgroup of large studies (≥ 1000 participants), the pooled mortality was 52% (95% CI: 21–83; I² = 99.9%) (Supplementary Figure S3, S4 and 5).

By age group

Pediatric mortality (46%, 95% CI: 23–68; I² = 89.7%) appeared slightly lower than that observed among adults (56%, 95% CI: 46–65; I² = 98.6%). However, the wide overlap of confidence intervals and the limited number of pediatric studies do not support any definitive conclusion regarding a significant difference between age groups (Supplementary Figures S6 and S7).

Factors associated with mortality in EVD

Among the clinical factors reported across studies, hemorrhagic manifestations were strongly associated with mortality in EVD (OR = 3.52; 95% CI: 1.65–7.48). Diarrhea also appeared to be potentially associated with an increased risk of death, although the corresponding estimates remained imprecise (OR = 6.64; 95% CI: 0.85–51.66). In contrast, age analyzed as a continuous variable showed no significant association with mortality, and the very high heterogeneity observed limits the overall interpretability of this finding (Fig. 4, Supplementary Figures S8 and S9). Reported odds ratios were primarily derived from unadjusted analyses, as adjusted effect estimates were inconsistently reported across the included studies.

Association between hemorrhagic manifestations and Ebola virus disease (EVD) mortality

Assessment of publication bias

Publication bias was evaluated using a funnel plot and formal statistical tests. Mild visual asymmetry was observed in the funnel plot. Egger’s test was non-significant (β₁ = 1.71; SE = 2.20; z = 0.78; p = 0.436), and Begg’s test was also non-significant (Kendall’s score = 3.0; z = 0.16; p = 0.876), indicating no statistically detectable publication bias (Fig. 5).

Funnel plot assessing publication bias in the meta-analysis. The effect size is plotted against the standard error of each study. In the absence of publication bias, studies are expected to be symmetrically distributed around the pooled effect estimate. Asymmetry in the funnel plot may reflect publication bias, small-study effects, or substantial between-study heterogeneity

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses excluding studies at higher risk of bias and assessing the influence of large studies did not materially change the pooled mortality estimate, which remained consistent with the primary analysis.

Certainty of evidence

Using the GRADE framework, the certainty of evidence for overall pooled mortality was rated as low because of substantial between-study heterogeneity and the observational design of most included studies. The certainty of evidence for the association between hemorrhagic manifestations and mortality was rated as moderate, supported by a consistent direction of effect and a relatively large pooled effect estimate. In contrast, certainty for associations involving diarrhea and age was rated as very low because of serious imprecision, inconsistency, and the limited number of contributing studies. Full certainty assessments are presented in Supplementary Table S2.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides an updated estimate of the overall mortality of EVD, together with an analysis of the main clinical determinants associated with death. The pooled estimate obtained (54%, 95% CI: 45–63) confirms that EVD remains one of the most lethal viral infections in humans, consistent with case fatality rates reported in previous outbreaks, which range from 25% to 90% depending on the viral strain and healthcare context [1, 4–6]. These findings are also consistent with comprehensive reviews describing persistently high EVD mortality despite advances in clinical management [28].

Although previous reviews and narrative summaries have described the epidemiology, clinical presentation, and management of EVD [2, 8, 28], few studies have systematically quantified pooled mortality estimates together with clinical determinants of death across multiple outbreaks. Earlier publications were often descriptive, focused on single epidemic periods, or did not perform formal meta-analytic pooling of prognostic factors. The present review expands the available evidence by incorporating more recent studies from Central African outbreaks, including the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and by applying contemporary meta-analytic methods, including random-effects modeling, heterogeneity diagnostics, subgroup analyses, sensitivity analyses, publication bias assessment, and GRADE certainty appraisal.

The very high heterogeneity observed (I² = 98%) indicates substantial variability across epidemics and healthcare settings. This between-study heterogeneity likely reflects major contextual differences between Ebola outbreaks rather than methodological limitations alone. Several factors may explain this dispersion, including delays in access to treatment centers, differences in case management, variability in viral load at admission, and saturation of healthcare facilities, as reported in previous studies [7, 8, 10–12].

In addition, the included studies were conducted in different epidemic contexts, including the large West African outbreak (2014–2016) and subsequent epidemics in Central Africa, which differed considerably in terms of healthcare infrastructure, outbreak preparedness, and availability of specialized treatment centers. Differences in patient characteristics, such as baseline disease severity, timing of presentation, and viral load at admission, may also have influenced mortality outcomes. Furthermore, variations in supportive care practices and clinical management protocols across treatment centers, as well as methodological differences between studies (study design, sample size, and reporting of clinical variables), likely contributed to the observed heterogeneity. Overall, these findings suggest that EVD mortality is strongly influenced by epidemic context and the level of available healthcare resources. Subgroup analyses indicate that the West African epidemic (2014–2016) was associated with the highest mortality (56%), consistent with literature describing extreme strain on healthcare systems, delayed case detection, and major logistical challenges during that period [6, 7, 10, 11]. In contrast, more recent outbreaks in Central Africa (2018–2020) showed lower and more homogeneous mortality (43%), likely reflecting improvements in care organization, the introduction of standardized supportive care protocols, and access to innovative therapies such as monoclonal antibodies [29, 30]. This decline is consistent with reports from the Democratic Republic of the Congo documenting improved case management and expanded access to effective therapeutics during recent outbreaks [31].

Mortality patterns according to sample size did not reveal a clear trend. Variations observed in larger cohorts appear to be driven more by contextual and methodological differences than by sample size itself, as also reported by Richardson et al. [15] and Lamontagne et al. [30].

With regard to age, the data suggest slightly lower mortality among children (46%) compared with adults (56%). However, the wide confidence intervals and the limited number of pediatric studies preclude firm conclusions regarding a true age-related difference in mortality. These findings are consistent with those of Li et al. [9], who reported that age alone is not a strong independent predictor of mortality when viral load at admission and baseline clinical status are taken into account. The interpretation of pediatric mortality in Ebola virus disease remains challenging due to the limited availability of age-stratified data. Only a small number of studies specifically reported pediatric outcomes, age categories varied substantially between studies, and children were underrepresented in several cohorts, which may have reduced the precision of pooled estimates. Previous studies have suggested that factors such as viral load at admission, nutritional status, baseline disease severity, and delays in access to care may play an important role in determining outcomes among pediatric patients. However, these variables were not consistently reported across the studies included in this meta-analysis, which limited the ability to explore age-specific predictors of mortality.

Among the clinical factors associated with mortality, hemorrhagic manifestations were strongly associated with mortality (OR = 3.52), confirming their role as markers of severe disease in the pathophysiology of EVD, as demonstrated in several clinical studies [10, 11]. Diarrhea also appeared to be associated with increased mortality, although estimates remained imprecise, likely due to variability in clinical definitions and the limited number of studies reporting this parameter. Similar associations between markers of clinical severity and mortality have been described in previous clinical and observational studies of EVD outcomes [10–12]. The lack of a significant association between age (as a continuous variable) and mortality may reflect complex interactions between age, viral load, comorbidities, and disease severity, as previously suggested by Fitzpatrick and colleagues and Yan et al. [7, 12]. An important limitation of this analysis is that most pooled effect estimates were derived from unadjusted odds ratios reported in observational studies. Such estimates may be influenced by confounding factors, including baseline disease severity, viral load at admission, comorbidities, and delays in accessing treatment centers. Because these variables were not consistently reported across studies, it was not possible to perform pooled analyses based exclusively on adjusted estimates. Therefore, the associations identified in this meta-analysis should be interpreted with caution and should not be considered causal, as they may reflect residual confounding and complex interactions between clinical severity, viral load, and access to care.

Assessment of publication bias did not reveal significant bias. Although slight visual asymmetry was observed in the funnel plot, Egger’s and Begg’s tests did not indicate statistically significant asymmetry, suggesting that the observed dispersion is more likely attributable to extreme between-study heterogeneity rather than true publication bias [14]. The interpretation of funnel plot asymmetry should be approached cautiously in the presence of extreme heterogeneity and a relatively small number of included studies. Visual asymmetry may reflect true differences between outbreak contexts rather than selective publication. In addition, variability in study design, sample size, and clinical management practices may contribute to the observed dispersion of effect estimates.

Overall, this meta-analysis confirms that EVD mortality remains high, affecting more than one in two patients in the studied contexts. Accordingly, the pooled mortality estimate should be interpreted as a descriptive summary of reported outcomes across heterogeneous outbreak settings, rather than as a precise prognostic value applicable to individual epidemics or clinical contexts. Despite recent advances in therapeutics, timely access to care, initial viral load, and the quality of supportive care remain major determinants of prognosis. These findings underscore the importance of strengthening surveillance capacity, improving early access to specialized treatment centers, and ensuring the continued implementation of effective therapies particularly monoclonal antibodies to further reduce EVD-associated mortality.

Implications for practice and public health

The findings of this meta-analysis have important clinical and public health implications. Early recognition of hemorrhagic manifestations should prompt intensified supportive care and prioritization for advanced therapies. The observed reduction in mortality in more recent outbreaks highlights the critical role of early access to specialized treatment centers, standardized clinical protocols, and rapid deployment of trained healthcare teams. Strengthening surveillance systems and outbreak preparedness remains essential to further reduce EVD-associated mortality, particularly in resource-limited settings. Continued efforts to strengthen healthcare infrastructure, ensure rapid access to Ebola treatment units, and implement evidence-based clinical protocols remain essential to improving survival. Expanding access to effective therapies, including monoclonal antibodies, and improving early case detection are critical components of future outbreak response strategies.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several important strengths. First, it was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines and prospectively registered in PROSPERO, ensuring methodological transparency and reducing the risk of selective reporting. Second, the inclusion of studies from multiple epidemic periods and geographic regions allowed for a comprehensive assessment of Ebola virus disease (EVD) mortality across diverse healthcare and outbreak contexts. Third, the use of robust random-effects meta-analytic methods, complemented by subgroup analyses and formal assessments of heterogeneity and publication bias, strengthens the reliability and interpretability of the pooled estimates. Finally, the identification of key clinical determinants, particularly hemorrhagic manifestations, provides findings with direct relevance for clinical risk stratification and outbreak response planning. Several limitations should be acknowledged. The analysis relied exclusively on observational studies, limiting causal inference and increasing susceptibility to residual confounding, as well as selection and information biases. The high between-study heterogeneity reflects substantial variability in study design, case definitions, standards of care, and epidemic conditions, and suggests that pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution. In addition, important clinical variables such as viral load at admission, timing of presentation, comorbidities, and markers of disease severity were inconsistently reported and could not be included in the pooled analyses. Pediatric-specific data were limited, restricting conclusions regarding age-related differences in mortality. The literature search, although comprehensive and including PubMed (MEDLINE), Index Medicus Africa, and Google Scholar, did not include additional databases such as Embase or Cochrane CENTRAL, which may have limited the identification of some studies. Furthermore, subgroup analyses were exploratory and no formal statistical tests for subgroup differences were performed; therefore, comparisons between outbreak periods should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, although no significant publication bias was detected, selective reporting cannot be entirely excluded. These limitations highlight the need for standardized data collection, improved reporting of key clinical variables, and more rigorous study designs in future outbreaks.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrates that EVD remains associated with high mortality, affecting more than half of patients across studied settings. Although substantial heterogeneity exists between epidemics, the observed reduction in mortality in more recent outbreaks suggests that improvements in outbreak response and clinical management can meaningfully improve patient outcomes. Hemorrhagic manifestations emerged as a key clinical determinant of death, highlighting the importance of early recognition and prompt initiation of intensified supportive care. These findings have important clinical and public health implications, emphasizing that timely access to appropriate treatment remains a major determinant of survival. The decline in mortality observed in more recent outbreaks likely reflects several advances, including earlier identification of cases, improved surveillance systems, faster referral to specialized treatment centers, and the implementation of standardized supportive care protocols. In addition, the introduction of effective therapeutic strategies, particularly monoclonal antibody therapies, has contributed to improved outcomes in recent epidemics. These results underscore the need to continue strengthening healthcare infrastructure, ensuring early access to specialized care, and expanding the availability of effective treatments in outbreak settings. Sustained investment in preparedness, clinical training, and rapid response capacity will be essential to further reduce EVD-associated mortality, particularly in resource-limited settings where delays in diagnosis and treatment remain common.
