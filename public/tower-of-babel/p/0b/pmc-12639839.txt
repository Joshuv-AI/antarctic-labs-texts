European public opinion and foreign policy. A continuity dataset (1952–1970)
Authors: Luciana Fazio, Pierangelo Isernia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis dataset pools together a series of surveys on foreign and defense policy issues conducted by the United States Information Agency (USIA) of the United States in Germany, Great Britain, France, and Italy between 1952 and 1970. The data have been collected from different sources and merged, to offer a continuity dataset, namely, a dataset that includes a set of questions that have been asked more than once over almost 20 years in all 4 countries. The creation of the dataset had to face important technical challenges due to the obsolete multipunched format in which the original data were often stored and the scarce (and often poorly kept) documentation that accompanied them. Some of these technical problems were overcome thanks to the efforts of several assistants and researchers who retrieved the necessary information, cleaned and transferred these surveys to a readable format. The final product of this effort is a cross-section-time-series data set containing the response over time of approximately 80,000 European individuals from four European countries over almost two decades on issues related to peace and war, foreign and defense policies, European integration, and Transatlantic relations. These data do not only shed a unique light at what the public in Europe was thinking about war and peace during the Cold War but they allow to analyze the relationship between policy-making and public attitudes in a comparative perspective over a long-range period.Keywords: Public opinion, Security policy, Defence policy, Germany, Great Britain, France, Italy, Survey data

Value of the Data

•Why are these data valuable?The dataset combines together a very rich set of survey questions, asked more than once, to approximately 80,000 people in 4 European countries during the 1950s and 1960s about topics related to defense and foreign policy. This is a unique source of data to study public opinion in foreign policy. This data can be useful for different theoretical and methodological purposes. We highlight here two in particular. First, they provide a long term perspective into the evolution of public attitudes toward European integration and, the USA, the USSR, NATO, war, and disarmament. Such a longer term perspective is particularly relevant for the study of Transatlantic relations, since they are characterized by multiple periods of crisis, each of which tend to be perceived as unique and existential for the sustainability of the Transatlantic community. This data would allow researchers in the field to put present (and past) crises in a wider comparative perspective. This is a particularly topical issue nowadays, with the present state of tension with the Trump administration. The second is the opportunity, using granular data, to apply new methodological tools (e.g. data mining and multilevel time-series cross-sectional analysis) to test arguments about the sources of stability in international relations in areas characterized by complex interdependence, multilevel governance and alliances behavior.

Background

In 1949, Paul Lazarsfeld [2] stressed “The obligation of the 1950 pollster to the 1984 historian”, urging the collaboration between historians and political scientists for a long-span data set on public attitudes and opinions. More than seventy years after this plea, it is probably time to assess whether such an obligation can be fulfilled and what consequence this might have for our present and future research on public opinion and public policy. This continuity guide is a testimony to the fact that this obligation was felt. The dataset builds a time series cross-section individual-level dataset in four European countries (France, Germany, Italy, and the United Kingdom) during the 1950s and 1960s. The data focuses on foreign and security policy. This dataset origins in the work of Donald Puchala, Ricard Merritt [3] and, especially, Karl Deutsch [4] who first developed the concept of Transatlantic Security Community to describe the evolution of the political relationships between the (Western) European countries and the United States after World War II. While some of these data have been exploited in the past, still the scattered nature of the data has always prevented a more systematic exploitation of them.

Data Description

The dataset is saved as a STATA (.dta) and as a Comma-separated values (CSV) file. It contains 340 variables and 81,755 observations. The number of respondents in Germany was 21,172, in France 19,852, in Great Britain 16,796, and in Italy 23,935, for a total of 81,755 respondents. All surveys were based on face-to-face interviews. All samples were recruited via quota sampling - the most popular method of sample selection at that time - using region, age and gender as main quotas. All respondents were at least 16 years old. It should be noted that the number of observations varies depending on the variable, as not all questions were asked in every survey; therefore, some variables have more observations than others. For example, variable 600, “Favor of US foreign policy”, has 40,694 observations, while variable 516, “Unified Europe and the US”, has 9414. This apparent divergence is due to the fact that the former was asked in nine surveys, while the latter was asked in only two. Additionally, it is essential to note that not all questionnaires cover all four countries, as illustrated in Table 1. Experimental Design, Materials and mMthods

This dataset includes the individual responses to all questions that have been asked at least twice on foreign and defense policy issues in four European countries – Germany, Great Britain, France, and Italy – between 1952 and 1970. This is then a repeated cross-sectional survey dataset. Individuals are different from sample to sample both across countries and over time. This format does not allow panel data analysis, but it allows, using repeated cross-sectional design (Brady and Johnston [5]) and pseudo-panels technique (Verbeek [6]), to analyze the evolution of attitudes on foreign and security issues and to explore interactions between public opinion and foreign policy.

To build such a dataset we explored a diversified set of archives and repositories. This process has been originally inspired by the meticulous collection of the United States Information Agency (USIA) surveys results reported in Richard L. Merritt and Donald J. Puchala [3], but it did not stop there. Beside the complete USIA XX-series set, here there are included a scattered set of ad-hoc surveys carried out in the early 50 s and late 60 s by other institutions (i.e., the ZA in Köln, Germany; the Archivio Dati per le Scienze Sociali (ADPSS) in Milan, Italy; the DPLS at the University of Wisconsin). For some of this data we are able to establish from the documentation that they were commissioned by the US government, but we are not able to assign them to the USIA XX-series.

The effort to find, acquire, clean, and set up the raw data files, built on these questions, and then to construct a unique file in which all these data are added has been a challenging effort, both technically and scientifically. This multi-step process is illustrated in Fig. 2. Dealing with surveys carried out and files built in the ‘50 and ‘60, when the flexibility and powerful storage capacity of modern PCs were not available, a lot of technical ingenuity had to be invested in making this data readable for present use. We mention here three of the most challenging problems. One was related to the electronic support on which some of this data was stored, namely the EBCD magnetic tapes. It was difficult to find an old mainframe computer in which a driver for magnetic 6-tracks tapes could be read (and we were fortunate enough to have a consultant old and experienced enough to have met such tapes in his life, in the person of Dr Robert Danziger at the European University Institute in Florence). A second challenge was represented by the outdated format in which some of these files were organized. In particular the multipunched format in which some of these data were saved made difficult a reading of the material. Last, the poor condition of the accompanying documentation did not help either. Fortunately, for some of those surveys, other Institutions have invested time and resource to make them available. In particular, some of the USIA XX-series were already cleaned and transferred to an SPSS format by the ZA in Köln (Germany) and by the Archivio Dati per le Scienze Sociali (ADPSS) in Milan (Italy). Some Italian surveys of the early 50 s conducted by DOXA were already being cleaned and transferred to an ASCII format by the DPLS at the University of Wisconsin. We thank these institutions for their collaboration. We benefitted enormously from the collaboration, comments and help of the Roper Center first at the University of Connecticut and then from 2015 on at Cornell University. The Roper Center at Cornell University is presently in the process of cleaning all USIA datasets in its availability until the early 2000s.

Data harmonization flowchart. The figure illustrates the multi-step pipeline from data collection, conversion, and cleaning, to the alignment of variables, recoding, and integration into a unified dataset.

For all survey questions we used the original English-American version as a reference text, albeit the original questions were submitted in the original language and we strived for collecting these original questionnaire as well.

Apart from these technical problems, we also had to cope with a comparability problem. Even though the common origin of several of these surveys contributes to an unprecedented level of repetition over time in many questions, a significant number of the issues related to over-time comparability still had to be addressed. This problem was particularly severe for socio-demographic variables, such as age (often recoded into classes that were not comparable across countries and over time), level of education and occupation because each survey often used different coding categories, with not much preoccupation for comparability across countries and over time. We decided however to provide comparable measures, attempting to strike an optimum balance between granularity of information and comparability across countries and over time. The recoding process was qualitative, as we collected all the response options and grouped those that matched into the same code (e.g., in the “Occupation” variable, “farmer” and “agricultural laborer” were coded with the same code). However, we also encountered many surveys with aggregated response options. For example, the “Education” variable in some surveys the response option was “No school, elementary school” while other surveys had two distinct categories: “No school” and “Primary school”. In this case, we opted to aggregate the latter into the same category to enhance compatibility between the various surveys. When in doubt, we prioritized the maximum level of disaggregation that was possible without sacrificing the comparability across surveys. For example, in variable 028, “Family income”, we used the following response options: 50,000–100,000; 50,000–250,0000; 100,000–199,999; and 100,000–500,000, since the surveys had been formulated this way, and grouping them would have meant losing information. The original responses can be found in the annex Codebook.

The final product of this effort is therefore a cross-section-time-series data set that contains the response over time of approximately 80,000 European individuals from four countries: France, Germany, Great Britain and Italy.

Examining the distribution of respondents on various topics contributes to shedding light on similarities and differences among the countries in their foreign and security policy orientations. The importance of contextual factors in explaining these differences cannot be minimized, although also socio-demographic differences sometime trump those related to nationality and orientation toward international affairs.

For example, in Table 5, we compare attitudes about the perception of the conventional military balance (a topical issue for most of the Cold War that is becoming relevant today as well) between the East and the West among women and men. It is interesting to note, for example that in Germany, France and Italy, a majority of both men and women consider that the Western and Communist forces are on balance in terms of nuclear weapons, in Great Britain most men feel the West is weaker, while women see it as stronger than the Communist camp.

Limitations

This research is based on data collected from different sources often with limited documentation available. We relied on the work first conducted by Donald Puchala [3] in the 1950s, whose results were enriched and integrated drawing upon sources from other institutions. While it is advantageous to integrate data from different sources, it can represent a limitation as each of them follows its own logic of cleaning and ordering. We also have some minor mismatches in sample size among different versions of the same dataset. Differences however are in the order of tens cases at most. In cases of multiple sources, we used the data source for which the best documentation was available. Another limit is related to open-ended questions. Due to the lack of background material on the responses, together with the limited storage capabilities of the early years of survey research, the answer to open questions have been lost and therefore these questions are not included in the dataset.
