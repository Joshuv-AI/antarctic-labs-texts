Molecular detection of shared pathogens in red foxes (Vulpes vulpes) from farming habitats in northwestern Spain
Authors: Diego Pérez-Maroto, Miguel Pérez-Pozuelo, Luis Eusebio Fidalgo, Ana María López-Beceiro, Patricia Barroso, María Montes-Bernardo, Luis J Royo, Amir Reza Varzandi, Anna Trisciuoglio, Natalia García-Álvarez, Ezio Ferroglio, Christian Gortázar, Ana Balseiro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWildlife species are increasingly inhabiting fragmented and human-modified landscapes, leading to more frequent contacts at the wildlife-livestock-human interface and creating opportunities for the circulation of shared pathogens with important implications for animal and public health, as well as for biodiversity conservation. Understanding pathogen dynamics in wildlife is therefore essential for integrated sanitary surveillance and risk assessment. In this study the occurrence of four shared pathogens – canine distemper virus (CDV), canine adenovirus type 1 (CAdV-1), Toxoplasma gondii and Neospora caninum - in red fox (Vulpes vulpes) inhabiting northwestern Spain was investigated to assess its potential role in pathogen circulation across the wildlife-livestock interface and identify factors associated with infection, i.e., the relationship between pathogen positivity and pathogen richness, individual traits (sex and age), and habitat type (farming or forest). The brains of 239 legally hunted red foxes were analysed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The highest prevalence was observed for CDV (7.53%; 95% CI: 4.5%–11.6%; 18/239), followed by T. gondii (7.11%; 95% CI: 4.19%–11.14%; 17/239) and N. caninum (4.60%; 95% CI: 2.31%–8.08%; 11/239), whereas only 1.67% (95% CI: 0.45%–4.22%; 4/239) of foxes tested positive for CAdV-1. CDV and T. gondii were significantly associated with farming habitats, highlighting the importance of red foxes in pathogen circulation where wildlife, livestock and humans coexist. These findings support the use of red fox as sentinel species for integrated sanitary surveillance and contribute to understanding pathogen transmission risks across human-modified landscapes.Keywords: Canine distemper virus, Canine adenovirus type 1, Toxoplasma gondii, Neospora caninum, PCR, Fox, Interface

Introduction

Over the past century, rapid human population growth has intensified land use and natural resource exploitation, driving large-scale ecosystem transformation worldwide [1]. Nevertheless, adaptable wild carnivores have persisted in these modified landscapes and, in some cases, have even increased in abundance [2]. Therefore, wildlife is increasingly inhabiting fragmented and human-modified environments, resulting in frequent interactions at the wildlife-livestock-human interface [3]. Within the One Health framework, the interface between wildlife, domestic animals, and humans is recognised as a driver of multi-host pathogen circulation, with important implications for animal and public health, as well as biodiversity conservation [4]. Wildlife species inhabiting anthropogenic landscapes can act as reservoirs or sentinels of multi-host pathogens, linking wild and domestic transmission cycles. In this context, monitoring wildlife health provides valuable information for early detection of infectious agents, risk assessment, and disease prevention strategies [5]. Generalist carnivores such as the red fox (Vulpes vulpes) are particularly suitable sentinels due to their ecological plasticity and frequent use of farming and peri-urban habitats. Specifically, the red fox has been proposed as a sentinel species for monitoring various aspects of ecosystem health, including exposure to environmental pollutants, e.g. [6], antimicrobial resistance [7], and pathogen detection, e.g. [8]. This role is supported by its ecological and biological characteristics, which include (i) its ecological plasticity to a wide range of environmental conditions, such as urban, peri-urban, and rural settings [9], where interactions with domestic animals are more likely, (ii) its opportunistic feeding behaviour, characterized by consumption of anthropogenic waste, predation on synanthropic species such as rodents, and scavenging [10], (iii) its high abundance and wide distribution [6], (iv) its importance as a reservoir host of numerous pathogens [8] and (v) its relatively easy accessibility in some countries, such as Spain, where it is legally classified as a game species.

Several pathogens circulating at the wildlife-livestock interface are of relevance due to their broad host range across multiple species. In this study, we focused on four shared pathogens of veterinary and public health relevance, representing different transmission routes and ecological cycles. These included two viruses, canine distemper virus (CDV) and canine adenovirus type 1 (CAdV-1), and two protozoan parasites, Toxoplasma gondii and Neospora caninum, all of which are widely distributed in Europe and have previously been detected in red fox populations [[11], [12], [13]].

CDV is a single-stranded RNA virus of the genus Morbillivirus that infects a wide range of domestic and wild species, particularly carnivores [14]. Domestic dogs are considered the primary source of infection for sympatric wildlife species [15]. Its main implications are related to wildlife conservation, due to its potential impact on susceptible carnivore populations, although its detection in non-human primates has also raised public health concerns [16]. CAdV-1 is a non-enveloped double-stranded DNA virus belonging to the genus Adenovirus [17]. Although CAdV-1 can infect several wild carnivores, its host range appears more restricted than that of CDV [12]. This virus has important implications in wildlife conservation, particularly in threatened species [18]. Toxoplasma gondii and N. caninum are protozoan parasites belonging to the phylum Apicomplexa [19]. Toxoplasmosis is one of the leading causes of abortion in small ruminants [19] and, as a zoonotic disease, represents an important public health concern. Cats and other felids act as definitive hosts, while infestation of intermediate hosts through ingestion of infected meat or carrion is also relevant for wild carnivores [20]. On the other hand, neosporosis is particularly important in bovine production. Domestic dogs and some wild canids act as definitive hosts, and wild carnivores may become infected through scavenging from intermediate hosts [21].

Despite increasing interest in the role of wildlife as pathogen reservoirs, information on the circulation of these pathogens in Spain remains limited, with only a few studies addressing this issue. Understanding their association with human-modified landscapes is therefore important for assessing pathogen dynamics at the wildlife-livestock-human interface and for informing evidence-based management and control strategies. [1]. In this context, this study aimed to (i) assess the prevalence of four shared pathogens (CDV, CAdV-1, T. gondii and N. caninum) in red foxes from northwestern Spain using molecular techniques, considering this species as a sentinel of ecosystem health; and (ii) evaluate the relationship between pathogen positivity and pathogen richness (defined as the number of different pathogens detected in each individual fox), individual traits (sex and age), and habitat type (farming or forest).

Materials and methods

Study area

This study was carried out in three provinces of Galicia, a region located in northwestern Spain covering an area of 29,574 km2 (42°23′60 N, 7°4′W) (Fig. 1), which is characterized by an oceanic/dry summer climate. It is in the Atlantic biogeographical region, with an elevation ranging from the sea level to 2127 m (mean altitude 456 m) and a predominant hilly topography. The annual mean temperature reaches 14 °C and annual mean precipitation ranges from 1000 to 1800 mm. The landscape is dominated by mixed forests, meadows and small human settlements. Forests are primarily composed by different species such as the common oak (Quercus robur), sweet chestnut (Castanea sativa) and eucalyptus (Eucalyptus globulus). Among wild mammals, wolves (Canis lupus), several species of mesocarnivores such as red fox, Eurasian badger (Meles meles), wildcat (Felis silvestris), pine and stone marten (Martes spp.), etc., and ungulates such as roe deer (Capreolus capreolus) and wild boar (Sus scrofa) are also present in the area.

Sampling

Between January and February 2024, a total of 239 red foxes (31 from A Coruña, 145 from Lugo and 63 from Pontevedra) were legally hunted by licensed hunters and subsequently necropsied by our team (Fig. 1). The animals originated from different habitat types, including livestock-agricultural (farming) areas (n = 77), peri-urban areas (n = 2), and forest habitats (n = 135). Habitat information was not available for the remaining 25 animals. During necropsy, sex (103 females, 135 males, and 1 unknown) and age class (67 juveniles, 124 adults, and 48 elderly individuals) were assessed for each individual. Specifically, age was estimated based on dental wear, and individuals were classified into juvenile, adult, and elderly categories according to the degree of tooth attrition [22]. Brain tissue was collected and stored at −20 °C until further processing for the molecular detection of CDV, CAdV-1, Toxoplasma spp. and Neospora spp.

Sample processing and molecular study

Since cerebellum has been established as one of the target regions for pathogen detection within the brain of red foxes [11], this tissue was selected for further molecular analysis. For CDV detection, RNA was extracted using the SpeedTools RNA Virus Extraction Kit (Biotools, Madrid, Spain). For the detection of CAdV-1, T. gondii and N. caninum, DNA was extracted using the SpeedTools Tissue DNA Kit (Biotools, Madrid, Spain). All extractions were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. qPCR protocols for CDV and CAdV-1 and conventional PCR protocols for T. gondii and N. caninum are shown in Table 1 and explained in detail in Supplementary material 1.

Statistical analyses

Before multivariate analysis, collinearity among predictors was explored. For each pathogen (CDV, CAdV-1, T. gondii and N. caninum), we fitted a set of generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) to assess the relationships between sex, age class, sampling habitat type and infection status (as a response; binomial type). In these models, sex, age class and sampling habitat type were included as fixed terms, and the province (three levels) as a random-effect term (see Table 2). The peri-urban category was excluded from the GLMMs because it included only two animals, precluding meaningful comparisons with the other habitat categories. GLMMs were fitted with a binomial error distribution and the logit link function. Additionally, cumulative link mixed models (CLMMs) for ordinal regression with logit function were performed to assess the relationship between pathogen richness at the individual level (0–2, as there were no foxes positive to >2 pathogens) and the aforementioned variables (Table 2).

Model selection was performed using the corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc), and the Akaike weight (wi), which is the probability that a model is the best among those compared (see Supplementary material 1 for detailed model selection process). Models which differed in less than two AICc units (ΔAICc <2) were considered potential best models. Once the best model/s was selected, we checked for normality and the absence of residual patterns in data variation. The models were fitted in the library “lme4” [27] in R software 4.0.2 [28]. Significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

According to our GLMM selection (Supplementary material 1), sampling habitat type was the main predictor of fox positivity for the different pathogens. However, the potential best models may also include age classes, depending on the pathogen considered. In the case of CDV, the probability of testing positive for this pathogen was significantly associated with sampling habitat type (p value ≤0.001), with lower predicted prevalence in forest habitats. The alternative best model for CDV (ΔAICc = 1.48; Supplementary material 1) also included age class as an explanatory variable, although it was not statistically significant (p value = 0.252).

Regarding CAdV-1, the best model included both sampling habitat type and age class, although neither were significantly associated with the risk of testing positive for this pathogen. For T. gondii, the best model included sampling habitat type as the only explanatory variable, which showed a significant effect (p value = 0.018). Positivity risk was higher in farming habitats compared to forest habitats. In the case of N. caninum, the best model included sampling habitat type as the only explanatory variable, although did not show a significant association with risk of testing positive. Only five (2.09%) of the tested foxes showed coinfections. Among them, four were simultaneously positive for CDV and T. gondii, and one was infected by CAdV-1 and T. gondii. The best model for pathogen coinfection included the sampling habitat type as explanatory variable. A marginally significant association was found between farming habitats and coinfection cases (p value = 0.077).

For studying the pathogen richness at the individual level (Fig. 2), the sampling habitat type was the only explanatory variable included in the best model. The habitat showed a significant effect (p value = 0.038), with a higher pathogen richness in farming habitats.

Discussion

This study provides evidence on the circulation of viral and protozoan pathogens in red fox in northwestern Spain, addressing an important knowledge gap on their prevalence in this region. Using molecular methods, we detected a moderate prevalence of CDV, CAdV-1, T. gondii, and N. caninum, and explored their association with different habitats (anthropized versus natural) and host-related factors, supporting the role of the red fox as a relevant sentinel species at the wildlife-livestock-human interface.

The prevalence of CDV (7.53%) was similar to that reported in foxes in northern Portugal (8.1%) and Italy (7.6%) using PCR [[14], [15]], but lower than those found in most European studies (e.g., northern Spain, northern Italy or Germany) using molecular techniques, which ranged from 14.92% to 30.5% [11], [29], [30]. The seroprevalence of CDV observed in foxes and other carnivores in different European countries, such as Portugal, Spain, France, Italy or Germany, was variable, ranging from 3.3% to 72.7% [[15], [31], [32]], suggesting marked differences in exposure levels across regions. Given the well-known epizootic dynamics of CDV in wildlife populations [33], temporal and spatial fluctuations in prevalence, especially in studies based on molecular detection, may be related to the occurrence and intensity of epizootic outbreaks, which reinforces the need for sustained and systematic wildlife surveillance. Regarding habitat influence on CDV risk, the significantly higher prevalence observed in farming habitats compared to forest areas suggests the relevance of anthropogenic environments for CDV transmission. This pattern is consistent with the dog-wildlife transmission hypothesis, whereby domestic and feral dogs act as key reservoirs facilitating spill over to wild carnivores [[15], [34]]. Although fox densities may be higher in rural than in forest habitats [35], other ecological factors (e.g., environmental contamination, prey availability, or habitat-specific exposure risks) may also influence contact rates and pathogen circulation. Previous studies showing infection peaks in domestic dogs preceding outbreaks in wildlife populations, such as lions in Africa, further support this transmission pathway [36]. In this context, anthropized environments are likely characterized by a higher density of domestic and free-ranging dogs, some of which (e.g., herding and hunting dogs) might remain unvaccinated, despite vaccination against CDV is highly recommended in dogs in Spain, but not mandatory [37]. This may increase contact rates and, consequently, the risk of CDV transmission to foxes [15]. Nevertheless, further molecular characterization of qPCR-positive samples, including viral genotyping, would be valuable to elucidate the origin of infection and to assess the potential involvement of the circulating European wildlife strain [14].

The prevalence of CAdV-1 (1.67%) was lower than those reported in Italy (6.25%), Germany (11%), the United Kingdom (UK) (18.8%) and Norway (70%) [[17], [38], [39], [40]] using PCR. However, our result is consistent with previous studies from Italy (ranging from 1.59% to 1.7%) [41], [42]. To the best of our knowledge, no previous studies were available on CAdV-1 prevalence in fox in Spain, although a prevalence of 14.1% was found in wolf (Canis lupus) in nearby areas [43]. Both wolves and foxes seem to act as reservoirs of CAdV-1, as clinical disease is rarely observed in infected individuals [43]. Indeed, the absence of clinical signs, together with prolonged viral excretion in faeces, saliva and urine (potentially lasting up to nine months [12]), suggests that red fox may constitute an important indirect source of infection for other wildlife species. Consequently, it may potentially pose a threat to susceptible and endangered species such as the brown bear (Ursus arctos) [18]. As expected, seroprevalence values reported in different studies were higher than those obtained by PCR, ranging from 3% to 100% [12], as they indicate previous exposure to CAdV rather than active infection. Regarding individual features, in our study, all CAdV-1 positive cases were detected in young animals (n = 4/4). Although the number of positive foxes was limited and no statistically significant differences were found between age classes, this finding may suggest a higher susceptibility to CAdV-1 infection in juvenile foxes, which have also been described in domestic dogs [40]. No significant differences were observed among the different habitats from where animals were sampled, which may be attributable to the low number of positive cases. However, previous studies have reported a higher seroprevalence among wild species inhabiting areas with greater human density [34].

We observed a prevalence of T. gondii (7.11%) generally lower than that reported in studies from different European countries (e.g., Poland, Czech Republic and Belgium) or regions (e.g., western Alps), where prevalences using PCR ranged from 7.4% to 20.21% in red fox [[20], [44], [45], [46]]. It was also lower than the only available prevalence data reported in foxes in Spain (51%) [47]. These differences may be explained by the type of sample analysed, since in our study brain samples were used instead of muscle tissue [48]. The central nervous system is known to have lower detection sensitivity for T. gondii DNA in early stages of the infection, even though brain is a target organ for the pathogen [19]. However, it should be noted that prevalences estimated using molecular techniques are generally much lower than those obtained using serological methods [48]. Nevertheless, the feeding behaviour of red fox makes it an interesting sentinel species for T. gondii circulation, as it can become infected through predation of small vertebrates, scavenging on infected carcasses, or ingestion of sporulated oocysts from the environment [13]. Specifically, rodents, which are an important component of the fox diet, are considered key intermediate hosts that play a relevant role in linking urban and forest ecosystems, thereby contributing to the maintenance and transmission of T. gondii in wildlife populations [49]. A positive association between farming habitats and T. gondii risk was found, which is biologically plausible given that this protozoan is commonly detected in sheep flocks. In these settings, foxes may become infected through scavenging on infected carcasses or placental tissues after abortion [19]. In addition, the frequent presence of domestic and feral cats in farm environments may increase environmental contamination with oocysts, enhancing the exposure of wildlife species [50].

Regarding N. caninum, the prevalence observed in this study (4.6%) was consistent with that reported in foxes from different European countries (e.g., Czech Republic and Belgium) (0%–6.6%) using PCR [[44], [45], [51]], but lower than values previously reported in northeastern Spain (10.7%) [52]. Overall, seroprevalence estimated for this infection in Europe ranges from 0 to 4.4% [21]. Within this context, differences in host ecology suggest that the Iberian wolf may represent a potentially more informative sentinel species for Neospora spp. than the red fox, since wolves are more likely to prey on or scavenge cervids, which are the main wildlife intermediate host [53]. In contrast, foxes are likely to have more limited direct exposure to these hosts due to their smaller body size and more opportunistic scavenging behaviour. Within the domestic cycle, foxes may become infected through scavenging on cattle carcasses or exposure to oocysts shed by dogs [21]. Thus, while red foxes may still reflect Neospora spp. circulation in both domestic and wildlife ecosystems, their epidemiological role as sentinels may be more limited compared to other carnivores with a more direct involvement in the parasite's transmission cycle [21]. Regarding habitat type, although a higher prevalence was observed in forest habitats (6.66%) compared to farming areas (2.59%), the low number of detections limits the ability to draw robust conclusions. Additionally, the absence of significant differences between habitats may be related to the low circulation of Neospora spp. or to the ecological and biological traits of red fox (i.e., wide home ranges, generalist behaviour), which may further homogenise infection risk across habitats. Nevertheless, urban areas seem to play a less relevant role in neosporosis epidemiology [52].

Although no significant differences between habitats were found in terms of pathogen richness or coinfection frequency, a high diversity of pathogens and a greater proportion of coinfections were observed in farming areas compared to forest habitats. Specifically, farming areas accounted for 80% of the coinfections, which may be linked to the higher presence of livestock-associated animals, particularly ruminants, dogs and cats, potentially resulting in higher infection rates.

One limitation of this study is that molecular analyses were performed exclusively on nervous tissue. Although this tissue is appropriate for the detection of neurotropic pathogens, the inclusion of additional tissues (e.g., liver or skeletal muscle) could have increased the sensitivity for detecting pathogens with broader or variable tissue tropism. Therefore, the reported prevalence values may underestimate the true occurrence of some pathogens.

Conclusion

This study provides insight into viral and protozoan pathogens shared between domestic animals and red foxes in northwestern Spain. Moreover, the red fox emerges as a useful sentinel species for the pathogens evaluated, owing to its trophic position and marked adaptability to human-modified environments. The statistically significant detection of CDV and T. gondii in farming habitats highlights the importance of the livestock-wildlife interface, where close contact between domestic animals and red foxes may facilitate pathogen circulation across ecological boundaries, acting as a bridge between anthropogenic and natural systems.
