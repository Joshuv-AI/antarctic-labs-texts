Endothelial Drp1 integrates VEGF-induced redox signaling with glycolysis through cysteine oxidation to drive angiogenesis
Authors: Sheela Nagarkoti, Young-Mee Kim, Archita Das, Dipankar Ash, Eric A. Vitriol, Tracy-Ann Read, Varadarajan Sudhahar, Md. Selim Hossain, Shikha Yadav, Stephanie Kelley Spears, Kelvin N. Orji, Maritza J. Romero, Rudolf Lucas, David Stepp, Eric J. Belin de Chantemele, Ruth B. Caldwell, David JR. Fulton, Tohru Fukai, Masuko Ushio-Fukai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Angiogenesis is essential for development and tissue repair after ischemia. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) act as signaling molecules that promote angiogenesis in endothelial cells (ECs) which mainly rely on aerobic glycolysis for energy production. However, how redox signaling couples to endothelial metabolism remains unclear. Here, we identify endothelial Drp1 as a redox sensor that links VEGF-induced H₂O₂ signaling to metabolic reprogramming and angiogenesis. Loss of Drp1 in ECs suppresses VEGF-driven angiogenic responses. Mechanistically, VEGF rapidly induces NOX4-dependent sulfenylation of Drp1 at Cys644, promoting disulfide bond with the metabolic kinase AMPK and subsequent oxidation of AMPK at Cys299/304 via mitochondrial fission-derived ROS. This pathway enhances endothelial glycolysis and angiogenesis. In vivo, mice with endothelial Drp1 deficiency or CRISPR-engineered redox-dead Drp1 (Cys to Ala) knock-in exhibit impaired retinal angiogenesis and post-ischemic neovascularization. Thus, endothelial Drp1 integrates mitochondrial redox signaling with glycolysis through cysteine oxidation-mediated Drp1-AMPK redox relay, thereby driving reparative neovascularization.

Introduction

Angiogenesis, the process of forming new blood vessels from quiescent endothelial cells (ECs), is essential for development, wound healing, and tissue repair in response to ischemic injury such as peripheral artery disease (PAD)1. Reactive oxygen species (ROS), particularly H2O2 derived from NADPH oxidases (NOXs) and from mitochondria at physiological levels, serve as signaling molecules that enhance vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-induced signaling and angiogenic responses in ECs, as well as in vivo postnatal angiogenesis2–6. However, the specific mechanisms through which diffusible H2O2 signals are transmitted to promote therapeutic angiogenesis remain elusive. ROS primarily function through the oxidation of reactive Cys residues, generating Cysteine sulfenic acid (Cys-OH) (sulfenylation), which facilitate disulfide bond formation with redox-sensitive target proteins to regulate their activity and function7,8. Additionally, ECs predominantly rely on aerobic glycolysis rather than mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation for ATP generation to drive angiogenesis9–12. However, the mechanistic links connecting NOX-mitochondrial ROS (mitoROS)-mediated redox signaling with EC metabolism (glycolysis) in VEGF-induced angiogenesis remain to be defined.

In ECs, mitochondria serve as dynamic biosynthetic and ROS signaling organelles that contribute to EC function and vascular homeostasis13. Mitochondrial dynamics, governed by fusion and fission processes, adaptively maintain mitochondrial quality and function to meet the needs of the local environment13. Mitochondrial fusion involves Mitofusins (Mfn1 and 2) and optic atrophy 1 (OPA1) at the outer and inner membranes, respectively. Conversely, mitochondrial fission is primarily governed by Dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1), a GTPase belonging to the dynamin family. Drp1 is recruited from the cytosol to the mitochondrial outer membrane, where it binds to Drp1 receptors such as Mff, Fis1, and MiD49 and Mid51. This interaction induces mitochondrial constriction and fragmentation in a GTP-dependent manner13–16. Drp1 GTPase activity is regulated through various post-translational modifications (PTMs), such as phosphorylation at Ser616 (which enhances activity) or Ser637 (which inhibits activity), and S-nitrosylation at Cys64414–16. When ECs are exposed to stressors like inflammation and diabetes, Drp1 activation leads to pathological mitochondrial fragmentation (hyper fission), resulting in excessive production of mitoROS and endothelial dysfunction, including cellular senescence, and impaired angiogenesis13,17–23. However, the role of endothelial Drp1 under conditions of physiological ROS production, such as in response to VEGF signaling, and its effects on angiogenesis in ECs, as well as postnatal neovascularization in vivo remain unexplored.

The intricate interplay among redox signaling, mitochondria, and glycolysis during angiogenesis remains largely unexplored. In this study, we show that endothelial Drp1 acts as a redox sensor that couples H₂O₂ signaling to angiogenic metabolism. In cultured ECs, VEGF rapidly induces CysOH formation of Drp1 at Cys644 via NOX4-derived H₂O₂, which promotes disulfide bond formation with the metabolic kinase AMPK and its oxidation at Cys299/304 through a mitochondrial fission-mitoROS axis. This Drp1-AMPK redox relay enhances glycolysis and angiogenic responses. Using endothelial-specific Drp1 knockout (KO) mice and CRISPR/Cas9-engineered redox-dead Cys-to-Ala Drp1 knock-in mice, we also demonstrate that endothelial Drp1 sulfenylation at Cys644 is required for both developmental retinal angiogenesis and reparative angiogenesis in response to tissue ischemia. Thus, our findings establish Drp1 as a central redox-metabolic integrator in vascular growth.

Results

Endothelial Drp1 is pivotal for both developmental and reparative angiogenesis in vivo

To elucidate the role of endothelial Drp1 in postnatal developmental angiogenesis, we generated conditional EC-specific Drp1 KO (Drp1ECKO) mice by crossing Drp1fl/fl mice with mice expressing Cre recombinase under control of the VE-cadherin promoter (Cdh5-Cre) (Supplementary Fig. 1a). We confirmed specific deletion of Drp1 protein or mRNA in ECs from aortas, retina, or skeletal muscles isolated from the Drp1ECKO mice compared to control wild-type (WT) mice (Drp1fl/fl or Cdh5-Cre+) (Supplementary Fig. 1b–d). Postnatal retinal angiogenesis was assessed in P5 pups using isolectin B4 (IB4) immunofluorescence (IF) staining of flat mount retina. Co-immunostaining of Drp1 with IB4 or tip cell marker (ESM1) in P5 pup retina reveals that Drp1 is broadly expressed in retinal ECs, including but not limited to tip cells (Fig. 1a). In Drp1ECKO mice, radial expansion of the vascular plexus toward the retinal periphery was markedly delayed, with a significant reduction in vascular length, number of branch points, tip cells, and filopodia in the sprouting front compared to WT controls (Fig. 1b). The specificity of Drp1 staining in ECs of P5 retina was validated by abolishing staining in ECs from Drp1ECKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 2a). To assess EC proliferation, we performed co-immunostaining of BrdU or ERG (a marker for endothelial nuclei; green) with IB4 (red) in P5 retinal flat mounts from WT and Drp1ECKO mice. The numbers of BrdU⁺ and ERG⁺ nuclei per IB4⁺ endothelial field were significantly reduced in the retinas of Drp1ECKO mice compared to WT controls (Supplementary Fig. 2b, c). These findings indicate that endothelial Drp1 governs vessel sprouting by facilitating tip cell filopodial extension during developmental angiogenesis. To explore the involvement of endothelial Drp1 in reparative angiogenesis in vivo, we employed a murine hindlimb ischemia (HLI) model, recognized as an animal model of PAD, which induces ischemia by femoral artery ligation and excision4,6. IF analysis revealed a marked increase in Drp1 protein expression that co-localized with CD31+ ECs in ischemic skeletal muscle compared with non-ischemic muscle at days 3, 7, and 14 following HLI (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). Consistently, Western blot analysis demonstrated a significant upregulation of Drp1 protein expression in ischemic muscle after HLI (Fig. 1d). This suggests an upregulation of Drp1 in ECs during reparative angiogenesis. Furthermore, Drp1ECKO mice exhibited significant reduction in limb perfusion recovery (Fig. 1e) and angiogenesis (CD31+ capillary density) (Fig. 1f) in ischemic muscles following HLI. The specificity of Drp1 staining in ECs of ischemic muscle was validated by abolishing its staining in ECs from Drp1ECKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 5a). To further evaluate the role of endothelial Drp1 in physiological angiogenesis, we utilized a skin wound healing model and found that Drp1ECKO mice exhibited delayed wound closure compared to WT controls (Supplementary Fig. 5b).

To confirm further the role of endothelial Drp1, we generated Tamoxifen-inducible EC-specific Drp1 KO (Drp1iECKO) mice by crossing Drp1fl/fl mice with Cdh5-CreERT2 mice24 (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Notably, ischemia-induced blood flow recovery and capillary density were significantly impaired in Drp1iECKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 6b, c). Thus, these findings suggest the crucial role of endothelial Drp1 in both developmental angiogenesis and post-ischemic reparative neovascularization in vivo.

We then examined underlying mechanisms using primary cultured ECs. VEGF stimulation predominantly induced mitochondrial fission within 30 min, which continued for at least 24 h. Drp1 is involved in VEGF-induced angiogenic responses in a GTPase activity-dependent manner in ECs

We next investigated the involvement of Drp1 in VEGF-induced angiogenic responses in cultured ECs. Knockdown of Drp1 using specific siRNA in HUVEC significantly inhibited VEGF-induced mitochondrial fission (Fig. 2c), EC migration (modified Boyden chamber assay) (Fig. 2d), EC proliferation (BrdU incorporation) (Fig. 2e), and capillary tube formation (fibrin beads assay) (Fig. 2f). We confirmed that there were no significant changes in cell viability in Drp1 siRNA-treated ECs (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Furthermore, VEGF-induced migration (wound scratch assay) was also markedly reduced in ECs isolated from the aorta, brain, heart, and lung of EC-Drp1ECKO mice (Supplementary Fig. 7b, c), suggesting that Drp1-dependent angiogenic effects in ECs are conserved among various vascular beds. Additionally, VEGF stimulation increased Drp1 GTPase activity in HUVECs (Supplementary Fig. 8a). Inhibition of Drp1 activity by overexpression of a dominant-negative mutant, Drp1-K38A (Drp1-DN), significantly suppressed VEGF-induced EC migration and proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 8b, c). To address the underlying mechanism, we examined the role of Drp1 in VEGF-stimulated key angiogenic signaling in ECs. Intriguingly, Drp1 depletion with siRNA did not exert significant effects on VEGF-induced phosphorylation of VEGFR2 and its downstream signaling, p-ERK, p-p38MAPK, or p-Akt (Fig. 2g). These findings suggest that Drp1 participates in VEGF-induced angiogenesis in a GTPase activity-dependent manner without affecting major angiogenic VEGFR2 signaling pathways.

VEGF induces Drp1 sulfenylation at Cys644 through NOX4, rather than phosphorylation, which promotes Drp1 activity and angiogenesis in ECs

We next examined whether VEGF activates or upregulates Drp1 to promote mitochondrial fission and angiogenic responses in ECs. VEGF stimulation (20 ng/ml) did not significantly affect the phosphorylation levels of Drp1 at Ser616 or Ser637, sites known to positively and negatively regulate Drp1 activity, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 8d). Additionally, VEGF had no notable impact on the expression of Drp1 protein or other mitochondrial dynamics-related proteins, including Mfn1 and OPA1, in ECs (Supplementary Fig. 8d). Since ROS, especially H2O2, derived from NOX including NOX4, at physiological levels play a critical role in VEGF signaling and angiogenesis in ECs28, we next examined the role of Drp1 in VEGF-induced ROS production in ECs. We next investigated whether Drp1 is a downstream target of VEGF-induced H2O2 to promote EC angiogenesis. Since ROS induce the oxidation of Cys residues in target proteins to form CysOH (sulfenylation), and Drp1 has a crucial redox-sensitive Cys644 residue29, we examined if VEGF induces CysOH formation of Drp1 in ECs. Using a biotin-conjugated CysOH trapping probe, DCP-Bio1, we observed that VEGF stimulation rapidly increased Drp1-CysOH formation within 5 min, peaking at 15 min, and gradually returning to basal levels within 60 min (Fig. 3b). Notably, VEGF-induced Drp1 sulfenylation was abolished by Nox4 shRNA (Fig. 3c), which efficiently reduced endogenous Nox4 expression (Fig. 3d) and inhibited VEGF-induced H2O2 production and angiogenic responses in ECs, as we previously reported28. To assess the role of Cys644 in VEGF-induced Drp1-CysOH formation, we engineered adenovirus expressing Drp1-C644A, where Cys644 is substituted with Ala644. VEGF-induced both Drp1-CysOH formation and Drp1 GTPase activity were significantly reduced in cells expressing Drp1-C644A compared to Drp1-WT (Fig. 3e, f). These results suggest that VEGF promotes sulfenylation of Drp1 at Cys644 through NOX4-derived H₂O₂, leading to enhanced GTPase activity in ECs.

We next investigated the functional role of Drp1 sulfenylation or Drp1 activation in VEGF-induced angiogenesis in ECs. Overexpression of Drp1-C644A significantly inhibited VEGF-induced EC migration (modified Boyden chamber or wound scratch assays) (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Fig. 10b) and EC proliferation (Fig. 3h) compared to control or Drp1-WT, which had no effects on apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. 10a). Overexpression of Drp1-WT enhanced EC proliferation and EC migration (wound scratch assay) (Fig. 3h and Supplementary Fig. 10b) compared to control. We also confirmed the comparable expression levels of Drp1-WT and Drp1-C644A in HUVECs transduced with the respective adenoviruses (Supplementary Fig. 10c). To further underscore the functional significance of endogenous Cys644 oxidation of Drp1, we generated “redox-dead” hCys644 (mouse mCys631) to Ala Drp1 (mDrp1C631A) knock-in (KI) mutant (mDrp1-KI) mice using CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Fig. 11a). Homozygous mDrp1KI/KI mice, confirmed by genotyping (Supplementary Fig. 11b), displayed no overt developmental abnormalities, remaining viable and exhibiting normal body weight compared to WT littermate. ECs isolated from mDrp1KI/KI mice exhibited marked inhibition of VEGF-induced EC migration (Fig. 3j) and EC proliferation (Fig. 3k) compared to WT-ECs. Taken together, these findings suggest that VEGF-induced sulfenylation of Drp1 at Cys644 is required for VEGF-induced angiogenic responses.

VEGF-induced sulfenylation of Drp1 at Cys644 facilitates mitochondrial fission and mitoROS production, which promotes glycolysis

It is reported that Drp1 promotes mitochondrial fission and mitochondrial ROS (mitoROS) production through its GTPase activity by translocating from the cytosol to mitochondria17, and that mitoROS is essential for VEGF-induced angiogenesis28. Thus, we investigated whether oxidation of Drp1 at Cys644 is required for VEGF-induced mitochondrial fission and subsequent mitoROS generation. Overexpression of the oxidation-resistant mutant Drp1-C644A significantly inhibited VEGF-induced mitochondrial fission, as shown by Mito-DsRed staining (Fig. 4a), and markedly reduced mitoROS levels, as assessed by Mito-SOX fluorescence (Fig. 4b), whereas Drp1-WT overexpression enhanced both responses (Fig. 4b). Consistent with this, Drp1 knockdown with siRNA significantly attenuated VEGF-stimulated mitochondrial ROS (mitoROS) generation at 15 min (Supplementary Fig. 9b) but had no effect on the early VEGF-induced cytosolic ROS production (Fig. 3a). In line with our previous findings that NOX4-derived cytosolic H₂O₂ acts upstream to trigger mitoROS production via a ROS-induced ROS release mechanism28, NOX4 overexpression-induced mitoROS was also suppressed by Drp1 siRNA (Supplementary Fig. 9c). Given that glycolysis serves as the primary source of ATP driving angiogenesis in ECs, we examined whether Drp1 Cys oxidation is involved in VEGF-induced glycolysis in ECs by analyzing the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR), a measure of glycolytic flux, using the XF24 extracellular flux analyzer. VEGF-induced sulfenylation of Drp1 at Cys644 is required for disulfide bond formation with AMPK and activation of AMPK in ECs

We next investigated the role of AMPK sulfenylation in VEGF-induced glycolysis and angiogenic responses. Overexpression of the oxidation-defective AMPK-C299/304A mutant significantly inhibited glycolysis stimulated by VEGF for 60 min, measured by Seahorse assay (Fig. 6e), and suppressed VEGF-stimulated EC proliferation (Fig. 6f) and EC migration (Fig. 6g). To assess the functional significance of Drp1 and AMPK sulfenylation, we overexpressed mutants Drp1-C644A and AMPK-C299/304A, individually and together in ECs. Both mutants similarly suppressed VEGF-induced glycolysis (Supplementary Fig. 14b) and EC migration (Supplementary Fig. 14c), with no additional inhibition observed upon co-expression. These findings indicate that VEGF-induced sulfenylation of Drp1 and AMPK occurs in a coordinated, rather than independent manner. Furthermore, treatment with the AMPK activator AICAR rescued the impaired migration of Drp1ECKO ECs (Fig. 6h), suggesting that Drp1 acts upstream of AMPK in promoting angiogenesis. Mechanistically, VEGF-induced sulfenylation of Drp1 at Cys644 facilitates its disulfide bond formation with AMPK, leading to subsequent sulfenylation of AMPK at Cys299/304 via the mitochondrial fission-mitoROS axis, thereby enhancing glycolysis and angiogenesis (Fig. 6i). Since Drp1- and AMPK-dependent glycolytic activation occurs rapidly (within 1 h), and PFKFB3 glycolytic enzyme is known to promote angiogenesis, we examined whether Drp1 regulates the expression of glycolytic genes. VEGF stimulation for 12 h significantly increased PFKFB3 expression in WT ECs, without altering other glycolytic genes. This VEGF-induced upregulation of PFKFB3 was markedly reduced in Drp1-KO ECs (Supplementary Fig. 15). These results suggest that Drp1 is essential not only for the early sulfenylation of AMPK but also for the later induction of PFKFB3 expression, both of which are critical for VEGF-driven glycolysis and angiogenic activity in ECs.

Discussion

The interplay among redox signaling, mitochondria, and glycolysis during angiogenesis remains poorly understood. Our study reveals a previously unrecognized role of endothelial Drp1 as a redox sensor that transmits VEGF-induced H2O2 signal to enhance aerobic glycolysis through oxidation at Cys644, thereby promoting developmental and reparative angiogenesis. Mechanistically, VEGF stimulation triggers mitochondrial fission by increasing NOX4-dependent sulfenylation of Drp1 at Cys644. This process facilitates the formation of disulfide bonds between Drp1 and AMPK, promoting translocation to mitochondria, and the increased proximity to mitoROS activates AMPK via Cys oxidation. Thus, this unexpected redox signaling cascade links Drp1 with AMPK (redox relay) to orchestrate aerobic glycolysis and angiogenic responses in ECs, contributing to postnatal developmental and reparative angiogenesis in vivo (Fig. 8).Fig. 8Schematic representation of the proposed model.Mitochondrial fission protein Drp1 functions as a redox sensor that transmits NOX4-derived H2O2 signals via sulfenylation of Cys644. This in turn drives glycolysis required for postnatal developmental and reparative angiogenesis. (Created by BioRender. Ushio-Fukai, M. (2026) https://BioRender.com/xeofyc6).

ECs predominantly rely on glycolysis, rather than mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation for ATP production to drive angiogenesis9,31,32. Despite this, mitochondrial dynamics, specifically the balance between fission and fusion, are crucial for maintaining mitochondrial function13. While global deletion of the mitochondrial fission protein Drp1 leads to early embryonic lethality33, the specific role of endothelial Drp1 in postnatal developmental and reparative angiogenesis in vivo has not been clearly defined. In the present study, we demonstrate that Drp1 is expressed across retinal ECs during developmental angiogenesis, extending beyond ESM1-positive tip cells34. We acknowledge that DLL4 is also a well-established and functionally critical regulator of tip cell identity and angiogenic patterning in the developing retina35,36. However, under our experimental conditions, commercially available DLL4 antibodies did not produce specific or reliable immunostaining. We also found that Drp1ECKO mice do not exhibit overt developmental abnormalities but display marked impairments in retinal vessel sprouting and ischemia-induced neovascularization. These findings align with previous reports demonstrating that pharmacological Drp1 inhibition using Mdivi-1 reduces pathological angiogenesis in the oxygen-induced retinopathy model37. However, Mdivi-1 has known off-target effects, including mitochondrial complex I inhibition38, which may confound its interpretation. Therefore, our study provides evidence that endothelial Drp1 plays a critical role in developmental and reparative angiogenesis in vivo. Interestingly, while loss of endothelial Drp1 impairs physiological angiogenesis, it is reported that EC-specific deletion of the inner mitochondrial fusion protein OPA1 in mice exhibit reduced pathological tumor angiogenesis via enhanced NFkB activation, independent of its role in mitochondrial fusion39. In addition, knockdown of another outer mitochondrial fusion protein MFNs is shown to block VEGF-induced angiogenesis by reducing expression of respiratory chain components40. Although the role of endothelial Drp1 in pathological angiogenesis needs to be explored, these findings support the notion that imbalance between mitochondrial fission and fusion impairs VEGF-induced angiogenesis and that both processes are essential for postnatal angiogenesis, albeit through distinct mechanisms.

In the present study using high-resolution confocal imaging27, we found that serum-starved HUVEC exhibit elongated mitochondrial networks under basal conditions. VEGF stimulation triggered rapid and sustained mitochondrial fission within 30 min. Drp1 depletion via siRNA, dominant negative (DN)-Drp1 (which inhibits Drp1GTPase activity), or ECs isolated from various organs such as aorta, lung, heart and brain of Drp1ECKO mice inhibited VEGF-induced EC migration, proliferation, and capillary tube formation, underscoring a critical role of Drp1 in VEGF-driven angiogenesis. Conversely, under pathological conditions such as diabetes or inflammation, where the balance between fusion and fission is disrupted, excessive Drp1 activation leads to mitochondrial fragmentation, leading to oxidative stress, EC dysfunction, including senescence18,40 and vascular pathologies41. These observations support a dual role for Drp1, wherein physiological activation supports angiogenesis, while hyperactivation under stress contributes to vascular damage.

Drp1 GTPase activity is modulated by various PTMs, including phosphorylation and redox-based modifications such as S-nitrosylation at Cys64414–16. In this, we did not observe a significant increase in Drp1 phosphorylation at either Ser616 or Ser637, nor in total Drp1 protein levels, following VEGF stimulation. This contrasts with findings by Huang et al.37, who reported a modest ( < twofold) increase in Ser616 phosphorylation in HUVECs upon VEGF treatment. Such discrepancies may be attributed to variations in experimental conditions, including cell confluency, passage number, and serum concentration. Instead, our data using the Drp1-C644A mutant demonstrate that VEGF rapidly induces sulfenylation of Drp1 at Cys644 via NOX4-derived H2O2, which enhances Drp1 GTPase activity, its mitochondrial translocation, mitochondrial fission and mitoROS production, ultimately promoting angiogenic responses. These findings are consistent with prior studies demonstrating that Drp1-dependent mitoROS production requires its GTPase activity17. Furthermore, it should be noted that Drp1-WT overexpression significantly enhanced EC proliferation and migration in the wound scratch assay compared to the control. However, this effect was not observed in the modified Boyden chamber assay. One possible explanation is that VEGF stimulation in the Boyden chamber assay may already drive EC migration to a maximal level, making it difficult to detect any additional enhancement by Drp1-WT. Alternatively, Drp1-WT overexpression may affect cell adhesion during the initial seeding on the insert membrane, which could influence the Boyden assay results.

The present findings underscore the temporal and compartment-specific organization of VEGF-induced redox signaling via Drp1 sulfenylation in ECs. VEGF rapidly induces NOX4-dependent cytosolic H₂O₂ within 5–10 min, which subsequently triggers mitoROS production at later time points through a ROS-induced ROS release mechanism involving coordinated crosstalk between NOX4 and mitochondria28,42. Consistent with this model, Drp1 depletion does not alter early cytosolic ROS, indicating that Drp1 does not regulate initial NOX4-derived ROS production. Instead, our data demonstrate that Drp1 acts as a redox sensor that transduces VEGF-induced, NOX4-derived cytosolic H₂O₂ signals via sulfenylation at Cys644, thereby promoting Drp1 mitochondrial translocation, mitochondrial fission, and subsequent mitoROS generation. Thus, sulfenylation-dependent activation of Drp1 functions as a molecular switch linking cytosolic ROS signaling to mitochondrial remodeling and redox amplification during angiogenic activation.

Notably, in resting ECs, Drp1 remains in a reduced and inactive state (Drp1-SH) by binding to the thiol reductase protein disulfide isomerase A1 (PDIA1)43. Thus, the basal reduced state of Drp1 is essential for Drp1-CysOH formation by Nox4-derived H₂O₂, initiating a fission-mitoROS signaling cascade that supports angiogenesis. Importantly, this physiological Drp1 sulfenylation contrasts with the excessive sulfenylation/activation of Drp1 observed in PDIA1-deficient ECs or under diabetic conditions, which leads to aberrant fission and endothelial senescence43. Further investigation is warranted to identify additional physiological or pathological conditions such as hypoxia or ischemia that may trigger Drp1 sulfenylation or other PTMs, such as SUMOylation or palmitoylation, leading to angiogenesis. It also remains to be determined whether other cysteine residues on Drp1 serve as redox-sensitive sites for VEGF-induced modification, and whether alternative modifications, including S-glutathionylation, S-nitrosylation, or S-sulhydration, play a role in VEGF-driven angiogenesis. The in vivo significance of Drp1-Cys644 oxidation is underscored by findings in redox-dead mDrp1-C631AKI/KI mice, which exhibit reduced developmental retinal angiogenesis and reparative neovascularization following ischemia. BMT experiments, in which irradiated mDrp1KI/KI mice were reconstituted with WT Drp1 BM, further support the critical role of Drp1-Cys644 oxidation in tissue-resident cells, including ECs, in regulating postnatal reparative angiogenesis. Although mDrp1KI/KI or Drp1ECKO mice do not exhibit embryonic lethality, both models display impaired developmental retinal angiogenesis in P5 pups, a phenotype comparable to that reported in Nox4ECKO mice44 and PDIA1ECKO mice45. Notably, we further confirmed impaired retinal vessel formation within the intermediate retinal layer at later developmental stages (P15) in both mDrp1KI/KI or Drp1ECKO mice. Collectively, these findings support a critical role for endothelial Drp1 and Drp1 cysteine oxidation in postnatal retinal angiogenesis. However, it will be important in future studies to determine whether this redox-dependent mechanism similarly governs physiological angiogenesis in other developing vascular beds, including the brain, heart, and kidney. In addition, defining the specific contribution of endothelial Drp1-Cys644 will require the generation and analysis of EC-specific mDrp1-C631A knock-in mice.

Although angiogenesis is an energy-intensive process in ECs that depends largely on glycolysis rather than mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation9, the role of mitochondrial dynamics in glycolytic regulation remains unclear. Our findings reveal that VEGF-induced Drp1 sulfenylation at Cys644, driven by NOX4-derived H₂O₂, promotes glycolysis via the mitochondrial fission–mitoROS axis. Notably, expression of the Drp1-C644A mutant or Mito-catalase significantly impaired VEGF-induced glycolysis, highlighting the importance of redox-modified Drp1 in this process. Mechanistically, we identified AMPK, a key regulator of metabolism and angiogenesis, as a redox-sensitive Drp1 binding partner. Using Co-IP, BiFC, mitochondrial fractionation and IF assays, our data suggest that VEGF-induced H2O2 rapidly induces Drp1-Cys644 OH, which facilitates its translocation to mitochondria and disulfide bond formation with AMPK-SH. This interaction promotes sulfenylation of AMPK at Cys299/304 and activation via the mitochondrial fission-mitoH₂O₂ axis. Our findings are in line with previous report showing that H₂O₂ (250 μM) stimulation oxidizes AMPK at Cys299/304, resulting in its activation in HEK293 cells30. That study proposed that the AMPKαβγ complex exists in an inactive, open conformation and that H₂O₂-mediated cysteine oxidation triggers an allosteric transition to an active, closed state. Thus, we propose that the redox-dependent association of Drp1 with AMPK at mitochondria facilitates AMPK cysteine oxidation at Cys209/304 and subsequent allosteric rearrangement to the active conformation.

Notably, Drp1-CysOH levels increase following VEGF stimulation, peaking at 15 min and gradually declining to baseline by 60 min. In contrast, VEGF-induced Drp1-AMPK binding begins at 15 min and persists for at least 60 min. This temporal pattern supports a model in which sulfenylated Drp1 forms a disulfide bond with AMPK-SH, leading to a decline in unbound Drp1-CysOH levels while promoting stable Drp1-s-s-AMPK complex. Notably, in Drp1ECKO ECs or mDrp1-C644AKI/KI ECs, VEGF stimulation induces AMPK phosphorylation while AMPK substrate phosphorylation is decreased. This decrease is accompanied by reduced AMPK sulfenylation at Cys299/304, suggesting that AMPK activation is not solely governed by phosphorylation but also requires redox modification at these cysteine residues.

We found that H₂O₂ stimulation significantly increased AMPK sulfenylation in Drp1-WT ECs, but not in Drp1-C644A ECs, suggesting that Drp1 sulfenylation mediates H2O2-induced AMPK redox activation. Overexpression of the AMPK-C299/304A or Drp1-C644A mutant suppressed VEGF-induced phosphorylation of ACC and AMPK substrates, glycolysis, and angiogenesis. Co-expression of both mutants did not produce additive effects, implying Drp1 and AMPK function in the same redox pathway. These findings support a model in which VEGF-induced H₂O₂ promotes glycolysis and angiogenesis via sulfenylation-dependent Drp1-AMPK disulfide bond formation. The idea that Drp1 acts upstream of AMPK is further supported by the ability of the AMPK activator AICAR to rescue EC migration in Drp1-deficient cells. Future work will identify the specific AMPK cysteine(s) that interact with Drp1-Cys644OH and explore additional downstream effectors of sulfenylated Drp1. Since VEGF-induced expression of glycolytic genes also plays a role in the chronic phase of angiogenesis9, we examined the involvement of Drp1 in this process. Our results showed that VEGF stimulation for 12 h significantly increased the expression of the key glycolytic enzyme PFKFB3 in WT ECs, which was attenuated in Drp1-KO ECs. Taken together, these findings suggest that VEGF promotes endothelial glycolysis through a biphasic mechanism involving early-phase Drp1 sulfenylation and subsequent oxidative activation of the metabolic kinase AMPK, followed by late-phase induction of PFKFB3. This coordinated metabolic and transcriptional reprogramming enhances angiogenesis necessary for reparative neovascularization.

Notably, AMPK is classically known to inhibit anabolic processes via mTOR suppression during energy stress. However, emerging evidence suggests that AMPK can also support angiogenesis and EC proliferation under growth factor stimulation. Our findings suggest that VEGF-induced H2O2 activates AMPK via Drp1 sulfenylation, independent of AMP/ATP levels. This redox-driven AMPK activation promotes glycolysis to support EC migration and proliferation, rather than triggering a broad energy-stress response. In Drp1-KO or Drp1-C644A ECs, reduced AMPK-mediated ACC phosphorylation may permit lipid synthesis, but impaired mitochondrial dynamics and glycolytic energy production via disruption of the Drp1-AMPK redox axis may more significantly limit EC proliferation.

Collectively, our findings reveal a previously unrecognized mechanism by which VEGF-induced redox signals are sensed and transduced into metabolic and angiogenic responses. Specifically, we identify the mitochondrial fission protein Drp1 as a redox sensor that responds to NOX4-derived H₂O₂ through sulfenylation at Cys644. This oxidation event activates a mitochondrial fission–mitoROS signaling cascade that triggers oxidative activation of AMPK via a disulfide bond between Drp1 and AMPK in ECs. This redox relay is essential for VEGF-driven glycolysis and angiogenesis. Using both endothelial-specific Drp1 KO mice and CRISPR-Cas9-generated redox-dead mDrp1-C631A KI mice, we demonstrate the physiological relevance of Drp1 oxidation in regulating both developmental and reparative angiogenesis in vivo. These findings define a Drp1-AMPK redox relay linking VEGF-induced mitochondrial redox signaling with metabolic reprogramming and angiogenesis, identifying a redox-sensitive target for therapeutic angiogenesis in ischemic cardiovascular diseases.

Methods

Animal study

The use of mice was in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and relevant ethical regulations. The animal protocol used in this study was approved by the institutional Animal Care committee and institutional Biosafety Committee of Augusta University. All mice were housed in the Augusta University animal facility under controlled environmental conditions, with temperature maintained at 22.5 °C and relative humidity between 50 and 60%. Animals were kept on a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle and housed in individually ventilated cages containing 2–5 mice per cage. Male and female mice aged 8–12 weeks were used for all experiments. Mice were fed a standard chow diet (irradiated laboratory rodent diet; TEKLAD Global 18% protein diet, #2918), containing a minimum of 18% crude protein, 5% crude fat, and a maximum of 5% crude fiber.

Generation of conditional EC-specific Drp1 KO (Dp1ECKO) mice and inducible EC-specific Drp1 KO (Drp1iECKO) mice

Dp1ECKO mice were generated by crossing Drp1fl/fl mice (from Dr. Hiromi Sesaki)33 with VE-Cadherin (Cdh5)-Cre mice (JAX:017968) which have been backcrossed with C57BL/6J mice and used at 8–12 weeks. Tamoxifen-inducible Drp1iECKO mice were generated by crossing Drp1fl/fl mice with Cdh5-CreERT2 mice (from Dr. Ralf Adams)24,46 on a C57BL/6J background. To induce postnatal deletion of endothelial Drp1, we administered tamoxifen (50 mg kg−1 of body weight) by intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection into control, wild type (WT)(Drp1fl/fl or Cdh5-Cre negative) mice, or Drp1fl/fl/Cdh5-CreERT2 positive mice. Injections were performed once per day for 10 days with 2 days break, followed by a washing period for 2 weeks to obtain Drp1iECKO mice.

Generation of CRISPR/Cas9-engineered mDrp1(C631A) knock-in (KI) mutant (mDrp1-KI) mice

The mDrp1(C631A) mouse line was generated using Cas9 mRNA (TriLink Bio, L6125100), sgRNA and single-strand DNA homology-directed repair (HDR) method. The sgRNA sequence (5′- CCGAGAACAGCGAGATTGTG AGG −3′) was designed using the MIT CRISPR design tool. The single-stranded oligonucleotide, 5′- TAGCCAGTTCCAGTTGCAAGAAAACTGT CTGCCCGAGAACAGCGAGATgcTGAaGTTATTGAAAGACTTATCAAATCATATTTTCTAATTGTCAGAAAGAATATTC −3′, incorporating the mDrp1(C631A) mutation and a silent mutation in exon 17 was used as a template for CRISPR-Cas9 HDR-mediated gene editing in WT mice (C57BL/6). The sgRNA, Cas9 mRNA and single-stranded oligonucleotide encoding mDrp11 C631A were then injected into the cytoplasm of mouse zygotes and viable two-cell stage embryos were transferred to pseudopregnant female mice by the transgenic core facility at Augusta University. We analysed the founders using a combination of PCR and next-generation amplicon sequencing methods to detect the precise mutations and confirm successful generation of mDrp1(C631A)-KI mice. For genotyping offspring, we designed a PCR strategy that was sensitive enough to differentiate between WT and mDrp1(C631A) alleles that differ by 3 nucleotides. There is common reverse primer Dnm1l wtR1: 5′ CCATGGAACCACACTGCTACC −3′ and Dnm1L wt F2 (primer name) 5′- GCCCGAGAACAGCGAGATTGT-3′ and Dnm1L mutant F1 is 5′ GCCCGAGAACAGCGAGATgcT −3′. The PCR products were 404 bp for WT and mDrp1(C631A). For sequencing confirmation of mDrp1C631A mutation, a 625 bp region flanking the C631A mutation was PCR-amplified with forward primer 5′- CCACAGAAAGGCCATGCTGTC −3′ and reverse primer 5′- CCATGGAACCACACTGCTACC −3′ for both WT and mutant alleles and the 625 bp amplicons were subjected to Sanger sequencing.

In vivo angiogenesis models

All experiments were conducted at 8–12 weeks old male or female littermates of Drp1fl/fl or Cdh5-Cre+ (control, wild type (WT)) and Drp1ECKO or Drp1iECKO mice; or C57Bl6 (WT) and mDrp1-KI mice.

Mouse retinal angiogenesis model

Eyes from postnatal day 5 (P5) mice were enucleated and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. Retinas were dissected and permeabilized overnight in PBS containing 1% BSA and 0.5% Triton ×-100. The permeabilized retinas were incubated with biotin-conjugated isolectinB4 (IB4) (20 μg/ml, Sigma-Aldrich), followed by Alexa Fluor 546-conjugated streptavidin (Invitrogen). After washes, samples were flat-mounted using Vectashield (Vector Labs) mounting medium and imaged under Keyence fluorescence microscope. The total length, number of branch points and tip cells of IB4-positive vessels in the retina were quantified on composite high-magnification images using Image J software, as we reported26,45. In vivo proliferation of ECs in the retina was measured using the BrdU assay after i.p. injection of 300 μg of BrdU (Sigma-Aldrich, B5002) per P5 pup weighing about 3 g and then co-stained with anti-BrdU and anti-IB4 antibodies.

Hindlimb ischemia model

Mice were subjected to unilateral hindlimb surgery under anesthesia with intraperitoneal administration of ketamine (87 mg/kg) and xylazine (13 mg/kg) as we reported4,6,47. Briefly, the left femoral artery was exposed, ligated both proximally and distally using 6–0 silk sutures and the vessels between the ligatures were excised. We measured ischemic (left)/nonischemic (right) limb blood flow ratio using a laser Doppler blood flow analyzer (PeriScan PIM 3 System; Perimed).

Mouse skin wound healing model

Briefly, the dorsal surface of each mouse was clipped, and a depilatory cream (Nair body cream) was applied to remove hair. The skin was allowed to dry, and Betadine was applied for antisepsis. A full-thickness 3-mm skin punch biopsy (Euro Med, Disposa-Derm; Cooper Surgical, USA) was then created on the dorsal skin of each mouse. Wounds were dressed with a Bioclusive transparent, oxygen-permeable wound dressing (Johnson & Johnson). For kinetic analysis, digital images of the wounds were captured on days 0, 3, 5, and 7, and the wound diameters were measured using Image J (v.1.52) to determine the wound closure rate.

Bone marrow transplantation (BMT)

BMT was performed as we previously reported43,47. BM cells were isolated by density gradient separation. Recipient mice were lethally irradiated with 9.5 Gy and received an intravenous injection of 3 million donor BM cells 24 h after irradiation. As reported before transplantation efficiency has been validated47. Hindlimb ischemia was induced at 6–8 weeks after BMT.

Isolation of primary ECs from mouse aorta

To isolate ECs from mice, the thoracic aorta was cut into 1 mm rings. Each aortic ring was opened and seeded onto a growth factor-reduced matrix with the endothelium facing down. The segments were cultured in EC growth medium for about 4 days. The endothelial sprouting starts as early as day 2. The segments were then removed, and the cells were cultured continually until they reached confluence. The ECs were harvested using neutral proteinase and cultured in EC growth medium for another two passages before experiments, as we reported25,26.

Isolation of primary mouse ECs from mouse lung, heart and brain

Briefly, mice (4–5 weeks old) were euthanized and the heart, lungs, and brain were excised. Each organ was finely minced into small pieces and digested in type II collagenase solution (17101015, Gibco, USA) in serum-free M199 medium. After digestion for >45 min, the tissue suspension was washed, passed through a 20 G needle, and filtered through a 70 µm strainer. The filtrate was resuspended in M199 medium with 10% FBS and centrifuged at 400 × g for 10 min. Cells were washed three times, the cell pellet was resuspended with anti-CD31 magnetic beads and incubated on a rocker for 30 min. CD31-positive ECs were then isolated using a magnetic sorter and plated onto gelatin-coated 6-well plates for culture.

Cell culture and transfection

The primary HUVECs (human Umbilical Vein Endothelial Cells) from Lonza (CC-2519, USA) were cultured in EndoGRO (EMD Millipore) supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Atlanta Biological)(growth medium), and used for experiments until passage 6. Cos1 cells (ATCC) were cultured in DMEM (Gibco-BRL) with 10% FBS. For siRNA transfection, overnight serum-starved (0.5% FBS containing culture media) HUVECs were transfected with siRNAs (30 nM) for siControl (Ambion) or siDrp1 (Sense: ACUAUUGAAGGAACUGCAAAAUAUA[dT][dT]; Antisense: UAUAUUUUGCAGUUCCUUCAAUAGU[dT][dT]) from Sigma using Oligofectamine (Invitrogen, 12252011). For plasmid DNA transfection, cells were transfected with DNA ( ~ 6 μg) using polyethylenimine (PEI, Polysciences, USA) as we reported26. Adenovirus was transduced in HUVECs as we reported28. After transfection, cells were changed to a growth medium and further incubated for 48 h at 37 °C before experiments.

Mitochondria structure analysis

Overnight serum-starved (0.5%FBS) HUVECs were transfected with Mito-DsRed vector with the mitochondrial targeting sequence or incubated with MitoTracker Red FM (Invitrogen, M22425), followed by VEGF (20 ng/ml) stimulation for indicated time. Mitochondrial structure was imaged by confocal microscopy43.

Immunofluorescence analysis in cultured cells27,43

Cells were seeded onto coverslips and cultured for at least 2 h prior to fixing and staining. Thereafter, cells were fixed with either 4% paraformaldehyde (cat#15710, Electron Microscopy Sciences) for 10 min at RT and then permeabilized for 3 min with 0.2% Triton-× 100 (Millipore Sigma) Over-extracted cells were treated with 0.4% Triton-× 100. Cells were then washed three times with DPBS and stained overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in PBS. Next, they were washed twice with PBS for 5 min, incubated with secondary antibodies (diluted 1:1000) for 2 h at RT in PBS. The following antibodies were used: Rabbit anti-Tom 20 (cat# 11802-1-AP, Proteintech). Preabsorbed secondary antibodies used were anti-mouse IgG 647, anti-rabbit IgG 568 at a 1:1000 dilution. Coverslips were mounted onto slides using ProLong Diamond (Thermo Fisher). All slides were cured at RT in the dark for 2 days prior to imaging.

Quantification of mitochondria morphology

Cells were plated onto laminin-coated coverslips for 90 min, fixed and stained overnight for Tom20 to label mitochondria. Cells were imaged using the Nikon CSU-W1 spinning disk confocal microscope with a 100 × 1.49NA SR objective. Identical image acquisition settings were applied to all experimental groups27. Confocal z-stacks were denoised using Denoise.ai and deconvolved using the Blind algorithm at 20 iterations using NIS-Elements software. This combination of algorithm and number of iterations was chosen because it yielded the highest signal to noise without being destructive to the morphology of Tom20-stained mitochondria. 3D binary objects were created from the deconvolved z-stacks using intensity-based thresholding. The minimum threshold intensity value was manually chosen for each image, and a clean filter was applied to remove punctate background IF from the 3D binary mask.

Modified boyden chamber migration assay

The upper insert (8-μm pores coated with 0.1% gelatin) containing serum starved HUVEC suspensions (6 × 104 cells) were placed in the bottom 24-well chamber containing fresh media with 0.5 % FBS and stimulants. The chamber was incubated at 37 °C for 6 h. The upper insert membrane was fixed with 4% PFA for 10 min and stained with crystal violet. Cells remaining on the top of the transwells were removed with cotton swabs and migrated cells at six random fields were imaged with a Nikon digital camera, and the number of cells was counted by using NIH Image J software as we reported26,45.

Cell proliferation assay

The siRNA transfected cells (100,000 cells) were plated on coverslips in 0.5% FBS EndoGro for 24 h to synchronize cells, followed by incubation with 5 µM Bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) with or without VEGF (20 ng/mL) for 24 h. The BrdU incorporation was detected in fixed, permeabilized and 2 N HCl-treated cells followed by staining with anti-BrdU antibody. The cells were taken photos with fluorescence microscope (Keyence, BZ-X700) using ×40 objective. The percentage of BrdU-labelled cells was determined by counting as BrdU-positive/total nuclei as we reported26.

Fibrin bead angiogenesis assay

The capillary-like sprouting fibrin bead assay was performed with minor modifications40. HUVEC and fibroblasts (ATCC) were exposed to M199 medium with 10% FBS. HUVEC were attached to dextran-coated Cytodex 3 beads (GE Healthcare, 17048504, Cytiva) and embedded at 250 beads per well in a 24-well plate in a fibrin clot. Fibroblasts were then seeded as a monolayer over the clot at 1.5 × 105 cells per well. Clots were cultured under standard conditions in EGM-2 medium and medium was changed every day for 5–7 d. Bright-field and fluorescent images were taken, and the sprout number was quantified. The number of sprouts was measured as the number of independent sprouts extruding from each bead and normalized to the number of beads counted, as we reported26.

Wound scratch cell migration assay

Confluent ECs were scraped using sterilized 10-μL pipette tips, washed with 0.1% serum media, and stimulated with 20 ng/ml of VEGF for 16 h, Images were captured immediately at 0 h and at 16 h after the wounding, as we reported48.

DCP-Bio1 assay to detect Cys-OH formed (sulfenylated) proteins

To measure sulfenic acid (CysOH) formation (sulfenylation) of proteins, cells were lysed in degassed-specific lysis buffer [50 mM HEPES, pH7.0 at room temperature, 5 mM EDTA, 50 mM NaCl, 50 mM NaF, 1 mM Na3VO4, 10 mM sodium pyrophosphate, 5 mM Iodoacetamide, 100 µM DTPA, 1% Triton-×-100, protease inhibitor, 200 unit/mL catalase (Sigma Aldrich, 219008), 200 µM DCP-Bio1 (KaraFast, USA)] and then DCP-Bio1-bound proteins were pulled down with streptavidin beads (Thermo scientific, USA) overnight at 4 °C. DCP-Bio1-bound CysOH formed, sulfenylated-proteins were determined by immunoblotting with specific antibodies, as reported8,43,49.

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay

COS1 cells were transfected with DNAs (each 250 ~ 500 ng), including N-terminal Venus (VN)-flag-AMPK and C-terminal Venus (VC)-peptide (negative control) or VC-HA-Drp1-WT or VC-HA-Drp1-C644A using PEI for 24 h. The positive YFP signals by interaction between Drp1 and AMPK were taken by confocal microscopy, as we reported26,43. pECFP was used as a positive control for transfection.

Intracellular redox (ROS) measurement

To detect intracellular redox state (ROS), HUVECs were transfected with control siRNA and Drp1 siRNA for 48 h. After serum starvation for overnight, cells were stimulated with VEGF (20 ng/ml) for 30 min and then incubated with 5 μM CellRox Orange (Invitrogen, C10443) for 30 min at 37 °C. Fluorescence signal was measured by confocal microscopy (Zeiss LSM710) using the same exposure condition in each experiment. Relative fluorescence intensity was analyzed using LSM software, as we reported47.

MitoSOX (mitochondrial ROS) measurement

HUVECs were plated on 35 mm glass-bottom dishes and then serum-starved (0.5% FBS) for 3 h, followed by stimulated with VEGF (20 ng/ml) for 15 min. Cells were incubated with 5 μM MitoSOX (M36008) for 10 min at 37 °C. Fluorescence signals were measured by confocal microscopy with ×63 objective and relative fluorescence intensity was calculated using Image J. We confirmed that VEGF-induced increase in MitoSOX fluorescence was abolished by Adeno-Mito-catalase, as we reported28.

Cell viability assay

HUVECs were grown on 100 mm culture dishes and transfected with siDrp1 for 48 h. Cell viability was measured using CCK8 assay. For CCK8 assay, Cell Counting Kit 8 (WST-8/CCK8) (Abcam, ab228554) was used. CCK-8 solution (CCK8: medium, 1:10) was added into each dish and incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 1 h. The spectrometric absorbance at 450 nm was measured using the microplate reader (BioTek).

Drp1 GTPase activity assay

Cells were lysed in lysis buffer (50 nM HEPES, pH7.5, 120 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 10 mM Na pyrophosphate, 50 mM NaF, 1 mM Na3VO4, 1% Triton ×-100, protease inhibitors) and total cell lysate (1 mg) was used for immunoprecipitation using Drp1 antibody (Santa Cruz) for overnight at 4 °C. Anti-Drp1 immunoprecipitates were incubated with 50 μL protein A/G beads (Santa Cruz, sc-2003) for 2 h, and beads were washed three times with lysis buffer, and further washed three times with GTPase buffer (50 mM Tris, pH7.5, 2.5 mM MgCl2, 0.02% β-Mercaptoethanol). The GTPase activity was measured using the GTPase assay kit by following manufacturer’s instruction (KA1610, Abnova).

Seahorse XFe24 glycolysis stress test

HUVECs were seeded in the Seahorse XF24 cell culture plate (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA). Wells coated with 0.2% gelatin were used for background subtraction. Cells were washed with Seahorse media supplemented with Glutamine, then Seahorse XF24 glycolysis stress test (Agilent, Catalogue # 103020-100, Santa Clara, CA) was employed to evaluate glycolytic capacity according to manufacturer’s instructions, as we reported50.

Immunofluorescence

IF staining was performed in fresh frozen 7 μm thick OCT tissue sections that were stained with primary anti-mouse CD31 antibody (BiosciencesBD Pharmingen™ Purified Rat Anti-Mouse CD31, 550274) (1:300). Secondary antibodies were Alexa Fluor 488 or 546-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG and goat anti-mouse IgG (Invitrogen) (1:500). Tissue sections were mounted using Vectashield (Vector Laboratories) containing DAPI for nuclear counter-staining. Images were taken using a fluorescence microscope (Keyence, BZ-X700).

Western blot analysis and Immunoprecipitation

Cells were lysed in buffer [50 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 5 mM EDTA, 100 mM NaCl, 1% Triton ×-100, protease inhibitors (10 μg/ml aprotinin, 1 mmol/L phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, 10 μg/ml leupeptin), and phosphatase inhibitors (50 mmol/L sodium fluoride, 1 mmol/L sodium orthovanadate, 10 mmol/L sodium pyrophosphate)]. Lysates, both with or without immunoprecipitation (IP), were used for Western immunoblotting (IB), as we reported48,49. Antibodies are listed in Table 2 in Supplemental information.

Antibodies

Antibodies used for IF analysis and western blotting analysis are summarized in Tables 1 and 2 in the Supplementary Information.

Mitochondria and cytosolic fractionation

Mitochondria and Cytosol of HUVECs was fractionated by using Mitochondria/Cytosol Fractionation Kit (ab65320). Briefly, cells were washed and resuspended in cytosolic extraction buffer containing DTT and protease inhibitors. The cells were incubated on ice for 10 min and then homogenized with 40 strokes using a Dounce homogenizer. The homogenates were centrifuged at 700 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. This supernatant was further centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C to obtain the cytosolic fraction. The resulting pellet, containing crude mitochondria, was lysed in mitochondrial extraction buffer mix, and the mitochondrial fraction was collected.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was isolated by using Tri Reagent (Molecular Research Center, Inc.) and phenol/chloroform. Reverse transcription (RT) was carried out using a high-capacity cDNA RT kit (Applied Biosystems) with 2 µg of total RNA. The PCR was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol using ABI PRISM 7000 Sequence Detection System 26 (Applied Biosystems, CA) and QuantiFast SYBR Green PCR Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, Foster City, CA). Samples were all run in triplicates to reduce variability. The expression of genes was normalized and expressed as fold changes relative to HPRT. Primers used for RT-PCR are summarized in Table 3 in the Supplementary information.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. We performed blinded to group allocation during data collection and analysis. Data were compared between groups of cells and animals by unpaired two-tailed Student t-test when one comparison was performed or by ANOVA for multiple comparisons. When significance was indicated by ANOVA, the Tukey post-hoc, Holm–Šídák’s multiple comparisons test was used to specify between group differences. Values of *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001 were considered statically significant. Statistical tests were performed using Graph pad Prism v8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).
