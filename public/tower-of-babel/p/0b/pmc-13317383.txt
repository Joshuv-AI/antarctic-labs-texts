Screening for urinary abnormalities in healthy school children living in an African urban megacity
Authors: Agathe Bikupe Nkoy, Therance Tobo Matoka, Orielle Mafuta Minimbu, Bienvenu Matondo Odio, Elena Levtchenko, Pépé Mfutu Ekulu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUrinary screening is a useful and non-invasive tool for identifying occult kidney disease in asymptomatic children and may be an important initial step in preventing progressive kidney disease. However, this screening is not commonly performed in children living in low-resource countries, including the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC). This study aimed to determine the prevalence of albuminuria and its associated factors among healthy school children living in DRC. In addition, the study examined the prevalence of urinary abnormalities detected by dipstick in this population.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted in 17 schools in Kinshasa. A multistage sampling procedure was applied, and 497 healthy school children aged 6 to 16 were randomly recruited. Anthropometric and clinical data were collected. Using a single-sample measurement, urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) was determined by an immunoturbidimetric method, and dipstick tests were performed to detect other urinary abnormalities (leukocyturia, nitrituria, proteinuria, hematuria). Albuminuria was defined as ACR ≥ 30 mg/g. Logistic regression was used to identify clinical factors associated with albuminuria.ResultsAmong the 497 school children (208 boys and 289 girls), 58 had single-sample albuminuria, corresponding to a prevalence of 11.7%. There was no significant association between albuminuria and clinical variables based on univariate logistic regression analysis. Dipstick urinary abnormalities were present in 113 children (22.7%). Specifically, leukocyturia, nitrituria, proteinuria, and hematuria were detected in 14.3%, 1.6%, 8.2%, and 1.6% of participants, respectively. Leukocyturia and nitrituria were significantly more common among girls (p < 0.001).ConclusionUrinary abnormalities are common among asymptomatic school children in the DRC. Although a single measurement was performed, these results raise the relevant question of the usefulness of urinary screening in children living in resource-limited settings. However, the feasibility, cost, and potential benefits of such a program warrant further evaluation in these settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-05151-1.

Introduction

Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) is experiencing an increasing prevalence of chronic non-communicable diseases (NCDs), including chronic kidney disease (CKD) [1]. The latter is associated with high morbidity and mortality and severely impacts socio-economic development [2]. Without appropriate preventive and therapeutic measures, CKD progresses to kidney failure. At this stage, the treatment requires kidney replacement therapy (KRT), dialysis or transplantation, which is generally nonexistent or unaffordable in many low-income countries [2, 3]. Therefore, kidney disease screening is of paramount importance in low-income countries as early interventions are likely to be effective in reducing the rapid progression toward kidney failure, which likely leads to death in such settings [2].

Effective screening for kidney disease in children includes dipstick urinalysis and measurement of albuminuria. A dipstick is an inexpensive, widely available, and non-invasive tool for the early detection of kidney diseases [4]. Indeed, dipstick analysis can detect urinary abnormalities, such as proteinuria and hematuria, which may be the only early signs of kidney diseases, including glomerulonephritis, IgA nephropathy, and membranous nephropathy [5]. In addition, a dipstick can detect leukocyturia and nitrituria, which are suggestive of urinary tract infection (UTI) [6]. The latter may lead to CKD and hypertension (HTN) later in life if left untreated [5]. Importantly, urinary screening programs using dipsticks have demonstrated successful results for many years in several East Asian countries, including Japan, Korea, and Taiwan [7]. These programs enabled early detection of kidney disease and early intervention, thereby reducing the incidence of kidney failure among children living in these countries [7, 8].

On the other hand, albuminuria has been used as a marker of kidney and cardiovascular damage for more than four decades [9]. Indeed, albuminuria is recognized as an early marker of CKD in adults and children [10]. Most studies evaluating albuminuria prevalence in the general population have been conducted in adults. In the pediatric healthy population, a few studies have reported albuminuria prevalence ranging from 2.2% to 12.8% [9]. Furthermore, some risk factors have been associated with the development of albuminuria or proteinuria in children, including prematurity, low birth weight, use of herbal medicines, HTN, and obesity [11–13].

Several studies worldwide have investigated the prevalence of albuminuria (and other urinary abnormalities) and its risk factors among healthy children. However, data on children living in SSA, especially in Central Africa, are scarce. Therefore, the primary aim of the present study was to determine the prevalence of albuminuria and its associated factors among healthy school children living in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC). Secondly, the study examined the prevalence of urinary abnormalities detected by dipstick in this population.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted within the framework of a school-based project aimed at screening and managing kidney diseases in Congolese children. The sample size was calculated based on an expected albuminuria prevalence of at least 9.7% [14], a precision of 3%, a design effect of 1, and a nonresponse rate of 20%. We determined that ≥ 468 participants needed to be recruited. A total of 515 participants were recruited in the present study.

We used a multistage sampling method based on a World Health Organization (WHO) surveillance protocol (the WHO STEPwise approach to chronic disease risk factor surveillance) [15]. Healthy children aged 6–16 years were randomly recruited from 17 schools in Kinshasa over a 12-month period, from January through December 2019, as previously described [16]. Kinshasa, the capital of DRC, is an urban city divided into four main administrative districts. One district was randomly selected from the four main districts. Next, within that district, we selected two of the five communes using probability proportional to size (number of schools per commune). Then, 17 schools were randomly selected from the two communes, with probability proportional to size. The selected schools were public and private, located in various socioeconomic status areas within the selected district. The schools were identified from the DRC Ministry of Primary, Secondary, and Professional Education’s full list of schools. In each school, we randomly selected six classes using the full list of pupils. Finally, at least five pupils in each class were systematically selected. Pupils were recruited after obtaining consent from parents or legal guardians and assent from older children. Participants were excluded if they had a fever on the day of enrollment or a history of acute febrile illness one week prior to the study.

Clinical assessment and anthropometry

A self-administered questionnaire containing questions on personal details for each child was sent to parents before the day of data collection. This questionnaire included sociodemographic data (birth date, sex, address, educational level), the child’s personal history (low birth weight defined as a birth weight < 2500 g, prematurity, recurrent UTI, sickle cell disease, congenital cardiac defect, diabetes mellitus, previous acute kidney injury, preexisting kidney disease), and family medical history (diabetes mellitus, CKD, HTN).

At enrollment, anthropometric data (weight, height, body mass index) and blood pressure (BP) were collected from all participants by a trained medical staff. Body weight and height were measured using standard methods and calibrated devices. Weight, height, and body mass index (BMI) percentiles were determined using reference growth charts for children aged 2 to 20 years [17]. BMI percentiles were categorized based on the Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) definitions of BMI categories for children and teens, as follows: underweight (< 5th percentile), healthy weight (5th percentile to < 85th percentile), overweight (85th to < 95th percentile), and obesity (95th percentile or greater) [18]. In the present study, higher BMI was defined as overweight or obesity. BP was measured on the right upper arm with participants in a sitting position after 5 min of relaxation, using an automated sphygmomanometer (OMRON M2 Basic, OMRON HealthCare co., Ltd, Kyoto, Japan) at heart level. The average of three BP measurements was used for the analysis. BP values were reported to the 2017 American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) tables, height- and sex-specific percentiles. In the present study, elevated BP at one visit was defined as systolic BP (SBP) and/or diastolic BP (DBP) ≥ 95th percentile [19].

Urinary abnormalities assessment

Participants were asked to provide a fresh morning urinary sample in a clean container, previously labelled with the participant’s study number and given at school on the day of enrollment, shortly before testing. Urinary samples were subsequently tested by trained medical staff. The latter received training on performing and interpreting urinalysis before starting data collection. Female adolescent subjects were invited to provide urinary samples remote from the menstrual period. Dipstick test strips (Siemens Multistix 10 SG) were used for the semi-quantitative determination of leukocytes, nitrites, proteins, and blood, following colour bands for each test based on a visual reading. Results were expressed as negative, trace, 1+, 2+, 3+ (or sometimes 4+). This study defined dipstick urinary abnormalities (leukocyturia, nitrituria, proteinuria, and hematuria) as values ≥ 1 + on test strips.

Urinary albumin-creatinine ratio (ACR) was determined using an immunoturbidimetric method with a DCA Vantage Analyzer (Siemens Healthineers Global, Erlangen, Germany). Single-sample albuminuria was defined as ACR ≥ 30 mg/g. Moderate and severe increased albuminuria were defined as ACR 30–299 mg/g and ACR ≥ 300 mg/g, respectively [20].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 29.0.1.1 (IBM SPSS Statistics). Continuous variables are presented as mean with standard deviation (SD) or median with an interquartile range (IQR). Qualitative variables are presented as counts and percentages. Independent groups were compared using the Student’s t-test, chi-square test, or Fisher’s Exact Test, as appropriate. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with albuminuria. We first performed univariate logistic regression, selecting covariates based on their clinical or epidemiological relevance to albuminuria development. These covariates included: age, sex, higher BMI, elevated BP, and history of prematurity. Since none of them reached statistical significance, we did not build a multivariate model.

The remaining 18 participants were excluded, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The general characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1. The mean age, weight, height, and BMI were significantly higher in girls than in boys.

Prevalence of single-sample albuminuria

The median ACR of the participants was 6.4 mg/g (IQR 4.2–12.9 mg/g). As shown in Fig. 2, 11.7% of participants (95% CI: 9.1–14.7%) were identified with moderately increased albuminuria, with a median of 73.4 mg/g (IQR 43.8–110.4 mg/g). The frequency of albuminuria was not different between boys and girls [25/208 (12.0%) vs. 33/289 (11.4%), p = 0.888]. None of the participants presented with severely increased albuminuria.

Prevalence of urinary abnormalities detected by dipstick

Table 2 shows the distribution of urinary abnormalities by sex. Urinary abnormalities were detected in 113/497 children (22.7%, 95% CI: 19.3–26.6%). Hematuria, proteinuria, and leukocyturia + nitrituria were found in 1.6% (95% CI: 0.8–3.0), 8.2% (95% CI: 6.1–10.9%), and 14.9% (95% CI: 12.0–18.2%), respectively. Some participants presented more than one abnormality. Indeed, among 74 participants with leukocyturia and nitrituria, 7 also had proteinuria, and 3 had hematuria (Fig. 3). Leukocyturia combined nitrituria was more prevalent in girls than boys (OR 8.67, 95% CI 3.87–19.32, p < 0.001).

Factors associated with single-sample albuminuria

Characteristics of the study participants, depending on the presence of proteinuria or albuminuria, are presented in supplementary materials (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). Demographic, anthropometric, and clinical variables were not significantly different between participants with and without albuminuria. In addition, none of the previously reported risk factors, such as prematurity, low birth weight, higher BMI, or elevated BP, were significantly associated with albuminuria after univariate logistic regression (Supplementary Table S3). Likewise, no statistically significant associations with proteinuria were detected in this population.

Discussion

This study is among the few in Central Africa to report the prevalence of urinary abnormalities in a large cohort of apparently healthy children.

Albuminuria

Several studies have reported the prevalence of albuminuria in the adult population. In the pediatric population, relevant studies on albuminuria are limited and mainly conducted in high-risk populations (diabetes, obesity, hypertension, HIV, sickle cell disease) [9]. To date, albuminuria is known as a strong independent predictor of kidney disease and cardiovascular mortality in high-risk populations and healthy ones [9, 21]. In the present study, 11.7% of apparently healthy children presented with albuminuria. This high prevalence of albuminuria can be explained by potential contributors, such as greater intra-individual variability from day to day and increased orthostatic variation in albumin excretion among children, as acknowledged by other authors [22, 23]. Importantly, this prevalence is based on a single-sample ACR measurement. Thus, albuminuria observed in our study does not reflect the true rate of kidney disease and cannot be considered CKD in the absence of repeated ACR measurements to confirm its persistence. Future longitudinal studies, including a larger number of healthy children, performing first-morning or 24-h urine collection and repeated ACR measurements, are warranted. This could allow us to understand the natural progression of albuminuria and determine the true prevalence of CKD in this population.

Furthermore, the prevalence of albuminuria in the present study is similar to that of 9.7% reported in a previous cohort of Congolese healthy children aged 2–18 years, recruited from popular churches in Kinshasa (DRC), using a single-sample ACR quantitative measurement [14]. This result is also consistent with previous observations in large population-based studies conducted in Australia and the United States [22, 23]. Indeed, using a stratified multistage sampling design and a single-random urinary sample, Larkins et al. reported a 12.8% prevalence of albuminuria (ACR ≥ 30 mg/g) in Australian children aged 5–18 [22]. Similarly, among US adolescents aged 12–18 years recruited in a nationally representative, stratified, multistage cross-sectional study conducted between 2009 and 2010, the prevalence of single-sample albuminuria (ACR ≥ 30 mg/g) was 14.8% [23].

In contrast, the prevalence observed in the present study is lower than observed in the Netherlands (23.4%) [24], Nigeria (33.2%) [25], and Mexico (45.7%) [21]. Although the study in The Netherlands [24] used quantitative ACR measurement, the discrepancy in albuminuria prevalence observed in our study may be related to differences in participant age (20–40 months versus 6–16 years). Interestingly, the authors attributed the high prevalence of albuminuria (ACR ≥ 30 mg/g) observed in their study to low urinary creatinine levels in toddlers, reflecting lower muscle mass than in older children and adults. In the two other studies [21, 25], the authors used dipstick tests (Micral-Test), a semi-quantitative method for albuminuria detection. Although the Micral-Test is specific for detecting human albumin, it is less accurate than quantitative albuminuria testing and may yield false-positive results [26]. This could justify the high prevalence reported in both studies. Nevertheless, the study from Mexico was conducted among children living in a rural community with a high prevalence of CKD of unknown origin (CKDu) [21]. In addition, the prevalence of albuminuria was based on repetitive measurements (persistent albuminuria after at least 2 positive albuminuria tests within 12-week intervals). The authors speculated that this alarming prevalence could be driven by environmental factors that warrant further investigation to reduce the burden of CKDu in this Mexican community.

Factors associated with albuminuria

Several factors have been associated with proteinuria or albuminuria in children, including prematurity, low birth weight, use of herbal medicines, elevated BP, and obesity [11–13]. Recent evidence showed an increasing prevalence of childhood HTN and obesity in resource-limited settings, including SSA. In the present study, elevated BP (at one visit) and obesity were found in 39.3% and 6.0%, respectively. This prevalence of elevated BP ranges from 0.2 to 38.9%, as reported in studies among African children and adolescents [27]. This heterogeneity seen between studies in the prevalence of elevated BP may be attributed to differences in sample size, type of cuff size used, BP measurement device used (automated versus manual), number of measurements taken (single versus multiple occasions), and the various guidelines used to classify elevated BP [27]. However, the prevalence of elevated BP in the current study is higher than expected. This may be partially due to a white-coat effect. In addition, elevated BP was defined using a single BP measurement rather than multiple measurements, as recommended [19, 27]. In a study involving school-aged children living in Nigeria, the authors reported a significant reduction in HTN prevalence when BP was taken on three different occasions within 2 weeks (6.1%, 3.5%, and finally 3.1%) [28]. This finding highlights the need to follow children to confirm or rule out HTN diagnosis. Although several studies have reported association between albuminuria and risk factors mentioned above, no statistically significant associations were found in the present study. It should be noted that the lack of association with albuminuria must be interpreted with caution, as the small number of participants with albuminuria in the present study may be underpowered to detect modest associations.

Urinary abnormalities by dipstick

In this study, the overall prevalence of dipstick urinary abnormalities was 22.7%. This prevalence is much higher than reported in Nepal (5.5%), India (5.8%), and Nigeria (9.6%) [29–31]. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in sample size, age groups, screening frequency (single versus repeated), screening methods, and the type of urinary abnormalities detected. For instance, in a study of 2,243 asymptomatic Nepalese school children aged 5–15 [29], the authors reported a prevalence of dipstick urinary abnormalities of 5.5%. It should be emphasized that this prevalence was determined solely based on proteinuria and hematuria. Using a multistage stratified randomization study, Akor et al. [31] reported a prevalence of 9.6% of urinary abnormalities among 650 healthy Nigerian school children aged 5–12. Urinary abnormalities were defined as the presence of proteinuria, hematuria, nitrituria, glycosuria, ketonuria, bilirubinuria, and urobilinogenuria. It should be noted that in the aforementioned studies [29, 31], the authors did not test for leukocyturia, representing the most common abnormality detected in the present study. However, it is worth mentioning that in our study, urinary abnormalities were measured only once. This might overestimate the true prevalence of these abnormalities, as demonstrated by some studies that performed repeated urinalysis [4, 29]. These authors found a significant decrease in the prevalence of these abnormalities among children tested positive at the first screening.

The overall prevalence of proteinuria was 8.2%. A comparable result was previously reported in the DRC, where proteinuria was detected in 7.4% of apparently healthy adolescents and young adults [32]. Worldwide, the reported prevalence of proteinuria in children ranges from 0.08 to 29.2% [11, 12, 31, 33, 34]. Several factors could explain this wide range in proteinuria prevalence, including differences in sample size, the study population selection criteria (random versus non-random sampling), the frequency of screening (once versus repeated measurements), and the various definitions of proteinuria. For example, in a cohort of school-aged Turkish children [11], the authors used a quantitative method (urinary protein-creatinine ratio/PCR). In contrast, most authors used a semi-quantitative method (dipstick from different manufacturers) with various cutoff values to determine the frequency of proteinuria. It should be acknowledged that the semi-quantitative dipstick method for proteinuria measurement is less accurate than quantitative determination [10].

In the current study, leukocyturia combined with nitrituria was the most common urinary abnormality detected by dipstick, with a high prevalence of 14.9%. These two abnormalities are suggestive of infection, but do not confirm the diagnosis of UTI without microscopic and bacteriological analyses. Notably, the dipstick analysis is highly susceptible to contamination, especially in girls. The higher prevalence of leukocyturia and nitrituria among girls may reflect higher rates of bacteriuria in females, due to their short urethras, making them more susceptible to ascending bacterial infections [31, 35].

In addition, dipstick analysis depends on the techniques used for urine collection and storage. Thus, the collection method should be taken into account when interpreting these findings. In this study, we took precautions to provide clean containers and collect urinary samples in the morning hours, shortly prior to testing, to avoid contamination from inadequate storage and transport, and from time delays between collection and testing. Future studies that perform first-morning urinary collection and culture are required to determine the true prevalence of asymptomatic UTI in this population.

It is worth mentioning that there is no consensus on the effectiveness of urinary dipstick screening programs for kidney diseases in children worldwide. The primary concern is the uncertainty of whether early detection of kidney disease in childhood will allow effective interventions and reduce the number of individuals progressing to kidney failure. An additional concern is the cost-effectiveness of such a program [36]. In Asian countries, dipstick screening programs had significant impacts and provided an early opportunity to reduce the incidence of kidney failure in children [7, 8]. However, American and European experts do not share this opinion. In 2007, the AAP recommended discontinuing routine urinary screening in children since this program was cost-ineffective [36]. Nonetheless, the usefulness of this screening program has yet to be evaluated in African children. Although urinary dipstick tests are cheaper, implementating such a screening program in a resource-limited setting like the DRC should be rigorously examined, balancing benefits, harms, cost-effectiveness, and feasibility.

Strength and limitations

The present study is one of the few population-based assessments of urinary abnormalities in a large cohort of children living in Central Africa. In addition, albuminuria was determined using a quantitative method capable of detecting low albumin concentrations. However, some limitations are to be highlighted. First, the cross-sectional design, with a single measurement of urinary abnormalities, did not allow for the confirmation of their persistence, as recommended by the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines. For instance, in a large cohort of Chinese children from infancy to 17 years old, Zhong et al. found a 70% decrease in the prevalence of urinary abnormalities from the first to the second screening, suggesting that most abnormalities identified at the first screening were transient [4]. Second, the relatively smaller number of participants with albuminuria did not allow further identification of associated factors. Future longitudinal studies, including a larger number of children and repeated ACR measurements, are expected to contribute to the understanding of albuminuria progression and examine associated factors. Thirdly, the presence of leukocytes and nitrites on dipstick testing was suggestive of UTI, although this was not confirmed by urinary culture. Finally, since the study was conducted in Kinshasa, the results may not be generalizable to the entire pediatric population of the DRC or Central Africa. Nevertheless, this study provides preliminary data that may be useful for evaluating the utility of implementing an awareness and screening program for childhood kidney diseases in this resource-limited setting. This evaluation should further explore the feasibility, cost, and potential impact of such a program in this setting.

Conclusion

The present study revealed that albuminuria and dipstick urinary abnormalities are common in asymptomatic school children living in a megacity in the DRC. These findings underscore the need for frequent urinary screening among school children in resource-limited settings. The impacts, cost-effectiveness, and feasibility of such a screening should be further evaluated in these settings.
