Leiomyosarcoma First Signaled by Chest Pain
Authors: Khalid Shakfeh, Valentin Suma, Georges El Khoury, Cristina Font, Erol Belli, Thomas Zeyl, Sharon Natanzon, Omer Mohamedahmed, Farah El-Sharkawy Navarro, Khadeeja Esmail

ABSTRACT

Careful consideration of all imaging findings is important, especially in instances where 1 modality suggests a benign mass and another suggests malignancy.

Case Presentation

A 49-year-old man presented with 1 month of worsening exertional chest pain and dyspnea. He described the chest pain as a substernal pressure that worsened with exertion and improved with rest. On arrival, all vital signs were within normal limits. Physical examination revealed bibasilar crackles and a diastolic rumble heard best at the cardiac apex. Laboratory evaluation was remarkable for a platelet count of 76 × 103/μL and an N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide value of 2,846 pg/mL. Remaining laboratory values, including serial high sensitivity troponins, were within normal limits. Chest radiography was significant for mild cardiomegaly with interstitial pulmonary edema and small pleural effusions. Electrocardiography revealed normal sinus rhythm (Figure 1). Past Medical History

He had no known past medical history, although he admitted to having poor access to medical care.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) revealed an ejection fraction of 50% to 55%, a severely dilated left atrium (LA) containing a 4.1 × 4-cm (maximal diameter) heterogeneous mass that protruded into the left ventricular cavity in diastole with each beat, resulting in significant turbulence in mitral inflow and mitral valve (MV) obstruction (Video 1). The MV apparatus appeared structurally normal. At a heart rate of 73 beats/min, there was a mean transmitral gradient of 12.7 mm Hg. Pressure half-time was prolonged at 183 ms. Pulmonary artery systolic pressure was at least 42 mm Hg. It was concluded that the patient had severe MS and possible left atrial thrombus, and further imaging was recommended.

Cardiac computed tomography angiography revealed an 8.8 × 5.0 × 8.6-cm lobulated, low-density filling defect that filled the left atrial appendage (LAA) and extended into the LA and through the mitral annulus with mass effect on the MV (Figure 2). The mass measured 30 Hounsfeld units without delayed enhancement, consistent with thrombus or tumor.Figure 2Cardiac Computed Tomography AngiographyLarge, multilobulated filling defect within the left atrium and left atrial appendage causing significant inflow obstruction of the mitral valve.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a very large, lobular, heterogeneous, and pedunculated mass with T1-intermediate intensity and T2 hyperintensity originating from the lateral wall of the LA without associated thrombus (Figure 3). Mild heterogeneity was observed on T2-weighted imaging. Minimal heterogenous delayed enhancement was seen with gadolinium-based contrast. Transesophageal echocardiogram revealed a heterogenous mass with solid and cystic components, with redemonstration of severe mitral stenosis (MS) (Video 2) along with severe mitral regurgitation and extension into the left upper pulmonary vein and LAA (Video 3). The mass appeared to originate within the LAA via a broad-based, sessile, ill-defined attachment extending into the LA and late gadolinium enhancement (Video 4). During diastole, the mass protruded into the left ventricle, resulting in severe MS and mitral regurgitation. Notably, ultrasound-enhancing agents revealed internal microbubbles within the mass, indicative of prominent internal vascularity (Video 5). Blood cultures that were obtained on admission were negative.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential evolved over the course of his hospitalization. Regarding the index chest pain, acute coronary syndrome was ruled out. The chest pain was ultimately determined to be secondary to the cardiac mass.

Initial TTE suggested possible thrombus. However, the true size and heterogeneous nature of mass were not fully appreciated because of suboptimal windows. Additionally, there was only a trace pericardial effusion. Interestingly, although there appeared to be valve deformity in diastole with Doppler evidence of MS, supportive of left atrial thrombus, the actual etiology of the valvular dysfunction was “invisible” on standard views as the distal protruding part of the mass was cystic and appeared echo lucent.

Cardiac myxoma was included in the differential given MRI findings of a left-sided, T1-intermediate and T2-hyperintense mass with minimal heterogenous late gadolinium enhancement. Additionally, advanced imaging demonstrated significant mobility with tumor prolapse through the MV. Importantly, thrombus was ruled out by MRI because of contrast enhancement. Transesophageal echocardiography also favored malignancy given the significant vascularity and a well-visualized pericardial effusion. Endocarditis was unlikely because of negative blood cultures and no other predisposing risk factors.

Management

After completion of imaging, cardiothoracic surgery was consulted for resection. Left and right heart catheterizations were requested. Left heart catheterization revealed angiographically normal coronary arteries. Right heart catheterization revealed elevated left- and right-sided filling pressures with moderate pulmonary hypertension and a normal cardiac index. By hospital day 4, the patient underwent resection. The initial surgical impression favored a myxoma with lateral attachments and a stalk on the coumadin ridge. Because the mass extended into the atrial wall, a portion of the LA was excised in full thickness to the insertion site and then underwent primary repair. The MV annulus was dilated but otherwise intact; thus, it was repaired with a 34 Physio ring (Edwards), and the LAA was excluded with a 45-mm clip. No residual tissue was seen.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Final measurements of the resected mass were 8.5 × 7.5 × 4.0 cm (Figure 4) and 2.5 × 2.0 × 0.6 cm. Histopathologic evaluation revealed a malignant spindle cell neoplasm with myxoid features involving the endomyocardium, with positive margins at the resected insertion site. A high mitotic count and large areas of necrosis were found. Immunohistochemical studies were positive for smooth muscle actin, muscle specific actin, caldesmon, and desmin, supporting smooth muscle differentiation (Figure 5). Vascular, neural, and melanocytic markers were negative. These were consistent with a high-grade leiomyosarcoma with myxoid features. There was no evidence of MS or residual mass on repeat TTE. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 5 with plans for radiation-oncology follow-up. Three months later, he returned with symptoms similar to his index hospitalization. A repeat TTE demonstrated recurrence of the left atrial mass (Video 6). On repeat computed tomography, there appeared to be new involvement within the right atrium and pleural effusions, the sampling of which was consistent with metastasis. After a multidisciplinary discussion, palliative chemoradiation was offered, and, ultimately, he enrolled in hospice.

Discussion

Primary malignancy cardiac tumors (PMCTs) are incredibly rare, accounting for <0.03% of all tumors.1 Among PMCTs, sarcomas are the most common, accounting for 64.8%. Lymphomas account for 27%, and mesotheliomas account for 8%.2 Leiomyosarcomas account for <10% of primary cardiac sarcomas. Given their rarity, large epidemiologic studies are thus far not possible. However, based on reported cases of cardiac leiomyosarcomas, they generally originate from the LA, present between the fourth and sixth decades of life, and often involve the pulmonary veins and MV. There is no sex predilection. They may present with nonspecific symptoms including chest pain and shortness of breath.

On cardiac MRI, they generally exhibit heterogeneity because of areas of necrosis, hemorrhage, and variable cellularity with T1 isointensity and T2 hyperintensity due to increased tumoral edema, often with “dark T2 areas” due to necrosis.3 Significant and highly heterogeneous delayed enhancement with gadolinium is highly indicative of malignancy, as is hypervascularity with first-pass perfusion sequences.4

Similarly, on echocardiogram, PMCTs are generally irregular with ill-defined borders and highly heterogeneous with hypo- or hyperechoic features because of variable areas of necrosis, hemorrhage, or tumoral cell proliferation. They may also exhibit broad-based attachment. The use of echo contrast can be diagnostic in such cases, indicating the presence of a hypervascular mass that is highly specific for malignancy.5 Pericardial effusions are often associated with PMCTs as well.

This case is unique in relation to its imaging findings and size of the mass. The mass in our patient did not have the mixed hyperintensity and highly heterogenous late gadolinium enhancement, as is usually seen on cardiac MRI, but confidently excluded thrombus. Transesophageal echocardiography most accurately predicted malignancy. Regardless, the mass caused functional severe symptomatic MS and mitral regurgitation, and resection was indicated. Although MRI is the gold standard for characterization of intracardiac masses, appropriate attention should be given to echocardiographic findings, especially when there is a discrepancy between the identified natures of the masses (ie, malignant or benign).

This case highlights the importance of multimodal imaging in such challenging cases. Imaging findings were discrepant. Echocardiography favored a malignant process, whereas radiographic imaging was inconclusive. The diagnostic challenge continued even intraoperatively, with initial impressions favoring a myxoma as the mass appeared to be pedunculated. Regardless of the etiology, surgery was indicated considering the severe obstruction to MV outflow. In cases of conflicting imaging findings, it would be reasonable to consider intraoperative frozen sections for preliminary diagnosis and to confirm negative margins.

Conclusions

This case demonstrates an unusually large cardiac leiomyosarcoma and how the differential evolved with each additional test. The patient had originally presented with suspected unstable angina and was discharged with a diagnosis of cardiac leiomyosarcoma. Despite cardiac MRI being the gold standard, the malignant nature of this mass was better characterized by echocardiography.
