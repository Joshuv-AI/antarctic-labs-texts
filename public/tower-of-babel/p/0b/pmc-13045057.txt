Completeness of reporting in abstracts of randomized controlled trials and factors associated with complete reporting: a meta-research study
Authors: Corentin Chapron, Lina Ghosn, Carolina Riveros, Gabriel Baron, Isabelle Boutron
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe CONSORT statement for reporting abstracts of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) provides a list of essential items to report to ensure reliable interpretation. We evaluated the completeness of reporting of RCT abstracts published in high-impact-factor journals and examined factors associated with adherence to the CONSORT for abstracts.MethodsOn March 5, 2024, we searched PubMed for abstracts of RCTs published in the top five journals (ISI Web of Knowledge 2023) in 22 medical fields, including Medicine, General & Internal, from July 1, 2023, to February 29, 2024. We assessed the completeness of reporting of the 15 items of the CONSORT for abstracts in duplicate via a standardized data extraction form. The primary outcome was the mean number of items adequately reported. The prespecified factors potentially associated with adherence to CONSORT for abstracts included study-specific factors (income category of the country, funding type, and sample size) and journal-specific factors (type of journal [specialized vs. general medicine], CONSORT endorsement, abstract word count limit, and structured abstract).ResultsAmong 1267 citations, 610 abstracts published in 82 distinct journals were included. The mean (SD) number of items adequately reported was 10.1 (2.5). Among the 610 abstracts included, the quality of reporting varied across items; some items were adequately reported (97% (n=594) for participants, 100% (n=610) for interventions, 96% (n=585) for objectives) and others were poorly reported (29% (n=175) for trial design, 36% (n=221) for funding sources, 32% (n=197) for blinding and 13% (n=81) for randomization). The multivariate linear-mixed effects model showed that a sample size above 100, the CONSORT endorsement and Medicine, General & Internal journals were associated with a higher mean number of items adequately reported.ConclusionsReporting of RCT abstracts remains incomplete particularly for key items including trial design, random sequence generation, blinding, and funding source disclosure. CONSORT endorsement, publication in a general medical journal and larger sample size were associated with better adherence to CONSORT for abstracts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41073-026-00196-0.Keywords: Randomized Controlled Trial, Completeness of Reporting, Reporting guidelines, CONSORT for Abstracts, CONSORT, Meta-research

Background

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are the gold standard for evaluating the efficacy of medical interventions as randomization minimizes bias [1–5]. Clear and thorough reporting of RCTs is essential for supporting evidence-based decision-making [6].

To standardize this reporting, the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) statement was developed in 1996 [7], and updated in 2010 [8] and 2025 [9]. It provides comprehensive guidance for reporting randomized controlled trials, covering essential elements such as trial design, participants, interventions, outcomes, participant flow, numbers analysed, outcomes and estimations along with registration and funding sources. The CONSORT statement is now endorsed by most journals including the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) and some journals enforce its implementation (e.g., submission of a CONSORT checklist).

Abstracts are a critical component of a research manuscript as they often serve as the sole accessible part of a study [10, 11]. It is therefore imperative that they provide clear, transparent, and sufficient information about the trial's conduct and results [12]. To enhance the reliability and usefulness of abstracts, the CONSORT Group developed an extension-specific to abstracts (CONSORT for abstracts), which outlined key reporting requirements [13].

To our knowledge, no recent studies have evaluated the completeness of reporting across a large sample of medical journals.

Objective

This study aimed to 1) assess the reporting of abstracts of RCTs published in high-impact general and specialized journals and 2) examine factors associated with adherence to the CONSORT for abstracts.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a meta-research study to evaluate the completeness of reporting in abstracts of RCTs. This study is guided by the standards of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) statement [14].

Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies included two-arm parallel-group, individual RCTs that reported the results of the primary analysis, with no restrictions on the type of intervention or comparator type. We excluded reports describing secondary, post-hoc, or interim analyses of RCTs. Multi-arm trials were excluded because they involve more complex designs and require additional reporting elements beyond the core CONSORT guidelines, which may be associated with systematically different trial characteristics.

Data sources

On March 5, 2024 we searched MEDLINE via PubMed for RCT abstracts published between 1 July 2023 and 29 February 2024. We limited the research to RCTs published in the top five journals from each of the following twenty-two medical fields: Medicine, General & Internal, Allergy, Anesthesiology, Cardiac & Cardiovascular Systems, Clinical Neurology, Critical Care Medicine, Dentistry, Oral Surgery & Medicine, Dermatology, Emergency Medicine, Endocrinology & Metabolism, Gastroenterology & Hepatology, Genetics & Heredity, Hematology, Infectious Diseases, Obstetrics & Gynecology, Ophthalmology, Sport Sciences, Pediatrics, Psychiatry, Rheumatology, Tropical Medicine, and Urology & Nephrology.

We ranked the journals on the basis of the Journal Citation Reports (via the ISI Web of Knowledge 2023) [15].

Search strategy

The search combined the journal name, publication date filters and article type with "Randomized Controlled Trial". The full strategy is available in Appendix 1.

Selection process

We imported retrieved studies to Rayyan software [16] and removed duplicates. Two reviewers (CC and CR) independently screened titles and abstracts, resolving disagreements by consensus.

Data collection process

Two researchers (CC and CR) independently extracted the data and evaluated the completeness of reporting for the included studies on the basis of the CONSORT for abstracts statement. The two researchers also extracted information related to the included studies and journal characteristics. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus and a third researcher was consulted when needed.

Data items

Evaluation of the completeness of reporting in abstracts

We developed a data extraction and evaluation form considering 15 of the 17 items from the CONSORT for Abstracts checklist. We excluded two items namely author contact details and recruitment status (see Appendix 2 for the full item list). The item ‘Authors’ was not assessed because it applied only to conference abstracts. Similarly, the item ‘Recruitment’ was excluded since all included studies had already been completed, making the item irrelevant to our sample.

To ensure consistency in evaluation and facilitate consensus, we developed rules for assessment (see Appendix 3). The form was piloted on the first thirty studies to ensure consistency.

Each item was scored “yes” if completely reported, “partial” if partially reported, or “no” if not reported. For the analysis, an item was considered “adequately reported” if it was scored as “yes” (completely reported) or “partial” with partial reporting (coded as 1) versus “no” (coded as 0, not reported). The total score for each abstract therefore ranged from 0 to 15.

We also collected data on baseline characteristics, including year of publication, and disease domain.

Potential factors associated with adherence to CONSORT for abstracts

Based on the published literature, we pre-specified seven potential factors that could affect adherence to CONOSRT guidelines. These factors are either study-specific or journal-specific. The study-specific factors considered were: country of conduct income [15–17], type of funding [17, 18], and sample size [17, 18]. The journal-specific factors considered were the type of journal (specialized versus general medicine), CONSORT endorsement (the journal policy on the use of CONSORT for reporting RCTs) [17], the requirement for a structured abstract [19], and the abstract word count limit [17, 19, 20].

The two reviewers extracted the information needed from the full-text publication or the trial registry. The data included the journal name and type, sample size, country of conduct and type of funding. We classified countries into several categories on the basis of their income per the World Bank [21] criteria: lower middle/low income, mixed (for the studies that were conducted in countries with different income levels), upper middle income, and high income. When the country of conduct was not reported, we considered the first author’s affiliation. We classified funding as public, private/mixed (private and public), unclear or not reported. Private and mixed funding were grouped for analysis.

On 15 May 2024, we consulted each journal’s 'Instructions for Authors' webpage and collected information regarding abstract specifications and reporting guidelines, including the maximum allowable word count for the abstract and whether a structured abstract format was needed.

We then looked for information about the CONSORT endorsement. We examined each journal policy on CONSORT which is typically stated under “Instructions to Authors”. We recorded an endorsement only if the instructions contained an explicit recommendation or requirement for authors to follow CONSORT guidelines when reporting randomized trials. The absence of any such statement was classified as no endorsement. We collected information about the reported sample size and categorized them into three groups: fewer than 100 participants, 100 - 300 participants, and more than 300 participants.

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome was the mean (SD) number of items adequately reported out of the 15 considered items. The completeness of reporting per item was also reported as a percentage. We present the completeness of reporting stratified by type of journal.

Univariate and multivariate linear mixed effects models, including a random intercept for journals to account for variability between them) were fitted to assess associations between the primary outcome, defined as the number of items adequately reported, and the seven factors listed above. These candidate covariates were determined a priori and were included as fixed effect. Backwards selection was performed, with the final model including variables with a p value less than 0.05. Results of models were expressed as coefficients with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). All the statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.2.3.

Results

Study selection

Among the 1,267 records retrieved, 610 abstracts of RCTs published across 82 distinct journals were eligible and included in our study (PRISMA flow diagram, Fig. 1). We excluded 540 records for several reasons, including records of multi-arm trials, secondary analyses, other study designs and reports that included more than one RCT.

Reporting completeness of individual CONSORT for abstract items

We evaluated the completeness of reporting for the included studies based on the CONSORT for abstracts statement. We report hereby the results of the assessment in abstract sections:

Methods

The reporting of methodological information has varied. The majority of abstracts clearly described the study interventions (100%, n = 610; item 5), the eligibility criteria of participants and the study setting (97%, n = 594; item 4), and the specific study objectives (96%, n = 585; item 6). The primary outcome was defined in 82% of abstracts (n = 503; item 7). The studies were identified as randomized in the title in 75% of the included abstracts (n = 455; item 1).

Other methodological details were reported less consistently. Only 29% of the abstracts (n = 175; item 3) clearly stated the trial design (e.g., parallel, crossover, cluster, superiority, non-inferiority, or factorial), and blinding was adequately described in 32% (n = 197) of abstracts (item 9). The method of random sequence generation (randomization) was reported in just 13% of the included abstracts (n = 81; item 8).

Results

Reporting of outcomes was generally better, though gaps remain. Among the 610 included abstracts, the results for the primary outcome were reported in 91% of abstracts (n = 558; item 13). Participant flow was less well documented: numbers randomized per group were reported in 57% of abstracts (n = 345; item 10), and numbers analysed were reported in 43% (n = 261; item 11). Harms were described in 53% of the abstracts (n = 322; item 14).

Conclusions and other information

Conclusions were almost universally reported, with 99% of abstracts (n = 604; item 15) providing a clear summary of the study’s main findings.

Trial registration was reported in 74% of abstracts (n = 450; item 16), whereas funding sources were described in 36% (n = 221; item 17).

On average, abstracts reported 10.1 (SD 2.5) of the 15 checklist items. Only 2% of abstracts (n = 13) adhered fully to all CONSORT for abstract items. (Details in Table 3)

Discussion

We assessed the completeness of reporting of RCT abstracts across a large and recent sample adequately reported, published in 22 medical fields. Our results showed that incomplete reporting remains high for several items including trial design, random sequence generation, blinding, and funding source disclosure. The results also showed that publication in a general medicine journal, the explicit journal CONSORT endorsement and larger studies were associated with better reporting. Notably, general medical journals outperformed specialty journals in terms of adherence to CONSORT for abstracts, which suggests that journal editorial standards may influence reporting practices.

Our findings are consistent with those of previous reviews, which have frequently identified suboptimal adherence to the CONSORT for Abstracts. For example, Wang et al [22] reported < 5% compliance in reporting blinding and randomization and very low reporting for the numbers analysed, the results and precision. Verbalov Cindro et al [17] noted very poor reporting of randomization (3%) and blinding (11%).

Consistent with prior work, funding source disclosure was also often lacking [19, 23], a concern given that industry-funded trials are more likely to report favorable outcomes [17, 24]

Compared with earlier review by Ghimire et al. [25], our sample showed higher reporting rates over time for items such as trial registration, objectives, and harms. For example, trial information in the title was reported in 75% of abstracts in our sample versus 59% in Ghimire et al. [25]; the study objective was reported in 96% versus 73%; and harms in 53% versus 43%. We also compared our results with those of Hopewell et al. [26] who included a sample of studies published in 2006 in high impact factor journals, the completeness was higher for most items.

This trend toward improvement has also been observed at the manuscript level: Srinivasan et al. [27] reported an increase in mean CONSORT adherence in full text from 27% in 1966-1990 to 57% in 2010 to 2024.

This study has several limitations. First, our assessment of journal policy relied on author instructions, which may not fully reflect actual reporting practices in published abstracts. Second, our sample focused on high-impact journals, which may not represent reporting practices across all journals, particularly those with lower visibility or fewer editorial resources. Third, certain study designs, such as cluster-randomized or crossover trials, were excluded, which may limit the applicability of our findings to these designs. Fourth, our scoring method combined 'partial' and 'yes' responses for analysis. While this provides a valid, conservative measure of guideline awareness and implementation, it may overestimate the level of complete adherence to the CONSORT for Abstracts checklist. Finally, a formal study protocol was not published. While all the methodological decisions were made a priori and documented internally, the absence of public preregistration is a recognized limitation that reduces the transparency of our analytical process and its safeguards against post hoc decision-making. Nonetheless, this study has its several strengths, including a large and systematic sample covering many specialties and journals, comprehensive inclusion criteria, and rigorous duplicate screening and extraction. This enhances the generalizability of our findings.

Implications and future research directions

Our study supports previous findings that the reporting of abstracts still needs improvement in several key methodological items. The observed difference in reporting completeness highlights the need for strict adherence to the CONSORT for abstracts guidelines. This could be facilitated if more journals required a CONSORT for abstracts checklist for submission.

Future work should focus on developing and rigorously evaluating strategies to improve reporting in both the writing and editorial stages of publication. The use of structured writing aid tools such as COBWEB during manuscript preparation has already been shown to enhance the completeness of reporting [28]. Similarly, implementing tools such as COBPeer [29] within the peer-review process may assist editors and reviewers in identifying missing elements and promoting methodological transparency. More recently, the rapid development of large language models has opened new possibilities for automating the assessment of reporting quality and detecting incomplete items in real time. Although these emerging technologies show promise, their effectiveness, reliability, and feasibility within editorial workflows must be carefully evaluated before widespread adoption. Building on initiatives such as the work of Srinivasan et al. [27], which demonstrated that large language models can audit RCTs and identify reporting gaps, there is growing potential to apply such AI-driven tools to increase compliance with reporting guidelines. Although these emerging technologies show promise, their effectiveness, reliability, and feasibility within editorial workflows must be carefully evaluated before widespread adoption.

Conclusion

Reporting of RCT abstracts has improved for methodological items but remains incomplete for key items, highlighting the need to adopt measures to identify gaps in reporting at all stages prior to publication. A larger sample size, CONSORT endorsement, and type of journal were associated with better reporting of abstracts. Targeted interventions for authors, editors and reviewers are needed to further improve the completeness of abstract reporting.
