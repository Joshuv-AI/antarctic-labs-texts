Smartphone-based point-of-care analyzer for rapid hemoglobin and potassium monitoring in end-stage renal disease and perioperative renal transplantation patients
Authors: Yiling Fang, Shengquan Wu, Ze Wu, Yibin Wang, Qiangqiang Fu, Yun Miao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to develop a smartphone-based analyzer capable of detecting hemoglobin and potassium levels, with clinical validation using a perioperative renal transplant cohort. We implemented azide-methemoglobin and potassium-tetraphenylborate reactions to detect hemoglobin and potassium levels respectively. A smartphone-based system using ambient light sensors and a Phyphox application was used to measure the light intensity changes, with concentrations calculated via calibration curves. 461 hemoglobin measurements and 481 serum potassium test results from 133 patients with end-stage renal disease admitted for pre-transplant evaluation and 51 perioperative renal transplantation patients were analyzed. According to the Bland–Altman analysis, hemoglobin and potassium measurements showing biases of −1.80 g/L (95% limits of agreement: −7.40 to 3.79 g/L) and 0.015 mM (95% limits of agreement: −0.566 to 0.596 mM), respectively. The diagnostic accuracy was acceptable, with overall rates of 86.55% for anemia and 87.73% for dyskalemia. Error grid analysis indicated that 0.40% of hemoglobin values exceeding the ±10.00% limits and 5.81% of potassium values surpassing the ±0.05 mM limits. The analyzer demonstrated remarkable efficiency, achieving median turnaround times of 2 and 29 min for hemoglobin and potassium, respectively. Additionally, we analyzed 221 critical laboratory values from pre-transplant and post-transplant patients. The analysis identified electrolytes and acid-base disorders as the second most frequent pre-transplant and most frequent post-transplant critical alerts and dyskalemia predominated within this category. Similarly, anemia predominated within the pre-transplant and post-transplant hematologic emergencies. In conclusion, the analyzer offers advantages including portability, accuracy, stability and rapidity, and demonstrates significant clinical value for renal transplant recipients.

Introduction

Kidney transplantation is often the treatment of choice for patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [1]. Perioperative challenges remain a primary hurdle in kidney transplantation. On the one hand, candidates for renal transplantation often present with comorbidities related to their primary disease and chronic kidney disease (CKD), including anemia, hyperkalemia, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular diseases, and infections [2–4]. These comorbidities may be associated with an increased risk of postoperative complications, such as delayed graft function (DGF) [5]. On the other hand, the widespread use of expanded criteria donor kidneys, along with the increasing proportion of overweight and elderly candidates, poses new challenges for perioperative management in kidney transplantation [6].

Anemia is one of the most common complications of CKD [7,8], with a prevalence exceeding 50% in patients with ESRD [9]. Although kidney transplantation can partially correct anemia in the long term, recipients remain at a substantially elevated risk of anemia in the immediate postoperative period due to intraoperative blood loss and perioperative complications such as DGF, acute rejection episodes, and infections. Studies show that the incidence of anemia within the first month after kidney transplantation ranges from 77.2% to 88.6% [10–12]. Proactive monitoring of hemoglobin (Hb) levels and timely intervention during the perioperative period play a critical role in mitigating cardiovascular complications, decreasing all-cause mortality rates, and facilitating the recovery of graft function [5,13,14].

Dyskalemia is a common form of electrolyte disturbance in CKD. Given the potential to precipitate life-threatening arrhythmias, pre-transplant hyperkalemia may necessitate surgical delays or cancellations [15]. Kidney transplant recipients are also a high-risk group for hyperkalemia. In addition to the shared risk factors in patients with CKD, pre-transplant potassium levels and the use of calcineurin inhibitor drugs are associated with post-transplant hyperkalemia [16,17]. The risks of hypokalemia in CKD appear to be underestimated. Studies indicated that hypokalemia occurs at a frequency comparable to that of hyperkalemia in patients with CKD [18], and is positively associated with mortality in the dialysis population [19,20]. In summary, potassium abnormalities represent a common threat during the perioperative period of kidney transplantation. Prompt identification and management of dyskalemia are critical to ensuring successful transplant surgery and optimizing postoperative recovery [21].

Currently, standardized detection techniques for Hb and serum potassium are well-established. The reference method for Hb measurement is the cyanmethemoglobin method [22], while clinical laboratory methods for serum potassium detection include flame photometry and ion-selective electrode (ISE) techniques [23]. However, the reliance on large analytical instruments and supporting infrastructure inevitably leads to prolonged analysis times and increased costs, hindering rapid clinical decision-making and timely interventions.

Smartphones integrate multifunctional capabilities, such as high-resolution image capture, optical sensing, and data processing, storage, visualization, and transmission, demonstrating substantial potential for portable rapid diagnostics [24]. In our previous study, we developed a smartphone-based portable Hb analyzer, achieving robust analytical performance [25]. In this study, we further adapted the Hb analysis system to enable rapid detection of Hb and serum potassium levels and focused on patients in the perioperative period of renal transplantation.

Materials and methods

Ethics and informed consent

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University (NFEC-2024-365) and written informed consent was obtained from all research subjects or their guardians. In addition, as voluntary donations by citizens have become the only source of organs for transplantation in China since 2015, it can be guaranteed that none of the organs in this study were procured from executed prisoners and that all organs were procured after informed consent or authorization.

Reagents and consumables

Hemocue HB 201 microcuvettes (Hemocue, Ängelholm, Sweden) were used for point-of-care Hb testing. Rapid detection of serum potassium was based on the Potassium Turbidimetric Assay Kit (Elabscience, Wuhan, China), which includes potassium ion standard solutions and microplate strips. For other reagents and consumables, please see Supplementary Table S1.

Apparatus

Solidworks 2021 (Dassault Systèmes, Paris, France) was used to design the analyzer model, which was exported in a .stl file format and three-dimensionally-printed using polylactic acid. The analyzer consists of a light-emitting diode (LED) light source, lithium battery, light intensity regulator, microwell sample-holding analyzer, power switch, and HUAWEI Mate 40 smartphone (Figure 1A). The total weight of the core components, excluding the smartphone, is 80 g.

For Hb measurement, users can insert the microcuvette into the slot and align it with the LED light source and ambient light sensor, thereby altering the received light intensity (Figure 1B). To detect potassium, a microplate strip is inserted into the holder and pushed into a designated slot to achieve the same effect (Figure 1C). The Phyphox application (RWTH Aachen University) was used for light intensity recording.

The handheld centrifuge for the 1.5-mL Eppendorf tubes consists of a lithium battery, miniature 130 small motor, motor speed controller, and switch with a total mass of 50 g (Figure 1D). The centrifuge achieves a maximum speed of 10,000 rpm, which is sufficient for serum separation applications.

Detection principle and calibration curve constructions

The detection of Hb was based on a modified azide-methemoglobin reaction followed by colorimetric analysis [26]. The detailed procedures were reported in our previous studies [25,27]. Briefly, after adding the sample, the reaction proceeded for 45 s, during which the LED light was turned on and the Phyphox application was activated to measure the incident light intensity. Upon completion of the reaction, the cuvette was inserted into the smartphone analyzer to measure the transmitted light intensity, and the Hb concentration was calculated on the basis of the calibration curve.

Potassium was quantified by turbidimetric analysis using sodium tetraphenylborate to form a white precipitate [28]. Specifically, the serum sample was mixed with a protein-precipitating agent in a 1:9 ratio according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Then, the mixture was centrifuged at 1,100 g for 10 min using a handheld centrifuge. The supernatant was transferred to a microwell and reacted with sodium tetraphenylborate for 5 min. During the reaction, the microwell strips were placed in a sample holder, and the LED light was turned on to acquire the incident light intensity. After the reaction, the sample was aligned with the optical path to measure the transmitted light intensity, and the potassium concentration was determined using a calibration curve.

Calibration curves were respectively constructed using standard solutions diluted in nuclease-free water. For Hb detection, the standards were diluted to 0, 40, 80, 120, 160, and 200 g/L, and a curve was generated using the aforementioned method. Potassium ion standard solutions were prepared at 0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, and 0.8 mM according to the manufacturer’s instructions. These standards were directly reacted with sodium tetraphenylborate, and analyzed to establish a potassium calibration curve.

Assay performance validation

Residual venous blood samples were collected from the Department of Laboratory Medicine at Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University, to evaluate repeatability and robustness of the analyzer. Hb and serum potassium levels were standardly measured using the Sysmex XN-9000 Hematology Analyzer (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan) and Beckman AU5831 Chemistry Analyzer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, USA), respectively.

To assess the repeatability of the rapid testing, six samples with varying concentrations were analyzed eight times using the analyzer, and repeatability was evaluated by calculating the coefficient of variation (CV).

We further investigated the effect of hyperlipidemia and hyperbilirubinemia on colorimetric measurements. Blood samples with triglyceride levels >1.7 mM or total bilirubin levels >17.1 μM were selected and analyzed in triplicate for Hb or potassium levels. The consistency between the rapid testing results and laboratory reference values was systematically compared.

Application of the analyzer in patients during the perioperative period of kidney transplantation

To further evaluate the feasibility of the analyzer for the perioperative management of kidney transplant recipients, we prospectively enrolled patients with ESRD admitted to the Department of Transplantation at Nanfang Hospital from August 2024 to January 2025 and patients who underwent kidney transplantation surgery during this period.

During routine venipuncture procedures throughout hospitalization, we collected one drop of whole blood or 200 μL of ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-anticoagulated blood according to the specific laboratory tests, for rapid Hb and serum potassium testing respectively. To be noted, the EDTA-anticoagulated samples were processed using a handheld centrifuge at 3,000 rpm for 10 min to obtain the serum, so that potassium testing is not fully point-of-care. The hemolyzed specimens were excluded from the analysis. We evaluated the agreement between the smartphone-based analyzer and standard laboratory test results, while assessing the diagnostic performance of the smartphone-based analyzer for dyskalemia and anemia classification. Furthermore, we compared the turnaround times (TATs) (defined as the time from blood collection to result reporting) to evaluate time efficiency.

Anemia classification criteria followed age-specific and sex-specific World Health Organization Guidelines [29], with hyperkalemia and hypokalemia defined as potassium concentrations of >5.0 and <3.5 mM, respectively [15].

Analysis of laboratory tests with critical values in patients before and after kidney transplantation

To investigate the occurrence of critical Hb and serum potassium values in patients with ESRD and renal transplant recipients, we retrospectively analyzed critical value records from the Department of Transplantation at Nanfang Hospital from January 2023 to February 2025. The analysis focused on the frequency of critical values for serum potassium and Hb in patients with ESRD and renal transplant recipients and their proportion among all critical value items. Repeated critical values of the same test item in a single patient during one hospitalization were considered as one episode. Details of the critical value items involved in this study and thresholds established by Nanfang Hospital are presented in Supplementary Table S2.

Statistical analysis

Data were processed and analyzed using SPSS 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, USA), and GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, USA). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. For continuous variables, normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and paired t-tests were used for consistency analysis, while non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median and range, and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were employed to evaluate between-group differences.

Linear regression and the coefficient of determination (R2) were used to evaluate the correlation between the smartphone-based analyzer and laboratory test results. The intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) was used to evaluate the agreement between the two measurement methods. Bland–Altman plots were generated to visualize the bias, 95% limits of agreement (LOA), and clinically acceptable limits between the two measurement methods. The acceptable percentage errors of the two indicators are defined by the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments [30]: Hb level, ±7% and potassium level, ±0.5 mM. The sensitivity and specificity of the smartphone-based rapid testing were calculated for the qualitative diagnosis of anemia severity and dyskalemia, and weighted kappa coefficients were used to assess diagnostic agreement.

To further evaluate the impact of the smartphone-based analyzer results on clinical decision-making, a modified Clarke error grid analysis, as described by Morey et al. [31], was employed to visualize the comparison between rapid Hb measurements and laboratory test results. According to the practice guidelines for transfusion published by the American Society of Anesthesiologists [32], we defined the region of clinical interest as Hb levels between 60 and 100 g/L, with ±10% set as the maximum allowable error margin. Given the absence of an established error grid paradigm for rapid serum potassium testing, we adapted the Morey error grid approach for clinical decision analysis of potassium levels. The region of interest for serum potassium was set at 3.5–5.0 mM, with a clinically acceptable error limit of ±0.50 mM. More details are presented in the Results section.

All tests in this study were performed at a two-sided significance level of 0.05.

Results

Smartphone-based analyzer

In this study, we modified the smartphone-based Hb analyzer to meet the needs of patients during the perioperative period of kidney transplantation, enabling the rapid detection of two items under the same incident light wavelength while retaining the portability of the analyzer. The procedure for serum potassium detection in clinical settings involves the following sequential steps: serum extraction, protein precipitation, key reactions, and turbidimetric measurement (detailed workflow shown in Figure 1E). With the assistance of a handheld centrifuge, this workflow eliminates reliance on large-scale centrifuges or specialized equipment and can be completed using a basic medical workstation.

Calibration curves for Hb and potassium detection were established (Figure 2A and B). Within the detection ranges of 0–200 g/L for Hb and 0–0.8 mM for potassium, both analytes exhibited strong linear relationships with absorbance, achieving R2 values of 0.994 and 0.998, respectively. Notably, the potassium assay required pretreatment with a 9-fold volume of the protein precipitant, so that the detectable range for serum potassium extends to 0–8.0 mM, adequately satisfying clinical requirements.

Assay performance validation

The repeatability analysis demonstrated high stability for rapid Hb and potassium measurements across three concentration levels, with CV values ranging from 0.77% to 4.50% for Hb and from 3.20% to 4.94% for potassium. Although a trend toward higher CV values was observed at lower potassium concentrations, all results remained within the acceptable threshold of <5% (Figure 2C and D).

To investigate potential interference from triglycerides and total bilirubin, we evaluated Hb levels in 10 venous blood samples with triglyceride concentrations of 1.81–4.93 mM and another 10 samples with total bilirubin levels of 27.8–233.6 μM. The Bland–Altman analysis revealed a mean bias of −1.02 g/L compared with laboratory tests, with a 95% LOA ranging from −5.45 to 3.41 g/L. Only one sample (5.00%) fell outside the 95% LOA and exceeded the clinically acceptable limit (Figure 2E), indicating minor interference from elevated triglyceride or bilirubin levels. Similarly, potassium levels were assessed in 10 serum samples with triglyceride concentrations of 1.79–4.99 mM and 10 samples with total bilirubin levels of 29.3–442.5 μM. The two methods showed good agreement with a minimal bias of 0.003 mM, and all samples fell within the LOA range of −0.172–0.178 mM (Figure 2F). Detailed information on triglyceride and bilirubin levels and the corresponding test results for each sample is provided in Supplementary Tables S3–6.

Application of the analyzer in patients during the perioperative period of kidney transplantation

This study enrolled 133 patients with ESRD admitted for pre-transplant evaluation and 51 patients in the perioperative period following kidney transplantation. A total of 461 paired Hb measurements were obtained, comprising 132 pairs from patients with ESRD and 329 pairs from renal transplant recipients. For serum potassium, 481 paired measurements were collected, including 128 pairs from patients with ESRD and 353 pairs from renal transplant recipients. The R2 and ICC values between the laboratory tests and smartphone-based measurements are summarized in Table 1, demonstrating a strong correlation and agreement between the two methods. Bland–Altman analysis revealed that the analyzer systematically underestimated Hb levels with a mean bias of −1.80 g/L and a 95% LOA ranging from −7.40 to 3.79 g/L. Of these, 38 paired measurements (8.24%) fell outside the 95% LOA, and 20 pairs (4.34%) exceeded the clinically acceptable error limits (Figure 3A). For potassium, the smartphone-based analyzer results showed excellent concordance with laboratory results, exhibiting a negligible bias of 0.015 mM and 95% LOA of −0.566–0.596 mM. While 20 paired measurements (4.16%) lay outside the 95% LOA, 37 pairs (7.69%) surpassed the clinically acceptable error limits (Figure 3B).

The error grid analysis (Figure 3E and F) categorizes clinical decision risks into three zones: green (clinical decisions unaffected by measurement errors), yellow (potential for error-induced clinical misjudgments, ideally with samples in this zone <5%), and red (critical clinical errors, where no samples should reside) [31]. Only two Hb data points (0.40%) were within the yellow zone, whereas 28 potassium data points (5.81%) were within the yellow zone, slightly exceeding the 5% clinically acceptable threshold. However, 17/28 (60.71%) of these measurements exhibited concordant normal range results between the laboratory and rapid test results, resulting in minimal impact on clinical decision-making.

Rapid testing for Hb and serum potassium achieved a significantly shorter TAT than laboratory testing. Rapid Hb testing achieved a median TAT of just 2 min, representing a 71-min reduction compared with the median TAT of central laboratory testing. For potassium, the rapid method exhibited a median experimental TAT of 29 min, 90 min faster than the central laboratory median TAT (Table 4).

Epidemiology of anemia and dyskalemia in patients before and after kidney transplantation

As shown in Supplementary Table S7, the prevalence of anemia in our prospective ESRD cohort reached 72.73% (96/132), predominantly comprising mild-to-moderate cases, although severe anemia (Hb <80 g/L) was also observed. Among the 51 renal transplant recipients, all patients demonstrated at least one episode of abnormal Hb levels during serial testing, including 15.69% (8/51) with isolated mild anemia, 56.86% (29/51) with at least one moderate anemia episode, and 27.45% (14/51) with severe anemia. Supplementary Table S8 presents the distinct potassium imbalance patterns; hyperkalemia was dominant in patients with ESRD (23.43%, 30/128), whereas hypokalemia was more common post-transplantation. Of the 51 renal transplant recipients, 49.02% (25/51) developed at least one hypokalemic episode during hospitalization and 33.33% (17/51) developed hyperkalemia. Notably, 15.69% (8/51) of the patients exhibited potassium fluctuations, with the majority (6/8, 75.00%) transitioning from hyperkalemia to hypokalemia.

To further characterize the critical value distributions, we retrospectively analyzed 221 critical alerts stratified into preoperative (n = 125) and postoperative (n = 96) groups (Table 5). Blood electrolytes and acid-base disorders constituted the most frequent critical alert in renal transplant recipients and ranked second among patients with ESRD after elevated serum creatinine. Potassium abnormalities dominated this category, accounting for 63.33% of preoperative and 50.00% of postoperative electrolyte-related alerts (Figures 4A and B). Hematologic critical values were less common overall, ranking fourth preoperatively and third postoperatively. However, anemia-driven alerts represented most hematologic critical values, comprising 71.43 and 53.85% of preoperative and postoperative cases, respectively (Figures 4C and D).

Discussion

Point-of-care testing (POCT) has gained widespread adoption in critical care settings, particularly for the rapid assessment of Hb levels, electrolytes, and acid-base balance using blood gas analyzers [33]. Compared with blood gas analyzers, our smartphone-based analyzer has a distinct value proposition. For serum potassium, multiple studies demonstrated that blood gas analyzers exhibit substantial bias, with a wide LOA often exceeding the clinically acceptable range of ±0.50 mM, due to their use of heparinized whole blood and susceptibility to pre-analytical variables such as sample collection [33–37]. In contrast, our analyzer demonstrated minimal bias and a narrow LOA (Table 6), which is particularly crucial for managing potassium levels given its narrow therapeutic window. In terms of testing efficiency and the analyte menu, blood gas analyzers undoubtedly achieve true point-of-care testing with a comprehensive panel covering hemoglobin, electrolytes, blood gases, and routine metabolic parameters. However, our analyzer involves multiple manual steps, resulting in a longer turnaround time, and is dedicated to the simultaneous detection of only Hb and serum potassium. This distinction defines the divergent application scenarios: blood gas analyzers are primarily used in clinical settings demanding high timeliness, such as operating rooms, emergency departments, and intensive care units, although high cost limits their widespread adoption in routine hospital wards. Our hemoglobin-potassium analyzer, targeting patients with CKD and kidney transplant recipients, has portable components and a low cost. Thus, it holds promise for deployment in general wards, outpatient clinics, and even primary healthcare institutions, thereby potentially achieving broad population coverage.

Globally, CKD affects >10% of the population, with a particularly high disease burden in Latin America, the Middle East, and Southeast Asia [38]. In these regions with a considerable CKD prevalence and relatively limited healthcare resources, widespread implementation of rapid and low-cost testing for Hb and serum potassium could substantially reduce mortality, while reducing healthcare costs through timely interventions. Our findings are consistent with this clinical imperative. In renal transplant recipients, anemia was universal perioperatively and likely attributable to intraoperative blood loss and transplant-related factors such as DGF. Potassium imbalances constitute another major category of critical parameters during the perioperative period of kidney transplantation. Postoperatively, patients exhibited particularly complex potassium fluctuations due to multiple factors including high-dose immunosuppressants, DGF, polyuria during early graft recovery, and intravenous fluid management. These observations underscore the vital role of POCT in monitoring patients with CKD and renal transplant patients.

Although numerous Hb-POCT analyzers exist, including noninvasive technologies [39], our smartphone-based analyzer demonstrated superior sensitivity, specificity, and lower bias in a prior validation (see Ref. [25], Table 1). The present study further corroborates these findings and confirms its reliability for transfusion decision-making in patients during the perioperative period of kidney transplantation. Conversely, smartphone-based potassium POCT remain underdeveloped with limited real-world data [27,28,40]. Although Murray et al. [41] pioneered home potassium monitoring in renal transplant recipients using the Abbott i-STAT system, their study revealed poor agreement between fingertip blood-based measurements and standard laboratory tests, highlighting the ongoing challenges in potassium POCT development. In comparison, our smartphone-based rapid potassium detection method was successful. The results of this analyzer exhibit strong agreement with laboratory measurements and demonstrate clinically acceptable diagnostic accuracy for dyskalemia and the associated decision-making risks.

Beyond its advantages in detection performance, our analyzer also demonstrates considerable cost-effectiveness. As shown in Supplementary Table S1, the total manufacturing cost for the analyzer along with the handheld centrifuge is $6, and the cost per test is $1. In comparison, internationally mainstream portable point-of-care devices such as Abbott i-STAT and Siemens Epoc are considerably more expensive, with market prices reaching thousands of dollars per instrument and single-test costs amounting to tens of dollars. Furthermore, our analyzer shows greater potential for large-scale dissemination in resource-limited settings. With just one 3D printer and corresponding raw materials, it enables personalized, low-cost, and batch production adaptable to various smartphone dimensions. Moreover, the analyzer’s simple structure, with potential points of failure limited to components like the LED light source and battery, results in minimal regular maintenance costs. If component damage occurs during use, replacing the unit may be more economical than making specialized repairs. In summary, our analyzer offers substantial economic advantages.

It must be acknowledged that our analyzer has some limitations. First and foremost, although our system eliminates the need for large laboratory instruments, the potassium detection protocol remains constrained by its reliance on trained personnel, a dedicated operational space, and multiple manual steps. This not only increases the total turnaround time but also constitutes a primary source of operational variability, imposing a substantial demand for operator training and proficiency. Consequently, these factors limit the analyzer’s generalizability across diverse clinical settings. For instance, the current device is unsuitable for environments demanding extreme rapidity, such as the operating room or emergency department, and it cannot be used for home-based monitoring owing to these procedural requirements. Secondly, compared to Hb measurement, the serum potassium assay demonstrated greater variability and reduced sensitivity, particularly compromising its diagnostic performance for hypokalemia. The potential for misclassification poses a risk of delayed diagnostic and potassium supplementation decisions, which could potentially lead to life-threatening arrhythmias in patients. Therefore, clinical caution is required when interpreting borderline hypokalemia results. It is crucial to emphasize that our analyzer should be regarded primarily as an efficient screening and trend-monitoring tool. Definitive diagnosis of suspicious samples still depends on confirmation by the laboratory gold standard method. Finally, our analyzer remains at a preliminary stage of technical validation, and several practical challenges inherent to clinical translation have not been fully addressed. These include the susceptibility of the device to environmental factors such as temperature and ambient lighting, consistency of results across different smartphone models, standardization of operational procedures, and requisite regulatory approval processes for medical devices.

Addressing the current limitations of the device requires concurrent advancements in both core technological optimization and the standardization of operational protocols. For technical optimization, we propose the following future research directions. 1) Develop a microfluidic blood filtration element capable of precise potassium ion recovery to enable automated serum extraction; 2) design an integrated microfluidic-based automated serum reaction platform incorporating a precipitation zone (pre-loaded with protein precipitant), filtration membrane, and detection chamber (containing lyophilized sodium tetraphenylborate) to minimize operational variability and enhance testing efficiency; and 3) create a portable potassium detection system using the higher sensitivity of ISE methodology that requires minimal serum volume for accurate potassium level assessment while improving diagnostic performance for hypokalemia. Regarding operational workflow systems, we aim to establish a standardized POCT team to refine all aspects of the POCT management framework. This includes developing standardized operating procedures, maintaining operator certification records, implementing regular training and competency assessments, and performing periodic device calibration and quality control measures. With further integration of automated microfluidics and standardized workflows, followed by subsequent regulatory compliance processes, this system could be adapted for widespread perioperative monitoring in transplant centers and resource-limited settings.
