Exertional inspiratory capacity and operating lung volumes in health: a population-based study
Authors: Beate Stubbe, Paulo T. Müller, Anne Obst, Sabine Kaczmarek, Robina Mick, J. Alberto Neder, Ralf Ewert
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
RationaleInspiratory capacity (IC) should increase during exercise to allow unrestrained tidal volume expansion, mitigating uncomfortable respiratory sensations. However, preliminary evidence suggests that this is not universally seen in healthy individuals. Whether this negatively impacts on inspiratory reserves and dyspnoea remains unclear. To gain novel insights on the regulation of operating lung volumes in community-dwelling individuals, we determined the association of a lack of increase in IC during incremental cardiopulmonary exercise testing (dynamic inspiratory capacity (ICdyn)) with demographic, anthropometric and respiratory functional data in a large population-based study.MethodsWe reviewed general characteristics, pulmonary function and cycle ergometer data from 1065 healthy individuals (51.5% women, aged 20–86 years) with normal lung function.Measurements and main resultsICdyn remained unchanged or decreased from rest to peak exercise (Δ (peak–rest) ICdyn ≤0 L) in 245/1065 individuals (23.0%). Tidal volume increased to a lower extent after the ventilatory threshold compared to earlier exercise in these subjects; thus, peak end-inspiratory lung volume (EILV) and EILV/total lung capacity were similar to subjects showing ICdyn >0 L despite higher EILVs. Albeit statistically significant, between-group differences in peak dyspnoea scores were modest (p=0.049). Multivariable regression revealed that age (particularly in women), higher body mass index and higher resting airway resistance were independently associated with ΔICdyn ≤0 L (p<0.001).ConclusionsElderly women and overweight/obese individuals may fail to increase ICdyn during incremental exercise due to dynamic hyperinflation. Careful regulation of tidal volume, however, avoids critically high inspiratory constraints and limiting exertional dyspnoea.

Introduction

Activity-related breathlessness is a common complaint of apparently healthy individuals in the community [1, 2]. The underlying reasons beyond deconditioning remain poorly understood in the absence of detectable abnormalities in resting cardiopulmonary investigations [3].

In this context, it is well recognised that uncomfortable respiratory sensations brought on by exercise are minimised by a providential decrease in the end-expiratory lung volume (EELV), giving “extra room” for tidal volume (VT) expansion in the setting of a relatively stable total lung capacity (TLC) [4]. Thus, inspiratory capacity measured dynamically in free-breathing conditions (ICdyn) typically increases during incremental cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) in healthy individuals, i.e., Δ (peak–rest) ICdyn >0 [4, 5]. However, the prevailing view on what constitutes normalcy, namely a monotonous increase in ICdyn from rest to peak exercise [6], is grounded in relatively small physiological studies of carefully selected groups showing narrow ranges of age and body dimensions [7–9]. Interestingly, there is preliminary evidence that a positive change in ICdyn from rest to peak exercise is not universally seen in apparently healthy individuals [10, 11]. For instance, lower body size and different patterns of inspiratory muscle recruitment may hinder women's ability to increase ICdyn to the same extent as men [12]. Moreover, elderly women and obese individuals may show reduced expiratory flow reserves [13, 14], putting them at increased risk of developing expiratory flow limitation and, consequently, dynamic hyperinflation [14]. These theoretical constructs, however, contrast with the recent findings of Molgat-Seon et al. [15], who did not find sex-based differences in the frequency and predictors of expiratory flow limitation during peak exercise. To better understand the effects of aging and obesity on the physiological underpinnings of exertional dyspnoea in apparently healthy men and women, we reasoned that the measurement of ICdyn and operating lung volumes in a large cohort from the community would be particularly informative.

The aim of this study, therefore, was to investigate the behaviour of ICdyn and the derived operating lung volumes during incremental CPET in the population-based Study of Health in Pomerania (SHIP). We were specifically interested in uncovering the resting characteristics associated with a lack of increase in exercise ICdyn (ΔICdyn ≤0) and its physiological (inspiratory constraints) and sensory (exertional dyspnoea) consequences.

Methods

Study design and participants

The SHIP project is a population-based epidemiological assignment run by the Community Medicine Research Network of the University Medicine Greifswald, collecting data on multiple health outcomes from the general population of Pomerania, Northeast Germany. A detailed description has been provided elsewhere [16]. A total of 6753 individuals in two independent SHIP sub-cohorts (SHIP-2 (n=2333) and SHIP-TREND 0 (n=4420)) (figure E1 and table E1) were considered for potential inclusion in the current study. We selected those who had undergone basic and advanced pulmonary function tests and an incremental CPET with evidence of maximal effort as indicated by peak respiratory exchange ratio >1.05 and peak heart rate >90% of age-predicted. Individual tests were also reviewed to ensure adequate ICdyn performance, i.e., a minimum of three flow–volume loops of acceptable quality and between-breath EELV variability <0.2 L, immediately preceding the ICdyn manoeuvre (figures E2 and E3). After applying exclusion criteria based on clinical data and abnormal pulmonary function test results (online data supplement), the final study population comprised 1065 healthy individuals (51.5% women) (figure E1). The study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki as reflected by the approval of the Ethics Committee of the University of Greifswald (SHIP/2015/17/D). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Pulmonary function tests

Spirometry and measurement of “static” lung volumes (including TLC), total and specific airway resistance (MasterScreen Body/Diff®, VIASYS Healthcare®, JAEGER Höchberg, Germany) and maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP) were performed according to current American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society recommendations [17, 18], with the results being compared with normative values established previously in SHIP [19, 20]. A snorkel with a nose clip was used was used during the procedure.

Incremental CPET

A symptom-limited CPET employing a calibrated electromagnetically braked cycle ergometer with seat height adjustment (Ergoselect 100, Ergoline, Bitz, Germany) was performed according to a protocol modified from Jones et al. [21]. The protocol started with 3 min resting, 1 min unloaded cycling and increasing workload in a stepwise manner by 16 watts·min−1, aiming for limiting symptoms in 8–12 min. The CPET metabolic and ventilatory measurements were averaged as 10 s bins (MasterScreen™ CPX, CareFusion®, Höchberg, Germany). Based on the visual inspection of minute ventilation (V′E)/O2 uptake (V′O2) and V′E/CO2 output (V′CO2) and time-aligned end-tidal partial pressure for O2 and CO2 (PETO2 and PETCO2, respectively) during exercise, a certified technician identified the emergence of the “first” ventilatory threshold (VT1) [22]. At this point, ICdyn was recorded (in addition to rest and peak exercise). Specifically, the resting ICdyn was measured with the subject seated on the cycle ergometer after ventilation had stabilised over the resting time preceding the warm-up period. Details regarding the assessment of dyspnoea (0–10 category-ratio Borg scale) [23] and the performance of ICdyn manoeuvres are described in the online data supplement. Based on ICdyn, VT and TLC, the operating lung volumes were obtained as follows: EELV=TLC−ICdyn and EILV=EELV+VT) [6]. We separated the population into two groups, as follows: those showing the expected increase in ICdyn with exercise (ΔICdyn >0 L) and those showing unchanged or reduced ICdyn (ΔICdyn ≤0 L). Limits of normality and reference values for key CPET variables were those presented in a previous SHIP publication [24].

Statistics

Statistical analysis was performed using the SAS 9.4 package (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Given that the Shapiro–Wilks test revealed a non-Gaussian distribution for most variables, data are summarised as median (interquartile range) unless otherwise specified. Between-group comparisons were performed with nonpaired Wilcoxon or Chi2 testing as appropriate. To adjust for multiple testing, the false discovery rate according to Benjamini and Hochberg [25] was used. In order to show whether there were differences between individuals with ΔICdyn ≤0 L and ΔICdyn >0 L during the measurement of the inter-breath curves for the parameters EELV and EILV, we used the analysis of variance with repeated measures. Associations of demographic, anthropometric and resting and exercise functional parameters with ΔICdyn ≤0 L were established by univariate and multivariable logistic regression. p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

ICdyn at rest and during exercise

ΔICdyn ≤0 L was found in 245/1065 individuals (23.0%). These individuals showed higher ICdyn at rest (p<0.05) but similar ICdyn at the ventilatory threshold compared with those who had ΔICdyn >0 L. Whereas ICdyn increased by ∼16% between rest and the ventilatory threshold in the latter group, it slightly decreased in the former group (figures 1a and 2). From the ventilatory threshold, ICdyn slightly increased to peak exercise in the group with ΔICdyn >0 L, whereas it decreased by ∼6% in the other group (p<0.05 between groups at peak exercise) (figures 1a and 2).

Operating lung volumes during exercise

Given similar TLC, the higher ICdyn at rest in subjects showing ΔICdyn ≤0 L was secondary to lower EELV compared to their counterparts. Since VT was similar between groups, EILV was also lower in the former group (p<0.05) (figure 2). Whereas subjects with ΔICdyn >0 L showed the expected decrease in EELV with exercise progression, those showing ΔICdyn ≤0 L did increase EELV, i.e., they developed dynamic hyperinflation. Interestingly, VT and EILV increased to a small extent from the VT1 to peak exercise compared to milder exercise in subjects showing ΔICdyn ≤0 L; thus, peak EILV did not differ between the groups (p>0.05) despite higher EELV in the latter group (figure 2).

Resting parameters associated with unchanged or decreased ICdyn during exercise

In the subgroup of elderly participants (aged≥70 years), women showed a higher prevalence of ΔICdyn ≤0 L than men (40.9% versus 27.8%, respectively; p<0.05) (figure 1b). Other age groups showed no significant difference in the prevalence of ΔICdyn ≤0 L between sexes (figure 1b). Of note, those showing ΔICdyn ≤0 L were mostly older and heavier with a higher prevalence of obesity than those with ΔICdyn >0 L (figure 1b and table 1). Figure 3 depicts the frequency of ΔICdyn. Moreover, women aged≥70 years and women with severe obesity (body mass index (BMI) ≥40 kg·m−2) tended to have lower ΔICdyn levels than their male counterparts (figures 4, 5a and 5b). In the group with BMI ≥40 kg·m−2, women also had higher VT/ICdyn than men (figure 5c); this difference was not seen in any of the age groups (figure 5d). The MIP was consistently and significantly lower in women than in men across BMI and age ranges (p<0.05 for all) (figures 5e and 5f). There were no differences in smoking history between those with ΔICdyn ≤0 L and those with ΔICdyn >0 L (p=0.146) (table 1).

Of note, peak Borg dyspnoea scores in absolute values and corrected for V′E were significantly higher in the ΔICdyn ≤0 L group, though the individual scores distribution was similar between groups (table 3).

Discussion

This is the first population-based study to prospectively measure ICdyn and operating lung volumes at selected time-points (rest, ventilatory threshold and peak) during incremental cycle ergometer CPET in community-dwelling men and women with a wide age range. We identified a substantial number of individuals (∼ a quarter of our sample) in whom ICdyn did not follow the expected response profile of an increase from rest to peak exercise (ΔICdyn >0 L), being associated with increasing EELV, i.e., dynamic hyperinflation. Hyperinflators were elderly (particularly women aged 70 years and older) and heavier than those showing ΔICdyn >0 L, presenting with higher resting airway resistance. Interestingly, subjects from the former group did not increase their VT after the ventilatory threshold to the same extent than their counterparts, resulting in largely similar EILV. As a corollary, the expected negative sensory consequences of hyperinflation (exertional dyspnoea) were minimised despite higher operating lung volumes.

Methodological considerations

Most of the current understanding on the role for a low ICdyn in causing exertional dyspnoea came from relatively small mechanistic studies involving patients with obstructive and/or restrictive lung diseases [26–28]. We have addressed this issue using data from SHIP, a population-based project conducted in Northeast Germany [16]. To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has prospectively measured ICdyn in such a large number of men and women assessed in primary care. Thus, this sample is likely to be highly representative of the healthy population which might be referred to incremental CPET. Given the wide range of BMI within age groups in each sex, the CPET database of SHIP is particularly well-suited to explore the underpinnings and consequences of inter-individual variations in exercise ICdyn.

Another original feature of this study is the measurement of ICdyn at the ventilatory threshold. Thus, we could, for the first time, follow the trajectory of ICdyn in healthy individuals under the modulating influence of lower (rest to ventilatory threshold) versus higher (ventilatory threshold to peak) ventilatory demands as the exercise progressed. Since the likelihood of reaching critically high inspiratory constraints is a direct function of the prevailing ventilation [5], measurement of ICdyn at the ventilatory threshold as well as peak exercise meant we could better explore the dynamic interaction between metabolically driven events at the muscle level and their respiratory consequences.

Interactions between demographic and physiological features to determine ICdyn

The longitudinal evolution of ICdyn as exercise intensified was particularly informative (figures 1a and 2). Median ICdyn increased from rest to the ventilatory threshold in those showing ΔICdyn >0 L, remaining relatively stable thereafter despite a substantial increase in ventilatory demands. Increased airway conductance (i.e., decreased sRtot) in the setting of decreased RV and RV/TLC was likely instrumental to allowing faster expiratory flows and a downward shift in the EELV during exercise. Those showing ΔICdyn ≤0 L faced the opposite scenario (table 3). Jointly, these factors may have conspired to decrease ICdyn and increase EELV as ventilation accelerated after the ventilatory threshold (figure 2).

Contrary to our expectations, biological sex did not systematically influence the likelihood of ΔICdyn ≤0 L. Smaller airway dimensions relative to lung size in women compared with men may predispose to expiratory flow limitation at high levels of ventilation [15, 29], potentially leading to dynamic hyperinflation and decreasing ICdyn. Such an effect, however, is more likely under high levels of ventilation in women fitter than the average nontrained individual from the community [29]. Thus, a clear signal for sexual dimorphism was found only in the elderly group (≥70 years) who were particularly prone to showing ΔICdyn ≤0 L. As previously outlined by Wilkie et al. [30], elderly women are at higher risk of showing expiratory flow limitation than young women and they may not be able to consistently reach TLC under dynamic conditions. Our results are also consistent with those recently reported by Hijleh et al. [31] who found greater decrements in inspiratory reserves (i.e., higher mechanical constraints) in elderly women. It was noteworthy that subjects with higher resting ICdyn showed greater decrements in this variable. Considering their lower EELV and higher BMI, this might reflect the deleterious effects of overweight/obesity on the predisposition to expiratory flow limitation at lower operating lung volumes [32, 33]. Additionally, a specific breathing pattern disorder cannot be fully excluded, though no subjects fulfil the extant criteria for dysfunctional breathing on CPET [34, 35].

Aging is associated with loss of elastic recoil of the lungs, which also predisposes them to expiratory flow limitation [36–38]. Similar to the case in women, however, mechanical constraints secondary to expiratory flow limitation have been described only in fit elderly individuals at near-maximum exercise [14]. Although overt expiratory/abdominal muscle fatigue is unlikely, this does not rule out some preventive adaptations in light of the diminished flow-generating capacity in both groups.

Women tend to recruit the accessory inspiratory muscles during exercise to a greater extent than men [39]. We speculate that some degree of de-recruitment of these, as ventilation increases, would lessen the descending neural drive. Despite our extra care in obtaining reliable ICdyn, it should be acknowledged that female and elderly individuals typically show higher breathing frequency at iso-ventilation (and similar duty cycle) than male and younger individuals [10], which may decrease their ability to perform maximal inspiration during a shorter inspiratory time. Finally, whatever the neuromechanical mechanisms by which humans tightly couple demand (VT) to capacity (ICdyn), this task is conceivably more challenging in those with lower capacity (i.e., women). Thus, it is conceivable that any minor disturbances in “fine-tuning” tidal expansion relative to ICdyn are more likely to cause a high VT/ICdyn in women than men.

Physiological and sensory consequences of a lack of increase in ICdyn during exercise

Lower room for tidal expansion (higher VT/ICdyn) was the natural consequence of progressively higher VT colliding with unchanging or decreasing ICdyn in the subset of hyperinflators [4, 5]. Dynamically increasing the operating lung volumes, however, may have allowed obese and elderly subjects to use greater expiratory flows at high lung volumes [40], allowing greater ventilation in the setting of resting expiratory flow limitation [41]. The potential drawback of ventilating at higher operating lung volumes, the trend to develop greater inspiratory constraints [42], was largely offset by careful regulation of VT. In fact, if VT after the ventilatory threshold had increased to a similar extent compared to mild exercise in these subjects, EILV would systematically reach TLC and intolerable dyspnoea in the setting of an increasing EELV (figure 2). Given the relatively modest between-group differences in end-exercise dyspnoea, it seems unlikely that it had a decisive mechanistic role in explaining the lower maximal exercise capacity shown in those with ΔICdyn ≤0 L (table 3). As a whole, these results illustrate the remarkable ability of the ventilatory controller to avoid an excessively high EILV/TLC even when the “floor” for tidal expansion (EELV) dynamically increases in response to high ventilatory demands – as we, and others, repeatedly showed in disease states [43, 44].

Study strengths and limitations

In the SHIP study, only strictly well-performed IC measurements, according to broadly accepted guidelines, were eligible for the final analysis. The inter-individual coefficient of variation for IC during exercise is 12–20% and has been reported to be higher at end of exercise [6]. However, our strict eligibility criteria for ICdyn measurements should further enhance this estimate. We cannot exclude that some women and older individuals developed dynamic inspiratory muscle weakness, contributing to a lower ICdyn at higher levels of ventilation [14], despite our regression model ruling out an association of MIP with ΔICdyn ≤0 L. Our data are applicable only to cycle ergometry, though there is some preliminary evidence that, at least in obese patients with COPD, the operating lung volumes behave similarly at iso-ventilation on a treadmill [6]. From a practical perspective, our results indicate that more research is required to establish a reliable cut-off to indicate a pathological decrease in ICdyn. As depicted in figure 2, there is a sizeable fraction (how much %) of healthy subjects showing ΔICdyn as low as −0.5 L.

Conclusions

Clinical interpretation of changes in ICdyn during incremental CPET should consider that dynamic hyperinflation may occur in elderly (particularly women) and obese subjects. However, the negative sensory consequences (exertional dyspnoea) are minimised by careful regulation of VT, causing similar EILVs at peak exercise. These results emphasise the key importance of inspiratory reserve regulation to minimise uncomfortable respiratory sensations in healthy individuals facing the challenges brought by higher operating lung volumes.
