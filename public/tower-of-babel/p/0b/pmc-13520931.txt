Seizures and blindness onset zone in 7 horses undergoing radiographic myelography in lateral recumbency under inhalation anesthesia
Authors: Monica Aleman, Krista Estell, Mathieu Spriet, Alessia Cenani, K Gary Magdesian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSeizures and blindness are potential complications associated with myelography.Hypothesis/ObjectivesDescribe, classify, and determine onset zone of seizures and blindness in horses undergoing radiographic myelography in lateral recumbency.AnimalsSeven young adult horses.MethodsRetrospective study. Horses with seizures or blindness associated with myelography were included.ResultsThree horses with general proprioceptive ataxia and 4 neurologically normal horses (Warmblood = 4, Thoroughbred = 3), ages 9 (range, 3-16) years old. Iohexol (300 mg I/mL) from 50 to 60 mL (0.09-0.1 mL/kg) were administered intrathecally. Six horses had focal facial motor seizures contralateral to the side of recumbency with expansion to generalized seizures in one horse. The seizure onset zone was determined to be the right parietal cortex based on electroencephalography. Absent menace response of the left eye with bilateral normal dazzle and pupillary light reflexes were supportive of cortical blindness (right occipital cortex) in 5 horses. Seizures were managed using short- (diazepam, 3 horses; midazolam, 2; both, 1) and long-term (phenobarbital = 2/6) antiseizure medications with clinical resolution within 2-8 h post-myelogram. Blindness resolved within 8-48 h post-myelogram.Conclusions and clinical importanceContralateral focal facial motor seizures and blindness with an onset zone in the parietal and occipital cerebral cortex, respectively, ipsilateral to the side of recumbency are possible adverse effects with characteristic features in horses undergoing radiographic myelography in lateral recumbency. These signs are transient with full resolution of seizures within hours and up to 2 days for the blindness.Keywords: contrast, epileptic, iohexol, paroxysms

Introduction

Cervical myelography is widely used in equine referral hospitals for the investigation of spinal cord disease localized within the cervical and first thoracic spinal cord segments with cervical vertebral compressive myelopathy (CVCM) as a differential diagnosis.1–3 Both radiographic and computed tomography (CT) myelography studies investigating alterations of the contrast column dimensions, dural diameter, and shape of the spinal cord in support of compressive myelopathies continue to expand.4–11 The first reports of cervical myelography in the horses date to the 1970s.12,13 Since then, different contrast materials, concentrations, and dosages have been used for myelography in horses, including metrizamide (166, 180 mg I/mL),12–16 iopamidol (300, 370 mg I/mL),17 and iohexol (180, 240, 300, 350 mg I/mL).14,18–20

Complications associated with anesthesia, myelographic procedure, and contrast material have been reported in horses.11,14,21,22 The 3 largest studies in equids reporting complications associated with radiographic cervical myelography performed in lateral recumbency under general anesthesia consisted of 133, 194, and 278 cases using metrizamide (180 mg I/mL) and iohexol (300 and 240 mg I/mL), respectively.11,21,22 Adverse reactions associated with increased volume of contrast and longer anesthesia time were reported in 34% of equids in a multi-institutional study.22 Neurologic complications during and after the procedure ranging from mild to severe and temporary to fatal have been described in humans and dogs.23,24 Certain contrast agents appear to be more neurotoxic than others, as in the case of metrizamide.23 Because of metrizamide’s high risk of severe neurologic complications and death, this agent has not been used in humans since the 1980s.23 Similarly, less adverse effects were described in horses when using iohexol (180 mg I/mL) compared with metrizamide (180 mg I/mL).14 Since then, iohexol has become the most common contrast agent used in equine practice.22

Reported neurologic complications post-myelogram in equids include obtundation (0.4%-24%), worsening of ataxia (9%-14%), seizures (0.8%-11%), neck pain or stiffness (1%), meningitis (0.8%), blindness (0.7%), altered behavior (0.4%), head shaking (0.4%), and hyperesthesia (0.4%).21,22 Fever or hyperthermia has been recorded in 9%-18% of cases.11,21,22 Reports of seizures post-myelogram from earlier studies in equids varied from 2% to 67% with metrizamide,14,21 and 0.8% to 25% with iohexol.6,14,22 Despite these reports, the onset zone, spread, and type of seizures have not been documented in horses. Therefore, our aim was to investigate the seizure onset zone (SOZ), defined as the region of the cerebral cortex in which seizures originate, and semiology in horses undergoing radiographic myelography in lateral recumbency under general anesthesia. Based on neurologic examination, our hypothesis was that the SOZ would be in the ipsilateral side of recumbency with clinical manifestations in the contralateral side. Blindness has been seldom reported, but it might be missed if only involving one eye or if dysfunction is partial or intermittent. We also further characterized blindness when present in our study.

Materials and methods

Retrospective study.

Animals and inclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria consisted of horses with seizures, blindness, or a combination of both, associated with radiographic myelography under inhalation anesthesia at our institution. A search and review of the electronic medical records database using a combination of keywords: myelogram-seizures, myelogram-blindness, iohexol-seizures, and iohexol-blindness identified 7 horses from 2005 to 2025. These included 3 client-owned clinical horses with spinal cord disease and 4 research healthy neurologically normal horses from a previous published study with different objectives (IACUC #18925).6 Electroencephalography (EEG) was only performed in the clinical cases as part of the diagnostic evaluation and standard of care. Horses undergoing CT myelography were not included.

Radiographic myelogram

Full description of anesthesia premedication, general inhalation anesthesia, myelography, and anesthesia recovery is provided in Supplementary material.25,26 All 7 horses were placed in right lateral recumbency for atlanto-occipital (AO) myelography (injection, neutral, flexed, extended views) and intrathecal iohexol (300 mg I/mL) was used. Immediately after contrast administration, the horse’s head was elevated for 5-10 min for contrast distribution.

Neurologic examination post-myelogram

Behavior, mentation, and cranial nerves were evaluated in the recovery stall and rechecked when the horse was returned to the housing stall. Gait evaluation was made at the time the horses were walked back from the recovery stall, and the next day after recovery from anesthesia (18-24 h).

Seizures and blindness

Seizures were classified based on clinical presentation and further defined by EEG.27 Seizures were classified as: (1) focal (repeated blinking; nystagmus; chewing; twitching of ears, eyes, nose, mouth, face, and neck), (2) focal with generalization, and (3) generalized if the entire body was involved. Other manifestations of non-motor seizures included blindness, hyperesthesia, and autonomic dysfunction (eg, salivation, urination). Blindness was described as unilateral vs bilateral, ipsilateral vs contralateral, central vs peripheral, partial, and intermittent, transient, or permanent.

Electroencephalogram

Electroencephalographic (EEG) and electrocardiographic (ECG) studies were performed once while the horse was standing at a time considered safe by the clinician. Intravenous sedation (xylazine hydrochloride 0.3 mg/kg) was used for placing electrodes.28 Paroxysms in support of epileptic discharges (ED) consisted of spikes (duration < 70 ms), sharp waves (duration 70-200 ms), spike and wave discharges, or multiple spike complexes. These were classified as focal (originating from a single localized focus), multifocal (involving > 3 independent foci), or generalized (involving all areas simultaneously). Other paroxysmal discharges (PD) different from ED were also recorded.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics included median and range for non-parametric data.

Results

Horses

Six horses developed seizures (3 cases, 3 normal horses), 4 of which were also blind (2 cases, 2 normal), and 1 neurologically normal horse was blind with no observed seizures (Table S1). Breeds included 4 Warmbloods (WB; 2 mares, 2 geldings) and 3 Thoroughbreds (TB; 1 mare, 1 stallion, 1 gelding) with a median age of 9 (range, 3-16) years old, and a weight of 535 (range, 500-590) kg. Clinical cases (TB = 1, WB = 2) had general proprioceptive ataxia grades ranging from 2 to 3+ of 5. No other neurologic abnormalities were detected. The neuroanatomical localization was C1-T2 myelopathy in WB breeds as manifested by spinal ataxia, tetraparesis, and proprioceptive deficits with a mix of lower motor neuron (LMN; hypometria, LMN paresis, weakness) signs in the thoracic limbs and upper motor neuron (UMN; hypermetria, UMN paresis) signs of the pelvic limbs. In the 1 TB, ataxia, UMN tetraparesis, proprioceptive deficits, and hypermetria of all limbs localized the disease to the C1-C5 spinal cord segments.

Radiographic myelography

Myelography was uneventful. The volume of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) removed before intrathecal administration of contrast ranged from 50 to 60 mL (median, 50 mL) corresponding to 0.08-0.1 mL/kg. Volume of contrast administered ranged from 50 to 60 mL (median, 60 mL) which corresponded to a dosage from 0.09 to 0.1 mL/kg. In all horses, the time of infusion of the contrast lasted approximately 3-5 min. There was no myelographic evidence of a compressive lesion. Radiographs of the cranium obtained in 2 horses showed contrast material highlighting the sulci of the brain (Figure 1). Horses were under general inhalational anesthesia for 60-90 min (median, 75 min).

Neurologic examination post-myelogram

Two of six horses displayed seizure activity noted as horizontal nystagmus with changing direction, rigid pulled back ears, and facial twitching while recumbent in the recovery stall. Six horses (including these 2) displayed unilateral (left-sided, contralateral to the side of recumbency) focal seizures consisting of facial twitching (eg, ears, nose, mouth), repeated chewing and protrusion of the tongue, and blinking repeatedly within 30 min to 4 h after standing from anesthesia. Two of these horses progressed to whole body twitching (ipsilateral to focal seizures) and mild lateral flexion of the body contralateral to the side of twitching, with 1 horse progressing to generalized tonic-clonic seizures.

While recumbent and recovering from anesthesia, horizontal nystagmus was recorded in 3 horses and initially interpreted as a sign often seen early during the anesthesia recovery phase. One of these three horses had relaxed ears and no facial twitching as expected during recovery, whereas the other 2 horses had rigid ears pulled back with fast twitching movement while having nystagmus. These 2 horses progressed to have facial twitches contralateral to the side of recumbency consistent with seizures. One of these 2 horses started intermittent paddling movements without attempts to stand. This horse received diazepam (0.05 mg/kg IV twice) and seizures stopped. The 2 horses with seizure-triggered nystagmus made failed attempts to stand and remained in sternal recumbency for 80 min and 2 h, respectively, until they were able to stand after arrival to the recovery stall. The other horses stood up within 20-40 min after arrival to the recovery stall. The horse that stood promptly (20 min) collapsed twice after standing because of the severity of its ataxia but was able to remain standing after the last attempt.

Five horses appeared unilaterally blind in the left eye based on absent menace response while in recumbency and once standing, with horses being startled after bumping their heads against the wall on the side with absent menace response. Absent menace response was contralateral to the side of recumbency. Horses had a menace response and appeared visual in the right eye. Dazzle and pupillary light reflexes (PLRs) were intact in all horses. No other cranial nerves deficits were detected.

After recovery from anesthesia, all 7 horses appeared hyperresponsive to physical (touch; whole body hyperesthesia) and environmental stimuli (sound and movement of personnel). All horses were left in the padded recovery stall for 1 h after standing because of concerns of seizures. Ataxia appeared mildly worse (1 grade) in 1 horse when walking back to the stall compared with the baseline examination. Horses with blindness walked with reluctance from the recovery stall to the housing stall which was interpreted as being associated with blindness. These horses were noted bumping the wall with the left side of the head. Three horses displayed unilateral facial focal motor seizures on the left side within an hour of being back in the housing stall (2 h after standing). These episodes lasted approximately 30 s. Based on clinical observation, seizures (twitching on left side) were classified as focal with a focal onset in the right cerebral cortex. In one horse, these became generalized with collapse, tonic-clonic seizures, and the horse appeared to be unconscious.

Based on neurologic examination, lack of menace response in the left eye with normal dazzle reflex and PLRs bilaterally, right-sided cortical blindness was diagnosed. The neuroanatomical localization was the right cerebral cortex. A definitive diagnosis for proprioceptive ataxia was not reached in these horses. No cytological CSF abnormalities were observed and indirect fluorescent antibodies for the detection of Sarcocystis neurona and Neospora hughesi (causative pathogens of equine protozoal myelitis) were not found in serum and CSF from diseased horses. Serum vitamin E concentrations were normal. Electromyography in the 2 WB horses showed prolonged insertional activity, fibrillation potentials and positive sharp waves in the muscles innervated by the C6 and C7 spinal cord segments. Necropsy was not performed in diseased horses because they were discharged from the hospital and retired at home.

Seizure management

Medical intervention consisted of single (2 horses) or repeated doses (4 horses) of diazepam or midazolam at a dosage of 0.04-0.1 mg/kg IV. Seizures were not observed in one horse with central blindness. Additional doses (1-2) were administered to 4 horses. Phenobarbital was administered in 2 horses in which seizures continued to occur intermittently after the third dose of benzodiazepines. A loading dose of phenobarbital in the horse with generalized seizures consisted of 12 mg/kg IV diluted in 1 L of 0.9% saline solution and administered over 20 min. Seizures were not observed after the initiation of phenobarbital IV in this horse. Blindness resolved in 3 horses within 8-12 h and between 20 and 48 h in the remaining 2 horses, after recovery from anesthesia. The horse with increased grade of ataxia returned to baseline grade within 24 h after the myelogram. All horses recovered uneventfully and were discharged from the hospital within 1-3 days (24 h, 5; 48 h, 1; 96 h, 1) post-myelogram on no medication (5 horses) or PO phenobarbital (2 horses; dosage, 3 mg/kg q12h for 2-3 days). Based on follow-up telephone conversations, all horses remained seizure free and visual after discharge with owners reporting no adverse effects.

Electroencephalography

Electroencephalography was performed in 3 of 7 horses shortly after a seizure and while under the effects of diazepam or midazolam to facilitate the placement of electrodes, despite the risk of obscuring paroxysmal activity because of drug effects. Because of safety concerns, the study period was limited to 20-25 min. Intermittent paroxysmal activity consisting of ED was seen in the right cerebral cortex at the central, parietal, and occipital areas (Figure 2). The onset zone was at the right parietal cerebral cortex in all horses. Heart rate at the onset of single isolated paroxysmal activity remained within the normal range (Figure 2A), whereas with increased paroxysmal activity, horses became tachycardic and restless, which interfered with the ECG recording (Figure 2B and D). Activation procedures such as photic and auditory stimulation while recording the EEG were not performed to avoid overstimulation. After the results of the EEG and for illustrative purposes, images of a CT myelogram of a horse (not included in the study) in right lateral recumbency were retrieved to show the location and distribution of contrast material within the cranium while in this position (Figure 3) to further investigate a possible association with the lateralization of signs observed in our horses.

Discussion

The focal onset zone of seizures post-myelogram in horses under lateral recumbency was ipsilateral to the recumbent side in the parietal cerebral cortex. However, paroxysmal activity also was noted in other dependent areas of the brain such as central (temporal) cortex and occipital cortex in horses with blindness. Cortical blindness was evident as lack of menace response contralateral to the recumbent side with normal bilateral dazzle reflex and PLRs, and paroxysmal activity on EEG detected in the occipital cerebral cortex. Seizures and blindness were transient with full resolution. Clinically, seizures resolved within 2-8 h post-recovery from anesthesia, whereas cortical blindness resolved within 8-48 h post-recovery. Hyperesthesia and increased grade of ataxia also resolved within 24 h. Permanent neurologic deficits associated with myelography were not observed or reported several days after discharge.

Description of the type of seizures and SOZ associated with myelography in horses from other studies is lacking.6,14,21,22 According to the International League Against Epilepsy, seizures in the horses of our study were classified as focal or generalized (http//www.ilae.org; March 6, 2026). Six horses had facial focal motor seizures with 2 horses progressing to body twitching ipsilateral to the side of facial twitching. One of these horses further progressed to generalized tonic-clonic seizures with loss of consciousness. Focal seizures lasted 30 s to 2 min, whereas generalized seizures ranged from 1 to 2 min with intermittent focal motor seizures occurring between the generalized seizures. The neuroanatomical onset zone of seizures was determined to be the right cerebrum based on neurologic examination.

The SOZ, defined as the region of the cerebral cortex with the earliest recognizable paroxysmal activity on an EEG (origin) was determined in our study.29,30 Focal PD in this zone have the potential to spread to other areas of the cerebral cortex (ipsilateral, contralateral, or both) or trigger clinical seizures or result in both. Furthermore, based on amplitude, frequency, and time frequency of the SOZ, different patterns have been described in humans with paroxysmal activity.29 Based on EEG, the SOZ in our horses occurred in the right parietal cerebral cortex with extension to the right central (temporal) region of the brain, which are the most dependent areas when in lateral recumbency. Determining if a specific SOZ pattern predominates in horses after a myelogram was not possible because of the low number of cases included. However, it was evident from our study that paroxysmal activity originates in the parietal cortex with focal expansion ipsilateral to the side of recumbency, and in one case to generalized expansion resulting in generalized tonic-clonic seizures.

The EEG also featured ED and other paroxysmal activity different from ED in the occipital cortex ipsilateral to the side of recumbency (right side) which likely resulted in blindness in the contralateral eye (OS). This finding further supported the clinical observations of cortical blindness of OS involving the right occipital cortex; which was based on absent menace response of OS with normal bilateral dazzle reflex and PLRs. The dazzle reflex or photic blink reflex is a subcortical reflex that indicates light stimulating the visual pathway from the retina, optic nerve and tract to the level of the lateral geniculate nuclei (LGN). Axons project from the LGN to the rostral colliculi from which tectonuclear pathways stimulate the facial nucleus for eye closure.31 Because the pathway of this reflex does not involve the cerebral cortex, animals with central blindness maintain the reflex.31

Management of seizures in these horses was successful using both, short- and long-term antiseizure medications with apparent resolution within a few hours. However, the influence of antiseizure medication in preventing clinical manifestations of seizures likely played a role in this observation. An EEG would have been required to further investigate possible subclinical paroxysmal activity. The clearance and transit time within the subarachnoid space from the site of injection of contrast material are unknown in horses. A study using radiolabeled allogeneic mesenchymal stem cells injected intrathecally in the AO space in healthy horses determined the transit time to the lumbosacral (LS) space to be 5 h, with remaining radiolabeled cells in the AO space after 24 h.32 This finding raises the possibility that the contrast material might take a few hours to reach the LS space, which might explain the resolution of signs within hours in horses post-myelography with some horses remaining blind for longer period of time when perhaps all contrast material is cleared from the brain.

The ideal contrast agent should be pharmacologically inert, water soluble, miscible with CSF, and radiopaque at an isotonic concentration.33 Iohexol is a second generation nonionic contrast agent that reportedly has fewer neurotoxic effects post-myelogram than first generation nonionic contrast agents such as metrizamide.14,24 The largest study in veterinary medicine investigating risk factors for seizures after myelography with iohexol included 503 dogs.24 Seizures were reported in 3% of this population and several factors were associated with higher risk, such as breed, size of the dog, site of injection (AO), multiple injections, total volume of contrast, location of the lesion, and disease process (eg, compressive myelopathy).24 Volume of contrast appears to be a key factor associated with the frequency of seizures based on several studies in dogs and horses.6,22,24,34 It appears that total volume is more important than contrast concentration from existing data.18,24,33 Based on risk assessment studies in dogs, the total volume of contrast rather than a calculated dose in mL/kg should dictate the amount to be used.24 A specific dose or total volume has not been determined objectively in horses. In one study, a total volume of 50 and 60 mL for horses < 550 and >550 kg, respectively, was proposed in attempts to minimize the development of seizures.6 A total volume of contrast material of 56 + 15 mL was higher in horses with adverse reactions compared with 52 + 12 mL in horses with no adverse reactions associated with myelography in a multi-institutional study.22 In our study, all horses were >500 kg and received a total contrast volume of 50-60 mL which corresponded to a median dosage of 0.1 mL/kg. Conclusions about total volume of contrast cannot be made from our study because of the low number of horses included and lack of comparison with horses that did not develop seizures with similar contrast volume.

Removal of a volume of CSF similar to the intended volume of contrast material to infuse is a common practice.21 In our study, a lower volume (50 mL) was removed than the infused volume (60 mL). The effects of the combination of less CSF volume collected with a higher total volume of contrast material infused in expansion of the subarachnoid space, increased intracranial pressure, and alterations in circulation of CSF affecting clearance of contrast material from the brain are unknown.

Warmbloods and TB undergoing radiographic myelography are overrepresented in the English veterinary literature.11,21,22 However, breed did not appear to pose a higher risk for seizures in a recent study.11 Horses manifesting seizures in our study included WB and TB exclusively. However, other breeds have been reported to have seizures after myelography.22 CVCM has been reported to be a common problem in some horse breeds including WB and TB.3 Cervical compressive lesions might alter the caudal flow of contrast material by slowing its circulation and remaining longer at more cranial sites, including intracranially. A recent study reported a potentially higher seizure risk within the first 24 h after myelography in horses with cervical compressive myelopathy (n = 7/44) compared with horses without compression (n = 14/150), but this difference was not statistically significant.11 Compressive myelopathy did not appear to play a role in the development of seizures in our horses (3 diseased, 4 neurologically normal), but without advanced imaging studies (CT myelogram, MRI) or necropsy evaluation, CVCM could not be entirely ruled out in diseased horses.

Duration of anesthesia (longer anesthesia time) has been reported to be a risk factor for seizures post-myelogram in horses (any adverse reaction, 101 + 27 min; no adverse reaction, 96 + 31 min).22 This association has not been found in dogs.24 Furthermore, dogs undergoing surgery after myelography (prolonged anesthesia) had a lower risk of seizures compared with dogs not having surgery after myelography.24 Therefore, it is possible that the combination of prolonged contact time of contrast with the brain (eg, duration of anesthesia) with a high total volume of contrast might increase the risk of seizures in horses, and not the duration of anesthesia alone. However, the current recommendation is to avoid prolongation of anesthesia in horses because of the possible risks and complications in large animals compared with small animals.35 Current practice at our hospital includes head and neck elevation during recovery from anesthesia to promote the movement of contrast caudally and to minimize contact time of contrast with the brain. In our study, horses with ongoing nystagmus concurrent with facial twitching and rigid ears took longer to stand. This finding should alert the clinician to ongoing paroxysmal activity of the brain that could support seizures.

Limitations of our study included the low numbers of horses with seizures and blindness post-myelogram, lack of comparison with other horses undergoing myelography without seizures, limited number of horses having an EEG performed, and effects of antiseizure medication on the EEG. Also, the various myelogram protocols in terms of CSF volume removed, total volume of contrast infused, duration and protocol of anesthesia, and range of dosages of antiseizure medication used based upon clinician discretion added variability to the study. However, these limitations did not preclude reaching the objective of the study and supporting our hypothesis.

We described the type and onset zone of seizures and blindness post-myelography in horses. Our findings contribute to the definition, classification, and neuroanatomical focus of seizures based on neurologic examination, identification of the SOZ based on EEG, and definition and neuroanatomical localization as cortical blindness with an onset zone identified on EEG. Seizures usually are focal and transient with resolution within hours. Cortical blindness might be more common than reported previously and could easily be missed, especially in cases with partial or intermittent dysfunction if not examined closely. Similarly, blindness is transient, but it might take longer to resolve as compared with seizure activity. These findings should alert the clinician of these potential adverse effects that, although transient, if not addressed could have detrimental consequences.

Appendix: Seizures and blindness post-myelogram

Materials and methods

Retrospective study.

Animals and inclusion criteria

An IACUC protocol was not needed for the clinical cases because myelography and EEG studies were performed as part of standard of the diagnostic workup and care with owner consent. Cases undergoing CT myelography were not included here because of different study protocols including various horses’ head and body positions.

Radiographic myelography

Intravenous catheters were placed aseptically in the left jugular vein to deliver drugs and IV fluids. Premedication drugs administered varied on an individual basis and consisted of single doses of antimicrobials and anti-inflammatory drugs. Antimicrobials used included procaine penicillin at 22 000 IU/kg IM or potassium penicillin at 22 000 IU/kg IV, and gentamicin at 6.6 mg/kg IV. Anti-inflammatory drugs included flunixin meglumine at 1.1 mg/kg IV or dexamethasone sodium phosphate 0.04-0.06 mg/kg IV. After premedication with xylazine hydrochloride at 1 mg/kg IV, anesthesia was induced with diazepam hydrochloride at 0.05 mg/kg IV, and ketamine hydrochloride at 2.2 mg/kg IV in 6 horses. One horse was premedicated with xylazine at 0.05 mg/kg IV and induced with glyceryl guaiacolate 59 mg/kg IV and thiopental 4 mg/kg IV. Two horses also received propofol at 0.5-1 mg/kg IV at the discretion of the attending anesthesiologist. All myelograms were performed under general inhalation anesthesia with isoflurane after orotracheal intubation with 100% oxygen.

Horses were placed on right lateral recumbency to perform the myelogram according to protocol for our institution. Anatomical landmarks for AO CSF centesis as described previously were identified and aseptically prepared.26 An 18G by 3.5″ spinal needle was used for the puncture. After puncture, a volume of 50-60 mL of CSF (0.08-0.1 mL/kg) was removed from the subarachnoid space before intrathecal administration. Volume of contrast (iohexol 300 mg I/mL) administered ranged from 50 to 60 mL (median, 60 mL) which corresponded to a dosage from 0.09 to 0.1 mL/kg, within 3-5 min through an IV administration extension set. Immediately after contrast administration, the head was elevated for 5-10 min. Radiographic myelogram included neutral, flexed and extended images of the cervical vertebral column from the caudal aspect of the occipital bone to the first thoracic vertebra. The position of the horse for these views was in right lateral recumbency with no head elevation. When available, radiographs of the cranium were reviewed to determine the presence of contrast material.

After myelography, horses were recovered from anesthesia in a padded box stall and placed in right lateral recumbency after xylazine IV administration. Horses with general proprioceptive ataxia were assisted with head and tail ropes for horses with ataxia grades 1-3 of 5 or a lifting device (large animal lift; http://www.largeanimallift.com) for grades 3+ or 4, according to a modified grading score of ataxia.25 In brief, grade 1 consists of subtle or intermittent deficits, grade 2 persistent mild, grade 3 persistent moderate (readily seen by inexperienced observer), grade 4 persistent severe at risk of collapse, grade 5 recumbent unable to stand.

Neurologic examination post-myelogram

For safety purposes and to avoid overstimulation after anesthesia recovery, horses were allowed 30 min after standing to become more stable on their feet before attempting neurologic examination. However, menace response was evaluated while recumbent, but when horses appeared awake for interpretation. Behavior, mentation, and cranial nerves were evaluated at the recovery stall and then again when back at the hospital stall. Gait evaluation was made at the time the horses were walked back from the recovery stall to the housing stalls and the day after anesthesia (18-24 h after recovery from anesthesia).

Seizures and blindness

Seizures were described, defined, and classified based on their clinical presentation and further defined by EEG.27

Electroencephalogram

Electrodiagnostics included electroencephalogram (EEG) and electrocardiogram (ECG). Two EEG machines, depending on availability, were used: (1) Nihon Kohden (Neurofax 2110, Nihon Kohden America Inc, Foothill Ranch, CA, USA) with integrated photostimulator and video; and (2) Nihon Kohden digital wireless EEG system (Neurofax Wireless Input 1000A, Nihon Kohden America Inc., Foothill Ranch, CA, USA) with integrated video.

Electrodes nomenclature and placement were based on a modified 10-20 system used in humans and described in horses.28 Number designation followed human EEG protocol with even and odd numbers denoting right and left side, respectively, and vertex denoted as “z.” Disposable subcutaneous needle electrodes (UCD: Grass/Astro-Med, West Warwick, RI, USA) were denoted as follows: Z = ground (1 electrode), F = frontal (3: F3, Fz, F4), C = central (3: C3, Cz, C4), P = parietal (3: P3, Pz, P4) and O = occipital (2: O1, O2) regions. The electrodes were placed subcutaneously in the scalp located at an equidistance of about to 2 cm from each other in a cranial to caudal and transverse direction as per UCD EEG protocol.28 A bipolar (rostral to caudal and transverse) montage was used. The impedance was checked at the beginning of each recording to ensure interelectrode impedances were less than 10 kΩ to minimize artifacts. Movement artifacts were recorded when noted with the aid of the video recording.

Waveform nomenclature consisted of δ (> 0 to < 4 Hz), θ (4 to < 8 Hz), α (8 to < 13 Hz), β (13 to < 40 Hz), and γ (> 40 Hz). Slow wave sleep was determined by the presence of background slowing (δ activity), vertex sharp waves, sleep spindles, and/or K-complexes. Paroxysms in support of ED consisted of spikes (duration < 70 ms), sharp waves (duration 70-200 ms), spike and wave discharges, or multiple spike complexes. ED were classified as focal (if originating from a localized focus), multifocal (if involving >3 independent foci involving both hemispheres) or generalized (if involving all electrode channels simultaneously). Other paroxysmal activity different from ED was also recorded. EEG recordings were performed and reviewed by a veterinary neurologist (MA).
