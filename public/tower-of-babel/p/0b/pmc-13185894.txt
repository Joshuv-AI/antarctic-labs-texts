Evaluating Kleinia odora leaves as a novel phytogenic feed additive to enhance early growth, feed efficiency, European performance efficiency index, and carcass yield in broilers
Authors: Maged A Al-Garadi, Abdulaziz A Al-abdullatif, Abdulmohsen H Alqhtani, Mohammed M Qaid, Hani H Albaadani, Faisal A Alshamiry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Broilers supplemented with 0.5 g/kg K. odora powder exhibited increases in body weight of 6.73% and 6.19% on days 14 and 24, respectively, compared to the control.•Broilers fed 0.5 g/kg K. odora showed an improvement in weight gain, FCR, and EPEF by 8.35, 7.75, and 17.01% during days 8-14 compared to the control.•Overall BWG and EPEF improved significantly by 6.35% and 12.31%, respectively, in broilers receiving 0.5 g/kg K. odora, indicating improved overall growth efficiency.•Feed intake remained unaffected in all treatments, suggesting that the increase in performance was due to improved nutrient utilization rather than increased consumption.•K. odora, particularly at a low dose (0.5 g/kg), enhanced lean tissue deposition and overall carcass yield while reducing abdominal fat, whereas higher doses (1.5 g/kg) showed opposite effects, highlighting both the benefits and limits of K. odora supplementation.Keywords: Broiler performance, Carcass yields, European production efficiency factor, Plant-based growth promoters, Starter period

Introduction

The poultry industry continues to explore natural feed additives that can enhance growth performance and carcass yield while reducing reliance on synthetic growth promoters. Phytogenic additives, derived from herbs and medicinal plants, have gained attention because of their antioxidant, antimicrobial, digestive-stimulating, and metabolic-modulating activities (Rafiq et al., 2022; Basiouni et al., 2024; Aminullah et al., 2025; Nechitailo et al., 2025). Many plant species native to arid and semi-arid regions of the Middle East contain diverse bioactive compounds-such as terpenoids, flavonoids, and phenolics-that may improve nutrient utilization, gut integrity, and early-life growth efficiency in broilers (Rahmouni et al., 2021; Pashaei et al., 2024; Al-Garadi et al., 2025; Rehman et al., 2025).

K. odora is a wild medicinal plant abundantly distributed in the southern regions of Saudi Arabia and traditionally consumed by both animals and humans, indicating a favorable safety profile (Shehata et al., 2018; Velazhahan et al., 2024). It belongs to the genus Kleinia (Family Asteraceae, Tribe Senecioneae), which comprises about 40 species distributed across Yemen, Ethiopia, Somalia, the Middle East, Madagascar, and India (Alfaifi et al., 2020; Al-Fatimi, 2024; Tegegne et al., 2025). Plants in the Asteraceae family are known for producing essential oils, sesquiterpenes, triterpenoid saponins, flavonoids, and phenolic compounds, many of which exhibit gastroprotective, antioxidant, cytotoxic, antimicrobial, antiprotozoal, anti-obesity, and anti-inflammatory activities (Al Musayeib et al., 2013; Shehata et al., 2018; Awuchi and Morya, 2023; Velazhahan et al., 2024; Abou Auda, 2025).

Phytochemical investigations show that K. odora is particularly rich in oxygenated sesquiterpenes and triterpenes, including lupeol, epilupeol, lupenone, lupeol acetate, ursolic acid, brein, oleanolic acid, friedelin, and sphingolipid glucosides (odoraside A and B). These compounds are well recognized for their anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective effects (Al Musayeib et al., 2013; Shehata et al., 2018). Shehata et al. (2018) reported potent anti-inflammatory activity from triterpenes isolated from the aerial parts of K. odora. In addition, K. odora extract demonstrated metabolic and detoxifying potential (Velazhahan et al., 2024). Ethnoveterinary practices also support its use for treating abdominal and nerve pain, underscoring its therapeutic value and long-standing safety in humans and animals.

Despite this rich phytochemical and pharmacological profile, K. odora has not been scientifically evaluated as a dietary phytogenic additive in broiler production. This represents a critical knowledge gap, especially given the global shift toward natural growth promoters and the need to improve early-life performance, feed efficiency, and carcass development. The starter phase represents a pivotal period in broiler production, marked by rapid development of both the immune system and the gastrointestinal tract (Qaid et al., 2023a; Jonchère et al., 2025). Early growth (0-24 days) is a sensitive phase during which nutritional interventions can significantly shape metabolic programming, muscle accretion, and feed conversion efficiency. When applied during the early production phase, PFAs are particularly beneficial, as they promote gut development, maintain microbial homeostasis, and enhance nutrient utilization (Qaid et al., 2021; Obianwuna et al., 2024). Identifying optimal inclusion levels is essential, as excessive phytogenic supplementation may impose metabolic load or alter organ function.

Therefore, the present study evaluated graded dietary levels of K. odora powder on growth performance, feed intake, feed conversion ratio, EPEF, carcass traits, and organ development of broilers during early growth. To our knowledge, this is the first controlled investigation assessing the potential of K. odora as a phytogenic feed additive for broilers. The findings aim to provide foundational evidence for its safe and effective integration into commercial poultry feeding strategies.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

The study complies with the ARRIVE guidelines, demonstrating transparency in experimental design, animal care, and reporting. The research adhered to all applicable Saudi Arabian regulations and received ethical approval from the King Saud University (KSU) Committee on Animal Care and Welfare (KSU-SE-20-34). The authors applied appropriate bias reduction methods, including randomization, blinding of outcome assessments, and clearly defined inclusion and exclusion criteria, which collectively strengthen the validity and reproducibility of the findings.

Species collection and identification

Fresh mature leaves (3.5-5.0 cm in length) of K. odora, were collected in June 2022 from valleys and mountainous surrounding the Ibb Governorate, Republic of Yemen (13.9721° N, 44.1625° E). The leaves were air-dried at room temperature for two weeks, then shipped to the Department of Animal Production, KSU. The plant material was taxonomically identified and authenticated by a botanist with exsiccate registration No. 9048 at the Department of Botany, Faculty of Science, KSU. Finally, the dried leaves were ground using a blender to obtain a fine powder (particle size: 0.25-0.30 mm).

Housing, experimental design, and diet

This study was carried out between July 1 and July 30, 2022. The data collection process began on June 7, 2022, and continued through July 24, 2022, encompassing key periods for measuring performance and carcass traits. Data analysis was subsequently conducted from August 25 to August 30, 2022. This study was carried out at the KSU Experimental Poultry Research Unit using 240 1-day-old Ross 308 chicks, obtained from Al Khomasia Hatchery®, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. On arrival, the chicks were sexed by feathers and weighed individually, and the male broiler chicks were selected. This approach reduces experimental variability but represents a limitation, as responses in mixed-sex flocks may differ under commercial production conditions. Then, chicks randomly divided equally into 4 treatment groups. Each group consisted of 10 replicates of 6 birds each. The broilers were treated with one of four treatments. Four dietary treatments were formulated by adding K. odora at 0, 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 g/kg to a standard broiler basal diet.

The K. odora supplementation was added on top of the basal diet for broilers (1-24 days) in the mash feed formula based on the recommendations of the Ross 308 Management Guide (Aviagen, 2019, New York, NY, USA) (Table 1). Feeders and nipple drinkers have free access to feed and water. The chicks were housed in electrically controlled, heated battery cages, with a room temperature of 35°C on arrival and a gradual decrease of 0.5°C per day until it reached 21-22°C. The temperature in the poultry house was maintained for 24 hours. The relative humidity was between 65% and 85%. The average temperature outside the barn was about 26.4°C, and humidity was light to moderate. The photoperiod was 23 h on:1 h off.

All chicks were kept under the same conditions in a cage with a length of 58 cm, a width of 50 cm, and a height of 35 cm. The stocking density was 6 birds/0.30 m2. All chicks were vaccinated against Newcastle disease virus (NDV), infectious bronchitis virus (IBV), and infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV) at the hatchery (Fort Dodge Animal Health-USA).

Growth performance indices and European production efficiency factor

The chicks of each replicate were weighed individually on days 0, 7, 14 and 24, and the feed consumption of each replicate was recorded on days 1-7, 8-14 and 15-24. For each group, the average gain (gain in each period = final body weight - initial body weight), the average FI (weighing the remaining feed at the end of each period and subtracting it from the total amount offered at the beginning of the same period), the FCR (the ratio of FI to gain) and the European production efficiency factor (EPEF) were calculated. Viability percentage = 100 - mortality percentage. EPEF = [Viability percentage x body weight (kg)] / [FCR (kg feed per kg gain) x age (day)] x 100.

Characteristics of the carcass

On day 14 and 24, the weight of a randomly selected broiler from each replicate (n = 10 birds/group) was approximated to the average weight, and then the cervical dislocation was performed to evaluate carcass characteristics. The slaughter procedures of broilers were conducted in full compliance with Islamic halal guidelines in Saudi Arabia followed ethical standards approved by the KSU institutional committee, ensuring humane handling and minimizing animal suffering. Slaughter weight and carcass weight (excluding head, neck, feathers, shanks, abdominal fat, and eviscerated organs) were measured. The carcass yield was calculated as the ratio between carcass weight and slaughter weight. Breast, leg, wing, kidney, pancreas, immune organs (bursa, spleen, and thymus), and offal (heart, gizzard, proventriculus, and liver without gall bladder) were collected to calculate the percentage of organs. The percentage yield for each organ was estimated in relation to carcass weight (Suliman et al., 2023).

Statistical analysis

Data were subjected to one-way ANOVA using the general linear model (GLM) of SAS (2012) software from the Statistical Analysis System (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Results

Growth performance indices and European performance efficiency factor

The effects of K. odora feeding on the average body weight of broilers on days 0, 7, 14, and 24 are shown in Table 2. All chicks had similar initial weights at baseline (P > 0.05), confirming proper randomization between treatment groups. Although numerically higher body weight was recorded at day 7 in all K. odora groups, particularly at 1.5 g/kg (160.5 g), the differences were not statistically significant (P = 0.483). On day 14, birds receiving 0.5 g/kg K. odora showed a significantly higher body weight (386.8 g) compared to the control group (362.4 g), while the 1.5 g/kg group showed an intermediate response (P = 0.015), indicating a positive but non-linear dose effect. Therefore, the 0.5 g/kg K. odora group consistently showed better growth, especially on day 24, suggesting that it contains the optimal amount of K. odora.

Effects of dietary Kleinia odora powder on growth performance and European performance efficiency index (EPEF) in broilers.

As shown in Table 2, during days 0–7, there were no significant differences between treatments (P = 0.571) in BWG, although the 1.5 g/kg group showed the highest numerical gain (16.25 g/day). On days 7–14, the 0.5 g/kg group had a significantly higher BWG (32.45 g/day) than all other treatments (P = 0.010), notably outperforming the 1.0 g/kg group (28.96 g/day), with a notable cubic effect (P = 0.002). Furthermore, birds fed 0.5 g/kg K. odora reached the highest BWG on days 15-24 (69.31 g/day; P = 0.042), showing a strong cubic response pattern (P = 0.007). Consequently, over the entire period (days 0–24), treatment with 0.5 g/kg was observed to result in a significantly higher mean BWG (43.04 g/day; P = 0.005), while the control and 1.0 g/kg groups were statistically lower, supporting a dose-dependent non-linear effect (cubic response pattern).

In all periods (days 0-7, 8-14, 15-24, and 0-24), FI was not significantly affected by K. odora supplementation (P > 0.05), indicating that K. odora had a minimal effect on feed palatability or voluntary intake. The 1.5 g/kg group had the highest total FI (46.23 g/day), but this did not result in a significant increase (P = 0.677).

On days 0-7, FCR improved in all K. odora groups compared to the control group, with the best efficiency in the 1.5 g/kg group (1.03 g/g), although not significantly (P = 0.140). On days 8-14, FCR improved significantly (P = 0.007) in the 0.5 g/kg group (1.31 g/g), while the 1.0 g/kg group had the worst efficiency (1.48 g/g). The pattern followed a strong cubic trend (P = 0.002). No significant differences were observed at days 15-24 and 0-24 (P = 0.559 and P = 0.170, respectively), although the 0.5 g/kg group consistently had a numerically lower (better) FCR. On days 0–7, all K. odora treatments improved the EPEF, particularly at 1.5 g/kg (224.2), but without statistical significance (P = 0.290). On days 8-14, the EPEF was significantly higher in the 0.5 g/kg group (a strong cubic effect; P = 0.0003) (214.6; P = 0.001), while the 1.0 g/kg group had the lowest value (174.4). On days 15-24, EPEF peaked in the 0.5 g/kg group (461.9), with marginal significance (P = 0.050) and a cubic trend (P = 0.026). Overall (day 0-24), the 0.5 g/kg group showed a significantly higher EPEF compared to the other treatments (427.0; P = 0.004), confirming optimal performance at this inclusion rate.

Carcass traits

On day 14 of life, the inclusion of K. odora in the diets of broilers significantly affected several carcass traits in broilers (Table 3). Live body weight (BW) was not significantly different among treatment groups (P = 0.259), but a trend towards a linear increase was observed (P = 0.080). Carcass weight showed a non-significant trend (P = 0.090) with a notable linear effect (P = 0.018), indicating a modest improvement in carcass at low K. odora supplementation. Carcass yield differed significantly between treatments (P = 0.005), with the 0.5 g/kg group having the highest yield (59.8%) and the 1.5 g/kg group the lowest (56.8%). A strong linear effect was observed (P < 0.001), indicating a potential decrease in yield at higher levels. Breast yield exhibited significant improvements with K. odora supplementation (P = 0.030), showing a linear (P = 0.032) and cubic (P = 0.049) response. The 0.5 and 1.5 g/kg groups had higher breast yield (42.75% and 42.68%, respectively) than the control (40.58%). Leg yield was also affected (P = 0.04), with a significant linear effect (P = 0.02), highest in the 0.5 g/kg group (36.63%). Among internal organs, the heart yield showed significant increases with K. odora (P = 0.020) and demonstrated a strong linear trend (P = 0.004), particularly in the 1.5 g/kg group (1.29%). Conversely, pancreas yield was significantly affected (P = 0.006) and demonstrated a strong cubic trend (P = 0.002), with a decrease at 1.5 g/kg (0.721%) compared to other values. The liver yield exhibited a significant linear increase (P = 0.010), while other organs like the gizzard, proventriculus, and lymphoid organs (thymus, bursa, or spleen yield) showed no significant differences (P > 0.05).

Effects of dietary Kleinia odora powder on carcass characteristics in broilers at day 14 of age.

At 24 days, dietary supplementation with K. odora significantly affected several carcass parameters (Table 4). Live body weight and carcass weight were both influenced (P = 0.004 and P < 0.0001, respectively), with the 0.5 g/kg group showing the highest live BW (1080 g) and carcass weight (656 g). The final BW was significantly highest in the 0.5 g/kg group compared to the 1.5 g/kg group (1,032 g), indicating a clear optimal response at the lowest supplementation level. Carcass yield also improved significantly (P = 0.003), peaking in birds fed 0.5 g/kg K. odora (60.78%). Among carcass components, breast yield increased significantly (P < 0.0001) in the 0.5 g/kg group (43.1%) compared to the control (41.0%). In contrast, abdominal fat decreased significantly (P < 0.0001) in the 0.5 g/kg group (1.17%) compared with the control (1.86%) and the 1.5 g/kg group (1.71%), with an intermediate value (1.45%) observed at 1.0 g/kg. Liver yield increased linearly in a dose-dependent manner (P < 0.0001), reaching a peak of 7.0% in the 1.5 g/kg group. This increase could reflect a hepatic response to phytochemical metabolism or stress. Polynomial contrasts revealed significant cubic effects (P < 0.002) for live BW, carcass weight, carcass yield (P < 0.0001), breast (P < 0.0001), and abdominal fat (P < 0.0001), suggesting a nonlinear dose-response relationship, with optimal performance at the moderate inclusion level (0.5 g/kg). Overall, moderate dietary inclusion (0.5 g/kg) of K. odora enhanced live BW, carcass yield, and breast proportion while reducing abdominal fat deposition in broilers at 24 days of age.

Discussion

The present study provides initial evidence that Kleinia odora powder can support early growth performance, feed efficiency, EPEF, and carcass characteristics in broiler chickens, particularly at the 0.5 g/kg inclusion level. These responses are consistent with the herb’s bioactive profile, including triterpenes and oxygenated sesquiterpenes, which have reported anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and cytoprotective properties (Al Musayeib et al., 2013; Shehata et al., 2018; Tegegne et al., 2025).

The focus on the starter phase (1-24 days) reflects its critical role in digestive and immune system development, influencing nutrient utilization and subsequent performance (Qaid et al., 2023b). Early exposure to phytogenic additives is particularly important, as they can support gut maturation, microbial stability, and villus development during the starter phase (Azzam et al., 2020; Qaid et al., 2021; Banday et al., 2024). Although most commercially reared broilers are typically slaughtered between 24 and 30 days, future studies should assess K. odora supplementation over the entire growth period to evaluate its effects on overall performance, carcass characteristics, and health outcomes.

Performance differences were most pronounced from day 7 onward, with 0.5 g/kg K. odora consistently producing the highest BWG and EPEF, while 1.0 and 1.5 g/kg showed no proportional gains, suggesting a threshold response typical of phytogenic additives. During days 1–7, no differences were observed, likely due to limited exposure and immature gut physiology, though the slightly lower FCR at 1.5 g/kg indicates early tolerance. The advantage of 0.5 g/kg persisted through day 24, with overall BWG and EPEF increases of 6.35% and 12.31%, respectively, without changes in feed intake or FCR, indicating performance improvements independent of consumption. The performance improvements resulted from enhanced physiological efficiency, inferred solely from unchanged feed intake, is not fully supported. To strengthen this conclusion, additional data on intestinal morphology, digestive enzyme activity, or tight junction protein expression would be needed. Higher inclusion levels did not enhance outcomes and occasionally reduced efficiency, consistent with reports that excessive phytogenic supplementation may disrupt nutrient utilization or reflect adaptive metabolic responses (Fallah and Nobakht, 2014). The relatively lower performance observed at 1.0 g/kg compared to 0.5 g/kg, yet comparable to 1.5 g/kg and the control, likely reflects normal biological variability rather than a consistent dose-dependent effect.

Carcass outcomes at days 14 and 24 further confirm the growth performance trends. Birds receiving 0.5 g/kg K. odora had greater carcass weight, carcass yield, and breast muscle percentage, indicating that early improvements in BWG translated into superior tissue accretion. Breast muscle deposition is particularly sensitive to oxidative and inflammatory stress (Li et al., 2024). The absence of adverse effects on immune organ weights (bursa, thymus, spleen) indicates that K. odora did not impair immune development. This is consistent with its ethnoveterinary use in Saudi Arabia, Yemen, and Ethiopia, where the plant is traditionally consumed by livestock and humans (Shehata et al., 2018; Velazhahan et al., 2024).

The linear increase in liver percentage at higher inclusion levels (1.0 and 1.5 g/kg) likely reflects greater hepatic processing of phytochemicals, as triterpenes are known to stimulate metabolic and detoxification pathways. In broiler chickens, an increase in liver weight can be either a normal physiological response particularly during the first week post-hatch (Alshamy et al., 2019) or a sign of metabolic stress and toxicity (Kumari et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2024). In the present study, values remained within the physiological adaptive range reported for phytogenic additives, suggesting no toxic effect. However, higher K. odora inclusion levels may impose additional metabolic load without improving performance. K. odora appears safe and effective at moderate inclusion levels, supporting its use as a functional additive and reinforcing the importance of dose optimization to maximize benefits while minimizing risk (Dong et al., 2024; Urban et al., 2024). The absence of mortality differences further supports the overall safety of K. odora at the tested doses. Many Asteraceae plants exhibit hepatoprotective effects due to their flavonoids, phenolics, and alkaloids (Basiouni et al., 2024; Shawon et al., 2024), but excessive bioactive intake can still elevate hepatic activity. This comparison highlights the potential advantages of K. odora, including its specific bioactive profile and possible synergistic effects, which may differentiate its efficacy from other Asteraceae phytogenics. Although abdominal fat was lower (1.17%) at 0.5 g/kg compared with the control (1.86%) and 1.5 g/kg (1.71%) groups, with an intermediate value at 1.0 g/kg, these observations alone are insufficient to establish a dose-dependent metabolic effect. In the absence of lipid metabolism biomarkers or enzymatic evidence, the findings should be interpreted descriptively rather than mechanistically. This adds value to K. odora as a potential natural lean tissue–enhancing additive. Thus, K. odora may improve carcass quality by redirecting energy utilization from fat synthesis toward lean tissue growth. Polynomial contrasts revealed significant cubic effects for live BW, carcass weight, carcass yield, breast, and abdominal fat, suggesting a nonlinear dose–response relationship, with optimal performance at 0.5 g/kg K. odora inclusion level. In addition, a cubic response in pancreas and proventriculus weights at 0.5 and 1.0 g/kg suggests a potential stimulatory effect of K. odora on digestive organ development, possibly reflecting enhanced digestive activity (Matur et al., 2010). However, the absence of digestive enzymes and assess intestinal health measurements limits confirmation of improved nutrient utilization.

Kleinia odora is a medicinal herb traditionally used for its therapeutic properties. Chemical analyses from previous studies show that K. odora contains a range of bioactive compounds, including lupeol, epilupeol, ursolic acid, oleanolic acid, (+)-epi-bicyclosesquiphellandrene, caryophyllene, α-caryophyllene, and α-pinene, with antioxidant, antimicrobial, antiprotozoal, and anti-inflammatory potential (Rokade et al., 2026; Al-Taweel et al., 2004; Al Musayeib et al., 2013; Shehata et al., 2018; Banu, 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). While these properties may contribute to observed growth and carcass responses, the lack of direct measurements of oxidative markers, inflammatory cytokines, digestive enzymes, or gut microbiota in this study prevents confirmation of specific mechanistic pathway. This aligns with reports of antioxidant, immune modulation, and antimicrobial benefits of phytogenics (Windisch et al., 2008; Bahramikia et al., 2022; Nobakht, 2022; Maodaa et al., 2024). Velazhahan et al. (2024) suggests that K. odora may contribute indirectly to improved gut health and hepatic function. While bioactive compounds in K. odora (triterpenoid saponins, phenolics, sesquiterpenes) may contribute to digestive and metabolic modulation, the current data are insufficient to confirm these effects, as reported for other Asteraceae species (Boeing et al., 2023; Kmail, 2024).

Across all measured parameters, the 0.5 g/kg dietary inclusion level consistently produced the most favorable outcomes, whereas higher levels (1.0 and 1.5 g/kg) did not yield proportional improvements and, in some cases, resulted in diminished performance. This suggests that K. odora exhibits a threshold response typical of phytogenic additives: low to moderate levels exert beneficial physiological activity. Overall, the findings indicate that K. odora powder is a promising phytogenic additive for early broiler production. Future studies should investigate digestive enzymes, nutrient digestibility, gut morphology, microbiota, oxidative and inflammatory biomarkers, and hepatic metabolism to better understand its effects and optimize dosing under commercial conditions.

Conclusions

In general, 0.5 g/kg appears to be the most effective dose to improve growth, carcass yield, and breast development in broilers without negatively affecting organ development, especially in the early growth phase. This dose appears to be optimal for improving carcass quality without compromising organ health. This improvement is likely related to the antioxidant, antimicrobial, and digestive effects of the flavonoids and terpenoids of K. odora. However, higher doses (≥1 g/kg) may reduce carcass yield and disproportionately affect internal organs, suggesting a threshold above which exposure to phytochemicals may affect metabolic balance, indicating potential metabolic stress. These results support the strategic use of K. odora as a functional feed additive but underline the importance of dose optimization.
