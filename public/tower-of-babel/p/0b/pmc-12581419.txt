The effect of different glass ionomer cements and surface coating applications on fluoride release and microhardness values
Authors: Huseyin Tezel, Mirna Mulabdic, Dilek Akin, Fikriye Selin Barhan, Cigdem Atalayin Ozkaya, Timur Kose, Ozlem Sogut
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe glass-ionomer cements have many advantages such as the ability to form chemical bonding at the tooth-restoration interface, release and recharge of fluoride, low cytotoxicity and high biocompatibility. But their low mechanical properties and susceptibility to moisture contamination during hardening limit their usage the regions exposed to high stress. The surface coatings used to protect the physical and mechanical properties of the material reduce fluoride release. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the effects of different glass-ionomer cements and surface coatings on fluoride-release and microhardness.MethodsThree glass-ionomer cements; Ketac Molar Easymix (conventional), EQUIA Forte Fil (high-viscosity hybrid), and ChemFil Rock (high-viscosity zinc) were tested. Each cement formed 21 specimens, subdivided into control, petroleum jelly, and coat groups. Samples were submerged in deionized water, with fluoride release measured using an ion-selective electrode on days 1, 3, 7, 14, 21, 28, and 56. Microhardness was assessed using a universal testing device at baseline and after 130 days.ResultsMicrohardness was assessed using a universal testing device at baseline and after 130 days. EQUIA Forte Fil showed the highest fluoride-release, while ChemFil Rock had the lowest (p ≤ 0.05). Surface treatments reduced fluoride-release for all materials, with no difference between petroleum jelly and coat groups. The highest microhardness was in EQUIA Forte Fil/control (baseline), and the lowest was in EQUIA Forte Fil/coat (p ≤ 0.05). Both EQUIA Forte Fil/control and ChemFil Rock/control showed decreased microhardness over 130 days (p ≤ 0.05).ConclusionsThe results demonstrate that the coating of glass-ionomer surfaces reduce fluoride release via preserving surface integrity but it may potentially limit remineralization and anticariogenic benefits. The surface coating reduces the deformation in the structure of the material and microhardness change. These effects should be consider on treatment planning.

Background

The glass ionomer cements consist of an aqueous solution of ion-releasable aluminosilicate glass and acrylic acid copolymer. The powder part contains calcium, fluoride and to a lesser extent sodium and phosphate minerals, while the liquid part contains itanoic acid, maleic acid, tartaric acid and water [1, 2]. According to their clinical usage, they are classified as type I bonding cement, type II restorative material and type III cavity liner and fissure sealant [3].

The glass-ionomer cements have many advantages such as the ability to form chemical bonding at the tooth-restoration interface, release and recharge of fluoride, low cytotoxicity and high biocompatibility. But their low mechanical properties and susceptibility to moisture contamination during hardening limit their usage the regions exposed to high stress [4, 5].

The glass ionomer is a material that can be bonded to the tooth structure self-adhesively. This bonding is mainly formed by the wetting of the tooth surface with free polyacrylic acid followed by the formation of an ionic bond between the carboxyl group in the cement liquid and calcium ions in the tooth structure. Additionally, the cement also shows a micromechanical bonding to the dentinal tubules [6, 7]. The hardening of glass ionomer cements starts with the contact of powder and liquid and acid attack to the glass particles starts. As the glass particles dissolve, calcium, aluminum and fluoride ions are released into the environment [8, 9]. The salt bridges begin to form between the dissolved cationic ions and the polyacid chains (calcium polyacrylate and aluminum polyacrylate), resulting in the formation of silica-rich hydrogels surrounding the unreacted glass particles [8, 9]. The calcium ions release faster than aluminum. Therefore, calcium ions are more dominant in the initial setting reaction, whereas in the maturation stage, the reaction continues with the formation of salt bridges by aluminum ions [8, 10]. The viscosity of the cement increases with the formation of calcium polyacrylate salts in the first minute, but it has not yet completed the maturation. While the air drying at this stage causes cracks on the surface, the exposure to excess moisture causes the reduce in the mechanical properties of the cement due to the dissolution of anions and cations providing matrix formation [2, 8]. It is recommended to protect the material with petroleum jelly or a resin coat until the complete of maturation to prevent this phenomen. The formation of aluminum polyacrylate salts starts at 10 min and the material gains its final mechanical properties in this phase and moreover the reaction is largely completed within 24 h [11, 12]. The hydrogel, which continues to exist after the setting reaction is completed, enables ion exchange both within the cement and the external environment [1].

The main advantage of glass ionomer cements is the ability to release fluoride. The fluoride release gives the material anti-cariogenic and antibacterial properties [7]. Fluoride is released from the cement at the highest level for the first 24 h after the glass ionomer curing reaction begins, and this is called as ‘the burst effect of fluoride’. After this initial burst, the fluoride continues to be released more slowly and in a controlled amount. The level of fluoride release can be influenced by various factors. In different studies, it has been reported that surface coatings used to protect the physical and mechanical properties of the material reduce fluoride release [13, 14]. The initial high fluoride release immediately after the curing reaction is an important advantage, but a balanced release is reported to be important for mechanical and adhesive properties [15]. In the long term, however, the controlled release provides continuous and sustainable protection against caries formation [16]. The presence of numerous material variables that interact with each other makes it necessary to determine which ones will be beneficial based on the patient’s condition and to implement a specific procedure.

In the literature, there are different studies evaluating the effects of different glass ionomer cements and surface coating processes on the efficacy, mechanical properties and fluoride release values separately [14, 17, 18]. Our study aims to analyze the effect of glass ionomer cements with different contents and different surface coating processes on fluoride release, while also providing data on changes in microhardness values and providing a perspective on the relationship between the two parameters. The first null hypothesis is that the different glass ionomer cements and surface coating applications do not have a statistically significant effect on fluoride release and microhardness values. The second hypothesis is that the different glass ionomer cements and surface coating applications have a statistically significant effect on fluoride release and microhardness values.

Methods

Three different types of glass ionomer materials as conventional glass ionomer cement (Ketac Molar Easymix-3 M ESPE, USA), high viscosity hybrid glass ionomer cement (EQUIA FORTE FİL Forte Fil-GC Corporation, Japan) and high viscosity zinc glass ionomer cement (ChemFil Rock Rock-Dentsply, USA) were tested in this study. For each of three materials, 21 cylindrical-shaped specimens with a diameter of 7.6 mm and a height of 2.1 mm were prepared in standardized silicone molds according to the manufacturers’ instructions. The microscope glass slide was used on both sides of the molds to obtain a flat surface and to avoid air bubbles in the samples. The excess cement overflowing from the molds was removed. After the completion of chemical curing reaction (10 min), the specimen was removed from the mold with light finger pressure and polished with discs (Tor-VM) and rubbers (Kenda-Coltene). Three subgroups (control (without any surface sealant), petroleum jelly, coat) were formed for the tested three materials (n = 7). In the petroleum jelly groups; the agent (Vaseline-Unilever, Turkey) was applied to the surface with the help of an applicator in a thin layer. In the coat groups; the coat (G Coat Plus-GC, Japan) was applied to the surface with the help of an applicator and polymerized for 20 s with a 750 mW/cm2 beam power capacity device (Bluephase C5-Ivoclar Vivadent, Liechtenstein).

Then, the each sample was placed in 15 ml centrifuge tubes containing 5 ml of deionized water and kept at room temperature until the measurement time. Due to the fluoride retention property of glass, the polyethylene test tubes were preferred to determine the values of fluoride ions. The tubes with the specimens were shaken every 24 h. The fluoride levels released from the materials to the solution were measured on the 1 st, 3rd, 7th, 14th, 21 st, 28th and 56th days. On each of the measurement days, the samples were removed from deionized water and washed with 0.5 ml distilled water, and then left to stand in deionized water at 37 °C in a new tube.

The fluoride release measurement

The measurement of fluoride release values was performed by ion selective electrode method. Orion 3 STAR (electron corporation) device and Orion Fluorid electrode (Thermo Orion Model 9609BN, combination fluoride electrode with BNC connector) were used. The used solutions were as follows; deionized water, standard fluoride solutions (0.1–10 ppm solutions prepared by diluting 100 ppm standard fluoride solution (Natriumfluorid rein; Erg. B. 6, Ph. Nord,1963; NaF, MERCK; E. Merck Darmstadt, Ernste Vergiftungsgefahr beim Verschlucken) with deionized water) and ionic strength adjusting solution (TISAB II with CDTA (Total Ionic Strength Adjustment Buffer) solution (2008 Thermo Fisher Scientific, Beverly, MA, 01915; USA, 940909)).

Before the measurement of fluoride concentration, the accuracy of the measuring device and the electrode slopes were checked according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Then, 0.5 ml of TISAB II with CDTA (Total Ionic Strength Adjustment Buffer 2008 Thermo Fisher Scientific, Beverly, MA, 01915; USA, 940909) solution was added to each sample to obtain constant ionic strength and pH. Before pH measurement, the electrode was calibrated by using standard pH values 4 and 7. For each liquid sample, the electrode was immersed in the liquid and the pH value was read on the pH meter. For each liquid sample, three values were read and their averages were calculated. After the measurement, the sensor was removed from the liquid sample, washed in distilled water and dried with a paper towel. The same process was repeated for all other samples.

Microhardness measurement

The vickers microhardness measurement was carried out in a universal testing device (Shimadzu Micro Hardness Testers HMV-2, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) by applying a force of 50 g to the surfaces of the samples for 30 s. The projection of the pyramid the device and transferred to the computer screen. In fluorescent light, 50 Hz frequency and appropriate exposure, the image of the inverted pyramid image formed by the pyramid-shaped tip sticking in the sample surface was taken. The measurements were performed after the image was automatically improved on the computer. Three measurements were made from each sample before it was placed in the solution (basal) and at the end of the 130th day after it was removed from the solution. In total, 189 measurements were performed at each time interval. The methodology of the study is summarized in the flowchart (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0.(IBM Corp. Released 2017, Armonk, NY) package program, R 3.5.2 software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; http://r-project.org) and SAS Version 9.3 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) statistical package program. The distribution of microhardness and fluoride release variables were tested by ANOVA and Kolmogorov-Simirnov test, and the non-parametric tests were used since the data not show normal distribution. Kruskal-Wallis and then Dunn (with Bonferroni correction) test were applied in the analysis of fluoride release and microhardness data. All hypothesis tests were performed at the 0.05 significance level.

Discussion

The glass ionomer cement which has an anticariogenic effects with the fluoride is one of the most used materials in dentistry [6]. However, it is a water-sensitive material during the setting time [19]. The high-viscosity glass ionomer have improved mechanical properties, fast setting reaction compare to conventional glass ionomer cement [20]. However, they remain sensitive to moisture the during initial setting phase.For this reason, the application of a protective surface coating is recommended, as studies have shown it can enhance mechanical properties such as surface hardness [20]. In this study, the fluoride release and microhardness values of current restorative materials containing glass ionomer were determined. The conventional glass ionomer cement (Ketac Molar Easymix), high viscosity glass ionomer cement (EQUIA Forte Fil, ChemFil Rock) and their coated form were evaluated in the study. Base on the results of this study, the null hypothesis which states that the different glass ionomer cements and surface coating applications do not have a statistically significant effect on fluoride release and microhardness values was rejected.

The application of a protective surface coating is mandatory/recommended for many moisture sensitive glass ionomer cements [20]. Specifically, some evidence indicates that coating might have a positive effect if kept in place for only 2 h and then removed [21]. Another report highlighted that the coating reduces surface roughness, which can be clinically advantageous in minimizing plaque accumulation [22]. Some studies reported that the coating effects/increases the releasing and recharging capacities of fluoride [17, 20, 23]. The surface coat has been suggested to have no or negative effect on fluoride release and the nano filled surface coating agent has been reported to cause lower fluoride release than customized (petroleum jelly) coat [17, 20, 24]. In this study the coating caused a dramatic reduction in fluoride release in EQUIA Forte Fil and there was no difference for the coating agents.

The fluoride release is affected by factors such as the composition of the glass ionomer cement and the kinetics of the setting reaction [25]. Some studies have reported that the highest fluoride release was observed in EQUIA Forte Fil similar to this study [26, 27]. The other studies obtained that the least fluoride release was detected in ChemFil Rock material in line with the findings of this study [28, 29]. In one study, Ketac Molar Easymix had better fluoride release values than EQUIA Forte Fil contrary to this study [28].

The mechanical tests have an important role for predicting the clinical performance of a material [30]. The ions released from the material may cause a surface deformation of the restoration over time [13]. Ketac Molar Easymix, EQUIA Forte Fil and ChemFil Rock showed above the threshold of Knoop microhardness values [31]. The microhardness value of EQUIA Forte Fil was compatible with the microhardness value of enamel [31]. Although the some studies observed similarity between the microhardness values of ChemFil Rock and EQUIA Forte Fil [26, 32], the other studies reported that ChemFil Rock has lower microhardness than EQUIA Forte Fil [33, 34]. In this study, there was a decrease was found in the microhardness values of EQUIA Forte Fil/control and ChemFil Rock/control groups between the baseline and 130th day. This result was thought to be due to the fact that no coating agent was applied to the surface. The ion transfer from the material to the solution was caused the alteration of the structure of materials in control group. Moreover, Ketac Molar Easymix has been reported to have higher hardness values than EQUIA Forte Fil and ChemFil Rock similar to the control groups of this study [35].

The moisture sensitivity problem in glass ionomer cements can be overcome by surface coating on the material. The coating provides to limit abrasive wear and early fractures during the maturation of glass ionomer cement until it reaches the highest strength level [33]. The mechanical properties are maintained as long as the coating is on the restoration [22]. Some studies have reported that the application of surface coating to glass ionomer cements improved their mechanical properties such as surface hardness [33, 36]. In another study, the materials have not been reported to gain extra strength when coated [21]. The surface coating gave rise to a blurred image for evaluation of microhardness values in this study. The petroleum jelly caused a more muddy appearance than coat on the surface. For this reason, the microhardness measurement of petroleum jell groups were challenging and the current findings may not perfectly reflect the real microhardness values of tested materials. Therefore, the microhardness values of materials were evaluated within control groups. However, within the coat groups, there was an increase in the microhardness values of EQUIA Forte Fil and Ketac Molar Easymix groups. The coating application was considered to preserve the structure of the tested materials.

This study has limitations such as the inability to measure the microhardness values of surface-coated materials accurately. The presence of the coating agent after application was not evaluated within the scope of this study. However, it is possible that the measured values were influenced by the presence or degradation of the coating. Under clinical conditions, the long-term presence of the coating agent is uncertain. Factors such as masticatory forces, saliva, and pH changes may affect the integrity of the coating over time. Among these factors, chewing forces may have a particularly significant impact on the persistence of the coating. Evaluating the surface characteristics of the specimens during the measurement period could provide supportive data for interpreting both fluoride release and microhardness values. This should be addressed in future studies.

There are many other test parameters including compressive strenght, diametral tensile strength, flexural strenghth, fracture toughness, surface roughness, microleakage and acid erosion for evaluating the phsiycal propeties of the restorative materials. In addition, the tested materials are exposed to different factors such as masticatory force, brushing force and changes in temperature and pH in oral conditions. Therefore, the obtained data do not fully reflect clinical situations. Further in vitro studies simulating oral conditions and clinical studies are required to support the findings.

Conclusion

Based on the findings of this study, the surface treatments seem to reduce fluoride release from the tested materials. The results suggest that EQUIA Forte Fil represents a significant improvement in terms of fluoride release and vickers hardness. The surface coating limits the release of fluoride and reduces the deformation in the structure of the material and microhardness change. The limitation of fluoride release may also limit the remineralisation and anticariogenic effect of fluoride. These should be taken into consideration when selecting materials with coated/uncoated form for the treatment plan.
