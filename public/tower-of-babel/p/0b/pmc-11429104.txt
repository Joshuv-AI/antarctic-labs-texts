Establishment of a patient and public involvement and engagement group to support clinical trials in Pakistan: Initial lessons learned
Authors: Timo Tolppa, Arishay Hussaini, Nikhat Ahmed, Arjen M Dondorp, Shehla Farooq, Monaza Khan, Adnan Masood, Srinivas Murthy, Saima Saleem, Zahyd Shuja, Shahnaz Zaman, Madiha Hashmi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient and public involvement and engagement (PPIE) in clinical trials is increasingly recognized as vital for ensuring research relevance and accessibility. Despite its proven benefits, PPIE remains limited, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, and more examples of effective strategies for involvement are needed. This commentary outlines the establishment of a PPIE group for clinical trials in a lower-middle-income country setting with limited research infrastructure.Main BodyWe established Pakistan’s first ever PPIE group for clinical trials within a new clinical trials unit at Ziauddin University in Karachi. The objectives of our project were focused on group formation, redesign of informed consent documents for trials, and dissemination of trial results to the public. Recruitment strategies involved referrals from clinicians and existing collaborators as well as engagement at public events, distribution of advertising leaflets and social media posts. Ten potential members were selected based on motivation, commitment and ability to contribute critically, with six members continuing their involvement long-term. An existing tool designed to establish the access needs of public partners was adapted to our project to help us document and account for members’ expectations and support requirements. The process of using the tool enabled deep engagement, clarified roles, and fostered trust between coordinators and group members. Patient and public members gained confidence about the legitimacy of the project and felt more comfortable participating in the first group meeting. Lessons learned emphasize the importance of wide-ranging engagement efforts and transparent discussions about expectations to build effective collaborative relationships.ConclusionOur experience demonstrates the feasibility of establishing a PPIE group for clinical trials in Pakistan and highlights strong public interest for research involvement. The use of a formal tool to document needs, prior experiences and expectations encouraged relationship-building and helped coordinators make relevant accommodations for members. Background

The active involvement of patients and the public in decisions about the design, implementation, conduct and dissemination of randomized controlled trials is increasingly acknowledged to be essential in ensuring accessibility, acceptability and relevance of this form of research for the populations they are intended to serve [1–4]. Aside from ethical and moral imperatives, studies to date have shown the benefits of patient involvement to trial quality and efficiency as it leads to the selection of more relevant outcome measures and improves enrollment rates [5–8]. However, even in high research output countries where patient and public involvement is well-established with support from key organizations, including INVOLVE in the United Kingdom, support units for the Strategy for Patient-Oriented Research in Canada, and Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Institute (PCORI) in the United States, direct involvement in trial design and conduct continues to be limited [9–13].

Patient and public involvement and engagement (PPIE) groups are one of many ways for researchers and trial teams to enable public involvement in trials [5, 14, 15]. Existence of PPIE groups with a broad remit within trial groups, organizations or networks (i.e. not confined to one specific project) could facilitate early patient and public involvement in all aspects of a trial by making resources and expertise easily accessible [16, 17]. Unfortunately, this practice is limited in low- and middle-income countries and little is known about setting up PPIE groups in resource-limited contexts from the perspectives of patients, public members and researchers [18]. More broadly, there is a dearth of evaluations on involvement activities, case studies of good practice as well as published accounts on the effectiveness of different tools and approaches to effective partnership [19–21]. Such operational information and advice is key to make PPIE groups a reality and to overcome commonly faced challenges in involvement work, such as tokenism, recruitment, retention of diverse members and organization of meetings [5, 7, 10, 22, 23].

Over the past year, our team established a PPIE group at a newly accredited clinical trials unit at Ziauddin University in Karachi, Pakistan. This active partnership with patients and members of the public seeks to support the design, implementation and dissemination of high-quality patient-centered trials as well as ensure that trial participation is equitable and trial outputs are meaningful to patients and the public in Karachi. While one other health-related PPIE initiative in Pakistan was set up for a feasibility study [24], as far as we are aware, our group is the first to bring trialists together with patients and the public to work on interventional clinical trials. Our commentary aims to: (1) describe the establishment of this first-ever PPIE group in Pakistan to support the implementation of clinical trials; and (2) share our experience of using a tool to support effective engagement within the initial stages of our PPIE group. It contributes to the wider involvement literature by demonstrating the feasibility of establishing such a PPIE group in a lower-middle-income country setting with limited research infrastructure and outlines practical lessons learned about effective approaches to enabling engagement in such contexts.

Establishment of a PPIE group for clinical trials in Pakistan

Our public involvement initiative was created in the context of the opening of a new clinical trials unit at our partner institution, increasing involvement in local and international trials, and recognition for the need to ensure acceptability and relevance of research studies, particularly ones designed in high-income countries, to the local community in Karachi, and identify barriers and solutions to participation. A project team consisting of three coordinators (Table 1), a local lead (MH) and a steering committee with senior investigators (AD, SM) was established in late 2022. Following the acquisition of seed funding from Mahidol Oxford University Tropical Medicine Research Unit, the PPIE project was officially launched in June 2023. The specific objectives for the first 15 months of our project, proposed by the coordinators and later confirmed by the PPIE group, were three-fold: (1) to establish a PPIE group consisting of patients, their families, carers, community leaders and the public; (2) to create research materials in a relevant format to support the informed consent processes for two ongoing trials and (3) to disseminate published trial results to patients and the public.

Recruitment of patient and public members

Members for the PPIE group were identified and recruited over six months through physician referral, professional contacts (e.g. partner organizations), public engagement events, advertising leaflets and by directly approaching trial participants, patients and families attending our partner hospital; all strategies previously employed by others [25, 26]. Various groups were approached via email to ask for their support to reach specific populations, including for example a national non-governmental organization working to promote mental health and the faculty of nursing and midwifery at our partner institution who work directly with disadvantaged populations. A commonly cited recruitment strategy of contacting patient-led committees, charities or advocacy groups was not possible as no such groups exist locally [25–27]. While our project team did not have any strict eligibility criteria for choosing our PPIE members, we strived for diversity in terms of sex, ethnicity, educational background and attitudes towards research, and assessed whether potential members had a few key characteristics that we felt were important, including motivation, interest and confidence to voice opinions. These characteristics have been previously identified as crucial indicators of PPIE members’ ability to effectively participate in discussions, co-lead the group and sustain their involvement [25, 26, 28]. Unlike some prior suggestions, we did not feel that previous research engagement experience, trial participation or high educational attainment were qualities that our PPIE members needed to possess [25]. Not only would these requirements have significantly impacted group diversity, but we also felt strongly that it was our responsibility to make PPIE in research accessible to all and reduce bias inherent in PPIE groups. However, while not a strict selection criterion, we did view prior experience of community engagement in some capacity as a desirable quality. Potential members had an informal discussion with one of the coordinators (AH) to understand motivations for joining the PPIE group and confirm that individuals could commit the time required to attend meetings every 6 weeks. The potential member and coordinator jointly made a decision about inclusion in the group.

No consensus exists about the optimal group size for PPIE in research, though several authors have made suggestions based on their experiences [28–30]. Following this advice and pragmatic considerations, we decided to create a group of five to ten members. Consideration was given to the fact that the group needed to be large enough to include a wide range of perspectives yet small enough to encourage meaningful engagement in a comfortable environment where effective relationships could be developed. Previous case reports have noted that three members is not sufficient and more individuals are needed to prevent potential negative impacts of dropouts [28]. Ultimately, we were able to identify ten PPIE members aged between 33 and 59 (Table 2), of which four unfortunately had to leave after initially joining the group. Three did not have sufficient time to dedicate to the project due to changed personal or professional commitments and one sadly passed away. All members were working full-time at the start of the PPIE project and had some prior experience of non-research related community engagement, while one had been a participant of trials themselves. Negative experiences of healthcare served as a rationale for two members to join the group. Four members were sceptical about clinical trials, questioning their applicability and relevance in Pakistan, though no one opposed their conduct.

Preparation ahead of the first meeting

From the outset, we felt it was important for our project to be rooted in existing theoretical frameworks and cumulative involvement experiences reported in the literature. As such, we conducted an informal environmental scan prior to the first meeting with the PPIE group to identify best practices, guidelines, tools and resources to support the work of our group. Several existing engagement and involvement frameworks (e.g. Patient Engagement in Research framework, Public Involvement Impact Assessment Framework, the Involvement Matrix, PCORI Engagement Rubric) were reviewed in conjunction with published experiences and evaluations of PPIE groups and initiatives [15, 22, 25, 28, 31–35]. This led us to use the National Institute for Health Research INVOLVE definition of public involvement and follow the United Kingdom standards for public involvement in research, in the absence of locally relevant definitions and frameworks [36, 37]. Specifically, we conceptualize involvement in our endeavor as trials being carried out ‘with’ or ‘by’ members of the public rather than ‘to’, ‘about’ or ‘for’ them [37]. The environmental scan additionally helped us establish our training plan, initial draft of our terms of reference and process for researchers to request input from our group (Additional file 1) as well as refine our overall project and evaluation plans.

A common thread throughout all reviewed sources included the need to provide patient and public partners individually-tailored support to enable engagement and clarity regarding expectations [15, 28]. Indeed, this has been identified as one of the main strategies to enable effective PPIE [20]. As such, we sought to understand the access and support needs as well as expectations of our group members prior to the first meeting, and chose to do so by using the ‘My Involvement Profile’ created by ‘Shaping Our Lives’, a United Kingdom non-profit led by disabled people and service users [38]. This freely available template was chosen due to its comprehensiveness, ease of use and inclusion of questions about interests and skills alongside those on access and support. Other tools lacked structured questions about the background of our members, which we felt were important to understand and document to build meaningful relationships.

‘My Involvement Profile’: experience of use

Modifications

The original’My Involvement Profile’ was modified (see Additional file 2) to the needs of our project. An entire section asking about expectations was added in order to tailor our approach to the expectations of our group, and later compare the experiences of our members with their initial expectations. Other additions included a project-specific introduction, context-specific response options as well as questions about demographics, previous experiences and opinions regarding trials. Members were also asked about their preferences regarding methods of communication, their personal information and images being used in marketing materials. Questions regarding travel and overnight accommodation were removed in view of all activities taking place locally.

Process

The ‘My Involvement Profile’ for each PPIE member was completed during a one-hour in-person meeting attended by two coordinators (AH, TT) at the preferred time and location (i.e. home, cafe, office) of the member. Members were reimbursed for their travel. At the beginning of the meeting, coordinators shared their interests and reasons for conducting this work, as well as background on the project and broader research group. Afterwards, all fields of the ‘My Involvement Profile’ were completed together with the PPIE member on a laptop. The coordinators later shared the final typed ‘My Involvement Profile’ in the member’s preferred language for review with the finalized Profile added to a project-specific password-protected shared drive accessible only to the coordinators. The original ‘My Involvement Profile’ was designed as a document to be sent to participants for completion electronically or on paper. Our approach was chosen to enable two-way conversations about expectations and needs, and limit the burden on members to complete paperwork.

Coordinator perspectives

The two PPIE coordinators involved in the meetings to complete the ‘My Involvement Profile’ both felt that the goal of determining the needs and expectations of members was achieved, and that the completion of the profiles contributed to the success of the PPIE group. The process managed to assuage concerns about the commitment and understanding of the PPIE members regarding the initiative, and provided confidence to the coordinators, also new to public engagement, about the direction of the project. The in-depth conversations built the foundations of a strong relationship and trust between members and coordinators by identifying common values, interests and goals. In some instances, the exercise of completing the ‘My Involvement Profile’ helped us screen out individuals who were not a good fit for our PPIE group before they joined. Reasons why individuals were not suitable included lack of time for engagement and motivations not consistent with the values of our group. Practically, the completion of the ‘My Involvement Profile’ ensured that the coordinators built in prayer and smoking breaks to the agenda, advised catering services about allergens, and provided all written materials in both Urdu and English as some would have found the Urdu script challenging. These accommodations would not have been in place without the meetings as they were not anticipated. Coordinators felt that the process ensured better engagement during the first meeting, as members came brimming with ideas and thoughts they had been mulling over since completing the ‘My Involvement Profile.’ Beyond the first meeting, the completed ‘My Involvement Profiles’ have been used to tailor further training sessions to the existing skills, competencies and experiences of the members. As an added benefit, finding out about the skills, experiences and goals of the PPIE members was hugely inspirational for the coordinators and reaffirmed their commitment to the PPIE initiative. The only challenge identified by the coordinators about the completion of the ‘My Involvement Profile’ was the time needed to plan, conduct and document the discussions. However, the effort was considered to be minimal compared to efforts that would have been needed if the benefits of this process were not realized.

Patient and public member perspectives

Three past and all six current patient and public members were asked whether the completion of the ‘My Involvement Profile’ together with the coordinators was a valuable use of their time prior to attending the first group meeting and whether the exercise impacted how they felt before or during the meeting. All but one individual felt it was a good use of their time, primarily because it clarified the purpose of the PPIE initiative and their own role within the group. One member said that they would have dropped out of the group without having the opportunity to learn more about the initiative and expectations. The detailed process of completing the ‘My Involvement Profile’ legitimized the initiative to several members and provided reassurance that “this is a properly organized social group”, as one person put it. Discussions during the meeting also encouraged members to reflect on their role and contribution to the group in a more critical manner. One past PPIE member we spoke to did not find the completion process a valuable use of their time as it did not provide any additional information. This individual did not end up joining any meetings in the end, and thus their perspective was likely influenced by a lack of interest in the PPIE initiative. Over half of the members felt that the completion of the ‘My Involvement Profile’ positively influenced their participation in the first PPIE group meeting, as they felt more prepared, confident and had better clarity about their role. Meeting the coordinators in-person ahead of the first meeting also made some feel more comfortable participating in future meetings as it reduced the impact of perceived power imbalances. No one expressed that the process negatively affected their participation. Members felt that the ‘My Involvement Profile’ was comprehensive and did not require further modifications.

Lessons learned

Our PPIE group is, to the best of our knowledge, the first-ever patient and public involvement initiative in Pakistan created to support the implementation of clinical trials. Engagement with patients, members of the public and a range of healthcare professionals in the process of establishing this group has revealed broad interest and excitement for the inclusion of the public voice in health research. The early stages of this PPIE project have highlighted two overarching lessons: (1) wide-ranging engagement with a variety of individuals and groups supported by sufficient resources is crucial for recruitment and initial relationship-building, and (2) discussing and documenting expectations prospectively promotes the development of effective collaborative relationships and addresses asymmetric power relationships. These lessons have been summarized in a plain Urdu video (Additional file 3).

Similarly to previous case studies on PPIE, we found the process of establishing the group, recruiting members and completing the ‘My Involvement Profile’ time-consuming and resource-intensive [28]. Our coordinators carried out ten public presentations, published nearly 40 social media posts, reached out to over 30 clinicians as well as staff representing different organizations, and engaged with over 50 patients, trial participants and family members. However, the effort has paid off in many ways and has been essential to the success of the initiative, including the identification of ten inspirational and committed PPIE members. The engagement efforts have allowed us to benefit from the advice, experiences and ideas of a wide variety of collaborators and establish new partnerships. For instance, staff at the Centre of Biomedical Ethics and Culture in Karachi were excited about the PPIE initiative and graciously agreed to carry out an introductory workshop on research ethics to our PPIE members free of charge. Ultimately, one should not embark on the journey of PPIE without being willing to put in the energy to nurture strong mutually-respectful collaborations. Adequate resources, time and dedicated staff are required for this to be achieved, including funding that is notoriously difficult to obtain for operational aspects of PPIE [14, 25]. Importantly, prior local work or experience in PPIE was not necessary to successfully establish a group under the leadership of junior members of the research team.

The focus on initial relationship-building and discussions about expectations corresponds well to commonly reported solutions to overcome identified barriers and prevent challenges in public involvement for research [5, 25, 39]. Namely, the use of the ‘My Involvement Profile’ allowed for us to build reciprocal deep relationships amongst coordinators and group members as well as developing clear expectations, which were formally documented. One of our key concerns at the start of the initiative was the impact of the coordinators on the ability of PPIE group members to effectively and honestly contribute to discussions. One of the coordinators is a retired professor and the two others are doctors, with one being a White European male unable to speak more than basic Urdu. In the paternalistic and hierarchical culture of Pakistan where doctors and White Europeans are held in high regard, we were concerned that our characteristics would create perceived asymmetric power relations hindering effective engagement at the start of the initiative. To our surprise, this has not occurred to the best of our knowledge. We attribute this to our selection criteria (i.e. individuals capable of voicing their opinions), as well as the time we spent developing relationships and trust with our group members during the meetings to complete the ‘My Involvement Profile.’

Our experiences regarding the operationalization of PPIE are shared with the acknowledgement that they do not contribute to the much-needed evidence on the impact of public involvement [5, 17]. A formal longitudinal evaluation of our PPIE group has been designed into our project plan and is currently underway. However, public involvement in research is quickly becoming the gold standard, thereby necessitating practical tools and approaches to support researchers in achieving effective engagement across all settings. We believe this account of our initial experiences constitutes a small step forward in attaining this goal. We also recognize that operationalizing PPIE is just the beginning. Post-establishment, our group has started to work with researchers, ethicists and regulators to improve informed consent processes, and with community leaders and filmmakers to educate the public about their research rights. We aim to share further lessons learned as we develop our role in the research ecosystem in Pakistan and find the best ways to advance the voice of the public in all aspects of clinical trials.

Conclusions

Our experience demonstrates the feasibility of establishing PPIE in Pakistan. In fact, we have found there to be a great deal of interest from patients, their families and members of the public to engage in research. We hope our experience encourages, inspires and supports others to pursue the worthy goal of patient and public involvement in all types of clinical research in low-, middle- and high-income countries alike. Both the coordinators and public members of our group felt that the completion of the ‘My Involvement Profile’ before the first formal group meeting was a good use of time to enable the provision of individually-tailored support and clarity regarding expectations. The amended ‘My Involvement Profile’ allowed us to build legitimacy, trust and a foundation for meaningful relationships with our members through in-depth discussions. More than the tool, we believe these benefits were achieved through our approach which has emphasized broad-ranging engagement, commitment of significant time and resources to relationship-building and use of the ‘My Involvement Profile’ tool as a vehicle to have meaningful conversations with our members. Our group will continue to identify and share such effective strategies with the hope of promoting meaningful involvement of patients and the public in clinical trials.
