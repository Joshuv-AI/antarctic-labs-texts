Background

Growing teratoma syndrome (GTS) is a rare clinical condition that arises after chemotherapeutic treatment for a non-seminomatous germ cell tumor (NSGCT). It is characterized by a paradoxical expanding tumor mass during or after chemotherapy despite normalization of tumor markers, with an entirely mature teratoma histological component upon resection. [1–3]. This phenomenon was first brought into light in 1977 by DiSaia et al. who described a chemotherapy induced conversion of immature teratoma to mature elements in three patients with malignant ovarian germ cell tumors. [1].

The incidence of GTS is estimated to be around 1.9 to 7.6% in NSGCTs of the testis. [4]. The prevalence of ovarian GTS is unknown, though some case series have reported the incidence rate to be ranging between 12 and 20%. [5–7]. Nguyen et al. reported a crude incidence of 40% for developing GTS for those who received adjuvant chemotherapy for ovarian immature teratoma.[8]. An increased awareness is probably contributing to the increased incidence.

GTS is unresponsive to both chemotherapy and radiotherapy making complete surgical resection the sole treatment option. [3, 9, 10].

Here we present two patients diagnosed with malignant germ cell tumors of the ovary, who experienced paradoxical increase in tumor size with significant decline in tumor markers during chemotherapy.

Case presentation

Case 1: A 10-year-old Black African female was referred to us with history of progressive abdominal distension for 3 months associated with symptoms of pain and early satiety. A computed tomography (CT) imaging of the chest and abdomen showed a tumor arising from the pelvis between the rectum and urinary bladder with no metastatic lung nodules. On admission, the patient had markedly elevated serum tumor markers: alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) 20,000 ng/ml, beta-human chorionic gonadotropin (B-HCG) 1022 IU/L. Based on the clinical and imaging findings, a malignant ovarian germ cell tumor was diagnosed. Chemotherapy with JEB (carboplatin 600 mg/m2, etoposide 120 mg/m2, bleomycin 15 mg/m2) was initiated. She received 3 cycles before an interim assessment was undertaken for tumor resectability. Repeat computed tomography (CT) scan showed a progressive tumor compared to the diagnostic imaging studies (Fig. 1). The tumor markers had declined significantly (Table 1). At this point the patient was also presenting with symptoms of intra-abdominal tumor compression with pain and discomfort. Surgery was the only viable option. Right oophorectomy was done successfully (Fig. 2). Tissue sent for histopathology revealed complete teratomatous features with multiple lineages represented including skin, cartilage, bone, respiratory epithelium and smooth muscle (Fig. 3). Based on the histological findings of mature teratoma in a patient with enlarging tumor mass during chemotherapy despite declined tumor markers, a diagnosis of ovarian GTS was made. Post operatively the patient continued with adjuvant chemotherapy (carboplatin 600 mg/m2, etoposide 120 mg/m2, bleomycin 15 mg/m2) to complete 6 cycles. Five months following therapy completion, she is with normal tumor markers and no evidence of disease.

Case 2: A 12-year-old Black African female referred to our facility two months after a laparotomy for an intra-abdominal mass. Right oophorectomy was done leaving behind residual adhering mesenteric tumors. Histopathology revealed malignant germ cell tumor of the ovary, Fédération Internationale de Gynécologie et d’Obstétrique (FIGO) stage IIIB. By the time she was admitted at our facility, the symptoms of abdominal swelling had recurred with significantly elevated tumor markers; AFP 28,393ng/ml, B-HCG 32.7 IU/L. She received 4 cycles of JEB (carboplatin 600mg/m2, etoposide 120mg/m2, bleomycin 15mg/m2). On clinical assessment the abdominal distension was increasing and on CT imaging there was significant disease progression (Fig. 4). The initial tumor board consensus was to switch to a 2nd line chemotherapy regimen, VeIP (vinblastine 3mg/m2, ifosfamide 1500mg/m2, cisplatin 20mg/m2). After receiving 2 cycles of VeIP it was noted that the tumor was still growing in size causing abdominal compression syndrome (Fig. 5). The patient was experiencing abdominal pain and discomfort with significant weight loss. Upon review of the tumor markers, they were noted to have almost normalized (Table 2). At this point the board consensus shifted in favor of ovarian GTS. The patient was taken to theater where wide tumor mass excision was performed. Pathology revealed a complete mature teratoma histology (Fig. 6). She completed therapy with two additional cycles of JEB in January 2025. She is without disease and with normal tumor markers.

Discussion

The term growing teratoma syndrome as a separate clinic entity was first put forth by Logothetis et al. in 1982. Three specific criteria, namely (1) the enlarging tumor mass during or after chemotherapy; (2) normalization of previous raised tumor markers; and (3) a pure teratoma histology post-resection, have been used to define GTS. [2]. Our two cases had the classical presentation except for the complete normalization of tumor markers. In both cases the AFP levels reduced significantly from the baseline but were not normalized by the time of tumor resection. In one case review of GTS among intracranial germ cell tumors, four of the six cases had elevated AFP despite the absence of immature teratoma or yolk sac tumor (YST) in resected specimen. [11]. Lorusso et al. also reported a case of immature ovarian teratoma which received four courses of BEP chemotherapy post tumor resection. All other tumor markers normalized except for AFP. A CT scan revealed liver masses which harbored a mature teratoma component upon resection. [12]. After surgery the AFP levels normalized in both of our cases. This could be explained by the possibility of a small residual yolk sac element which persisted in a tumor with overwhelming mature teratoma component post chemotherapy.

The most common site for GTS is the retroperitoneum, but has also been described in other sites including the lungs, mediastinum, supraclavicular lymph nodes, inguinal lymph nodes, forearm, brain, mesentery and liver. [6, 13].

Three prevailing hypotheses have been put forth to explain the etiology of GTS: (1) chemotherapy destroys only the immature malignant cells leaving behind the benign mature teratoma component which continues to grow causing GTS. [9, 14, 15]. (2) Chemotherapy alters the cell kinetics transforming a totipotent malignant germ cell into a benign mature teratoma which is insensitive to chemotherapy. [1]. (3) Malignant cells may spontaneously differentiate into benign tissue, with chemotherapy implicated in extending the course of the disease to allow “spontaneous evolution” to occur. [16].

Andre et al. in their study series suggested that the presence of mature teratoma elements in the primary tumor, incomplete resection leaving behind residual tumor and metastatic disease unresponsive to chemotherapy as predicting factors for the eventual development of GTS. [9, 17]. The second case in our report underwent initial surgery which left behind a residual tumor probably leading subsequently to the development of GTS.

Both of our patients had symptoms of abdominal compression syndrome at some point due to enlarging tumor masses. Depending on the location, GTS can cause compression of the surrounding organs causing symptoms associated with pain, bowel obstruction, renal insufficiency and tissue necrosis. [18, 19].

Imaging studies can assist in the diagnosis of GTS. Features on CT scan which are suggestive of GTS include increased mass density, well circumscribed margins, and the onset of internal calcifications with cystic changes and fatty areas. [20, 21].

Complete surgical resection of mature teratoma is currently the only curative treatment for GTS. [9, 10]. The overall outcome for treated GTS is generally favorable if no residual mass is left behind. Spiess et al. reported a median 5-year survival rate of 89%. [22]. Aggressive surgery might be justified when dealing with bulky disease as local recurrence is associated with incomplete resection. [23].

GTS has a small but actual risk of evolving into malignancy. The mature teratoma can transform into aggressive form of cancer like sarcoma, squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma. [9, 24]. Despite successful surgery, ongoing surveillance is required to ensure early detection of malignant changes prompting timely intervention.

Post-surgical resection surveillance for GTS patients relies mainly on physical examination and imaging. Regular follow-up with CT scans every six months during the first year is recommended, then yearly for at least the next four years. Tumor marker surveillance is warranted if markers were elevated at initial diagnosis, to monitor for recurrence of immature teratoma. [25, 26].

Conclusion

We have very few cases of GTS reported in scientific literature from sub-Saharan Africa. GTS is more often than not mistaken for disease progression or recurrence. High index of suspicion for a GTS should be present when an abdominal mass is progressive during or after chemotherapeutic treatment for ovarian immature teratoma despite declined tumor markers. Complete tumor resection is currently the gold standard treatment for GTS.
