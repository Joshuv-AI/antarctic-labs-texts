Refractory “status dyskineticus” in a child with post-herpes simplex virus 1 N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor encephalitis: a case report

Background

Relapsing neurological symptoms following herpes simplex virus 1 (HSV-1) encephalitis are often attributed to immune-mediated mechanisms, including anti–N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) encephalitis. Refractory status dyskineticus (RSD), a severe subset of status dystonicus characterized by a hyperkinetic movement disorder phenotype alongside dystonic features, represents a rare but serious manifestation of autoimmune encephalitis. The management of encephalitis-associated movement disorders in the neurocritical care remains challenging.

We describe the case of a 2-year-old male with RSD secondary to post-HSV-1 anti-NMDAR encephalitis, emphasizing the therapeutic challenges and the role of stepwise escalation in immunotherapy.

Case presentation

A previously healthy 2-year-old male was admitted to a regional hospital with seizures, fever, and progressive encephalopathy. Brain MRI revealed a meningoencephalitis pattern with left temporomesial lobe involvement and bilateral leptomeningeal enhancement, suggestive of HSV-1 meningoencephalitis. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis revealed pleocytosis and elevated protein, and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) confirmed HSV-1 in the CSF. Intravenous (IV) acyclovir was initiated, leading to seizure control and partial recovery of ambulation over the following weeks.

One month later, the patient developed again encephalopathy, with vomiting, behavioral regression, drowsiness, and dyskinetic movements. He was readmitted and transferred to our tertiary care center for further evaluation. Clinical examination revealed severe orofacial dyskinesia, purposeless limb movements, and the inability to walk or engage with his environment (an additional movie file shows this in more detail [see Additional file 1]). No HSV reactivation was detected in the CSF. Electroencephalography (EEG) showed a disorganized background activity with diffuse epileptiform abnormalities, which partially improved with clobazam, although without noticeable clinical effect. No electrographic seizures were recorded during dyskinetic status. Repeat brain MRI demonstrated extensive clastic lesions involving both hemispheres (Fig. 1). Acyclovir was reintroduced, and IV immunoglobulin (2 g/kg) therapy and high-dose IV methylprednisolone (30 mg/kg/day) were administered due to suspected autoimmune encephalitis following HSV-1 infection. CSF and serum samples tested positive for NMDAR antibodies. Despite first-line immunotherapy, the movement disorder progressed to severe dystonia, opisthotonus, and choreoathetoid movements, eventually leading to ballismus (an additional movie file shows this in more detail [see Additional file 2]). To exclude alternative causes, a metabolic/genetic workup including testing for Wilson’s disease was performed and returned negative. Due to worsening refractory status dyskineticus, complicated by hyperthermia and hypercapnia, the patient was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) and required mechanical ventilation. Following the consensus recommendations of Vogt et al. (2024), pharmacologic management was intensified, including clonidine, benzodiazepines, anticholinergics, baclofen, gabapentin, anesthetics, and dopaminergic agents (levodopa) for RSD control. Antipsychotics (olanzapine, promazine, and high-dose aripiprazole at 20 mg/day) were also introduced to address prominent agitation. Ten days after first-line immunotherapy, a second-line regimen with rituximab and rapamycin commenced. Four weekly doses of rituximab (375 mg/m²) were administered, but no clinical improvement was observed. At that point, after four weeks, one cycle of therapy with bortezomib was started, for a total of four doses, which led to gradual resolution of SD and discharge from the ICU in spontaneous ventilation one month later. Twelve weeks after the autoimmune encephalitis onset, follow-up CSF and serum samples tested negative for NMDAR antibodies (Fig. 2). The patient continued to show progressive recovery, regaining the cognitive and motor skills achieved following viral encephalitis. At 6-month follow-up, the patient remained on rapamycin, which is planned to be continued for at least two years. He demonstrated ongoing neurological recovery, with no reported relapses or additional complications, suggesting a sustained response to the immunomodulatory regimen.

Discussion and conclusions

Herpes simplex virus 1 encephalitis (HSE) is the most common form of sporadic viral encephalitis and represents a life-threatening condition, with a first peak of incidence in childhood [1].

Recurrence of neurological symptoms following initial recovery is increasingly recognized as being driven by immune-mediated mechanisms, including the production of antibodies targeting neuronal surface antigens such as the GluN1 subunit of the NMDAR [2] and dopamine-2 receptor (D2R) [3]. Post-HSE autoimmune encephalitis frequently presents with a constellation of neurological and psychiatric features, including hyperkinetic movement disorders, seizures, autonomic dysregulation, developmental regression, and behavioral changes [4]. In our patient, brain MRI performed during the acute phase revealed extensive malacic and gliotic changes, predominantly involving the temporal and perinsular regions, consistent with post-herpetic necrotic injury (Fig. 1). These findings are aligned with those reported by Armangué et al. (2018), who observed that patients developing autoimmune encephalitis after HSV1 encephalitis were more likely to exhibit necrosis with cystic lesions on follow-up MRI compared to those who did not [2]. Anti-D2R antibodies were not assessed in our case due to the prompt identification of NMDAR antibody positivity. Nevertheless, their potential role in post-HSV1 hyperkinetic relapses is increasingly recognized, as described by Mohammad et al. [3].

The abrupt onset of hyperkinetic manifestations in autoimmune encephalitis has broadened the spectrum of the well-described status dystonicus, giving rise to a subset known as status dyskineticus (SD) [5], even if a clear definition and categorization of this entity is still lacking [6]. Status dystonicus is a medical emergency characterized by sustained, severe dystonic movements, with the presence of one or more end-organ metabolic decompensations (hyperthermia, major electrolyte abnormalities, renal failure, myoglobinuria, or elevated serum CK levels) [7]. In NMDAR encephalitis, the predominance of a hyperkinetic movement disorder phenotype alongside dystonic features supports the more precise use of the term “status dyskineticus” to describe this life-threatening condition. Children with status dystonicus or status dyskineticus often require intensive care, and up to 12.5% of severe cases result in death [8]. In a cohort of 172 patients with NMDAR encephalitis, SD was reported in 8% of cases [9]. Although the precise pathophysiology remains unclear, NMDAR antibodies in the CSF may drive severe disinhibition of brainstem central pattern generators involving dopaminergic pathways or cause dysfunction in cortical, subcortical, and brainstem circuits, particularly the basal ganglia [10, 11]. Recent consensus guidelines emphasize the need for individualized management plans for dystonia and dyskinesia, including early initiation of movement disorder–targeted therapies and optimization of neurocritical care [12]. While deep brain stimulation (DBS) was not considered in our patient due to the favorable response to pharmacological treatment, DBS has been reported in the literature as a therapeutic option in refractory cases of SD associated with NMDAR encephalitis [9, 13, 14]. Managing SD in autoimmune encephalitis remains challenging. Given the underlying autoimmune nature of the disease, immunotherapy strategies are crucial. Second-line agents such as rituximab are commonly used for severe cases of NMDAR encephalitis [15]. When the response is inadequate, further escalation is necessary [16]. Bortezomib inhibits NF-κB activation and the ubiquitin–proteasome system, affecting the differentiation, survival, and function of several immune cells, with plasma cells being particularly susceptible to apoptotic cell death due to their high rate of antibody synthesis. Plasma cell– and antibody-mediated mechanisms are implicated in anti-NMDAR encephalitis, in which NMDAR antibodies induce internalization of the receptor from the neuronal surface [17]. Current consensus guidelines on NMDAR autoimmune encephalitis recommend prompt escalation to second-line agents such as rituximab if no improvement is seen within two weeks of initiating at least two first-line therapies. Further escalation may be considered for patients with severe and refractory disease after 1–3 months of second-line therapy, although specific guidance on third-line regimens remains lacking [15]. The combination of rituximab and bortezomib has been shown to effectively deplete both short-lived and long-lived plasma cells, as well as plasmablast precursors, which may contribute to the persistence of autoimmune inflammation [16]. Additionally, rapamycin supports immune regulation by targeting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, modulating the proliferation of B and T lymphocytes, particularly abnormal T cells, and enhancing regulatory T-cell expression. These effects have led to its effective use in treating autoimmune diseases [18].

In this case, the sequential use of rituximab followed by bortezomib and rapamycin, was crucial in achieving a good clinical outcome. Furthermore, the escalation of symptomatic therapies to manage refractory movement disorders, including a range of pharmacological agents, was also essential in reducing the severity of status dyskineticus.

This case highlights the importance of early recognition and aggressive treatment strategies in pediatric patients with autoimmune encephalitis. A comprehensive, multidisciplinary approach in neurocritical care, including prompt escalation and management of movement disorders, is key to improving outcomes and reducing long-term neurological morbidity in children with this complex and life-threatening condition.
