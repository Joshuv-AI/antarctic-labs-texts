To Develop and Implement a Preclinical Operative Dentistry Module to Enhance Class-II Cavity Preparation Training
Authors: Preet Kanwal Atwal, Gagandeep Kwatra, Ritu Jain, Kapil Krishan Dua, Shivani Khanna, Vivek Vardhan Gupta
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:A competent dental undergraduate at the preclinical level will ensure a confident transition into clinics and quality treatment for patients. Cavity preparation of a tooth is one of the key competencies in dentistry. A preclinical training module was introduced to enhance the skill training and improve students’ learning.Aim:This study aims to develop and implement a preclinical operative dentistry module to enhance Class-II cavity preparation training.Methodology:Feedback was taken from the 3rd year undergraduate batch regarding the preclinical operative dentistry training. Based on the feedback, a training module was developed and implemented for Class-II cavity preparation for 2nd year dental students. The three-stage intervention included a preparatory intervention with a Prebrief PowerPoint presentation, followed by a live demonstration of the Class-II cavity preparation and access to a video demonstration. In the third stage, formative assessments were conducted with the help of an assessment rubric. At the end, student and faculty perceptions were gathered using a feedback survey.Results:Most students (98%) were satisfied with the training. All the faculty strongly agreed that the module should be part of future training. Video demonstration and rubric-based assessments were testified by 93% of students as the most helpful element of the module. Major themes after thematic analysis were a better understanding of the procedure, enhanced visualization, the ability to identify areas for improvement, and boosted confidence for final exams.Conclusions:The preclinical training module successfully met the needs of the participating undergraduate students. Video demonstration proved a beneficial digital learning resource.

Introduction

Dentistry requires visualization, attention to detail and manual dexterity to achieve good restorative work. Skill training is an important part of dental training. Students often find cavity preparation in preclinical training an arduous task. Al-Saud’s study concluded that difficulty in learning and performing dental tasks like class II cavity preparation was associated with the complexity of the procedure.[1]

Conventional dental training incorporates demonstrations and cavity preparation training on typodont teeth mounted on Phantom heads. Without an appropriate instructional design, this may be insufficient, so, training should be continuously evaluated. The study aimed to identify the challenges with the current training method and incorporate innovative ways to improve the training.

Methodology

An educational intervention study was conducted in the department of conservative dentistry and endodontics. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee. (Ref: BMHR-IEC/Apprvl-R-Proj-Faimer/23–08–340/CDC). As this was a pilot study, the complete batch of forty 2nd year B.D.S students of preclinical operative dentistry classes were invited to participate. All participants gave their prior consent and an orientation was held before the training session. Three faculty members who were involved in the preclinical training of the students were also sensitized to the new module.

Feedback survey

The preclinical operative dentistry training at our dental college was evaluated by an online feedback survey using Google Forms. The 3rd year students (n = 31) who had recently completed their preclinical training participated. The survey revealed several challenges faced by the students during the training period [Figure 1].

Preclinical operative dentistry module preparation

A core faculty group consisting of the primary researcher, the head of the department, and two medical education experts from other departments was formed. Based on the feedback obtained from the 3rd year batch, a preclinical operative dentistry training module was developed. The study tools including a PreBrief PowerPoint (PPT) presentation on Class-II cavity preparation, an assessment rubric, and a 10 min 40 s-long video of Class-II cavity preparation, were developed. Three subject experts reviewed the modules for its content validation.

Intervention

A three-staged intervention was planned.

First or the preparatory stage

The students were shown a PreBrief PPT presentation (20 min) before the demonstration of the Class-II cavity preparation on a typodont jaw. The presentation covered the steps of Class II cavity preparation and the common errors in the procedure.

Second stage

The students were divided into four batches, of ten students each. The faculty demonstrated the steps for cavity preparation in these small batches immediately after the PPT presentation. A prerecorded video demonstration of class II cavity preparation was then shared with the students on their WhatsApp group. The students were guided to utilize the video for revision and enhance learning.

Third stage

One month after the intervention, formative assessments were conducted. Ten students were assessed in each session to ensure direct observation of the procedure by the trainer. At the same time, the rest of the students practiced cavity preparation on the phantom head. In a 2 h practical class, the first 60 min were dedicated to the small group of students undergoing formative assessment. Forty minutes were given for cavity preparation and the remaining 20 min were utilized for immediate, one-on-one feedback to the students. The students’ cavity preparation was graded using a validated assessment rubric and feedback was based on the scores obtained. The various elements of a prepared cavity to retain restoration and bear occlusal forces without fracture were incorporated in the rubric. Three rounds of formative assessments were done for each small group spaced throughout the year.

Data collection and analysis

A 10-item questionnaire was developed to evaluate students’ perceptions of the module. The questionnaire was prepared by the core group and validated by three experts. The first five questions had five-point Likert Scale options. The other five questions were open-ended. A five-item questionnaire with two open-ended and three questions with five-point Likert options was also developed to gather faculty responses to the module. The questionnaire was formed on Google Forms and the responses were collected at the end of the last formative assessment. The confidentiality of participants was maintained by keeping the responses to questionnaires anonymous. Descriptive analysis was done for the responses to the Likert scale questions. A thematic analysis was conducted for the responses to the open-ended questions.

Results

Student’s responses to the feedback survey

The feedback survey results showed that 77% of students found Class-II cavity preparation the most difficult part of preclinical operative dentistry training. Lack of visualization during a demonstration was identified as the biggest challenge. Other challenges in learning were: a lack of a sound cognitive base before the demonstration and overlooked detail due to the pace of the demonstration. About 65% of the students felt that one demonstration was insufficient to understand the Class-II cavity preparation [Figure 1].

Students’ and faculty perceptions of the preclinical module

The preclinical operative dentistry training module was considered satisfactory by most of the students (98%). Video demonstration and assessment rubrics were deemed very helpful during preclinical training by 93% of students [Figures 2 and 3]. The faculty involved in the training were satisfied and strongly agreed that the module be implemented in future preclinical sessions.

Discussion

The evaluation of the preclinical operative dentistry training at Christian Dental College Ludhiana brought to light the difficulties faced by the students during the training period. One of the challenges identified was an insufficient knowledge of the topic before the demonstration (13%). In response to an open-ended question a student noted, “Most of the students don’t know what is external and internal line angle, and what is clearance …how to bevel.” A good foundation of theory, especially one that correlates theory to practice, is a predecessor to any practical skill acquisition. A prebrief PPT presentation was introduced for the above reason. In our study, 85% of students found the PPT presentation before the demonstration to be helpful. The thematic analysis of open-ended questions regarding the usefulness of PPT presentation suggested audio-visual (AV) aids helped conceptualize and better understand the procedure. There is evidence to support that “Knowing How and Knowing Why” is important for the transfer of psychomotor skills during training.[2]

Live demonstration to transfer skills has remained the gold standard of skill training as it allows students to interact and ask questions as the procedure is being demonstrated.[3] However, in our study, the feedback identified that despite providing a demonstration, students sought additional sessions to understand the procedure. Another challenge recognized was poor visualization of the procedure in a demonstration done in a large group. Other investigators have reported similar issues.[4] Due to the tight timelines of academic schedules, repeated demonstrations for the same procedures are not feasible. The video demonstration was added to the module for the above reason. The developed module aimed to augment the demonstration without replacing it with a video demonstration. George et al. concluded that video demonstration is a good supplement to teaching clinical skills in large groups. In the above study, two main points that favored video demonstrations were flexible access and better AV features.[5] Iqbal et al. reported e-learning through video to enhance the learning of preclinical operative dentistry skills.[6] Although many studies have reported video demonstrated practical either superior or at par with traditional methods, combined use of traditional demonstration with video demonstrations has superior training outcomes compared to traditional methods alone.[78]

The students found the video resource very helpful (93%). One student felt the video to be the “best thing we would have asked for.” Another student said, “Far better than the demos we get in class as I could pause, resume, and replay it multiple times.” According to the codes derived from thematic analysis, the video demonstration facilitated revision, aided visualization of steps missed during the demonstration, and enhanced cavity preparation. Repeatedly viewing the video, the significance of a stepwise and systematic approach to making a Class-II cavity was reinforced. A review of video as a method of teaching psychomotor skills in nursing favored the use of videos as the teaching-learning method especially when shared via smartphones.[7] This easy access to learning resources favors repeated viewing. Another study highlighted the role of video demonstrations of cavity preparation in improving psychomotor skills by augmenting self-assessment.[9] In addition, procedural videos have been reported to decrease the stress and anxiety in preclinical trainees.[10]

A quality assessment propels learning to a higher level. Rubrics are known to remove the subjectivity of assessments and the feedback provided is precise, clear, and documented.[11] During the formative assessment test, the students were provided feedback based on the scores on the assessment rubric using the Pendleton model. The clearly defined criteria of the assessment rubric help the students develop an ability to self-assess their work. This was reflected by our results as students reported an ability to identify mistakes. Many studies have reported improved academic performance when self-assessment is performed.[1213] In addition, the inter-examiner agreement is also better in rubric-based assessment as compared to other methods like checklists, and glance and grades.[14]

A study reported an evident decay in performance due to a decrease in the retention of clinical skills following simulation training.[15] This study emphasizes the need for deliberate practice with feedback. In the developed training module, the immediate feedback during the formative assessments helped students improve their cavity preparation. In addition, during the formative assessments, the cavities were made within the allocated time, which helped them prepare for the final examination.

Studies have shown that a structured multimodal training module with a blended approach to learning is positively perceived by students.[1617] Our study findings are in agreement, with most of the students expressing satisfaction with the module.

Limitations

This is a single-center study with only one batch of students. The number of assessors involved in preclinical training was small, resulting in limited feedback.

Implications

With an increasing focus on emerging curricula for achieving competencies and skills, the study provides insight into how a blend of factors (triage of digitalization of resources, a sound cognitive base, and ongoing assessment with specific feedback) positively impact psychomotor skill training. The results also emphasize that the digitalization of learning resources is well-received by students and should become a standard practice in medical education.

Future directions

We recommend creating a repository of video demonstrations to serve as digital resources in the skills laboratory for the students. We also believe that assessment rubrics are powerful tools to incorporate self-assessment in competency-based medical education and more long-term studies can be conducted to develop and evaluate assessment rubrics for different competencies.

Conclusions

We believe a well-planned and multimodal training module helps students achieve their learning objectives. In the developed preclinical training module, the video demonstration formed a handy beneficial learning resource and the assessment rubric helped provide quality feedback ensuring the student’s satisfaction.
