Re‐conceptualizing Parkinson's disease as a lifelong neurobiological trajectory: A framework for prevention
Authors: Ayodeji Johnson Ajibare, Abraham Olufemi Asuku, Olabode Oluwadare Akintoye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractParkinson's disease (PD) is a chronic, progressive neurodegenerative disorder. No disease‐modifying therapies exist. This review proposes that PD susceptibility begins with epigenetic changes and neuroimmune activity—factors that alter gene expression and immune responses—during the vulnerable PD lifespan. Human evidence is mostly indirect or contradictory. We present this as a testable trajectory, drawing on diverse epidemiologic, experimental, and mechanistic evidence to identify intervention opportunities. We adopt a life‐course perspective focused on the brain's plasticity. We focus on critical developmental periods that increase PD vulnerability by rendering dopaminergic neurons more susceptible to damage. Specifically, we examine two key mechanisms: the induction of a pro‐inflammatory epigenetic state and mitochondrial dysfunction, frequently triggered by early‐life stress, malnutrition, or neurotoxicant exposure. We discuss how these mechanisms can be studied across epidemiologic, experimental, and mechanistic research. Integrated evidence suggests that early adverse exposures may set the stage for higher PD susceptibility. This occurs through epigenetic, neuroimmune programming, and mitochondrial vulnerabilities in dopaminergic systems. In contrast, endogenous neuroplasticity promotes neuroprotection. Long‐term physical activity, cognitive training, and enriched environments build strong neurobiological reserves by enhancing neurogenesis, improving synaptic function, and reducing neuroinflammation. A life course perspective shows how factors interact over time to shape neurobiological pathways of vulnerability or resilience to PD. This review synthesizes current mechanistic understanding, identifies preventive strategies, and aims to apply this knowledge to clinical practice and public health policies to reduce the global burden of PD.Keywords: epigenesis, genetic, neuroimmunomodulation, neuronal plasticity, Parkinson's disease, risk factors

INTRODUCTION

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative condition. It is marked by the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta and abnormal alpha‐synuclein deposits within Lewy bodies. 1 PD prevalence increases with age and varies by sex and region; globally, cases are projected to rise from 2021 levels to 25.2 million by 2050, with all‐age prevalence reaching 267 per 100,000. 2 Data show men are more likely to have PD, and risk increases with pesticide exposure, living in agricultural settings, and head injuries. 3 PD presents with four motor symptoms: bradykinesia, resting tremor, rigidity, and postural instability. 4 Non‐motor symptoms, like cognitive impairment, mood and sleep issues, and autonomic problems, can appear years before motor signs, making up a significant part of the disease burden. 5 Pharmacotherapy provides symptomatic relief, but there is no practical disease‐modifying option. Early‐stage prevention and treatment strategies are needed. To achieve this, we require a better understanding of the neurobiological processes involved in PD. 6

Recent models focus on genetic predisposition and adult environmental exposure. However, these account for only a minority of sporadic cases. The proposed paradigm shift is this: PD should be viewed through a neurodevelopmental lens rather than solely as a later‐life phenomenon. The Lifelong Neurobiological Trajectory model sees PD risk as a dynamic continuum from conception to old age. This risk results from interactions of intrinsic biology and external environmental forces. This course determines cumulative vulnerability or resistance to dopaminergic degeneration. 7

The natural plasticity of the nervous system means it can change and reorganize in response to the environment. 8 This offers a mechanism for how the disease process first forms. Neurogenesis, synaptogenesis, and activity‐dependent processes are most adaptable in youth, but significant capacity remains in adulthood. Adaptive plasticity in PD applies in two ways. First, early‐life adversity such as chronic stress, nutritional deficits, or neurotoxic exposure may disrupt dopamine circuits. 9 These disruptions are mediated by epigenetic changes, causing a pro‐inflammatory epigenetic state or hindering mitochondrial biogenesis. This creates neurobiological vulnerability and raises future PD risk. 10 Secondly: Interventions such as physical activity and cognitive engagement promote plasticity. They trigger protective mechanisms, including increased levels of neurotrophic factors, preserved mitochondrial integrity, and improved resolution of neuroinflammation. 11

These findings provide new mechanistic insights into the best timing and method for brain protection, given the link between adaptive neurobiology and PD development. The life course approach examines health outcomes over time and shows how biological and environmental factors interact to shape disease pathways. Research links early‐life factors like low birth weight, maternal smoking, and childhood infections to increased PD risk. 12 These factors impair neurodevelopment and increase PD risk in adulthood by altering the complement or resilience of dopaminergic neurons. 13 In contrast, prolonged physical and cognitive activity, along with nutritious food, strengthen the brain's defenses against neurodegeneration by activating neurogenesis, improving synaptic plasticity, and reducing oxidative stress. 14 Combining lifespan data with disease indicators enables the identification of modifiable risks and the creation of targeted prevention strategies at all life stages. The life course approach thereby shifts disease management toward prevention and lifelong brain health at later stages.

Developmental plasticity is the process by which early experience shapes neural structure and function, setting the initial point of risk or resilience. 15 The life course approach gives context. It shows how plastic changes occur and interact with later exposures, building the neurobiological course of PD. Epigenetic modifications act as a key bridge. They convert early environmental signals into lasting changes in gene expression that guide neurodevelopment and later PD risk. 16 This review aims to pull together new knowledge about how these frameworks explain PD mechanisms and inform prevention.

Specifically, this review addresses the following key questions, aiming to provide a comprehensive and actionable roadmap for the field:

How do early‐life events and developmental processes mechanistically influence the risk of PD in later life, particularly through the modulation of developmental plasticity and epigenetic programming that shapes the initial Lifelong Neurobiological Trajectory?

What specific neurobiological mechanisms underlie the persistence of neuroplasticity in the adult brain, and how can these be therapeutically harnessed to course‐correct or enhance resilience within the PD trajectory?

How can the life course approach help design comprehensive PD prevention strategies? These would span from conception through old age. They would consider total exposures, critical periods, and the changing interplay of mechanistic pathways along the Lifelong Neurobiological Trajectory?

What are the critical gaps in current mechanistic research, and what bold future directions are needed to translate this novel framework into effective clinical practice and public health policy for PD prevention, ultimately aiming to alter the disease landscape fundamentally?

By addressing these questions, this review seeks to bridge the gap between basic mechanistic science and clinical application, offering a comprehensive, paradigm‐shifting perspective on how developmental and life‐course factors contribute to PD pathogenesis and prevention. Ultimately, it is hoped that it will inspire new research directions and inform public health strategies that reduce the global burden of PD. EARLY LIFE: LAYING THE FOUNDATION FOR NEURAL HEALTH

Early life brain development: the genesis of a neurobiological trajectory

The human brain, a complex organ composed of diverse cell types and functionally interconnected structures, originates from the neuroectoderm. 17 The distinction between the differentiation, migration, and maturation of subtypes of neural cells in prenatal and postnatal development is essential to studying the mechanistic basis of neurodevelopmental disorders 18 and to guiding the differentiation of human neural stem cells in a directed manner to use in experiments and clinical applications (Figure 1). Once the neural tube closes, the brain begins to grow rapidly. These later developmental stages utilize a similar gliogenic/neurogenic developmental program to produce cells of various compositions in any brain region. The prosencephalon, mesencephalon, and rhombencephalon are separated by segmentation events, and these further divide into such regions as the telencephalon and diencephalon. 19 The most significant brain development period, marked by peak levels of neurogenesis, neuronal migration, and synaptogenesis, occurs during prenatal and early postnatal life. 20 However, the most important maturational processes, such as myelination and the development of intricate connectivity patterns, extend into late adolescence. 21 This broad developmental course, as depicted in Figure 1, defines a window of mechanistic susceptibility; insult during this window can profoundly affect underlying developmental processes, 22 even though only minor functional deficits or overt pathology are apparent later in life. This early programming thus plays a central role in the development of the Lifelong Neurobiological Trajectory, which leads to the development of neurodegenerative disorders like PD. The epigenetic blueprint: programming PD vulnerability in early life

Epigenetic regulation, such as changes in DNA methylation and histone modifications, is a primary regulator of gene expression and, therefore, affects neural health during early development. 23 They serve as a biological interface where environmental signaling of some sort is coded into developmental gene expression programs. 24 CpG‐rich methylation has been associated with neuronal lineage specification, synaptic connection formation, and the development of multifaceted neural networks. 25 Gene expression controlled by methylation also regulates genes critical to dopaminergic neuron identity, maintenance, and function; i.e., changes in epigenetic states during development may predispose them to degeneration later in life. 26 Chronic maternal stress and nutritional perturbations (e.g., folate deficiency) during gestation have been shown to alter epigenetic regulation by regulating DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) and demethylases. Correspondingly, histone acetylation modifications facilitated by histone acetyltransferases (HATs) and histone deacetylases (HDACs) affect transcription accessibility and transcription. 27 The impaired development and functionality of dopaminergic neurons and synaptic performance can be directly impaired by reduced neuroprotective gene acetylation (such as brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF)) or altered non‐coding RNA regulation. Early‐life epigenetic programming can thus establish a susceptible neurobiological condition in some contexts by regulating genes involved in neuronal resilience, oxidative stress response, protein processing (e.g., alpha‐synuclein clearance), and neuroinflammation. 28 As a result, early epigenetic programming dysfunction in the form of perinatal conditions leads to an initial deficit of the epigenetic complement or intrinsic resilience of dopaminergic neurons, which increases the risk of developing PD later in adulthood. 10 This idea is the key to the neurodevelopmental origin of the PD hypothesis.

At the moment, this interface accommodates gene‐level examples. In the brains and peripheral tissues of individuals with PD, SNCA (synuclein alpha) regulatory regions, particularly intron‐1, exhibit varying degrees of methylation, with some variation within each tissue and cell type in terms of effect size and direction. 29 , 30 In neuron‐enriched cortex, there are PD‐associated methylation differences in PARK7 (DJ‐1) and NR4A2 (NURR1) that may affect the cortex's capacity to handle oxidative stress and dopaminergic differentiation, albeit with other factors involved. 31 Chromatin state alterations at PD‐associated loci persisted after toxin exposure and are explained by histone acetylation and higher‐order chromatin interactions altered by paraquat and rotenone, which are experimental PD toxin models. 10 , 32 In addition to the single genes, differences in PD brains involve region‐specific shifts within synaptic and mitochondrial‐associated genes between 5‐methylcytosine and 5‐hydroxymethylcytosine. 33 Overall, the data presented provide a partial basis to build a mechanistic, albeit epigenetically exposed, bridge to PD biology. This hinges on multiple unresolved inconsistencies across tissues and cohorts. 34

Recent empirical evidence suggests that epigenetic changes induced by environmental factors during early development may persist across multiple generations, thereby influencing PD susceptibility in subsequent generations. 10 This intergenerational epigenetic transmission explains the severe, life‐long consequences of intra‐utero or neonatal environmental factors on subsequent‐life brain wellbeing. The persistent effects of early‐life environmental factors on PD predisposition underscore the need for preventive measures to alter these epigenetic responses. Notably, epigenetic patterns are not fixed; they are dynamic and correctable, which creates new possibilities for intervention and health management. When combined with pharmacological agents such as HDAC inhibitors, lifestyle interventions, including exercise and nutrition, hold promise for reversing maladaptive epigenetic marks and enhancing brain plasticity. 35 Therefore, further inquiry into epigenetic control during brain growth is essential for identifying specific therapeutic targets that can mitigate disease risks and bolster brain resilience throughout the lifespan.

Early life events: mechanistic programming of PD risk

While traditional epidemiological research on chronic diseases has often focused on adult life, there is growing recognition of the profound influence of early life events on adult health outcomes. A life‐course approach is increasingly advocated to determine the relative contributions of genetic and environmental factors in chronic diseases such as PD, as depicted in Figure 3. Indeed, multiple lines of evidence link early‐life experiences to PD risk through specific mechanistic hypotheses. Animal models, for instance, have demonstrated that prenatal exposure to lipopolysaccharide (LPS) endotoxin, a potent immune activator, can induce a persistent neuroinflammatory state in the developing brain. 36 This early neuroinflammation primes microglia, leading to a sensitized immune response that can exacerbate later insults and contribute to the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in adult mice, 37 suggesting a causal link between early immune activation and later neurodegeneration. There are also historical epidemiological reasons to believe that PD is more likely to occur in those born during the 20th‐century influenza pandemic, and mechanistic hypotheses include direct viral neurotropism or neuroinflammation during critical developmental stages. 38 Nevertheless, such results are not always universal. On the other hand, previous studies have shown that a history of childhood infections such as chickenpox and scarlet fever has a negative association with the risk of developing PD. 39 This fact can be attributed to the hygiene hypothesis, which states that initial and controlled immune system exposures promote the formation of a well‐developed and balanced immune system in the future. 40 This may mechanistically imply a lower propensity to chronic and dysregulated neuroinflammation, which is a major pathogenesis of PD. This highlights how early the Lifelong Neurobiological Trajectory can influence the inflammatory side of the trajectory.

Environmental neurotoxicants in early life: the “first hit” hypothesis

The brain's innate plasticity is dynamically modulated across developmental stages, significantly influencing how sensory experiences and environmental exposures shape brain structure and function. Before the full maturation of many significant neural networks, early life represents a period of heightened plasticity and, thus, increased vulnerability to environmental neurotoxins. 41 During pregnancy, the fetal brain actively prepares to process external information, laying down foundational neural circuitry. While PD can take decades to manifest, numerous non‐genetic factors are believed to play a role at different stages of its development. Epidemiological studies have extensively linked middle‐ and late‐life variables such as coffee consumption, smoking, and chemical exposures to PD risk. 42 However, assessing early‐life exposures is inherently challenging, leading to fewer studies examining their contribution to PD.

Nevertheless, a compelling mechanistic theory suggests that early‐life influences are critical. The prenatal and postnatal phases are periods of intense neurogenesis, neuronal migration, and proliferation, during which the fundamental brain structure is established. 43 Exposure to neurotoxins during these sensitive periods can lead to a neurodevelopmental origin of PD, in which the initial complement of dopaminergic neurons is reduced, or their intrinsic resilience is compromised. Animal models of PD have shown that early exposure to neurotoxins can increase the incidence of the disease in adulthood. 44 These early insults can act as a “first hit” in a multi‐hit hypothesis, priming the system for later insults. The early exposure primes the system for later insults, leading to a reduced threshold for subsequent neurodegeneration. 45 These early mechanistic deviations might only become apparent decades later, as the neuronal attrition associated with normal aging unmasks the damage, or they may exacerbate the effects of later‐life environmental factors, accelerating the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons.

Maternal and prenatal factors: shaping the vulnerable brain

Early‐life stressors, including maternal stress (prenatal), early postnatal stress, early social isolation, and early postnatal maternal separation, have been consistently linked to the development of neurological disorders as they can mechanistically impact brain formation and subsequent behavior. 46 While PD is primarily idiopathic, research indicates that both genetic predispositions and environmental factors, often intertwined with early‐life stress, can contribute to its development. The motor symptoms of PD typically emerge only after significant degeneration of central nervous system (CNS) neurons, often years before clinical signs become apparent. 47

Evidence suggests that factors such as birth season and maternal age at delivery may be linked to PD risk, with some studies even indicating a higher risk for left‐handed females. In recent years, there has been increased interest in the complex mechanisms of CNS plasticity, particularly in how the brain adapts to external stimuli via activity‐dependent pathways that drive synaptic plasticity. 48 Living conditions significantly influence human health and neural plasticity. Numerous studies report that excessive exposure to environmental factors detrimentally affects a healthy lifestyle and increases the risk of acquiring neurodegenerative disorders. Environmental or toxic compound exposure can induce neuronal cell death in PD, exacerbating symptoms by rendering the brain more susceptible to subsequent physiological chronic stress. 49

Certain environmental poisons, for example, rotenone, 1‐methyl‐4‐phenyl‐1,2,3,6‐tetrahydropyridine, 6‐hydroxydopamine (6‐OHDA), paraquat, and MPTP, are regarded as primary environmental toxins that cause PD because they can generate reactive free radicals and induce oxidative stress, leading to neuronal death. 50 Mechanistically, rotenone and MPTP are known to block the mitochondrial complex I, which is an essential part of the electron transport chain, resulting in energy loss and reactive oxygen species. 51 This dysfunction of the mitochondrion has a direct adverse effect on the energy‐intensive processes of dopaminergic neurons. 6‐OHDA is a neurotoxin selectively taken up by dopamine transporters, rendering dopaminergic neurons vulnerable to oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. Paraquat generates superoxide radicals, which cause oxidative damage. 52 Such mechanistic activities are especially harmful in the course of rapid neurogenesis and synaptogenesis, which causes permanent destruction or a depleted supply of robust neurons.

Furthermore, persistent mental stress has been shown to elevate inflammatory processes, which could contribute to the degeneration of nigral cells in PD patients. 53 For instance, in depressed patients, stress has been observed to cause microglia to malfunction, leading to dysregulation of inflammatory markers like transforming growth factor (TGF)‐β, interleukin (IL)‐1β, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)‐α, IL‐6, and IL‐10, thereby aggravating PD symptoms. 54 This chronic neuroinflammation can directly contribute to dopaminergic neuron vulnerability and death. The increasing evidence indicates that stress‐related variables mediate the interaction between genetic and environmental factors involved in PD, thus causing neurobiological susceptibilities that appear early in life and then affect the pathogenesis of PD. 55 Figure 2 below illustrates how early‐life environmental factors converge to program an individual's susceptibility to PD.

CHILDHOOD AND ADOLESCENCE: ENGINEERING NEUROBIOLOGICAL RESILIENCE

PD requires a strong diagnostic toolkit at the initial stage. The delay in PD diagnosis stems from the fact that clinical symptoms, such as motor disturbances, often appear long after neuronal loss is considerable, making therapy development challenging. 56 The existing diagnostic tools need the manifestation of advanced pathological changes to serve as a sign, which eventually restricts the effectiveness of restorative interventions. Faster advancement in the treatment of PD will thus require the discovery of viable biomarkers that will enable early detection and timely treatment intervention.

Hyposmia or anosmia and constipation are arguably the two most informative clinical features of prodromal disease, being present 10 or more years before the onset of motor symptoms. These prodromes are consistent with early involvement of the olfactory bulb and enteric nervous systems, and have been incorporated into the MDS research criteria for prodromal PD. 57 , 58 , 59 Their inclusion helps to delineate the premotor window where prevention and risk‐stratified monitoring may be possible. Several biomarkers, such as cerebrospinal fluid, neuroimaging, oxidative stress, and inflammation indices, are being investigated to detect early PD. 60 These markers are practical diagnostics and progression monitoring tools that can be used separately or in combination, and they may record the early stages of the development of pathological processes, including alpha‐synuclein aggregation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuroinflammation. The clinical spectrum of PD extends beyond motor symptoms like rigidity, tremor, and postural instability to include non‐motor manifestations such as dementia, autonomic dysfunctions, and eye movement abnormalities. 61 In later stages, cognitive decline, insomnia, depression, and metabolic dysregulation also commonly emerge. Early identification of these diverse risk factors and prodromal symptoms, particularly during childhood and adolescence, could enable proactive strategies to mechanistically mitigate future disease burden by targeting the earliest signs of neurobiological vulnerability, thereby allowing for early course‐correction of the Lifelong Neurobiological Trajectory. 62

Strategies for early interventions in childhood and adolescence: engineering resilience

Childhood and early environmental stimuli profoundly influence our subjective construction of reality and brain development. This period represents a second critical window of plasticity, offering a unique opportunity to engineer resilience against neurodegenerative processes. 63 Interventions during these formative years can leverage the brain's plasticity to build a robust neurobiological foundation, counteract earlier negative programming, or establish a buffer against future insults. 64 The concept of the neurobiological reserve here is not merely a general notion. However, it refers to a quantifiable set of neurobiological properties, including synaptic density, mitochondrial capacity, and neurotrophic factor levels, that can be actively enhanced. This multi‐modal approach to resilience engineering is key to shaping a favorable lifelong neurobiological trajectory (Figure 3).

Environmental enrichment (EE): sculpting a resilient brain architecture

Environmental enrichment, a stimulating and varied environment that promotes motor, sensory, and cognitive engagement, represents a powerful blend of social and intricate inanimate stimulation. Research consistently demonstrates that EE significantly impacts almost all essential components that sustain synaptic plasticity over time. 65 Mechanistically, EE promotes neurogenesis in regions like the hippocampus, increases dendritic arborization and synaptic density, enhances the expression of neurotrophic factors such as BDNF and glial cell line‐derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF), and improves cerebral vascularization. 66 These changes collectively foster robust neural networks and enhance neurotrophic support. Typically, EE exposes individuals to a range of sensory inputs, social interactions, and cognitive tasks beyond their usual living conditions. Early‐life EE can foster robust neural networks in PD prevention and enhance neurotrophic support, thereby increasing the brain's neurobiological reserve and resilience to later‐life insults that might otherwise contribute to PD pathogenesis. Studies have specifically explored its potential for treating and managing PD, demonstrating its capacity to ameliorate motor deficits and protect dopaminergic neurons in animal models. 67 This suggests a direct mechanistic impact on the dopaminergic system.

Nutrition: fueling neuroprotection and epigenetic modulation

Nutritional interventions, often in conjunction with standard medical therapy, are increasingly recognized for their role in managing chronic conditions, including PD, by addressing nutrient deficiencies or supporting specific physiological functions. 68 The past decades have witnessed a notable surge in the use of functional foods and food supplements to promote health and complement modern lifestyles. For PD management and prevention, diets rich in phytochemicals—such as flavonoids (found in berries, pears, and celery) and sulforaphane (in broccoli)—and Omega‐3 DHA, present in fish, shellfish, and plant oils, are recommended. 69 Mechanistically, these compounds exert neuroprotective effects by reducing oxidative stress by activating Nrf2 pathways, modulating inflammatory responses by inhibiting pro‐inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF‐α, IL‐1β), and supporting mitochondrial function and biogenesis. 70 Importantly, specific nutrients can also act as epigenetic modulators, influencing DNA methylation and histone modification patterns to promote the expression of neuroprotective genes or suppress pro‐inflammatory pathways. Caffeine, a known neuroprotectant, also holds promise in PD prevention, primarily through its antagonism of adenosine A2A receptors, which are implicated in dopaminergic neuron vulnerability and neuroinflammation. 71 Consuming these neuroprotective foods early in life can contribute to establishing a robust neurobiological foundation, potentially preventing or delaying the onset of PD later in life by directly influencing cellular resilience pathways and optimizing the epigenetic landscape.

Physical activity: a potent inducer of neuroplasticity and neurogenesis

While no single treatment can completely ameliorate PD, a substantial body of research supports the neuroprotective and symptomatic benefits of exercise. Experimental studies increasingly demonstrate the efficacy of physical activity in preventing PD, delaying its onset, and slowing its progression. Regular exercise improves motor function, balance, and overall quality of life and enhances happiness and sleep patterns. 72 Specific activities such as treadmill gait training, virtual reality interventions, brisk walking, Nordic walking, balance training, dancing, resistance training, and aerobics contribute to symptom relief and improved neurophysiological function. 73 Mechanistically, physical exercise promotes neurogenesis in the subventricular and subgranular zones of the hippocampus, enhances synaptic plasticity by increasing long‐term potentiation, and upregulates the expression of neurotrophic factors like BDNF and GDNF. 74 BDNF, in particular, supports the survival and differentiation of dopaminergic neurons. Exercise also improves mitochondrial function and biogenesis, reduces neuroinflammation by modulating microglial activation, and enhances cerebral blood flow. 75 Critically, physical activity can also counteract some of the negative epigenetic programming established in early life, for instance, by upregulating BDNF expression, which might have been suppressed by early‐life stress. Initiating and maintaining physical activity during childhood and adolescence can thus build a significant neurobiological reserve, making the brain more resilient to age‐related neurodegeneration and environmental challenges by directly strengthening its intrinsic protective mechanisms and potentially reversing early mechanistic deficits.

Cognitive stimulation: enhancing cognitive reserve and neural efficiency

Cognitive decline is one of the most prevalent non‐motor symptoms of PD, significantly impacting daily living. This can manifest as impairments in executive function (e.g., planning and coordinating multi‐step tasks), visuospatial abilities (e.g., three‐dimensional navigation, distance estimation), and broader deficits in language, memory, and concentration. 76 Although pharmacological interventions offer minor cognitive benefits, non‐pharmacological approaches, collectively termed cognitive stimulation, are gaining prominence. This form of rehabilitation, often delivered by occupational therapists, aims to enhance cognitive function and sustain an individual's engagement in daily roles and activities. Cognitive stimulation encompasses a variety of techniques designed to challenge and activate neural circuits. 77 Mechanistically, a cognitively active lifestyle is believed to guard against neurodegeneration by promoting synaptic plasticity, increasing dendritic complexity, and enhancing neuronal efficiency. It contributes to the development of cognitive reserve, which is the brain's ability to cope with pathology by using existing neural networks more efficiently or by recruiting alternative neural pathways. 78 This increased reserve can delay the clinical manifestation of PD symptoms, even in the presence of underlying neuropathology, by providing a buffer against neuronal loss and dysfunction. By fostering cognitive engagement from an early age, individuals can strengthen these protective mechanisms, and, synergistically, cognitive stimulation can enhance the benefits derived from physical activity by promoting the integration of newly generated neurons into functional circuits. 79

INTERVENTION STRATEGIES ACROSS THE LIFESPAN: FROM PREVENTION TO TREATMENT

Brain plasticity in adult life: modulating the lifelong neurobiological trajectory

Neuroplasticity, the brain's remarkable capacity to modify, change, and adapt its structure and function throughout life in response to experiences and environmental demands, is critically important in the context of PD. 80 The adaptive capacity of the CNS is represented in the form of various mechanisms such as synaptic plasticity, structural plasticity (neurogenesis, gliogenesis, dendritic arborization), and neural circuit reorganization. It is worth noting that adult neuroplasticity allows constant preservation of the neurobiological reserve and enables the process of mechanistic course correction of the Lifelong Neurobiological Trajectory of PD. 81

Therefore, a clear understanding of the potential of this ongoing plasticity to be exploited is essential, as it could prevent the development and clinical presentation of the disorder by enhancing neuronal resilience and compensatory systems, even in the presence of early predispositions. Therefore, the current debate focuses on the role of lifestyle, diet, specific environmental exposures, and cognitive involvement as risk factors of the disease course through active adult brain plasticity and related molecular pathways. Exercise and physical activity: resculpting neural circuits and enhancing mitochondrial function

Empirical studies have shown that regular physical exercise is associated with a lower risk of disease progression and a slight slowing of its progression. As such, evidence indicates that exercise is a potent regulator of adult neuroplasticity. In diverse modalities such as walking, dancing, Tai Chi, and exercise programs, regular movement has been found to enhance motor function and postural stability. 82 At the mechanistic level, depending on the type, frequency, and duration of the exercises, neurogenesis in areas of the cortex, temporal cortex, hippocampus, and numerous subcortical locations improves dendritic arborization and synapses, 83 particularly in the temporal cortex, hippocampus, and most subcortical locations, and the upregulation of neurotrophic factors such as BDNF and GDNF. BDNF sustains the survival, differentiation, and physiological functioning of dopaminergic neurons, which explains its direct opposition to the ongoing neurodegeneration mechanisms. 84 Exercise also exerts significant effects on mitochondrial performance and biogenesis; therefore, it increases cellular energy production and suppresses oxidative stress, both of which are highly relevant to neuronal health and resistance. 85 Moreover, exercise modulates neuroinflammatory reactions by preserving microglial phenotype to a more neuroprotective anti‐inflammatory phenotype and by increasing cerebral blood flow, which facilitates nutrient and oxygen delivery to the neurons, thereby maximizing nutrient delivery to the brain. All these processes lead to heightened brain plasticity and resilience, reducing the risk of PD and slowing down the neurodegenerative process. In addition, exercise may induce epigenetic modifications in adulthood that reverse maladaptive developmental programming and alter lifelong neurobiological trajectories. 86

Diet and nutrition: modulating systemic and neural homeostasis

Dietary choices play a significant role in maintaining brain health and actively reducing PD risk by influencing systemic and neural homeostasis. Foods rich in antioxidants, such as berries and leafy greens, are believed to help protect against neuronal damage by combating oxidative stress and inflammation, key mechanisms in PD pathology. 87 Mechanistically, these compounds activate endogenous antioxidant pathways, such as the Nrf2 pathway, and inhibit pro‐inflammatory signaling cascades, thereby directly mitigating cellular damage. The Mediterranean diet, characterized by its emphasis on fruits, vegetables, whole grains, legumes, nuts, olive oil, and moderate consumption of fish and poultry, has been consistently linked with a lower PD risk. 88 Its neuroprotective effects are attributed to its high antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, and healthy fat content, which support neuronal membrane integrity and mitochondrial function.

This dietary pattern can also positively influence the gut microbiome, which is increasingly recognized as a modulator of brain health and neuroinflammation, thus providing a multi‐systemic mechanistic benefit.

Furthermore, coffee and, more specifically, caffeine consumption have demonstrated neuroprotective effects, primarily by antagonizing adenosine A2A receptors, which are highly expressed in the striatum and modulate dopaminergic neurotransmission and neuroinflammation. 89 While other diets may not directly reduce PD risk, maintaining adequate vitamin D levels is also associated with neuroprotection, potentially modulating neuroinflammation and supporting neuronal survival. 90 These nutritional strategies support neuronal health and resilience, fostering an environment conducive to sustained brain plasticity and actively mitigating the cumulative mechanistic burden contributing to PD.

Social and cognitive engagement: building and leveraging cognitive reserve

Maintaining robust social interactions and consistently engaging in mentally stimulating activities can significantly reduce the risk of PD in adults by preserving cognitive function and enhancing neural resilience. 91 Learning new skills, performing cognitively challenging activities such as solving puzzles, and engaging with social networks are all continuous exposures to cognitive stimuli that enhance synaptic plasticity, increase dendritic arborization, and strengthen neural connections. 92 This activity forms the basis of cognitive reserve, that is, the ability of the brain to compensate for pathological sequelae through more effective use of existing neural networks or the activation of new circuits. This enhanced ability has the power to delay the clinical manifestation of Parkinsonian symptoms despite the occurrence of neurodegeneration, hence providing a shield against neuronal loss and dysfunction. 93 Meditation, regular relaxation techniques, and maintaining a healthy balance between work and home life are also considered neuroprotective, primarily by decreasing chronic stress. Mechanistically, chronic stress can induce neuroinflammation, impair neurogenesis, and alter synaptic plasticity, making stress reduction a direct modulator of brain health and a critical component of maintaining a favorable neurobiological trajectory. 94 These activities collectively foster a brain environment that supports sustained plasticity and resilience, actively combating mechanistic fatigue where the brain's capacity for plasticity and compensation diminishes with age.

Environmental factors: minimizing mechanistic insults

In adulthood, proactive avoidance of common environmental toxins is a crucial strategy for reducing PD risk by minimizing ongoing mechanistic insults. This includes minimizing exposure to household pesticides, industrial pollutants from chemical factories, and agricultural chemicals used in mechanized farming. 95 Mechanistically, many of these compounds (e.g., rotenone, paraquat) are known to induce oxidative stress, inhibit mitochondrial complex I, and promote alpha‐synuclein aggregation, directly contributing to dopaminergic neurodegeneration. 96 Appropriate protective equipment in hazardous occupations is also essential to prevent direct neurotoxic exposure. Furthermore, engaging in physical activity in natural environments with low traffic and air pollution can provide additional neuroprotective benefits, as clean environments reduce exposure to neurotoxicants and promote overall well‐being. 97 These lifestyle factors collectively operate through various mechanisms to drive brain plasticity, promoting neurogenesis and synaptogenesis, enhancing dopaminergic function, reducing circulating inflammation and oxidative stress, and improving cerebral blood flow and mitochondrial function, all of which contribute to a more resilient brain and actively reduce the mechanistic burden associated with PD. 98

Cell replacement trials

Cell replacement therapies. An additional approach aims to repair the loss of striatal dopaminergic innervation by transplanting dopaminergic progenitor cells derived from pluripotent stem cells. The first human clinical trial using iPS cell‐derived dopaminergic progenitors was conducted in Japan and demonstrated successful survival and dopamine production by the transplanted cells, with no tumor formation. 99 At the same time, a Phase I study using hESCs (bemdaneprocel/DA01) demonstrated acceptable safety and provided radiologic evidence of graft viability and exploratory evidence of improved motor function in a small group of patients in an open‐label trial, 100 , 101 , 102 and larger trials are currently being planned.

Pharmacological interventions: targeted modulation of pathological pathways

While primarily symptomatic treatments, specific pharmacological interventions for PD also play a crucial role in supporting neuronal function and actively modulating disease progression by optimizing the neural environment. 103 Levodopa (l‐DOPA) remains the gold standard treatment, replenishing dopamine levels and improving motor symptoms by restoring dopaminergic neurotransmission. Adjunct medications like MAO‐B inhibitors increase synaptic dopamine availability by inhibiting its enzymatic breakdown, thereby enhancing neural cell physiological protection and improving motor fluctuations. 104 Beyond these, the research explores alpha‐synuclein‐targeted therapies to address the core proteinopathy by inhibiting aggregation or promoting clearance. Growth factor administration supports neuronal survival and regeneration by activating specific signaling pathways. Mitochondrial function enhancers seek to improve cellular energy metabolism and reduce oxidative stress, directly addressing key mechanistic drivers of PD pathology. 105 These emerging strategies represent direct attempts to intervene at the mechanistic level to preserve neuronal integrity and support brain plasticity, offering opportunities for mechanistic course correction within the Lifelong Neurobiological Trajectory.

Non‐Pharmacological interventions: engineering functional plasticity

Non‐pharmacological approaches are integral to PD management and can significantly enhance brain plasticity and resilience. Lifestyle modifications, including programmed exercise, gait training, and resistance training, directly impact motor learning, increase BDNF production, and improve synaptic function. 106 Cognitive training, dual‐task training, memory exercises, and problem‐solving activities target executive function, processing speed, and working memory, thereby enhancing cognitive reserve and functional plasticity. Speech and language therapy, particularly through voice exercises, articulation training, swallowing therapy, and the Lee Silverman Voice Treatment, improves communication and swallowing functions by promoting neuroplastic changes in relevant motor and sensory cortices. 107 Occupational therapy aims at activities of daily living, environmental adaptation, energy conservation, and activity adaptation to maintain independence and quality of life. Interventions can also involve interaction with green spaces to facilitate functional performance and well‐being. More sophisticated, nonpharmacological neuromodulation methods, including deep brain stimulation (DBS), transcranial magnetic stimulation, and transcranial direct current stimulation, can alter neural networks and induce plasticity, thereby improving motor functioning. For example, long‐lasting alterations in basal ganglia circuits induced by DBS can be translated into long‐term motor improvement. 108 Future opportunities in providing personalized and adaptive interventions to facilitate neuroplasticity in PD and related disorders are promising, and using emerging technologies such as virtual reality, wearable devices, remote monitoring, and AI‐guided treatment planning can offer significant opportunities in this field, as can dietary or environmental epigenetic interventions.

Evidence linking adult lifestyle to PD risk reduction: modulating the trajectory slope

Numerous studies provide compelling evidence for the role of adult lifestyle factors in reducing PD risk, often with clear mechanistic implications. Extensive prospective cohort studies, such as the Harvard Alumni Health Study and the NIH‐AARP Diet and Health Study, have consistently demonstrated that increased and regular physical activity is directly associated with greater reductions in PD risk, supporting the mechanistic role of exercise in promoting neurotrophic support and mitochondrial health. 109 Dietary and lifestyle cohort studies, including large adult populations, indicate that patterns rich in vegetables, fruits, fish, and moderate caffeine intake, together with regular physical activity and social engagement, are associated with lower PD risk, potentially through antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory pathways. 110 Conversely, evidence from agricultural cohorts links chronic pesticide exposure with increased PD risk, supporting toxicant‐related neurodegeneration. 111 Prospective longitudinal data show that REM sleep behaviour disorder is a high‐risk prodromal state for later parkinsonism or dementia, with plausible mechanistic links to alpha‐synuclein pathology and neuroinflammation. 112 Longitudinal cohort findings also suggest that sustained cognitive engagement and higher cognitive reserve are associated with better cognitive trajectories and lower dementia risk in PD. 113 Collectively, extensive scientific data demonstrate that a combination of healthy lifestyle choices, including a balanced diet and non‐smoking, significantly reduces the risk of PD in adults. These findings underscore the ongoing opportunity throughout adulthood to leverage brain plasticity and mitigate the mechanistic drivers of PD to prevent and manage PD, thereby actively modulating the slope of the Lifelong Neurobiological Trajectory toward a healthier outcome.

CHALLENGES IN TRANSLATING DEVELOPMENTAL PLASTICITY TO PD PREVENTION: FRONTIERS FOR A NEW PARADIGM

Developmental plasticity, or the inherent ability of an organism to change its phenotype in response to environmental stimuli during critical periods, is an important pillar of survival and evolutionary adaptation. 114 However, the possibility of this deep biological flexibility is significant, and the translational challenges of converting the mechanism to practical, disease‐preventative approaches are immense. Such issues are not beyond solution; rather, they represent critical frontiers in current and future studies and innovations necessary to fully exploit the potential of the Lifelong Neurobiological Trajectory framework. As shown in Figure 5, they are based on the complex interplay between early‐life experience and its subsequent neurodegenerative sequelae and on the practical and ethical constraints that limit the success of exploring the potential of such plasticity in the development of interventions.

Timing and critical windows of mechanistic vulnerability: Unraveling temporal precision

Empirical findings show that neural structures, particularly the brain, exhibit their greatest plasticity during the early phases of development, making these phases extremely vulnerable to environmental manipulation. 115 Malnutrition, chronic stress, or environmental toxins are some of the adverse factors that can program long‐term health effects during these critical developmental windows through a mechanism that results in increased vulnerability to neurodegenerative disorders such as PD. 116 This programming involves alterations in fundamental cellular processes such as neurogenesis, neuronal migration, synaptogenesis, and the establishment of neurochemical pathways. However, precisely elucidating these critical windows during which specific mechanistic interventions would most effectively prevent PD remains a formidable challenge. For instance, while early‐life nutrition or toxin exposure may profoundly affect brain development, altering the initial complement or resilience of dopaminergic neurons, the clinical manifestation of PD can be delayed by decades. 117 This long latency period obscures the direct mechanistic links between early insults and later pathology, making it difficult to pinpoint the optimal timing and nature of interventions. A key mechanistic question remains: What are the precise molecular and cellular signatures defining a critical vulnerability window, and how can these be non‐invasively detected to guide intervention timing? Overcoming this requires sophisticated longitudinal studies with high‐resolution temporal data on molecular and cellular changes.

Complexity of gene–environment interactions and delayed mechanistic manifestation: Decoding the interplay

The interplay between genetic predispositions and environmental factors is crucial to developmental plasticity and subsequent disease risk. Early‐life environmental cues can mechanistically modulate the expression of specific genetic variants associated with PD risk, often through epigenetic mechanisms, creating complex gene–environment interactions. 118 These interactions are often too intricate to predict how particular interventions (e.g., specific dietary changes, avoidance of certain toxins) will specifically affect long‐term PD risk in diverse populations. The same environmental factor can elicit vastly different mechanistic effects depending on an individual's unique genetic background and epigenetic landscape, complicating the development of universal prevention strategies. 119 Furthermore, the long latency between early neurodevelopmental or environmental insults and clinical PD onset obscures direct causal links and complicates mechanistic attribution. This temporal disconnect makes it challenging to design and evaluate the efficacy of early‐life interventions, as the mechanistic pathways initiated in early life may only manifest their full pathological consequences much later, requiring prolonged follow‐up studies. A critical mechanistic question is: What are the specific epigenetic mechanisms that mediate these gene‐environment interactions, and how can they be therapeutically targeted to mitigate risk across diverse genetic backgrounds? This frontier demands advanced multi‐omics approaches and sophisticated computational modeling.

Trade‐offs in adaptive plasticity and maladaptive programming: Navigating biological compromises

While developmental plasticity enables organisms to cope with environmental challenges, these adaptations can sometimes come at a mechanistic cost. The Predictive Adaptive Response (PAR) hypothesis proposes that early exposure to adverse environments confers short‐term survival benefits by rewiring metabolic or stress response pathways, but at the cost of creating long‐term disease vulnerability, such as PD. 120 Such a dichotomy requires the strict distinction between adaptive plasticity, which is truly protective of longevity, and maladaptive plasticity, which, despite immediate coping abilities, nonetheless enhances chronic inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, or inefficient protein clearance. Intervention engineering to reduce the occurrence of such long‐term mechanistic trade‐offs requires a complex understanding of both the biological outcomes of early‐life adaptation and the downstream pathways involved in Parkinsonism. 121 Therefore, today, the question is: which particular adaptive responses, although beneficial in the short run, mechanistically predispose individuals to neurodegeneration in the future, and how can such maladaptive programs be prevented? This conceptual problem forces a reevaluation of what should be considered healthy development when evaluated not in terms of adulthood but in terms of lifetime disease risk.

Ethical and practical challenges in early‐life mechanistic interventions: Bridging science and society

Any dietary or environmental epigenetic intervention during early life must be timed to coincide with critical developmental windows to trigger adaptive plasticity, but this timing poses significant ethical and practical challenges for research. The later appearance of most disorders, as well as the possible unpredictable mechanistic effects, complicates the determination of safety and efficacy. Equity is also an issue when population‐level stratification by susceptibility, whether genetic or environmental, occurs, as this can exacerbate existing health disparities. Moreover, logistical and financial impediments to the application of large‐scale, long‐term interventions in real‐world settings underscore the need for rigorous cost‐effectiveness studies and evaluations of their effects on society as a whole. This new frontier thus demands methodological soundness, a wholesome ethical system, and robust social responsibility. One research question is how early‐life processes can be altered in a manner that is mechanistically justified, ethically justifiable, fair, and long‐term safe? This challenge will demand novel designs for clinical trials and the concerted development of public health policy.

Lack of longitudinal mechanistic data: The imperative for comprehensive cohorts

Translating insights from developmental plasticity into effective PD prevention strategies critically depends on comprehensive, long‐term studies that track individuals from early life through adulthood and collect detailed mechanistic data. 122 However, such longitudinal data are rare, and existing studies often lack the granular resolution to identify specific early‐life factors linked to PD risk and their underlying mechanistic pathways. For instance, current cohorts may not have sufficient data on early‐life epigenetic profiles, specific neuroinflammatory markers, or detailed assessments of mitochondrial function during critical developmental windows. Without robust longitudinal mechanistic evidence, designing, validating, and implementing interventions that leverage developmental plasticity to prevent PD remain challenging. This frontier highlights the urgent need to establish extensive intergenerational cohort studies with detailed data collection on environmental exposures, biological markers reflecting mechanistic changes, and neurological outcomes across the lifespan, enabling the mapping of complete mechanistic pathways from early life to clinical manifestation. It is similar to a contemporary Manhattan Project for the prevention of neurodegenerative diseases: a global, collaborative initiative with sufficient funding and organizational capabilities. Investigators will produce the data to model individual neurobiological trajectories and test interventions in silico and prospective longitudinal cohorts.

CONCLUSION: A NEW PARADIGM FOR PD PREVENTION

PD is a growing health issue globally, with no clear preventative measures. This review redefines PD as a lifelong neurobiological trajectory influenced by developmental and late‐life exposures, and not a late‐life condition. The result of that trajectory is the formation and maintenance of intrinsic biological programs through continuous interactions with environmental stimuli, mediated by brain plasticity. Empirical findings suggest that brain development in early life, influenced by maternal health and early environmental experiences, may shape epigenetic and developmental patterns that predispose to PD vulnerability later in life. 123 The molecular processes early in the stages are driven by coordinated efforts of DNA methylation, histone modifications, and non‐coding RNAs, which influence neuronal differentiation, synapse integrity, and the resilience of the dopaminergic system, thus pinpointing essential molecular targets for future therapeutic approaches. Thereafter, in childhood, adolescence, and adulthood, continued physical activity, mental stimulation, and a nutritious diet also facilitate neuroplasticity by increasing neurogenesis, synaptogenesis, and mitochondrial biogenesis, and by actively clearing neuroinflammation, 124 reducing the mechanistic burden that accumulates over time, and dynamically altering the PD trajectory by modulating its slope. Complementary strategies, including pharmacological advancements, brain‐stimulating treatments, and digital medicine applications, further bolster brain resilience against PD by targeting specific neurobiological pathways and offering opportunities for mechanistic course correction.

While the journey towards effective PD prevention is complex, facing challenges such as the disease's long latency period, the intricate gene‐environment interactions, the potential for maladaptive plasticity, and ethical considerations surrounding early‐life interventions, these challenges highlight the critical need for innovative mechanistic research and policy. These are not roadblocks but frontiers for a new era of neurodegeneration research.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS: A MECHANISTIC ROADMAP FOR A PREVENTABLE PD

To translate the insights from developmental plasticity and the life‐course approach into tangible clinical practice and public health policy, we propose a fundamental reorientation of the field, guided by a mechanistic roadmap for PD prevention:

Intergenerational Longitudinal Mechanistic Studies: Establishing large‐scale, prospective longitudinal cohort studies that track individuals from conception through multiple generations is paramount. These studies must move beyond epidemiological correlations to include granular mechanistic data: early‐life epigenetic profiles, specific neuroinflammatory markers, detailed assessments of mitochondrial function, and alpha‐synuclein seeding capacity. Such comprehensive data will enable precise mapping of complete mechanistic pathways from early‐life programming to clinical manifestations, and the development of “digital twins” to model individual neurobiological trajectories and test intervention strategies in silico.

Targeted Mechanistic Intervention Trials: Future research must prioritize the design and implementation of intervention trials targeting specific mechanistic nodes identified in early life. This includes testing novel “developmental neuroprotectants” or “epigenetic modulators” (e.g., specific HDAC or DNMT inhibitors) that can reverse maladaptive programming or enhance protective gene expression. These trials must be designed to assess the long‐term impact on PD risk and neurobiological markers, moving beyond broad lifestyle advice to precision prevention.

Biomarker Development for Early Mechanistic Deviations: The framework necessitates the development of novel, highly sensitive biomarkers that reflect early mechanistic deviations (e.g., specific epigenetic signatures, early inflammatory markers, subtle changes in dopaminergic neuron health or alpha‐synuclein pathology), long before clinical symptoms appear. These biomarkers would be crucial for identifying individuals at risk, enabling early intervention and patient stratification for clinical trials, and ultimately guiding personalized prevention strategies.

Public Health Policy Informed by Mechanistic Insights: The insights from the life‐course approach must inform public health policies aimed at reducing population‐level PD risk. This includes advocating for stricter environmental regulations to minimize exposure to known neurotoxins (e.g., specific pesticides, industrial pollutants) based on their mechanistic effects on dopaminergic neurons. Furthermore, promoting universal access to quality maternal and child healthcare and implementing public health campaigns that encourage lifelong physical activity, cognitive engagement, and healthy dietary patterns should be framed in terms of their known mechanistic benefits for neuroplasticity, mitochondrial health, and neuroinflammation. These policies should recognize the cumulative mechanistic impact of exposures across the lifespan.
