Risk of malnutrition, food insecurity, dietary quality, and associated factors among Malawian older adults at hospital admission: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Getrude Mphwanthe, Clare Reynolds, Clare Corish, Lustia Mndoliro, Triza Columbus, Jonathan Misolo, Felistace Mtande, Sarah Browne
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalnutrition adversely affects health outcomes and healthcare costs; however, limited data are available on the prevalence and burden of malnutrition in older adults at hospital admission in developing countries. Therefore, this study aimed to undertake malnutrition screening among older adults at hospital admission and examine the relationship between nutritional status and a range of health and socio-ecological factors.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted in a large public referral hospital in Malawi, targeting older adults (aged ≥ 60 years). Participants were screened for risk of malnutrition using the Mini Nutritional Assessment-Short Form (MNA-SF) within 24–48 h of hospital admission. Those at risk of malnutrition/malnourished using the screening tool (MNA-SF) were subsequently assessed to determine a malnutrition diagnosis using the Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition (GLIM). Physical function was assessed using the Katz Index of Independence in Activities of Daily Living (Katz Index ADL) and handgrip strength, appetite using the Simplified Nutritional Appetite Questionnaire (SNAQ), food insecurity using a 12-month food insecurity experience scale, and dietary quality with a 30-day food frequency questionnaire. Logistic regression examined the factors associated with the risk of malnutrition/ malnutrition.ResultsThe mean age of 315 older adults was 66.83 ± 6.78 years (range: 60–93 years), and 58.4% (n = 184) were male. Using the MNA-SF, the prevalence of malnutrition risk was 39.7%, and 40.3% were malnourished. Of those who were at risk of malnutrition/malnourished using the MNA-SF, 75% were diagnosed with malnutrition utilizing the GLIM criteria. Risk of malnutrition/malnutrition, screened using MNA-SF, was associated with poor appetite (AOR 2.581; 95% CI 1.310–5.086; p = 0.006), severe functional impairment (AOR 5.182; 95% CI: 1.469–18.279; p = 0.011), polypharmacy (AOR 2.673; 95% CI:1.112–6.426; p = 0.028), and severe food insecurity (AOR 2.293; 95% CI 1.015–5.176; p = 0.046).ConclusionDuring the study period in 2023, the majority of older adults admitted to a large public hospital in Malawi were at risk of malnutrition/malnourished, with multiple associated factors. Routine malnutrition screening using validated tools on admission, subsequent diagnosis of malnutrition using the GLIM criteria, and the availability of adequate nutrition support, including inpatient and discharge care pathways, are needed to prevent further deterioration of nutritional status in this vulnerable population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06463-x.Keywords: Older adults, Malnutrition, Food insecurity, Dietary quality, MNA-SF

Introduction

Malnutrition in older adults before, during, and after hospitalization poses a significant burden for individuals and health systems; however, it is not well recognized in healthcare in developing countries [1]. The population of older adults has increased in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), and it is believed that declining birth rates and improved healthcare will result in further increases [2, 3]. Malnutrition is common in older adults, with multifactorial causes, including physiological processes associated with aging accompanied by inadequate dietary intake, food insecurity, poly-morbidity, increased metabolic demand during illness, and impaired nutrient absorption [4–6]. Malnutrition is associated with increased risk of both morbidity and mortality, for example, impaired immune function, a high rate of hospital-acquired infections, delayed wound healing, functional impairment, longer hospitalization, poor prognosis, and escalating hospital costs [4–8].

The prevalence of malnutrition in older adults (aged > 60 years) within Africa ranges from 2.2% to 77.8% [9, 10], depending on whether assessed in the community or clinical setting. The pooled prevalence among the older adult population has been estimated at 17–18% [9, 11], with wide country-level differences that are influenced by socio-economic and healthcare systems in respective countries. Studies of older adults within SSA using the full Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA) or MNA-Short Form (MNA-SF) reported that risk of malnutrition ranges from 17 to 57.8% [12–15] and malnutrition from 16.6 to 81% [12–16]. Studies that screened older adults using MNA-SF and diagnosed malnutrition utilizing the Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition (GLIM) criteria reported malnutrition prevalences of 51% [17] and 61–63% [18]. In addition, food insecurity affects the accessibility and availability of healthy and nutritious foods and overall dietary quality and, in both SSA and elsewhere, is associated with malnutrition in older adults [5, 13, 19–21]. Data from Malawi are sparse, with just one community-based study from 1998 [22] included in a recently published review [11].

The recently published scoping review on the prevalence and related assessment practices of adult hospital malnutrition on the African continent concluded that greater awareness is needed, and routine nutrition screening with appropriate nutrition support should be implemented as a matter of urgency in African hospitals [1]. Early nutrition screening using validated tools, identifying patients at risk of malnutrition within the first 24–48 h of hospital admission, and re-screening at regular intervals during hospitalization, is a crucial and essential first step in the nutrition care process [23–25]. Unfortunately, in Malawi, screening for risk of malnutrition is not routinely done. Hence, patients needing medical nutrition therapy and appropriate multimodal interventions from an interdisciplinary team for improved nutrition and health outcomes are missed. To our current knowledge, no studies in Malawi have examined risk of malnutrition in hospitalized older adults at hospital admission. Therefore, this study aimed to undertake malnutrition screening among older adults at hospital admission and examine the relationship between nutritional status and a range of health and socio-ecological factors.

Materials and methods

Study design

A hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted at Kamuzu Central Hospital (KCH), one of Malawi’s largest public referral hospitals in the capital city, Lilongwe. The study was undertaken between June and November 2023. It was approved by the National Committee on Research in the Social Sciences and Humanities, National Commission for Science and Technology, protocol approval No: P.05/23/756. Permission to conduct the study was obtained from KCH, and all eligible participants provided written informed consent or thumbprint for those who could not write after the purpose of the study was explained.

Participants

Four clinical dietitians trained in malnutrition risk screening at KCH used non-probability consecutive sampling [26] to recruit older adults (aged ≥ 60 years) [9] within 24–48 h of hospital admission [23, 24]. We calculated a sample size of 315 using openEpi version 3 software (https://www.openepi.com), taking into consideration that the prevalence rate of malnutrition (undernutrition) in older adults in Africa is reported to range from 2.2 to 77.3% [9] at a 95% confidence level. The inclusion criteria were older adult patients (aged ≥ 60 years) who could stand independently for weight and height measurements in the following wards: general medical, renal, oncology, surgical, and orthopedic. Of 343 older adults approached (Fig. 1), 315 (91.8%) met the inclusion criteria. Twenty-eight were excluded for the following reasons: two (0.6%) had edema, three (0.9%) ascites, four (1.2%) required hemodialysis, four (1.1%) could not stand independently for weight and height measurements, six (1.7%) were discharged before being assessed, five (1.5%) could not provide informed consent, and four (1.2%) refused to participate (Fig. 1).

Participant recruitment procedure

Data collection

Using a bespoke questionnaire (see supplementary material) and face-to-face interviews, the clinical dietitians collected data on (1) socio-demographic and economic characteristics including, age, sex, marital status, education level, and source of income; (2) lifestyle factors including, smoking status and alcohol intake; (3) clinical characteristics including, primary diagnosis, presence of chronic conditions, poly-morbidity, defined by two or more chronic conditions [27], polypharmacy, defined by ≥ 5 prescribed medications [28, 29], daily use/intake of dietary multivitamins and/or multimineral supplements. Hospitalization within the previous 12 months was also recorded.

Anthropometric measures

Weight and height

Each participant’s weight (kilogram, kg) to the nearest 0.1 kg was measured using a Seca scale (Seca 803, SECA GmbH & Co. KG, German) and height (centimeter, cm) to the nearest 0.1 cm using a stadiometer (Seca 213, SECA GmbH & Co. KG. German) to derive body mass index (BMI, kg/m2) which was categorized according to World Health Organization (WHO) criteria: BMI < 18.5 kg/m2, underweight, 18.5–24.9 kg/m2, normal weight status, 25–29.9 kg/m2, overweight, and ≥ 30 kg/m2, obese [30].

Mid-upper arm circumference

Using a non-elastic measuring tape, the mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) was measured using the left arm to the nearest 0.1 cm. Participants with a MAUC of < 19 cm were classified as severely underweight, ≥ 19–22.9 cm as moderately underweight, and ≥ 23 cm with normal nutritional status [31, 32].

Screening for risk of malnutrition

Risk of malnutrition was assessed using the Mini Nutritional Assessment Short-Form (MNA®-SF) [33]. The MNA-SF questionnaire asks participants if, over the past three months, they have experienced reduced food intake, weight loss, difficulties in mobility, psychological stress, neuropsychological problems, or BMI < 19 kg/m2 [33]. The MNA-SF has a score of 0 to 14; a score of 14 to 12 indicates well-nourished, 11 to 8, at risk of malnutrition, and 7 to 0, malnourished [33].

Diagnosing malnutrition

Participants who were at risk of malnutrition/malnourished using the MNA-SF were further assessed for malnutrition using the GLIM criteria. Participants were diagnosed with malnutrition if they met at least one phenotypic and one etiologic GLIM criterion [34]. The phenotypic criteria included unintentional weight loss over the past three months or BMI < 20 kg/m2 for participants aged less than 70 years and BMI < 22 kg/m2 for those aged 70 years and above [34], or reduced muscle mass, as measured by a MUAC of less than 23 cm [31, 32]. The etiologic criteria included participant experience of reduced food intake or difficulty assimilating food due to loss of appetite, digestive problems (such as diarrhea or vomiting), or swallowing difficulties [34] or disease burden/inflammation associated with acute disease or injury-related conditions, such as major infections, burns, or chronic disease-related conditions, such as cancer, chronic kidney disease, or congestive heart failure [34, 35].

Appetite assessment

The Simplified Nutritional Appetite Questionnaire (SNAQ) [36, 37] was used to assess appetite. SNAQ includes four questions: participant appetite, feeling of fullness after eating, taste of food, and number of meals per day [36, 37]. SNAQ has a score ranging from 4 to 20. A score < 14 indicates poor appetite and ≥ 14 indicates good appetite [36, 37].

Physical function assessment

Functional status was assessed using the Katz Index of Independence in Activities of Daily Living tool (Katz Index ADL) [38, 39]. The Katz Index ADL measures the ability to perform activities of daily living independently, including bathing, dressing, toileting, transferring, continence, and feeding. The Katz Index ADL score of 6 indicates full function, 4-moderate impairment, and ≤ 2 indicates severe functional impairment [38, 39]. Handgrip strength (HGS) was measured twice using a hand-held dynamometer (CAMRY EH 101) with participants in a seated position using both the dominant and non-dominant hand at maximal contraction until the pointer/needle stopped moving for three seconds. Recorded values were averaged [40, 41]. Low HGS was defined as < 16 kg for females and < 27 kg for males, respectively [42].

Food insecurity assessment

Food insecurity was assessed using the 12-month food insecurity experience scale (FIES) questionnaire [43]. The FIES has eight questions that ask if the participant, over the past 12 months, due to limited resources and or money, had worried about not having enough food, being unable to eat healthy and nutritious foods, ate few kinds of foods, skipped meals, ate less than usual, ran out of food, felt hungry but did not eat, or went for the whole day without eating [43]. A “yes” response has a score of one, and a “no” score of zero [43] with the total score ranging from 0 to 8 [43]. A score of 0 to 3 classifies a participant as food secure, 4 to 6 as moderately food insecure, and 7 to 8 as severely food insecure [43, 44].

Dietary quality assessment

Dietary quality was assessed using a 30-day food frequency questionnaire with food groups categorized as healthy or unhealthy to derive a Prime Diet Quality Score (PDQS) [45]. The healthy food groups include dark green leafy vegetables, cruciferous vegetables, deep orange vegetables and tubers, other vegetables, deep orange fruits, citrus fruits, other fruits, beans, peas, soy products, nuts and seeds, poultry, fish, whole-grains, liquid oils, low-fat dairy, and eggs [45]. The unhealthy food groups include white roots and tubers, red meat, processed meat, refined grains and baked goods, sugar-sweetened beverages, sweets, sugary snacks, and ice cream, and fried foods [45]. For the healthy food groups, consumption of 1 time/month or less and 2–3 times/month was coded zero, 1–2 times/week and 3–4 times/week as one, and 5–6 times/week or 1 time/day or ≥ 2 times/day as two, while the unhealthy food groups were coded reversely. The total PDQS ranges from 0 to 42 [40]; a higher score indicates better diet quality. In this study, based on the PDQS percentiles, a score of ≤ 18 indicated low diet quality, 19 to 20, moderate diet quality, and ≥ 21, high diet quality, for which the 25th percentile score was 17, the 50th percentile score was 19, and the 75th percentile score was 21.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were undertaken using IBM SPSS- version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, USA). Descriptive statistics, including percentages and frequencies, were performed on all categorical variables, and independent sample t-tests and analyses of variance (ANOVA) were performed on continuous variables.

The dependent variable was categorized into well-nourished as zero and risk of malnutrition/malnourished as one. Unadjusted binary logistic regression was conducted, and all independent variables with p < 0.05 were included in the multiple logistic regression. Pearson correlation analysis (r ≥ 0.7) indicated high correlation. The Variance Inflation Factor was used to assess for multicollinearity of all independent variables included in the multiple logistic model. P-values were two-tailed, and all statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

A total of 315 older adults (41.6% female, n = 131) with a mean age of 66.83 ± 6.78 years (range: 60–93 years) participated, with the majority (68.9%) in the 60–69 years age range (Table 1). More males (84.1%, n = 155) were married (p < 0.0001), smoked (p = 0.001), and had high alcohol intake (p < 0.0001) compared with females (Table 1).

Clinical characteristics

Overall, the five primary diagnostic categories were infectious diseases (21.9%), cancer (14.0%), orthopedic diseases (13.3%), hypertension (13.0%), and gastrointestinal diseases (11.1%). Poly-morbidity was present in 23.5% of the participants, and 28.9% reported polypharmacy (Table 1). Significant differences (p < 0.0001) existed in the primary diagnoses between males and females (Table 1).

Risk of malnutrition using MNA-SF

Overall, 40.3% of the participants were malnourished, 39.7% were at risk of malnutrition, and 20% were well-nourished (Table 1). A similar proportion of males and females were at risk of malnutrition and malnourished (p = 0.068).

Malnutrition diagnosed using GLIM criteria

The 252 participants at risk of malnutrition/malnourished using the MNA-SF (Table 1) were further assessed for malnutrition using the GLIM criteria, with 75% (n = 189) classified as malnourished. Malnutrition was significantly (p = 0.006) higher in males (81.2%; n = 121) than in females (66.0%; n = 68).

Appetite status

In comparison to good appetite, poor appetite was more frequently reported by participants who were at risk of malnutrition/malnourished (61.5%) using MNA-SF than those who were well-nourished (25.4%) (p < 0.0001, Table 2).

Physical function

Using the Katz Index of ADL (p < 0.0001), moderate to severe functional impairment was more evident in participants at risk of malnutrition/malnourished compared to the well-nourished (Table 2). Similarly, mean handgrip strength (p < 0.0001) was lower in the participants at risk of malnutrition/malnourished (14.16±5.48) than in the well-nourished (17.03±5.03 kg, Table 2).

Food insecurity status

Overall, 54.6% of the participants were food secure (Table 2). Among those who were food insecure, severe food insecurity was more prevalent among participants screened at risk of malnutrition/malnourished (39.3%) than among well-nourished participants (15.9%) (p = 0.002, Table 2). Additionally, the participants at risk of malnutrition/malnourished responded “yes” to the FIES questions, as depicted in Fig. 2.

Dietary quality

The mean PDQS of the well-nourished participants (19.73 ± 3.19) was significantly higher than that of the at-risk of malnutrition/malnourished participants (18.44 ± 3.18) (Table 2). Overall, 45.7% of participants had low diet quality, 25.1% had moderate diet quality, and 29.2% had high diet quality (Table 2). Among participants screened at risk of malnutrition/malnourished, regular consumption (5–6 times/week or daily) of foods such as eggs, beans, peas, soy products, low-fat dairy, nuts, and seeds, fruits, and whole grains was limited to a minority of participants (Fig. 3)

Factors associated with risk of malnutrition/malnutrition

Table 3 depicts the unadjusted and adjusted logistic regressions. The multiple logistic regression included the variable achieving p < 0.05 in the unadjusted model. In the unadjusted logistic regression, the odds of being at risk of malnutrition/malnourished on admission to hospital significantly (p < 0.05) increased with being married, former/current alcohol intake, polypharmacy, moderate to severe functional impairments, poor appetite, severe food insecurity, and low diet quality. In the multiple logistic regression, the risk of malnutrition/malnutrition was significantly associated with poor appetite (AOR 2.581; 95% CI 1.310–5.086; p = 0.006), severe functional impairment (AOR 5.182; 95% CI: 1.469–18.279; p = 0.011), polypharmacy (AOR 2.673; 95% CI:1.112–6.426; p = 0.028), and severe food insecurity (AOR 2.293; 95%CI 1.015–5.176; p = 0.046).

Discussion

Using validated screening tools, our study found that risk of malnutrition/malnutrition, severe food insecurity, and low dietary quality are highly prevalent in older adults on admission to the hospital in Malawi. Risk of malnutrition/malnutrition on admission were significantly associated with poor appetite, reduced functional independence, polypharmacy, and severe food insecurity.

In this study, 80% of older adults were at risk of malnutrition/malnourished on admission, a finding similar to that reported by other studies conducted in healthcare and community settings within the African region using MNA/MNA-SF [12–16]. This indicates an unrecognized public health problem leading to poor prognosis and quality of life during and after illness. Poor appetite is common in older adults, and during illness, there is an increase in nutritional needs due to changes in metabolic demands, which can further exacerbate malnutrition. Poor appetite is associated with reduced food intake, unintentional weight loss, deteriorating nutritional status and malnutrition, more extended hospitalization, functional decline, and mortality [46–49]. In this study, over half of the participants experienced poor appetite, which is clearly associated with future weight loss and malnutrition [50].

A decline in physical function among older adults may limit access to food, cooking and eating abilities, and other daily activities, including health-related social needs, hence negatively affecting quality of life [51, 52]. Moderate to severe functional impairment was significantly associated with risk of malnutrition/malnutrition in the current study. We also noted that reduced handgrip strength, an indicator of physical function, was generally low in the study participants, regardless of their nutritional status (Table 2). Although handgrip strength is affected by nutritional status [53, 54], the small proportion of normally nourished participants and heterogeneity in disease conditions and severity among our study participants are likely to have influenced our findings. Reduced functional independence increases risks of dietary inadequacies, malnutrition, and mortality in older adults [12, 51, 52, 55–58]. The Mature Adults Cohort of the Malawi Longitudinal Study of Families and Health reports that the majority of older adults (average 60 years old) have handgrip strength well below what would be expected despite frequent engagement with physical work and exercise [59]. A 7-year European longitudinal study of adults aged>60 years reported that malnutrition and ADL dependence were linked, and the combination of malnutrition and ADL dependence was most strongly associated with mortality [52]. This is supported by a multi-cohort meta-analysis that identified difﬁculty walking and climbing stairs, prior hospitalization, and hospitalization (in addition to age and marital status) as common determinants of incident malnutrition [60]. Prolonging strength and functional independence appear to be important approaches to malnutrition prevention, and monitoring physical function could be considered alongside malnutrition screening in Malawian hospital protocols.

Although the proportion of older adults with polypharmacy in this study was low (n = 91 [28.1%], Table 1), in both unadjusted and adjusted models, polypharmacy consistently remained significantly associated with risk of malnutrition/malnutrition (Table 3). Polypharmacy has multiple side effects that can affect nutritional status, such as nausea, diarrhea, vomiting, dry mouth, reduced food intake due to poor appetite, as well as drug-nutrient interactions [36]. Recent systematic reviews, meta-analyses [29, 61], and cross-sectional studies [62, 63] report a consistent association between polypharmacy and malnutrition risk/malnutrition in older adults. However, whether polypharmacy itself contributes to poor nutritional status or is a result of a clinical condition that requires multiple medications remains unclear. More research on how the number and category of prescribed medications can influence nutritional status is required [61].

Previous studies have reported that food insecurity increases the risk of malnutrition, hospitalization, and health care costs and reduces dietary quality and health outcomes in older adults [13, 20, 64–66]. Similarly, severe food insecurity was associated with the risk of malnutrition/malnutrition in the present study. Food insecurity was more prevalent in participants at risk of malnutrition/malnourished than in those who were well-nourished. The mechanisms for managing food insecurity reported by participants included skipping meals, going the full day without eating, eating less than usual, and eating fewer foods (Fig. 1). Since malnutrition can be disease-related or hunger-related [5, 24], screening for malnutrition and food insecurity should be undertaken concurrently in healthcare settings in Malawi and similar economically disadvantaged countries. Furthermore, to ensure continuity of nutrition care, those at risk of malnutrition/malnourished must be linked to community nutrition-related interventions after hospital discharge.

Although the data on dietary quality in older adults are limited in Malawi, a recent policy brief by HelpAge International and Malawi Network of Older Persons’ Organizations reported low dietary diversity and inadequate food consumption among older adults in Malawi [67]. A high-quality diet is critical for effective recovery from illness; in the present study, the 30-day diet quality of participants was generally low, especially in those who were at risk of malnutrition/malnourished. The frequency (per week) of consumption of foods rich in protein, such as eggs, poultry, fish, nuts and seeds, beans, peas and soy products, and dairy products, was limited, with the majority reporting 1 time/month and or 2-3 times/month (Fig. 3) providing some indication that before hospitalization, low diet quality was associated with risk of malnutrition/malnutrition, in the unadjusted model (Table 3). Inadequate diet quality and low diet diversity in older adults have been previously reported to be associated with incident malnutrition, reduced quality of life, and worse clinical/health outcomes [5, 13, 19, 68, 69]. Therefore, ensuring that older adults have access to appropriate nutrition-related interventions that can improve diet quality before, during, and after illness is essential for maintaining optimal nutritional status [69].

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include the use of validated tools that are frequently used to screen for risk of malnutrition/malnutrition, functional status, appetite, food security, and dietary quality. Hence, the interrelationships observed are accurate given our current understanding of such tools, and our results can be compared internationally. Limitations include the study's cross-sectional nature, which prevents the determination of causal or predictive relationships. Additionally, our study sample was limited to older adults admitted to hospital with heterogeneous primary diagnostic categories. We also acknowledge that although household income influences food insecurity, household income data of study participants were not collected. A further potential limitation is that our study set out to determine risk of malnutrition and the factors associated with risk rather than the prevalence and severity of malnutrition as diagnosed using the GLIM criteria. The diagnosis of malnutrition using the GLIM criteria was made using the data collected, and enabled us to provide data on the prevalence of malnutrition diagnosis using GLIM for global comparative purposes, but does not provide data on the severity of malnutrition or the factors associated with a malnutrition diagnosis per se.

Conclusion

Using a malnutrition screening tool validated and recommended for use with older adults, risk of malnutrition/malnutrition at hospital admission are high in older adults in Malawi, and are significantly associated with poor appetite, reduced functional independence, polypharmacy, and food insecurity. The study findings highlight the urgent need for routine malnutrition risk screening among older adults on admission to the hospital and diagnostic assessment and documentation of malnutrition severity for those identified at risk of malnutrition. Given the high prevalence of food insecurity and low dietary quality on admission, referral to food and nutrition programs is necessary to ensure continuity of nutrition care and prevent further deterioration of nutritional status, improve prognosis and quality of life, as well as contribute to reduced healthcare costs.
