Protocol for iterative in vivo selection of highly metastatic ovarian cancer cell populations and metastasis modeling in mice
Authors: Yuqi Wang, Mangze Hu, Yilong Zou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe molecular basis underlying ovarian cancers’ multi-route metastasis remains poorly defined. This protocol details iterative in vivo selection of highly metastatic cancer cell populations and metastasis modeling in ovarian cancer. We outline steps for inoculation of ES-2-GFP-Luc, monitoring tumor growth and visceral spread, isolation and culture of tumor-derived derivatives, reinoculation of ES-2-MC1-Hep to establish ES-2-MC2-Hep, and assessment of metastatic colonization efficiency. This protocol enables mechanistic studies of distant metastasis in gynecological cancers.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Wang et al.1Subject areas: Cell isolation, Cancer, Model Organisms

Before you begin

Ovarian cancer metastasis presents a formidable challenge, as most patients exhibit widespread disease at the time of diagnosis.2 Clinical observations indicate that ovarian cancer metastasizes through multiple complex routes, including peritoneal, hematogenous, and lymphatic pathways.3 Key challenges in elucidating the mechanisms underlying metastasis include limited access to tissues from diverse metastatic sites and the lack of appropriate animal models that faithfully and efficiently recapitulate the clinical features of ovarian cancer metastasis. Herein, we establish a de novo metastatic mouse model that captures the major processes of clinical ovarian cancer metastasis, providing a valuable tool for investigating the mechanisms underlying ovarian cancer metastasis with precision.1

This protocol provides a detailed description for the development of a mouse model for ovarian cancer distant metastasis, enabling investigations into metastasis biology, biomarker discovery, therapeutic evaluations, and preclinical development of treatment strategies. Essential materials for this protocol include: immuno-deficient host mice, GFP-firefly luciferase labeled ES-2 cells (ES-2-GFP-Luc), RPMI 1640 basic culture medium, Trypsin, Penicillin-Streptomycin antibiotics, fetal bovine serum (FBS), Phosphate buffer saline (PBS), D-luciferin sodium salt, Tumor Dissociation kit, Red Blood Cell Lysis buffer, BioSpace Optima small animal imaging system, gentleMACS C tube, gentleMACS Octo Dissociator with Heaters, fluorescence-activated cell sorter (MA900 Multi-Application Cell Sorter), and M3 Vision software.

To construct the ES-2-GFP-Luc cell line, the cDNA encoding firefly luciferase (Luc) was cloned into the pCDH-Cop-GFP vector, which had been linearized with EcoRI and NotI, using a T4 ligase. The pCDH-Cop-GFP-Luc lentiviral vector was co-transfected with the packaging plasmids pLP1, pLP2, and pLP/VSVG at a 3:1:1:1 mass ratio into HEK293-T cells using the Lipofectamine 2000 reagent to generate GFP-Luc lentiviruses. Viral supernatant was harvested 48 h post-transfection and filtered through 0.45 μm membranes to remove cellular debris. ES-2 cells were subsequently infected with the GFP-Luc lentivirus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 5–10 for 48 h, followed by flow sorting of GFP+ ES-2 cells using the MA900 Multi-Application Cell Sorter. After expansion of the ES-2-GFP-Luc cells, in vitro bioluminescence imaging (BLI) was performed to confirm stable expression and activity of the firefly luciferase labeling marker (Figure 1).

Innovation

Understanding the molecular mechanisms driving ovarian cancer metastasis has been hindered by the lack of suitable in vivo models that accurately reflect the complexity of clinical diseases. Ovarian cancer spreads through multiple routes—including via the peritoneal fluid, blood circulation, and lymphatics—yet existing experimental models fail to capture these multifaceted dissemination processes. Here, we present a de novo metastasis mouse model developed through repetitive in vivo selection to enrich for ovarian cancer cell populations with enhanced metastatic potential to distant organs. This model closely mirrors the clinical features of ovarian cancer metastasis, including organotropism and colonization dynamics. Unlike conventional models that often rely on artificial strategies or focus solely on peritoneal spread, our approach enables the study of spontaneous and systemic dissemination of cancer cells, thereby offering a more physiologically-relevant system. This work provides a powerful tool for uncovering the molecular drivers of ovarian cancer metastasis, identifying potential biomarkers, and evaluating therapeutic strategies in a preclinical setting. It bridges a critical gap between experimental modeling and clinical relevance, paving the way for deeper mechanistic insights and the development of more effective metastasis-targeted interventions.

Preparation of D-luciferin sodium salt reagent

Timing: 30 min

This protocol employs luciferase-based bioluminescence imaging to monitor the growth of tumor cells in vivo.4,5 Bioluminescence is generated by the oxidation reaction between the enzyme firefly luciferase and its natural substrate, D-luciferin. The bioluminescence emitted by D-luciferin has a maximum wavelength (λmax) of 562 nm, which can be detected and measured using a bioluminescence imaging (BLI) system. We recommend preparing D-luciferin sodium salt at a stock concentration of 15 mg/mL (100×) in 1×PBS, with a working concentration of 0.15 mg/mL (1×) for in vitro BLI and 150 mg/kg for in vivo BLI. Prepare aliquots of sterile-filtered stock in appropriate volumes to minimize repeated freeze-thaw cycles.

CRITICAL: Ensure that the D-luciferin reagent remains protected from light during preparation, application, and storage. The maximum storage duration is about one year at −20°C or −80°C.

Preparation of enzymes for tumor dissociation

Timing: 30 min

This protocol generates single-cell suspensions from xenografts by combining mechanical dissociation with enzymatic digestion. Prior to tissue dissociation, users should prepare Enzymes H, R, and A according to the manufacturer’s instructions for the Tumor Dissociation Kit (human, MACS, Cat# 130-95-929) and aliquot them into appropriate volumes to minimize repeated freeze-thaw cycles. Following dissociation, the cells should be processed immediately for downstream applications, including cell culture or isolation.

CRITICAL: Users need to sterile-filter Enzyme H before aliquoting. The aliquots of these enzymes can be stored for up to 6 months at −20°C.

Inoculation of ES-2-GFP-Luc cells in nude mice

Timing: 2 h

However, alternative injection approaches may be employed depending on the specific anatomical characteristics of the metastasis being studied.

Expected outcomes

By following this protocol, users can generate ovarian cancer cell lines enriched with clones exhibiting enhanced metastatic potential across multiple target organs and via different routes of dissemination, thereby establishing a metastatic model that partially recapitulates the clinical characteristics of ovarian cancer metastasis.

For generating the ES-2 derivatives with enhanced visceral metastatic potential, we first implanted 5 × 106 ES-2-GFP-Luc cells into each nude mouse via intraperitoneal injection. Seven days after injection, we observed the wide-spread peritoneal metastases in the peritoneal cavity using in vivo BLI. Additionally, at day 14 post-i.p. injection, we detected simultaneous distant metastasis in the livers and lungs through ex vivo BLI (Figure 2).

We isolated the ES-2-MC1-Hep and ES-2-MC1-Lung cells from the metastatic livers and lungs, respectively, of nude mice that had been i.p.-injected with ES-2-GFP-Luc cells. After culturing and expanding these cells, we re-inoculated 1 × 106 ES-2-MC1-Hep or ES-2-MC1-Lung cells into each nude mouse via i.p. injection. Nine days post-injection, we observed efficient intraperitoneal growth of ES-2-MC1-Hep cells. However, we found that ES-2-MC1-Lung cells displayed low intraperitoneal re-growth rates for unidentified reasons. As a result, we further enriched the ES-2-MC2-Hep and ES-2-MC2-Lung cell populations from the metastatic livers and lungs, respectively, of nude mice that had been i.p.-implanted with ES-2-MC1-Hep cells (Figure 3).

Limitations

Ovarian cancer metastasizes through diverse routes including the transcoelomic and hematogenous pathways. The initial stage of ovarian cancer metastasis involves the detachment of cancer cells from the primary tumor and their growth in the peritoneal fluid. Motivated by the clinical metastatic characteristics of ovarian cancer, we employed an iterative in vivo selection process7,8 starting with intraperitoneal injection, considering the challenges associated with performing orthotopic injections and modeling of ovarian cancer cells. To be noted, this strategy bypasses the early steps of the metastatic cascade, particularly the detachment of tumor cells from neighboring cells and extracellular matrix in the primary tumors. Hence, the metastatic efficiency of ovarian cancer cell clones selected through our strategy in spontaneous metastasis models established via orthotopic injection warrants further evaluation. Additionally, not all solid tumors primarily metastasize via the transcoelomic route, which is a unique feature frequently associated with abdominal cancers including ovarian cancer. Therefore, the strategy we practiced in this protocol is only applicable to solid tumors that can metastasize through the peritoneal route. In addition, the cell clones selected by our protocol are derived from the human ES-2 cell line and cannot be used for establishing immunocompetent mouse models of metastasis.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Due to the aggressive growth of ES-2-GFP-Luc or ES-2-MC1-Hep cells following intraperitoneal implantation, mice succumb to metastatic burden prematurely, hindering the adequate establishment of distant visceral metastasis (steps 6–13).

Potential solution

Establishment of distant visceral metastasis is a prolonged and complex cascade of events. We recommend closely monitoring the development of ascites in nude mice following intraperitoneal injection of ES-2-GFP-Luc or ES-2-MC1-Hep cells. If excessive ascites accumulation compromises host animal health, surgical drainage can be performed to extend their survival with appropriate ethical approval, thereby allowing sufficient time for metastatic lesions to establish.

Problem 2

ES-2-GFP-Luc cell growth in vivo cannot be monitored by either in vivo or ex vivo BLI following intraperitoneal injection (steps 6–9).

Potential solution

In this protocol, we monitored the in vivo growth of ES-2-GFP-Luc and its derivatives using the luciferase-luciferin bioluminescence imaging system. ES-2 cells were labeled with a fused GFP-firefly luciferase construct, and GFP+ ES-2 cells were sorted by flow cytometry to generate a stable ES-2 cell line expressing GFP-Luciferase. However, GFP expression alone does not guarantee a sufficient amount of enzymatically-active luciferase to be expressed. We recommend assessing the enzymatic activity of luciferase in ES-2-GFP-Luc cells via in vitro BLI to ensure sufficient reactivity before implanting them into nude mice via intraperitoneal injection. On the other hand, BLI imaging may not be sensitive enough to image rare cell populations. In this case, fluorescent imaging using GFP or antibody-based immunofluorescence staining against the firefly luciferase protein may be employed to trace the minor tumor cell populations.

Problem 3

The low efficiency of cell sorting in isolating GFP+ cells derived from metastatic livers and lung of nude mice i.p.-injected with ES-2-GFP-Luc or ES-2-MC1-Hep cells (steps 18 and 27).

Potential solution

The parameters for cell sorting could be tuned towards higher cell viability, including reducing the flow speed, using larger chip size, etc. Meanwhile, if the challenge is due to low fluorescent signal, using parental cells with higher GFP expression levels as starting materials for the experiment is recommended. Specifically, a GFP+ cell sorting efficiency exceeding 10% is considered acceptable.

Problem 4

Although significant tumor cell growth in the peritoneal cavity can be observed, in vivo BLI signals cannot be detected following injection with D-luciferin (steps 6–9).

Potential solution

The rapid growth and excessive accumulation of tumor cells in the peritoneal cavity may lead to systemic absorption and metabolic abnormalities in nude mice, potentially disrupting the luciferase-luciferin catalytic reaction. Simultaneously, to minimize the impact of anesthesia on luciferin absorption and metabolism, we recommend administering intraperitoneal luciferin injection prior to anesthesia during in vivo BLI. Finally, during image acquisition, mice should be placed on a heating pad to maintain optimal body temperature, which facilitates luciferin absorption and metabolism.
