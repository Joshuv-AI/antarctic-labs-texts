Residual Stroke Risk Among Patients With Atrial Fibrillation Prescribed Oral Anticoagulants: A Patient‐Level Meta‐Analysis From COMBINE AF
Authors: Linda S. Johnson, Alexander P. Benz, Ashkan Shoamanesh, John W. Eikelboom, Michael Ezekowitz, Robert P. Giugliano, Lars Wallentin, Christian T. Ruff, Renato D. Lopes, Sanjit Jolly, Richard Whitlock, Christopher B. Granger, Stuart Connolly, Jeffrey S. Healey
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite oral anticoagulation, patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) remain at risk of ischemic stroke and systemic embolism (SE) events. For patients whose residual risk is sufficiently high, additional therapies might be useful to mitigate stroke risk.Methods and ResultsIndividual patient data from 5 landmark trials testing oral anticoagulation in AF were pooled in A Collaboration Between Multiple Institutions to Better Investigate Non‐Vitamin K Antagonist Oral Anticoagulant Use in AF (COMBINE AF). We calculated the rate of ischemic stroke/SE among oral anticoagulation–treated patients with a CHA2DS2‐VASc score≥2, across strata of CHA2DS2‐VASc score, stroke history, and AF type, as either paroxysmal or nonparoxysmal. We included 71 794 patients with AF (median age 72 years, interquartile range, 13 years, 61.3% male) randomized to a direct oral anticoagulant or vitamin K antagonist, and followed for a mean of 2.1 (±0.8) years. The median CHA2DS2‐VASc score was 4 (interquartile range, 3–5), 18.8% had a prior stroke, and 76.4% had nonparoxysmal AF. The overall rate of stroke/SE was 1.33%/y (95% CI, 1.27–1.39); 1.38%/y (95% CI, 1.31–1.45) for nonparoxysmal AF, and 1.15%/y (95% CI, 1.05–1.27) for paroxysmal AF. The rate of ischemic stroke/SE increased by a rate ratio of 1.36 (95% CI, 1.32–1.41) per 1‐point increase in CHA2DS2‐VASc, reaching 1.67%/y (95% CI, 1.59–1.75) ≥4 CHA2DS2‐VASc points. Patients with both nonparoxysmal AF and CHA2DS2‐VASc ≥4 had a stroke/SE rate of 1.75%/y (95% CI, 1.66–1.85). In patients with a prior stroke, the risk was 2.51%/y (95% CI, 2.33–2.71).ConclusionsAF type, CHA2DS2‐VASc score, and stroke history can identify patients with AF, who despite oral anticoagulation have a residual stroke/SE risk of 1.5% to 2.5% per year. Evaluation of additional stroke/SE prevention strategies in high‐risk patients is warranted.

The benefit of anticoagulation to prevent stroke in atrial fibrillation (AF) is firmly established. 1 , 2 Warfarin reduces stroke risk by two thirds, and direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) either further reduce stroke, reduce bleeding, or both. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 However, several issues limit the long‐term effectiveness of oral anticoagulation, including a high rate of both temporary and permanent discontinuation, 8 , 9 , 10 and poor patient adherence to dosing regimens. 8 , 11 , 12 , 13 Fewer than 2 in 3 patients have been shown to fill prescriptions for ≥80% of days of medication doses, 8 and 2‐year persistence rates of ≈50% have been reported. 10 , 14 Observational studies also report prescription of inappropriately low doses in 10% to 35% of patients with AF, resulting in a lack of effect on stroke risk. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 Finally, even with optimal anticoagulation some patients experience breakthrough events.

Stroke risk among patients with AF depends on clinical risk factors. While the ABC AF and CHADS2 risk scores predict risk, 21 , 22 , 23 the CHA2DS2‐VASc score is the most widely used, 24 and is recommended in guidelines. 1 However, CHA2DS2‐VASc may underestimate the effect of a history of stroke on recurrent stroke rates, 25 which is doubled in patients with a history of stroke compared with those without. 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 Furthermore, in subjects with stroke despite ongoing oral anticoagulation, the subsequent ischemic stroke rates are as high as 7% to 8% in the first year. 31 , 32 AF burden, 33 as well as whether AF is paroxysmal or nonparoxysmal, also predict the absolute stroke risk, independent of clinical stroke risk factors, with a higher risk among individuals with nonparoxysmal AF. 34 , 35 Thus, using simple tools such as prior stroke history, CHA2DS2‐VASc score, 36 and AF type, it should be possible to identify patient groups that have a high residual stroke risk despite oral anticoagulation, who may benefit from additional stroke‐prevention therapy.

COMBINE AF (A Collaboration Between Multiple Institutions to Better Investigate Non‐Vitamin K Antagonist Oral Anticoagulant Use in AF) contains individual‐level harmonized data from all landmark trials of DOACs. 37 We aimed to use this unique database to describe residual risks of ischemic stroke and systemic embolism (SE) according to CHA2DS2‐VASc score as well as history of stroke and AF pattern.

METHODS

Study Population

COMBINE AF consists of 77 282 individuals included in one of the major DOAC trials: RE‐LY, which randomized 18 113 subjects to 150 mg twice‐daily dabigatran (n=6076), 110 mg twice‐daily dabigatran (n=6015) or warfarin (n=6022), 7 ARISTOTLE, which randomized 18 201 subjects to 5 mg apixaban daily (n=9120) or warfarin (n=9081), 3 AVERROES, which randomized 5599 subjects to either 5 mg apixaban daily (n=2808) or aspirin (n=2791), 38 ENGAGE‐AF TIMI 48, which randomized 21 105 subjects to 60 mg edoxaban (n=7035), 30 mg edoxaban (n=7034) or warfarin (n=7036), 4 and ROCKET‐AF, which randomized 14 264 subjects to either 20/15 mg rivaroxaban (n=7131) or warfarin (n=7133). 39 Patients with moderate‐to‐severe mitral stenosis or mechanical valves were excluded from all trials. The COMBINE AF database has been described in detail previously. 37

For these analyses, we exclude subjects randomized to aspirin in AVERROES. We also excluded subjects with missing data for history of stroke, and CHA2DS2‐VASc score, 1 subject with missing data for time to first stroke event, and 2676 subjects with a CHA2DS2‐ VASc score<2. The derivation of the study population is presented in Figure 1. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. Institutional review board approval has been obtained for all COMBINE component trials and the subjects provided written informed consent.

Data Collection and Variable Definitions

Data collection in COMBINE AF has been described in detail previously. 6 ROCKET‐AF included 202 subjects with new‐onset AF, which we consider paroxysmal. Subjects were classified according to their randomized treatment as either warfarin, standard dose DOAC (20/15 mg rivaroxaban, 60/30 mg edoxaban, 5/2.5 mg apixaban, and 150 mg dabigatran), or low‐dose DOAC (30/15 mg edoxaban or 110 mg dabigatran). Dose reduction regimens mandated by protocol, for example, due to age, 3 , 38 renal failure, 3 , 4 , 38 , 39 low body weight, 3 , 4 , 38 or concomitant use of quinidine, verapamil, or dronedarone, 4 did not influence these classifications. Stroke/SE rates are reported according to these classifications, as well as overall, and for key study results also separately for patients randomized to either warfarin or approved doses of DOACs (excluding 7030 patients randomized to 30/15 mg edoxaban in ENGAGE‐TIMI 48). Patients randomized to dabigatran 110 mg, which is an approved dose, for example, in Europe, were retained in these analyses.

The primary outcome was a combined end point of ischemic/unspecified stroke or SE, according to original trial definitions, which were similar across trials, with the stroke outcome adjudicated by a blinded committee of stroke neurologists, using computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging of the brain when available. 31

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are reported as mean±SD, except for age and CHA2DS2‐ VASc scores, which are reported as median and interquartile range, and compared using Student t test and the Mann–Whitney U test, respectively. Categorical variables are reported as n (%) with between‐group comparisons using Pearson χ2 test.

Incident event rates are calculated overall and according to randomization status, with censoring at the time of the efficacy outcomes, and given as cases per 100 person‐years at risk with 95% CIs. The primary analysis calculates incidence rates until first stroke or SE, with reporting across CHA2DS2‐ VASc scores, according to drug class and dose, AF type, and for subjects with and without a history of stroke. To estimate the risk increase for stroke across strata of CHA2DS2‐VASc score, by AF type, and in subjects with and without prior stroke, we used stratified Cox models with clustering on COMBINE component trial, and robust SEs using the Lin and Wei method. Incidence rates after permanent discontinuation of study drug are calculated for subjects without a stroke event before permanent discontinuation and with at least 10 days of follow‐up after time of permanent discontinuation. Information regarding alternative antithrombotic therapy after permanent discontinuation was not available.

In order to assess the influence on stroke/SE incidence rates by the competing risk of mortality, cumulative incidence functions based on competing risk regression models with the Fine and Gray method are presented with truncation after 3 years of follow‐up, at which time only 12% of subjects remained at risk. 6 We also present unadjusted Kaplan–Meier failure functions.

Finally, in order to assess the effect of recurrent events on stroke rates, an analysis using the Anderson and Gill version of the conditional risk set model was conducted, with time at risk until efficacy censoring. 40 Ischemic stroke or SE events that occurred on the same day as the first postrandomization ischemic stroke or SE are retained in the conditional risk model analyses.

RESULTS

The study population includes 71 794 subjects, among whom there were 1974 (2.7%) subjects with at least 1 stroke/SE event, and 167 (0.2%) recurrent stroke/SE events during the course of the studies (Figure 1). Mean follow‐up was 2.1 (±0.8) years, during which time there were also 6400 (8.9%) deaths. The median age was 72 (interquartile range 13) years and 61.2% were male. A history of stroke was reported in 18.8%. At enrollment the median CHA2DS2‐VASc score was 4 (interquartile range 3–5); 61.3% of the population had a CHA2DS2‐Vasc score≥4. Further baseline characteristics according to AF type are reported in Table 1. A total of 28 226 subjects were randomized to warfarin, 30 798 subjects were randomized to standard‐dose DOAC, and 12 770 subjects were randomized to low‐dose DOAC.

Among subjects with a history of stroke (19% of the study population), the risk of recurrent stroke was 2.51% (95% CI, 2.33–2.71) overall per year (Table 4). Subjects with a prior stroke had higher rates of stroke/SE (CHA2DS2‐VASc adjusted HR 1.70 [95% CI,1.53–1.90, P<0.001]), and lower risk increase per additional CHA2DS2‐VASc score point compared with those without a history of stroke (Figure 4, Figure S3), HR 1.10 (95% CI,1.10–1.16, P<0.001) per additional CHA2DS2‐VASc score point among patients with a history of stroke, and HR 1.32 (95% CI,1.26–1.37, P<0.001) per additional CHA2DS2‐VASc score point among patients without a history of stroke. Among patients with a history of stroke randomized to approved doses of DOAC, the risk of recurrent stroke was slightly lower, 2.39% per year (95% CI, 2.20–2.59). A CHA2DS2‐VASc score of 6 in a subject without a prior stroke was associated with a lower stroke/SE rate than a CHA2DS2‐VASc score of 4 in a subject with a prior stroke (Table 4). A cumulative incidence function with a competing risk of mortality is presented in Figure 4. In subjects with a history of stroke and CHA2DS2‐ VASc score≥3 (18% of the study population) the annual stroke/SE rate was 2.52% (2.34–2.72), and the corresponding rates for CHA2DS2‐ VASc scores ≥4 (17% of the study population) and ≥5 (13% of the study population) were 2.62% (95% CI, 2.43–2.84), and 2.72% (95% CI, 2.49–2.96). Among subjects with a history of stroke, nonparoxysmal AF, and a CHA2DS2‐ VASc score≥4 (13% of the population), the annual stroke rate was 2.69% (95% CI, 2.46–2.94). Results from the conditional risk set model yielded slightly higher annual stroke/SE rates and are reported in Table S3.

Stroke rates after permanent discontinuation of study drug were calculated among 16 011 (22.3%) of subjects who had not had a stroke/SE before permanent discontinuation. Median time to discontinuation was 246 days (interquartile range, 75–518 days) and 14% of the overall population had discontinued after 1 year. Reasons for permanent discontinuation are given in Table S4.

After permanent discontinuation, there were 684 stroke/SE events, at an annual rate of 3.61% (95% CI, 3.35–3.89) overall. In subjects without and with a history of stroke the annual stroke/SE rates after permanent discontinuation were 3.00% (95% CI, 2.74–3.28) and 6.48% (95% CI, 5.67–7.40), respectively.

DISCUSSION

Despite treatment with OACs in a randomized control trial setting, the residual stroke/SE risk remains high in many patients, particularly in subjects with higher CHA2DS2‐VASc scores, nonparoxysmal AF, or a history of stroke. Three quarters of the patients enrolled in these anticoagulation trials had nonparoxysmal AF, and those who also had a CHA2DS2‐VASc score≥4 had an annual stroke/SE risk of 1.8%. One fifth of the study population had a prior stroke, which was associated with a particularly high risk: 2.5% per year. Thus, despite the use of oral anticoagulation, half of the patients in the seminal DOAC trials had a residual stroke risk of 1.8% to 2.5% per year.

While these results apply to a clinical trial population, previous nationwide registries indicate that the more general population of individuals with AF have a high proportion of these risk factors for stroke despite anticoagulation. 25 , 41 , 42 CHA2DS2‐VASc scores ≥4 were found in 38% and 40% of Danish and Swedish patients with a hospital diagnosis of AF and who were not receiving oral anticoagulants, 25 , 41 and among 48% of patients in the Taiwan National Health Research Institutes with nonvalvular AF. 42 Nonparoxysmal AF, which is not included in the CHA2DS2‐VASc score, was associated with higher unadjusted stroke/SE risks, a finding in line with previous studies from the component trials in COMBINE AF, 35 , 43 , 44 , 45 and a recent meta‐analysis. 23 A similar proportion (≈75%) of subjects in this study had nonparoxysmal AF as in the international Real‐life global survey evaluating patients with AF (RealiseAF) study of patients with AF in 26 countries. 46

A history of stroke remains an important risk factor for stroke among patients with AF, including those who are receiving oral anticoagulation. Previous studies using observational data sources indicate that a large proportion of patients with AF have had a prior stroke: 19% in Taiwan, 14.5% of newly diagnosed patients with AF in the United Kingdom, 47 and 13% in Sweden. 41 , 42 These data are consistent with the 19% with a history of stroke reported in this study. These data pertain to subjects with a history of stroke at any previous time, but as shown in recently published data from COMBINE AF, stroke risk is even higher in the year subsequent to a first stroke, at 7%. 31 High stroke rates have also been reported using pooled data from European cohorts, 32 , 48 as well as from the COMBINE AF component trials. 28 , 29 , 30

Besides these factors related to AF type, age and comorbidities, factors related to inappropriate use of OACs, including inappropriate dose selection, 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 temporary and permanent discontinuation of medication, 8 , 9 , 10 , 49 and low adherence also contribute to residual stroke risk in patients who receive oral anticoagulation. 8 , 11 , 12 , 13 Premature permanent discontinuation, which occurred in more than 1 in 5 subjects, resulted in high stroke/SE risk: 3.6% overall and 6.5% annually in subjects with a history of stroke. The permanent discontinuation rate was somewhat lower than in the Global Registry on Long‐term Oral Anti‐thrombotic Treatment in Patients with Atrial Fibrillation (GLORIA‐AF) and similar to the Outcomes Registry for Better Informed Treatment of Atrial Fibrillation II (ORBIT‐AF II), 50 , 51 both of which captured discontinuation at study follow‐up visits. Studies using filled prescription registers have reported higher discontinuation rates, however: 20% after 1 year in the United Kingdom, 10 and ≈50% after 1 year in Germany and Denmark. 8 , 14 This may reflect higher discontinuation rates outside of a clinical study setting, or a conflation of adherence and persistence; patients who do not take all doses (ie. low adherence) may fill fewer prescriptions. Unfortunately, no information regarding use of antithrombotic drugs after discontinuation of study drug is available in COMBINE AF.

Potential interventions exist that could reduce residual stroke/SE risk, including efforts to improve adherence, overview of potential interactions between anticoagulants and other prescribed medication and dietary factors, and optimization of medical therapy and lifestyle interventions to control stroke risk factors. 52 In patients with vitamin K antagonist treatments, efforts to improve the time in therapeutic range of drug need to be considered. The left atrial appendage (LAA) is the origin of the majority of all thrombi in patients who do not have a mechanical valve or rheumatic mitral stenosis. 53 In the LAA occlusion study III (LAAOS III), surgical LAA occlusion in addition to usual care, including anticoagulation in ≈80%, cut stroke/SE rates by a third in patients with AF undergoing cardiac surgery, 54 with a similar effect of LAA closure in patients with and without oral anticoagulation. 55 LAA closure can also be achieved with a cardiac catheterization technique, for example, with the Watchman, LARIAT, and the Amplatzer Amulet, 56 and has been shown to be noninferior to oral anticoagulation in high‐risk patients in the PRAGUE‐17 trial, where the mean CHA2DS2‐VASc score was 4.7±1.5. In the EWOLUTION registry of 1020 patients implanted with a Watchman device, of whom half had a CHA2DS2‐VASc score ≥5 and 45% had a history of stroke/transient ischemic attack, the stroke rate was 1.3% annually, 57 and the periprocedural complication rate was 2.7%. 58 Percutaneous LAA occlusion in addition to anticoagulation in patients with high residual stroke risk thus has the potential to substantially reduce AF‐related stroke incidence, but there is as yet no evidence to support this combination. The effectiveness of this approach will be tested in the upcoming LAAOS‐4 trial (NCT 05963698), which will enroll anticoagulated patients with nonparoxysmal AF alone, or paroxysmal AF and a history of stroke, and a CHA2DS2‐VASc score ≥4.

Limitations

Despite the strengths of this study, including the unique and high‐quality data on which it is based, some limitations exist that should be considered. First of all, it is important to note that COMBINE AF comprises clinical trial patients, and in some respects may not be generalizable to all patients, due to factors related to the selection into the trial. Patients with mechanical heart valves or moderate–severe mitral stenosis, 37 creatinine clearance of <either 30 or 25 mL/min, high bleeding risks, oral anticoagulation use for other reasons, and other factors that may influence ability to adhere to study protocol are excluded. 3 , 7 , 29 , 38 , 39 The second limitation is that this selection of patients in randomized trials tends to result in lower‐risk populations than registries with higher compliance with therapies; thus these results may not be fully generalizable to the general population. Given the greater baseline risk of patients in real‐world cohorts and their lower adherence to anticoagulation, our estimates of the residual risk of stroke may be too low. Furthermore, patients who discontinue study drug in a clinical trial may do this for other reasons compared with patients who discontinue in clinical practice, and may therefore differ in regard to their baseline risk of outcomes. Furthermore, the COMBINE AF database regrettably does not contain information regarding use of other anticoagulation after permanent discontinuation of study drug. Finally, these analyses pertain to subjects who are offered anticoagulation use, but it should be noted that underuse of anticoagulation in patients with AF in whom guidelines would recommend such treatment remains common. 59

CONCLUSIONS

A substantial proportion of patients with AF have residual stroke risks 1.5% to 2.5% yearly despite treatment with oral anticoagulants; these high‐risk patients can be identified by their AF type, CHA2DS2‐VASc score, and prior stroke history. Permanent discontinuation of oral anticoagulation is associated with substantial stroke risk.
