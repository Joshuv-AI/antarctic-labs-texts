Dictyostelium discoideum chemotaxis is altered by hypoxia to orient streaming toward higher oxygen levels
Authors: Satomi Hirose, Julie Hesnard, Kenichi Funamoto, Jean-Paul Rieu, Christophe Anjard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDictyostelium discoideum (Dd) exhibits a unique life cycle marked by its transition from single-cell amoeboid movement to multicellular development in response to environmental stimuli. While its chemotactic response to cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) has been extensively studied, experiments are usually carried out in a high oxygen (O2) environment (21% O2, also called normoxia) that might not reflect the natural condition for a soil amoeba as low O2 (hypoxia) is common underground. Our recent research has unveiled a novel phenomenon termed aerotaxis, wherein Dd cells migrate towards O2-rich environments under hypoxia.ResultsWe tested Dd response when submitted to two different stress sources: starvation and hypoxia. While both stresses induced a motile response, namely chemotaxis and aerotaxis, we were able to decouple both types of responses to explore potential shared mechanisms. Importantly, aerotaxis appeared to be operated independently of known chemotactic pathways, demonstrating unique signaling pathways and cellular responses. Aerotaxis contributed to Dd developmental processes by letting cells escape from acute hypoxia. Even though chemotactic response could occur at < 2% O2, it did not lead to well organized or stable streaming. Furthermore, in an O2 gradient, aggregation centers for chemotactic response appeared preferentially at higher O2 levels. RT-qPCR analysis showed that hypoxia only slightly reduced the expression of genes required for chemotactic response, suggesting that the bias toward high O2 might occur at another level. Reoxygenation of cells that had been starved in hypoxic conditions for 18 h allowed rapid aggregation but required de novo protein synthesis.ConclusionAerotaxis and chemotaxis mechanisms do not interact directly. However, when cells are exposed to both starvation and hypoxia, both mechanisms can be combined to direct the migration of cells toward places with higher O2 levels. As hypoxia is frequent in the soil where Dd usually grows, formation of aggregation centers at or close to the soil surface, where O2 is abundant, will be advantageous. Low O2 levels do not preclude cells from participating in streaming but seem to reduce or delay their ability to produce stable aggregation centers, resulting in a bias favoring centers that form at higher O2 levels.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12860-025-00557-5.

Background

The social amoeba Dictyostelium discoideum (Dd) is an obligate aerobic organism that lives in the soil, feeding on bacteria. Dd exhibits a distinctive life cycle, transitioning between unicellular and multicellular forms. In nutrient-rich conditions, Dd lives as individual amoeboid cells. Upon starvation, however, the cells initiate a developmental program involving aggregation and differentiation, ultimately leading to the formation of fruiting bodies that ensure spore dispersal [1, 2]. This transition relies on the capacity of Dd to sense and respond to a wide range of environmental cues, including chemicals, temperature or light [3–5], electric fields [6, 7], and oxygen (O2) [8]. A central component of this developmental response is chemotaxis toward cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), a key signaling molecule that mediates long-range intercellular communication and directs coordinated migration [9, 10]. Chemotactic cells polarize and elongate along cAMP gradients, relaying the signal to their neighbors and organizing into head-to-tail streams that enhance collective motility [11, 12]. Although the initial positioning of aggregation centers appears largely random, competition and fusion events eventually lead to evenly spaced territories, each comprising approximately 10⁵ cells. At the molecular level, starvation triggers a rapid and temporally regulated transcriptional reprogramming [13], inducing genes involved in signal production and sensing, such as adenylyl cyclases responsible for cAMP synthesis, and cAMP receptors (CARs) required for gradient detection. As development proceeds, Protein Kinase A (PKA) becomes activated and promotes the transition to later stages, including terminal differentiation. The developmental process is also closely associated with the induction of several cell adhesion proteins. Notably, the contact site A glycoprotein (CsaA) and the calcium-dependent adhesion molecule (CadA) are expressed shortly after the onset of development [14, 15]. Later on, the transition from the unicellular to the multicellular stage is governed by the polymorphic adhesion molecules TgrB1 and TgrC1, which also play a central role in allorecognition [16]. Although the molecular basis of this coordinated motility is well understood, the initial establishment and maintenance of aggregation centers remain less clear [17].

In recent years, using a double-layer microfluidic device able to control precisely O₂ gradients, we found that Dd single cells in a nutrient medium detect and respond to O2 gradients below ~ 2% O₂, exhibiting both directional migration toward higher O₂ (aerotaxis) and increased speed in low-O₂ (aerokinesis) [18, 19]. Using a simple confinement assay consisting of covering a spot of Dd cells with a cover glass, we also showed that O2 consumption induces self-generated O₂ gradients with a concentration below 0.1% O₂ at the spot center and around 0.5% O2 at the edges [18]. This O₂ gradient combined with aerotaxis triggers the formation of a robust migrating dense cell ring [18], also observed in an independent study [20]. However, the molecular mechanisms behind the O2 sensing in Dd cells and how it affects motility remain unknown. Our previous work excluded the involvement of PhyA, the Dd homolog of mammalian prolyl hydroxylase (PHD) enzymes, which regulate the hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) modification and trigger the transcriptional response to hypoxia [19]. Moreover, we showed that the aerotactic migration and O2-sensing of Dd cells are not hampered by the Reactive Oxygen Species/Reactive Nitrogen Species (ROS/RNS) and inhibition of mitochondrial functions [19].

Previous experiments were performed on vegetative cells, while in the present study, we investigate the response of the starved cells to hypoxia and O₂ gradients. As starvation and lack of O2 (hypoxia) are common stresses for a soil-dwelling micro-organism, we decided to explore how Dd responds when subjected to both. To this end, we first conducted experiments under controlled O2 gradients using a microfluidic system and employed chemical treatments to decouple aerotactic responses from starvation-induced signaling and cAMP-mediated chemotaxis. We further explored how O2 availability modulates chemotaxis by analyzing the spatial patterns of aggregation under different O₂ concentrations. To complement these observations, we performed RT-qPCRs to assess O2-dependent gene expression changes. Finally, we evaluated the effect of reoxygenation on streaming formation and investigated the role of de novo protein synthesis in this process.

Methods

Cell culture

The wild types AX2 and KAX3 of Dd cells (S00001 and S00034) were provided by National Bio-Resource Project Cellular slime molds (NBRP Nenkin). The cAR1 and cAR3 null mutant RI9 cell line was a kind gift from Prof. Satoshi Sawai from The University of Tokyo, Japan. AX2 was used for all experiments unless otherwise mentioned. Cells were cultured axenically with HL5 medium (HLF2; Formedium, United Kingdom) containing 10 g/L glucose and 10 U/ml penicillin-streptomycin (P/S) (15140-122; Gibco, United States) on cell culture dishes at 22 °C or in shaking culture at 180 rpm. For starvation conditions, cells were cultured in phosphate buffer (PB) solution (12 mM KH2PO4, 8 mM Na2HPO4, pH 6.5) or the glucose-free HL5 medium.

Oxygen gradient experiments in microfluidic devices

Experiments under O2 control were done in microfluidic devices as we previously reported [19, 21] and described in Supplementary Figure S1. Briefly, the device has two gas channels to supply gas mixtures at predefined O2 concentrations, above the media channels where Dd cells were seeded. An O2 gradient was generated in the media channels by supplying the gas mixtures at 0% and 21% O2 balancing with nitrogen at 30 mL/min (Figure S1B). The O₂ gradient was calculated using COMSOL Multiphysics (ver. 5.5, COMSOL AB, Sweden). For the detailed features and performance of the device, please see our previous publication [21].

Dd cells were harvested from the culture dishes and introduced into the media channels in the microfluidic device with the HL5 medium. Cell density was 2 × 106 cells/mL and 1 × 107 cells/mL for a short-term experiment to observe aerotaxis and a long-term experiment to observe cell aggregation under starvation, respectively. After incubation for 20 min to adhere Dd cells to the bottom surface of the media channels, the HL5 medium was replaced by fresh one or PB solution for starvation conditions. Caffeine (C2042; Tokyo Chemical Industry, Japan) was added to HL5 medium at a final concentration of 3 mM, while thapsigargin (029-17281; Fujifilm Wako Chemicals, Japan) was added at 2 or 5 µM as indicated. For accurate comparisons, one of the three media channels in each device was used for experiment under the control condition, while the other channels were used simultaneously for experiments under different conditions. The device was placed in a stage incubator (TP-CHSQ-C, Tokai Hit, Japan) mounted on an inverted microscope (IX83, Olympus, Japan), and controlled at 22 °C. Just after gas mixtures were supplied to the gas channels, time-lapse imaging was started at a 5-min frame rate. As previously described, we calculated the strength of the aerotaxis with the aerotactic index (AI), defined as the ratio between the distance migrated in the direction along the O2 gradient and the total length of cell trajectory (Figures S1C-E), in the same way as chemotactic index was calculated [19]. Moreover, the distribution of initial aggregation centers was investigated manually as it corresponded to the initial points of convergence of chemotactic cells.

Streaming assay in homogeneous hypoxic and normoxic conditions

The assay was adapted from a standard streaming assay to allow multiposition videomicroscopy simultaneously in a homemade hypoxic chamber as well as in ambient conditions. AX2 cells were washed once with developmental buffer (DB) solution (20 mM sodium phosphate buffer, pH 6.5; 1 mM CaCl₂; 2 mM MgSO₄) and then seeded in two different 6-well plates (at 1 or 2 × 10⁶ cells per well). After 30 min, 2 ml of light paraffin oil (8012–9 S-1, VWR, USA) was slowly added on the top of each well to allow imaging for over 24 h without evaporation or condensation. One plate was incubated in the hypoxic chamber preconditioned with the different O₂ levels (1%, 2% or 4% O2) while the other was maintained under normoxic (21% O₂) atmospheric conditions. The O₂ concentration in the hypoxic chamber was monitored with an optical fiber probe coupled with its respirometer (OXROB3 robust probe and Firesting oximeter, Pyroscience, Germany). Both plates were positioned on a motorized videomicroscopy platform allowing imaging of both hypoxic and control conditions. This platform, controlled by LAS X software (Leica, Germany), included a binocular microscope (MZ16, Leica), a motorized stage (XY-Scanning stage 150 × 100 mm, Marzhauser, Germany), a CCD camera (DMC2900, Leica) and a transmitted light base (TL300, Leica) to obtain dark field images of a large field of view at low zoom. Images were taken at a 10-min frame rate and were subsequently analyzed and filtered using Adjust Contrast, Enhance Local Contrast and Crop built-in functions of ImageJ. Image subtraction using a one-frame difference was performed to enhance contrast using the Image calculator “subtract” built-in function of ImageJ.

Reoxygenation was induced by opening the hypoxic chamber and stopping the gas flow. Cycloheximide (C7698, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) from a 200 mM stock in DMSO was added at 400 µM 30 min prior to reoxygenation to inhibit protein synthesis. DMSO (D8418, Sigma-Aldrich) alone was used in the control condition. Cycloheximide was added approximately 18 h after the cells were placed under 1% O₂, as preliminary experiments showed that reoxygenation after 24 h of hypoxia sometimes resulted in poor or absent aggregation, even in the absence of treatment. The experiment was performed three times.

RT-q-PCR

A total of 2 × 10⁶ cells per well were washed once with DB and then seeded in 35 mm single well plates. Two plates were incubated in our hypoxic chamber preconditioned with 1% O₂, while the other two were maintained under normoxic (21% O₂) atmospheric conditions. The O2 concentration was monitored with the above-mentioned optical fiber probe coupled with its respirometer. Cells were harvested after 6 and 24 h of incubation in both O2 conditions. Additionally, 2 × 10⁶ cells were collected directly from HL5 medium to serve as the standard condition t0.

Total RNA was extracted (74134, RNeasy Plus Mini Kit, Qiagen, Germany), quantified and adjusted to 500 ng to synthesize cDNA (11483188001, First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit for RTPCR, Roche, Switzerland) using random primers at 250 ng/µl (F415L, DyNAmo Flash SYBR Green qPCR Kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). cDNAs were diluted at 1/10 to perform qPCR (LineGene 9600 PLUS, Bioer, China), using the following primers:

pkaC-F 5’-GTACTGGAACATTTGGAAAG-3’, pkaC-R 5’-GAATGGATGAGAGAATTGAT-3’,

tgrB1-F 5’- GGAGTGGTTACTATCAATGGTC-3’, tgrB1-R 5’-GGTTTAGTGCCAGATGTGTAG-3’,

cadA-F 5’-GGCTCAAGGCAGTACAAACA-3’, cadA-R 5’-GGTCATTTCATATGAACCAGC-3’,

csaA-F 5’CGCATCGTTACAATGCCAATT-3’, csaA-R 5’-AAGACCATTACCACCTGTAGA-3’,

rnlA-F 5’-GCACCTCGATGTCGGCTTAA-3’, rnlA-R 5‘-CACCCCAACCCTTGGAAACT-3’.

cxgs-F 5‘-AAGTTGTTAAATCTCAACTC-3’, cxgS-R 5’-TTTATCAACGCCATATTTAA-3’.

The experiments were performed in triplicate for each gene. The relative gene expression was determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method [22] using rnlA as a housekeeping gene and the t0 condition as the standard.

Results

Aerotaxis is altered under severe starvation

We began our study by evaluating how starvation influences aerotactic behavior using our previously described double-layer microfluidic device with O2 gradient control (Supplementary Figure S1). For this purpose, we observed Dd migration directed by a positive O2 concentration gradient not only in nutrient medium (HL5 medium), but also in PB solution without nutrients or glucose-free HL5 cell medium, and calculated the aerotactic index AI (Fig. 1A). Under the starvation condition with PB solution, the maximum AI was decreased compared to the conditions with HL5 medium and the glucose-free HL5 medium (Fig. 1C). These data suggest that lack of glucose has no effect on aerotaxis while severe starvation decreased cell capacity to migrate towards O2.

Aerotaxis has different mechanisms from known cAMP chemotaxis

Subsequently, we investigated whether aerotaxis shares common signaling mechanisms with established starvation-induced responses and chemotaxis. It has been reported that the starvation response of Dd cells is transduced by an increase in intracellular calcium ions [23] that can also be triggered by addition of thapsigargin, a Ca2+ uptake inhibitor of the ER, resulting in enhanced aggregation. Under the microfluidic-controlled O2 gradient with supplementation of thapsigargin to the HL5 medium, aerotaxis tended to decrease (Fig. 1B, D), showing a similar tendency to that observed with PB solution. Therefore, signaling pathways relating to starvation might be interfering with the aerotactic response without abolishing it.

Next, association of aerotaxis with cAMP relay was investigated by supplementing 3 mM caffeine, an inhibitor of cAMP relay [24], to the HL5 medium or PB solution. It is well known that caffeine blocks aggregation of Dd under starvation. Aerotactic migration under O2 gradient was reduced but not blocked by caffeine (Fig. 2A, C). The reduction could be explained by non-specific effects of caffeine that acts through multiple targets [25]. This implies that the cAMP relay is not directly involved in aerotaxis. To confirm the independence of aerotaxis from cAMP relay, the experiments were also performed with the RI9 strain which is deficient in the cAMP receptors cAR1 and cAR3. Lacking those genes, RI9 cells in PB solution are not able to start cAMP relay. We checked that these cells did not show aggregation under starvation (not shown). However, RI9 cells exhibited reduced but still significant aerotaxis, pointing toward an independence of aerotaxis from cAMP relay (Fig. 2B, D). Knocking out cAR can affect quite a lot of cell functions such as actin polymerization and various kinase activities, via signal transduction pathways downstream of cAR [26]. This might be the reason for the lower AI of RI9 cells as compared to their parent strain KAX3 (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, while cell polarity was markedly emphasized during chemotaxis (Fig. 2E), for instance by elongated cell shapes, no major change in cell shape and polarity was observed during aerotaxis (Fig. 2F). Together, these observations indicate that starvation signaling attenuates aerotactic migration, while cAMP relay is not directly associated with aerotaxis.

Severe hypoxia disrupts cAMP chemotaxis but not wave propagation

While aerotaxis appeared not to share signaling pathways with chemotaxis or the starvation response, we investigated whether aerotaxis is somehow involved in survival during starvation and cell development. For that, Dd migration under controlled O2 conditions was observed under starvation in PB solution for a longer period and at higher cell density (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Movie M1). Under the O2 gradient, Dd cells showed motility only at O2 concentrations of > 0.5% O2. As clearly visible in Supplementary Movie M1, Dd cells around the most hypoxic region below that level remained quiescent; they rounded up and did not migrate. In contrast, those located at slightly higher concentrations showed aerotaxis and then started to migrate toward aggregation centers as during chemotactic migration in usual normoxic conditions. The initial aggregation centers, defined as the points toward which cells converged, were not distributed randomly but presented a clear shift toward high O2 concentrations, resulting in a chemotactic motility going mostly upward along the O2 gradient (Fig. 3B). In the seven different microfluidic channels, we observed only a single aggregation center occurring under 10% O2, and most formed at > 15% O2 (Fig. 3C). A simple explanation would be that the chemotaxis of cells in hypoxic conditions was impaired. However, careful examination of the movie showed that the cells started coordinated motility toward an aggregation center at the same time across the O2 gradient. Once streaming progressed, it split into multiple small aggregates, as observed in normoxic conditions.

To better understand the effect of hypoxia on chemotactic aggregation, time-lapse experiments under submerged uniform hypoxic conditions were performed in 35 mm dishes (Fig. 4). After a few hours of starvation in DB solution, the cells appeared to increase motility and dark lines corresponding to cells elongating synchronously in response to a cAMP wave were observed using dark field (Supplementary Movies M2-M4). These spiral lines started from potential aggregation centers in normoxia that rapidly led to organized streaming regardless of the O2 concentration in the chamber. The observed dark lines were somewhat less contrasted than the ones previously described [27] because of the typical dark field setups with the long distance between the light source and the cells imposed by the size of the hypoxia chamber and the multi-position platform. Nevertheless, they were sufficient to identify the formation of waves especially when using a basic image subtraction method.

Dark bands were formed under 1% O2 at a similar timing as under normoxia (Supplementary Figure S2). However, they did not induce formation of stable aggregation centers and bands often started to appear in multiple areas in the field of view, resulting in a disorganized pattern (Fig. 4A, middle panel) that sometimes faded away (see Supplementary Movie M2). Interestingly, upon reoxygenation, the cells quickly streamed and aggregated within 2–3 h (Fig. 4A, right panel, and Supplementary Movie M2).

When the same experiment was performed under 2% O2 (Supplementary Movie M3), the dark bands were followed by organized streaming toward aggregation centers with some slight delay compared to the normoxia control (Figs. 4B, left and middle panels, and Supplementary Figure S2). However, the streams quickly dissociated as individual cells moved away from each other at high speed (Fig. 4B, right panel). The dissociation tended to start in one area, seemingly the initial aggregation center, and to propagate steadily downstream at a speed of about 30 to 40 μm/min, like in a chain reaction. In the presented movie, dissociation started at 19 h after the starvation in the upper left corner and propagated toward the middle, reaching its maximum after 23.5 h, when streams started to reform on the top left while dissociation continued further down. The new streams then dissociated repeatedly. The amount and timing of dissociation were variable from one assay to the next but seemed to spread from a “disaggregation center”. Some amount of disaggregation, leading to stream reorganization, was also observed in the Supplementary Movie M2 during the reoxygenation of cells starved under 1% O2.

Under 4% O2, streams were formed and converged into aggregates almost like in normoxia (Fig. 4C, left and middle panels), but in some movies, dissociation events briefly occurred (Fig. 4C, right side of right panel) and then, the cells quickly rejoined nearby streams and formed stable aggregates, albeit with some delay (see Supplementary Movie M4).

Taken together, these results suggest that hypoxia does not disrupt cell polarity in response to cAMP waves, but inhibits cell aggregation at 1% O₂ and interferes with cell–cell association at 2% O₂. The O2 threshold required to sustain stable streaming structures is estimated to be around 4% O₂, though the gradient assays suggest a bias toward even higher levels.

Hypoxia does not inhibit aggregation gene expression

RT-qPCRs were performed to determine if inefficient or unstable streaming in hypoxic conditions was due to poor expression of aggregation-specific genes (Table 1). The hypoxia-induced gene cxgS [19] was included as a control, as it encodes a facultative subunit of cytochrome c oxidase thought to increase the enzyme’s affinity for O₂ under low O₂ conditions [28]. CxgS showed 50 to100-fold induction at both 1% and 2% O2 after 6 and 24-h starvation, showing that hypoxia can induce gene expression, even during starvation. On the other hand, CxgS was repressed after 6-h starvation in normoxic conditions, thus behaving like a growth-specific gene. This is consistent with RNAseq data showing that CxgS was downregulated after 2 h of starvation and remained low afterward during development [29]. acaA, encoding the adenylyl cyclase A (ACA) was induced 300-fold after 6 h starvation and even more after 24 h. ACA is a key protein responsible for cAMP production during early development that will allow both further aggregation-specific gene induction and the organization of chemotactic response for streaming. While hypoxia somewhat reduced ACA induction to 100-200-fold, it remained within error bars of normoxic conditions. The expression of pkaC, a crucial protein kinase for induction of most early aggregation-specific genes, was also similar under hypoxic and normoxic conditions after 6-h starvation but then declined somewhat after 24 h under hypoxia. Since 2% O2 resulted in unstable streams of cells, we checked the expression level of adhesion genes that are involved in cell-cell adhesion during aggregation. The RNA level of cadA barely changed in any condition while csaA was somewhat less induced under hypoxic conditions after 6-h starvation but reached a level similar to that under normoxia after 24 h. Like other highly induced genes we analyzed, the high experimental variability between repeats resulted in values that were within error bars of each other, meaning that the potential differences were not significant. This was true also for tgrB, which was induced 2 to 9-fold after 6-h starvation in all conditions and 100-300-fold after 24 h. Together, these analyses show that hypoxia only slightly reduces the expression of genes required for chemotactic response, suggesting that the bias toward high O2 might occur at another level.

Aggregation upon reoxygenation requires de novo protein synthesis

As transcriptional analysis did not reveal any major defects in classical aggregation-specific genes, we performed an additional experiment to determine if defects in protein synthesis were responsible for the lack of proper aggregation upon hypoxia (Supplementary Movie M5). Cells were starved under normoxia and 1% O2 as previously (Fig. 5A), but the protein synthesis inhibitor cycloheximide or DMSO was added after 18-h incubation (Fig. 5B). By then, aggregation was finished in the normoxic control (Fig. 5B, left panel). Reoxygenation was then started 30 min later, resulting in rapid aggregation for the DMSO-treated cells but not for the cycloheximide-treated ones (Fig. 5C, central and right panels, respectively, and Supplementary Movie M5). These experiments indicate that de novo protein synthesis is required for streaming to proceed, but it does not exclude some regulation at transcriptional level.

Discussion

Dictyostelium is a model organism for cellular motility and displays a wide range of responses to environmental clues. Positive and negative chemotaxis to a variety of molecules has been documented, but the cAMP response remains the best studied. Oxygen is a ubiquitous molecule that can diffuse across membranes and constitutes an odd signal to direct cell motility. Thus, the molecular mechanism behind aerotaxis in Dd remains a mystery. Our present results, summarized in Fig. 6, confirm that it is different from chemotaxis based on cAMP and classical G protein-coupled receptors. In particular, aerotaxis functions only within a narrow range of ~ 0.5% to 2% O2 at most. It has to be noted that, in water, atmospheric 2% O2 results in a concentration of ~ 25 µM, which might be too high to provide spatial information for the molecular mechanism controlling aerotactic motility. In comparison, cAMP acts in the nanomolar range to orient cell motility [30]. While starvation, like hypoxia, ultimately leads to a lack of energy production in the form of ATP, Dd responds differently to these challenges. When exposed to both simultaneously, Dd cells respond within minutes to low O2 by aerotaxis to move out of the danger zone, whereas it takes hours for chemotaxis to initiate, as it requires the expression of a large group of specific genes. The directionality of aerotaxis is far weaker than that of cAMP chemotaxis (0.15 vs. 0.9) as measured by the aerotactic or chemotactic indexes [31]. Furthermore, aerotactic cells lack the cell polarity associated with chemotaxis (Fig. 2F). It is therefore likely that the specialized actin polarization observed during chemotaxis is absent during aerotaxis. These results are compatible with a previous study that the G-beta protein was not involved in the sensing mechanism for aerotaxis, in contrast to cAMP chemotaxis [20].

We have previously shown that low O2 does not prevent transcription nor translation, as thousands of genes were specifically upregulated in response to this stress [32], and transcription of aggregation-competent genes was barely affected by hypoxia. However, our results show that hypoxia affects cAMP chemotaxis in a more subtle way: while cells can chemotax at 1% O2, they cannot establish a stable directionality but can follow the signal produced by cells that are forming an aggregation center at a higher O2 level. The cells can thus use the efficiency of chemotaxis to move up an O2 gradient in an O2 range where aerotaxis does not work. Our results also show that it is possible to uncouple the chemotactic response from the formation of aggregation centers without gene mutation or the use of drugs, which opens a new way to study those two events and how they are normally coupled. We have shown that such coupling requires the biosynthesis of unknown proteins in an O2-rich environment. Although stable aggregation centers do form under a uniform 4% O2, this might not correspond to an absolute threshold but to a minimum, with higher O2 allowing better or faster aggregation center formation. This is consistent with the observation of only one aggregation center forming under 5% O2 in our gradient experiments, while most occur under >15% O2 (Fig. 3). It has been proposed that initial aggregation centers correspond to random areas of higher than average cell density that produce more cAMP than the neighboring cells [17]. As nearby cells move closer, the density increases further and the formation of other aggregation centers in the vicinity is prevented. When O2 diffusion is limited, the increase in cell density will result in rapid depletion due to respiration. Dd appears to have evolved a fail-safe mechanism to prevent aggregation at a site where high cell density would be detrimental. Respiration in a non-vascular multicellular structure more than three cells deep usually results in anoxia leading to a necrotic core (reviewed in [33]). Such a problem will be exacerbated if the outside O2 level is already low. The cycloheximide experiment showed that the formation of an aggregation center required the synthesis of one or more proteins that was restricted by low O2. While the responsible proteins are not identified, the fact that chemotaxis functions under low O2 excludes the core proteins required for cAMP relay, cell polarity, or motility. Some signaling pathways are known to modulate cAMP chemotaxis [34] but do not fully match our results. The conditioned medium factor (CMF) is accumulated by growing cells and then secreted upon starvation. CMF acts as a quorum sensing factor to allow streaming only when cell density is high enough by limiting the activation of the cAMP signaling pathways downstream of CAR1 [35, 36]. Exposure to 1% O2 could result in a lack of secretion of CMF or inactivate downstream response, but such blockage also prevents the accumulation of csaA mRNA [37], while we observed a near-normal level of induction. Moreover, the high expression of the late-streaming gene tgrC1 and the presence of bands in dark field under 1% O2 suggest that the cAMP relay functions sufficiently to induce gene expression and chemotactic motility. However, we cannot completely exclude the possibility that the CMF signaling pathway or some of its components are involved in preventing the streaming of starving cells in 1% O2.

It is not possible to determine whether the mechanism behind the stream disaggregation observed under 2% O2 is a variant of the inhibition of streaming under 1% O2 or whether it uses a completely different signaling pathway. The second hypothesis seems more likely since under 2% O2, there were clear aggregation centers and streams were initially well formed with cells touching and following each other. This means that cell-cell adhesion has been initiated, involving first CadA and then CsaA proteins [38]. The rapid disaggregation propagating downward along the stream involves breaking this cell-cell adhesion, from head to tail, combined with some coordinated cell motility away from each other for some time. The high speed of propagation of this reverse streaming suggests some mechanical cues could be involved: The sudden breaking of the adhesion by a cell on the front (head) of the stream would cause a change in tension in the cell behind that would then snap away from the next cell down the stream (tail). However, the implication of chemical signals to initiate or propagate the disaggregation sounds likely. One hypothesis would involve an overstimulated countin factor (CF) signaling pathway that is known to break streams when the local cell density is too high [39]. However, the classical overstimulation of this pathway observed in the smlA null mutant resulted in the formation of many tiny aggregates rather than the complete dissolution of the streams and aggregation centers [40, 41]. CF is a high molecular weight multiprotein complex that is constantly secreted by starving cells [42] and breaks streaming when reaching high local concentration within a stream. The slow diffusion rate of large complexes appears incompatible with the fast-spreading wave of disaggregation we observed under 2% O2, which simultaneously affected multiple streams that were not connected each other and often >1 mm apart. It does seem possible that this disaggregation occurs through a modified CF pathway or reuse of some of its components. Chemorepulsion like the one observed with AprA [43] or 8CPT-cAMP [44] might also be at play. AprA is normally secreted during cell growth and will induce peripheral cells to move away from a dense colony through the GrlH receptor [45]. It is therefore unlikely that AprA is directly involved, but another ligand might be used in hypoxia. 8CPT-cAMP is a synthetic analog of cAMP that acts through CAR1 to trigger chemorepulsion [46]. While 8CPT-cAMP is not known to occur naturally, it is possible that Dd produces an analogous molecule during hypoxia since this chemorepulsion mechanism is clearly functional and efficient [47]. Whatever molecular mechanism is used, the purpose seems to be to avoid forming compact multicellular structures in low O₂, as competing cells’ respiration would further deplete the limiting resource. The disaggregation would thus allow the cells to “breathe” before trying to aggregate again in a potentially better environment.

Dictyostelium has clearly evolved mechanisms to limit poor outcomes when faced with multiple challenges simultaneously. Since low environmental O2 levels were the norm till ~ 600 million years ago, before the Neoproterozoic oxygenation event and the generalization of multicellularity, it is likely that mechanisms to sense and avoid hypoxia appeared before the chemotactic system resulting in multicellular fruiting bodies [48]. Indeed, the ancestral response to starvation in amoebae is the formation of unicellular microcysts that would limit the requirement to find a high O2 environment.

Conclusion

Our work has uncovered unexpected interactions between two different common stress responses: to the lack of food or the lack of O2 necessary to metabolize food efficiently. In Dd, both stresses induce cell motility to reach a better outcome. While chemotaxis of Dd has been widely studied as a survival strategy, aerotaxis in eukaryotes has only been studied in recent years. Our understanding is still limited, but aerotaxis seems to enhance survival strategy through a mechanism completely different from the known chemotaxis, as opposed to electrotaxis [6]. Aerotaxis acts rapidly and doesn’t require de novo protein synthesis [18] but provides a poor guidance efficiency [31], whereas chemotaxis is slower to initiate but more efficient (Fig. 6). Surprisingly, hypoxia modulates the cAMP chemotactic response to avoid low O2 and instead promotes aggregation at an O2 concentration that is above the functional range of aerotaxis. The underlying mechanisms remain unknown but appear to influence both the position of aggregation centers and the stability of chemotactic streams to guide cells to an O2-rich environment. This would be a useful strategy for a soil amoeba that forms multicellular structures to increase its dispersal above ground. Thus, elucidating these mechanisms and their effects on chemotaxis could lead to the discovery of a hidden mechanism in O2 response and survival strategy potentially conserved in eukaryotic cells, including human cells.
