From pharmacokinetic-guided FVIII prophylaxis to low-dose Emicizumab prophylaxis: a pilot study revealed a flush of hope
Authors: Juan Ye, Kun Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Limited studies investigated the low dose emicizumab prophylaxis, especially in patients switched from pharmacokinetic (PK)-guided FVIII prophylaxis. A retrospective study was conducted among inhibitor-free boys with severe hemophilia A (SHA). They received PK-guided FVIII prophylaxis for six months and then switched to low dose emicizumab regimen for one year. The patients’ demographic data, PK profiles and clinical outcomes (annualized bleeding rates and zero bleeding proportions) were collected from six-month pre-switch to one-year post-switch. The target joints and direct cost of treatment were also calculated. Twenty boys with SHA were enrolled and the median age was 4.3 years (IQR 3.1–5.8). With a median dose and infusion frequency of 25.3 IU/kg and 3.5 infusions per week, their trough FVIII level is 3.5 IU/dL with range of 2.0-5.8 IU/dL. After their switch to emicizumab, the median dose was 3.5 mg/kg/month with range of 3.0–4.0 mg/kg/month. Significantly improved annualized bleeding rate [0(0, 0.75) vs. 2(2,4), P < 0.0001], annualized treated bleeding rate [0(0, 0.75) vs. 1.0 (0, 3.5), P < 0.01], annualized joint bleeding rate [0(0,0) vs. 0(0, 3.5), P < 0.05] and zero bleeding proportion [75% vs. 20%, P < 0.01] were observed. Compared with FVIII, low dose emicizumab showed a much lower direct cost [median, 26676 vs. 35568 dollars yearly, P < 0.01]. Participants received well improved clinical outcomes of reduced annualized bleeding rates and increased zero bleeding proportions. The emicizumab prophylaxis demonstrated a significant decrease in direct cost of 25% compared with FVIII infusions. PK-guided individualized emicizumab prophylaxis could be expected to enhance cost-effectiveness further.

Introduction

Hemophilia A (HA) is a rare, inherited, bleeding disease due to the deficiency of coagulation factor VIII (FVIII), with the updated prevalence of 17.1 cases per 100,000 males [1, 2]. Prophylaxis is still the standard treatment now, as recommended by the latest guidelines [2–4]. The prophylactic dosing regimen was initially proposed in Sweden which set a fixed dosing regimen (25-40IU/kg, every other day) to reach a steady trough FVIII level for bleeding control (the aim was to eliminate spontaneous bleeds at that time) [5]. With the discovery of inter-individual variability of pharmacokinetics (PK) among patients with hemophilia A, flexible dosing regimens instead of fixed dosage and dosing interval has been proved to be a better choice [6, 7]. Through PK tests (which help HA patients obtain their individualized PK profiles) and recommended target FVIII level, personalized prophylaxis showed great potentials to improve clinical outcomes and reach a higher cost-effectiveness of treatment, which could be vital especially in those source-limited regions. However, PK-guided prophylaxis could not solve the issues for those in need of a very high target trough FVIII level. Because high trough FVIII level is naturally based on escalated dosage and short infusion intervals, which caused much pain and heavy burden on patients.

The therapy for hemophilia has been evolving for the last few decades. For instance, the development of extended half-life time FVIII products initially decreased the frequency of infusions. Nowadays, the non-factor therapies also play a vital role in hemophilia care. Emicizumab is the first non-factor bispecific monoclonal antibody approved for hemophilia, with subcutaneous injection once every 2 to 4 weeks [8]. In its well-conducted clinical trials (HAVEN series), participants received reduced bleeds, resolution of injured joints and improved quality of life [9, 10]. However, its widespread has been precluded because of the high annualized cost of recommended dose and limited support from medical insurance as well, which is pretty common in poor countries [11]. Although a few studies on low-dose prophylaxis with emicizumab has been investigated, but their mixed population limited the validness of conclusion, such as mixed inhibitor state and a wide monthly emicizumab dose [12–14].

Therefore, we proposed an idea to check if low-dose emicizumab prophylaxis is a good choice for HA patients who received PK-guided prophylaxis but not satisfied, either with the bleeding control or other reasons. Indeed, there is still scarce of data on how a low dose emicizumab prophylaxis performs in pediatric patients. As a result, this retrospective study was conducted to investigate the clinical outcomes and direct cost of drugs among the pediatric patients in China who switched to low dose emicizumab prophylaxis from PK-guided FVIII prophylaxis.

Methods

Ethical approval

The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University(2025-05-EMI), which also approved a consent waiver.

Study design and participant enrollment

This is a single-center retrospective study based on the clinical visit and chart review among HA patients. The participants were identified as: (1) previously had PK tests and then received PK-guided prophylaxis [15] for at least 1 year (with unchanged dosing regimen for the last half of the year); (2) switched to low-dose emicizumab (3–4 mg/kg/month) for at least 1 year. The primary clinical outcomes (annualized bleeding rates and zero bleeding proportions) were calculated during two periods: FVIII period (the last 6 months of PK-guided prophylaxis) and emicizumab period (the 1 year of emicizumab prophylaxis), and then and then compared between them.

The patients with low dose emicizumab prophylaxis were screened first by phone call and online chat to confirm if they had their PK profiles of previously used FVIII concentrates. The inclusion criteria are similar to our previous PK study [6], which could be condense into: “pediatric inhibitor-free patients with severe HA who have done PK tests, and the results were available”. The additional requirement is their afterwards switch to emicizumab prophylaxis for one year after their previous PK-guided prophylaxis which should last for at least 1 year. Eight-three HA patients on emicizumab prophylaxis were confirmed with previous PK-guided prophylaxis. After exclusion of the patients with short emicizumab period (< 1 year), Totally 62 HA patients who had PK-guided FVIII prophylaxis for more than one year and then switched to emicizumab for more than one year were identified. Finally, 20 participants were enrolled after exclusion of those in lack of results of emicizumab concentrate and equivalent FVIII activity or simply fall out of the target emicizumab dose. Clinical outcomes

The bleeding details were obtained from their routine recordings and verified by doctors to calculate the different annualized bleeding rates, including annualized bleeding rate (ABR), annualized treated bleeding rate (ATBR), annualized joint bleeding rate (AJBR) and annualized spontaneous bleeding rate (ASBR). The zero bleeding proportions were calculated from the proportion of the number of patients with no bleeds among the number of all participants(N = 20). An index joint with ≥ 3 spontaneous bleeds in a consecutive six-month period was defined as a target joint in accordance with previous study [2]. Resolution of target joint bleeding was considered when ≤ 2 bleeds into the index joint within a consecutive twelve-month period. The bleeding rates and zero bleeding proportions were calculated with the patients’ record of bleedings, and the bleeding details were confirmed by two doctors individually with patients and their parents.

Emicizumab prophylaxis and laboratory test

All the patients received first four emicizumab injections of 3 mg/kg weekly and then followed their maintenance treatment of emicizumab with reduced monthly dosage. The dose and dosing interval are flexible as long as the month dosage is 3–4 mg/kg/month. The concentrate of emicizumab and equivalent FVIII level were to be measured in all participating patients during the middle of maintenance period with the previously reported methods [16].

Direct cost of FVIII and emicizumab prophylaxis

To directly compare the cost of FVIII and emicizumab prophylaxis, we obtained the official website price of the FVIII products involved without consideration of other factors like medical insurance. Since the price of their FVIII products are very similar, we set the price with similar integers for convenient calculation. The price used is 137 dollars for 250 IU bottle and 247 dollars for 500IU bottle. The price for emicizumab is 1111.5 dollars for 30 mg and 2223 dollars for 60 mg. The final cost was calculated as a total fee of FVIII or emicizumab for a year.

Statistics

Since the sample size is small, the measurement data was reported as median(mean) with interquartile or/and range. Categorical data was described as number and proportion, like patient number and zero bleeding proportions. For comparison of annualized bleeding rates and direct yearly cost before and after the switch, paired Wilcoxon test was used. Fisher’s tests were utilized to compare the zero bleeding proportions. The analysis and figure generation were conducted using GraphPad Prism for Mac (Version 9.1).

Dosing regimen of FVIII and emicizumab prophylaxis

During their half-year FVIII prophylaxis period, the median trough FVIII level was 3.5 IU/dL, ranging from 2 to 5.8 IU/dL. In detail, 20% of them (N = 4) had a trough level > 5IU/dL, while the 35% of them (N = 7) had a trough level in 2-3IU/dL, and the other 45% (N = 9) hold a trough FVIII level of 3-5IU/dL. During the FVIII prophylaxis period, a median dose of 25.3 IU/kg per infusion and frequency of 3.5 infusions per week were observed. Among the participants, totally 4 target joints were confirmed in three individuals initially, including 2 knees and 2 ankles. After switch to emicizumab, all of them received an escalation period of 4 weeks as described, the concentrate of emicizumab and equivalent FVIII level were tested during the middle of maintenance period. The median emicizumab concentrate is 43.0 ug/mL with a range of 36.4–51.3 ug/mL, and for equivalent FVIII level it is a median of 12.5 IU/dL ranging from 10.7 to 15.5 IU/dL.

Discussion

The FVIII prophylaxis has been the basic treatment of hemophilia A for a long time. With the discovery of inter-individual variability in PK profiles like half-life time, clearance and in vivo recovery, personalized FVIII treatment have been proved to be a better choice other than fixed dosing regimens. As for the target trough FVIII level in prophylaxis, the traditional aim of 1IU/dL which was designed to eliminate spontaneous bleeds has been replaced with the updated recommendation of 1-3IU/dL by World Federal of Hemophilia [2]. The new WFH hemophilia guideline discards the previous target trough FVIII level because many previous studies have revealed the unsatisfied bleeding control, continuous progression of joint injury and weaken quality of life.

In our previous studies, the PK-guided prophylactic protocol was investigated and revealed its ability to help HA patients find their individualized dosing regimen [15]. Most HA patients obtained improved clinical outcomes of decreased bleeding rates and joint protection by flexible dosing regimen aiming at an individualized target FVIII level within the recommended trough FVIII level range. However, a few patients still experience bleeds even with a comparatively high trough FVIII level. As for them, the cost for a higher trough FVIII level would sometimes be insufferable, such as daily infusions and extremely high dose which is not commonly recommended. In our study, we identified those patients. Among the 20 participants enrolled, 80% of them (N = 16) still experience different kinds of bleeds with a trough FVIII level of 2IU/dL, and 11 individuals still bleeds with trough level above 3 IU/dL. Actually, they practically represent those HA patients with severe bleeding phenotype and thus in need of a higher trough level. Another four participants with zero bleeds in our study chose to abandon FVIII prophylaxis for the heavy burden of venipunctures showed. Therefore, this analysis could potentially provide an optimized alternative for these patients.

Since the clinical trials of emicizumab prophylaxis was reported, its convenience and efficacy has been proved to be transformative [17]. Combined with its easy subcutaneous injection with long interval, a reduced dosing regimen (3–4 mg/kg/month) was implied in this study due to the limited affordability in developing country like China. The clinical outcomes were impressive with greatly reduced ABR (mean 0.35 vs. 3.1), ATBR (mean 0.35 vs. 2.0), AJBR (mean 0.1 vs. 1.5) and ASBR (mean 0 vs. 0.4). Similar improvement could also be found in other zero bleeding proportions in spite of a statistically significant Pvalues. Our result was similar to previous low dose emicizumab studies. In Rucha’s study, the median ABR and AJBR also reached zero under a 3 mg/kg/month dosing regimen without a loading dose. Another study also showed the same median ABR and ATBR (both are zero) with us, but their patients had a wider emicizumab dosage (mainly 3–5 mg/kg/month) [11]. Bansal et al. also revealed parallel outcomes, where ABR decreased to 0 in eight and six patients after 48–52 weeks of low dose emicizumab prophylaxis [14]. However, another case series study by Chuansumrit et al. demonstrated much higher ABR (median, 4; mean, 3.8), which should attribute mostly to their very low dose with a range from 1.05 to 1.66 mg/kg/month [13].

Although great bleeding control was achieved, we noticed the only two joint bleeds after switch to emicizumab were induced by sports and not companied with a previous FVIII infusion. As previously reported, the full dose emicizumab prophylaxis could provide similar protection with the consistent FVIII level of 10-20IU/dL [18]. The equivalent FVIII level of our patients mostly ranged 11–15 IU/dL, which is good for daily routine but not suitable for high-intensity exercise [18]. Thus, it is also important to educate the patient emicizumab prophylaxis, such as the potentially necessary extra FVIII usage before competitive sports. Actually, the “mixed therapy” which combines prophylactic emicizumab and provisional FVIII infusions may be one of the optimized answers for HA patients to reach a free life.

Besides, our study also confirmed the correlation between emicizumab monthly dose and emicizumab concentrate (r = 0.72, P < 0.001) or equivalent FVIII level (r = 0.84, P < 0.001), as well as the positive relationship of emicizumab concentrate and equivalent FVIII level (r = 0.62, P < 0.01). Figure 2 This was consistent with previous reports., either with full dose or reduced dose of emicizumab prophylaxis [12, 19, 20]. Thus, it would be a more feasible choice to take use of emicizumab concentrate or equivalent FVIII level to adjust the dosage of emicizumab at maintenance period. However, there do exist few patients with high emicizumab dose were also inexpertly found with relatively low emicizumab concentrate or equivalent FVIII level. For instance, one patient with monthly emicizumab dose of 3.7 mg/kg was recognized with equivalent FVIII level as low as 11.8 IU/dL, while another individual with equivalent FVIII level of 14.1 IU/dL was correlated with a monthly emicizumab dose of 3.6 mg/kg. Similar results could also be found in other studies. In Mao’s study, different patients with a monthly dose of 4 mg/kg could have a wide range of emicizumab concentrate (36.9–54.6 ug/mL) and equivalent FVIII level (8.1–16.2 IU/dL) [12]. Therefore, the interindividual variability of emicizumab’s PK profiles was proved. In addition, a previous DosEmi study has proposed an ideal emicizumab concentrates of 30 ug/mL as a target level, based on data from their phase III ~ IV studies [21]. Another study suggests a target emicizumab concentrate of 48.1 ug/mL for entail bleeding control [12]. Bases on individualized PK profiles and target emicizumab concentrate, the individualized PK-guided emicizumab would be a modern therapy in the future.

In addition, we also compared the direct cost of FVIII and emicizumab among the participants in our study. In developing countries like China, the emicizumab has not been covered by medical insurance. Consequently, HA patients could not afford the cost of emicizumab prophylaxis at a full dose. The young age and low median bodyweight of our participants just actually reflects the fact of limited affordability of emicizumab of them. The older HA children (usually with a heavy bodyweight) would not choose emicizumab because the price is too expensive even with a low dose regimen as current abandon by medical insurance. The simple analysis of the direct cost of drugs showed that low dose emicizumab prophylaxis actually have advantages in drug cost compared with FVIII prophylaxis (median, 26676 vs. 35568; mean, 29334 vs. 39320 dollars per year). As Figure-3(B) showed, only three patients (Patient No.4, Patients No.14 and Patient No.17) have increased direct cost after the switch. Considering the strong and obvious improvements in bleeding rates, the increased cost tends to be cost-effective as well. More importantly, the other 17 patients received both reduced bleeds and lower direct cost at the same time if set aside the effects of medical insurance. Considering the bodyweight of our participants is relatively low, this discrepancy could be even greater for older children. The true cost was not calculated due to the very different medical insurance policy in different regions, which will weak the conclusion. If covered by medical insurance in the future, emicizumab low dose prophylaxis may be widespread with affordable cost as well as the great ability of bleeding control. This message would also be useful for those medical policy makers to take into consideration.

Our study has limitations. Firstly, although the clear improvement of reduced bleeds and resolution of target joints has been demonstrated among our patients, but the restriction of our small number of participants calls for a large cohort study with prospective experimental design, which could further valid the conclusion. Secondly, due to real-world study with a retrospective study design, the dosing intervals of vary from every 10 days to every 21 days (3 weeks). Instead of the standard dosing regimen, our flexible dosing intervals may introduce potential variability in the distribution of peaks and troughs (emicizumab concentrate) during the maintenance period like previously reported. However, considering the consistent clinical outcomes among HA patients with different standard protocols (every week/2 weeks/4 weeks), there is no evidence supporting the obviously unfavorable to our conclusion. But further study on the potential impact on pharmacokinetics and outcomes is still necessary. Lastly, although confirmed by doctors, there is potential bias in clinical outcomes as it was collected by patient-reported bleeding logs.

Conclusion

In summary, we conducted a retrospective study among the sever HA children who received PK-guided FVIII prophylaxis and then switched to low dose emicizumab prophylaxis. Great improved clinical outcomes were observed in these patients with high trough FVIII level before, such as lower annualized bleeding rates and decreased zero bleeding proportions. The target joints were all resolved as well. The comparison of direct cost showed that the low dose emicizumab prophylaxis offers a much higher cost-effectiveness than FVIII if the two drugs are equally treated by medical insurance.
