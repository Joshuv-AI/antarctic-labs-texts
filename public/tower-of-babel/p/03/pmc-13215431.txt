Factors predicting health-enhancing physical activity in patients with chronic widespread pain following a person-centred health plan
Authors: Sofia Juhlin, Kaisa Mannerkorpi, Anette Larsson, Anna Bergenheim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRegular physical activity improves symptoms and overall health in people with chronic widespread pain. However, many struggle to stay active over time, highlighting the need for strategies that support long-term physical activity.PurposeTo investigate if person-centred health plans that include physical activity can support participants with chronic widespread pain to increase and maintain their level of physical activity and improve in physical function after 12 months, and to analyse factors that characterise those who achieve the level of physical activity recommended by the World Health Organization.Materials and methodsA secondary analysis of a randomized controlled trial was conducted. Participants (n = 102) with chronic widespread pain were recruited from primary health care rehabilitation centers. All participants received person-centred health plans including physical activity. The intervention group got support through a digital platform and the control group through a single telephone call. The participants also completed questionnaires at baseline and 12 months. Analyses included changes in physical activity measured as hours of activity, and physical function, assessed with a physical test and a questionnaire, as well as identification of predictors of achieving the recommended physical activity level.ResultsAt baseline, 55% of the participants reached the recommended level of physical activity, as compared to 62% at the follow-up. A higher baseline level of physical activity (OR [95% CI]: 1.23 [1.04, 1.47]) and a positive experience of physical activity in terms of physical relaxation (OR [95% CI]: 0.64 [0.42, 0.97]) were associated with an increased likelihood of achieving the recommended physical activity level.ConclusionParticipants with positive experiences of physical activity were more likely to achieve the recommended physical activity level. Patients with negative experiences may need more support to reach a higher physical activity level.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03434899.Keywords: Pain, chronic pain, exercise therapy, person-centred care, physiotherapy, primary health care

Introduction

Chronic widespread pain (CWP) has been described as pain lasting over three months located along the spine, in both sides of the body and above and below the waist [1]. CWP is also often characterised by a complex presentation of symptoms comprising tenderness, fatigue, psychological distress, impaired physical capacity and reduced general health [2]. CWP is included in the pain type nociplastic pain, which is defined as ‘pain that arises from altered nociception despite no clear evidence of actual or threatened tissue damage causing the activation of peripheral nociceptors or evidence for disease or lesion of the somatosensory system causing the pain’ [3]. In nociplastic pain, the pain becomes a disease in itself, rather than a secondary symptom of another condition [4]. The aetiology of nociplastic pain and CWP can partly be explained by disturbances in pain regulation due to central sensitisation and dysfunction in the endogenous pain inhibition [5,6].

Due to pain and other associated symptoms, people with CWP often have a decreased level of physical activity, which could increase the risk of poor general health, the development of lifestyle-related diseases and excess mortality [7–9]. Regular physical activity improves general health and reduces the risk of health-related diseases [10]. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends a level of physical activity of 150 min per week at a moderate intensity, or 75 min per week at a high intensity, or an equivalent combination of the two, to improve overall health [10]. Physical activity is recommended as a first-line treatment for CWP, and there is strong evidence supporting its efficacy in alleviating symptoms and improving physical capacity [11–14]. Regular physical activity has been found to effectively improve pain and other features associated with CWP such as mood disorders, depression, fatigue, physical function, quality of life, and health status [14,15]. Pain relief following physical activity can partially be explained by a normalisation of peripheral endogenous pain mechanisms [16]. Cognitive behavioural therapy has also been shown to be effective in reducing pain and other symptoms associated with CWP, with robust evidence supporting its use in the management of chronic pain. Moreover, evidence for the effectiveness of combining physical activity with cognitive behavioural therapy is increasing [11,13]

Despite the proven positive effects of physical activity, many people with CWP struggle to maintain the motivation to stay physically active in the long term [17]. This may be partly due to increased pain intensity during and after physical activity [18], as well as other factors such as reduced muscle strength [19], uncertain and fluctuating abilities [20], depression [21], stress level and disability [22].

Person-centred rehabilitation aims to involve the patient as a partner. The patient is a person with abilities, needs and specific wishes, and is the expert of her/his own condition [23]. When adopting a person-centred approach in rehabilitation planning, the focus is on the person′s own resources, barriers and goals [23]. Person-centred rehabilitation can enable persons with CWP to regularly engage in physical activity [24,25]. A health plan is a written plan with activities to improve health that is created by the patient and the health care professional together [25,26]. A person-centred health plan including physical activity has been shown to reduce fatigue in some participants with chronic pain [27], and person-centred exercise has been found to improve symptoms, function and health in people with CWP [24].

People with CWP often need support to be able to adjust and increase their level of physical activity [28,29]. Physiotherapists have the expertise to assist patients in increasing their physical activity. In order to reduce symptoms and improve health, strategies are needed to help individuals with CWP maintain regular physical activity over time and reach the level of physical activity recommended by the WHO.

The aim of the present study is to investigate if person-centred health plans that include physical activity can support participants with CWP to increase and maintain their level of physical activity and improve in physical function after 12 months, and to analyse factors that characterise those who achieve the level of physical activity recommended by the WHO.

Materials and methods

Design

This study presents a secondary predictive analysis of data obtained from a randomised controlled trial (RCT) investigating the effect of a person-centred health plan that included physical activity, which was delivered with the support of an eHealth intervention, compared to a follow-up by telephone. No benefit was found for the intervention group compared to the active control group regarding the outcomes pain intensity or level of physical activity after 6 months [30]. Nor were there any significant differences after 12 months between the randomisation groups regarding change in pain, physical activity level, or other symptoms. One reason for this could be that both groups received an intervention involving a person-centred health plan. As a result, all participants from both groups in the RCT were treated as a single cohort in the analyses in the current study. The secondary analysis was performed in spring 2024. The RCT is registered and described at: ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT03434899. The study adhered to the STROBE guidelines.

Ethics

The study was approved by the Regional Ethical Review Board in Gothenburg at the University of Gothenburg (approval number 1025-17). The study adhered to the principles stated in the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants received both written and oral information about the study, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Participants

The participants had been recruited to a RCT 12 months earlier. The criteria for the study population are described in detail in a previous publication [30]. In short, the inclusion criteria were individuals with CWP aged 20–65 years, and the exclusion criteria were other severe disorders or disorders affecting the ability to be physically active, not speaking Swedish, pregnancy, or other ongoing treatments.

Recruitment

A total of 102 participants completed the 12 months follow up and were included in the secondary analyses in the present study. The participants were recruited from primary health care centres in five cities in the western part of Sweden during 2018–2019. The recruitment process is described in a previous publication [30]. In short, 701 patients with CWP who had previously participated in a pain management programme were invited to join the study. First, they received a letter with written information about the study. This was followed by a phone call to assess their interest in participating and screen for inclusion and exclusion criteria. To reach additional participants, an advertisement in a local newspaper was used in one of the five cities. An additional 15 people contacted the study coordinator and were screened by phone. In total, 425 people were reached and screened. Finally, a total of 139 participants with CWP were included in the RCT.

The health plan

The participants had an individual meeting with a physiotherapist to develop a health plan that included physical activity and, if needed, stress reducing activities. The intervention has also been described in a previous publication [30]. The difference between the groups in the RCT was the type of support received after the creation of the health plan. The intervention group had one follow-up meeting with the physiotherapist and then received support through a digital e-health platform for one year. The active control group had one follow-up phone call. The health plan was designed according to the participants’ own needs, preferences, goals, obstacles and resources according to a person-centred model utilised in a previous study of patients with rheumatoid arthritis [27]. The person-centred model was based on the model established at the Centre for Person-centred Care (GPCC) at the University of Gothenburg, where the participants’ narratives were in focus, and the health plan was created in partnership between the participant and the physiotherapist. The level of physical activity in the health plan was individualised for each participant and gradually increased during the study period. The health plan aimed to support the participants in reaching the level of physical activity recommended by the WHO: 150 min/week at moderate intensity or 75 min/week at a high intensity, or an equivalent combination of both [10].

Measurements

Data was collected using questionnaires and a test of physical function at baseline (from March 2018 to April 2019) and after 6 and 12 months. Data at six months has been presented previously [30]. The analyses in the present study included data from baseline and the 12-month follow-up.

Descriptive data included age, duration of widespread pain (years), number of pain localisations, pharmacological treatment, tobacco use, country of birth, education level [1–4], family status, economic status [1–3], work status (hours/percentage), sick-leave status, and disability pension.

Physical function was assessed by the 1-minute chair-stand test [31]. The participants were asked to stand up and sit down as many times as possible for 1 min. The 1-minute chair-stand test has shown acceptable inter-rater reliability in CWP [31]. The minimal clinical important difference has been suggested to be between 1.9–5.4 stands in other patient populations [32].

Self-reported questionnaires

The Leisure Time Physical Activity Instrument (LTPAI) (hours) measures physical activity during leisure for one week and is assessed as light, moderate and vigorous activity. The questionnaire has shown satisfactory test-retest reliability and validity in people with CWP [33]. The subscales of different intensities were used to analyse the level of physical activity in the present study. The subscale assessing vigorous intensity physical activity was weighted as estimated time multiplied by 2 in the logistic regression analyses. This was done to match the recommendations of physical activity from the WHO, where the recommended level is half the time (75 min/week) if the activity is performed at high intensity compared to a moderate intensity (150 min/week).

The Short Form 36 (SF–36) (0–100 points) measures health-related quality of life. Higher score indicates better quality of life [34]. In this study, the sub scale of physical function was used. The Swedish version has been found to have sufficient reliability and validity in chronic pain [35]. The minimal clinical important difference in the sub scale of physical function has been suggested to be between 2 and 35 points in other patient populations [36].

Baseline values of the following questionnaires were used as independent variables in the logistic regression analyses:

The Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire (FIQ) (0–100) measures overall health status in 10 subscales, where a lower score indicates better overall health status [37]. The total score and the subscales of pain and fatigue experienced in the last week were used in the present study. The FIQ total score has shown satisfactory test-retest reliability and validity in patients with fibromyalgia [37].

The Stress and Crisis Inventory (SCI-93) (0–140 points) measures clinical stress symptoms using 35 items rated from 0 to 4, where a higher score indicates higher level of stress symptoms [38]. The test has shown satisfactory test-retest reliability and validity in CWP [38].

The General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES) (10–40 points) assesses the level of self-efficacy using 10 items rated from 1 to 4, where a higher score indicates higher self-efficacy. The GSES has been tested for validity in different cohorts and has shown high internal consistency in the general population [39].

The Experience of Physical Activity (EPA) (22–154 points) assesses experiences of physical activity using 22 questions rated on a scale from 1 to 7. The instrument includes five subscales that assess different aspects of the physical activity experience: physical relaxation, wellbeing, activity beliefs, activity-related symptoms, and activity habits. A lower score indicates a better experience. The EPA has shown satisfactory face validity in CWP [40].

Statistical analyses

Descriptive data, and changes in physical activity level and physical function from baseline to the 12 months follow up are presented as means, standard deviations (SDs), medians and ranges for continuous variables, and as counts (n) and percentage (%) for categorical variables.

Logistic regression analyses were used to identify predictors for reaching the recommended level of physical activity assessed using the LTPAI at the 12-month follow-up. The dependent variable was ‘not reaching the recommended level/reaching the recommended level of physical activity’. This was defined as achieving 150 min per week of weighted physical activity, calculated by summing the minutes of moderate-intensity activity and twice the minutes of vigorous-intensity activity, in accordance with WHO guidelines recommending 150 min of moderate or 75 min of vigorous activity per week.

In univariable logistic regression analyses, independent variables included educational level and baseline values of pain (FIQ-pain), fatigue (FIQ-fatigue), overall health (FIQ-total), the five subscales of experiences of physical activity (EPA), self-efficacy (GSES), clinical symptoms of stress (SCI-93), self-assessed physical function (SF-36 subscale), physical function (1-minute chair-stand test), weighted physical activity (LTPAI), and randomisation group. These analyses were then adjusted for randomisation group and baseline weighted physical activity. Variables significantly associated with reaching the recommended physical activity level (p < 0.05) in the adjusted analyses were then included in a multivariable logistic regression analysis, alongside with randomisation group and baseline weighted physical activity. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The strength of the OR can be evaluated by its value, where 1.0–1.49 indicates a weak association, 1.5–2.99 a moderate association, and ≥3 a strong association [41]. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was calculated to evaluate the discriminatory ability of the predictors. AUC values between 0.7 and 0.8 indicate acceptable discrimination, 0.8 to 0.9 indicate excellent discrimination, and values above 0.9 indicate outstanding discrimination [42,43].

Variable importance in the multivariable analyses was assessed using Shapley values, which decompose the overall model performance into contributions from individual variables [44]. Model performance was quantified using Tjur’s discrimination index [45], which measures the mean probability of the event (i.e. reaching the WHO-recommended physical activity level) among individuals experiencing versus not experiencing the event. Confidence intervals for performance metrics were calculated using non-parametric bootstraping with M = 2,000 bootstrap replicates.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R Software, version 4.4.1 (R Core Team, Vienna Austria). Shapley values were calculated using the domir R package, Version 1.2.0.

Discussion

The present study found that a cohort of participants with CWP who co-created a person-centred health plan with a physiotherapist and then followed the health plan for 12 months had increased their mean level of physical activity in light, moderate and high intensities at the 12-month follow-up. Even though the changes may seem minor, small increases in physical activity have been found to lead to health improvements [46]. Since about half of the participants had already achieved the level of physical activity recommended by the WHO at baseline, further increases may have been more difficult to achieve. Despite the well-known positive effects of reaching the recommended level of physical activity according to the WHO – both on symptoms and overall health – some patients with CWP tend to push themselves too hard, ignore the pain, and subsequently experience worsened symptoms. Patients with CWP are a heterogeneous group, and some may need to reduce their activity levels to achieve a better balance, rather than being encouraged to increase their physical activity [47]. This underscores the importance of physical activity tailored to each patient’s specific needs. The present study adds to the knowledge that person-centered health plans can support patients with CWP in making small increases in physical activity over time. Patients with chronic pain have reported that it is important that healthcare providers listen to their needs and adapt a supportive approach when promoting physical activity [48], which are central elements of person-centred rehabilitation. This is further supported by the results in a previous RCT, which showed that person-centred rehabilitation contributed to positive outcomes in physical function, health status and pain in patients with CWP [24]. Since the standard deviations for all outcomes are high, indicating substantial variability, changes over time should be interpreted with caution.

Some of the participants reduced their amount of physical activity from baseline to the 12-month follow-up. A retrospective descriptive review [49] of the participants’ health plans found that the most common reason for not being physically active was health-related problems such as increased pain, fatigue and disease related symptoms. Other reasons were stress, time-constraints and contextual factors [49]. This is supported by previous research showing that patients with CWP often find it difficult to maintain a higher level of physical activity over time, partly due to increased pain during and after activity, as well as uncertain and fluctuating abilities, depression, stress, or disability [18,20–22]. The participants who did not increase or reduced their level of physical activity may have needed more support than was provided with the person-centred health plan. In particular, the participants in the active control group of the RCT received only limited support during the study period. It is unclear whether more intensive support would have increased physical activity, but it is plausible and consistent with research showing that people with chronic pain need extra support to overcome barriers to be physically active [28].

If people with CWP can increase or maintain a higher level of physical activity, it can lead to improved health and a reduced risk of health-related diseases and excess mortality [7–9]. Identifying factors that support the maintenance of regular physical activity is therefore essential. In the present study, a positive experience of physical activity at baseline, particularly in the dimensions of physical relaxation, wellbeing and activity beliefs, appears to be associated with succeeding in reaching the WHO recommendations at the 12-month follow-up. A previous positive experience with physical activity can increase the motivation to resume or continue being physically active. This could partly be explained by the fact that exercise can influence the release of hormones that trigger the reward system, leading to a sense of satisfaction and a positive experience. If a person has experienced this, the likelihood of continuing to engage in physical activity increases [50]. Based on this knowledge, physiotherapy treatment should aim to design or select appropriate physical activities, particularly for patients with negative experiences associated with physical activity. It is important that these individuals are also able to enjoy their training and experience its positive effects. The present study adds to the knowledge that experiences and thoughts about physical activity affect the chances of succeeding in increasing and maintaining a higher level of physical activity over time. The importance of perceptions and attitudes toward physical activity is also supported by a predictive study [51], which showed that exercise self-efficacy, perceived barriers, and intention to exercise were associated with higher volumes of moderate and vigorous physical activity in patients with CWP [51].

A higher baseline level of physical activity was found to be associated with an increased likelihood of reaching the recommendations for physical activity at the 12-month follow-up. Participants with a higher initial level of physical activity require less additional activity to reach the WHO-recommended level, which may make it easier for them to succeed. A previous study of patients with CWP found that physical health, assessed by the SF-36, was associated with maintaining physical activity in the long-term [52]. The physical function subscale of the SF-36 questionnaire was not associated with reaching the recommended level after 12 months in the present study. However, the physical health score in the SF-36 includes more dimensions than the physical function subscale used in the present study, which may explain the differences in results.

The analyses of variable importance showed that the mean probability of reaching the WHO-recommended level of physical activity differed by 27 percentage points between those who achieved and those who did not achieve the recommendations. This is a relatively low degree of explanation, indicating that other factors not investigated in this study may influence the participants′ ability to reach the recommendations. Therefore, the results from the multivariable analysis should be interpreted with caution, and all EPA-variables that were significant in the univariable analyses may be important for successfully achieving the recommended level of physical activity.

Strengths and limitations

Physical function was assessed both objectively with the 1-minute chair-stand test and subjectively with the SF-36- physical function subscale, which reflect different aspects of physical function. More tests of physical function could have been used to obtain a broader picture of the participants’ physical function. However, the 1-minute chair-stand test has shown a good relationship with other tests of physical function, such as the 6-minute walk test and handgrip strength, in patients with fibromyalgia, indicating that the test may be representative of overall physical function [53]. There was a notable amount of missing data (19%) in the 1-minute chair-stand test at the 12-month follow-up. This primarily because some of the participants chose to complete only the questionnaires, which could be done at home, whereas the 1-minute chair-stand test required a visit to the rehabilitation centre. The observed improvement in physical function should be interpreted with caution.

In the present study, the subscale of the LTPAI that assessed high-intensity physical activity was weighted as estimated time multiplied by 2 and calculated together with minutes of moderate-intensity activity in the logistic regression analyses. Examples of moderate-intensity activities in the LTPAI, which cause slight breathlessness, include walking, cycling, light jogging and moderate gardening. High-intensity activities, which cause noticeable breathlessness, include walking quickly, running, other aerobic or resistance exercises, or heavy gardening [33]. According to the WHO, moderate intensity refers to activities that are performed at 3–6 metabolic equivalent of task (METs), for example, walking briskly, vacuuming or raking leaves, which is similar to the definition in the LTPAI. High intensity is 6 or higher METs according to the WHO, for example, walking very quickly, running, taking an aerobics class or shovelling snow [10], further supporting that the intensity levels in the LTPAI are comparable to the WHO definition of intensities. The LTPAI is, however, a subjective measure of physical activity. A limitation in the present study is that physical activity levels were not also measured objectively. The physical activity level was not the sole outcome in the RCT, and it was therefore not the main focus to assess it both subjectively and objectively. However, including objective measurements, such as accelerometer, would have provided a broader picture of activity levels. Additionally, although the LTPAI total score has shown satisfactory test-retest reliability and validity in patients with CWP, the test-retest reliability was lower for the high-intensity scale than for the light and moderate intensity scales [33].

The participants in the present study were recruited from primary healthcare settings, where most of the patients with CWP are treated in Sweden, which increases the clinical relevance of the results. However, the fact that the participants were recruited to the RCT knowing that it included an intervention with physical activity may have contributed to a study sample that was more positive toward physical activity than the general CWP population, which affects the generalisability of the results.

Research and clinical implications

The intervention contributed to increased physical activity in some participants; others may not have needed change or required different or more support. This suggests that individualized rehabilitation is needed, with both treatment and support tailored to each patient’s specific needs. The participants with a higher level of physical activity and a more positive experience of physical activity at baseline were more likely to achieve a health-enhancing level of physical activity after following a health plan independently for 12 months. Patients with CWP with negative experiences of physical activity seems to need more support to be able to reach and maintain a higher level of physical activity. For physiotherapists in primary care, it may be beneficial to explore patients’ previous experiences and perceptions of physical activity when developing a health plan and planning how to support patients in increasing and maintaining their level of physical activity. Future studies are needed to test interventions aimed at maintaining physical activity over time and to examine more intensive or tailored support for subgroups of people with CWP who have negative experiences of physical activity or lower activity levels. Such studies should preferably include objective measures of physical activity alongside self-reported instruments.

Conclusion

Twelve months after receiving a person-centred health plan that included physical activity, some participants had increased their levels of physical activity and physical function, with 62% reaching the level of physical activity recommended by the WHO. Achieving this level was associated with higher baseline physical activity and more positive experiences of physical activity, particularly with regard to physical relaxation.
