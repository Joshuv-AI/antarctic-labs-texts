First telomere-to-telomere gapless genome assembly of the blackleg fungus Plenodomus lingam
Authors: Tao Luo, Mingde Wu, Jing Zhang, Guoqing Li, Long Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBlackleg disease, caused by the ascomycete fungus Plenodomus lingam (syn.: Leptosphaeria maculans), is the most devastating disease of oilseed rape worldwide. As a model organism for studying plant-fungus interactions, P. lingam has been extensively studied, yet telomere-to-telomere (T2T) genome assemblies have been lacking. Here, we report a high-quality T2T gapless genome assembly of P. lingam ‘brassicae’ strain 479-2, generated using MGI short reads, PacBio/Nanopore long reads, and Hi-C data. The assembly comprises 19 nuclear chromosomes totaling 40.74 Mb, each capped by telomeric repeats (CCCTAA/TTAGGG), and a complete circular mitochondrial genome of 169,227 bp. Key quality metrics include: 46.08% GC content, 30.09% repeat content, 98.5% complete BUSCOs (fungi_odb10), a consensus quality score of 45.86, and a low error rate of 2.59 × 10−5. Functional annotation predicted 9,818 protein-coding genes, including 807 cytochrome P450s, 464 carbohydrate-active enzymes, 808 secreted proteins, and 287 candidate effectors. This T2T assembly provides a gold-standard genomic resource for studies of pathogenicity, effector evolution, and host adaptation in P. lingam, and will advance research on plant-pathogen co-evolution.Subject terms: Fungal genomics, Pathogens

Background & Summary

Phytopathogenic fungi pose major threats to global agricultural productivity and food security, with filamentous species like Plenodomus lingam (syn.: Leptosphaeria maculans) causing catastrophic crop losses1,2. P. lingam, the causal agent of blackleg disease, is recognized as the most destructive pathogen of oilseed rape (Brassica napus). This pathogen typically causes average annual yield losses of 10–15% in oilseed rape, and during severe epidemics, yield losses can reach up to 90%, thereby exerting significant impacts on global canola production3. Beyond its agricultural significance, P. lingam is a model for studying fungal-plant interactions, particularly effector-triggered immunity and host specialization.

The first genome sequencing study of P. lingam (JN3 v1) revealed that it harbors a characteristic two-speed genome consisting of GC-equilibrated and AT-rich compartments, with AT-rich regions enriched in transposable elements and effector genes shaped by repeat-induced point mutation, laying a genomic foundation for rapid adaptive evolution4. To date, five P. lingam genome assemblies (JN3 v1, JN3 v2, Nz-T4, CBS 260.94, and CAN1) have been deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information, but these suffer from incomplete contiguity: scaffold counts range from 33 to 14,287, and none achieve T2T gapless continuity (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/?taxon=5022). Gaps and unanchored scaffolds hinder analyses of chromosome structure, repeat dynamics, and effector gene clusters, which are critical for understanding pathogenicity5.

To address this limitation, we sequenced P. lingam ‘brassicae’ strain 479-2 (isolated by Shanghai Customs from Canadian imported B. napus seeds) using a multi-platform approach: MGI short reads (158× coverage, 6.50 Gb) for K-mer analysis and polishing, PacBio long reads (923× coverage, 38 Gb) for de novo assembly, Nanopore long reads (729× coverage, 30 Gb) for gap-filling and Hi-C data (583× coverage, 24 Gb) for chromosome scaffolding. K-mer frequency analysis estimated the genome size at ~41.18 Mb (Fig. 1a), ~2% smaller than the CAN1 assembly and ~10% smaller than JN3 v2. Using ITS sequence phylogenetic analysis and whole-genome average nucleotide identity (ANI) analysis, we confirmed that 479-2 belongs to P. lingam ‘brassicae’. Whole-genome (ANI) quantification revealed that 479-2 shared the highest ANI with CAN1 (99.83%), a value marginally higher than that between 479-2 and JN3 (99.24%), and between JN3 and CAN1 (99.23%) (Fig. 2).

The final T2T assembly (designated T2T 479-2) comprises 19 gapless chromosomes (40.74 Mb, N50 = 2.32 Mb), covering approximately 99% of the estimated genome (Fig. 1b–d). Compared with two existing assemblies (CAN1: GCA_022343315.1; JN3 v2: GCA_900538235.1), the T2T 479-2 achieves a marked improvement in genome contiguity (average scaffold length: 2.14 Mb vs. 0.88 Mb for CAN1 and 1.39 Mb for JN3 v2; Table 1). Notably, all 33 scaffolds of the JN3 v2 assembly and 46 out of 48 scaffolds of the CAN1 assembly were successfully anchored and merged into 19 intact chromosomes in the T2T 479-2 assembly (Fig. 3a,b). Two scaffolds (NW_027093943.1, NW_027093946.1) from the CAN1 genome were undetectable in the 479-2 genome. Sequence alignment of NW_027093943.1 against the MGI short-read sequencing data of the 479-2 genome resulted in an alignment rate of merely 0.39%. This indicates that the NW_027093943.1 sequence harbored in the CAN1 genome is truly absent from the 479-2 genome, reflecting a certain level of strain-specific genomic variation among different P. lingam ‘brassicae’ isolates. Sequence NW_027093946.1 corresponds to the mitochondrial genome, while the 479-2 assembly described herein is a nuclear genome assembly with the mitochondrial genome excluded; consequently, NW_027093946.1 failed to align to any sequences in the 479-2 genome (Table 2). Furthermore, the T2T 479-2 assembly eliminates all chromosomal gaps (0 gaps vs. 1 gap in CAN1 [Fig. 3c] and 468 gaps in JN3 v2), thereby achieving a fully gapless genome.

Repeat annotation analysis revealed that repetitive elements accounted for 30.09% (12.25 Mb) of the T2T 479-2 genome. Among all repetitive element categories, LTR retrotransposons were the predominant group, with 2,828 copies spanning 9.20 Mb, which accounted for 22.58% of the entire genome and approximately 75% of the total repetitive sequences. In contrast, DNA transposons (0.70%), LINEs (0.06%), and others (including simple repeats, microsatellites, and RNA-related elements, 2.01%) contributed only minor proportions, and Penelope elements were not detected in this strain. Unclassified repeats constituted 4.74% of the genome. Comparative analyses with other isolates (CAN1 and JN3) revealed that the overall repetitive content of 479-2 (30.09%) was slightly lower than that of CAN1 (33.36%) and JN3 (36.86%). Notably, the LTR retrotransposons content was highly conserved between 479-2 (22.58%) and CAN1 (23.09%), suggesting that LTR expansion is a conserved feature across these Canadian isolates. In contrast, JN3 exhibited a significantly higher LTR proportion (27.78%) and harbored additional Penelope elements, indicating isolate-specific recent transpositional activity (Table 3, Fig. 4). Collectively, the genome size of 479-2 is smaller than that of JN3 and CAN1, a difference attributable to both intrinsic intraspecific biological variation and the markedly fewer repetitive sequences in 479-2 relative to JN3 and CAN1.

We conducted structural annotation of the T2T 479-2 genome via an integrative approach combining ab initio gene prediction, homology-based annotation, and transcriptome-assisted evidence. This approach predicted 9,818 protein-coding genes, with 98.5% of the single-copy BUSCOs from the fungi_odb10 database identified as complete, indicating high annotation completeness (Table 4). Compared with previous annotations, the unified Funannotate-based annotation framework produced more consistent gene model statistics across strains, including increased mean CDS length, elimination of very short CDS models (<150 bp), and more stable proportions of single-exon transcripts (Table 5). Collectively, these improvements indicate enhanced reproducibility, structural accuracy, and cross-strain comparability of the genome annotation. For functional annotation, we identified 807 cytochrome P450 proteins and 464 carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) by querying the P450 and CAZy databases, respectively. Additionally, we detected 808 secreted proteins and 287 candidate effectors using SignalP, TMHMM, and EffectorP (Table 4 and Fig. 5a). For non-coding loci, 173 rRNAs, 145 tRNAs, and 48 ncRNAs were identified in the genome, with total length of 313,085 bp, 7,464 bp, and 5,486 bp, respectively (Table 4). Collectively, these annotations provide a comprehensive overview of P. lingam ‘brassicae’ genomic features and offer valuable insights into its pathogenic mechanism and genomic organization.

Additionally, the mitochondrial genome of 479-2 (169,227 bp) was de novo assembled based on MGI sequencing data. GetOrganelle assembly resulted in a single circular contig with overlapping terminal sequences, supporting a circular mitochondrial genome configuration. The genome harbors 13 standard fungal core protein-coding genes (nad1-nad6, nad4L, cob, cox1-cox3, atp6, and rps3), two ribosomal subunits (rns and rnl), and 33 tRNA genes (Fig. 5b).

In summary, this study presents both a telomere-to-telomere nuclear genome assembly and a complete mitochondrial genome for P. lingam, representing a pivotal advance for blackleg research. These high-quality genomic resources facilite in-depth analyses of telomere biology, chromosome-level synteny, and effector gene evolution, which are all essential for developing sustainable and durable control strategies against blackleg disease.

Methods

Sampling, DNA extraction and sequencing

P. lingam ‘brassicae’ strain 479-2 was obtained from Shanghai Customs (isolated from Canadian imported B. napus seeds). The strain was cultured on potato dextrose agar (PDA) plates at 20 °C for 7 days. Mycelial plugs (5 mm diameter) were transferred to potato dextrose broth (PDB) and incubated at 20 °C with shaking (150 rpm) for 7 days. Mycelia were harvested and sent to Frasergen Information Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) for DNA extraction using the CTAB method6. Second-generation sequencing was performed on the MGISEQ-2000 platform, while third-generation sequencing was conducted using both the PacBio RSII and Oxford Nanopore PromethION platforms. Hi-C sequencing data were also generated using the MGISEQ-2000 platform.

Genome assembly

The initial genome assembly of PacBio long reads was performed using MECAT27 (parameters: GENOME_SIZE = 42000000 MIN_READ_LENGTH = 10000 CNS_ OVLP_OPTIONS = “-kmer_size 13” CNS_PCAN_OPTIONS = “-p 100000 -k 100” CNS_ OUTPUT_COVERAGE = 30 TRIM_OVLP_OPTIONS = “-skip_overhang” TRIM_PM4_ OPTIONS = “-p 100000 -k 100” FSA_OL_FILTER_OPTIONS = “--max_overhang = -1 --min_identity = -1” CLEANUP = 0). The assembly was then error-corrected with MGI short reads using Pilon v1.238 (parameters: -changes-vcf). Next, Hi-C reads were used to link the corrected contigs into scaffolds with Juicer v1.69 and 3D-DNA v18092210. Manual connection and calibration were performed using Juicebox v1.11.0811, and TGS-GapCloser v1.0.312 was employed to fill gaps and extend telomeres based on ultra-long ONT reads. The presence of telomeres at the ends of each chromosome was confirmed using FindTelomeres.py (https://github.com/JanaSperschneider/FindTelomeres). Purge_dups (https://github.com/dfguan/purge_dups.git) was then used to remove haplotigs and heterozygous duplications from the genome (parameters: −2 -f 1 -T cutoffs). Iterative error correction was performed using NextPolish v1.4.113 (parameters: task = best rerun = 3 max_depth = 100), based on PacBio long reads and MGI short reads. The final result was a gap-free, chromosome-level reference genome of P. lingam ‘brassicae’.

Genome annotation

The total number of repeat regions across the genome was identified using Earl Grey14. Non-protein coding RNA genes, such as rRNA, tRNA and ncRNA, were predicted using barrnap v0.9 (https://github.com/tseemann/barrnap), INFERNAL (cmscan) v1.1.4 and the Rfam 14.9 database15. Protein-coding gene annotation was subsequently conducted using the Funannotate v1.8.15 pipeline (parameters: --min_intronlen 10 --min_protlen 50), which integrated ab initio gene predictions, transcriptome-based annotations, and homologous protein alignments16. The detailed annotation process was as follows: ab initio gene prediction was performed using AUGUSTUS v3.3.317; A total of 8 transcriptome data from P. lingam ‘brassicae’ conidia and mycelium samples under different growth conditions were used to assist the annotation of the 479-2 genome18 (Table 6). The transcriptome sequences were assembled using Trinity v2.8.519, and then aligned to the genome using HISAT v2.2.120 for further annotation. Additionally, protein sequences from publicly available P. lingam ‘brassicae’ strains at NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/?taxon=5022) were provided to Funannotate v1.8.15 for homologous protein-based annotation. Subsequently, 9,818 protein-coding genes involved in cytochrome P450s and carbohydrate-active enzymes were identified by querying the P450 and CAZy databases, respectively21,22. Effector protein candidates were predicted using SignalP v5.023, TMHMM v2.024, and EffectorP v3.025.

Genome assessment and visualization

Basic genome assembly statistics were generated using QUAST v5.2.026 and assembly-stats v1.0.1 (https://github.com/rjchallis/assembly-stats). The completeness of the genome was assessed with the fungi_odb10 dataset via BUSCO v4.1.427, using default parameters. To evaluate the accuracy of the new genome assembly, MGI paired-end sequencing reads generated in this study were mapped to the assembly using BWA-mem v0.7.1728 and SAMtools v1.1529. Merqury v1.330 was used to calculate consensus quality (QV). Mummer v3.2331 and NGenomeSyn v1.4232 were applied to analyze genome collinearity, with default settings. Lastly, circlize v0.4.1133 was used to generate a circular plot of the T2T 479-2 genome assembly.

Mitochondrial genome assembly, annotation and visualization

The circular mitochondrial genome was assembled and annotated using MGI data through GetOrganelle v1.7.7.134 (parameters: -F fungus_mt), MFannot35 and MITOS236. The mitochondrial genome map was drawn using circlize v0.4.1133.

Technical Validation

DNA sample quality

The DNA quality was assessed using Qubit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and Nanodrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA).

Sequencing data assessment

Quality control of the short-read sequencing data was performed using fastp v0.2344. The MGI reads showed a GC content of 43.4%, with 96.3% and 87.9% of bases having Phred quality scores above Q20 and Q30, respectively. The Hi-C sequencing reads had a GC content of 48.2%, with 96.2% of bases exceeding Q20 and 88.3% exceeding Q30.

Evaluation of the genome assembly

To evaluate the completeness of the genome assembly, Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) analysis was performed using the “fungi_odb10” lineage dataset. The assembly contained 98.8% of the 758 expected single-copy orthologs, suggesting that the assembly is of excellent completeness and quality. In addition, A consensus quality of 45.86, an error rate of 2.59186e-05, and a 96.72% alignment rate of MGI reads collectively indicate the high accuracy and completeness of the genome assembly (Table 4).

Assessment of genome annotation

Compared with previous annotations, the unified Funannotate-based annotations display more consistent gene model statistics across strains (Table 5), including increased mean CDS length, the absence of very short CDS models (<150 bp), and more stable proportions of single-exon transcripts, collectively indicating improved reproducibility and comparability.
