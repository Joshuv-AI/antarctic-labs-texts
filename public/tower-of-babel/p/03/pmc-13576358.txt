Assembly and characterization of the first complete mitochondrial genome of Epimedium sagittatum (Sieb. et Zucc.) Maxim (Berberidaceae):an invaluable traditional Chinese medicine
Authors: Yihui Gong, Yunxiang Zheng, Guihua Zhou, Xiangyi Lei, Haotian Liu, Bin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpimedium sagittatum (Sieb. et Zucc.) Maxim is an invaluable traditional Chinese medicine plant known for its properties of tonifying kidney yang, strengthening bones and muscles, and dispelling rheumatism. The chloroplast (cp) genome of E. sagittatum have been sequenced, offering critical insights for breeding and phylogenetic research. However, the mitochondrial (mt) genome of E. sagittatum remains uncharacterized, limiting comprehensive insights into its genomic evolution.ResultsIn this study, we assembled the first complete mt genome of E. sagittatum employing Illumina and Nanopore sequencing technology and subsequently investigated comparative analysis with its closely related species. The mt genome of E. sagittatum was assembled as a multi-branched structure with a length of 339,191 bp, within a GC content of 46.91%. Our annotation results have shown 39 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 22 tRNA genes, three rRNA genes and four pseudogenes in the E. sagittatum mt genome. The analysis of sequence repeats has detected 79 simple sequence repeats (SSRs), 10 tandem repeats and 255 dispersed repeats in the E. sagittatum mt genome. A total of 720 C to U RNA editing sites of the 34 PCGs was predicted in E. sagittatum. The codons exhibited a strong preference for A or U bases in the E. sagittatum mt genome. The analysis of nucleotide diversity (Pi) highlighted differences in genetic variability across the tested genes, with atp9 gene exhibiting the highest genetic variation. Selection pressure analysis showed that most genes were affected by negative selection during evolution, whereas ccmB, rps10, and rps12 underwent positive selection in different plants. Additionally, a Bayesian phylogenetic tree showed that E. sagittatum was closely related to E. wushanense and E. pubescens. In total of 14 homologous fragments totaling 8,954 bp were identified between the cp and mt genomes of E. sagittatum.ConclusionsThis study presents the first assembled and annotated mt genome of E. sagittatum, which provides a valuable genetic resource for the Epimedium genus and lays the foundation for investigating the phylogenetic relationship and genetic variation of this invaluable medicinal plant.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09358-0.

Introduction

Mitochondria, as double-membrane semi-autonomous organelles, are widely existed in eukaryotes, and play an important role in plant growth, reproduction and synthesis of intracellular compounds [1]. In eukaryotes, the structure of the mitochondrial (mt) genome is relatively conserved, most of which have closed circular structures, with representative species including Cymbidium ensifolium (L.) [2], Cymbaria mongolica Maxim [3] and Aquilegia amurensis Kom [4]. but a few species have linear structures, with representative examples containing Oryza sativa L. [5], Quercus acutissima Carruth [6], and the cytoplasmic male-sterile S-type (CMS-S) mitochondria of Zea mays L [7]. Previous studies have revealed significant differences in the size and structure of mt genomes in different organisms [8]. The length of mt genomes ranged from 200 to 2700 kb [9, 10]. The mt genomes of various organisms exhibit distinct characteristics, while amino acid and gene sequences show highly consistent among species within the same genus. The presence of abundant non-coding DNA in plant mt genomes, including dispersed repetitive sequences, intergenic regions, introns, and foreign DNA sequences, is the main reason for the significant difference in mt genome size [11]. The non-protein-coding genes include rRNA and tRNA genes. Notably, tRNA genes in plant mt genomes exhibit unique characteristics in their origin. Some of tRNA genes were inherited from ancestral mitochondria, while the others were acquired through horizontal transfer of chloroplast-derived sequences [12]. Extensive intramolecular recombination mediated by repetitive sequences has been comfirmed within the mt genomes of higher plants [13]. The progress of mt genome recombination mechanisms has been studied with representative species containing Trachelospermum jasminoides (Lindl.) Lem [14], Rehmannia glutinosa Gaertn [15], and Rhodomyrtus tomentosa (Aiton) Hassk [16]. Therefore, the recombination events are considered as a critical driver of mt genome evolution, facilitating adaptive diversification. Due to their compact size and ease of sequencing, mt genomes have emerged as pivotal molecular markers for studying plant phylogenetic relationship and evolutionary genetics [17].

Epimedium sagittatum is an invaluable traditional Chinese medicine that belongs to the Berberidaceae family in the Epimedium genus. E. sagittatum is mainly cultivated in Southern and Eastern China. E. sagittatum primarily contains icariin, epimedin A, epimedin B, and epimedin C, which exhibits high anti-aging and anticancer activities [18]. E. sagittatum exhibits pharmacological activities that include tonifying kidney yang, strengthening the body, and dispelling rheumatism [19]. Modern pharmacological studies have revealed its therapeutic effects, including anti-inflammatory, anti-osteoporotic, and anticancer (specifically against breast cancer) properties [20–22]. Epimedium presents one of the most complex challenges in botanical taxonomy and phylogeny, standing out as a notoriously intricate genus for classification and evolutionary analysis. The updated taxonomic of Epimedium organized the genus into two subgenera, four sections, and four series, with classification criteria drawing on geographical distribution alongside leaf and flower morphology [23]. Phylogenetic studies utilizing multiple molecular markers, containing the internal transcribed spacer (ITS), trnK-matK and atpB-rbcL intergenic spacer regions, and amplified fragment length polymorphisms (AFLPs) consistently confirmed the five clades of Epimedium [24–27]. Previous researches indicated that genomic DNA regions are not ideal to distinguish Epimedium species [28]. It needs to be developed more effective molecular markers to study the relationships among Epimedium species, as well as the origin, and evolution of the genus in China. Although the chloroplast (cp) genome of E. sagittatum has been sequenced, the mt genome of E. sagittatum has not yet been reported. The characterized mt genome of E. sagittatum comparison with the related species can contribute to identifying genetic or structural variations in the evolutionary history of Epimedium species. Consequently, assembling and characterizing the mt genome is critical to elucidating its genetic structure and advancing molecular marker development for this species.

The current study focused on characterizing the mt genome of E. sagittatum and offered insights into the genetic changes that have emerged during its evolution. Our findings will help to better understand mitogenomic evolution for the Epimedium genera and offer valuable genomic information for identification, molecular marker, evolutionary and the breeding studies of E. sagittatum.

Materials and methods

Plant materials, DNA extraction and sequencing

The fresh young leaves of E. sagittatum were collected from Hunan Hechen Seed Industry Technology Co., Ltd. in Xiangtan, Hunan, China (27°52′N, 112°50′E). The specimens of E. sagittatum has been deposited in Hunan University of Humanities, Science and Technology with the voucher number: JYYYH20250403. Total genomic DNA was isolated from the young leaves of E. sagittatum using Plant DNA Mini Kit (D311, Genepioneer Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Nanjing, China). The quality and concentration of the isolated samples was detected using an OneDrop OD-1000 spectrophotometer (OneDrop Biotech, Hangzhou, China).

Qualified samples underwent sequencing procedures at Genepioneer Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). Second-generation sequencing was performed using a NovaSeq6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), and quality control filtering of the raw short reads was obtained utilizing Fastp v0.23.4. The filtration parameters were set as follows: (1) removing the adapter and primer sequences from raw reads; (2) discarding the reads with an average quality score below Q5; (3) excluding the reads containing an uncertain number of bases more than 5. Third-generation sequencing was determined using a Nanopore PromethION platform (Nanopore, Oxford, UK), and the quality control filtering of the raw long reads was obtained through Filtlong v0.2.1 with the parameters setting as –min_length 1000 –min_mean_q 7.

Mitogenome assembly and annotation

The long-read sequencing data were aligned with the plant mt gene database (https://github.com/xul962464/plant_mt_ref_gene) with Minimap2 v2.1 [29], which selected the sequences with sizes more than 50 bp as candidates. The sequences containing multiple core genes were selected as seed sequences. Subsequently, the long-read sequencing data were aligned with the seed sequences employing Minimap2 v2.1, and sequences with overlaps longer than 1 kb were screened and added to the seed sequence to achieve all long-read sequencing data of the E. sagittatum mt genome. The long-read sequencing data were checked with Canu [30], and the short-read sequencing data were compared with the checked long-read sequencing data by Bowtie2 (v2.3.5.1). The short-read and long-read sequencing data were stitched using Unicycler (v0.4.8) software with default parameters. The stitching results were visualized and adjusted using Bandage (v0.8.1) software. A map of E. sagittatum mt genome was conducted with OGDRAW online tool (https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/OGDraw.html. The encoded protein genes and rRNAs were by BLAST comparison against published plant mt genomes and manually adjusted based on related species. The tRNAs of E. sagittatum mt genome were annotated using tRNAscanSE (http://lowelab.ucsc.edu/tRNAscan-SE/) [31]. The open reading frames (ORF) were predicted using the online tool Open Reading Frame Finder (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gorf/gorf.html) with a minimum length of 102 bp, excluding redundant and overlapping sequences.

Codon usage analysis

The relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) values of the PCGs in the E. sagittatum mt genome were analyzed using CodonW (v1.4.4) [32], and codon usage was further investigated with Perl scripts to filter unique CDS.

Repeat sequence identification

The simple sequence repeats (SSRs) of the E. sagittatum mt genome were analyzed utilizing the Misa v1.0 software, and the lengths of one to six nucleotides were set as 10, 5, 4, 3, 3, and 3, respectively [33]. The tandem repeats were detected using trf409.linux64 with the parameters setting as 2, 7, 7, 80, 10, 50, and 2000 -f -d -m. The Blastn v2.10.1 program was used to investigate dispersed repeats, and the parameters were set as -word_size 7 and evalue 1e-5. The visualization of the repeat sequences was conducted using the circos v0.69–5.

RNA editing sites analysis

The RNA-editing sites of the PCGs in E. sagittatum mt genome were predicted employing PmtREP (http://cloud.genepioneer.com:9929/#/tool/alltool/detail/336) with a critical value of 0.2 [34]. The mt genomes of six closely related species were downloaded from the NCBI database (Table S1). RNA-editing sites analysis of six closely related species: Trifolium pratense L, Prunella vulgaris L, Houttuynia cordata Thunb, Plantago ovata Forssk, Arabidopsis thaliana L, and Artemisia argyi H was comparatively performed.

Nucleotide diversity (pi) and selective pressure analysis

The PCGs of T. pratense, P. vulgaris, H. cordata, P. ovata, A. thaliana, and A. argyi were used for calculating the Pi values of the PCGs in E. sagittatum mt genome. The mt PCGs of the tested species and E. sagittatum were aligned using the Mafft v7.427 (https://mafft.cbrc.jp/alignment/software/) online tool [35], and the Pi values of each PCG gene in E. sagittatum were analyzed using DnaSP5. Subsequently, the Ka/Ks ratios were calculated with KaKs_Calculator v2.0 (https://sourceforge.net/projects/kakscalculator2/).

Phylogenetic and Homology analysis

For phylogenetic analysis, a total of 16 species mt genomes that are closely related to E. sagittatum were obtained from the NCBI database (Table S2), with Ginkgo biloba L (NC_027976) selected as an outgroup. Common PCGs among these mt genomes were selected and aligned employing MAFFT v7.427 with –auto mode [36]. The aligned sequences were connected at the beginning and end, trimmed using trimAl v1.4.rev15 with default parameters. A majority-rule consensus tree was generated following Bayesian phylogenetic inference performed in MrBayes v3.2.7. The analysis was run under a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) framework for 1,000,000 generations, with sampling occurring every 100 generations. The first 25% of sampled trees were discarded as burn-in before constructing the consensus tree.

The cp genome of E. sagittatum (MN027267.1) was downloaded from the NCBI database. Homologous fragments between the cp and mt genomes of E. sagittatum were identified using BLASTN with the following parameters: a length of 30 bp, matching rate ≥ 70%, and e-value ≤ 1e−5 [37].

Synteny construction

A multiple synteny plot of the E. sagittatum mt genome with the closely related species was performed using Nucmer (4.0.0beta2) with –maxmatch parameters.

Results

E. sagittatum mt genome assembly and structure characterization

For the assembly of the E. sagittatum mt genome, 18,278,317,460 raw data and 60,524,230 bp clean data (Q20 = 99.13% and Q30 = 96.99%) were generated through the Illumina sequencing (Table S3). Subsequently, a total of 24,314,465,005 bases and 1,970,557 reads were obtained employing the Nanopore sequencing strategy with a mean read length of 12,338 bp. The N50 value of subreads was 19,855 bp in size (Table S4). An Illumina + Nanopore mixed assembly strategy was used to simplify the E. sagittatum mt genome structure into a single linear molecule (Fig. 1A), and a total length of 339,191 bp was finally achieved, with the GC content of 46.91%. A schematic diagram of the long-read-assembled mt genome was generated with Bandage (v0.8.1) software. The schematic included 16 contigs, their sequence length and sequencing depth presented in Fig. 1B. The overlapping region between two contigs, indicated by the black line, was utilized to assemble a complex multibranched structure. Resolution of the key branch nodes was achieved through Unicycler (v0.4.8) using long-read sequencing data. Following resolution of branch nodes by Unicycler (v0.4.8), the resulting linear genome sequence is presented in Fig. 1C. The specific solution path is 6–14-copy-11–18-copy-17-copy-copy-15-copy-10–16-2–16-copy-7–17-copy-3–18-17–15-9–5–12-copy-14-copy-copy-13-copy-1–12–14–13-4 (Fig. 1C). Codon usage bias

Codon usage bias analysis was performed on 39 PCGs of E. sagittatum mt genome. As shown in Fig. 2 and Table S5, both the start codon (AUG) and the tryptophan (UGG) displayed RSCU values equal to 1. The other PCGs of E. sagittatum mt genome also showed a common codon use preference. The Alanine (Ala) exhibited a high preference for the use of GCU, and its RSCU value was the largest among mt genome PCGs, reaching 1.5742, followed by Tyrosine (Tyr)’s preference for the use of UAU, and its RSCU value was 1.526. In addition, within the mt genome of E. sagittatum, 29 codons exhibited RSCU value greater than 1. Among these, the majority (93.10%; 27 codons) ended in A or U, while a minority (6.90%; 2 codons) contained C or G at the third nucleotide position.Fig. 2RSCU analysis of the E. sagittatum mt genome

Repetitive sequences

There were three types of repetitive sequences in the mt genome of E. sagittatum. A total of 79 SSRs (Table 3) were identified in the E. sagittatum mt genome. The pentamers and monomers were the most abundant types of SSR motifs, accounting for 43.04% and 26.58% of the total SSRs, respectively. The monomers contained 8 of Adenine (A) and 10 of thymine (T), respectively. The most frequently observed dimer SSR motifs were identified as AT, TA, and TC repeats, accounting for 66.67% of the total (Table S6). Furthermore, only five pentamer SSRs were detected in the E. sagittatum mt genome. However, no hexamer SSRs were observed in this genome. In E. sagittatum, ten tandem repeats were identified with a matching degree of more than 77% and a size of 12 ~ 39 bp (Table 4). In addition, a total of 255 dispersed repeats with the length ≧30 bp were observed (Fig. 3 and Table S7), including 143 palindromic (about 56.08%) and 112 forward repeats (about 43.92%). No reverse or complementary repeats were identified in the E. sagittatum mt genome (Fig. 3). The total length of these obtained dispersed repeats was 32,001 bp, accounting for 9.43% of the total mt genome. Nucleotide diversity and selective pressure

Pi is an effective tool to evaluate genetic variation in DNA sequences among distinct species, where highly variable regions can be selected as suitable candidate molecular markers for population genetics studies [38]. Figure 5 showed the Pi values of the 31 PCGs and 3 rRNA genes in the mt genomes of E. sagittatum and other six related closely species. The Pi values of these genes varied from 0.01261 to 0.16267, with most genes having values less than 0.1. Among these genes, atp9 exhibited the greatest genetic variation, showing a Pi measurement of 0.16267. Additionally, The rps4 (Pi = 0.10879), atp4 (Pi = 0.10801), atp8 (Pi = 0.09599) and atp1 (Pi = 0.09283) genes displayed notable genetic diversity, while the sdh4 (Pi = 0.01926) and nad7 (Pi = 0.03519) genes were the most conserved PCGs. Additionally, the three rRNAs genes (being rrn5, rrn26 and rrn18) also exhibited conservation, with Pi values of 0.01261, 0.01768 and 0.03231, respectively (Table S8). Overall, the Pi values of the PCGs exhibited significant variation among the tested species.Fig. 5Nucleotide diversity analysis of the E. sagittatum mt genome

Nucleotide diversity analysis of the E. sagittatum mt genome

Six species of plants (namely, T. pratense, P. vulgaris, H. cordata, P. ovata, A. thaliana, and A. argyi) were selected and used to analyze the values of Ka/Ks in 31 PCGs related to mt genome of E. sagittatum. As showed in Fig. 6, twenty-eight genes (about 90.32%) had Ka/Ks < 1, demonstrating that these genes underwent negative selection during evolution and exhibiting conservation in synonymous substitutions. In contrast, three genes (being ccmB, rps10 and rps12) had Ka/Ks > 1, indicating that ccmB rps10 and rps12 genes underwent positive selection during evolution. Phylogenetic analysis

According to the DNA sequences of 28 conservative mt PCGs, including atp1, atp4, atp6, atp8, atp9, ccmB, ccmC, ccmFc, ccmFn, cob, cox1, cox2, cox3, matR, mttB, nad1, nad2, nad3, nad4, nad4L, nad5, nad6, nad7, nad9, rps10, rps12, rps3, and rps4, the phylogenetic analysis of 16 species containing E. sagittatum was reconstructed (Fig. 7). The Bayesian phylogenetic tree was divided into nine groups, namely, Leguminosae, Cruciferae, Asteraceae, Lamiaceae, Plantaginaceae, Berberidaceae, Saururaceae, Chloranthaceae, and Ginkgoaceae. E. sagittatum was clustered into group six among other Epimedium species, forming a sister branch to other related Epimedium species within the Berberidaceae clade. E. sagittatum was closely related to E. wushanense (PV694989.1) and E. pubescens (PV695558.1), revealing that E. sagittatum belongs to the Epimedium in the Berberidaceae family. Homologous fragments transferred from cp to mt

The cp-like sequences within the mt genome were detected through comparative analysis with the complete cp genome sequence of E. sagittatum (GenBank accession: MN027267.1) obtained from the NCBI database. The homologous sequence (8,892 bp) accounted for 5.66% of the total cp genome. In contrast, the homologous sequence on the mt genome was 5,234 bp in size, representing 1.54% of its total sequence (Fig. 8 and Table S9). Based on the sequence similarity, a total of 14 homologous fragments to the cp genome were obtained in the E. sagittatum mt genome, with totaling length 8,946 bp, accounting for 2.64% of the complete mt genome. Five complete genes, including trnP-TGG, trnW-CCA, trnD-GTC, trnH-GTG, and trnM-CAT, were detected, with 44.62% of homologous fragments in rrn18 gene (Fig. 8 and Table S10). We also observed that 13 insertion regions in the E. sagittatum cp genome, including seven complete genes, 2 PCGs (being petG and rps7) and 5 tRNA genes (being trnW-CCA, trnP-UGG, trnD-GUC, trnH-GUC, and trnM-CAU), with some homologous fragments of rps12, ndhB, rbcL, rrn16S, ycf3, and trnV-GAC genes were found in the cp genome of E. sagittatum. The largest length of homologous fragment was 2500 bp. Discussion

Mitochondria are essential organelles responsible for generating ATP, the primary energy currency that powers diverse cellular functions. Previous studies have demonstrated that the expansion of angiosperm mt genomes can be attributed to both the progressive aggregation of diverse repetitive elements and the assimilation of exogenous genetic material through horizontal gene transfer or intracellular gene transfer mechanisms [39, 40]. Due to the high recombination rates, the mt genome structure of angiosperm display many types, including circular, highly branched, linear, sigma-shaped, and networked [41]. In this work, a complete mt genome assembly for E. sagittatum was successfully obtained. Its mt genome exhibited a specific linear structure, similar to the mt structures of Punica granatum L [42] and Picea sitchensis (Bong.) Carrière [43]. The assembly of E. sagittatum contained 16 contigs and formed a complex, multi-branched structure (Fig. 1B),whereas the assembly of Prunus davidiana (Carr.) Franch had 14 contigs and also generated a multi-branched structure [44]. The total length of E. sagittatum mt genome was 339,191 bp, and its genome size differed significantly from those of Ribes nigrum L (450,227 bp) [45], Fritillaria ussuriensis Maxim (737,569 bp) [46] and Echinacanthus longipes H. S. Lo & D. Fang (810,200 bp) [47]. With a mt GC content of 46.91%, E. sagittatum exhibited minor differences compared to those above species, revealing that GC content of angiosperms has remained highly conserved throughout species evolution.

Although the mt genomes of plants show longer sizes and and more complex variations than mt genomes of animal [48, 49], their coding sequences were still highly conserved [50, 51]. A total of 35 PCGs (14 core and 21 variable genes) were annotated in the Agropyron cristatum (L.) Gaertn. mt genome [52], whereas 25 core and 14 variable genes (including four pseudogenes) were annotated in E. sagittatum. The variable genes are relatively few, but the core genes are highly abundant. The loss and transfer of mt PCGs led to these divergences during the processes of evolution. Nearly all PCGs were lost in the Zostera mt genome [51], while the highest number of PCGs (n = 42) was lost in the Haplomitrium mt genome [53]. Furthermore, significant differences in the number of mtPCGs were found among different species. Notably, the nuclear, plastid, and mt genomes often experience lateral transfer of DNA fragments throughout evolution; however, the complete functional inactivation of genes during major genomic rearrangements can result in the loss of one or more PCGs. Based on the annotation of mtPCGs in E. sagittatum, we preliminarily suggested that this species has undergone significant genetic changes during its evolution from a common ancestor, which may have facilitated its environmental adaptation.

RSCU is defined as the ratio of a codon's actual usage frequency to its theoretical usage frequency [54]. The RSCU value with greater than1 reveals that codon usage is higher than other synonymous codons [55]. In our study, among 29 high-frequency codons, 93.10% of codons ended with A or U at the third codon site in the E. sagittatum mt genome. The same phenomenon was also observed in the mt genomes of Evolvulus alsinoides L [56]and Mangifera indica L [57], which was attributed to plant adaptation to the environment during long-term evolution [58].

RNA editing is a common process of nucleotide modification, which mainly occurs in the organelles of higher plants such as chloroplasts and mitochondria, and plays an important role in gene expression and regulating plant cytoplasmic inheritance-related traits [59, 60]. The common occurrence of RNA editing is regulated by single-base conversion, of which C-to-U conversion is the most common [61, 62]. In E. sagittatum, a total of 720 RNA editing sites were identified across 34 PCGs. Reportedly, 731 RNA editing sites among 39 PCGs and 623 RNA editing sites across 39 genes were detected in the mt genomes of Ribes nigrum L [45] and Viburnum chinshanense Graebn [63], respectively. All the RNA editing sites were observed at the first or second codon in the E. sagittatum mt genome, of which were C-to-U conversions. This phenomenon was found in the mt genomes of Angelica biserrata (R. H. Shan & C. C. Yuan) [64] and Bupleurum chinense Franch [65], respectively. 500 RNA editing sites (about 69.44%) changed at the second codon position. Furthermore, 53.1% of RNA editing sites of the Agropyron cristatum (L.) Gaertn mt genome occurred at the second codon position [66]. Moreover, the hydrophobicity of amino acids has a closely relationship with the folding and secondary structure formation of proteins [67]. The editing amino acids transferred stop codons can terminate protein synthesis in advance, which has also been reported in the mt genomes of Scutellaria tsinyunensis C. Y. Wu & S. Chow [68] and Primula valentiniana Hand.-Mazz [69]. The RNA editing sites obtained in this work offered valuable information to predict gene functions during the evolution process of E. sagittatum.

Repeat elements are postulated to play an important role in shaping the mt genomes due to inter-molecular recombination [70]. They are abundant in the mt genomes and have been widely utilized in gene expression regulation, adaptive evolution and population genetics studies [71]. In this genome, three types of repeat elements, namely, SSRs, tandem repeats and dispersed repeats, were observed. With tetramer repeats being the most abundant type, they accounted for 43.04% of the total SSRs. These tetramer repeats might be selected as potential molecular markers in Epimedium for future studies on species identification,adaptive evolution and interspecific genetic variations. Furthermore, the SSRs of E. sagittatum were mainly located at the intergenic spacer regions and resulted in high content of AT (53.08%). Consistent with the findings in other angiosperms such as Luffa cylindrica (L.) M. Roem, Brassica rapa L.var. Purpuraria, and Lactuca sativa L. var. ramosa Hort, the calculated AT content of SSRs further contributes to the high AT richness identified in plant mt genomes [72, 73]. Subsequently, compared with ten tandem repeats, a total of 255 dispersed repeats containing palindromic and forward repeats, were observed in the E. sagittatum mt genome. In our study, a total of 32,001 bp repeats were detected in the E. sagittatum mt genome, representing 9.43% of the total E. sagittatum mt genome. These identified repeats might facilitate intermolecular recombination in mt genomes during Berberidaceae evolution. These abundant repeat elements might not only promote the frequency of intermolecular recombination but also change the sizes and structures of plant mt genomes [74–78].

Pi can reveal the variation of nucleic acid sequences among different species, and the regions with highly variation can be served as candidate molecular markers for population genetics [79, 80]. In E. sagittatum, the largest Pi value of 34 PCGs was atp9 gene, illustrating that atp9 gene could be selected as a candidate molecular markers for the Epimedium species. The atp9 gene was widely annotated in plant mt genome, and played an important role in the ATP synthase [68, 80]. The atp9 gene was identified as a highest variation region in the mt genome of F. ussuriensis: an endangered medicinal plant in China, which also involved in the ATP synthase [46]. Moreover, five highly variable regions, including atp9, rps4, atp4, atp8 and atp1 were observed and designated as available molecular markers. Five highly regions (atp1, nad2, nad6, cox2, and cob,) and four highly regions (rpl5, atp8, and rps3) were identified as candidate molecular markers in the mt genomes of Lactuca sativa L.var. ramosa Hort [73] and Ilex metabaptista Loes. ex Diels [79], respectively. In addition, three rRNA genes, namely, rrn5, rrn26 and rrn18, with low Pi values were relatively conserved, with similar results finding in the F. ussuriensis [46] and I. metabaptista [79] mt genomes. The molecular markers identified in this study mingt be instrumental in distinguishing E. sagittatum from other Epimedium species.

The Ka/Ks ratio also serves as a key metric for analyzing sequence divergence and evolutionary relationships among PCGs across species [80]. The Ka/Ks ratio illustrates evolutionary selection pressures (positive, neutral, or negative) acting on PCGs in different species [81, 82] Using E. sagittatum as reference, we detected 31 PCGs in seven closely related species of plants and the results revealed that 90.32% of the genes had Ka/Ks < 1, which indicated that ATP synthase-, NADH dehydrogenase-, cytochrome c oxidase-related genes underwent negative selection compared with E. sagittatum during the evolutionary process. However, ccmB rps10 and rps12 underwent positive selection among different species. Previous studies have shown most genes were affected by negative selection in the seed plants [83]. Four genes, namely, rps4, cob, mttB, and ccmB, were affected by positive selection as compared with A. cristatum [52]. Through analyzing the Ka/Ks ratio, we can find subtle variations in homologous PCGs across species, which can further enable predictions about species' evolutionary trajectories and contribute to building more accurate evolutionary relationships and taxonomies.

With the development of high-depth sequencing technologies, a large amount of genomeic data was extensively utilized to reconstruct the phylogenetic relationship among different species [84, 85]. The phylogenetic relationships between E. sagittatum and other 15 related species were analyzed employing a Bayesian phylogenetic tree among 28 PCGs. Phylogenetic analyses of 16 species showed that E. sagittatum was closely related to E. wushanense and E. pubescens. Based on cp genomes, six Epimedium species, being E.acuminatum, E.dolichostemon, E.pseudowushanense, E.lishihchenii, E.koreanum, and E. sagittatum were well cluster into a group, indicating that E. sagittatum was closely related to E. lishihchenii [86]. Molecular phylogenetic analyses confirmed that Epimedium was divided into five distinctive clades [24–27]. Epimedium, a genus within the Berberidaceae family, contains approximately 58 species and holds significant value in both evolutionary studies and commercial applications [87]. In China, Epimedium has been used as a medicinal herb for more than 2,000 years and contains about 48 species. Furthermore, many species (e.g., E. sagittatum, E. pubescens, and E. acuminatum) exhibit extensive infra-specific variation in both morphology and medicinal composition [87]. The resulting phylogenetic tree revealed clear evolutionary relationships among the species. As an invaluable traditional Chinese medicine, E. sagittatum may have developed some unique characteristics in its mt genome to regulate the biosynthesis of epimedin and other nutritional compounds.The first reported E. sagittatum mt genome will offer valuable information for phylogenetic and evolutionary studies of Berberidaceae family in the future.

Horizontal gene transfer is easily occurred within the plant mt genomes due to a large number of recombination phenomena [88]. In our study, a total of 14 homologous fragments within the E. sagittatum mt genome were identified, accounting for 2.64% of its full length. Similar phenomena have been observed in other Chinese medicine plants such as Leonurus japonicus Houtt (28 homologous fragments, including 5.51%) [89], Viola diffusa Ging(No. 24, 2.72%) [90], and Astragalus membranaceus (Fisch.) Bunge (No. 22, 0.4%) [91]. Six genes, including five complete genes (being trnP-TGG, trnW-CCA, trnD-GTC, trnH-GTG, and trnM-CAT) and rrn18, were transferred from cp to mt genomes of E. sagittatum. Most of the transferred genes were tRNAs. Among chloroplast-derived gene transfers to mt genomes, only tRNA genes retain functionality [92, 93]. Previous studies have shown that chloroplast-derived tRNA genes are essential for the translation of plant mt genomes [94]. Most of the plastid fragments migrating to mitochondria are tRNA genes, pseudogenes and intergenic sequences [5], and most of the pseudogenes encode important cp proteins. Four pseudogenes, namely, rpl5, rps11, rps14 and rps7, were identified in this genome. Previous studies have reported that a large number of tRNA genes migrated from cp to mt genomes are more conducive to meeting the needs of protein synthesis, contributing to plant evolution and adaptation [95, 96]. As a result, the increased number of tRNA genes in E. sagittatum might increase the adaptability to the Chinese environments. However, the potential mechanism and role of these transferred genes are worthy of further study in the future.

To explore divergent regions between E. sagittatum and the related species, the arrangement of homologous genes or sequences was identified through a covariance study. Based on the results, the highest degrees of synteny with A. argyi and T. pratense were 32.93% and 25.38% in the dot plot, respectively. E. sagittatum exhibited significant collinearity differences with the related species. The results indicate extensive genomic rearrangements in the E. sagittatum mt genome.

Conclusions

High-quality sequencing data of the E. sagittatum were achieved using a hybrid approach integrating Illumina and Nanopore platforms. Next, the E. sagittatum mt genome comprises 339,191 bp, containing 68 unique genes (39 PCGs, 22 tRNAs, 3 rRNAs and 4 pseudogenes), and exhibits a multi-branched structure. Analysis of codon usage revealed a bias toward A or U-ended codons within this genome. A total of 79 SSRs, 10 tandem repeats and 255 dispersed repeats were observed within the genome. In E. sagittatum, 720 RNA editing sites were obtained within the 34 PCGs. Phylogenetic analysis showed that E. sagittatum was closely clustered with E. wushanense and E. pubescens. The low Pi values observed in most PCGs indicate a conserved mt genome in E. sagittatum. Most genes underwent negative selection during evolution, whereas ccmB rps10 and rps12 exhibited positive selection in different plants. Additionally, Most of the transferred sequences from cp genome to mt genome of E. sagittatum were tRNA genes. Our findings provide valuable insights for genetic variation and genetic evolution not only in E. sagittatum but also in related species.
