Electromagnetic stimulation for overactive bladder in myelomeningocele children: randomized controlled trial
Authors: Heba A. KHALIFA, Walaa E. HENEIDY, Mostafa ABDELRAZEK, Mohamed A. BEHIRY, Engy B. MOUSTAFA, Mohamed M. MAZEN, Ayman M EL MAKAKEY, Nehad A. ABO-ZAID
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDMyelomeningocele (MMC) is a congenital anomaly associated with neurological and urological complications, including overactive bladder. Overactive bladder is manifested by symptoms such as urgent and frequent urination, incontinence, and recurrent urinary tract infection, all of which can negatively affect children’s quality of life.AIMTo investigate the efficacy of pulsed electromagnetic stimulation on overactive neurogenic bladder in MMC children.DESIGNRandomized controlled trial.POPULATIONForty children with MMC and overactive neurogenic bladder.METHODSChildren were recruited and allocated randomly into two groups of equal sizes; their ages ranged from four to twelve years. The sham PEMS group received standard anticholinergic medical treatment and sham pulsed electromagnetic stimulation for 20 minutes, while the real PEMS group received active pulsed electromagnetic stimulation (PEMS) for 20 minutes along with the same medical treatment as the sham group. For each group, treatment was delivered three times/week for three consecutive months. Urodynamic studies (UDS) were conducted, and daily scores for the frequency and severity of incontinence episodes were recorded. All the measured outcomes were evaluated at baseline and three months post-intervention.RESULTSAfter treatment, UDS variables increased significantly in the real PEMS group compared to the sham PEMS group; detrusor leak point pressure increased, as did maximum cystometric capacity, expected maximum cystometric capacity, and first uninhibited detrusor contraction volume, while daily incontinence scores significantly decreased.CONCLUSIONSAdding pulsed electromagnetic stimulation to conventional therapy was associated with controlling neurogenic overactive bladder in MMC children.CLINICAL REHABILITATION IMPACTPulsed electromagnetic stimulation has a notable effect on the neurogenic overactive bladder in MMC children.

Introduction

Myelomeningocele (MMC) is a congenital defect that can significantly alter a person’s life, due to incomplete neural tube closure during the 4th week of pregnancy. Intrauterine trauma to the exposed spinal cord causes incontinence, permanent paralysis, and cognitive impairment.1 Neurogenic bladder is among the most common and serious comorbid conditions in children with MMC, occurring in more than 90% of cases. Its significance underscores the need for individualized management approaches and early diagnosis. Specific urinary symptoms in each patient are determined by the location and type of neurological damage.2

Complications that may be associated with a neurogenic bladder in child include, but are not limited to, urinary incontinence, recurrent urinary tract infections (UTIs), vesicoureteral reflux, progressive kidney damage, and kidney failure.3 Neurogenic bladder and subsequent renal failure may contribute to morbidity and mortality in patients with MMC, particularly through urosepsis and progressive kidney disease.4

Addressing neurogenic dysfunction in the lower urinary tract in children presents a significant challenge for specialists in pediatric urology. The primary goals include preserving kidney function, reducing the incidence of UTIs, achieving urinary continence, and promoting independence as these children grow into adulthood.5 However, such management cannot fully prevent the urinary sequelae of neurogenic bladder from significantly affecting an individual’s quality of life and causing considerable psychological and physical distress.6

The main treatments to reduce bladder distension and limit the development or progression of hydronephrosis and subsequent kidney failure are anticholinergic drugs and clean intermittent catheterization (CIC).7 Unfortunately, not all children respond to anticholinergics. Recurrent UTIs is another complication associated with CIC.7 If these methods fail, surgical procedures such as bladder augmentation will be performed by the urologist; however, these procedures have metabolic side effects, including metabolic acidosis, increased risk of calculi formation, mucus production and an elevated increased risk of malignancy.8, 9

Magnetic field stimulation is a conservative, novel intervention that non-invasively stimulates the nervous system by activating the pelvic floor muscles10 and sacral roots (S2-S4)11 to address urinary incontinence without causing pain or adverse effects.12 It has been documented in previous research as a promising method for suppressing detrusor hyperactivity in females with idiopathic overactive bladder, stress incontinence, lumbosacral plexopathy, and spinal cord injury.13-15 Therefore, the purpose of our study was to evaluate the effectiveness of PEMS in controlling neurogenic overactive bladder in children with MMC. We hypothesized that PEMS could benefit children with MMC in managing neurogenic overactive bladder.

Materials and methods

Blinding and allocation

A randomized, controlled, single-blind research was conducted in the outpatient clinic at the Faculty of Physical Therapy, Cairo University, from May 2025 to December 2025. Informed consent was signed by the parents of each child prior to enrolment. The study adhered to good clinical practices guidelines, and the Declaration of Helsinki, and was approved by the Ethics Committee for Clinical Research at The Faculty of Physical Therapy, Cairo University, with the registration number P.T.REC/012/002423. This study has been registered under the identification number NCT04187027 on ClinicalTrials.gov.

Study population

The study was conducted on forty MMC children with neurogenic overactive bladder. All children were referred by experienced neurologists and urologists with a confirmed clinical diagnosis. The presence of uninhibited detrusor contractions was confirmed by a urodynamic study. Children of both sexes were included, with ages ranging between four and twelve years. All participants were medically and psychologically stable and able to respond accurately to verbal instructions. Children with damage of upper urinary tract (Detrusor Leak Point Pressures (DLPP) >40), children with any neurological disorders other than spina bifida, cardiovascular disorders, or hearing or visual impairments were excluded. Children with a previous history of bladder surgery, those with any implanted metal, and uncooperative children were also excluded.

Randomization

A total of 48 children with MMC were enrolled after eligibility assessment. Five children were excluded for not meeting the selection criteria, and three others were excluded because their parents declined participation. Randomization was performed for 40 cases, which were allocated randomly into two equal parallel groups using random allocation software to minimize selection bias. An external assessor performed the randomization using sealed envelopes. Treatment was delivered by a trained and skilled physiotherapist, while another therapist, who was unaware of the patients’ group allocation, conducted the assessments. This approach was used to eliminate bias. The randomization chart of participants is represented in Figure 1.

Outcomes measures

The personal information of each child was recorded on a data collection sheet. Prior to the collection of data, each child’s parent was fully informed about the purpose and procedures of the study, and individual assessments were conducted. Each child was treated at approximately the same time of day in a quiet room. Urodynamic study and daily incontinence score were used to assess the children at base line and after three months of treatment.

Primary outcome

Urodynamic study (UDS) is the golden standard method for diagnosing urinary and bladder dysfunctions. It assesses both the static and dynamic components of bladder functions.16 This approach is considered the most objective means of detecting dysfunctions in the lower urinary tract during both phases: filling/storage and voiding.17 Maximum Cystometric Capacity, Expected Maximum Cystometric Capacity, Frist Uninhibited Detrusor Contraction, and Detrusor Leak Point Pressures were assessed for each child using the same technique as described in literature:18, 19

Secondary outcome

Daily incontinence score: the frequency and severity of incontinence episodes were quantified based on a 0-3 scale: A score of zero corresponds to complete dryness; one represents nighttime wetness once per day, which is defined as mild; two indicates wetness less than 50% of the time between CIC, which is considered moderate; and three represents wetness more than 50% of the time between CIC is rated severe. Improvement was defined as a reduction of two or more grades in daytime incontinence score.24

Interventions

All participating children received standard anticholinergic drugs (Oxybutynin) according to their body weight,3 divided into two doses (0.3 mg/kg/day). PEMS was applied to both groups using two different modes of delivery; children in the real PEMS group received 20 minutes of pulsed electromagnetic stimulation (PEMS) over the sacral region, while the children in the sham PEMS group received the same program, but with PEMS was administered in a sham mode. Treatment sessions toke place three times per week for three consecutive months for both groups (a total of 36 sessions for each child). Attendance records were used to ensure fulfilled attendance of the treatment sessions (90% attendance was considered acceptable):

real PEMS was administrated using (PMT Qs; ASALASER, Arcugnano, Vicenza, Italy). During application, children were instructed to lie prone, and the device applied over the sacral region (S2-S4) without skin contact (by radiation) for 20 minutes, at a frequency of 5 Hz, and low intensity output (15%) for 5 s/min;25

sham PEMS was applied using a sham coil, which has a similar appearance and sound but does not produce a magnetic field during use. The frequency and duration of therapy were the same as in the real PEMS group.

Sample size

The sample size was determined using F tests (MANOVA: Special effects and interactions), with a Type I error (α) = 0.05, power (1-α error probability) = 0.80, Pillai’s V = 0.21, and effect size f2 (V) = 0.2658 with comparing two independent groups for five different measures was conducted using the G* power program version 3.1 (Heinrich-Heine-University, Düsseldorf, Germany). 38 patients (at least 18 in each group) were the proper minimum number of samples for this research. An unpublished pilot study with ten cases (5 in each group), that used the MCC, expected MCC, FUDC, DLPP, and daily incontinence score as outcome measures, was used to estimate the effect size.

Statistical analysis

For statistical evaluation, the SPSS package program version 25 for Windows was used. SPSS, Inc., IBM, Chicago, IL. Levene’s test and Shapiro-Wilk test were applied to examine the homogeneity and normality of the data, respectively. Mean and standard deviation were used to summarize parametric quantitative data, while median and interquartile ranges were used to summarize non-parametric quantitative data. Frequency and percentage were used to summarize the categorical data. The independent t- test was applied for comparison of age and weight, and chi-square test was applied for comparison of sex distribution between groups. Wilcoxon rank and Mann Whitney tests were used for comparison of daily incontinence scores pre and post intervention, within and among the two different groups, respectively. The Multivariate MANOVA test was used to compare (MCC, Expected MCC, FUDC volume, and DLPP), with treatment group (sham versus real) as among subjects’ factors and time of evaluation (baseline and after treatment) as the within subjects’ factors. For all statistical analysis, P<0.05 was considered as significance level.

Data availability

The data associated with the paper are not publicly available but are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Discussion

Physiotherapists and pediatric urologists face a complex challenge in managing neurogenic overactive bladder dysfunction in children with MMC. Their key objectives include safeguarding kidney function, reducing of urinary tract infections, and achieving continence, all of which can significantly improve quality of life. So, our trial was conducted to investigate the impact of PEMS on neurogenic overactive bladder in children with MMC. The results of our study showed that the intervention was associated with statistically significant improvements in MCC, expected MCC, FUDC, and DLPP, as well as a significant decrease observed in daily incontinence scores in two different groups, with greater improvement observed in real PEMS group after three consecutive months of the rehabilitation program.

In this study, the selection of children with MMC is in line with Ab et al.,26 that reported detrusor over activity was accompanied by sphincter over activity in 40% of cases. Supraspinal lesions affecting urine storage and bladder emptying, secondary to myelodysplasia-related anatomical malformations, may contribute to detrusor overactivity.

The results were associated with a statistically significant improvement in all measured UDS parameters in both groups, with more favorable outcomes in real PEMS group. This improvement may reflect the neurophysiological effects hypothesized in studies on adults with SCI studies.

Limited studies have investigated the effect of PEMS in adults with detrusor hyperreflexia after spinal cord injury, with no studies have clarified the underlying mechanism of detrusor inhibition in children with MMC. Based on findings from adult SCI studies,25, 27 we hypothesized that PEMS may suppress detrusor contractions through sacral root inhibition via three inhibitory pathways: Sphincter-Detrusor Inhibition (Pelvic Floor Reflex), sympathetic Activation (Edvardsen’s Reflex), and Spinal/Supraspinal Interneuronal Inhibition). In addition, PEMS may stimulate the pelvic floor muscle’s motor endplates and efferent nerves, thereby improving the strength of muscle and its endurance this comes in agreement with Juszczak et al. It may also modulate the firing rate of the somatic nerves supplying pelvic muscles, which could influence tone of the sphincter.28

The exact mechanism by which magnetic stimulation suppresses detrusor contraction in children with myelomeningocele has not yet been clarified. Therefore, our explanation remains hypothetical and is based on findings reported in adults with detrusor hyperreflexia after spinal cord injury.27 It is possible that PEMS may acts through sacral root neuromodulation, inhibition of afferent C-fiber activity, activation of pelvic floor reflexes, and suppression of impulses to detrusor motor neurons, thereby reducing unstable detrusor contractions and improving bladder storage function.14-19 However, these mechanisms have not been directly confirmed in children with MMC, and further experimental and clinical studies are needed to clarify the underlying neurophysiological basis of the observed clinical benefit.

Magnetic stimulation may suppress the micturition reflex and could prevent detrusor contraction, as suggested by adult studies. The afferent branches of muscle nerves may inhibit voiding during fight-or-flight responses, while those of the anorectal nerves could suppress voiding during defecation. In addition, filling the bladder increases urethral sphincter activity, which may be associated with inhibition of the detrusor contraction; a similar effect may occur during filling of the bladder due to stimulating the activity of sympathetic nervous system (Edvardsen’s reflex). We hypothesized that PEMS may enhance these inhibitory mechanisms in children with MMC by increasing urethral sphincter activity, that could potentially induce relaxation of detrusor muscle.29

Similar to Sheriff et al., unstable human detrusor contractions may be suppressed by magnetic stimulation. As a result, functional magnetic stimulation (FMS) may represent a more acceptable and practical neuromodulation technique and could serve as a successful non-invasive alternative to electrical stimulation using implanted electrodes.25, 27

Improvement in the sham PEMS group may be attributed to anticholinergic drugs (Oxybutynin) which are primarily antimuscarinic drug; it could increase bladder capacity and relax the detrusor muscle by potentially inhibiting the action of the neurotransmitter acetylcholine at muscarinic receptors.30

The findings of this research were associated with a statistically significant reduction in daily incontinence score and greater improvement in UDS variables in the real PEMS group. This is in line with Liang et al.,31 who reported that adding anticholinergic drugs (Oxybutynin) to PEMS may increase bladder capacity and prolong the time between urinations without impacting bladder emptying, by enhancing urine storage and promoting detrusor relaxation.

Magnetic field stimulation has a powerful penetrating capacity and can activate deep tissues which are difficult to stimulate using electrical stimulation. This was supported by Quek, who determined that, unlike electrical stimulation, which reduces exponentially with tissue resistance, magnetic stimulation passes through tissues with minimal attenuation and diminished only in proportion to the square of the distance. Due to this fact, magnetic stimulation could be more effective on neural tissue and may reduce the localized discomfort at the application site.29 No adverse effects or complications were reported by any of the children in this research.

On the contrary, the current findings are in conflict with Morris et al., who reported that magnetic stimulation was not associated with a positive impact on controlling idiopathic detrusor over-activity in women after six weeks of application. The discrepancy between our results and those of Morris et al., may be related to differences in patients’ populations, and shorter time of treatment protocol in their study.32

Previous studies have investigated the therapeutic effects of magnetic stimulation over relatively short treatment periods. For example, Tezer et al., investigated the impact of magnetic stimulation (20 minutes sessions twice weekly for six weeks) combined with bladder training in women with idiopathic overactive bladder,15 also Lin et al., investigated the effect of pelvic floor transcutaneous magnetic stimulation combined with bladder training in children with overactive bladder, using a short treatment duration (20 minutes. per for two weeks).33 Likewise, Raheem and Abdel-Hady evaluated the therapeutic effect of magnetic stimulation combined with biofeedback patients with multiple sclerosis overactive bladder (for 30 minutes sessions over six weeks).34 Collectively, these findings suggest that magnetic stimulation can provide meaningful short-term symptom relief; however, the durability of its benefit may be limited, and repeated or longer treatment may be necessary to maintain the response.

The interpretation of our findings should take into account the complex bladder-sphincter dysfunction characteristic of MMC. In children with MMC, detrusor overactivity often coexists with overactive sphincter activity or detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia, and urodynamic studies indicate that this mixed pattern is common rather than exceptional.30 Therefore, the observed benefit of PEMS may primarily reflect improvement in the detrusor/storage component of dysfunction, while the voiding phase may remain partially unaffected. This may explain why urodynamic parameters and clinical outcomes improved, although the overall neurogenic dysfunction was not completely resolved.35 Future studies should assess both storage and voiding parameters, including sphincter activity and voiding efficiency, to better define the full therapeutic effect of PEMS in this population.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Our research represents the first single-blind randomized controlled trial assessing the effect of PEMS on the neurogenic OAB in children with MMC using non-invasive, safe, relatively inexpensive, and well-tolerated neuro-electrophysiological technique that can be applied without discomfort passing through clothing and without the need for implanted devices.

Further research is recommended to evaluate the impact of PEMS on severe forms of overactive bladder accompanied by UTIs (DLPP>40). The study was limited to a period of 3 months only; therefore, future studies are required to assess the effect of longer duration of intervention with follow-up periods to determine the sustainability of observed statistical improvements over time. Additionally, follow-up after cessation of PEMS is necessary to get the accurate and best result of the study to validate if this improvement is permanent or not due to the short duration of benefit remains a critical limitation, because symptom improvement may decline after treatment discontinuation. Other studies are also needed to investigate the effect of PEMS on voiding efficiency in children with MMC.

Conclusions

The findings of our study suggest that pulsed electromagnetic stimulation may have a positive effect on neurogenic overactive bladder in children with myelomeningocele. We believe that adding pulsed electromagnetic field therapy to standard anticholinergic medical care may provide additional control of neurogenic overactive bladder and may be suitable before the need to implement electrical device stimulation in pediatric patients with lower urinary tract dysfunction.
