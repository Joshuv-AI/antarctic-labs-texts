Echocardiographic Characterization of Major League Soccer Players
Authors: Aakash Bavishi, Chinyen Chuo, George Chiampas, Robyn E Bryde, Jonathan H Kim, Dermot Phelan, Margot Putukian, John A Gallucci, Brad Mehl, Holly Silvers, Matthew W Martinez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere are limited data detailing cardiac structural changes associated with intensive athletic training in professional soccer athletes. The lack of sport-specific cardiac reference ranges may complicate differentiation of physiologic athletic remodeling from underlying cardiac pathology.ObjectivesThe objective of the study was to characterize echocardiographic cardiac remodeling patterns in a large cohort of male professional soccer athletes and provide sport-specific reference data for clinical interpretation.MethodsWe performed a cross-sectional echocardiographic study of 1,368 male Major League Soccer athletes screened during the 2018 to 2023 seasons using standardized transthoracic echocardiography protocols in accordance with American Society of Echocardiography guidelines.ResultsAthletes ranged in age from 21 to 39 years (mean age 25.8 [3.7] years). Among the cohort, 324/1,368 (23.7%) athletes were Black, 392/1,368 (28.7%) Hispanic, and 623/1,368 (45.5%) White. Increased left ventricular (LV) internal diameter in diastole (>58 mm) was present in 115/1,368 athletes (8.5%; 95% CI: 7.0%-10.0%), whereas 262/878 athletes (29.8%; 95% CI: 26.8%-32.8%) demonstrated right ventricular enlargement. The mean LV mass index was 97.2 [16.9] g/m2 (95% CI: 96.3-98.1), with 187/1,326 athletes (14.1%; 95% CI: 12.2%-16.0%) meeting the criteria for LV hypertrophy. Eccentric hypertrophy was present in 127/1,326 athletes (9.6%), concentric hypertrophy in 60/1,326 athletes (4.5%), and concentric remodeling in 241/1,326 athletes (18.2%).ConclusionsIncreased cardiac dimensions and concentric remodeling patterns are frequently present in professional soccer athletes. These findings provide sport-specific echocardiographic reference data that may assist clinicians in distinguishing physiologic athletic remodeling from cardiovascular pathology in elite soccer athletes.Key words: echocardiography, screening, sports cardiology

Exercise-induced cardiac remodeling develops in response to the hemodynamic demands of intensive training. Increased left ventricular (LV) mass either due to mild LV chamber enlargement and/or increased wall thickness has been consistently described in several studies evaluating athletes engaged in a wide variety of sporting disciplines.1, 2, 3, 4 Importantly, the specific sport and the degree of static and dynamic exercise significantly influences the extent and type of cardiac remodeling that occurs.5

Soccer is the most popular sport globally and is an organized sport in which the incidence of sudden cardiac death is increased compared to most other individual sport types.6,7 As such, differentiation of sport-specific cardiac adaptions from pathologic changes, particularly those that occur in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) and other genetic cardiomyopathies is essential in the clinical evaluation of elite soccer players.8,9 However, comprehensive studies reporting normative reference values for cardiac measurements are lacking. Although reference echocardiographic values in elite youthful U.S. national team soccer players have been published,10 data are lacking from older professional soccer athletes. The purpose of this study was to provide detailed echocardiographic characterization of cardiac remodeling in a large cohort of male Major League Soccer (MLS) athletes and to provide a framework of reference values specific to professional soccer players.

Methods

Study population

At the time this study was collected, MLS preseason cardiac clearance required that all players undergo an annual comprehensive 12 point American Heart Association history and physical exam along with an electrocardiogram and a transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) every 2 years. Preseason TTEs were performed by an independent company (Athletic Heart) using standardized acquisition protocols by a limited number of trained and vetted sonographers. All studies underwent centralized over-read within the Athletic Heart platform by an experienced echocardiographer. For the purposes of this analysis, the entire data set was subsequently re-reviewed, with confirmation and remeasurement of key parameters by an experienced reader to ensure measurement consistency. Oversight of imaging interpretation and adjudication of discrepancies was provided by the league cardiologist using a centralized electronic medical record system.

For this study, 1,368 athletes clinically screened during preseason were included. Subgroup analysis by race/ethnicity was performed using self-identified groups including White, Black, Hispanic, and athletes of other race/ethnicity; athletes selecting more than 1 race/ethnicity were categorized within the “other” group. Athletes with incomplete echocardiographic data or known or newly discovered structural cardiac pathology, including HCM, dilated cardiomyopathy, anomalous coronary artery origin, or significant aortopathy, were excluded from the analysis.

The review and analysis of the TTEs for this study was approved by the MLS institutional review board. This study was reviewed and deemed exempt by the Institutional Review Board internally by the MLS Medical Assessment Research Committee for ethical standards and scientific rigor (Protocol #CARD-MLS-062018). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the analysis of deidentified data that protected the anonymity of the subjects.

Transthoracic echocardiograms

TTEs were performed using commercially available systems. All two-dimensional measurements were performed in accordance with the American Society of Echocardiography (ASE) guidelines on chamber quantification.11 LV internal diameter diastole (LVIDd), LV interventricular septum (IVS), and posterior wall thickness (PWT) were measured in the parasternal long-axis view at end-diastole. When feasible, the aortic root and ascending aorta were further evaluated in accordance with ASE recommendations, with measurements obtained at the sinus of Valsalva and proximal ascending aorta using the leading-edge to leading-edge technique in end-diastole. Visualization of the ascending aorta was optimized by obtaining high parasternal views, including transducer repositioning to a higher intercostal space when necessary to improve alignment with the ascending aorta.

The left atrial (LA) anteroposterior diameter and aortic root diameter (AoR) were measured from the parasternal long-axis view. LA diameter was measured at end-systole. LA volumes were calculated using the biplane area-length method and indexed to body surface area (BSA) (calculated as the square root [height (cm) × weight (kg)]/3,600). Right atrial volumes were calculated using a single plane measurement in the apical four-chamber view and indexed to BSA. The LV ejection fraction (EF) (LVEF) was calculated using the modified Simpson’s biplane method substituted with visual assessment in technically limited studies. Right ventricular (RV) basal diameter was measured in an apical 4-chamber RV focused view. LV hypertrophy (LVH) was defined as LV mass index (LVMI) > 115 g/m2, calculated using the linear method (Devereux formula) and indexed by BSA.11 LV relative wall thickness (RWT) was calculated as 2 × (PWT)/LVIDd.

Reproducibility

Reproducibility was assessed using 20 studies in which core dimensions were measured and Intraclass Correlation Coefficients were calculated for each measure by 2 independent readers.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are presented as means with 95% CIs or with SDs, and categorical data are summarized by frequencies and percentages in reporting of baseline characteristics. Comparisons of echocardiographic variables across racial groups (White, Black, and Hispanic athletes) were performed using Welch one-way analysis of variance to account for unequal group sizes and variance. Welch-corrected independent sample t-tests were used for two-group comparisons. Chi-square testing was used for categorical comparisons of LV geometric patterns across racial groups. When an overall group difference was identified, Games-Howell post hoc testing, which inherently adjusts for multiple comparisons, was used to assess pairwise comparisons between racial groups. Simple linear regression was used to assess the associations between cardiac measures and BSA or height. The plots were generated with R statistical software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and Microsoft Excel.

Results

Baseline patient characteristics

Baseline characteristics of the study population are shown in Table 1. The study included 1,368 male athletes aged 21 to 39 years (mean age, 25.8 [3.7] years). A total of 324 (23.7%) athletes were Black, 623 (45.4%) were White, 392 (28.7%) were Hispanic, and 29 (2.1%) belonged to other racial groups. The mean height was 180.8 (7.3) cm; weight 78.3 (8.1) kg, and BSA 1.98 (0.14) m2. BSA differed across racial groups (Welch one-way analysis of variance [ANOVA]; P < 0.01) (Table 2). On Games-Howell post hoc analysis (Table 3), Hispanic athletes demonstrated lower BSA compared with both White and Black athletes, whereas BSA was otherwise similar between White and Black athletes.

Left ventricular dimensions

The LVIDd ranged from 38.2 to 64.8 (mean 52.3 [4.0]; 95% CI, 52.0 to 52.5) mm, with the distribution of LVIDd shown in Figure 1. LV internal diameter in diastole (LVIDd) differed across racial groups (Welch one-way ANOVA, P < 0.001) (Table 2). On Games-Howell post hoc analysis (Table 3), Black athletes demonstrated smaller LVIDd (5.15 cm) compared with White athletes (5.28 cm), whereas Hispanic athletes (5.21 cm) did not differ significantly from either group.

LV dilation defined as LVIDd >58 mm was present in 115/1,368 athletes (8.45%, 95% CI, 7.0% to 10%). When adjusted for BSA, 27/1,368 (2.0%; 95% CI: 1.2% to 2.7%) players had indexed LVIDd >3.1 cm/m2. There was an approximately linear association between LVIDd and BSA extending to the uppermost biometrics of the athlete group as demonstrated in Figure 1. LV end-diastolic volume index (LVEDVi) ranged from 31.5 to 117.3 (mean 68.7 [14.0]; 95% CI: 67.8, 69.7 mL/m2). Overall, 469/1,368 (34.3%; 95% CI: 31.8% to 36.8%) of athletes had LVEDVi > ASE cutoff of 74 mL/m2 (Figure 2).

The distribution of maximal PWT and IVS is shown in Figure 3. PWT ranged from 6.2 to 12.9 mm (mean 9.84 [1.2]; 95% CI: 9.77-9.91) with 580/1,342 athletes (43.2%; 95% CI: 40.5% to 45.8%) having an PWT >10 mm. IVS ranged from 5.68 mm to 12.8 mm (mean 9.4 [1.3]; 95% CI: 9.37 to 9.50) with 421/1,342 athletes (31.4%; 95% CI: 28.9% to 33.9%) having IVS >1.0 cm. PWT differed significantly across racial groups (Welch one-way ANOVA, P < 0.001) (Table 2). Games-Howell post hoc testing (Table 3) demonstrated greater PWT in Black athletes (1.02 cm) compared with both White (0.98 cm) and Hispanic athletes (0.97 cm), whereas no significant difference was observed between White and Hispanic athletes. In contrast, IVS did not differ significantly across White, Black, and Hispanic athletes (Welch one-way ANOVA, P = 0.07) (Table 2).

LVMI mean was 97.2 (16.9 g/m2; 95% CI: 96.3-98.1) with 187/1,326 (14.1%; 95% CI: 12%-2% to 16.0%) athletes meeting the ASE criteria for LVH (>115 g/m2). With respect to gradations of LVH as per ASE, 146/1,326 (11%; 95% CI: 9.3%-12.7%) athletes had mild LVH with LVMI 115 to 131 g/m2; 37/1,326 (2.8%; 95% CI: 1.9%-3.7%) had moderate LVH with LVMI 132 to 149 g/m2; and 4/1,326 (0.3%; 95% CI: 0.01%-0.6%) athletes had severe LVH with LVMI ≥149 g/m2. Based on their LVMI and RWT, athletes were categorized into 1 of 4 categories according to ASE criteria (Figure 5): normal geometry, concentric remodeling, eccentric hypertrophy, and concentric hypertrophy. Of the 1,139 athletes with normal LV mass, 898 (67.7%) had normal LV geometry, whereas 241 (18.2%) had concentric remodeling (Figures 6 and 7). Of the 187 athletes with LVH, 127 (9.6%) had eccentric LVH and 60 (4.5%) had concentric LVH (Figures 6 and 7). The distribution of LV geometric patterns differed significantly across racial groups (chi-square test, P < 0.001), with Black athletes demonstrating a higher prevalence of concentric remodeling (26.0%) and a lower prevalence of normal geometry (58.5%) compared with White athletes (15.6% and 72.4%, respectively) and Hispanic athletes (16.2% and 67.6%, respectively).

Left ventricular ejection fraction

The mean LVEF for this athlete group ranged from 45.0% to 75.6% with a mean of 60.4% (4.8; 95% CI: 60.1-60.8). Overall, 8/771 (1.0%; 95% CI: 0.3%-1.8%) athletes had LVEF <50% with lowest LVEF of 45%. All athletes with EF <50% had normal augmentation with an ejection fraction above 65% on subsequent stress echocardiogram.

Athletes with LVEF <50% had similar LVIDd at 54.2 mm (95% CI: 51.6, 56.7) vs 52.1 mm (95% CI: 51.8, 52.4) (P = 0.162) and LVMI at 104.0 g/m2 (95% CI: 88.7, 119.4) vs 94.5 g/m2 (95% CI: 93.3, 95.8) (P = 0.266) compared to athletes with LVEF ≥50%. LVEF did not differ significantly across racial groups (Welch one-way ANOVA, P = 0.99) (Table 2).

Left atrial size

The mean LA volume index (LAVI) was 34.2 (9.8; 95% CI: 33.6-34.7) mL/m2. Of 1,278 athletes that had LA volume measured, LA enlargement (LAVI >34.0 mL/m2) was present in 558 (43.7%: 95% CI: 41.0% to 46.4%) athletes. With respect to gradations of left atrial enlargement as per ASE definitions and of the 1,278 athletes, 278 (21.8%; 95% CI: 19.5% to 24.0%) athletes had mild LAE with 34 < LAVI ≤41 mL/m2, 156/1,278 (12.2%; 95% CI: 10.4%-14.0%) athletes had moderate LAE with 41 < LAVI ≤48 mL/m2, and 124/1,278 (9.7%; 95% CI: 8.1%-11.3%) athletes had severe LAE with LAVI >48 mL/m2. LAVI differed significantly across racial groups (Welch one-way ANOVA, P = 0.003) (Table 2). Games-Howell post hoc testing (Table 3) demonstrated greater LAVI in Black athletes (35.5 mL/m2) compared to Hispanic athletes (32.9 mL/m2).

Right ventricular size

RV basal dimension was measured in 878 athletes. RV basal dimension ranged from 33 to 62 mm (mean 39.2 [4.6]; 95% CI: 38.9-39.5). A total of 262/878 athletes (29.8%, 95% CI, 26.8%-32.8%) had basal RV dimension >41 mm, one of the criteria defining RV enlargement per ASE guidelines. Athletes with enlarged RV had larger LVIDd compared to those with normal RV size (mean LVIDd: 53.6; 95% CI: 53.1-54.1 mm vs 52.1; 95% CI: 51.8-52.4 mm, respectively, with P < 0.001.) RV basal dimension did not differ significantly across White, Black, and Hispanic athletes (Welch one-way ANOVA, P = 0.15) (Table 2).

Aortic root dimension

The distribution for AoR is shown in Figure 7. AoR ranged from 21.2 to 42.8 (mean 31.1 [3.1]; 95% CI: 30.9-31.3) mm, with 5/1,264 (0.40%; 95% CI: 0.1%-0.9%) athletes meeting criteria for aortic root enlargement by ASE criteria (AoR >40 mm). When indexed for BSA, 3/1,264 (0.24%; 95% CI: 0.0%-0.6%) athletes had an AoR Diam Index >2.1 cm/m2. Correlation curves with 95% CI of AoR compared with height and BSA are shown in Figure 8. AoR indexed to BSA differed significantly across racial groups (Welch one-way ANOVA, P < 0.001) (Table 2). On Games-Howell post hoc testing (Table 3), Hispanic athletes had greater AoR indexed to BSA (1.61 cm/m2) compared with both White and Black athletes (1.56 cm/m2), whereas values were similar between White and Black athletes.

Reproducibility

For interobserver variability, the Intraclass Correlation Coefficients were 98.6% for LVIDd, 95.7% for AoR, and 91.0% for RV basal dimension. The mean intraobserver difference was 0.15 mm for LVIDd, 0.35 mm for AoR and 0.7 mm for RV basal dimension.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study provides the largest reference echocardiographic data set (n = 1,368) of male professional soccer players and is one of the largest racially and ethnically diverse echocardiographic data sets of any professional athlete group.12,13 Specifically with respect to the Hispanic population, there is a paucity of published athlete data and our large data set of 392 Hispanic athletes adds significantly to the literature.

There were several key findings (Central Illustration). Modest LV cavity enlargement (8% with LVIDd >58 mm, 34% with LVEDVi >74 mL/m2) and LVH (14% by LVMI) were common but remain below thresholds associated with HCM and other cardiomyopathies. Reduced LVEF was rare (<0.1%) with lowest LVEF of 45% but normal augmentation of the EF including an increase in stroke volume >20% during postexercise echocardiography testing was present in all 8 athletes with abnormally low LVEF at rest. RV enlargement (30%) and LAE (44%) defined by ASE criteria were common in this cohort while aortic root enlargement was rare (0.4%) in this cohort. In aggregate, our findings suggest the physiologic ranges of cardiac remodeling and phenotypes associated with elite soccer training and competition and provide potential sports-specific reference values which may be valuable in clinical practice when distinguishing more extreme forms of exercise-induced cardiac remodeling from pathologic structural changes as a consequence of mild forms of genetic cardiomyopathies.

Echocardiographic Remodeling in Professional Soccer Athletes

In this large cross-sectional echocardiographic study of 1,368 male Major League Soccer athletes, physiologic cardiac remodeling was common. Left ventricular dilation, right ventricular enlargement, increased left ventricular wall thickness, and concentric remodeling patterns were frequently observed. Left atrial enlargement was also common, while aortic root enlargement was rare. These findings provide sport-specific echocardiographic reference data that may assist clinicians in distinguishing physiologic athletic remodeling from potential cardiovascular pathology. ASE = American Society of Echocardiography; LA = left atrial; LV = left ventricular; MLS = Major League Soccer; RV = right ventricular.

Compared to data from Churchill et al10 of United States national team elite soccer players, our study had key similarities and differences. Similar findings included average LV end-diastolic diameter (52 mm vs 52 mm) and the proportion of athletes with maximal wall thickness >10 mm (43% in our study vs 41%). Although both studies demonstrated that increased RV basal diameter was common, a much higher proportion was seen in their study (54% vs 30%). Patterns of LV remodeling were also different with 35% of male athletes observed with normal geometry or eccentric remodeling compared to 68% in this study. Concentric hypertrophy was present in 19% of patients compared to 4.5% in this analysis. Potential explanations for differences include a much larger sample size of male athletes in this study (N = 1,368 vs N = 112) and difference in demographics with the mean age of 26 years in this study vs 18 years in their study and 24% Black athletes in our study vs 34% Black athletes in their study. Another key difference is that LVH for males was defined as LVMI >115 g/m2 in this study compared to 103 g/m2 using the area length method in Churchill et al.

Normative echocardiographic data help distinguish training related cardiovascular remodeling from pathologic phenotypes. Although there are normative reference data from collegiate and professional basketball players in the National Basketball Association and football players from the National Football League, similar large-scale reference data are missing in professional soccer players.8,9 Among previously studied large athletic cohorts, reference data have been useful in defining the upper physiologic limits of LV wall thickness (LVWT) and indexed chamber volumes that differentiate athletic remodeling from HCM and dilated cardiomyopathies.14 Given the varying dynamic and static component of individuals sports, it is critical to have sport specific data for clinicians. In these data presented, we observed the distribution of LVWT and upper level of normal are different in male soccer players (12-13 mm) compared to basketball players (13-14 mm) or football linemen (14-15 mm).5,8,9,14 These differences may improve the precision of clinical decision making among elite soccer athletes. Although aortic root dimensions may also demonstrate sports specific remodeling, our study also demonstrates that an aortic root > 40 mm may warrant further evaluation.15

Imaging-derived concentric LVH in the general population predicts long-term cardiovascular morbidity.16 Within the paradigm of exercise-induced remodeling, concentric LV geometry has also recently been questioned as a commonly expected structural adaptation.17 For example, particularly among male American-style football athletes, the presence of concentric LVH represents a maladaptive phenotype associated with arterial stiffening and higher resting blood pressure.18 In the present cross-sectional study, the effects of resting blood pressure and exercise blood pressure were not captured. As such, characterizing longitudinal changes in blood pressure and corollary effects on cardiac phenotypes and the prevalence of unrecognized hypertension in elite soccer players all represent critical future research directions.

One key strength of our cohort was the racial diversity with 324 Black (24%), 392 (29%) Hispanic, and 623 (46%) White athletes. Prior studies have reported racial differences in cardiovascular adaptions to exercise, including more frequent concentric LV remodeling, greater LVWT and higher LVMI specifically in comparing Black athletes compared to White athletes.9,19 In this soccer specific cohort, we found racial differences in LV geometry, LAVI, and aortic root size. It is imperative that future studies examining reference imaging ranges for athletes include more diverse cohorts. Large, racially and ethnically diverse echocardiographic data sets may ultimately contribute to reductions in false-positive findings testing and therefore mitigation of racial disparities in sports cardiology.20

Study Limitations

There were several limitations to our study. This was a cross-sectional study including different athletes at various ages and levels of training so causality between training levels and findings is not possible. Blood pressure data were not collected which limits further assessment of concentric LVH. Baseline exercise data and specific training regimen were not collected and analyzed. Data on potential confounders including the use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, performance enhancing drugs, or other substance use was not available. Social determinants of health data and country of origin was not collected. Reproducibility was not assessed for key parameters such as LVWT, LVMI, and LV volumetrics. Future studies are still warranted and include the need for analyses of female professional soccer players and longitudinal assessments to better define long-term changes in cardiac structure and function and the causal role athletic training plays in observed cardiac phenotypes.

Conclusions

In this large multiracial and ethnic cohort of professional soccer players, we provide comprehensive reference echocardiographic data. Our findings support the need for sport-specific interpretive standards. Establishing robust, sex, race/ethnicity, and sport-specific reference values will assist in distinguishing athletic remodeling from pathophysiology.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: Professional soccer athletes commonly demonstrate physiologic cardiac remodeling, including increased LV cavity dimensions, RV enlargement, and concentric LV geometry. Sport-specific echocardiographic reference values may improve differentiation of physiologic athletic remodeling from potential underlying cardiovascular pathology during cardiovascular screening and clinical evaluation of elite athletes.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: Future longitudinal studies incorporating blood pressure assessment, multimodality imaging, and clinical outcomes are needed to better define the long-term significance of concentric remodeling patterns and establish more robust sport-, sex-, and race/ethnicity-specific echocardiographic reference standards in elite athletes.
