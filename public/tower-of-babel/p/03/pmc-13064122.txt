Analysis of risk factors and development of a predictive nomogram for chronic postsurgical pain after hip arthroplasty: a study protocol in a Chinese tertiary hospital
Authors: Ruping Ni, Zhaowen Gao, Zhenghua Jiang, Xiaorui Hao, Mingyuan Zhang, Dongli Lu, Zhaochun Wu, Ping Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChronic postsurgical pain (CPSP) after hip arthroplasty is a major complication that affects patients’ long-term quality of life. However, reliable tools for the individualised prediction of CPSP risk after hip arthroplasty are lacking. This study aims to develop and validate a nomogram model to predict CPSP risk in patients undergoing hip arthroplasty.Methods and analysisThis prospective observational cohort study will consecutively recruit 300 patients undergoing primary hip arthroplasty at the Department of Orthopaedics and Joints, Nanping First Hospital Affiliated with Fujian Medical University. The primary outcome is CPSP assessed at 3 months postoperatively (Visual Analogue Scale score ≥4). Candidate predictor variables have been identified based on literature review and clinical expertise, and include demographics, comorbidities, preoperative pain, psychological status and surgical and perioperative management. The dataset will be randomly split into development and internal validation sets in a 7:3 ratio. We will employ Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator regression to select variables and will use multivariable logistic regression to build the final prediction model. Internal validation will be performed using bootstrap resampling (1000 repetitions). The model’s discrimination, calibration and clinical utility will be assessed using the C-statistic (area under the curve), calibration plots and decision curve analysis, respectively. The final model will be presented as a nomogram.Ethics and disseminationThe study protocol has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanping First Hospital (Approval No: NPSY202412034). All participants will provide written informed consent. Introduction

Hip arthroplasty is heralded as among ‘the most successful operations of the 21st century’.1 It is widely used to treat destructive hip joint diseases and is an effective intervention for traumatic and end-stage degenerative hip conditions.2 Studies worldwide indicate consistent and rapid increases in the number of hip arthroplasty procedures performed.3 4 Furthermore, projections suggest that the number of such procedures will increase from 208 600 in 2005 to 572 000 by 2030.5

Postoperative pain is a critical factor influencing recovery after hip arthroplasty, and safe, effective pain management is crucial for enhancing postoperative rehabilitation.6 7 Inadequate control of acute postoperative pain can lead to chronic postsurgical pain (CPSP), defined as pain persisting for >3 months after surgery, excluding other causes.8 The reported incidence rates of CPSP vary widely, from 5% to 65%,9 10 with orthopaedic surgeries showing a high incidence. CPSP is persistent and detrimental and can significantly impair patient quality of life long after hip surgery and is often accompanied by emotional issues such as anxiety and depression. CPSP is associated with various factors, including patient age, sex and obesity; surgery duration; postoperative deep vein thrombosis; timing of initial hip mobilisation post surgery; preoperative and predischarge anxiety or depression; surgical approach; and anaesthesia technique.11,16

Developing predictive models for postoperative pain, implementing preventive and individualised treatments and identifying risk factors for CPSP could aid in predicting and preventing its onset.17 18 Various risk factors for CPSP following hip arthroplasty have been reported,19 but no studies have yet integrated these factors to quantitatively assess individual patient risk in clinical practice. This study aims to analyse factors associated with CPSP in adult patients undergoing hip arthroplasty, develop a risk prediction model based on logistic regression (LR) and construct a visual nomogram. Nomograms are intuitive and effective tools for multivariate prediction and are widely used in clinical prediction model research to enhance model operability and clinical acceptance. However, most such studies have focused on other types of surgery20 and have employed retrospective designs.16 This study is the first to construct a prediction model using a prospective cohort design in hip arthroplasty. The predictions of this model can provide a reference for early clinical interventions for CPSP. Early identification of high-risk patients can facilitate individualised interventions, reduce the incidence of CPSP and improve patient outcomes.

Methods and analysis

Research subjects

This protocol describes a prospective observational cohort study to be conducted in the Department of Orthopaedics and Joints, Nanping First Hospital affiliated with Fujian Medical University. We will consecutively enrol patients scheduled for primary elective unilateral hip arthroplasty between November 2024–July 2026 to develop and validate a clinical prediction model for CPSP following hip arthroplasty. Figure 1 presents the study flowchart. This protocol was written in strict accordance with the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) and the TRIPOD for Artificial Intelligence guidelines21 (online supplemental Appendix 1).

Sample size

Based on previous studies, we estimated the incidence of CPSP after hip arthroplasty to be approximately 23.30%–31.61%.22 Following the ‘events per variable’ (EPV) principle for prediction model sample size calculation (EPV ≥5–10),23 and assuming that approximately 14 variables will enter the final model after initial Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) screening, the required number of CPSP events is at least 70. Therefore, the estimated total sample size is 70 participants/(0.2330–0.3161)=221–300. We will randomly allocate the total sample in a 7:3 ratio, with approximately 210 and 90 patients in the model development and internal validation sets, respectively.

Variables

Outcome variable: The study outcome is pain attributed to the hip surgery (VAS pain score ≥4)24 assessed at 3 months postoperatively. Symptom severity will be rated using the VAS, where 0 represents no pain, 1–3 represents mild pain (not affecting sleep), 4–6 represents moderate pain, 7–9 represents severe pain (cannot sleep or waking due to pain) and 10 represents excruciating pain. A research assistant will ask patients about their pain severity and mark the scale, or the patients will circle the number that best represents their pain level. The patients will be categorised into high-risk (moderate-to-severe pain) and low-risk groups. The variables will be assigned a score of 1 for patients with a VAS score ≥4 and 0 for those with a score <4.

Predictor variables: Based on an extensive literature review11,1619 and clinical expert opinions, the following potential predictor variables will be collected preoperatively and in the early postoperative period:

Preoperative variables: Demographics (age, sex, body mass index, smoking history, alcohol history), comorbidities and history (hypertension, diabetes, aetiology of hip joint injury, preoperative VAS pain score), emotional status (preoperative anxiety and depression status assessed using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS)(online supplemental Appendix 2).

Postoperative variables: Surgical and anaesthesia-related variables (type of surgery (total hip arthroplasty, hemiarthroplasty, hip resurfacing arthroplasty), incision length, duration of surgery, anaesthesia technique, American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status classification (I–III), duration of anaesthesia, intraoperative blood loss, time to first ambulation postoperatively, postoperative systolic and diastolic blood pressure, postoperative analgesia regimen), laboratory indicators (postoperative C-reactive protein concentration, white blood cell count, haemoglobin level, haematocrit level, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio, red blood cell distribution width, albumin concentration, fasting blood glucose level, alanine aminotransferase level, aspartate aminotransferase level and creatinine level).

Discharge/follow-up variables: VAS pain score at 3 months postoperatively.

Model development

Data preprocessing: The patterns of missing data will first be assessed. If data are missing at random and the proportion is <5%, multiple imputation will be used. If the missing proportion is >5% or data are not missing at random, the variable may be excluded from analysis or subjected to sensitivity analysis. Outlier handling: Outliers will be identified and addressed using appropriate methods (eg, based on percentiles or boxplot methods) according to the variable distribution. Standardisation or normalisation: Continuous variables will be standardised or normalised as required by the model before modelling.

Variable selection: In the development set (n=210), LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation will first be used for variable selection using the lambda.1se value to select the most predictive variables.

Model construction: Subsequently, the selected variables will be incorporated into a multivariate LR model to build the final prediction model.

Model performance evaluation and nomogram presentation

Discrimination: The ability of the model to distinguish between patients with and without CPSP will be assessed by calculating the C-statistic (area under the curve) and its 95% CI.

Calibration: Calibration will be assessed by plotting calibration curves and calculating the calibration-in-the-large intercept and slope, supplemented by the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, to evaluate the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes.

Internal validation: Internal validation will be performed using bootstrap resampling (1000 repetitions) on the development set to calculate the optimism-corrected C-statistics and other performance metrics.

Clinical utility: Decision curve analysis will be used to evaluate the net clinical benefit of the model across different probability thresholds.

Model presentation: The final prediction model will be visually presented as a nomogram.

Software and statistical analysis

All analyses will be performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, V.29.0, and R software (RStudio V.4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables will be described as mean±SD or median (IQR) based on their distribution. Categorical variables will be described as frequencies (percentages). Baseline characteristics will be compared between the development and internal validation sets using absolute standardised differences (ASD), with ASD >0.1 considered a meaningful difference.

Patient and public involvement

The patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this study.

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study, we will first screen for independent risk factors of CPSP after hip arthroplasty and subsequently use them to develop and validate a predictive nomogram based on LR. The influence of the identified independent risk factors on CPSP will also be elucidated. The validity of the model will be tested using internal validation. Based on these findings, the incidence of CPSP after hip arthroplasty, various influencing factors and comparisons with existing models will be discussed in detail. We will also conduct stratified analyses based on age, sex and type of arthroplasty (total hip replacement vs femoral head replacement) to verify the predictive performance of the nomogram across different patient subgroups.

Selection of key predictors and their expected roles

The selection of candidate predictors in this study is grounded in a comprehensive literature review and clinical expertise.11,1619 Previous studies have identified that demographic factors such as age and sex may influence pain perception and recovery trajectories. Preoperative pain intensity is closely associated with heightened central sensitisation and has been confirmed as a significant risk factor for persistent postsurgical pain. Emotional status, particularly anxiety and depression assessed by the HADS, plays an important role in the development of CPSP through its influence on pain modulation pathways and coping mechanisms. Surgical factors, including operative duration and anaesthesia technique, may affect the degree of tissue trauma and inflammatory responses, while postoperative laboratory indicators such as C-reactive protein and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio reflect systemic inflammatory status, which is involved in the pathophysiology of CPSP. By incorporating these clinically relevant variables, our model aims to capture the multifactorial nature of CPSP development.

Modelling approach: rationale and considerations

We chose LR as the primary modelling algorithm due to its high interpretability, which is crucial for clinical decision-making. LR provides explicit coefficients that translate into a nomogram, offering clinicians a transparent and intuitive tool for individualised risk assessment. To enhance model stability and prevent overfitting, we will employ LASSO regression for variable selection. LASSO effectively reduces dimensionality by shrinking less important coefficients to zero, thereby selecting the most parsimonious set of predictors. This approach improves model generalisability and mitigates the risk of multicollinearity.

While tree-based algorithms such as random forest and XGBoost may achieve superior predictive performance in certain contexts, they are often characterised as ‘black boxes’ with limited interpretability, which may hinder clinical adoption. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that exploring alternative algorithms could provide valuable insights into model performance. In future research, we plan to compare LR with ensemble learning methods to assess potential improvements in predictive accuracy and to evaluate the trade-off between interpretability and performance. The current study prioritises the development of a clinically accessible tool, with the nomogram serving as a practical interface that facilitates risk communication and individualised intervention.

Comparison with previous studies and study strengths

Although prediction models for CPSP after hip arthroplasty have been previously reported,16 their data were collected retrospectively from a limited number of patients. In contrast, our study is based on a prospective cohort design. Candidate predictors have been selected based on published data, expertise and clinical relevance to ensure the feasibility and utility of the final model, which aims to offer good clinical practicality. Furthermore, we will perform 10-fold cross-validation within the LASSO regression analysis, repeated 10 times, to select the optimal predictors based on the best lambda value.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its single-centre design necessitates internal validation to assess predictive performance, and the model’s generalisability requires external validation across diverse geographical regions and healthcare settings. Second, although a wide range of clinical variables are included, the model incorporates fewer biomarkers and does not consider genetic or more complex psychosocial variables, potentially limiting its comprehensive predictive capability. To strengthen the clinical applicability of our nomogram and the identified risk factors, future research should explore multiregional and multicentre collaborations to increase the sample size and validate our findings. Additionally, while we have prioritised interpretability using LR, future studies may extend our work by applying machine learning algorithms such as random forest or XGBoost to evaluate whether improved predictive performance can be achieved without compromising clinical utility.

Ethics and dissemination

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanping First Hospital on 25 December 2024 (Approval No: NPSY202412034) and is registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry on 6 August 2025. All participants will provide written informed consent before enrolment. All information collected during the study will be de-identified and kept strictly confidential. The results of this study will be published in a peer-reviewed journal. This manuscript constitutes the study protocol.
