Invasive pyogenic spondylitis with retropharyngeal and hepatic abscesses caused by hypermucoviscous klebsiella pneumoniae: A radiologic case report
Authors: Ikki Nokita, Yukihito Nakamura, Shuntaro Matsuda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHypermucoviscous Klebsiella pneumoniae (HmKP) is an emerging pathogen with strong invasive potential, particularly in immunocompromised patients. We present the case of an 84-year-old man with type 2 diabetes mellitus who developed systemic infection including liver abscess, retropharyngeal abscess, and cervical pyogenic spondylitis caused by HmKP. Despite clinical improvement with appropriate antibiotics, follow-up MRI demonstrated vertebral collapse. This case highlights the necessity of radiological follow-up in patients with HmKP infections.Keywords: Hypermucoviscous Klebsiella pneumoniae, Liver abscess, Retropharyngeal abscess, Pyogenic spondylitis, MRI

Introduction

In recent Japanese surveillance studies, HmKP has been increasingly isolated in healthcare-associated infections [1]. HmKP is a hypervirulent variant known to cause life-threatening infections, particularly in individuals with underlying conditions such as diabetes mellitus. It can lead to abscess formation in multiple organs and may present with subtle initial imaging findings. Recently, HmKP has gained global attention due to its capacity for causing community-acquired invasive infections, especially in East Asia [2]. The hypervirulence of HmKP is linked to genetic factors such as rmpA and iucA, which enhance capsule production and iron acquisition, respectively [3]. This pathotype differs from classical Klebsiella pneumoniae in both clinical behavior and molecular profile. While liver abscess is a well-known manifestation, simultaneous involvement of deep neck spaces and spine is rarely reported and highlights the organism’s capacity for hematogenous spread [4]. We herein describe a rare case of invasive HmKP infection involving the liver, retropharyngeal space, and cervical spine.

Case presentation

An 84-year-old man with type 2 diabetes mellitus, without history of biliary disease or relevant family history, presented with a 4-day history of fever and chills. Physical examination revealed a temperature of 39.2°C and left-sided cervical tenderness. Laboratory values included: leukocytes 13,200/µL (normal 3500–9000/µL), platelets 98,000/µL (normal 150,000–350,000/µL), C-reactive protein 18.4 mg/dL (normal <0.3 mg/dL), AST 45 U/L (normal 10–40 U/L), ALT 52 U/L (normal 7–56 U/L), ALP 298 U/L (normal 44–147 U/L), and total bilirubin 1.2 mg/dL (normal 0.3–1.2 mg/dL). Despite mild liver enzyme elevation, the patient did not report abdominal pain and had no history of biliary disease. Tazobactam-piperacillin was empirically initiated. Blood and urine cultures subsequently grew Klebsiella pneumoniae with a positive string test, confirming the hypermucoviscous phenotype. Two days into hospitalization, the patient developed worsening throat pain and spiking fever. Contrast-enhanced CT revealed a low-density lesion in the liver consistent with an abscess (Fig. 1A, white arrow) and a rim-enhancing retropharyngeal abscess (Figs. 1B and C, white arrows). MRI of the cervical spine (T2-weighted, sagittal plane) demonstrated hyperintensity and endplate irregularities at C3–C4 (Fig. 2A, white arrow), which were not apparent on initial imaging. Ceftriaxone was substituted for tazobactam-piperacillin following culture sensitivity results. The patient continued ceftriaxone for 1 month, during which the inflammatory response improved and the retropharyngeal abscess resolved. Subsequently, oral amoxicillin was continued according to the drug susceptibility results. Follow-up MRI performed 2 months later revealed vertebral collapse at C3–C4 and worsening intervertebral disc destruction (Fig. 2B, white arrow). Therefore, oral amoxicillin was continued for an additional 3 months to ensure that the condition did not worsen further.

(A) Cervical MRI (T2-weighted, sagittal) showing hyperintensity and endplate irregularities at C3–C4 (white arrow) and (B) Follow-up MRI (T2-weighted, sagittal) showing vertebral collapse and disc destruction at C3–C4 (white arrow).

Discussion

Hwang et al. have suggested that Klebsiella pneumoniae should be considered a causative agent in pyogenic spondylitis, particularly in patients with underlying diabetes or liver abscess [5]. HmKP differs from classical K. pneumoniae in clinical behavior, transmission, and management. Classical strains are mainly associated with nosocomial pneumonia or urinary tract infections, whereas HmKP frequently causes community-acquired invasive infections with hematogenous dissemination. Identifying HmKP has clinical implications: it predicts aggressive multiorgan spread, guides prolonged antibiotic therapy, and necessitates radiological follow-up. HmKP is associated with aggressive infections and multiple organ involvement. Its virulence is linked to increased capsular production and iron acquisition, facilitating abscess formation and immune evasion [2,3]. Although a positive string test is not definitive for genotypic hypervirulence, it correlates with clinically aggressive disease [4]. This case illustrates the importance of including HmKP in the differential diagnosis for pyogenic spondylitis with multiple abscesses, especially in diabetic patients. Imaging, particularly MRI, is essential not only for diagnosis but also for monitoring progression, as clinical symptoms may not reflect underlying bone destruction [5]. The pathogenesis of such disseminated infection likely involves hematogenous spread originating from the liver abscess, with seeding of distant sites such as the cervical spine and retropharyngeal space. Patients with diabetes mellitus are particularly susceptible to such dissemination due to impaired immune function [6]. Although spinal involvement has been sporadically reported in the context of HmKP infection, concurrent retropharyngeal abscess is exceedingly rare and can pose a risk of airway obstruction or descending mediastinitis [7,8]. This rarity underscores the novelty of our case and highlights the importance of vigilance for unusual sites of dissemination.Conclusion. Invasive HmKP infections can involve multiple organs simultaneously. Radiological monitoring is essential, as structural progression may occur despite clinical improvement. We recommend prolonged imaging follow-up in diabetic patients with HmKP infection, even after normalization of laboratory markers.

Conclusion

Invasive HmKP infections can cause severe multiorgan involvement, including pyogenic spondylitis. Radiological monitoring remains vital for guiding treatment and assessing structural outcomes. We recommend prolonged imaging follow-up in diabetic patients with HmKP infection, even after normalization of inflammatory markers.
