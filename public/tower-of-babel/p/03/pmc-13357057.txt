Angiotensin system inhibitors as adjunctive therapy in esophageal adenocarcinoma: hypothesis-generating insights from incidental use during trimodal treatment
Authors: Alberto Carmona-Bayonas, Paula Jimenez-Fonseca
Published: 2026

In this issue of The Journal of Thoracic and Cardiovascular Surgery, Geller and colleagues from the Massachusetts General Hospital Division of Thoracic Surgery present a thoughtful retrospective cohort study examining the incidental use of angiotensin system inhibitors (ASIs), specifically angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), during neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (CRT) for locally advanced esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) (1). Analyzing 375 patients treated with trimodal therapy between 2002 and 2017, the authors report that 25% of patients receiving concurrent ASIs during induction CRT experienced a meaningful improvement in both overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) compared with nonusers. Notably, this survival advantage persisted robustly after propensity score matching (PSM). In the matched cohort, median OS more than doubled from 26.2 to 58.8 months [hazard ratio (HR) 0.623, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.410–0.946], and DFS nearly doubled from 13.5 to 25.5 months (HR 0.651, 95% CI: 0.440–0.961). While no significant differences were observed regarding pathologic complete response (pCR) rates or tumor regression grade (TRG), the rate of disease recurrence was significantly lower among ASI users (34.4% vs. 49.4%). These findings contribute compellingly to the growing body of literature focused on drug repurposing in oncology and warrant careful, critical consideration.

The biological rationale for repurposing ASIs in oncology is increasingly compelling. Beyond its classical role in global cardiovascular and renal homeostasis, the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) operates locally within the tumor microenvironment (TME). The “classic” axis—angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE)/angiotensin II (Ang II)/angiotensin II type 1 receptor (AT1R)—promotes tumor progression through cellular proliferation, aberrant angiogenesis via vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) secretion, stromal desmoplasia through cancer-associated fibroblasts, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, and immunosuppression. In contrast, the “alternative” axis—angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2)/angiotensin-(1-7) [Ang-(1-7)]/Mas receptor (MasR)—exerts opposing, tumor-suppressive effects, acting as an anti-proliferative, pro-apoptotic, and anti-angiogenic force (2). Preclinical models support this paradigm, demonstrating that ASIs can reduce tumor solid stress and improve vascular perfusion, thereby undermining a critical mechanism of chemoresistance and enhancing the delivery of cytotoxic agents. Furthermore, RAAS inhibition can attenuate collagen deposition within the tumor stroma and potentiate the efficacy of immunotherapies. Losartan, for instance, has demonstrated profound stromal reprogramming effects in pancreatic cancer and blood-brain barrier modulation in glioblastoma, while broader meta-analyses suggest synergistic survival benefits when ASIs are combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors in urothelial and renal cell carcinomas (3-5).

This mechanistic promise, however, has long been overshadowed by epidemiological controversy. Early signals of harm, most notably Sipahi and colleagues’ 2010 meta-analysis of randomized trials linking ARBs to a modest increase in new cancer diagnoses, particularly lung cancer, triggered global regulatory scrutiny and intense debate (6). Subsequent large cohort studies, such as the 2018 UK analysis by Hicks et al., which associated long-term ACEI use with an elevated lung cancer risk (HR rising to 1.31 after ≥10 years), fueled concerns regarding potential bradykinin-mediated effects and detection bias stemming from ACEI-induced cough (7). Yet, rigorous methodological critiques have since reframed these associations. Immortal time bias, detection bias, and confounding by indication frequently distort observational data on chronic medications; when addressed through time-varying models or target-trial emulation frameworks, these apparent risks often attenuate or disappear. A recent 2025 multidatabase target-trial emulation study by Xie et al. (8), encompassing over 328,000 patients from the UK Biobank and China Renal Data System, has further refined this comparison. By leveraging a framework designed to minimize prevalent-user and immortal time biases, the authors found that ACE inhibitor initiation was associated with a significantly higher risk of 5-year all-cause mortality (HR 1.13; 95% CI: 1.07–1.19) and major adverse cardiovascular events compared with ARB initiation. These findings suggest that when pharmacological exposure is rigorously emulated, ARBs may offer a more favorable long-term survival profile, a distinction often obscured in standard observational cohorts.

Geller and colleagues’ work is a relevant addition to this evolving area precisely because it focuses on a homogeneous EAC population receiving standardized trimodal therapy (1). Noted strengths include its size, as the authors state it is the largest dedicated EAC cohort evaluating ASI use to date, the use of a prospectively collected institutional database combined with individual chart review, and the application of PSM to balance measured confounders such as age, performance status, and specific comorbidities. Notably, prior to matching, the prevalence of hypertension (95.7% vs. 30.4%) and diabetes mellitus (28.3% vs. 11.0%) was heavily skewed toward the ASI cohort. The observed survival advantage, driven largely by a reduced rate of recurrence, aligns with preclinical hypotheses of RAAS-mediated effects on micrometastatic control or TME remodeling rather than enhanced local radiosensitization. This is consistent with the authors’ finding of no significant differences in pCR or TRG between the treatment groups. The emphasis on DFS is particularly noteworthy; the lower recurrence rates among ASI users suggest a potential systemic rather than purely locoregional benefit, supporting the hypothesis that ASIs may disrupt metastatic niches or angiogenesis beyond the primary tumor bed.

Nevertheless, as with any retrospective analysis spanning a 15-year period (2002–2017), important methodological nuances warrant prudent interpretation (9). The study timeframe encompassed major shifts in the standard of care, notably the transition from cisplatin/5-fluorouracil to the carboplatin/paclitaxel (CROSS) regimen around 2012, alongside the widespread adoption of intensity-modulated radiotherapy. Concurrently, hypertension guidelines evolved to increasingly favor ASIs. Because PSM did not include treatment year or specific chemotherapy regimen as covariates, the observed survival signal might partially reflect temporal advancements in overall oncological care rather than a pure pharmacological effect.

Furthermore, the precise definition of pharmacological exposure remains vague. Critical details regarding ASI administration, such as specific drug dosages, duration of therapy prior to diagnosis, and strict medication adherence, are absent. This ambiguity leaves the analysis vulnerable to subtle forms of immortal time bias, which frequently distort registry-based medication studies even when exposure is seemingly defined at the start of induction. Additionally, the reliance on standard Kaplan-Meier and Cox proportional hazards models fails to account for competing risks. In a cohort where 95.7% of ASI users had hypertension and 28.3% had diabetes mellitus, non-cancer-related mortality is a substantial competing event that can significantly distort DFS and recurrence estimates. Finally, unmeasured confounding remains a formidable challenge; patients exhibiting this metabolic profile frequently receive concomitant medications, such as statins or metformin, which possess independent anticancer associations. Without explicit adjustment for concurrent polypharmacy, these effects cannot be fully disentangled.

While these limitations complicate the direct attribution of causality, they underscore the inherent complexity of evaluating real-world incidental drug exposure in cardio-oncology. Looking forward, this work highlights several promising lines of inquiry. Prospective randomized trials or studies utilizing rigorous target-trial emulation within large clinical registries, are necessary to test intentional ASI integration. Such studies should incorporate biomarker stratification, such as tumoral AT1R expression, alongside detailed monitoring of pharmacokinetics, dosing, and medication adherence. Furthermore, evaluating combination strategies with contemporary regimens, including the expanding role of immunotherapy in esophageal cancer, is particularly attractive given the emerging preclinical data on RAAS-immune synergy. Given their low cost, wide availability, and favorable safety profiles, ASIs remain appealing candidates for global drug repurposing efforts. However, their true oncological efficacy must be definitively isolated from the background noise of evolving multimodal care and cardiovascular polypharmacy.

In summary, Geller et al. present a hypothesis-generating analysis that advances the discussion on RAAS modulation in thoracic oncology. While their findings do not establish definitive causality or immediate practice-changing recommendations, they highlight a pharmacological avenue worthy of prospective validation. As the medical community continues to refine multimodal therapy for EAC, a disease that still carries a sobering prognosis with an estimated 5-year survival of less than 20%, drug repurposing efforts offer a practical, mechanism-informed strategy to potentially improve clinical outcomes. This retrospective work successfully lays the necessary groundwork for future, rigorously controlled investigations into this evolving subject.
