Effect of omega-3 fatty acids on adipose tissue: histological, metabolic, and gene expression analyses in mice fed a high-fat diet
Authors: Meryem Saban Güler, Hilal Yıldıran, Cemile Merve Seymen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Obesity is a metabolic disorder characterized by excessive fat accumulation due to energy imbalance. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of omega-3 fatty acids (n-3 FAs) on body weight, biochemical parameters, thermogenic gene expression, and adipose tissue morphology in a male C57BL/6 mice model of high-fat diet (HFD)-induced obesity initiated at 4 weeks of age. After a one-week adaptation, mice were randomly divided into four groups: a control group fed a standard diet (CD) and three experimental groups fed a HFD alone or enriched with 1.2% or 2.4% n-3 FAs. All groups were fed ad libitum for 12 weeks. Feed intake and body weight were recorded, and glucose and insulin tolerance tests were performed. Serum glucose, lipid profiles, insulin, leptin, and adiponectin levels were analyzed. Gene expression of uncoupling protein 1 (Ucp1), peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator-1 alpha (Pgc-1α), PR domain containing 16 (Prdm16), and G protein-coupled receptor 120 (Gpr120) was evaluated in brown adipose tissue (BAT), along with histological assessments of both BAT and white adipose tissue (WAT). n-3 FAs improved serum lipid profiles and glucose metabolism without significant changes in body weight or energy intake. Although no significant differences were observed in the expression of Ucp1, Pgc-1α, and Gpr120, Prdm16 expression was significantly higher in the 1.2% HFD group (p < 0.05). Histological analysis showed that n-3 FAs prevented BAT whitening, promoted WAT browning, and reduced inflammatory infiltration. Ucp1 immunoreactivity was significantly lower in the HFD group compared to the CD group (p < 0.05). These findings suggest that n-3 FAs exerts multifaceted protective effects on adipose tissue at genetic, biochemical, and histological levels, highlighting their potential in the management of diet-induced obesity.

Introduction

Obesity is a major public health concern worldwide due to its association with non-communicable diseases such as cardiovascular disorders, type 2 diabetes, and certain cancers1. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) data, in 2022, 2.5 billion adults aged 18 years and older were classified as overweight, and 890 million were reported as obese2. If current trends continue, it is predicted that by 2035, more than 4 billion people, approximately 51% of the global population, will be overweight or obese3.

Early-life nutritional status is crucial in determining obesity risk later in life. A meta-analysis reported that each + 1 unit increase in weight standard deviation (SD) scores during the first year of life is associated with a twofold increased risk of childhood obesity and a 23% increased risk of adult obesity4. Therefore, early preventive and therapeutic strategies are essential to mitigate long-term metabolic consequences5.

Adipose tissue is recognized as an endocrine organ involved in energy storage, immune function, and thermoregulation, making it a central target in obesity-related research6,7. In mammals, two main types of adipose tissue exist: white adipose tissue (WAT), which stores triglycerides, and brown adipose tissue (BAT), which dissipates energy through thermogenesis. Under certain stimuli, such as cold exposure or β-adrenergic activation, thermogenic “beige” adipocytes can also emerge within WAT, contributing to increased energy expenditure8,9.

Although diet plays a key role in the pathogenesis of obesity, the long-term success rates of dietary interventions targeting caloric restriction and increased energy expenditure tend to remain low, partly due to challenges with long-term adherence and sustainability, which has prompted the exploration of alternative strategies10–12. One promising approach is stimulating energy expenditure by enhancing BAT activity and promoting WAT browning13. Specific dietary components, particularly n-3 polyunsaturated FAs (n-3 PUFAs), have gained attention for their potential to modulate adipose tissue metabolism, reduce inflammation, and improve thermogenic capacity14–19. Although n-3 FAs are recognized for their anti-inflammatory and lipid-regulating properties, a better understanding of their mechanisms in promoting WAT browning and BAT activation and determining the ideal dosage and intervention duration is needed20.

Notably, studies indicate that the doses of n-3 FAs used in animal experiments are often much higher than human dietary requirements, making direct translation challenging21–23. Additionally, most experimental rodents are developmentally equivalent to adolescent or adult humans24,25. In the present study, dietary intervention was initiated immediately after weaning to assess the early impact of n-3 FAs during the transition from post-weaning to mature adulthood. This study investigated whether dietary n-3 FAs could mitigate obesity and associated metabolic disorders induced by a HFD initiated immediately after transitioning from breastfeeding by promoting WAT browning and enhancing BAT thermogenesis. Although n-3 FAs have been extensively investigated for their health-promoting effects, there is still a lack of standardized experimental protocols or guidelines that facilitate the translation of preclinical findings into human applications26. Since the lipid content in rodent diets reflects the percentage of energy distribution in human diets and can generate outcomes more comparable to those observed in humans, a standardized approach to n-3 FAs dosage calculation was adopted26. The study aimed to evaluate the effects of dietary n-3 FAs on body weight, some biochemical parameters, and thermogenic and histological characteristics of adipose tissue in rats fed a HFD after weaning. Furthermore, the findings obtained aim to contribute to developing recommendations regarding the efficacy of n-3 FAs in the prevention and treatment of obesity and related diseases.

Methods

This research was approved by Gazi University’s Animal Experiments Local Ethics Committee as a research project with registration number G.Ü.ET-22.031 on 24 February 2022, and all stages of the study were conducted in compliance with the committee’s principles and are reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Study design

This study was conducted between February and June 2023 at the Gazi University Laboratory Animal Breeding and Experimental Research Center (GUDAM) using 25 male C57BL/6 mice aged 3 weeks, sourced from multiple litters. All procedures, including care, dietary interventions, blood collection under anesthesia, and euthanasia, were performed at GUDAM. Throughout the 12-week experiment, the mice were housed in a controlled environment with a room temperature of 24 ± 2 °C, relative humidity of 35–40%, cage light intensity of 400 lx, a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle, noise levels below 85 dB, and a ventilation system providing 10–16 air exchanges per hour. After a one-week acclimation period, at 4 weeks of age, the mice were randomly assigned to four groups. During randomization, pups originating from the same litter were identified and appropriately distributed across different experimental groups. Additionally, baseline body weights were matched to ensure similar starting conditions before dietary interventions were initiated.

All groups were provided with feed and water ad libitum. The types of HFDs consumed by the mice according to their groups are shown in Table 1. The standard chow diet used in this study was a commercially available maintenance diet. The primary fat source was fractionated bypass fat, which is commonly used in rodent feed to provide a stable and digestible lipid supply. CD provided 24.6% protein, 7.7% fat, and 67.7% carbohydrates. In contrast, HFDs delivered 20% protein, 45% fat, and 35% carbohydrates. The n-3 PUFAs were supplied from menhaden oil, containing a mixture of long-chain n-3 FAs. In the 1.2% HFD, EPA and DHA provided 0.52% and 0.37% of total energy, while in the 2.4% HFD, they accounted for 1.05% and 0.76%, with the remainder from other n-3 FAs in menhaden oil.

The feed consumption of the mice was monitored daily, and their body weights were measured weekly at the same time using a precision scale (PRECISA BJ2100D). To evaluate the relationship between feed intake and body weight gain in mice, feed efficiency ratios were calculated by dividing the daily average body weight gain by the daily average feed intake27. At the end of the 12-week dietary intervention, the mice were euthanized by intracardiac blood collection under deep anesthesia, induced by the intramuscular administration of ketamine (45 mg/kg) and xylazine (5 mg/kg). This anesthetic combination is widely used in rodent studies and is known to induce hyperglycemia by suppressing insulin secretion28. It also increases circulating free fatty acid levels, likely through enhanced sympathetic activation.

Calculation of n-3 FAs

According to the Dietary Reference Intakes (DRI), an intake of 0.6–1.2% of total energy as n-3 PUFAs (α-linolenic acid (ALA)) is recommended from one year of age through adulthood29. In comparative studies, providing 1.2% of dietary energy from ALA better mirrored human eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) responses26. Additionally, supplementation with 1.2% (low dose) and 2.4% (high dose) fish oil per 100 g of diet was effective for brown adipocyte activation in BAT and browning in WAT in rodents30. Based on these data, the present study was designed to supply 1.2% of dietary energy from n-3 FAs, with an additional investigation of the effects of a higher 2.4% dose.

Adipose tissue collection and analysis

Representative adipose depots were collected, including epididymal, retroperitoneal, and inguinal WAT, and interscapular BAT (iBAT). Dissections were performed based on anatomical landmarks following euthanasia. H&E staining and immunohistochemistry analyses were performed on both iBAT and inguinal WAT, while gene expression analyses were conducted only in iBAT.

Analysis of lee’s index and adiposity index in mice

The body weights of the mice were measured prior to euthanasia. Immediately after euthanasia, body length (the distance from the nose to the anus) was measured using a non-flexible measuring tape31.

Biochemical analyses

Blood samples were collected into dry vacuum tubes containing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 15 min at + 4 °C using a Selecta Centronic—BL centrifuge to separate serum and plasma. The obtained serum samples were transferred into 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes and stored at − 80 °C until further biochemical analysis.

Serum glucose, triglyceride, total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, and LDL cholesterol levels were measured using a Mindray-BS400 automated biochemistry analyzer. Serum insulin, adiponectin, and leptin levels were assessed using sandwich enzyme immunoassay ELISA kits (Biossays Technology Laboratory, Shanghai, China). All biochemical analyses were performed using appropriate standard methodologies.

Glucose and insulin tolerance tests

The mice were fasted for 6 h (between 8:00 AM and 2:00 PM) before the metabolic tolerance tests, with free access to water.

Glucose tolerance test (GTT)

In the eighth week, glucose tolerance test (GTT) was performed by intraperitoneal glucose injection (1.5 g/kg body weight)33. Before the test, the mice were weighed, and the appropriate glucose solutions were prepared based on their body weights and transferred into Eppendorf tubes labeled with the corresponding mouse numbers. Before glucose administration, a small incision was made on the tail using a scalpel to measure baseline blood glucose levels with a glucometer (Lifechek Compact). Blood glucose levels were measured 30, 60, 90, and 120 min post-injection.

Insulin tolerance test (ITT)

In the 11th week, insulin tolerance testing (ITT) was performed by intraperitoneal injection of insulin (Humulin R) at a dose of 0.75 U/kg body weight. According to the literature, this dose is sufficient to distinguish between insulin-sensitive and insulin-resistant animals while minimizing the risk of hypoglycemia34. A 10% glucose solution was also prepared for emergency use in case of severe hypoglycemia symptoms. Before the test, the mice were weighed, and the appropriate insulin volumes were calculated based on their body weights and transferred into Eppendorf tubes labeled with individual mouse numbers. Baseline blood glucose levels were measured by making a small tail incision with a scalpel before insulin injection. Blood glucose levels were then measured 30, 60, 90, and 120 min post-injection.

For both GTT and ITT, glucose concentrations were plotted over time, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated individually for each mouse using the trapezoidal method.

Gene expression analysis in BAT

Ucp1, Pgc-1α, Prdm16, and Gpr120 gene expression analyses were performed on interscapular BAT samples. The BAT samples were collected into Eppendorf tubes, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and transported under cold chain conditions. Total RNA was extracted from 25 mouse BAT samples using the TRIzol reagent, following phase separation and precipitation protocols. RNA concentrations were measured spectrophotometrically and normalized to ensure consistency across samples, particularly for downstream qPCR analysis.

For cDNA synthesis, reverse transcription was performed using Oligo(dT) and Random Hexamer primers to capture pre-mRNA and mature mRNA transcripts. The resulting cDNA was used as a template for qPCR.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) analyses were conducted to determine relative gene expression levels using the BrightGreen 2X qPCR MasterMix-No ROX (ABM, Canada) on a LightCycler 480 system (Roche, Switzerland). Each gene’s amplification was optimized according to its specific annealing temperature (Tm). Fluorescence signals were monitored over 40 cycles, and Ct values were used to calculate relative expression levels using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method.

To correct for skewed data distributions, normalized gene expression values were log-transformed prior to statistical analysis, minimizing the impact of extreme values and improving the robustness of the results. β-actin was used as the housekeeping gene for normalization. Histological and immunohistochemical analyses

WAT samples from the inguinal region and BAT samples from the interscapular region were first fixed in 10% formaldehyde solution, then washed under running water for 24 h. Dehydration was performed through a graded alcohol series (70%, 80%, 90%, and 100%), followed by clearing in xylene and embedding in paraffin.

Sections of 4–5 μm thickness were obtained from the paraffin blocks and stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) histochemical evaluation and immunohistochemically stained using a primary antibody against Ucp1. The sections were examined and imaged using a Leica DCM 4000 (Germany) imaging system and LAS software.

H&E staining

Paraffin sections were incubated at 60 °C for 30 min, deparaffinized in xylene (3 × 5 min), rehydrated through a graded alcohol series (100%, 96%, 80%, 70%, 50%), and air-dried. After washing in running water for 10 min, sections were stained with Harris hematoxylin for 10–15 min, rewashed, differentiated briefly in 70% alcohol with 2–3 drops of glacial acetic acid, and rinsed. After 5–10 min of eosin staining, sections were dehydrated through graded alcohols, cleared in xylene (2 × 15 min), and mounted with Entellan. A degeneration scoring system was established based on H&E staining, rated numerically as 0 = None, 1 = Mild, 2 = Moderate, and 3 = Strong.

Immunohistochemical staining

Ucp1 immunostaining was performed on BAT and WAT sections to assess mitochondrial inner membrane Ucp1 protein expression, which is crucial for BAT thermogenic activity. Sections placed on poly-L-lysine-coated slides were incubated over-night at 37 °C and further processed at 57 °C for 1 h to facilitate deparaffinization. Slides were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded alcohols, and washed in distilled water.

Antigen retrieval was performed using 1 M citrate buffer (pH 6.0) in a microwave for 5 min × 3 cycles, then cooling at room temperature. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked using 3% hydrogen peroxide (Lot: O2Q46013, Thermo). After blocking nonspecific binding with Ultra V Block (Lot: PHLT811, Thermo), slides were incubated with the Ucp1 primary antibody (Bioss, bs-1925R) (1:300 dilution with Phosphate Buffered Saline [PBS]) without intermediate washing. Following PBS washes, slides were incubated with a biotinylated secondary antibody and streptavidin-peroxidase complex (Lot: PHLT811, Thermo), and visualized using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen (Lot: HD53495, Thermo). Background staining was performed with Harris hematoxylin, and slides were mounted with Entellan. Prepared slides were evaluated and imaged using a Leica DCM 4000 imaging system with LAS software.

Ucp1 immunostaining intensity was evaluated semi-quantitatively across groups using an H-score analysis. Immunostaining intensity was scored as follows: 0 = None, 1 = Weak, 2 = Weak to Moderate, 3 = Moderate, 4 = Moderate to Strong, and 5 = Strong. Two independent observers, blind to the treatment protocol, evaluated the immunostaining scores separately. The H-score was calculated using the formula: H-Score = ΣPi (i + 1), where i represents the staining intensity (0–5) and Pi represents the percentage of stained cells35.

Histological and immunohistochemical analyses were conducted by obtaining 10 serial sections per animal, with every fifth section collected throughout the tissue depth to ensure comprehensive sampling. In each selected section, one central and five peripheral microscopic fields were evaluated. Quantification of Ucp1 immunoreactivity was performed using the H-score method by two independent, blinded investigators to minimize observer bias.

Statistical analyses

The data obtained from the study were analyzed and visualized using SPSS 23.0 (Statistical Package for Social Sciences, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The normality of the data distribution was assessed using analytical methods. Descriptive statistics were calculated, including mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum values. One-way ANOVA was used to evaluate quantitative variables among four independent groups with normal distribution. In the case of a significant ANOVA result, post hoc tests were performed. Levene’s test was applied to assess the homogeneity of variances; if variances were equal, the Tukey test was used, whereas if variances were unequal, Tamhane’s T2 test was applied. In addition, to specifically assess the effect of n-3 FAs intake within the HFD background, planned contrast analyses were conducted to compare the HFD group with the 1.2 and 2.4 HFD groups. The CD group was included as a contextual reference, and comparisons between CD and each HFD-based group were also conducted separately. For quantitative variables that did not follow a normal distribution, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for group comparisons. In cases of significant results from the Kruskal-Wallis test, pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test, with Bonferroni correction applied. For repeated measures analysis, two-way ANOVA was used for parametric data, while the Friedman test was applied for non-parametric data. Post hoc comparisons were performed in the Friedman test using the Wilcoxon test with Bonferroni correction. For Ucp1 immunohistochemical staining, differences in H-scores among groups were evaluated using ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Statistical significance was considered at p < 0.05 within a 95% confidence interval.

Results

Feed, energy, and nutrient intake across groups

Feed, energy, and macronutrient intake were analyzed across the groups, as shown in Table 3. Daily average feed and energy intake were significantly higher in the CD group than in all HFD groups, as determined by one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s post hoc test (p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed among the HFD groups based on contrast tests. Feed efficiency ratios were significantly lower in the CD group compared to all HFD groups, as determined by the Kruskal–Wallis test; pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test (p = 0.002), with no significant differences observed among the HFD groups. Macronutrient intake also differed among groups. According to the results of one-way ANOVA followed by Tamhane’s T2 test, daily fat intake was significantly lower in the CD group compared to the HFD groups (p < 0.001). Although daily protein and carbohydrate intake were similar among the HFD groups based on contrast tests, they were significantly higher in the CD group, as determined by one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s post hoc test (p < 0.001).

Histological analysis of WAT and BAT

H&E staining findings of WAT

The observations from H&E staining of WAT samples from mice in the CD group are shown in Fig. 4. Histological evaluation of WAT revealed marked structural degeneration in the HFD group, while high-dose n-3 FAs intake alleviated these alterations and promoted the appearance of beige adipocytes. According to the literature analysis, degenerations commonly reported in adipose tissue studies were identified (see the degeneration criteria table), and all groups and subjects were evaluated based on whether or not they exhibited these degenerative changes. In the low- and high-magnification analyses of white fat pads obtained from the inguinal region of the CD group, the adipocytes were distinguished by their regular contours, uniform size, unilocular lipid components, flattened nuclei located at the periphery of the cells, and thin connective tissue elements containing blood vessels located between the adipocytes. The adipocytes displayed a normal histological appearance. No degradation was observed in the lipid components of the adipocytes, and they exhibited a homogeneous appearance (Fig. 4 (a)).

In the HFD group, low-magnification analyses revealed dilatation and congestion in the blood vessels located within the connective tissue between adipocytes. Increased connective tissue density and prominent infiltration were observed in this area. Some adipocytes showed increased volume and fusion, with a heterogeneous appearance due to lipid content disruption and evidence of myelination. Degenerated adipocytes were also distinctly observed (Fig. 4 (b)). High-magnification analyses of the HFD group revealed continued infiltration between adipocytes. In certain areas, adipocytes showed increased volume and fusion, myelination due to disruption in their unilocular lipid content, and contour irregularities. Some adipocytes exhibited an undulated appearance. Nuclear weakening was identified in many adipocytes (Fig. 4 (c)).

In the 1.2 HFD group, both low- and high-magnification analyses revealed that the connective tissue density between adipocytes had returned to normal, and the number of dilated and congested blood vessels had relatively decreased. The most notable characteristic of this group was the absence of adipocytes showing myelination or a heterogeneous appearance. However, it was observed that fused adipocytes were still present, and nuclear weakening was still evident in some adipocytes. The overall appearance demonstrated a tissue structure closer to normal than the HFD group (Fig. 4 (d)).

In the 2.4 HFD group, low-magnification analyses revealed that dilated blood vessels and adipocytes showing nuclear weakening were no longer observed, and the connective tissue between adipocytes had returned to its normal thickness and structure. It was noted that myelination and the heterogeneous structure caused by disrupted lipid content were absent in the adipocytes of this group. Adipocytes were generally observed with regular contours and uniform sizes. Compared to the 1.2 HFD group, the occurrence of fused adipocytes was significantly lower, although fusion was still observed in a few adipocytes (Fig. 4 (e)). The most striking finding in this group was the presence of “Brite (Brown-in-White)” or “Beige” adipocytes, which are BAT-like cells appearing among WAT. These cells were identified in the inguinal white fat pads obtained from the periepididymal region. Since these types of adipocytes are known to have glucose-regulating and anti-obesity effects, this finding suggests that high-dose n-3 FAs under HFD conditions may exert a protective effect by promoting the increase of the “Beige/Brite” adipocyte component. High-magnification analyses of this group showed that adipocytes had regular contours, homogeneous unilocular lipid components, and thin connective tissue elements, indicating a standard structure (Fig. 4 (f)).

In the HFD group, low-magnification analyses revealed that the fat tissue lobules were separated due to an increase in connective tissue elements located between the fat tissue. Dilated and congested blood vessels in this area were also identified. The most notable finding at this magnification, differing from the observations in the CD group, was the relative increase in unilocular WAT concurrent with a decrease in multivacuolar BAT (Fig. 5 (b)). High-magnification analyses of this group identified a wide presence of “whitened BAT,” a phenomenon described in the literature as “Whitening of BAT in HFD,” which had also been observed in low-magnification analyses. Alongside the relative reduction in brown adipocytes, a decrease in BAT activity was noted, attributed to metabolic dysfunction induced by the HFD. In areas with reduced BAT activity, enlarged lipid droplets were observed, indicating “whitening” of the tissue. Fusion and degeneration were distinctly evident in these “whitened” adipocytes (Fig. 5 (c)).

Low- and high-magnification analyses in the 1.2 HFD group revealed that the lobular structure, previously observed as separate due to increased connective tissue density, had disappeared, returning to a regular pattern. However, dilated and congested blood vessels were still observed in these areas. The number of “whitened” adipocytes was relatively reduced compared to the HFD group, and brown adipocytes had become more predominant. In areas where “whitened” adipocytes were present, fusion was no longer observed (Fig. 5 (d)).

In the 2.4 HFD group, low-magnification analyses revealed that the overall appearance of the tissue had returned to normal, with brown adipocytes becoming predominant and the volume of dilated blood vessels decreasing (Fig. 5 (e)). High-magnification analyses showed that compared to other groups except for the CD group, the number of “whitened” adipocytes was significantly reduced, and these adipocytes did not exhibit a degenerated appearance.

Immunohistochemistry staining findings of BAT and WAT

Immunostaining was performed on brown and white fat pads from all experimental groups using the Ucp1 primary antibody, which is localized in the inner mitochondrial membrane, facilitating ATP production and enabling the thermogenic activity of BAT. Immunostaining of BAT and WAT revealed that Ucp1 expression, a marker of thermogenic activity, was significantly reduced in the HFD group but partially restored with n-3 FAs intake, particularly at higher doses.

In the multivacuolar brown fat pads of the CD group, Ucp1 immunostaining revealed intense immunoreactivity at the cytoplasmic level in all adipocytes. Additionally, positive immunostaining was observed in the nuclei of some adipocytes, corresponding to the intensity of the immunoreactivity (Fig. 6 (a)).

In the BAT of the HFD group, a decrease in Ucp1 immunoreactivity was observed, consistent with histochemical findings, due to the increase in “whitened” adipocytes. The immunoreactivity, entirely at the cytoplasmic level, ranged from weak to moderate intensity in brown adipocytes. In contrast, in white adipocytes, it was weak and localized to membrane-proximal areas of the cytoplasm near the nuclei, owing to their unilocular characteristics (Fig. 6 (b)).

In the BAT of the 1.2 HFD group, Ucp1 immunostaining analysis revealed moderate cytoplasmic immunoreactivity in a greater number of brown adipocytes compared to the HFD group, corresponding to the relative reduction in “whitened” adipocytes. In this group, immunoreactivity in “whitened” adipocytes ranged from weak to moderate intensity (Fig. 6 (c)).

In the BAT of the 2.4 HFD group, Ucp1 immunostaining analysis revealed moderate to strong cytoplasmic immunoreactivity in both the brown adipocytes, which constituted the majority of the tissue, and the relatively few “whitened” adipocytes (Fig. 6 (d)).

The increased Ucp1 immunoreactivity observed in the 2.4 HFD group suggests enhanced thermogenic capacity in response to higher-dose n-3 FAs intake. This dose-dependent improvement aligns with the quantified immunostaining scores and supports the protective role of n-3 FAs in maintaining BAT function. The relatively lower Ucp1 levels in the HFD group indicate impaired thermogenesis, while partial restoration in the 1.2 HFD group reflects a moderate effect. These findings reinforce the dose-sensitive impact of n-3 FAs on brown adipose tissue activity.

Based on the statistical analyses of H-score data, Ucp1 immunoreactivity in the CD group’s BAT was significantly higher than in the other groups. In contrast, immunoreactivity in the HFD group was significantly reduced compared to the CD group (p < 0.05). Ucp1 immunoreactivity in the 2.4 HFD group was significantly increased compared to the HFD group (p < 0.05); however, the increase in immunoreactivity in the 1.2 HFD group compared to the HFD group was not statistically significant (Fig. 8). These findings suggest that high-dose n-3 FAs regulate the reduced thermogenic activity in BAT induced by metabolic dysfunction associated with obesity caused by a HFD.

Discussion

Increasing evidence highlights the importance of early-life nutritional interventions in modulating long-term obesity risk by influencing adipose tissue development and metabolic programming36–38. Early overnutrition, particularly during lactation and weaning, has been linked to persistent metabolic disturbances, emphasizing the critical for preventive strategies39,40. n-3 PUFAs are potential therapeutic agents for targeting adipose tissue dysfunction because they promote WAT browning, stimulate BAT thermogenesis, and modulate key metabolic pathways23,41–44. In this study, an HFD-induced obesity model, commonly used in experimental research for its ability to closely mimic human obesity-associated metabolic changes, was utilized45,46. Understanding how such dietary interventions affect adipose tissue remodeling, including thermogenic activation, is crucial for elucidating their role in obesity development47. While previous studies indicate that n-3 FAs supplementation in HFD-fed rodents generally does not significantly alter feed intake48–51, some report variable outcomes depending on strain, sex, or other subgroup factors52–54. The current study supports the view that HFDs tend to reduce feed intake due to their higher energy density and satiety-inducing effects48,55. Despite reduced intake, increased feed efficiency observed in HFD-fed animals reflects the greater caloric density and metabolic efficiency of dietary fat, highlighting the role of macronutrient quality in energy balance and fat accumulation31,56. These results highlight the importance of considering energy intake and nutrient composition in obesity development.

Regarding body weight gain, although no statistically significant differences were observed among the groups, the increased WAT mass in the 1.2 HFD group may indicate a potential effect of n-3 FAs intake on adipose tissue distribution. This finding aligns with previous studies indicating that improvements in body composition can occur independently of total body weight reduction50,57. Indeed, n-3 FAs have been associated with enhanced lipid oxidation, reduced adipocyte hypertrophy, and promotion of thermogenic activity in adipose tissue, particularly in metabolically active fat depots26,58. In this study, the lack of significant differences in body weight may be explained by the intervention duration and the modest degree of adipose tissue change, which may not have been sufficient to alter overall body mass. It is also possible that compensatory mechanisms such as changes in lean mass, fluid balance, or energy expenditure may have masked potential differences.

Overall, these findings emphasize that evaluating the effects of n-3 FAs on obesity should consider not only feed intake and body weight but also energy metabolism, fat distribution, and diet composition. Although n-3 FAs show promise in reducing obesity-related metabolic risks, dose, duration, and fatty acid composition remain critical variables43,59. WAT is the primary site for storing triglyceride and an important endocrine organ secreting adipokines such as leptin and adiponectin, thereby regulating lipid and glucose metabolism60. In various preclinical studies, n-3 FAs are known to modulate these metabolic processes, improving lipid profiles by lowering total cholesterol, triglycerides, and LDL cholesterol levels48,52,54,61. Multiple studies have demonstrated that dietary supplementation with EPA and DHA reduces serum lipids regardless of their specific ratio or dose48,52,54,62 likely through enhanced hepatic lipid oxidation and suppression of lipogenic gene expression57. Although the present study did not identify statistically significant differences in all lipid parameters following n-3 FAs supplementation, a trend toward favorable lipid regulation was noted. This suggests that n-3 FAs may exert physiological effects even when statistical thresholds are not met, especially in complex metabolic models such as HFD–induced obesity. The variability in responsiveness may depend on critical factors including the n-6/n-3 ratio, intervention dose and duration, and the background diet composition57,63,64.

In maintaining metabolic integrity, glucose regulation is as critical as lipid control. Although adipose tissue is not the primary site for insulin-dependent glucose uptake, it plays a key role in systemic insulin resistance65. Studies have shown that n-3 FAs supplementation in HFD-fed models improves fasting glucose and insulin levels46,61,63,66, likely through modulation of insulin signaling pathways67. Furthermore, EPA-enriched diets reduced diet-induced obesity and insulin resistance without affecting energy intake67, and interventions supplying 3.4% or 12.9% of energy from EPA + DHA significantly improved glucose and insulin levels52,65. These findings were supported by GTT and ITT improvements54,66,68. Nevertheless, results across studies remain inconsistent, with some reporting no significant improvement in glycemic markers, suggesting that efficacy may depend on intervention timing, duration, fat composition, or animal age57,62. In the present study, although statistically significant improvements in insulin sensitivity markers were not observed, minor favorable trends in glucose tolerance and insulin responsiveness were noted, particularly in the 2.4 HFD group. While these findings do not confirm a definitive metabolic benefit, they may indicate a modest physiological effect of n-3 FAs on glucose homeostasis under HFD conditions.

This study evaluated the expression levels of key genes involved in thermogenesis and metabolism in BAT. These genes regulate thermogenesis, mitochondrial biogenesis, adipocyte differentiation, and inflammation46,50,58. BAT is a significant target in obesity management because it enhances energy expenditure23. UCP1, located in the mitochondrial inner membrane, is critical for non-shivering thermogenesis. Studies have shown that n-3 FAs can upregulate Ucp1 expression, potentially through TRPV1 activation and β3-adrenergic signaling, thus promoting thermogenesis in brown and beige adipocytes23,57 However, in the present study, Ucp1 expression did not differ significantly among the groups. Similarly, although Pgc-1α, a master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis, has previously been reported to increase following n-3 FAs44,69, no statistically significant differences were observed in this study. Nonetheless, Pgc-1α expression levels appeared to be numerically higher in the n-3 FAs groups compared to the HFD group, which may indicate a modest modulatory influence. Prdm16, crucial for brown adipocyte differentiation, showed significantly higher expression in n-3 FAs groups, with the highest levels in the 1.2 HFD group. This supports previous findings that n-3 FAs can enhance brown adipocyte identity58,62. Gpr120 is a G protein–coupled receptor activated by long-chain fatty acids, including n-3 FAs. Its role in adipose tissue has been well-documented, particularly in mediating anti-inflammatory responses, enhancing insulin sensitivity, and promoting thermogenic activity in BAT54. Activation of Gpr120 leads to the upregulation of thermogenesis-related genes in BAT24. Given this functional relevance, we included Gpr120 in our analysis to evaluate whether the metabolic effects observed following n-3 FAs intake were associated with changes in Gpr120 expression. Although no significant differences were detected, numerically higher levels in n-3 FAs groups may indicate functional modulation consistent with prior studies53,70. In addition to gene expression analyses, histological evaluations contributed to elucidating the tissue-specific effects of n-3 FAs and provided supporting morphological evidence. In BAT, the typical multilocular adipocyte morphology was preserved in the n-3 FAs groups, particularly in the 2.4 HFD group, suggesting a protective effect against HFD-induced whitening. In WAT, an increase in multilocular-like cells and a reduction in adipocyte hypertrophy were observed in the n-3 FAs groups compared to HFD alone. n-3 FAs have been shown to reduce adipocyte hypertrophy and limit adipose tissue expansion48,71. Adipose tissue morphology appeared better preserved in the n-3 FAs groups, particularly at the higher dose (2.4 HFD), with smaller and more uniform adipocytes compared to the HFD group. This morphological improvement was accompanied by reduced signs of tissue disorganization and cellular infiltration. Given the established link between adipose tissue hypertrophy and chronic low-grade inflammation, these histological findings may reflect anti-inflammatory properties of n-3 FAs60,72. Furthermore, the reduced whitening in BAT and the presence of multilocular adipocytes in the 2.4 HFD group are suggestive of potential browning activity, even though this was not statistically confirmed by gene expression data. These results collectively imply that n-3 FAs may help preserve adipose tissue structure and support thermogenic function, although further studies are needed to validate these effects at the molecular level.

Although this study found no consistent statistical differences in Ucp1 expression among groups, immunohistochemical analyses indicated increased Ucp1 immunoreactivity in the CD and 2.4 HFD groups compared to HFD alone, suggesting a potential dose-related modulation by n-3 FAs. This finding aligns with previous evidence showing that n-3 FAs, particularly EPA, enhance mitochondrial biogenesis and thermogenesis in adipocytes73. EPA is considered to exert more pronounced thermogenic effects than DHA, and while combined EPA + DHA supplementation may offer synergistic advantages, EPA-rich interventions may be particularly effective21,44. The EPA-focused composition of the diet in this study may therefore partially explain the observed improvements in BAT morphology and Ucp1 activity.

Limitations and future directions

This study provides important insights into the metabolic and histological effects of dietary n-3 FAs in a HFD-induced obesity model; however, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the exclusive use of male mice limits the generalizability of the findings, as differences in adipose tissue distribution, thermogenic activity, and hormonal regulation between sexes may influence metabolic outcomes. Future research should include both sexes to evaluate sex-specific responses to n-3 FAs interventions. Second, the use of a non-purified standard chow diet for the control group represents a methodological limitation. While such diets are commonly used in rodent facilities, their complex and variable composition makes it difficult to directly compare them with the purified HFDs used in experimental groups. For more robust conclusions, future studies should employ purified, compositionally matched control and HFD formulations to isolate the effects of dietary fat quality and n-3 FAs intake.

Although gene expression analysis suggested a trend toward increased thermogenic activation, these findings were not consistently supported by statistically significant changes across all genes. Moreover, the study lacks protein-level validation (e.g., UCP1 via Western blot) and direct assessments of energy expenditure such as oxygen consumption or indirect calorimetry, which would provide stronger functional evidence for thermogenic enhancement. These limitations restrict the interpretation of transcriptional data and underscore the need for integrative approaches combining gene, protein, and functional assessments in future research.

Additionally, histological evidence indicated browning in adipose tissue; however, molecular assessments were limited to BAT. Future research should extend gene and protein expression analyses to inguinal WAT, particularly for markers such as Cidea and Elovl3, to better characterize the browning process. Similarly, evaluating genes related to lipogenesis and lipolysis may offer deeper mechanistic insights into the metabolic remodeling induced by n-3 FAs.

Another important consideration is the n-6/n-3 fatty acid ratio of the diets. In this study, safflower oil, naturally low in n-3 FAs, was used in the HFD to minimize background n-3 FA intake and enable clearer comparisons with the supplemented groups. While this approach allowed better isolation of supplementation effects, it also resulted in unbalanced n-6/n-3 ratios. The observed outcomes may reflect not only the effects of increased n-3 FAs intake but also the influence of alterations in the dietary n-6/n-3 ratio. These considerations emphasize the importance of controlling for both absolute n-3 FAs intake and the overall dietary fatty acid profile in future studies.

Finally, the diet in this study was formulated to provide 1.2% and 2.4% of energy from n-3 FAs, aligning with physiologically relevant levels in humans. However, the specific roles of EPA and DHA, as well as their optimal ratio and effective dose range remain to be fully elucidated. Given that dietary intake of EPA + DHA remains suboptimal in many populations, further investigation into dose–response effects and the long-term impact of these fatty acids is warranted. Clarifying these parameters will improve translational application and inform the development of evidence-based dietary guidelines targeting obesity and metabolic health.

Conclusions

In this study, concurrent intake of n-3 FAs in weaned mice fed a HFD, particularly at higher doses, appeared to preserve adipose tissue morphology, attenuate inflammatory features, and support browning characteristics, which may collectively contribute to improved metabolic outcomes under diet-induced obesity conditions. Early postnatal life represents a critical window for metabolic programming, during which dietary inputs can influence long-term energy homeostasis and adipose tissue function. The intake of n-3 FAs during this developmental stage may modulate adipose tissue characteristics by enhancing BAT activity and promoting the browning of WAT, as suggested by the histological findings in this study. These potential changes in adipose tissue phenotype may contribute to improved thermogenic capacity and reduced fat accumulation, thereby supporting a more favorable metabolic trajectory. Nonetheless, further mechanistic studies are warranted to confirm these effects and elucidate the pathways involved.
