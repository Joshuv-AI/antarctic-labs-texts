UPLC profiling of matcha extract capping biosynthesized copper oxide nanoparticles and their antimicrobial and cytotoxic potentials
Authors: Mervette El Batouti, Nouf F Al-Harby, Mohamed S Abdelwahab, Rasha A Metwally
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research demonstrates a green synthesis and subsequent functionalization process of copper oxide nanoparticles (CuO NPs) utilizing matcha green tea extract as a sustainable reducing and functionalizing agent. UPLC-MS analysis revealed the presence of significant bioactive phytochemicals, notably epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), which is implicated in the reduction process. Thorough characterisation (XRD, FT-IR, SEM) verified the synthesis of pure, crystalline, monoclinic CuO NPs with an average size of 16–24 nm. The nanoparticles had considerable antibacterial activity, exhibiting robust efficacy against Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus, with a minimum inhibitory concentration of 250 µg/ml. CuO NPs-matcha exhibited significant, dose-dependent cytotoxicity against MCF-7 and Caco-2 carcinoma cells, with IC₅₀ values of 122.1 and 161.9 µg/ml, respectively. Crucially, they demonstrated markedly reduced toxicity towards non-cancerous Vero cells (IC₅₀ = 333.2 µg/ml), yielding selectivity indices of 2.73 and 2.06, which confirms a favorable selective profile. Although the positive control doxorubicin showed greater potency (lower IC₅₀), the CuO NPs-Matcha demonstrated significantly higher selectivity for cancer cells, suggesting a potentially improved safety profile despite its lower efficacy. These findings underscore the matcha-synthesized CuO NPs as a highly promising and biocompatible nanomaterial for specific medicinal applications, particularly in antibacterial and anticancer therapies.Keywords: UPLC-MS, Matcha, Copper oxide nanoparticles, Anticancer, AntimicrobialSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Cancer, Chemistry, Drug discovery, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

The nascent field of nanotechnology has revolutionized multiple domains, including medicine, catalysis, electronics, and environmental remediation, due to the unique physicochemical properties of materials at the nanoscale1,2. Nanoparticles (NPs), which are fundamental to this domain, exhibit enhanced surface-to-volume ratios, quantum confinement effects, and tunable surface characteristics, enabling exceptional applications. Traditional nanoparticle synthesis methods, such as chemical reduction, sol–gel processes, hydrothermal synthesis, and physical techniques like laser ablation or evaporation, often rely on hazardous reducing agents (e.g., sodium borohydride, hydrazine), toxic solvents, high energy consumption, and generate harmful byproducts3,4. These drawbacks create significant environmental, safety, and economic challenges, requiring an urgent pursuit of sustainable solutions. Green synthesis has emerged as an efficacious method aligned with the principles of green chemistry, emphasizing the use of non-toxic solvents, renewable resources, energy efficiency, and minimized waste generation5,6. Using plant extracts as bio-factories for nanoparticle synthesis has garnered considerable attention. Plants yield various naturally occurring phytochemicals, including polyphenols, flavonoids, terpenoids, alkaloids, proteins, and vitamins, which serve as multifaceted biological agents7,8. These proteins can effectively reduce metal ions to zerovalent or oxide forms while stabilizing the resulting nanoparticles and preventing agglomeration through capping mechanisms9. This method eliminates the need for harsh chemicals, operates under mild reaction conditions, is cost-effective, easily scalable, and inherently biocompatible, making the resulting nanoparticles particularly attractive for biomedical applications10,11. Consequently, research into plant-mediated nanoparticle synthesis has intensified, exploring several botanical sources for the generation of metallic (Ag, Au, Pt) and metal oxide (ZnO, TiO₂, CuO, Fe₃O₄) nanoparticles with controlled dimensions, shapes, and characteristics12–20.

Among the diverse plants studied, matcha green tea (Camellia sinensis) was selected as a superior bio-reagent for the green synthesis of CuO NPs due to its uniquely high concentration of polyphenolic antioxidants, particularly epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), which serves as a potent reducing agent for copper ions. Furthermore, the complex phytochemical profile of matcha, confirmed by our UPLC-MS analysis, provides a natural mixture of capping agents (catechins, proteins, amino acids) that enhance CuO NPs. This synthesis not only adheres to green chemistry principles but is also used for the production of a functionalized nanocomposite (CuO NPs-Matcha), where the bioactive phytochemical corona synergistically enhances the intrinsic biological properties of the CuO core, as evidenced by our compelling antimicrobial and anticancer results. Matcha, a finely ground powder sourced from meticulously grown and processed green tea leaves, is distinguished by its significantly higher concentration of phytochemicals compared to conventional green tea, primarily due to shade cultivation methods that promote chlorophyll and amino acid production21,22. The vibrant green powder is abundant in polyphenolic antioxidants, especially catechins. Epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) is the principal and most potent catechin, noted for its substantial antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and potential chemopreventive properties23–25. Matcha contains significant quantities of various catechins (epicatechin, epicatechin gallate, epigallocatechin), the unique amino acid L-theanine, caffeine, chlorophyll, vitamins (A, C, E), and minerals26–29. This wide variety of phytochemicals, especially polyphenols containing several hydroxyl (OH) and carbonyl (C = O) groups, endows matcha extract with exceptional reducing and chelating capabilities30–32. These functional groups effectively facilitate electron transport, reducing metal ions and forming stable complexes, which enhances nanoparticle nucleation and controlled growth33,34.

Furthermore, these biomolecules function as effective capping agents, passivating the nanoparticle surface and ensuring colloidal stability35,36. The inherent antioxidant characteristics of matcha compounds further enhance the biocompatibility of the resulting nanoparticles37,38. Thus, matcha extract is a highly promising, renewable, and versatile green reagent for the efficient synthesis and functionalization.

CuO NPs are a metal oxide of substantial technological importance due to their varied properties, including high thermal stability, semiconducting attributes (bandgap ~ 1.22–2.1 eV), strong antimicrobial effectiveness, catalytic capabilities, photoconductivity, and sensing functions19,39. These characteristics enable their utilization across multiple fields, including gas sensors, catalysts, antimicrobial coatings, solar cells, batteries, and nanofluids16–18. The production of CuO NPs can be achieved through many methods, primarily categorized as physical, chemical, and biological processes. Physical methodologies include procedures like laser ablation and thermal decomposition. While these processes can produce pure nanoparticles free from solvent contamination, they are often energy-intensive, require sophisticated equipment, and offer limited control over size distribution. Chemical approaches are widely employed and include precipitation, hydrothermal/solvothermal synthesis, sol–gel procedures, and microemulsion techniques. Chemical precipitation is favored due to its simplicity and cost-effectiveness; however, it sometimes requires the addition of stabilizing chemicals to prevent aggregation and may involve the use of hazardous substances. Biological (green) synthesis employing plant extracts, such as matcha, offers a sustainable alternative16–18. This technique involves combining an aqueous extract from the chosen plant, which contains reducing phytochemicals, with a copper salt solution, such as CuSO₄. The bioactive compounds in the extract convert Cu2⁺ ions to Cu⁰, which subsequently oxidize in the presence of dissolved oxygen to produce Cu₂O and ultimately CuO nanoparticles, or directly facilitate the formation of CuO from Cu2⁺18. Also, The phytochemicals used to functionalized the developing nanoparticles, controlling their surface40–42. This method operates in ambient conditions, avoids toxic chemicals, utilizes renewable resources, and produces inherently biocompatible nanoparticles, making it especially advantageous for biomedical and environmentally focused applications. The choice of plant extract significantly influences the size, shape, crystallinity, and consequently, the properties of the produced CuO NPs43–46.

In conclusion, the environmentally sustainable production of CuO NPs using plant extracts, such as matcha, represents a significant leap forward in sustainable nanotechnology. This methodology leverages the inherent reducing and stabilizing properties of phytochemicals, thereby circumventing the environmental and safety concerns associated with conventional methods, while ensuring biocompatibility and cost-effectiveness. Examining potent sources, such as matcha, rich in bioactive chemicals, and enhancing synthesis techniques for critical nanomaterials, like CuO NPs, offers considerable promise for developing novel, sustainable nanotechnologies with varied applications.

This work substantially enhances the comprehension of the extraction and comprehensive profiling of phytochemicals in matcha extract, particularly within its polar and nonpolar fractions. Sustainable nanomaterial synthesis effectively utilizes plant-derived resources to produce advanced functional CuO NPs-Matcha with customized properties and improved application possibilities. This study utilized various analytical techniques, including UPLC-MS, IR, FT-IR, XRD, and DTGA. The antimicrobial and anticancer effects of CuO NPs -matcha have been investigated.

Preparation of matcha extract

A single batch of high-grade culinary matcha green tea powder was used for all experiments to ensure consistency in the phytochemical input for nanoparticle synthesis.

Preparation of aqueous matcha extract

5.0 g of matcha powder was mixed with 100 ml of Distilled water (DW) and stirred vigorously (800 rpm) at 60 °C for 2 h. The mixture was then centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was filtered through a 45 µm disc filter, and the resulting clear extract was stored at 4 °C for further use. A single batch of high-grade culinary matcha green tea powder was used for all experiments to ensure consistency in the phytochemical input for nanoparticle synthesis.

Preparation of methanolic matcha extract

5.0 g of matcha powder was mixed with 100 ml of methanol and stirred vigorously (800 rpm) at 60 °C for 2 h. The mixture was then centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was filtered through a 45 µm disc filter, and the resulting clear extract was stored at 4 °C for further use.

Synthesis of nanocomposite

Synthesis of CuO NPs

0.2 M copper nitrate was prepared in 50 ml DW, then 100 ml of aqueous matcha extract was added to the copper nitrate solution under vigorous stirring at 800 rpm for 2 h in a dark place, and the pH of the solution was maintained at 9 using 0.2 M NaOH at 25 °C. The color of the solution changes from blue to black-green, confirming the formation of CuO NPs. This solid was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min, washed repeatedly 3 times with DW, and collected and dried at 50 °C until completely dry, which yielded 3 g CuO NPs/5 g matcha powder.

Synthesis of CuO NPs modified matcha extract (CuO NPs-Matcha)

2.0 g of CuO NPs were mixed with 20 ml of methanolic matcha extract under vigorous stirring at 800 rpm for 2 h in a dark place at 25 °C. This solid was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min, washed repeatedly 3 times with DW, and collected and dried at 50 °C until completely dry.

Solvent extraction of matcha extract

To extract and identify phytochemicals in matcha, 5 g of matcha powder was soaked in 100 ml of methanol under vigorous stirring (800 rpm) in a dark place for 2 h at 25 °C. The clear methanolic extract (75 ml) was transferred to a separation funnel, and a solvent extraction process was performed using hexane (three times, 50 ml) to separate the nonpolar constituents. Finally, the crude methanolic extract, fraction in hexane, and fraction remaining in methanol were analyzed using two modes of UPLC-MS.

Antimicrobial activity determination

The antimicrobial activity of CuO NPs-Matcha was assessed via the agar well diffusion method and minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)47. The antimicrobial efficacy of the CuO NPs-Matcha composite at 15 mg/ml was assessed against Gram-positive bacteria (Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 13,565 and Bacillus subtilis ATCC 1088), Gram-negative bacteria (Escherichia coli ATCC 10,536 and Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 10,031), and fungal strains (Candida albicans ATCC 10,231 and Aspergillus niger ATCC 16,404) using an agar well diffusion method where the microbial inoculum (10 µl) was incorporated directly into the agar medium (20 ml). Specifically, standardized microbial suspensions with bacterial inocula adjusted to McFarland 0.5 standard solution (1.5 × 108 CFU/ml) and fungal inocula adjusted to 1–5 × 10⁶ spores/ml for A. niger or a 0.5 McFarland standard for C. albicans were thoroughly mixed with molten nutrient agar or sabouraud dextrose agar, respectively, before being poured into Petri dishes to create seeded agar plates. Once the agar solidified, 6-mm diameter wells were bored, and a volume of 100 µl of CuO NPs-Matcha, CuO NPs, and the matcha extract was added to each designated well. Standard positive controls (ampicillin, gentamicin, and nystatin) and a negative control (DMSO) were loaded into separate wells. The entire experiment was performed in triplicate to ensure reliability and reproducibility. The plates were subsequently incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and the antimicrobial activity was quantified by measuring the diameters of the zones of inhibition in millimeters. The final results represented the mean of the three replicas.

The MIC of CuO NPs-Matcha was determined using a standard broth microdilution method in a 96-well plate, whereby a primary stock solution was prepared by dissolving 1 mg of CuO NPs-Matcha in 1 ml of DMSO. A two-fold serial dilution was then performed directly in the plate, with each well containing a final volume of 100 µl of nutrient broth, creating a concentration gradient of the antimicrobial agent. Each well in the test series was subsequently inoculated with 10 µl of a standardized microbial suspension of the test pathogen (S. aureus, E. coli, and C. albicans). Appropriate controls were included on each plate: a positive control (broth plus inoculum) to confirm normal microbial growth and a negative control (broth only) to confirm medium sterility. Following incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, the MIC was determined by comparing the turbidity in each test well against the controls; the MIC was defined and recorded as the lowest concentration of CuO NPs-Matcha in the dilution series that resulted in no visible turbidity, indicating complete inhibition of microbial growth, which was clearly distinguishable from the visible turbidity present in the positive growth control. The final reported MIC value for each pathogen was the mode, or consistent value, observed across all three experimental replicates.

In vitro cytotoxicity of CuO NPs-Matcha

The cytotoxicity of CuO NPs-Matcha was examined against Vero cells (African green monkey kidney cells), as well as two carcinoma cell lines: Caco-2 (human colon carcinoma) and MCF-7 (human breast cancer) via MTT assay. Low-passage-number cells (passages 15–25) were grown in a 5% CO₂ incubator at 37 °C in RPMI medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 2 mM glutamine, 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 µg/ml streptomycin. In the experiment, adherent cells were seeded onto 96-well plates with 100 µl of medium at a density of 105 cells/well, while suspended cells were placed onto 0.5 × 105 cells/well. After 24 h, the cells were exposed to a concentration range of CuO NPs-Matcha from 31.5 to 1000 µg/ml for 24 h. Following the exposure period, cell viability was assessed by measuring the cells’ capacity to transform the yellow dye 3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5 diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT) (5 mg/ml in PBS) into a purple formazan product, with the absorbance measured at 595 nm. Using a triple MTT test conduction, the IC50 values were calculated by fitting the dose–response data to a four-parameter logistic model using GraphPad Prism software (9.0.0.121). The selectivity index (SI) was then computed from these IC50 values47,48.

Results and discussion

Characterization

IR analysis of matcha green tea and FT-IR of nanoparticles

The Infrared (IR) spectrum of the methanolic extract of matcha green tea (Camellia sinensis), Fig. 1A, provides critical insights into the functional groups of its bioactive constituents, which influence its efficacy as a reducing and stabilizing agent in nanoparticle synthesis. A broad absorption band centered at 3300 cm⁻1 indicates O–H stretching vibrations, signaling substantial hydrogen bonding among the many phenolic hydroxyl groups in catechins, flavonoids, and carboxylic acids46. A significant peak about 2920 cm−1 is ascribed to asymmetric C–H stretching vibrations in aliphatic methyl (–CH₃) and methylene (–CH₂–) groups, commonly associated with fatty acids, terpenoids, and amino acid side chains40. A specific band about 1705 cm−1 corresponds to C = O stretching vibrations, primarily attributed to ester carbonyl groups in gallate moieties (EGCG and epicatechin gallate) and carboxylic acids from organic acid constituents42. The spectrum exhibits two prominent peaks in the aromatic region: a strong band near 1600 cm−1 and a slightly less intense but distinct band around 1510 cm−1, both corresponding to C = C stretching vibrations in the benzene rings of polyphenols and flavonoids, thus confirming matcha’s exceptionally high catechin content49. Additional characteristics include a band about 1400 cm−1, attributed to O–H in-plane bending and C–H scissoring modes50–52. The complex fingerprint region (1200–1000 cm−1) displays overlapping contributions from C–O stretching in aryl esters (~ 1260 cm−1), phenolic C–O and ether C–O–C linkages (~ 1160 cm−1), and C–O stretching vibrations of polysaccharides, as well as potential C–N stretches from alkaloids like caffeine and the amino acid L-theanine (~ 1070–1030 cm−1)34,36–38. This spectral profile confirms the presence of polyphenols (catechins, flavonoids), alkaloids (caffeine, theophylline), amino acids (L-theanine), polysaccharides, and aliphatic compounds. The high abundance of electron-donating functional groups, especially phenolic –OH and carbonyl (C = O) moieties, essentially enhances the extract’s strong reducing capacity for metal ions. The simultaneous presence of aromatic systems, esters, ethers, amines, and hydroxyl groups improves chelation, capping, and stability of produced nanoparticles, thus ensuring colloidal integrity and influencing their morphological properties53.

(A) IR of matcha extract, (B) FT-IR of CuO NPs, and (C) FT-IR of CuO NPs-Matcha.

The FTIR spectrum of CuO NPs, Fig. 1B, synthesized using the aqueous matcha extract, confirms the successful formation of the metal oxide phase. The most definitive feature is the emergence of a strong, broad absorption band in the low-frequency region, typically between 500 and 600 cm−1, which is characteristic of the stretching vibration of the Cu–O bond. The significant reduction in intensity of the broad O–H stretching band compared to the pure matcha extract spectrum indicates the consumption of phenolic and hydroxyl groups, as well as the reduction of copper ions (Cu2⁺) to copper oxide. The simplicity of the spectrum, devoid of the complex organic fingerprints, confirms the efficacy of the aqueous matcha extract as a reducing agent and the subsequent formation of pure CuO NPs54.

The FTIR spectrum of CuO NPs-Matcha (Fig. 1C) offers critical insights into the surface functionalization and chemical interactions between the phytochemical capping layer and the metal oxide core, thereby confirming the successful green synthesis. A pronounced absorption band at 3420 cm−1 is ascribed to O–H stretching vibrations, notably broader and shifted from pure matcha extract (~ 3300 cm−1), indicating significant hydrogen bonding between the phenolic hydroxyl groups of catechins and surface-bound water molecules on CuO NPs, along with potential N–H stretching contributions from residual proteins or alkaloids44,55. The aliphatic C–H stretching region exhibits reduced peaks at 2925 cm−1 and 2850 cm−1 (asymmetric/symmetric –CH₂– stretches), signifying partial oxidation of alkyl chains during synthesis. The carbonyl (C = O) stretching band displays a significant redshift to 1630 cm−1, a 75 cm−1 downshift from the ester carbonyl peak of pure matcha at 1705 cm−1, indicating coordination between the electron-rich carbonyl oxygen atoms of gallate esters and the undercoordinated Cu2⁺ sites on nanoparticle surfaces, leading to the formation of stable metal–carboxylate complexes43. The aromatic C = C stretching vibrations (1600–1510 cm⁻1 in pure matcha) amalgamate into a singular broadened envelope at 1560 cm−1, signifying π-electron transfer from polyphenolic rings to CuO surfaces55. The fingerprint region exhibits a diminished intensity of matcha-specific peaks: the aryl C–O stretch at 1260 cm⁻1 diminishes due to phenolic –OH coordination, whereas the C–O–C ether stretch at 1160 cm−1 and polysaccharide C–O vibrations at 1075 cm−1 persist but broaden, signifying phytochemical binding without complete degradation40. The emergence of two significant, low-frequency bands at 530 cm−1 and 590 cm−1, ascribed to Cu–O stretching vibrations in the monoclinic tenorite phase, validates the formation of crystalline CuO. No detectable impurities of Cu₂O (absent at ~ 630 cm−1) or metallic Cu (missing at ~ 610 cm−1) were observed45. The absence of peaks within the 2000–2200 cm−1 range precludes the presence of copper carbide impurities. Collectively, these spectral alterations, specifically the redshift of the carbonyl stretch and the broadening of the O–H band, mechanistically validate the interaction between the galloyl/phenolic groups of matcha phytochemicals and the copper ions. This interaction, characterized by coordination between electron-rich oxygen atoms and the Cu2⁺ sites, confirms that the matcha phytochemicals act as multidentate ligands, functioning not only as reductants but also as effective capping agents that passivate the nanoparticle surface and ensure colloidal stability55. This study confirms that matcha phytochemicals perform dual roles: reductants to produce crystalline CuO cores and as capping agents to provide a stable bio-organic interface.

Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX)

The energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) analysis of the spectrum, Fig. 3A, provides definitive elemental confirmation of the synthesized CuO NPs. The spectrum reveals the presence of only two elements: copper (Cu) and oxygen (O). The quantitative analysis indicates a mass percentage of 61.27% for copper and 38.73% for oxygen, corresponding to an atomic percentage of 28.48% Cu and 71.52% O. The measured atomic ratio of O to Cu is around 2.51, roughly corresponding to the ideal stoichiometric ratio of 1:2 for pure copper (II) oxide (CuO)56. The absence of further elemental peaks within the instrument’s detection limits confirms the sample’s remarkable chemical purity and the practical synthesis of CuO without significant elemental contamination. The distinct and clear Cu-Kα and O-K emission lines in the spectra, free from extraneous peaks, further confirm the formation of a pure phase.

(A) Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) of CuO NPs, (B) EDX of CuO NPs-Matcha.

The EDX spectrum of the CuO NPs-Matcha composite (Fig. 3B) displays a notably complex element profile due to the integration of inorganic nanoparticles with the organic matcha matrix. Copper and oxygen are present, constituting mass percentages of 45.35% and 37.66%, respectively. The significant abundance of carbon (C) at 16.26 mass% clearly indicates the presence of organic compounds typical of matcha green tea, such as cellulose, polyphenols, and other carbon-derived biomolecules. Furthermore, the detection of trace amounts of nitrogen (N, 0.65%) and sulfur (S, 0.08%) corresponds with the presence of proteins, amino acids, or other nitrogenous and sulfurous organic constituents within the biological framework. The atomic percentages (15.96% Cu, 52.66% O, 30.29% C) clearly reveal this composite nature, illustrating the reduction of the copper signal by the carbonaceous matrix. Combining signals from both the inorganic CuO NPs and the organic host material, this elemental fingerprint provides conclusive evidence of the successful fabrication of the hybrid composite.

X-ray diffraction analysis

The X-ray diffraction pattern of the produced CuO NPs, depicted in Fig. 4A, confirms the successful formation of a highly crystalline material. All observable diffraction peaks are sharp and distinct, indicating a high degree of crystallinity. The peaks observed in the diffraction pattern are exclusively ascribed to the monoclinic tenorite phase of copper oxide (CuO), as evidenced by the clear and distinct reflections at 2θ values of approximately 35.5° and 38.7°, which correspond to the (002) and (111) planes, respectively. These reflections are in excellent agreement with the data from the JCPDS card No. 48–1548, confirming the phase identity of the synthesized material56. In addition to these key peaks, prominent reflections are seen at 32.5° (110), 48.7° (202), 53.5° (020), 58.3° (202), and 68.1° (220), all of which align with the typical diffraction planes for monoclinic CuO. These findings further validate the purity of the synthesized copper oxide phase. Notably, there is an absence of diffraction peaks that would correspond to other copper oxide phases, such as cuprous oxide (Cu₂O), referenced by JCPDS card No. 78–207657, or metallic copper (Cu), referenced by JCPDS card No. 04–083657. This lack of additional peaks confirms the remarkable phase purity of the CuO nanoparticles synthesized in this study. The exclusion of Cu₂O and metallic Cu diffraction peaks supports the high quality and homogeneity of the synthesized material, ensuring that it is predominantly in the tenorite form with minimal contamination56,58. The absence of diffraction peaks related to cuprous oxide (Cu₂O) or metallic copper (Cu) confirms the remarkable phase purity of the synthesized nanoparticles. The noted peak broadening is a characteristic feature of nanoscale crystallites, and the average crystallite size can be estimated using the Scherrer equation. While the data conclusively show the final product to be phase-pure CuO, the precise reduction–oxidation pathway from Cu2⁺ ions to CuO nanoparticles, potentially mediated by the polyphenols in matcha, warrants further investigation using surface-sensitive techniques, such as X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS).

(A) X- Ray Diffraction (XRD) of CuO NPs, (B) XRD of CuO NPs-Matcha.

The XRD pattern of the CuO NPs-Matcha composite, depicted in Fig. 4B, reveals a structure consisting of crystalline and amorphous phases. All primary diffraction peaks corresponding to the monoclinic CuO phase unequivocally identify the crystalline structure. The peaks, especially the doublet at 35.5°(002) and 38.7° (111), are distinctly identifiable and align closely with the pattern of pure CuO nanoparticles, confirming that the crystalline structure of the copper oxide nanoparticles is maintained after integration with the matcha substrate. This pattern exhibits a prominent, diffuse peak centered at an approximate 2θ value of 22°. This significant amorphous background is clear evidence of Matcha’s organic constituents, including cellulose, lignin, and various polyphenols. While the peak positions ((110), (002), (111), and (202) planes) remain identical57, confirming that the crystal structure of CuO is unchanged, the intensities of these peaks are significantly altered in the matcha-composite sample. This change in intensity suggests that the matcha phytochemicals have successfully coated the surface of the CuO nanoparticles. This surface layer interacts with the X-rays, affecting the scattering and diffraction efficiency without disrupting the underlying crystal lattice, which is a classic XRD indicator of a successful surface modification or functionalization. The crystallite size of the monoclinic CuO nanoparticles was estimated using the Scherrer equation, D = Kλ/βcosθ59, applied to the most intense (002) peak at 2θ = 38.7°. Using a shape factor K = 0.9, the X-ray wavelength λ = 0.15406 nm (Cu Kα), and the instrument-corrected full width at half maximum in radians, the calculated average crystallite size of CuO NPs was found to be approximately 28.1 nm, confirming the nanoscale dimensions of the synthesized material. The estimated crystallite size for the CuO NPs-Matcha is approximately 21.9 nm.

In most methods, surface Area Analysis Results show that CuO NPs-Matcha exhibits higher surface area values than CuO NPs (Multipoint BET, Langmuir, and DFT). Regarding micropore surface area (V-t method), CuO NPs-Matcha recorded 26.03 m2/g, higher than CuO NPs (18.79 m2/g), suggesting a greater contribution of micropores in CuO NPs-Matcha.

Thermal analysis

The thermal characteristics and stability of the synthesized CuO NPs were thoroughly investigated using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), Fig. 5A, with differential thermal analysis (DTA), Fig. 5C. The TGA curve exhibits exceptional thermal stability, with a total mass loss of less than 5% throughout the whole temperature range, signifying a very stable inorganic material. The minimal mass loss occurs in two distinct phases: an initial slight reduction below approximately 150 °C, attributed to the desorption of physically adsorbed water molecules from the extensive surface area of the nanoparticles, followed by a more gradual mass reduction up to 400 °C associated with the removal of chemically bound surface hydroxyl groups and the decomposition of any residual carbonaceous materials or carbonates formed during synthesis. The accompanying DTA curve, Fig. 5B, provides critical supplemental information, exhibiting a shallow, broad endothermic peak in the low-temperature range (50–150 °C) that correlates with the enthalpy of vaporization required for water desorption. The complete absence of significant exothermic peaks in the DTA profile, particularly within the 200–500 °C temperature range where organic combustion often occurs, definitively rules out the presence of considerable organic impurities or carbonaceous residues. The lack of endothermic activities at high temperatures indicates the absence of phase shifts or decomposition, hence confirming the stability of the monoclinic CuO phase after thermal treatment58. The flat baseline observed in both TGA and DTA curves above 200 °C strongly indicates the high purity and exceptional thermal stability of the synthesized nanoparticles, making them suitable for high-temperature applications where material integrity is essential 58,60.

The thermal characteristics and stability of the CuO NPs-Matcha were rigorously assessed via thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), Fig. 5D, differential thermal analysis (DTA), Fig. 5F, and derivative thermogravimetry (DTGA), Fig. 5D. The TGA curve has a complex multi-stage degradation profile that significantly contrasts with that of pure CuO NPs, highlighting the composite character of the material, which encompasses both inorganic and organic components. The preliminary mass reduction of approximately 3–5% below 150 °C is ascribed to the evaporation of physically adsorbed water molecules, which is more pronounced than in pure CuO owing to the hydrophilic properties of the matcha component. The most significant mass loss event occurs between 200 °C and 400 °C, leading to a substantial weight decrease of approximately 40–50%, due to the thermal decomposition and combustion of organic constituents in the matcha matrix, including cellulose, polyphenols, proteins, and other phytochemicals. This notable degradation phase is marked by a distinct exothermic peak in the DTA curve at around 300–350 °C, confirming the oxidative burning of organic matter. The DTGA curve clearly shows the maximum breakdown rate at around 320 °C, precisely measuring the thermal degradation kinetics. A continued gradual mass loss up to 600 °C is associated with the ongoing carbonization of residual organic matter and the elimination of more stable oxygen-containing functional groups. Above 600 °C, the curve stabilizes, yielding a residual mass of around 45–50%, indicative of the stable CuO inorganic framework. The detailed thermal profile reveals that the Matcha component acts as an effective stabilizing matrix, decomposing without residue while maintaining the CuO nanostructure and providing insights into the thermal stability limits for this nanocomposite material’s applications.

Based on the comprehensive UPLC-MS analysis of the three matcha extracts, crude methanol, hexane, and methanolic fraction, a clear conclusion emerges regarding the selective extraction and chemical composition of matcha components based on solvent polarity. The choice of extraction solvent dramatically dictates the chemical profile obtained from matcha. Methanol is optimal for extracting polar, bioactive compounds, such as catechins and theanine, with the ES + ve mode primarily used for their detection. Hexane selectively isolates a complex array of nonpolar lipids and caffeine, for which the ES-ve mode is unexpectedly vital. The presence of triglycerides in both the hexane and methanolic fractions underscores the complex nature of the matrix and the small amount of nonpolar material that methanol can co-extract.

Antimicrobial activity determination

First, the study evaluated the antimicrobial efficacy of CuO NPs-Matcha, CuO NPs, and matcha extract (15 mg/ml) compared to standard antibiotics by measuring the zone of inhibition against various pathogens. The evaluation of antimicrobial assay revealed distinct activity profiles for the synthesized materials against the tested microbial strains, as shown in Fig. 7A. The CuO NPs-Matcha composite demonstrated the most potent activity among the test substances, exhibiting zones of inhibition of 14.1 ± 0.26 mm against S. aureus, 23.0 ± 0.25 mm against E. coli, and 16.1 ± 0.36 mm against C. albicans. Notably, this composite material consistently outperformed its individual components, plain CuO NPs (11.27 ± 0.25 mm, 20.30 ± 0.36 mm, and 13.37 ± 0.47 mm, respectively) and matcha extract alone, which showed only marginal activity (8.27 ± 0.25 mm, 7.30 ± 0.26 mm, and 6.47 ± 0.35 mm, respectively). This significant enhancement suggests a synergistic interaction where phytochemicals in the matcha extract may improve the stability or bioavailability of the CuO NPs, thereby augmenting their microbial toxicity. Interestingly, the composite exhibited notably vigorous activity against the Gram-negative bacterium E. coli, with a zone size approaching that of the standard antibiotic, Gentamicin (27.1 ± 0.31 mm). This could be attributed to the targeted action of NPs on the thin peptidoglycan layer and outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria. However, against the other strains, the conventional antimicrobials, Ampicillin for S. aureus (20.5 ± 0.15 mm) and Nystatin for C. albicans (21.1 ± 0.32 mm) remained superior. It is crucial to note that the antimicrobial effect was strain-specific, as no detectable activity was observed for any of the test substances against B. subtilis, K. pneumoniae, or A. niger. In conclusion, the results demonstrate that the composite material, CuO NPs-Matcha, exhibits the most potent antimicrobial activity among the three tested substances, outperforming both plain CuO NPs and matcha extract alone.

Antimicrobial activity (inhibition zone) of CuONPs-Matcha, CuONPs and matcha extract (A) and MIC of CuONPs-Matcha (B).

According to the inhibition zone results, the antibacterial activity of the composite of CuO NPs-Matcha was evaluated by determining the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of CuO NPs-Matcha against the three pathogenic microorganisms (Fig. 7B). For S. aureus and E. coli, the MIC was found to be 250 µg/ml, indicating the concentration required to inhibit bacterial growth. The fungal pathogen C. albicans showed higher resistance, with an MIC of 1000 µg/ml. These results suggest that while the compound exhibits similar antibacterial activity against Gram-positive (S. aureus) and Gram-negative (E. coli) bacteria, it is significantly less effective against the fungal strain C. albicans, requiring a fourfold higher concentration for inhibition. The elevated MIC value for C. albicans may reflect intrinsic fungal resistance or the compound’s limited antifungal properties.

In different studies, the biogenically synthesized CuO NPs showed antimicrobial activity against several pathogens. The contact and disruption of the bacterial cell membrane can lead to the loss of cytoplasmic content, one of the postulated mechanisms of the antibacterial effect64. Also, a suggested mechanism is the downregulated expression of specific genes related to bacterial virulence65. The biosynthesized CuO NPs showed antimicrobial activity against S. aureus, E. coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Clostridium perfringens, Campylobacter coli, Listeria monocytogenes, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Moraxella catarrhalis, and Ralstonia solanacearum64–67. Different MIC values were observed through many studies focused on the biosynthesized CuO NPs. It was reported that Cylindrospermum stagnale-mediated CuO NPs showed antimicrobial potential with MICs of 1.5, 2.4, 1.7, 2.5, and 0.6 mM against C. albicans, K. pneumoniae, E. cloacae, P. aeruginosa, E. coli, respectively68. On the other hand, CuO NPs synthesized by Aerva javanica leaf extract showed a contant MIC of 128 μg/ml against all the tested pathogens (E. coli, P. aeruginosa, A. baumannii., S. aureus69. It was observed that the MIC of CuO NPs synthesized by Balanites aegyptiaca stem bark extract was 100 μg/ml for B. subtilis and E. Faecalis70.

Additionally, matcha exhibited antimicrobial activity against S. pneumoniae, S. aureus, and Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans71,72. The antimicrobial activity is firmly attributed to polyphenolic compound content, including flavonols, flavandiols, and phenolic acids22,73.

Based on our experimental data, we propose that the antimicrobial effect is primarily due to a synergistic combination of reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and metal ion release, rather than membrane disruption as the primary mechanism. The zones of inhibition for CuO NPs alone (11.27 mm for S. aureus and 20.30 mm for E. coli) confirm that the NPs themselves are potent antimicrobials, a well-documented effect largely attributed to Cu2⁺ ion release and subsequent ROS generation. The key finding is that the CuO NPs-Matcha composite consistently outperforms both of its individual components (e.g., 14.1 mm vs. 11.27 mm and 8.27 mm for S. aureus). This strong synergy is a classic indicator of combined oxidative stress mechanisms. We propose that the phytochemicals in matcha work in concert with the CuO NPs to amplify ROS production and overwhelm the microbial antioxidant defense systems. If physical membrane disruption were the dominant mechanism, we would expect a different activity profile. The fact that the composite is more effective against the Gram-negative E. coli (which has a complex outer membrane) than the Gram-positive S. aureus suggests a more complex, biochemical mechanism like ROS generation is at play. For the validation of our findings, direct in vitro mechanistic studies, including quantitative intracellular ROS detection using fluorescent probes (e.g., DCFH-DA), measurement of copper ion uptake via ICP-MS, and ultrastructural damage assessment via SEM/TEM, will be conducted to conclusively prove the synergistic oxidative stress mechanism. This will be followed by in vivo validation using an infected Galleria mellonella (wax worm) model to assess survival, microbial burden, and toxicity, providing a crucial bridge to future studies in mammalian wound infection models.

Because many infections are becoming increasingly resistant to antibacterial drugs, there has been a shift in focus in recent years toward traditional natural ingredients as a means of protection. There has been much research on the biosynthesis of metal and metal oxide NPs since it is less harmful to the environment than chemical and physical approaches74,75. This motivated us to investigate the antimicrobial effects of the matcha and CuO NPs combination.

In vitro cytotoxicity of CuO NPs-Matcha

The in vitro toxicity of CuO NPs-Matcha was shown in Fig. 8. The data provided in Fig. 9A shows the toxicity percentages of CuO NPs-Matcha at different concentrations of 31.25, 62.5, 125, 250, 500, and 1000 µg/ml across three different cell lines: Vero, Caco-2, and MCF-7. It was observed that the toxicity is dose-dependent, as concentration increases, and toxicity generally increases in all cell lines. MCF-7 is the most sensitive cell, reaching > 50% toxicity at 125 µg/ml, while Caco-2 shows moderate sensitivity, with ~ 87% toxicity at 250 µg/ml. Vero cells are the least sensitive cells, requiring 500 µg/ml to reach > 88% toxicity. From IC50 (the concentration at which the CuO NPs-Matcha causes 50% toxicity) calculations (Fig. 9B), the MCF-7 breast cancer cells exhibited the highest sensitivity, with an IC50 of 122.1 µg/ml, suggesting strong potential for anticancer activity. In comparison, Caco-2 showed moderate sensitivity (IC50 = 161.9 µg/ml). Meanwhile, Vero demonstrated the lowest sensitivity, with an IC50 of 333.2 µg/ml, indicating relatively low general cytotoxicity and a possible safety advantage for non-target tissues. The quantification of this selective toxicity was evaluated by the calculation of the Selectivity Index (SI), yielding selectivity indices of 2.73 and 2.06 for MCF-7 and Caco-2, respectively. Although the positive control doxorubicin showed greater potency (IC₅₀ values of MCF-7 = 1.53, Caco-2 = 2.44 and Vero = 2.56 µg/ml, respectively), the CuO NPs-Matcha demonstrated significantly higher selectivity for cancer cells, suggesting a potentially improved safety profile despite its lower efficacy.

On the other hand, the cytotoxic activity of the biogeneic CuO-NPs suggested that toxicity against neuroblastoma (N2A) cells was negligible at concentrations below 60 μg/ml69,82–85.

Additionally, matcha has been linked to a slew of health advantages as of late, and in vitro research has shown that it may have an effect that inhibits the growth and spread of cancer86 It was reported that matcha possessed high cytotoxic activity on WERI-Rb-1 human retinoblastoma cells and T47D breast cancer cells after 72 h87. Also, the functionalization of nanoparticles like silver with matcha exhibited a potential anti-inflammatory activity for treating inflammation-associated disorders63,88.

Researching the biogenic synthesis of matcha-based CuO NPs and their possible anticancer efficacy against Caco-2 and MCF-7 carcinoma is a significant focus of our investigation. Further studies, including Annexin V/PI flow cytometry and Western blot analysis for apoptotic markers, are the focus of our ongoing research to determine the precise mode of action that elucidates the mechanistic pathway. The window for treatment and safety profile can be confirmed by an additional in vivo study.

Conclusion

This study confirms the green synthesis of CuO nanoparticles using matcha extract and establishes a direct link between the extract’s phytochemical profile and the nanomaterial’s surface. The two-step synthesis procedure, which leverages the consistent phytochemical profile of matcha extract for both the initial reduction and subsequent functionalization, ensures a high degree of reproducibility. Furthermore, the reliance on simple, ambient-condition reactions in aqueous media provides a straightforward and scalable pathway for producing these bioactive nanocomposites, offering a clear advantage over more complex, energy-intensive multi-step chemical processes. The green synthesis and subsequent functionalization process effectively used the intricate mixture of bioactive chemicals in matcha, as conclusively identified by UPLC-MS analysis, which provided the fundamental evidence elucidating the mechanism and efficacy of the process. The complete UPLC-MS analyses of the three matcha extracts, crude methanol, hexane, and methanolic fraction show that solvent polarity selectively extracts and chemically composes matcha components. The chemical profile of matcha depends heavily on the extraction solvent. Methanol is optimal for extracting polar, bioactive compounds, such as catechins and theanine, with the ES + ve mode primarily used for their detection. Hexane preferentially isolates caffeine and a variety of nonpolar lipids, making the ES-ve mode unexpectedly important. Having triglycerides in both the hexane and methanolic fractions shows how complex the matrix is and how little nonpolar material methanol can co-extract. It is essential to note that, although a single batch of matcha was used to ensure internal consistency in this study, the inherent batch-to-batch variability of plant extracts remains a challenge for standardization. Future work aimed at scaling this process will require the establishment of quality control parameters, such as standardizing the extract based on the concentration of key biomarker compounds like EGCG, or moving towards the use of isolated, pure phytochemicals. Catechin phytochemicals facilitated the formation of crystalline, monoclinic CuO NPs confirmed by XRD, capped by an organic corona to form a high-surface-area composite confirmed by BET and SEM. This bio-organic capping was responsible for the material’s significantly enhanced biological activity. FT-IR and SEM analyses confirmed the successful formation of a phytochemical corona around the CuO NPs, which is inferred to enhance stability by providing steric hindrance. However, the absence of zeta potential measurements to directly quantify colloidal stability in suspension is a limitation of this study. Future work will include this analysis to fully characterize the nano-suspension’s behavior for biomedical and environmental applications. The CuO NPs-Matcha composite demonstrated selective and robust antimicrobial properties, evidenced by a significant zone of inhibition against E. coli (23.3 ± 0.6 mm) and MIC of 250 µg/ml against both S. aureus and E. coli, presumably attributable to the synergistic effects of the nanoparticle’s inherent toxicity and the preserved bioactive compounds. The composite exhibited significant anticancer potential, demonstrating dose-dependent cytotoxicity with high sensitivity towards MCF-7 breast cancer cells (IC50 = 122.1 µg/ml) and moderate efficacy against Caco-2 colon carcinoma cells (IC50 = 161.9 µg/ml), while showing considerably reduced toxicity towards non-cancerous Vero cells (IC50 = 333.2 µg/ml), indicating promising selective toxicity. This study goes beyond synthesis to explain how matcha’s phytochemical makeup affects CuO NPs’ structural, physicochemical, and functional properties. This makes matcha-mediated synthesis an excellent, environmentally sustainable technique for producing biocompatible nanomaterials for antimicrobial therapy and cancer. Scaling production and understanding its anticancer and antibacterial molecular processes are future research goals. Additionally, from an environmental perspective, this matcha-mediated synthesis offers a clean alternative to conventional chemical routes by eliminating the need for hazardous reagents, utilizing a renewable plant resource as a bioreactor, operating under energy-efficient ambient conditions, and minimizing the generation of toxic waste, thus fully aligning with the principles of green chemistry. While the matcha-mediated synthesis presented here is efficient and sustainable at the laboratory scale, future work aimed at industrial translation must address key challenges related to the batch-to-batch variability of the plant extract, the optimization of reaction parameters in large-volume reactors, and the replacement of centrifugation with more scalable downstream processing methods like tangential flow filtration.
