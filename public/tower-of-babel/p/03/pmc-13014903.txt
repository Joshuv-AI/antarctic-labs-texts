First-generation antipsychotic shortages in the United States: Utilization and switching patterns
Authors: Ashley Tabah, Neel Swamy, Clayton English
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has declared an increasing number of psychotropic shortages, which may adversely affect health outcomes by forcing psychotropic switches or rationing. There is a paucity of literature describing the impact of psychotropic shortages.ObjectiveTo describe antipsychotic utilization patterns following FDA-declared antipsychotic shortages or discontinuations.MethodsThis was a descriptive, pre-post analysis of the Komodo Healthcare Map® between January 2016 and September 2023. Individuals were included if they had a psychotic spectrum disorder diagnosis. Eligible individuals had a prescription drug claim for a first-generation antipsychotic (FGA) during the six months preceding the FDA-declared shortage or discontinuation date (“index date”) and a prescription drug claim for any antipsychotic in the twelve months after the index date. Individuals were excluded if the medication possession ratio (MPR) for their original shortage drug was below 80%, or if they had an inpatient hospitalization within six months of the index date. Switching was the primary outcome of interest and was defined as use of a new antipsychotic during the follow-up period with a MPR of at least 80%.ResultsAll oral FGAs had at least one manufacturer shortage during the study period, with 95,968 individuals qualifying for inclusion. Most were receiving haloperidol (n = 52,344, 54.5%) and perphenazine (n = 16,966, 17.7%). Switching was common among individuals receiving thiothixene (32.8%), trifluoperazine (55.2%), and molindone (100%). Time to first switch ranged from 22 (standard deviation [SD]: 21) to 103 (SD: 66) days.ConclusionShortages of FGAs may influence patients' switching to other agents, including second generation antipsychotics.Keywords: Antipsychotics, Drug shortages, Psychotropic switching, Medication utilization, Komodo® Healthcare Map, Prescription claims analysis

Background

Antipsychotic medications are the first-line pharmacotherapies for many psychiatric conditions, including schizophrenia and acute psychosis.1 Unfortunately, there is significant heterogeneity in antipsychotic response; approximately 1 in 3 people with schizophrenia will experience symptom persistence despite trialing multiple antipsychotics.2 Selecting a maintenance antipsychotic is often shaped by clinical assessment of patients' comorbidities, financial barriers, and medication adverse effect profiles. First-generation antipsychotics are effective in treating positive symptoms of schizophrenia and are often less expensive; however, they are typically associated with extrapyramidal symptoms. Second-generation antipsychotics often pose a lower risk of movement disorders, but many are associated with metabolic disturbances.3 Ultimately, schizophrenia often follows a heterogeneous course, with some individuals having sustained periods of improved functioning with minimal pharmacotherapy, while many require life-long treatment to mitigate psychotic symptoms and prevent psychiatric decompensation.

Over the last decade, the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has declared shortages or discontinuations for first-generation antipsychotics, creating an underexplored gap in understanding the impact on utilization.4 Shortages can force individuals to switch or discontinue an effective maintenance antipsychotic, potentially compromising treatment outcomes. We aimed to describe how antipsychotic utilization patterns changed after an FDA-declared antipsychotic shortage or discontinuation.

Methods

We performed a retrospective, descriptive, pre-post observational study of FDA-declared first-generation antipsychotic drug shortages or discontinuations in the United States using administrative claims data. We used nationally representative data from the Komodo Healthcare Map® from January 2016 to September 2023. These data contain insurance claims for 330 million lives in the United States from over 150 private and government payers and include tables with patient demographics and monthly insurance eligibility, medical (inpatient and non-inpatient) and pharmacy events. Shortages were identified using digital archived data from the FDA Drug Shortages Database.

Individuals were included in the analysis if they had an inpatient visit claim with a diagnosis of a psychotic spectrum disorder (e.g., schizophrenia), paranoid personality disorder, or schizoid personality disorder, as characterized by International Classification of Disease 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes (F20, F21, F22, F23, F24, F25, F28, F29, F600, F601). Individuals were also required to have six months of continuous treatment (medication possession ratio [MPR] ≥ 80%) with an oral first-generation antipsychotic that experienced an FDA-declared discontinuation or shortage during the study period, as well as at least one prescription claim for any antipsychotic in the 12 months following the discontinuation or shortage date (index date) (Fig. 1). Individuals were excluded if they had an inpatient psychiatric hospitalization in the six-month pre-index period.

Study design.

The primary outcome was the proportion of patients who experienced an antipsychotic switch following the index date. Switching was defined as use of a new antipsychotic during the 12-month post-index period, with a MPR of at least 80%. The antipsychotic was considered “new” if there were no claims for it in the six-month pre-index period. An individual was considered to have switched even if there were claims for their original drug in the post-index period. Additionally, we observed the proportion of individuals on each antipsychotic who switched, the number of switches (0, 1, 2, or ≥ 3), the time to their first new antipsychotic claim after index, and what antipsychotics were used in the post-index period. The analyses were performed using R Studio, version 4.2.1 (RStudio, PBC, Boston, MA).

Results

We identified shortages or discontinuations for all oral first-generation antipsychotics commercially available in the U.S. during the study period. Despite a shortage, certain medications were still commercially available because multiple companies manufactured the product. Thioridazine, molindone, thiothixene, and trifluoperazine were products identified with limited or single manufacturers at the time of the declared shortage or discontinuation. The Komodo Healthcare Map® sample included 95,968 individuals. Utilization by treatment was: haloperidol (n = 52,344, 54.5%), perphenazine (n = 16,966, 17.7%), fluphenazine (n = 13,152, 13.7%), thioridazine (n = 3894, 4.1%), loxapine (n = 3712, 3.9%), trifluoperazine (n = 2737, 2.9%), thiothixene (n = 2000, 2.9%), pimozide (n = 1156, 1.2%), molindone (n = 7, 0.007%). The mean age of the sample was 52.6 years (standard deviation [SD]: 15 years), 51,526 (53.7%) of the sample were male, and 53,992 (56.3%) were enrolled in Medicare for their prescription drug insurance. A total of 8763 participants (9.13%) experienced an antipsychotic switch following the index-date of the declared shortage or discontinuation (Table 2). The time to the first switch claim ranged from 22 (SD: 21) to 103 (SD: 66) days following the shortage or discontinuation date. Switching was most common among those prescribed thiothixene (32.8%), trifluoperazine (55.2%), and molindone (100%). Across all medication groups, more than 85% of documented switches were to one drug in the post-index period. Most individuals were switched to a second-generation antipsychotic with risperidone (18.1%), quetiapine (13.6%), olanzapine (11.7%), aripiprazole (11.6%), and perphenazine (9.1%) being the most common medications switched to across those switching treatment.

Discussion

We conducted a descriptive, pre-post analysis of antipsychotic medication utilization patterns after an FDA-declared shortage or discontinuation among individuals with psychotic spectrum disorders. We found that first-generation antipsychotic shortages or discontinuations were followed by a period of medication switching among some individuals. Specifically, we found that patients prescribed thiothixene, trifluoperazine, and molindone initiated a new antipsychotic after a shortage or discontinuation, and that the switch was typically to a second-generation antipsychotic.

Drug shortages and discontinuations may have important consequences in this patient population, as undertreatment (i.e., taking ineffective medication or being non-adherent to effective medication) is associated with psychiatric hospitalization and relapse.8 Due to the heterogeneity of clinical outcomes to treatment, patients who are forced to modify their maintenance antipsychotic therapy may switch to agents that are less effective or cause new adverse effects. For example, patients who switch from a first-generation antipsychotic to a second-generation antipsychotic may develop new metabolic sequelae or anticholinergic side effects. These new side effects could negatively impact a patient's perception of treatment, and in turn contribute to medication non-adherence.3 Of note, drug shortages may also encourage prescribers to switch their patients to newer treatments that are better aligned with contemporary practices.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to observe antipsychotic utilization patterns following antipsychotic drug shortages or discontinuations. Previous studies have focused on the impact of drug shortages in other clinical areas. One study referencing media reported data found that an FDA-declared national shortage of mixed amphetamine salts in October 2022 resulted in an 11% decline in the average monthly dispensing rate of amphetamine/dextroamphetamine salts.5 Additionally, in a national survey assessing the effect of oncology drug shortages in 68 US clinical practice sites, 75% of respondents reported that these shortages resulted in treatment delays, reduced doses, or having to transition patients to alternative regimens.6 Similarly, a small Australian-based study of people with ulcerative colitis found an association between a national drug shortage and medication switching.7 Our findings align with previous literature in suggesting that medication utilization patterns may change following a shortage.

One major strength of our study is the potential generalizability of our findings. The Komodo Healthcare Map® captures insurance claims data from a socioeconomically diverse sample of over 330 million adults in the United States. Additionally, an MPR threshold of ≥80% was utilized to define a clinically meaningful level of adherence in the pre- and post-index periods, allowing us to confidently infer that patients meeting this criterion underwent a medication switch. This threshold aligns with established standards in adherence research and ensures robustness in classifying treatment transitions.

Nevertheless, this study has limitations. First, given the descriptive nature of the analysis, we cannot make associative or causal claims between FDA-declared drug shortages and switch events in our sample. Second, shortages and discontinuations are often dose specific and time variant, which may not render a medication completely unavailable during declared shortages. Next, since our claims dataset did not include patient-specific outcomes, we were unable to account for switching due to factors unrelated to the drug shortage (such as adverse effects, treatment inefficacy, financial barriers, and patient preference for a new treatment or alternate drug formulation). Additionally, patients could meet the criteria for antipsychotic switching even if they had claims for the original first-generation antipsychotic during the post-index period. Thus, some treatment changes may not represent true switching.

Conclusion

In this descriptive study of individuals treated with first-generation antipsychotics, our results suggest that FDA-declared drug shortages or discontinuations could impact antipsychotic utilization patterns among adults with psychotic spectrum disorders. This study provides a preliminary approach for analyzing psychiatric drug shortages. Future research should attempt to link claims data with electronic health records to identify potential associations between shortages, forced switches, and clinical outcomes related to both efficacy and tolerability.
