Protocol for luminescent nanocrystal synthesis via modified ligand-assisted precipitation and X-ray scintillation film fabrication
Authors: Azimet A Karluk, Javeed Mahmood, Cafer T Yavuz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe field of all-inorganic metal halide nanocrystals currently faces the challenge of developing efficient approaches for the scalable, facile, and stable synthesis of nanocrystals (NCs). Here, we present a protocol for the acid-mediated modified ligand-assisted reprecipitation (LARP) synthesis of undoped and Sb-doped Rb3InCl6 nanocrystals. We describe the key steps for glassware and labware cleaning, precursor solution preparation, rapid injection-driven nanocrystal formation, purification and redispersion, and scintillator composite film fabrication. We further detail structural, optical, and X-ray imaging characterization procedures, providing a comprehensive workflow from nanocrystal synthesis to scintillator screen evaluation.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Karluk et al.1Subject areas: Energy, Chemistry, Material sciences

Before you begin

Innovation

This protocol describes the synthesis of Sb-doped Rb3InCl6 nanocrystals (NCs) via a modified ligand-assisted reprecipitation (LARP) method using an acid-mediated strategy. The procedure enables precise control over doping concentration and nanocrystal uniformity, while maintaining excellent colloidal stability and photoluminescence characteristics. The synthesis avoids the complexity and low yield of the traditional hot-injection technique,2,3,4 as well as the limitations of the conventional LARP method5,6 in synthesizing chloride-based all inorganic NCs, making it scalable and reproducible. The resulting NCs exhibited a high photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY), large Stokes shift, and strong radioluminescence. Additionally, the protocol includes the fabrication of a composite scintillator film with high spatial resolution and a linear X-ray response, which is suitable for medical imaging applications. CRITICAL: The entire protocol should be conducted with strict adherence to laboratory safety guidelines for each chemical reagent and its by-products (e.g., proper facilities, use of personal protective equipment (PPE), and chemical waste disposal procedures). Although this study avoids toxic lead-based materials, it involves the use of strong acids (e.g., hydrochloric acid), volatile solvents (e.g., methanol and hexane), and metal halide salts (e.g., InCl3 and SbCl3), all of which require appropriate handling. Hydrochloric acid (HCl) is highly corrosive and can cause serious burns or respiratory irritation upon contact or inhalation. Methanol is toxic and flammable and its exposure can affect the central nervous system. Hexane is a volatile organic compound that poses an inhalation risk and can cause skin and eye irritation. Metal chlorides can also pose hazards via ingestion or prolonged exposure. To mitigate risks, it is essential to wear gloves, lab coats, and eye protection, and to work in a well-ventilated fume hood during all experimental procedures. The handling of concentrated HCl and organic solvents requires particular care to avoid chemical burns and inhalation of vapors. All chemical waste should be collected and disposed of by following institutional hazardous waste protocols. Additionally, establishing a Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) and conducting a Job Hazard Analysis (JHA) are strongly recommended to ensure consistent, safe practices. These should include clear instructions for handling chemicals, emergency response procedures, and spill containment measures. Personnel must be trained on the associated hazards, have access to Safety Data Sheets (SDSs), and properly label all reagents and containers to ensure traceability and safety. By following these safety practices, researchers can minimize health risks and maintain a safe laboratory environment while working with these materials.

Purification of nanocrystals

Timing: 20–25 min

This step outlines the purification of synthesized nanocrystals to remove unreacted precursors and excess ligands.

6.Purification of nanocrystals via centrifugation and redispersion.a.Centrifuge the reaction mixture at ∼5,000 × g (7500 rpm, Megafuge™ 8 centrifuge; rotor-dependent) for 5 min.b.Carefully discard the supernatant and centrifuge the remaining white precipitate for an additional 2 min to remove any residual liquid.c.Redisperse the precipitate in 3 mL of hexane, then centrifuge at ∼3,000 × g (5500 rpm, Megafuge™ 8 centrifuge; rotor-dependent) for 5 min. Discard the supernatant to eliminate excess surface ligands.d.Redisperse the resulting white precipitate in 3.5 mL of hexane and centrifuge again at ∼3,000 × g for 5 min.e.Collect the final supernatant and filter through a 0.22 μm PTFE syringe filter to obtain a clear dispersion of purified nanocrystals (Figure 2).

Expected outcomes

We have developed a straightforward and scalable approach for synthesizing Sb-doped Rb3InCl6 nanocrystals and fabricating high-quality composite scintillator thin films. This method offers precise compositional control, reproducibility, and compatibility with low-cost solution processing, eliminating the need for high-temperature reactions, sophisticated setups, glove boxes, and inert conditions.

Following film fabrication, detailed structural and morphological characterizations, such as X-ray diffraction (XRD) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM), can be used to confirm the successful formation of nanocrystals and their uniform distribution within the PSU matrix. These results, along with photoluminescence and X-ray imaging tests, help validate the optical quality and X-ray sensitivity of the film (Figure 4).

The uniform morphology and narrow size distribution observed in the TEM images (Figures 4B and 4C) further confirmed the high crystallinity and monodispersity of the synthesized nanocrystals.

After the successful synthesis of Sb-doped Rb3InCl6 nanocrystals and fabrication of the composite scintillator thin films, their optical properties were evaluated through photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy and time-resolved photoluminescence measurements (Figure 5). All the measurements were performed under ambient conditions (∼50% RH). The specific equipment and instruments used for these characterizations are listed in the key resources table.

Note: The PLE and PL spectra of the Rb3InCl6:Sb nanocrystals were measured using 320 nm excitation and 510 nm emission wavelengths. The doped nanocrystals exhibited a characteristic PLE doublet at 321 nm and 342 nm. The PL decay behavior was analyzed using a bi-exponential fitting model, A(t) = A1exp(−t/τ1) + A2exp(−t/τ2), where A(t) is the absorbance (or signal) at time t, A1and A2are the amplitudes of the two exponential components, τ1and τ2are the corresponding decay time constants, and t is the time variable. Notably, the average PL lifetime of Rb3InCl6: Sb nanocrystals increased markedly upon 5% Sb doping, from 257 ns (undoped) to 1.65 μs, indicating an over six-fold enhancement in exciton lifetime due to the incorporation of Sb3+ ions. This substantial increase highlights the critical role of Sb doping in enhancing the optical performance of the nanocrystals, clearly distinguishing the photophysical behavior of the doped material from that of its undoped counterpart.

The photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) is a crucial parameter for assessing the optical performance of nanocrystal materials, particularly for applications in optoelectronic and scintillation devices. PLQY represents the ratio of the number of photons emitted to the number of photons absorbed, providing direct insight into the radiative efficiency of a material.7 A higher PLQY indicates fewer non-radiative losses and superior light-emitting efficiency, which is essential for scintillator performance in X-ray imaging applications.8,9

For the synthesized Sb-doped Rb3InCl6 nanocrystals, PLQY measurements were conducted using an integrating sphere setup under 320 nm excitation. The Sb-doped samples (5% Sb3+) showed significant enhancement, reaching a PLQY of ∼68.1% (Figure 6). This marked increase is attributed to the role of Sb3+ ions in passivating surface traps and facilitating radiative recombination processes, which is consistent with the extended PL lifetime previously observed. These results highlight the critical role of compositional engineering in the optimization of the optical properties of lead-free halide nanocrystals.

By optimizing the fabrication parameters, a flexible and optically transparent composite scintillator film with a thickness of ∼200 μm was successfully obtained. This film incorporated 70 wt.% Rb3InCl6:Sb nanocrystals uniformly embedded within the PSU matrix.

To assess the X-ray detection performance of the fabricated scintillation film, a standard indirect X-ray imaging setup was utilized. This setup comprises the following components: an X-ray tube source, an object holder, a scintillator film, a reflector mirror, and a CCD camera for digital image capture. The emitted scintillation light from the nanocrystal film is converted into a visible image, which is recorded and analyzed digitally (Figure 7).

The spatial resolution of the scintillator was determined using a standard X-ray line pair phantom. The results indicate a resolution of up to 20 line pairs per millimeter (lp mm−1). Notably, the film exhibits a clearly distinguishable boundary across the 5–20 lp mm−1 range, demonstrating excellent imaging fidelity (Figure 8). This confirms the high spatial resolution and effective X-ray detection capabilities of the newly developed Rb3InCl6:Sb nanocrystals@PSU scintillator screen.

Limitations

One of the main limitations of this synthesis is the high sensitivity of both the SbCl3 and InCl3 precursors to moisture and ambient conditions, which will affect the quality of the resulting Sb-doped Rb3InCl6 nanocrystals. Therefore, all weighing steps should be performed in a glovebox, maintaining an inert atmosphere (N2 or Ar) with O2 and H2O levels below 1 ppm, at ambient temperature, and under slightly positive pressure to prevent air ingress and the dissolution. The injection processes must be carried out rapidly to minimize exposure and prevent undesired hydrolysis or phase degradation. Specifically, the time from completion of weighing to the preparation of Solution B should not exceed 2–3 min, and after Solution B is prepared, it should be injected into the reaction system within 2–3 min to avoid decomposition or phase changes.

Troubleshooting

The strategy employed for synthesizing Sb-doped Rb3InCl6 nanocrystals and their subsequent integration into polymer composite scintillators offers a promising route for the development of lead-free, high-resolution X-ray imaging materials. This scalable and environmentally conscious approach opens new possibilities for tuning the optical and structural properties of nanocrystals through the precise control of the precursor composition, reaction environment, and matrix compatibility. However, achieving reproducible results requires careful mindfulness throughout the synthesis and film fabrication processes. To assist researchers in navigating potential setbacks, the following troubleshooting section outlines common issues encountered and offers practical solutions to ensure consistent performance and film quality.

Problem 1

Upon mixing InCl3 and SbCl3 with HCl, visible precipitation or turbidity may occur, which can negatively affect nanocrystal nucleation and size distribution (Step 3).

Potential solution

Carefully monitor and control HCl concentration. Maintain it below the critical threshold (0.19 mg μL−1). If precipitation occurs, discard the solution and remake it using a lower HCl volume. Ultrasonication for 5 min after adding HCl can help achieve complete dissolution.

Problem 2

Nanocrystals show weak luminescence or a broad size distribution, which may be caused by inaccurate injection speed, incorrect precursor and ligand ratios, or insufficient mixing during the reaction.

Potential solution

Ensure the rapid and consistent injection of precursor solution B into the stirring antisolvent (Solution A) at 1500 RPM. Double-check the precursor concentrations and volumes before synthesis. The ligand ratio is also crucial, as it affects the growth of NCs.

Problem 3

The resulting nanocrystal@PSU composite film exhibits surface defects, non-uniform thickness, or poor transparency, which may be attributed to disturbances during drying, inadequate nanocrystal dispersion, or rapid solvent evaporation (Step 7).

Potential solution

During drying, place the mold in a stable, vibration-free, and dust-free area away from airflow or direct light. Use gradual and thorough stirring (12 h) to ensure homogeneous nanocrystal dispersion in the PSU matrix. Avoid abrupt temperature changes, which may lead to uneven film formation.

Problem 4

The measured PLQY of the Sb-doped Rb3InCl6 nanocrystals is significantly lower than expected, which indicates suboptimal optical performance. This problem is most likely caused by the presence of surface defects or incomplete doping, which creates non-radiative recombination centers.

Potential solution

Optimizing the Sb doping ratio and concentrations beyond or below the optimal (∼5%) may lead to defect-induced quenching. Ensure sufficient purification steps are taken to remove excess ligands or by-products. Consider post-synthesis surface passivation or gentle annealing to improve PLQY.

Problem 5

Nanocrystals tend to agglomerate during incorporation into the PSU matrix, leading to uneven distribution and compromised film optical clarity. This is caused by poor compatibility between NC surface ligands and the PSU matrix or inadequate dispersion in DCM (Step 7).

Potential solution

Before mixing with PSU-DCM, ensure that NCs are thoroughly dispersed in ethyl acetate through ultrasonication. If aggregation persists, mild ligand exchange is considered to improve solubility or use a co-solvent system that enhances compatibility. Always perform the film casting slowly and in a controlled environment to minimize clustering.
