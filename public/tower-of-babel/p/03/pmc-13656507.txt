Regulatory T cell levels in migraine: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Emily Stuchfield-Denby, Samuel Adelou, Xavier Moisset, Radhouane Dallel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough recent research suggests that regulatory T (Treg) cells may be involved in migraine pathophysiology, no review has provided a comprehensive assessment of their levels in this condition. This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to assess Treg levels in migraine patients.MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed, Embase and Cochrane on July 5, 2024 (updated on March 19, 2026), and included all original studies providing data on Treg levels in migraine.ResultsAmong 1,162 studies identified, five were included in the narrative review and four in the meta-analysis. Data were pooled using a random-effects model. Standardized mean differences (SMD) were calculated to compare peripheral whole blood Treg levels between migraine and control groups. Our narrative review showed decreased Treg levels in three of five studies comparing migraine patients and controls, and in two of three studies on interictal episodic migraine patients and controls. Our meta-analysis of few but carefully selected data suggested a significant decrease in Treg cell levels in migraine (SMD − 4.21 [-7.76; -0.66], p = 0.02, I²=98.3%).ConclusionsOur findings suggest Treg levels are decreased in migraine and of potential interest in understanding migraine pathophysiology. However, the limited data available warrant further investigations to shed light on their relevance and therapeutic potential in this condition.Trial registrationPROSPERO CRD42023490445.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s10194-026-02443-7.

Introduction

Migraine is a complex neurological condition characterized by recurrent mild to severe headache attacks, affecting 14–15% of the population worldwide [1, 2]. Each year, 2.5% of these migraine sufferers transition to chronic migraine – defined by 15 or more headache days per month – accounting for 5.4 to 9.5% of migraine patients and approximately 1.2% of the general population [3, 4]. Despite significant advances in understanding migraine pathophysiology, the mechanisms involved in migraine triggering and chronification remain unclear. Studies have shown an increased prevalence of migraine, and even chronic migraine, in patients with chronic inflammatory diseases [5–7], suggesting potential immune dysregulations in this condition. Regulatory T (Treg) cells play a crucial role in maintaining peripheral tolerance, preventing autoimmunity, and controlling chronic inflammation [8]. Defects in Treg cell numbers and/or functions have been well documented in numerous autoimmune diseases [9, 10]. However, few studies have addressed Treg cell levels in migraine, with contradictory results, and none have investigated Treg cell functions in this condition. A meta-analysis evaluated Treg cell levels in migraine patients compared to controls, but contained text/figure and data inconsistencies [11], and other narrative reviews only partially described Treg cell involvement in the disease [12–14]. In this systematic review and meta-analysis of rigorously selected studies with appropriate methodology, we aim to provide a comprehensive and reliable assessment of the existing data regarding Treg levels in migraine.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) 2020 statement [15] and was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42023490445).

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted across PubMed, Embase and Cochrane (July 5, 2024; updated on March 19, 2026), using tailored search algorithms (Appendix 1). To ensure completeness, the reference lists of all included studies, as well as selected literature reviews and meta-analyses, were manually screened to identify any additional relevant publications.

Study selection

We included all original studies comparing Treg levels in Human biological samples from subjects with migraine, with no restriction regarding publication date. Studies were included in our narrative review if they reported Treg levels as categorical variables – classified as increased, unchanged or decreased. Studies providing Treg levels (as percentage of total cells, absolute counts, or graphical data) were considered in the meta-analysis. Studies were not included if they were book chapters, conference papers, letters, case reports, ongoing clinical trials, reviews or meta-analyses. The study selection process was discussed beforehand between E.S.D., X.M. and R.D. Systematic reviewing was conducted by E.S.D. and X.M, independently; any disagreements were discussed and resolved with R.D.

Data collection and outcomes

Data were extracted to a standardised worksheet. The following items were extracted from each study, where available: study details, participants characteristics, characteristics of migraine, sample type and characteristics, Treg levels (available numerically, recalculated pooling different groups, or deduced from graphs when unavailable numerically), method used for Treg measurement, and data relating to the Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network (SIGN) items. When the mean and/or standard deviation were not available, they were estimated according to the median and/or standard error of the mean (SEM) [16], or recalculated pooling different groups when subgroup data were available. If missing data prevented the inclusion of a study, we attempted to collect them by contacting the authors. The primary outcome for this review was Treg cell levels, reported and treated as categorical (qualitative assessment for the narrative review) and/or continuous variables (quantitative assessment). The secondary outcome was Treg cell levels according to migraine states: presence of aura (migraine with aura [MA] versus migraine without aura [MO]), migraine disorder (interictal episodic migraine versus controls), ictal migraine (ictal versus interictal migraine) and migraine chronification (interictal episodic migraine versus interictal chronic migraine); Treg levels were reported and treated as categorical (narrative review) and/or continuous variables (quantitative assessment).

Risk of bias assessment

Studies meeting the inclusion criteria were graded for methodological quality and assessed for internal validity using SIGN methodology checklists for case-control studies (n°4) [17].

Certainty-of-evidence assessment

Certainty-of-evidence was assessed using the GRADE tool [18].

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics (number of subjects, sex ratio, age, migraine characteristics, sample characteristics, and method used for Treg measurement) were summarized for each study sample and reported as mean and standard deviation and numbers (%) for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. Qualitative analysis described Treg levels as increased, unchanged or decreased. Meta-analyses took account of between- and within-study variability. To account for the clustering of participants within studies and to capture variability at both the within- and between-study levels (Treg quantification using different laboratory methods and reporting scales), we used random-effects models instead of simple statistical tests to assess standardized mean differences (SMD, using Hedge’s g effect size) and their 95% confidence intervals for Treg cells, employing the DerSimonian and Laird approach. SMD were estimated to assess the differences between groups (migraine or episodic migraine versus controls) and according to migraine characteristics (episodic or chronic migraine, MA or MO, and in the ictal versus interictal phase). Hedge’s g is considered to be a conservative estimate, which is useful for studies with small sample sizes, and the results may be interpreted as reflecting a small (g = 0.2–0.5), medium (g = 0.5–0.8), or large effect (g > 0.8). These estimates reflect the relative magnitude of group differences across studies rather than an absolute biological difference in Treg cell levels. Heterogeneity in the study results was assessed by forest plots (estimates and their 95% confidence intervals) and several indexes such as I². More precisely, I² is a common metric for measuring the magnitude of between-study heterogeneity and can be interpretated as followed: I² < 25%: low, I² between 25 and 50%: moderate, and I² > 50%: high. In order to account for the small number of studies available and to make the influence of each study included visible, leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata software (version 15, StataCorp, College Station, TX, US). Two-sided type I error was fixed at 5%. Results were interpreted according to the aforementioned rules of thumbs with caution due to the number of studies and/or number of patients for some studies. These analyses were consequently considered as exploratory. Figures were created using Stata software (version 15, StataCorp, College Station, TX, US) and R (version 4.5.0, R Core Team (2024). _R: A Language and Environment for Statistical Computing_. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria. https://www.R-project.org/.

Results

Characteristics

Our systematic search provided 1,162 results, and 17 additional records were identified through citation screening. All reports sought for retrieval are listed in Additional Table 1. After assessment, six studies were included from the systematic search [19–24], and no additional studies were collected via reference screening (Fig. 1, additional records not represented). All samples collected in these studies were peripheral (as opposed to blood taken from the jugular vein) whole blood from patients with spontaneous migraine (as opposed to experimentally induced migraine). Overall, five out of six studies involved only adults, and one study involved children (Table 1 and Additional Table 2). The SIGN assessment revealed sufficient quality for all articles included, and overall similar quality between studies (Additional Table 3).

Analysis

To avoid age bias in Treg levels, our analyses were performed on CD4+ Treg levels in adults only. Five studies were included in the narrative review [20–24], of which four were suitable for the meta-analysis [20–22, 24] (Table 1).

Narrative review

Overall, out of five articles (adding up to a total of 170 migraine subjects and 144 control individuals), three reported decreased proportions of Treg cells [20–22] (mixing both chronic and episodic MA and MO in two studies [20, 22]), and two showed unchanged proportions of Treg cells in migraine patients versus controls [23, 24]. Although reporting unchanged total Treg levels in interictal migraine patients versus controls (mixing chronic and episodic MO), Nurkhametova et al. revealed decreased proportions of CD39-positive Treg subpopulations in migraine patients [24]. When assessing different migraine states, no difference in Treg proportions was found in migraine patients with versus without aura (two publications [21, 22]). Three articles studied Treg proportions in interictal episodic migraine patients versus controls, of which two showed decreased [21, 22] and one unchanged levels [23] of Treg cells in migraine. Finally, one study compared Treg proportions in chronic versus episodic migraine and revealed no significant difference [22] (Fig. 2).

Meta-analysis

Our analysis of Treg levels in migraine patients (mixing chronic and episodic MA and MO) versus controls was based on four publications [20–22, 24] (adding up to a total of 121 migraine subjects and 94 control individuals), and showed a significant decrease in Treg levels in migraine versus controls (four studies, SMD − 4.21 [-7.76; -0.66], p = 0.02, I² = 98.3%, Fig. 3), and decreased Treg levels in MA compared to MO (two studies [21, 22], SMD − 0.46 [-0.88; -0.03], p = 0.035, I²=0.00%) (Additional Fig. 1). Too few publications were available to assess all migraine states (interictal episodic migraine, ictal and interictal, and episodic and chronic migraine). Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis revealed that the pooled effect direction remained consistent in three out of four analyses, though the magnitude varied across exclusions. Excluding Faraji et al. was the only removal that significantly altered the overall effect, rendering it non-significant, and highlighting its influence on the pooled estimate. Exclusion of Arumugam et al. and Li et al. caused the confidence interval to marginally cross zero while still yielding substantial effect sizes. Finally, removing Nurkhametova et al. pulled the pooled estimate further from zero (Additional Fig. 2). Raw data used for the meta-analysis are available in Additional Table 4.

Certainty of evidence

According to the GRADE tool, certainty-of-evidence was overall considered very low.

Discussion

This study suggests Treg levels may be decreased in migraine and of potential interest in understanding migraine pathophysiology.

Our narrative review enabled comparison of Treg levels across different migraine states, revealing predominantly reduced Treg proportions in migraine (three out of five studies reporting decreased proportions [20–22], with a fourth one showing reduced CD39+ Treg cells [24]), particularly in interictal episodic migraine (two [21, 22] out of three studies).

A prior meta-analysis reported decreased levels of Treg cells in individuals with migraine, but the analysis did not reach statistical significance and contained data inconsistencies [11]. Our meta-analysis, based on the same four articles [20–22, 24], showed a significant decrease in Treg cell levels in migraine participants compared to controls. Additionally, the comparison between groups of patients with MA and MO provided evidence of lower Treg levels in MA versus MO (two studies [21, 22]).

Implications of Treg cells in migraine pathophysiology

Treg cells play a key role in balancing inflammation, by engaging in several pathways, such as anti-inflammatory cytokine production (e.g., transforming growth factor-beta [TGF-β] and interleukin-10 [IL-10]), interleukin-2 (IL-2) sequestration [8], or by converting adenosine triphosphate (ATP) into adenosine, through surface enzymes CD39 and CD73, resulting in anti-inflammatory [25] and potential anti-nociceptive effects [26]. Imbalanced Treg and pro-inflammatory effector T (Teff) cell proportions have been associated with autoimmune disease pathophysiology [27], and elevated ATP levels have been suggested to play a role in long-lasting pain in migraine [28].

Recent animal studies have shown a Treg/Teff imbalance, with reduced Treg cell-related protein expression (forkhead box P3 [FOXP3] and Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 5 [STAT5]) and increased Teff cell related protein expression (Retinoic acid receptor-related-Orphan-Receptor-gamma t [RORγt] and Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3 [STAT3]) in the trigeminal nucleus caudalis of rats in a model of nitroglycerin-induced chronic migraine [29]. Other animal studies have revealed the potential of low-dose-IL-2 therapy in increasing trigeminal ganglia and blood levels of Treg cells [30] and in reducing pain hypersensitivity through the IL-10 and TGF-β pathways in mouse models of chronic migraine [31].

However, very little attention has been given to the role of Treg cells in human migraine pathophysiology. Our recent systematic review and meta-analysis, evidencing different cytokine profiles across migraine states, revealed increased peripheral blood levels of IL-6 and TGF-β in migraine patients compared to control participants, further supporting a possible Treg/Teff imbalance in this condition [32]. Our work points towards decreased Treg cell levels in migraine patients, but the implication of Treg cells may not be limited to their numbers. Indeed, despite showing unchanged total Treg proportions in migraine patients and controls, Nurkhametova et al. revealed reduced Treg expression of CD39 and CD73 in patients with migraine. This suggests that, besides a potential imbalance in Treg levels, phenotypic changes in Treg cells may lead to reduced suppressive functions in the disease [24]. Another recent study evidenced an increase in CD62L expression on Treg cells in migraine patients compared to controls, and a decrease in CD45RA expression on Treg cells in chronic versus episodic migraine, further suggesting phenotypic and functional changes in Treg cells in migraine [33]. By failing to regulate inflammation, Treg cells could contribute to migraine pain. Further investigations are needed, both to clarify their pathophysiological role in migraine, and to evaluate their potential as targets for novel therapeutic strategies for patients with refractory migraine.

Strength and limits

This review is subject to several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. The number of available studies was limited (four studies available), with small sample sizes and unidirectional effects that were not always consistent across comparisons. The DerSimonian-Laird estimator may provide overly narrow confidence intervals when the number of studies is very small, although alternative approaches also remain limited when the evidence base is extremely small and substantial between-study heterogeneity is present. Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis showed high influence of some of the four studies included on the overall effect. Considering the exploratory nature of the quantitative synthesis and the limited robustness of pooled estimates derived from four publications, we believe these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in larger, methodologically homogeneous studies. Another limitation is the heterogeneity in Treg phenotyping across studies, with Treg cells identified as either CD4+CD25+/hi (three studies), CD4+CD25+FoxP3+ (one study) or CD4+CD25+CD127− cells (two studies). Moreover, cytometry gating strategies varied from one study to another, and Treg proportions were defined from different parent populations, which may also induce data heterogeneity (Table 1). While these points may introduce bias in measuring absolute Treg cell levels, the comparison of Treg levels between groups of participants was not affected, as the same method was used for all the participants of the same study. Finally, hormonal fluctuation throughout the menstrual cycle is known to affect various immune parameters, including Treg cell levels [34]. However, none of the available studies reported the menstrual cycle phase at the time of blood sampling in female participants, making it difficult to control for hormonal influence. The heterogeneity and incomplete reporting regarding the use of prophylactic anti-migraine treatments, immunosuppressive or anti-inflammatory therapies, as well as the presence of comorbidities and variations in body mass index, may have also contributed to the variability observed across studies.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest a potential reduction in Treg cell proportions in migraine. Because of the limited data available, our findings should be interpreted cautiously, and viewed as an exploratory attempt at summarizing existing knowledge on the implications of Treg cells in migraine pathophysiology. Further investigations are needed to confirm this observation. Given their critical role in balancing inflammation and immune responses, a better understanding of Treg cell involvement in migraine pathophysiology could reveal new therapeutic opportunities for patients with migraine.
