A national multicenter analysis of hospitalization admission records for lymphatic malformation in China from 2016 to 2023
Authors: Lin Mei, Linyue Sun, Xiaodan Li, Nian Sun, Qiaoyin Liu, Xuexi Zhang, Zhiyong Liu, Yiran Meng, Ge Zhang, Shengcai Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLymphatic malformation (LM) is a low-flow vascular malformation caused by the abnormal development of the lymphatic system. This study aimed to provide an update on pediatric LM by investigating its clinical characteristics and hospitalization burden in multiple medical centers across China.MethodsThis study utilized data from the Futang Research Center of Pediatric Development (FRCPD). All hospitalization records from January 1, 2016, to December 31, 2023 (N = 10,974,595) were screened to identify children with LM. For these patients, a comprehensive analysis of clinical characteristics (e.g., demographics, lesion location, treatment, comorbidity/complication and ICU admissions) hospitalization burden (i.e., length of stay and total cost) was conducted.ResultsOur study comprised 24,207 inpatients with LM, predominantly from the eastern region. The typical hospitalized patient was a child aged 1–3 years or on trunk involvement. Strikingly, sclerotherapy was 1.57 times more common than surgical resection. The most frequent comorbidity/complication were infectious diseases. Treatment patterns also varied by anatomy: lesions in the trunk favored surgical resection, while other areas usually undergo sclerotherapy. Critically, hospitalization burden differed significantly based on lesion site, treatment received, and the presence of comorbidity and complication.ConclusionsBy analyzing a national cohort of pediatric inpatients with LM, this study provides contemporary insights into the clinical characteristics and hospitalization burden of this condition in China. These findings underscore the substantial healthcare impact of LM and are critical for informing future healthcare strategies and resource allocation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06881-9.Keywords: Hospital admission records, Lymphatic malformation (LM), Clinical characteristics, Hospitalization burden

Introduction

Lymphatic malformation (LM) is a slow-flow vascular malformation classified by the International Society for the Study of Vascular Anomalies (ISSVA). It represents a congenital developmental abnormality of the lymphatic system and is frequently driven by somatic mosaic mutations. Clinically, LM may present as macrocystic, microcystic, or mixed lesions, and in some cases may manifest as complex lymphatic anomalies involving the central lymphatic system and multiple organs [1]. According to the International Society for the Study of Vascular Anomalies (ISSVA) classification, LM are categorized into three morphological types based on cyst size: macrocystic (> 2 cm), microcystic (< 2 cm), and mixed, which combines features of both [2]. The estimated incidence ranges from 1 in 6,000 to 1 in 16,000 live births [3], with an overall prevalence of approximately 1 in 4,000 [4].

As LM progressively enlarges or involves anatomically critical regions, it may compress adjacent structures and give rise to a wide range of complications, including airway compromise, macroglossia, dysphagia, feeding difficulties, visual impairment, and compression of thoracic or abdominal organs. Owing to the unique anatomical characteristics of the head and neck region, lesions in this area are particularly prone to causing respiratory distress, swallowing dysfunction, speech abnormalities, and cosmetic deformity. In addition, the lesions may be complicated by recurrent inflammation or cellulitis, presenting with local pain, erythema, and swelling, which can further aggravate functional impairment and increase treatment complexity. Current treatment strategies mainly include sclerotherapy and surgical intervention. Although both approaches are effective, neither consistently achieves optimal outcomes, and both remain associated with incomplete resection, recurrence, and various adverse effects [5].

Subjects and methods

The Futang Research Center of Pediatric Development (FRCPD) is a large, non-profit medical consortium in China dedicated to advancing pediatric medical research [6]. Its network comprises 47 tertiary children’s hospitals, 37 of which routinely contribute annual hospitalization records. We conducted a retrospective analysis of this large-scale, multi-center FRCPD database to delineate the clinical characteristics and hospitalization burden of inpatients with LM, thereby providing a comprehensive epidemiological profile of this condition in Chinese children.

Data source and categories of children with LM

Cohort identification commenced with the screening of 10,974,595 admission records from the FRCPD database (2016–2023). Inclusion required a primary diagnosis of LM and that the admission represented the patient’s first hospitalization for the condition. Exclusion was applied to records lacking complete data on demographics, diagnosis, or parameters of hospitalization burden (e.g., length of stay (LOS) and total cost).

Subsequently, the following data were extracted for analysis: patient demographics (gender, age, region), lesion site (head, neck, trunk, limbs, unknown), treatment modality (sclerotherapy, surgical resection), comorbidity/complication (congenital, infectious, circulatory system diseases, and others), ICU admissions (yes or no) and hospitalization burden (LOS and total cost). Patients were categorized into five age groups: <1, 1–3, 4–6, 7–12, and 13–18 years. Geographically, the 37 participating hospitals were classified into seven major regions of China: Northeast, North, East, Northwest, Southwest, South, and Central China.

Statistical analysis

In this study, gender, age, region, lesion site, treatment method, comorbidity/complication were treated as categorical variables, while length of stay (LOS) and total cost were treated as continuous variables. Group comparisons for categorical variables were performed using the Pearson chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables (LOS and total cost) were found to be non-normally distributed by the Shapiro-Wilk test and were therefore compared using non-parametric tests, with post-hoc analyses conducted via the Steel-Dwass test. All analyses were carried out using JMP Pro, version 15.0. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics and disease situation of inpatients with LM

This study retrospectively screened 10,974,595 pediatric admission records from the FRCPD database (2016–2023), identifying 24,207 inpatients with LM (prevalence: 0.22%). As summarized in Table 1, males accounted for a higher proportion (57.97%) than females (42.03%), yielding a male-to-female ratio of 1.39:1. The age distribution was dominated by the 1–3 years subgroup (45.66%), followed by 4–6 years (22.32%), 7–12 years (15.73%), < 1 year (14.17%), and 13–18 years (2.13%). Geographically, the highest case volumes were from East China (38.84%), Central China (20.07%), and North China (13.65%) (Fig. 1).

The regional distribution of hospitalized children with LM

Among the 24,207 admission records, the trunk was the most frequently involved site (27.10%). The primary treatments for LM were sclerotherapy and surgical resection, with sclerotherapy being more commonly performed (ratio:1.57:1). Regarding associated conditions, infectious diseases were the most common comorbidity/complication (N = 417), followed by congenital diseases (N = 381) and circulatory system diseases (N = 188), as detailed in Table 1.

Subgroup analysis of inpatients with LM

A significant male predominance was observed across all lesion sites (χ²=57.55, p < 0.001; Fig. 2A/B, Supplementary Table 1). Analysis of lesion distribution by age revealed that the trunk was the most common site in most age groups. Conversely, the neck was more frequently involved in children aged < 1 year (χ²=378.01, p < 0.001; Fig. 3A, Supplementary Table 1). Furthermore, supplementary analysis showed that 358 of 24,207 hospitalized children with LM (1.48%) required ICU transfer because of critical illness. Truncal LM accounted for the largest proportion of ICU admissions (54.75%), with a statistically significant difference across lesion locations (Table 2, χ²=196.87, p < 0.001).

The hospitalization burden included LOS and total cost of inpatients with LM

Leveraging this multi-center database, we were able to characterize the hospitalization burden associated with LM. As shown in Table 3, inpatients with truncal lesions incurred the highest burden among the lesion site subgroups. Furthermore, the hospitalization burden was significantly greater for surgical resection than for sclerotherapy. The presence of comorbidity/complications was also associated with increased LOS and total cost. Collectively, these results demonstrate that hospitalization burden varies importantly by lesion site, treatment modality, and the presence of comorbidity/complications.

Discussion

Lymphatic malformation (LM) is a congenital, non-proliferative vascular anomaly resulting from aberrant development of the lymphatic system during embryogenesis. It originates from lymphatic endothelial cells (LECs) and demonstrates a predilection for the head, neck, and axillary regions. It typically arises between 6 and 10 weeks of gestation, when immature lymphatic tissues fail to establish normal connections with the venous or lymphatic systems. The incidence of LM is comparable between males and females, with no significant sex-based differences. Clinically, LM usually present as soft, compressible masses consisting of fluid-filled cysts or channels, which are evident at birth or 2 years of age [4]. Consistent with this clinical course, our findings indicate that children aged 1 to 3 years constitute the primary demographic requiring hospitalization for LM management. Moreover, LM may enlarge due to intralesional hemorrhage or infection. Superficial cysts are prone to fluid leakage, which can lead to localized infection, cellulitis, or even systemic sepsis [7]. In line with this, our data identified infectious diseases as the most frequent comorbidity/complication among hospitalized children with LM. Thus, antibiotic therapy remains an essential component of the clinical management of LM.

LM typically develops in anatomical regions rich in lymphatic vessels, such as the head, neck, abdomen, groin, mediastinum, and axilla. Previous studies report that approximately 75% of LMs occur in the head or neck region, where they can cause serious complications including speech disorders, dysphagia, and airway obstruction [8]. In the abdomen, LM is most commonly located in the mesentery, omentum, and mesocolon [9]. Intraperitoneal LM accounts for 3%-9.2% of all cases, while retroperitoneal LM is exceedingly rare, comprising less than 1% [10]. In the present study, the trunk was the most commonly affected region among hospitalized children with LMs(27.10%), which differs from previous reports describing the head and neck as the predominant site of involvement [11]. This discrepancy may be attributable to selection bias inherent to an inpatient database. Specifically, some localized or superficial LMs can be managed in the outpatient setting by laser therapy or local injection, whereas truncal lesions-particularly those involving the retroperitoneum or vital organs—often require more complex evaluation and treatment, with higher procedural risk and greater perioperative management needs. In contrast to head and neck LMs, which are often recognized early because of external deformity or functional compromise, truncal LMs involving the thoracic cavity, abdominal cavity, or retroperitoneum may remain clinically occult until severe complications such as hemorrhage, infection, rupture, intestinal obstruction, or volvulus develop. Consequently, hospitalized cohorts may disproportionately capture truncal LM cases with deeper anatomical involvement and greater disease severity, thereby inflating their apparent proportion. This may partly explain the overrepresentation of truncal LMs in our inpatient cohort. Our findings should therefore be interpreted as reflecting the disease spectrum of hospitalized LMs patients rather than the true anatomical distribution of LMs in the general pediatric population. Furthermore, we fully agree that distinguishing between incidentally detected abdominal LM and complication-driven abdominal LM is clinically important for appropriate interpretation of the findings. In the present study, because our data were derived exclusively from hospitalized patients, the vast majority of included abdominal LM cases are likely to represent the latter category—that is, lesions identified after the development of LM-related symptoms or complications requiring inpatient intervention, such as intestinal obstruction, pain, or infection. This interpretation is also indirectly supported by the relatively high hospitalization burden observed for the abdominal LM subgroup in our dataset. In contrast, incidentally detected abdominal LM without significant symptoms is more likely to be reported in outpatient settings or identified during routine physical examinations.

Lymphatic malformation (LM) is currently managed in clinical practice primarily through sclerotherapy, surgical resection, and laser therapy, with the principal goals of restoring or preserving function and addressing aesthetic concerns [12]. The selection of a specific treatment modality depends on the location, size, and clinical manifestations of the LM. Our findings reflect a clear anatomical preference in treatment selection: sclerotherapy was predominantly used for head/neck and extremity lesions, whereas surgical resection was more frequently applied to truncal lesions.

Surgical resection is mainly indicated for well-localized microcystic or macrocystic LM. In pediatric patients, invasive procedures usually require general anesthesia, and parental concerns regarding repeated anesthetic exposure further limit the use of surgery for purely diagnostic purposes. Therefore, surgical intervention is more often therapeutic than diagnostic in nature. Biopsy is generally reserved for selected situations, including diagnostic uncertainty, the need to exclude tumor, infection, or other vascular anomalies, and suspected complex lymphatic anomalies. In routine practice, surgery performed solely to obtain a diagnostic biopsy is rare. Published case reports further suggest that, in anatomically complex or diffuse infiltrative lymphatic anomalies, biopsy often serves primarily to confirm the diagnosis rather than as part of definitive treatment. For instance, one patient with extensive lingual LM underwent biopsy because the lesion was initially suspected to be a sublingual cyst; after pathological confirmation, conservative management with laser therapy and cryotherapy was chosen over radical excision because of the high risk of functional impairment, resulting in good long-term disease control and functional preservation [13]. Likewise, in diffuse pulmonary lymphangiomatosis/complex lymphatic anomalie's, definitive diagnosis may still require surgical biopsy when clinical and imaging findings are nonspecific, whereas subsequent management may rely on sirolimus for long-term disease control rather than resection [14].

However, complete excision is often challenging due to the complex and infiltrative nature of these lesions. Reported recurrence rates after surgical resection range from 13% to 33%, with a 2–6% risk of permanent nerve damage [15, 16]. Consequently, percutaneous sclerotherapy and pharmacotherapy play a crucial role in managing extensive or deep-seated lesions that are not amenable to surgery. Commonly used sclerosants include 3% sodium tetradecyl sulfate, doxycycline, and bleomycin. A single or limited number of sclerotherapy sessions may be sufficient for macrocystic or localized lesions. However, for microcystic, mixed, anatomically extensive, or functionally high-risk lesions, treatment often requires repeated sclerotherapy and may need to be combined with other modalities, such as surgical debulking, laser/ablative therapy, or oral medication. This is supported by previous studies. For example, in a 14-year single-center cohort, 25 pediatric LM patients underwent a total of 54 sclerotherapy procedures (mean, 2.2 sessions per patient; range, 1–13) [17]. Bouwman et al. reported that mixed LMs were more likely to require repeated sclerotherapy than other subtypes [18]. Zhao et al. also showed that complex head and neck LMs frequently require multimodal, staged, and repeated interventions [19]. Notably, bleomycin is the only sclerosant effective against microcystic disease [20], with one study reporting significant treatment response in 80% of macrocystic and 50% of microcystic LM cases during 1–3 years of follow-up [21]. Percutaneous sclerotherapy is also considered safe for infants, including newborns under one year of age [22]. Nevertheless, some studies have indicated that surgery-sometimes combined with sclerotherapy-may yield superior outcomes compared to sclerotherapy alone [23–26]. In addition, carbon dioxide laser therapy is a safe and effective option for superficial intraoral and cutaneous lesions, albeit with a relatively high recurrence rate [27].

Our findings reflect a clear anatomical preference in treatment selection: sclerotherapy was predominantly used for head/neck and extremity lesions, whereas surgical resection was more frequently applied to truncal lesions. It should be noted that laser therapy for LM is mostly performed in outpatient settings and was therefore not captured in this inpatient-based study.

Despite advances in treatment, approximately one-third of patients with refractory LM experience recurrence after surgical excision or sclerotherapy. The precise pathogenesis of LM remains incompletely elucidated; however, growing evidence indicates that multiple genes and regulatory factors collectively orchestrate lymphatic vascular development, and their dysregulation contributes to the initiation and progression of LM. Notably, recurrent somatic mutations in PIK3CA have been identified in the majority of LM cases, underscoring the central role of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway in its pathogenesis [28]. mTOR, a downstream effector of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, has been shown to be activated in lymphatic anomalies but not in normal lymphatic tissue. As an mTOR inhibitor, sirolimus has demonstrated therapeutic potential in LM by reducing lesion size and improving related symptoms. A randomized clinical trial in low-flow vascular malformations showed that sirolimus was more effective in LM than in venous malformations, suggesting that isolated LM may be an optimal indication for this therapy [29]. In addition, studies from our institution have shown that, in children with refractory LM, sirolimus appears to be more effective in macrocystic lesions than in microcystic lesions [30, 31]. These findings support the promising role of sirolimus in the treatment of pediatric LM.

This study represents the first systematic report on the hospitalization burden of pediatric LM. Our results demonstrated that sclerotherapy was associated with a significantly shorter length of stay and lower hospitalization expenses compared to surgical resection, which may partly account for its more frequent utilization in clinical practice.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design relies on the accuracy and completeness of medical records, which may affect data reliability. Future studies integrating both outpatient and inpatient data will be necessary to provide a more comprehensive characterization of the clinical spectrum of LM. Second, the analysis was confined to conventional treatments such as sclerotherapy and surgery, and did not include emerging targeted therapies like PI3K/mTOR inhibitors. Future studies should incorporate multimodal strategies-combining molecular targeted therapy, interventional procedures, and pharmacotherapy-to enable more comprehensive clinical evaluations.

Conclusion

By analyzing a large multi-center cohort of pediatric inpatients with LM, this study provides contemporary insights into the clinical characteristics and hospitalization burden of this condition in China. Although LM is histologically benign, it may cause substantial morbidity. Head and neck lesions, in particular, are associated with a markedly increased risk of acute adverse events, especially airway obstruction, which may become life-threatening. Such events are likely related to progressive lesion enlargement, secondary infection, or intralesional hemorrhage. Early recognition of clinically high-risk LM is therefore crucial for timely diagnosis and intervention, with the goal of reducing acute complications and improving prognosis. These findings highlight the significant healthcare resource utilization associated with LM and underscore the need for enhanced clinical awareness and informed resource allocation to better address the needs of this patient population.
