Semaglutide restores metabolic and structural homeostasis along the gut-heart-metabolic axis in a cafeteria diet-induced obesity model
Authors: Songul Doganay, Sevinc Yanar, İsmail Bolat, Inji Azmammadova, Merve Gulsen Bal Albayrak, Fatima Betul Nogay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObesity is a growing global health concern that leads to metabolic imbalances and progressive cardiac remodelling. This study investigated whether semaglutide (SMG) can counteract the disruption to the gut-heart-metabolic axis and associated myocardial injury caused by obesity. Twenty-one male Wistar rats were assigned to control, obese (OB), and obese+semaglutide (OB + SMG) groups. The control group was fed a normal diet, while the obese groups were given a cafeteria diet for 16 weeks. In addition, the OB + SMG group received 150 mcg/kg of SMG subcutaneously once a week for the last four weeks. The following were measured alongside HOMA indices: Lipid profile, cardiac enzymes, leptin, insulin, TMAO and LPS levels. Myocardial and intestinal samples were assessed biochemically and histopathological for the levels of MMP-2/MMP-9, TIMP-1, TGF-β, IL-1β, HO-1, occludin, ZO-1 and claudin. Obesity markedly increased body weight, fasting glucose, leptin, insulin resistance, the atherogenic index and cardiac risk, while also increasing LPS, TMAO, TGF-β, IL-1β, MMP-2/MMP-9 activity and decreasing TIMP-1. Semaglutide significantly reversed these abnormalities, improving metabolic indices, restoring the balance between MMPs and TIMPs, and reducing inflammatory mediators. Histological analysis revealed reduced cardiomyocyte degeneration and enhanced intestinal epithelial architecture in the OB + SMG group. Semaglutide notably enhanced the expression of occludin, claudin, and ZO-1, supporting strengthened intestinal barrier integrity, and restored HO-1 expression, indicating improved antioxidant defenses. These findings suggest that semaglutide provides cardioprotection in obesity by restoring gut barrier function, reducing inflammation and normalizing extracellular matrix turnover. By stabilizing the gut-heart-metabolic axis and mitigating structural myocardial injury, semaglutide may interrupt the progression from metabolic dysfunction to cardiomyopathy. This highlights its therapeutic potential in the treatment of obesity-related cardiovascular disease, extending beyond glycemic control.Keywords: Cardiac injury, Obesity, Metabolic Endotoxemia, Intestinal Permeability, SemaglutideSubject terms: Cardiology, Diseases, Medical research, Physiology

Introduction

Obesity is a chronic, relapsing disease marked by adipocyte hypertrophy, low-grade inflammation, and progressive insulin resistance that together disrupt endocrine homeostasis1,2. Diets high in saturated fat and refined carbohydrates, together with physical inactivity, heighten this imbalance and add metabolic pressure on gut and liver3. Typical features include hyperinsulinemia with excess leptin and relatively low adiponectin, together with atherogenic dyslipidemia and endothelial stress that increase cardiovascular risk4. Obesity also disrupts the intestinal epithelial barrier. Tight-junction disorganization increases paracellular permeability, allowing luminal lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to enter the circulation and sustaining metabolic endotoxemia. Beyond local effects, LPS signaling feeds back to metabolic organs, worsens insulin resistance and dyslipidemia, and realigns bile-acid and choline pathways in parallel with microbiota shifts5. Microbial trimethylamine (TMA), converted in the liver to trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO), further links gut ecology to cardiometabolic injury by affecting cholesterol trafficking, endothelial reactivity, and platelet responses. Taken together, barrier failure, LPS leakage, and TMAO, alongside the hormonal and lipid changes of obesity, form a self-reinforcing axis that connects the intestine to systemic cardiometabolic stress6.

In obesity, signals that start at the intestinal interface do not remain local. They enter the circulation and aggregate in the myocardium, where they reset immune and structural programs. Translocated microbial products and altered lipids trigger pattern-recognition receptors and the inflammasome, leading to maturation and release of interleukin-1β (IL-1β). IL-1β disrupts calcium handling and ion-channel function, weakening excitation-contraction coupling and amplifying innate immune activity in the heart. In this inflammatory setting, transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) is upregulated and drives fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transition, increases extracellular collagen synthesis, and suppresses matrix-degrading homeostasis7,8. Because the extracellular matrix (ECM) controls ventricular geometry, compliance, and electrical continuity, even small but chronic shifts in its turnover have large mechanical effects. Matrix metalloproteinases MMP-2 and MMP-9 accelerate degradation of basement-membrane and interstitial collagens, while tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases-1 (TIMP-1) restrains this proteolysis. Inflammatory and oxidative stress tilt this MMP-to-TIMP balance toward maladaptive remodeling. As this imbalance persists, myocardial fibrosis develops, with progressive interstitial collagen deposition that increases stiffness and deepens diastolic dysfunction, setting the stage for heart failure. Thus, beyond classical metabolic drivers such as dyslipidemia and insulin resistance, cytokine signaling and ECM dynamics provide a mechanistic bridge linking intestinal dysfunction to adverse cardiac remodeling9,10. Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) now occupy a central place in evidence-based obesity care. They enhance glucose-dependent insulin secretion, slow gastric emptying, and reduce appetite, leading to sustained weight loss and broad cardiometabolic gains in randomized trials. Available agents include daily and once-weekly long-acting formulations such as liraglutide and others11,12. Within this class, semaglutide stands out for its high receptor potency and once-weekly dosing. In a Semaglutide Treatment Effect in People with Obesity (STEP) trial, semaglutide 2.4 mg plus lifestyle modification produced substantial, durable weight loss beyond lifestyle alone13. In the “Semaglutide Effects on Cardiovascular Outcomes in People with Overweight or Obesity” (SELECT) outcomes trial, adults with overweight or obesity and established cardiovascular disease but without diabetes had fewer major adverse cardiovascular events with semaglutide than with placebo14,15. In obesity-related heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, semaglutide also improved symptoms, physical limitations, and weight16. While most investigations evaluate cardiovascular or intestinal pathways separately, early translational data suggest incretin-based therapy may also strengthen epithelial junctional integrity and permeability17. Taken together, these findings position GLP-1RAs as foundational therapy for obesity. Among them, semaglutide has a strong, broadly generalizable efficacy and safety profile, supporting evaluation along the gut-heart axis11,12. Most of the literature pull the gut and the heart apart. Cardiovascular studies with semaglutide rarely measure barrier integrity or microbial products, while reports on incretin-mediated barrier improvement seldom track myocardial remodeling within the same experiment14,15,17. As a result, the physiology that links intestinal permeability, endotoxemia, systemic metabolic load, and cardiac remodeling in obesity has been described in pieces rather than as one connected pathway. Building on this disconnect, the present study evaluated semaglutide using an integrated design. Barrier function, circulating inflammatory and lipid drivers, proximal cardiac signaling, and downstream structural remodeling were measured concurrently within a single model and time frame. This alignment enables a direct test of whether modifying the gut interface translates into measurable attenuation of cardiac remodeling in obesity.

Materials and methods

Animals and experimental procedure

This study was carried out at the Sakarya University Faculty of Medicine’s Experimental Animal Centre following approval from the Sakarya University Animal Experiments Local Ethics Committee. The study strictly adhered to the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines and was conducted following the national regulations for the ethical use and care of laboratory animals. The animals were housed in polycarbonate cages on racks within a climate-controlled room. The room was kept at a temperature between 22 ± 2 °C, with a humidity level between 40% and 60%, and a 12/12 light–dark cycle. All experimental procedures were performed in accordance with the Administrative Regulations on Experimental Animals.

Twenty-one four-week-old male Wistar albino rats were purchased from Sakarya University Experimental Animal Research Center (SUDETAM). The animals fed the same standard chow diet for one week to allow them to adapt, before being randomly divided into three groups:

Control group (1), the animals were fed only a standard diet for sixteen weeks (CON; n = 7); obesity group (2), the animals were fed a cafeteria diet for 16 weeks (OB, n = 7); obesity and treatment group (3), the animals were fed cafeteria diet for 16 weeks and treated with semaglutide (OB + SMG; n = 7) last four weeks. Semaglutide was given 150 mcg/kg subcutaneously once a week for four weeks (Novo Nordisk, Copenhagen, Denmark)18. The subcutaneous route was selected due to the peptide nature of semaglutide, which limits oral bioavailability because of gastrointestinal enzymatic degradation. The animals’ food consumption and weight gain were monitored constantly for 16 weeks.

Diets and induced obesity

The control group was fed standard rat chow with a macronutrient profile of 75% carbohydrates, 20% protein and 4.5% fat (3000 kcal/kg). The obesity groups were fed a cafeteria diet with a macronutrient profile of 48% carbohydrates, 11% protein and 41% fat (5004 kcal/kg)19,20. The cafeteria diet was selected over a conventional high-fat diet because its palatability and dietary variety more closely mimic Western-style eating patterns and reliably induce obesity and metabolic syndrome-like features in rodents21,22. The cafeteria diet included potato chips, a variety of biscuits, corn chips, crackers and cakes with cream or fruit fillings, as well as mosaic cakes, chocolates, control chow and protein-rich items such as skimmed milk powder (Table 1). All diets were offered ad libitum and refreshed daily. All diets were provided by Arden Research and Experiment, in Ankara, Türkiye.

Tissue collection

After sixteen weeks, the rats were anaesthetized with ketamine/xylazine (100/10 mg/kg)23, and blood was collected via cardiac puncture. The rats were then killed by exsanguination. The heart was then dissected. The blood samples were placed in tubes and left to rest at room temperature for 20 min. They were then centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 10 min to separate the serum. The samples were then stored at − 80 °C for analysis of the lipid profile, hormones and cardiac biochemical parameters24.

Preparation of tissue homogenates

Each rat heart tissue sample (100 mg) was homogenized in 1 ml of ice-cold phosphate buffer solution (50 mM, pH 7.4) using a homogenizer on ice. Following centrifugation at 10.000 g for 15 min at 4 °C, the resulting supernatant was collected for the determination of IL-1β, TGF-β, MMP-2, MMP-9 and TIMP-1.

Determination of serum parameters

Serum levels of triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), Creatine kinas-MB (CK, CK-MB), Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and fasting blood glucose (FBG) were analyzed using a spectrophotometrically biochemistry analyzer. Serum levels of adiponectin, leptin, insulin, lipopolysaccharides (LPS), trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) were measured with enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) commercial kits (Bioassay Technology Laboratory: Adiponectin Cat No: E0758Ra, Leptin Cat No: E0561Ra, Insulin Cat No: E0707Ra; LPS Cat No: E0756Ra, TMAO Cat No: EA0120Ra). ELISA measurements were performed following the steps outlined in the kit manuals. The absorbance of each well was measured at 450 nm. The results were expressed in as nanograms per milliliter (ng/mL), picograms per milliliter (pg/mL), milligram per deciliter (mg/dL) or other relevant units, depending on the kit’s specifications.

Determination of inflammation and matrix metalloproteinase levels in heart tissue samples

The levels of IL-1β, TGF-β, MMP-2, MMP-9 and TIMP-1 in the tissue samples were determined using commercial ELISA kits (IL-1β, Cat No: E0119Ra; TGF-β, Cat No: E0778Ra; MMP-2, Cat No: E0315Ra; MMP-9, Cat No: E0321Ra; TIMP-9, Cat No: E0322Ra).

The ELISA (Bioassay Technology Laboratory) assays were performed according to the instructions provided with each kit by the manufacturer. The absorbance values of the wells were measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. The concentrations in the tissue samples were determined by generating a standard curve and then using this to interpolate the absorbance values of the samples. The results were expressed as ng/mg of protein or in other relevant units, depending on the kit specifications.

Metabolic and cardiovascular indices

Insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), sensitivity (HOMA-IS) and β-cell function (HOMA-β) were evaluated using homeostatic model assessment indices. These indexes were measured using fasting plasma glucose and insulin concentrations.

HOMA-IR = [Fasting Blood Glucose (mg/dL) x Fasting Insulin Level (pg/mL) / 405]25.

HOMA-IS = 10.000 / [fasting glucose (mg/dL) × fasting insulin (pg /mL)]26.

HOMA-β = [fasting insulin (pg/mL) × 360/ (fasting glucose (mg/dL) -63]27.

Body mass index (BMI): The obtained body weight (g) and naso-anal length (cm) data were used to calculate BMI = [body weight (g)/naso-anal length (cm)2] formula28.

Atherogenic index = Log [(TG)/(HDL cholesterol)]29; Cardiac risk ratio=Total TG/HDL cholesterol30.

Histological procedure

At the end of the experimental protocol, intestinal and heart samples were fixed in a 10% buffered formalin solution for 48 h, after which they were embedded in paraffin blocks using routine tissue processing procedures. Four-micrometre-thick sections were taken from each block and prepared for histopathological examination. The sections were stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H/E) and examined under a light microscope (Leica Flexacam i5). Histopathological evaluations were performed to detect degeneration, necrosis and desquamation in the epithelial cells of the intestinal tissue and degeneration, necrosis and myocarditis in the heart tissue. The severity of the findings was graded as follows: absent (-), mild (+), moderate (++), and severe (+++).

Immunohistochemical analysis

Tissue sections were mounted on poly-L-lysine-coated slides, deparaffinized and dehydrated using routine tissue processing procedures. Endogenous peroxidase was then inactivated by immersing the sample in 3% H₂O₂ for 10 min. The tissues were then boiled in 1% antigen retrieval solution (TRIS-EDTA buffer (pH 6.1), 100x dilution) and allowed to cool to room temperature. To prevent non-specific background staining, the sections were incubated with a protein block for five minutes. The primary antibody (Occludin Cat. No. bs-10011R, dilution: 1:100; Claudin Cat No: BT-AP011838, dilution: 1/100; ZO-1 Cat. No. sc-33725, dilution: 1/100) was then applied to the tissues and incubated according to the instructions for use. Following the application of the secondary antibody, 3-amino-9-ethylcarbazole (AEC) chromogen was applied to the tissues. The stained sections were examined using a Leica Flexacam i5 light microscope. After examination, ImageJ analysis software was used to determine the intensity of immunopositivity in the tissues.

Immunofluorescence analysis

Tissue sections were placed on poly-L-lysine-coated slides, deparaffinized and dehydrated for immunofluorescence analysis. The sections were then boiled in a 1% antigen retrieval solution (citrate buffer, pH 6.1, 100× dilution) and left to cool to room temperature. To prevent non-specific background staining, the sections were incubated with a protein block for five minutes. The primary antibody (HO-1, Cat. No. bs-2075R, rabbit polyclonal, dilution ratio 1:100, USA) was then applied to the tissues and incubated according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A secondary immunofluorescence antibody (FITC, Cat. No. ab6717, rabbit polyclonal, dilution ratio 1:1000) was then applied and the slides were kept in the dark for 45 min. DAPI mounting medium (Cat. No. ab104140, dilution ratio 1:200, UK) was then dropped onto the sections and left in the dark for five minutes before a coverslip was placed on top. The stained tissues were examined under a Zeiss AXIO fluorescence microscope (Germany).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.5.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The Levene test was then applied to evaluate homogeneity of variances. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare groups. When significant differences were detected, a Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test was conducted to determine the differences between each pair of groups. The non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test was used to assess group interaction in H&E staining results, and the Mann-Whitney U test was employed to compare differences between groups. The results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation or standard error (SD/SEM), and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Semaglutide ameliorates metabolic and hormonal dysregulation in obese rats

Initially, we assessed the effect of semaglutide on metabolic disorders associated with obesity (Fig. 1). In the obese group, body weight, BMI and FBG levels were significantly higher than in the control group (Fig. 1a, b,c; p < 0.05-0.0001). Consistent with these findings, HOMA-IR values increased markedly, while HOMA-IS and HOMA-B values decreased significantly, indicating insulin resistance, decreased insulin sensitivity and impaired β-cell function (Fig. 1d, e,f; p < 0.05-0.0001). Treatment with semaglutide significantly improved these metabolic disturbances, evidenced by reductions in body weight, body mass index and fasting glucose alongside enhanced insulin sensitivity and β-cell function. There was a slight increase in serum insulin levels in the OB + SMG group compared to both the control group and the OB group (Fig. 1g; p < 0.01 − 0.001). The modest rise in insulin, together with the significant improvement in the HOMA-IR and HOMA-B indices, suggests an improvement in β-cell responsiveness and insulin sensitivity rather than a pathological increase in circulating insulin. Meanwhile, the reduction in leptin levels observed in the semaglutide-treated group, following the obesity-associated increase in the OB group, suggests a partial improvement in leptin resistance associated with obesity (Fig. 1h). Serum adiponectin levels did not differ significantly between groups; however, a non-significant increase was observed in the OB group compared with the control group, while a non-significant decrease was observed in the OB + SMG group compared with the OB group (p > 0.05) (Fig. 1i).

Semaglutide ameliorates dyslipidemia and endotoxin-associated cardiometabolic risk in obesity

To further elucidate the cardiometabolic protective effects of semaglutide, we examined its influence on the lipid profile, LPS levels, TMAO, and cardiovascular risk markers (Fig. 2). Obese animals exhibited significantly higher TC, TG and LDL cholesterol levels than control group (Fig. 2a, c; p < 0.05 − 0.001), while HDL cholesterol showed a non-significant increase in the OB + SMG group (Fig. 2d). Furthermore, the atherogenic index and TG/HDL ratio were significantly higher in the OB group, indicating an increased risk of cardiovascular disease (Fig. 2e, f; p < 0.0001). Semaglutide treatment significantly mitigated these changes by reducing TG and LDL cholesterol, as well as lowering the atherogenic index and TG/HDL ratio to near-control levels. Additionally, LPS levels were significantly higher in the obese group (Fig. 2h; p < 0.01), reflecting obesity-related metabolic inflammation. Conversely, semaglutide administration markedly decreased LPS concentrations. No significant differences were observed in TMAO levels among the groups (Fig. 2g). These results suggest that semaglutide improves lipid metabolism and mitigates endotoxin-associated cardiometabolic risk in obesity.

Semaglutide attenuates inflammation and extracellular matrix remodeling to protect cardiac function in obese rats

Semaglutide exhibited significant anti-inflammatory and cardioprotective properties in obese rats (Fig. 3). Compared with the control group, the obese rats exhibited significantly higher levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and TGF-β (Fig. 3a, b; p < 0.01 − 0.001), indicating the activation of inflammatory pathways. Semaglutide treatment effectively suppressed these increases (p < 0.0001) and demonstrated a marked anti-inflammatory response.

Similarly, markers of extracellular matrix remodeling were notably altered in obesity. MMP-9 expression, MMP-2/TIMP-1 and MMP-9/TIMP-1 ratio increased markedly (Fig. 3d, e, f, g; p < 0.0001), suggesting enhanced matrix degradation and tissue remodeling. Semaglutide treatment normalized these parameters, bringing them close to control values. TIMP-1 levels, which were significantly decreased in the obese group (Fig. 3e; p < 0.01), increased after semaglutide administration (p < 0.01), indicating the restoration of matrix balance and reduced remodeling activity. Although MMP-2 and CRP levels were highest in the OB group, they did not differ significantly between groups (Fig. 3c, h.; p < 0.05. This result suggests that MMP-9 and cytokines primarily mediate the important inflammatory and remodeling responses in obesity. Additionally, cardiac enzyme levels, including CK, CK-MB and LDH, were significantly higher in obese rats (p < 0.05-0.0001), reflecting myocardial injury and increased metabolic stress. Semaglutide treatment significantly reduced these enzyme levels, indicating decreased cardiac damage and enhanced myocardial integrity.

Semaglutide attenuates intestinal injury and restores barrier integrity in obese rats

Histopathological evaluation revealed normal villus morphology and an intact epithelial lining in the intestinal tissue of the control group (Figs. 2A and 4). In contrast, the OB group displayed severe epithelial degeneration, necrosis and extensive desquamation of villus epithelium, resulting in marked mucosal disorganization (Fig. 4Ab). The OB + SMG group showed a significant reduction in these degenerative changes compared with the OB group (p < 0.05), as evidenced by partial restoration of the villus structure and preservation of epithelial continuity (Fig. 4, 1Ac). Quantitative scoring confirmed a significant decrease in histopathological damage in the OB + SMG group compared with obese rats (p < 0.05; Figs. 1B and 4).

Discussion

In recent years, improvements in living conditions and an increase in high-calorie eating habits have led to a significant rise in obesity rates. Obesity is caused by an imbalance of energy, as well as the interaction of multiple factors. These include the hypothalamus’s and central nervous system’s appetite-regulating mechanisms, access to food, gastrointestinal status and psychological factors31,32. In this study, we investigated the protective and regulatory effects of semaglutide on metabolic, hemostatic and inflammatory changes, as well as cardiac remodeling resulting from intestinal barrier integrity disruption in a cafeteria diet-induced obesity model. Our study findings indicated that BMI, fasting glucose, insulin levels, and HOMA-IR decreased, while HOMA-IS and HOMA-B values ​​increased in the semaglutide-treated group. These results are consistent with a recent meta-analysis showing that weekly semaglutide injections induce sustained weight loss and improve cardiometabolic risk factors in overweight or obese individuals without diabetes33. This is consistent with the mechanistic hypothesis that semaglutide, through its GLP-1 receptor-mediated effects, protects pancreatic β-cell function and improves glucose tolerance by regulating insulin secretion34. In addition, semaglutide suppresses appetite, reduces energy intake, and thus contributes to weight loss, an effect that increases insulin sensitivity35,36. This effect is due to the fact that GLP-1RAs suppress appetite and reduce energy intake at a central nervous system level. They also improve metabolic balance by regulating glucose homeostasis and increasing insulin sensitivity in peripheral tissues37.

Adiponectin has strong insulin-sensitizing, anti-inflammatory, and anti-atherogenic effects. Its reduced levels in obesity, type-two diabetes and atherosclerosis likely result from inflammation, adipose tissue hypertrophy, and oxidative stress38. Circulating adiponectin levels do not always decrease uniformly in obesity models and may remain unchanged or show compensatory responses depending on disease duration and metabolic context39,40. In long-term diet-induced obesity, adiponectin regulation appears to be time-dependent and not solely determined by adiposity or weight loss40. In line with these observations, our results showed no significant differences in serum adiponectin levels between groups, despite a non-significant increase in the obese group and a decrease in the semaglutide-treated group. Notably, increased adiponectin has also been reported in diet-induced obesity models. In a cafeteria diet–induced obesity model, Pérez-Echarri et al. reported elevated circulating adiponectin and increased adipose tissue adiponectin expression, which were discussed in the context of obesity susceptibility and early leptin resistance, highlighting the context-dependent nature of adipokine regulation40. Similarly, Pellenz et al. demonstrated increased serum adiponectin levels in a cafeteria diet–induced obesity model, interpreting this finding as a reflection of complex adipokine regulation linked to obesity-associated metabolic and nutrigenomic adaptations rather than adiposity alone41. Together, these findings demonstrate that serum adiponectin levels do not necessarily decrease uniformly in diet-induced obesity but may instead reflect context-dependent regulatory responses.

In contrast, the reduction in leptin levels observed following semaglutide treatment may indicate an early and partial improvement in obesity-associated leptin resistance, without evidence of a global normalization of adipokine profiles. This effect is likely mediated by semaglutide’s weight-reducing and appetite-suppressing actions rather than a direct enhancement of leptin sensitivity. Martins et al. reported that semaglutide reduced circulating leptin levels and adipose tissue inflammation in obese mice, indicating improved adipocyte function42. Similarly, Zhu and Chen found that semaglutide reduced visceral fat and altered lipid metabolism, although normalization of adipokines appeared to occur more gradually. Furthermore, models of diet-induced obesity that use a cafeteria diet have consistently demonstrated hyperleptinemia without proportional changes in adiponectin. These models closely mimic human metabolic dysfunction and highlight the dysregulated interplay between leptin and adiponectin in diet-induced obesity43. The early effects of semaglutide include reduced hyperleptinemia and inflammation. However, adipokine normalization requires longer treatment.

Our results showed that the cafeteria diet resulted in greater weight gain and obesity than in the control group. This was associated with the onset of metabolic disorders at an early stage, including insulin resistance, increased triglyceride levels and elevated leptin levels. In our study, significant decreases in TG, LDL, and atherogenic indices were observed in the OB + SMG group. Furthermore, the improvement in the TG/HDL cholesterol ratio suggests that the lipid profile became more favorable in terms of cardiovascular risk. These findings demonstrate that the once-weekly dose of the semaglutide protects against hyperlipidemia, improves deteriorating lipid profiles and strengthens cardiovascular health. These results are consistent with previous studies44. Although semaglutide administration caused a slight, non-significant increase in HDL levels, this finding is consistent with studies reporting that GLP-1RAs have limited or no effect on HDL cholesterol45. Nevertheless, the significant reduction in triglycerides and atherogenic indices suggests that semaglutide has a favorable impact on overall lipid metabolism.

High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) has been identified as a significant cardiovascular risk factor46. In our study, CRP levels were higher in the obese group and lower in the SMG + OB group; however, no statistically significant difference was observed between the groups. Similarly, previous studies using cafeteria diet–induced obesity models have reported the development of low-grade systemic inflammation, although this inflammatory response does not always parallel changes in acute-phase markers46,47. CRP is an acute-phase protein primarily synthesized in hepatocytes under the regulation of IL-6, and its plasma concentration varies depending on the intensity, duration, and cytokine profile of the inflammatory response48. The chronic, low-grade inflammation characteristic of obesity is mainly associated with elevated IL-1β and TNF-α levels; however, this cytokine profile may not always reach the threshold required to stimulate hepatic CRP synthesis. Moreover, the short half-life and acute-phase–sensitive kinetics of CRP may limit its sensitivity in detecting chronic, low-intensity inflammation49. In the assessment of inflammation related to metabolic syndrome and cardiovascular risk, high-sensitivity CRP (hs-CRP) is generally preferred. In our study, however, routine CRP measurement was performed using an immunoturbidimetric method. Since hs-CRP has greater analytical sensitivity at low concentrations, subclinical inflammatory changes reported in the literature may not have been fully reflected in our findings.

Diet is one of the main factors that determines intestinal permeability levels. The cafeteria diet used in our study creates an imbalance in the gut microbiota due to its high content of fat and sugar50. This disrupts the structural integrity of the intestinal epithelial barrier, reduces the expression of tight junction proteins (such as occludin, claudin and ZO-1) and increases intestinal barrier permeability51. Such diets increase serum LPS levels and are associated with metabolic diseases, including obesity, diabetes, and insulin resistance. Increased intestinal LPS translocation makes cardiometabolic disorders worse by triggering systemic inflammation, leading to metabolic endotoxemia. Cafeteria diet feeding has been reported to induce metabolic endotoxemia in rats52. Increased intestinal permeability increases the inflammatory burden and may affect TMAO formation in the microbiota-liver axis51,53. LPS-mediated inflammation increases cardiovascular risk by altering hepatic flavin monooxygenase (FMO) activity and increasing TMAO synthesis. This results in adverse changes to the lipid profile, such as increased TG and LDL levels, and a deterioration in the atherogenic index54. Some experimental data suggest that acute LPS exposure can suppress FMO expression, indicating that the relationship between LPS and TMAO may depend on the duration of the diet and the level of inflammation53,55,56 While no significant difference in TMAO levels was observed between the groups in our study, the decrease in LPS, recovery of barrier proteins, and improvement in lipid profile support the conclusion that semaglutide treatment reduces endotoxemia-induced cardiometabolic load51,57. Semaglutide treatment interrupts this complex cycle via multiple mechanisms.

GLP-1 receptors are involved in regulating various cardiovascular and metabolic processes, and are located in the vascular endothelium, cardiomyocytes, immune cells and the central nervous system58. Activation of the GLP-1 receptor limits LPS translocation by strengthening the intestinal barrier and increasing the expression of tight junction proteins59. It also suppresses systemic inflammation and exerts a regulatory effect on the gut-brain-heart axis60.

In our study, the significant histopathological improvements and biochemical changes observed after semaglutide administration mirrored these mechanisms. Semaglutide treatment significantly reduced myocardial degeneration, necrosis, and inflammatory cell infiltration, consistent with its anti-fibrotic and anti-inflammatory actions. Our findings highlight that semaglutide not only improves cardiac structure and function but also exerts protective effects on intestinal integrity, supporting the existence of a gut metabolic heart axis in diet-induced obesity. The restoration of tight junction proteins (occludin, claudin, and ZO-1) and reduction in circulating LPS levels suggest that semaglutide alleviates metabolic endotoxemia, a known driver of systemic inflammation and myocardial remodeling. The down-regulation of IL-1β and TGF-β, coupled with the restoration of the MMP-9/TIMP-1 balance, indicates the suppression of fibroblast activation and extracellular matrix remodeling. These findings are consistent with the described GLP-1RA mechanisms. Furthermore, the decrease in cardiac injury biomarkers (CK, CK-MB and LDH) and the increase in HO-1 expression suggest reduced oxidative stress and enhanced myocardial integrity. Our results are consistent with those reported in previous studies demonstrating that GLP-1 agonists prevent excessive ECM degradation by suppressing cardiac fibroblast activity61,62. The potential of GLP-1RAs to have anti-fibrotic effects on the heart is due to their anti-inflammatory properties, such as the modulation of inflammatory mediators and oxidative stress63. The cardioprotective profile of semaglutide reported in experimental and preclinical studies aligns with these findings. In models of obesity or cardiac stress, activation of the GLP-1 receptor has been shown to reduce ventricular hypertrophy and interstitial fibrosis, preserve mitochondrial structure and function, and reduce oxidative stress via the Nrf2/HO-1 signaling pathway64,65. GLP-1 receptor agonists have been shown to increase the levels of Nrf2 and HO-1 in endothelial cells and cardiomyocytes when they are exposed to high glucose levels66. As in our study, previous studies have shown that GLP-1RAs increase Nrf2 nuclear translocation and HO-1 expression in these tissues under stressful conditions, thereby increasing oxidative damage. Following IF staining of heart tissue, an increase in HO-1 expression following semaglutide treatment suggests reduced oxidative stress and enhanced myocardial integrity. Our findings are supported by previous studies67.

Studies have also shown that GLP-1 receptor agonists reduce pro-inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β and TGF-β, restore the balance of MMP-9 and TIMP-1, and prevent excessive degradation of the extracellular matrix. This limits adverse cardiac remodeling68–70. Gallego-Colon et al. also demonstrated that exenatide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist (RA), may suppress the expression of MMPs induced by TNF-α by inhibiting the phosphorylation of components within the AKT signaling pathway71. Similarly, reducing the expression of TGF-β leads to reduced myocardial remodeling and extracellular matrix deposition by modulating the expression of fibrotic markers72. Furthermore, GLP-1RAs alleviate inflammation by decreasing the levels of TNF-α, IFN-γ, MMP-9 and other pro-atherogenic factors, thereby slowing the progression of atherosclerosis44.

This effect may be related to semaglutide’s oxidative stress-suppressing and cell damage-reducing effects. The literature reports that GLP-1 agonists increase excessive enzyme expression and suppress reactive oxygen species (ROS)73,74. In addition to, studies have demonstrated that GLP-1RAs have cardioprotective and anti-fibrotic effects that extend beyond glycemic control. Fibrous tissue in the myocardium is primarily produced by myofibroblasts that have differentiated from fibroblasts. Interference with processes such as TGF-β-mediated fibroblast activation and the production of ECM proteins by fibroblasts that transform into myofibroblasts disrupts the fundamental mechanism of cardiac fibrosis7,9,10,75. GLP-1 receptor agonists reduce profibrotic gene expression by suppressing glycolytic activity and histone acetylation in fibroblasts. In vascular tissues, they inhibit fibrotic markers such as MMP2/9, thereby reducing oxidative stress and improving endothelial function76. GLP-1RAs increase cellular resilience by preserving mitochondrial function in cardiomyocytes, regulating calcium balance and preventing lipid accumulation77. Furthermore, by increasing elastin production without promoting collagen accumulation, they support the preservation of flexible tissue structure rather than scar tissue78. Histological analysis reveals a reduction in fibrotic and necrotic regions, alongside improved villus morphology and myocardial structure. This provides morphological evidence of semaglutide’s protective actions on intestinal and cardiac tissues. Overall, these results indicate that semaglutide exerts its beneficial effects via a multi-organ mechanism involving modulation of inflammatory and oxidative pathways, reinforcement of intestinal barrier integrity and regulation of extracellular matrix turnover. The restoration of gut-metabolic-heart axis homeostasis and the recovery of structural and functional integrity in the cafeteria-diet-induced obesity model is achieved by semaglutide through these coordinated actions. These combined effects limit fibrosis and contribute to the restoration of myocardial structural integrity, as evidenced by reductions in cardiac injury biomarkers such as CK, CK-MB, and LDH. The decrease in myocardial damage biomarkers, such as CK, CK-MB and LDH, indicates that GLP-1 receptor agonists not only suppress fibrosis but also preserve cardiomyocyte integrity. These agents provide direct cytoprotective effects by reducing inflammation-related mitochondrial dysfunction, calcium imbalance and oxidative stress77. They also limit oxidative damage by activating the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway and preventing the release of muscle enzymes into circulation by reducing intracellular degradation processes74,78.

Although our study yielded promising results, several limitations should be acknowledged as these may affect the interpretation of the overall findings. For example, the short duration of semaglutide treatment may have limited the detection of long-term effects on adipokine balance, intestinal permeability and cardiac remodeling. Moreover, the lack of gut microbiota composition analysis remains a limitation of the present study. Furthermore, functional cardiac assessments, such as echocardiography or hemodynamic measurements, were not included. These assessments could have provided further insight into the physiological relevance of the observed biochemical and histological changes. Additionally, only male rats were used, which makes it difficult to evaluate possible sex-related differences in metabolic or cardiac responses. Despite these limitations, the findings provide strong evidence that semaglutide exerts protective effects on multiple organs in response to metabolic, intestinal and cardiac alterations induced by a cafeteria diet.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that semaglutide has protective effects on multiple organs by improving metabolic balance, reinforcing intestinal barrier integrity and reducing cardiac inflammation and fibrosis in a model of obesity induced by a cafeteria diet (Fig. 6). These results highlight the potential of GLP-1 receptor agonists in managing obesity-related cardiometabolic dysfunction. However, the short duration of treatment and limited scope of functional evaluations should be taken into account when interpreting the results. Overall, while basic studies including the present one suggest that GLP-1RAs may have protective and antifibrotic effects, clinical validation remains limited and further long-term studies are needed to confirm these benefits.
