Cationic and Anionic Peptides from Sheepskin Collagen as Effective Salt Substitutes for Improving the Quality of Low-Sodium Surimi Gels
Authors: Guohua Wang, Da Hong, Zexi Yin, Dan Huang, Ni Li, Shuzhen Cheng, Ming Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Growing demand for low-sodium surimi products has driven the search for safe salt alternatives. Anionic peptides (APPs) and cationic peptides (CPPs) were isolated from sheepskin collagen via Diethylaminoethyl (DEAE) chromatography. CPPs contained higher arginine (46.11%) and lysine (4.64%) than APPs (40.57% and 3.99%, respectively), while APPs were enriched in acidic amino acids like glutamic acid (3.88%). Comprehensive evaluations of low-salt silver carp surimi gels showed both peptides significantly improved gel strength and water-holding capacity (WHC). The water-holding capacity increased from 60.68% in the blank control group to 74.31% in the CPP-treated group, while that in the APP-treated group was 66.86%. Cooking loss was significantly reduced, decreasing from 40.64% in the blank control group to 28.57% in the CPP-treated group and 34.52% in the APP-treated group. The samples achieved a quality comparable to that of the NaCl-supplemented group, with CPP outperforming APP in terms of hardness and gel network density. The LF-NMR confirmed enhanced water retention by reducing free water (T22) and increasing bound water (T2b). The FTIR indicated a conformational shift from α-helix to β-sheet, and the SEM revealed denser networks with fewer large voids. The SDS-PAGE demonstrated enhanced myosin heavy chain (MHC) cross-linking, more pronounced in the CPP-treated samples. CPPs exerted stronger electrostatic attraction with negatively charged surimi proteins (isoelectric point 5.5), while APPs chelated Ca2+ to activate transglutaminase. These findings validate APPs and CPPs as promising salt substitutes, enabling low-sodium surimi production and high-value utilization of sheepskin by-products.

1. Introduction

Surimi is a concentrated myofibrillar protein product made from minced and washed fish meat. It is popular worldwide for its high nutritional value, low fat content, and versatile textural properties. Surimi-based products have become staples in many diets due to their convenience and palatability [1]. In surimi production, NaCl plays an indispensable role in gel formation, and is typically added at a 2–3% (m/m) concentration [2]. NaCl dissolves salt-soluble proteins such as actin and myosin in fish muscle. These proteins are the building blocks of the three-dimensional gel network that gives surimi its characteristic texture. Upon heating, the dissolved myosin molecules undergo tail entanglement and head-to-head association through hydrophobic interactions and disulfide bonds [3]. This process forms a stable water-entrapping gel structure that determines the quality and consumer acceptance of surimi products.

However, the moderate to high sodium content in traditional surimi products has raised serious health concerns. Excessive sodium intake is strongly linked to hypertension, cerebrovascular diseases, and other cardiovascular disorders [4]. With growing consumer awareness of healthy eating and stricter regulatory guidelines for sodium reduction there is an urgent and unmet need for safe, effective, and side-effect-free salt alternatives. These alternatives must replicate the texture-enhancing functions of NaCl without compromising product quality or safety. Numerous studies have explored potential salt substitutes for surimi, including KCl, hydrocolloids, plant extracts, and protein hydrolysates. However, many of these alternatives face significant limitations. KCl often imparts bitter or metallic off-flavors at effective concentrations [5]. Hydrocolloids such as carrageenan can improve water-holding capacity but fail to fully mimic the gel-strengthening effect of NaCl [6]. Plant extracts may introduce undesirable colors or tastes, and their stability in food matrices is often inconsistent [7].

Bioactive peptides from plants and animals have garnered extensive attention and motivated intensive research in the fields of food science, nutrition, and biomedicine due to their diverse and prominent functional advantages [8,9,10]. Sheepskin collagen peptides (SSCPs) have emerged as a promising candidate for salt replacement. Sheepskin is an abundant, low-cost by-product of the meat industry, and its valorization aligns with circular economy principles. SSCPs are functional short peptides obtained through the extraction and hydrolysis of sheepskin collagen. They are rich in bioactive amino acids, including glycine, proline, and arginine, which contribute to various biological activities such as antioxidant and anti-photoaging effects [11]. Unlike native collagen, which has an intact triple-helix structure, hydrolyzed SSCPs primarily adopt loose conformations like random coils and β-sheets. This structural feature exposes more active sites, facilitating interactions with myofibrillar proteins in surimi. SSCPs also exhibit good solubility, easy dispersion in gel matrices, and superior thermal stability. Additionally, sheepskin collagen is free from religious or disease-related constraints, offering advantages in safety and biocompatibility [12]. Previous research has shown that certain collagen components can activate endogenous transglutaminase in surimi, significantly enhancing gel strength. These properties make SSCPs a viable and sustainable option for developing low-sodium surimi products.

The texture-improving effect of NaCl on surimi gels is closely related to its ionic components. Both anions and cations from NaCl participate in protein interactions and network formation [13]. Existing studies have shown that when anion concentration is constant, increasing cation levels initially refines gel properties, but later coarsens them [14]. This phenomenon is likely due to excessive cation-induced protein aggregation which disrupts the uniformity of the gel network. However, most research on collagen peptides as salt substitutes has focused on mixed peptide fractions without considering the influence of ionic charge. To date, no dedicated studies have separated sheepskin collagen peptides into anionic and cationic fractions. There is a lack of systematic comparison of their distinct effects on surimi gel characteristics. The role of ionic charge in mediating peptide–myofibrillar protein interactions remains unclear. It is unknown how differences in molecular weight distribution and amino acid composition between anionic and cationic peptides affect gel quality parameters such as gel strength, water-holding capacity, and texture. The underlying mechanisms, including changes in protein, secondary structure, intermolecular interactions, and microstructure also need to be elucidated. This research gap hinders the development of targeted and efficient peptide-based salt substitutes for low-sodium surimi production.

This study addresses key research gaps by aiming to achieve the following: clarify the differential effects of anionic (APP) and cationic (CPP) peptides on low-salt silver carp surimi gel quality; reveal the associations between their molecular weight distribution, amino acid composition, and divergent gel property impacts (e.g., gel strength, water-holding capacity, texture); and elucidate the improvement mechanisms, including changes in protein secondary structure, intermolecular forces, and gel microstructure. We hypothesize that both peptides enhance low-salt surimi gel quality (with CPPs being superior due to their high arginine/lysine content) and induce a myofibrillar protein conformational transition from α-helix to β-sheet, which promotes protein aggregation, strengthens intermolecular forces (ionic bonds, hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions), and reduces cooking loss via dense gel network formation. To verify these hypotheses, APPs and CPPs were isolated using Diethylaminoethyl (DEAE) chromatography and characterized for molecular weight distribution, amino acid composition, and residual salt content. Their effects on surimi gels were evaluated via multi-dimensional assays: core quality parameters (texture, color, cooking loss) were measured, while protein secondary structure, intermolecular interactions, microstructure, water distribution/mobility, and protein cross-linking were analyzed using FTIR, solvent extraction, SEM, LF-NMR, MRI, and SDS-PAGE.

This study comprehensively clarifies the effects and mechanisms of APPs/CPPs on low-salt surimi gels, highlighting the role of ionic charge in peptide-mediated gel improvement. It provides a sustainable peptide-based strategy for low-salt surimi product development, promotes high-value utilization of sheepskin, aligns with circular economy principles, and meets consumer demand for nutritious, low-sodium foods.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Materials

Sheepskin collagen peptides were provided by Zhong Tai Kang Yuan Co., Ltd. Huludao, Liaoning, China. Silver carp surimi was purchased from Honghu Jingli Aquatic Products Co., Ltd., Jingzhou, Hubei, China. DEAE fillers were obtained from Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. Beijing, China. NaCl and KCl were acquired from Tianjin Dongli District Tianda Chemical Reagent Factory, Tianjin, China and used as analytical grade without further purification. A BCA protein quantification kit was supplied by Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China.

2.2. Isolation Preparation and Characterization of APP and CPP

DEAE cellulose was used as the stationary phase for peptide separation [15]. Elution was performed with deionized water, followed by gradient NaCl solutions on a GE Healthcare AKTA avant 25 system from GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA. Anionic peptides (APPs) were eluted at 30% w/v NaCl and cationic peptides (CPPs) were collected separately before lyophilization. Ultrafiltration was applied to remove residual NaCl from the APPs. Salt content was determined via silver nitrate titration with a 2-g lyophilized sample dissolved in 50 mL of water, with 1 mL of 50 g/L potassium chromate added and titrated with standard silver nitrate until a persistent brick red precipitate formed. Amino acid composition was analyzed by mixing a 1-g sample with an internal standard and water, followed by ultrasonic extraction for 30 min, centrifugation, and derivatization with 24-dinitrofluorobenzene at 95 °C for 150 ± 10 min, neutralization with 10% glacial acetic acid, and analysis via a Shimadzu LC-2010C. The internal standard solution was prepared by accurately weighing 25 mg of α-aminobutyric acid, dissolving it in water, and diluting to a volume of 50 mL, yielding a 0.5-mg/mL internal standard solution. Molecular weight distribution was measured by HPLC with a BIOBASIC SEC 60 column, using 55% methanol as the mobile phase flow rate of 0.5 mL/min, and a column temperature of 25 °C.

2.3. Preparation of Surimi Gels

Surimi gels were prepared according to the method of Qi [16]. Partially thawed silver carp surimi was cut into small pieces, and 80 g of surimi was weighed for each treatment group, placed in a cutter-mixer, then chopped at 1800 rpm for 1 min. Four treatment groups were set up in the experiment: the 1% (w/w) salt group, the 1% (w/w) APP group, the 1% (w/w) CPP group, and the blank control group without any additives. These are designated as NaCl, APP, CPP, and Con in the subsequent figures and tables, respectively. A total of 20 g of ice water was added to adjust the moisture content of the system to 78% [17], followed by intermittent chopping at 3000 rpm for 2 min (10 s of operation alternating with 10 s of pause). Approximately 30 g of surimi paste was taken in triplicate from each treatment group, transferred into centrifuge tubes, and centrifuged at 2000× g for 15 min. The centrifuge tubes used were Corning 50-mL centrifuge tubes with specifications of 29.1 mm outer diameter and 114.4 mm total height. The samples were then subjected to a two-stage heat treatment: they were first incubated at 40 °C for 60 min, followed by heating at 90 °C for 30 min. After cooling, the gel samples were stored at 4 °C for 24 h for subsequent analysis.

2.4. Determination of Macroscopic Quality Parameters

The surimi gel was cut into cylindrical pieces with a height of 1.5 cm and a diameter of 2.5 cm for the determination of gel strength and TPA. Gel strength was evaluated with a TA.XT.plus texture analyzer from Stable Micro Systems, Godalming, UK, using a P/0.5 probe in compression mode. Pre-test and post-test speed were set at 1.0 mm/s, trigger force at 5.0 g, and compression strain at 30%. Breaking distance breaking force and gel strength were recorded in triplicate at 25 °C. The formula used was the following: gel strength = breaking force × breaking distance. Textural properties were measured with a P/50 probe in two-cycle compression, with pre-test speed of 3.0 mm/s, a test speed of 1.0 mm/s, a post-test speed of 3.0 mm/s, a trigger force of 5.0 g, and a compression strain 50% to record hardness, springiness, cohesiveness, and chewiness. Hardness is defined as the maximum force during the first compression, which physically reflects the resistance of the sample to deformation under an external force. Gumminess is calculated as the product of hardness and cohesiveness. Chewiness is obtained as the product of hardness, cohesiveness, and springiness, all of which are derived from the force and displacement data in the curve. Water holding capacity (WHC) was determined by weighing a 0.5-g gel sample (W1), wrapping it in double-layered filter paper, and centrifuging at 4000 rpm for 15 min [18]. The water precipitated on the gel surface was gently blotted away with filter paper, then the mass of the gel after centrifugation was immediately weighed (W2). The surimi gel was cut into cylindrical pieces with a height of 1.5 cm and a diameter of 2.5 cm for whiteness determination, with three measurements conducted for each experimental group. Whiteness was determined by measuring L* a* b* with an UltraScan PRO colorimeter from HunterLab, Reston, VA, USA, on 2-cm diameter, 2-cm height cylinders. 2.5. Analysis of Water State and Distribution

LF-NMR (low-field NMR) measurements were conducted with a NMI20-030H-I analyzer from Niumag Corporation, Shanghai, China. An approximately 2-g cylindrical gel sample was placed in an NMR (Nuclear Magnetic Resonance) tube with the following parameters: sampling frequency of 200 kHz, 90° pulse width of 26.00 μs, 180°, pulse width of 52.00 μs, echo time 0.5 ms, and 8 scans [19]. Transverse relaxation time (T2) and peak area proportions were obtained via inversion analysis. MRI (Magnetic resonance imaging) was used to visualize water distribution, with samples imaged under standard conditions to observe free water presence and homogeneity.

2.6. Analysis of Protein Structure and Interactions

Chemical interactions were assessed with four solvent systems: SA (50 mmol L−1 NaCl), SB (0.6 mol L−1 NaCl), SC (0.6 mol L−1 NaCl 1.5 mol L−1 urea), and SD (0.6 mol L−1 NaCl 8 mol L−1 urea). A total of 2 g of minced gel was homogenized with 10 mL of each solvent for 30 s, kept at 4 °C for 1 h, centrifuged at 8000 r/min for 10 min, and the protein content in supernatant was measured by BCA assay [6]. A total of 20 μL of the protein supernatant was added to a 96-well plate, followed by 200 μL of freshly prepared BCA working solution. After thorough mixing, the mixture was incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min, and the absorbance at 562 nm was measured using a microplate reader. A standard curve was constructed using bovine serum albumin (BSA), and the protein content of the samples was calculated accordingly. The contributions of ionic bonds, hydrogen bonds, and hydrophobic interactions were calculated using Equations (4)–(6). ρSA, ρSB, ρSC, and ρSD represent the protein concentration values of the supernatant solutions obtained after treatment with different solvents, as determined by the BCA assay.(4)Index of ionic bonds=ρSB−ρSA(5)Index of hydrogen bonds=ρSC−ρSB(6)Index of hydrophobic interactions=ρSD−ρSC

FTIR (Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy) analysis was performed on freeze-dried, 24-h ground, and 60-mesh sieved gel powder mixed with KBr at a ratio of 1:100, pressed into pellets. Spectra were acquired over 400–4000 cm−1 with a Frontier spectrometer from PerkinElmer, Shelton, CT, USA. The amide I band (1600–1700 cm−1) was deconvoluted with Omnic software and Gaussian curve fitting via PeakFit v4.12 to quantify α-helix, β-sheet, β-turn, and random coil proportions [20]. SDS-PAGE was conducted by mixing 3 g of minced gel with 20 mL of 5% SDS heating at 85 °C for 1 h, centrifuging at 8000 rpm for 15 min, mixing the supernatant with 5× loading buffer heating at 100 °C for 10 min, loading 10 µL onto the gel, staining with Coomassie Brilliant Blue for 2 h, and destaining and imaging with a ChemiDoc Touch system from Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA [21].

2.7. Observation of Microstructure by SEM

Gel samples were cut into 1 × 1 × 1 mm cubes using a sterile razor blade to avoid structural damage. Samples were immediately placed in a freeze dryer—Christ Alpha 1–2 LDplus, Martin Christ Gefriertrocknungsanlagen GmbH, Osterode am Harz, Germany—pre-cooled to −55 °C, and freeze-dried for 24 h under a vacuum pressure of 0.01 mbar to remove moisture without altering the gel network [22]. Dried samples were mounted on aluminum stubs with double-sided conductive tape and sputter-coated with a 10-nm thick layer of gold-palladium alloy using a Quorum Q150R ES sputter coater, UK, to enhance electrical conductivity. Observations were performed with a SU1510 scanning electron microscope from HITACHI, Tokyo, Japan, at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV. Images were captured at a magnification of ×1000 for each sample with three different regions selected to ensure representativeness. Microstructural features, including pore size, distribution network density, and surface smoothness were recorded for subsequent analysis.

2.8. Statistical Analyses

All data were analyzed with GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 software from GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA, and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) with at least three replicates per group. Statistical significance was determined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant, with * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, and *** p < 0.001.

3. Results and Discussion

3.1. Molecular Weight Distribution, Amino Acid Composition, and Salt Content of Isolated Peptides

The molecular weight distribution of peptides is a key factor influencing their functional properties in food systems, as smaller or larger molecular weight fractions may interact differently with myofibrillar proteins. The isolated anionic peptides (APPs) and cationic peptides (CPPs) from sheepskin collagen showed distinct molecular weight profiles via DEAE chromatography (Figure 1A). For APPs, the fractions in the ranges of 500–1000 Da, 1000–1300 Da, and >1300 Da accounted for 16.45%, 32.53%, and 51.02%, respectively. In contrast, CPPs exhibited a predominantly higher molecular weight distribution, with the same ranges representing 2.95%, 23.15%, and 73.90% of the total, respectively. This difference in molecular weight distribution is likely attributed to the ionic charge-based separation by DEAE cellulose, which binds anionic components more strongly and elutes them at higher NaCl concentrations, while cationic components are eluted earlier with lower salt or deionized water. Previous studies have reported that low molecular weight peptides can enhance surimi gel properties by forming non-covalent interactions with myofibrillar protein residues due to their exposed active sites [23]. For APPs, the relatively higher proportion of low molecular weight fractions (500–1300 Da accounting for 48.98%) may contribute to their bioactivity through specific functional groups that interact with surimi proteins. On the other hand, the high proportion of CPP fractions above 1300 Da (73.90%) suggests a potential physical filling effect in the gel network, which can improve structural compactness [24].

Amino acid composition analysis further revealed differences between APPs and CPPs that correlate with their functional effects (Table 1). APPs showed a higher content of acidic amino acids, such as glutamic acid (3.88%), while CPPs were rich in basic amino acids, including arginine (46.11%) and lysine (4.64%), compared to APPs (40.57% arginine and 3.99% lysine). Arginine and lysine are known to play crucial roles in low-salt surimi gel formation. Arginine can compete with Na+ in reducing Cl- loss and improving gel network integrity under low-salt conditions [25]. Lysine assists in gel formation and enhances physicochemical properties when combined with transglutaminase [26]. The higher content of these basic amino acids in CPPs explains their superior performance in improving gel quality, as these residues can form more electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonds with surimi proteins. Therefore, CPPs can improve surimi gel through the synergistic effect of physical filling and electrostatic adsorption, rather than via independent mechanisms. Additionally, APPs’ higher glutamic acid content contributes to their anionic nature, which may influence interactions with metal ions such as Ca2+ in the surimi system. Glycine and proline, two other amino acids abundant in both peptides, are characteristic of collagen peptides and are known to enhance protein solubility and gel stability [27]. The differences in amino acid composition between APPs and CPPs directly affect their charge properties, molecular interactions with surimi proteins, and, ultimately, their gel-modifying effects.

Salt content determination was critical to exclude confounding effects from residual NaCl introduced during APP elution. Silver nitrate titration showed no significant difference in NaCl content between APPs and CPPs (p > 0.05) (Figure 1B). This result confirms that the functional differences observed between the two peptide fractions are not due to variations in salt concentration but rather to their inherent properties, such as molecular weight distribution, amino acid composition, and ionic charge. The successful desalination of APPs via ultrafiltration ensures that the experimental design accurately reflects the effects of the peptides themselves. This is particularly important because NaCl is a known modifier of surimi gel properties, and any residual salt in the peptide fractions could mask or exaggerate their effects. By verifying equivalent salt contents, the study establishes a reliable basis for comparing the performance of APPs and CPPs in low-salt surimi gels.

3.2. Macroscopic Quality Attributes of Low-Salt Surimi Gels Supplemented with APPs and CPPs

Gel strength is a primary indicator of surimi quality as it reflects the ability of myofibrillar proteins to form a cohesive network [28]. All three additive groups showed significant improvements in gel strength compared to the control group, with CPPs exhibiting gel strength comparable to NaCl and both being significantly higher than APPs (Figure 2A). This result aligns with the amino acid composition data in which CPPs’ higher arginine and lysine content likely promoted myofibrillar protein unfolding and aggregation. Gel strength is governed by the rate of protein unfolding and the formation of cross-links between protein molecules. For CPPs, the positive charge of CPPs (due to a high basic amino acid content) interacts with the negatively charged surimi proteins (dependent on the neutral pH (~7.0) of the surimi gel system, where surimi myofibrillar proteins (pI = 5.5) carry a net negative charge and CPPs (pI ~ 9.0) maintain a positive charge). This electrostatic attraction brings protein molecules closer together, facilitating the formation of a dense network. For APPs, the anionic nature of APPs may cause electrostatic repulsion with negatively charged surimi proteins. However, APPs chelate endogenous Ca2+ in surimi to activate transglutaminase, but this effect is moderate due to electrostatic repulsion between APPs (anionic) and surimi proteins, which may limit the uniform distribution of Ca2+. Therefore, the gel strength of APPs is lower than that of CPPs and NaCl.

Water-holding capacity (WHC) and cooking loss are closely related to gel network stability, as a well-formed network can trap water molecules and reduce moisture loss during processing. All additive groups showed significantly higher WHC (Figure 2B) and lower cooking loss (Figure 2C) compared to Con, with no significant difference between CPPs, APPs, and NaCl. WHC improvement in CPPs and APPs is attributed to the modified gel structure, which forms a more stable network to entrap water. For CPPs, the electrostatic bonds between CPPs and surimi proteins anchor the peptides within the network restricting water mobility. For APPs, the activation of transglutaminase via Ca2+ chelation leads to the formation of more cross-links, enhancing network stability. LF-NMR data later confirmed the reduced free water and increased bound water in these groups, supporting the WHC results. Cooking loss reduction is consistent with WHC improvements, as a stable gel network minimizes moisture evaporation during heating. Previous studies have reported that arginine can form hydrogen bonds with myosin, reducing water migration, which further explains the improved water retention in CPPs and APPs [29]. The correlation between WHC and gel structure stability indirectly confirms that APPs and CPPs enhance the structural integrity of low-salt surimi gels [30].

Textural properties, including hardness, gumminess, cohesiveness, and chewiness, are critical for consumer acceptance. The TPA analysis showed that all additive groups had significantly higher hardness, chewiness, and gumminess compared to Con, with CPPs exhibiting the highest hardness. Meanwhile, the TPA results reflect the physical properties of surimi gels; a higher value does not necessarily indicate an optimal surimi gel, and consumer acceptability should be specifically investigated. The low hardness in Con is due to weak intermolecular interactions between surimi proteins, which lead to a loose gel network with poor resistance to compression. NaCl dissolves salt-soluble proteins such as myosin and actin, promoting network formation and increasing hardness. For CPPs, the strong electrostatic attraction between CPPs and negatively charged surimi proteins causes rapid local protein aggregation, resulting in a denser network and higher hardness than in NaCl and APPs [31]. For APPs, despite the electrostatic repulsion with surimi proteins, the activation of transglutaminase via Ca2+ chelation still improves network formation, leading to higher textural properties than Con but lower than in CPPs. Gumminess and chewiness, which reflect the elasticity and energy required to chew the gel, also showed similar trends with CPPs and NaCl, outperforming APPs. These results indicate that both APPs and CPPs can compensate for the textural loss caused by low salt concentrations, with CPPs being more effective due to their unique amino acid composition and charge properties.

Color attributes, particularly whiteness, are important for surimi product acceptability as consumers prefer products with a bright white appearance. As shown in (Table 2), the control group Con and groups NaCl and APP showed no significant differences in whiteness, while CPPs exhibited a pronounced decrease. A whiteness value between 70 and 80 is considered acceptable; both CPPs and APPs met this criterion, while NaCl exceeded the upper limit [32]. The lower whiteness in CPPs is attributed to the Maillard reaction, which occurs between basic amino acids (arginine and lysine) in CPPs and reduces sugars in surimi. Basic amino acids are highly susceptible to the Maillard reaction, leading to browning and reduced whiteness [29]. For APPs, the higher content of acidic amino acids may inhibit the Maillard reaction, resulting in whiteness comparable to Con and NaCl. The L* value (lightness) showed no significant change in NaCl and APPs compared to Con, but CPPs had a marked reduction consistent with the whiteness results. The a* and b* values (redness and yellowness) were significantly higher in APPs than in Con, but lower in NaCl and CPPs. These color changes are associated with peptide–protein interactions and chemical reactions in the gel system. Despite the lower whiteness in CPPs, the value remains within the acceptable range, indicating that CPPs can be used as a salt substitute without compromising consumer acceptance of color.

Overall, CPPs’ high contents of arginine (46.11%) and lysine (4.64%) supply abundant positive charges that strengthen electrostatic attraction with surimi proteins, while their dominant high-molecular-weight fraction (>1300 Da, 73.90%) fills voids in the gel network. In contrast, APPs’ relatively high glutamic acid content (3.88%) causes partial electrostatic repulsion, and their larger proportion of low-molecular-weight components (500–1300 Da, 48.98%) results in uneven protein aggregation.

3.3. Water State Distribution and Mobility in Surimi Gels as Revealed Using LF-NMR and MRI

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) provides insights into water state and mobility in surimi gels by measuring transverse relaxation times (T2). Three distinct T2 peaks were observed in all the samples (Figure 3A), corresponding to bound water (T2b 0.1–10 ms), immobilized water (T21 10–200 ms), and free water (T22 100–1000 ms) [31]. Bound water is tightly associated with protein molecules, immobilized water is restricted by the gel network, and free water is freely mobile. The addition of APPs, CPPs, or NaCl caused a distinct leftward shift in the T21 peak, indicating reduced water mobility and tighter binding within the gel matrix [23]. For NaCl, this shift is due to NaCl enhancing myofibrillar protein solubilization and promoting the formation of a regular ordered network that restricts water movement. For CPPs and APPs, the shift is attributed to the presence of arginine in both peptides, which partially compensates for poor protein solubility under low-salt conditions, facilitating the development of a superior water-entrapping network [33]. The T22 peak (free water) showed a more pronounced leftward shift in CPPs than in APPs, indicating that CPPs are more effective at reducing free water mobility. This result aligns with the WHC data where CPPs had slightly higher water retention than APPs.

The proportional distribution of water states further confirms the effects of APPs and CPPs on water retention. Compared to the control group, all additive groups showed a significant increase in bound water (T2b) proportion and a marked decrease in free water (T22) proportion (Figure 3B). The CPP group had the highest bound water content and the lowest free water content, followed by NaCl and the APP group. The APP group retained a relatively higher free water content, which explains its slightly lower WHC compared to CPPs and NaCl. The increase in bound water is due to the formation of a denser gel network that can trap more water molecules. For CPPs, the electrostatic attraction between CPPs and surimi proteins forms a tight network with numerous small pores effectively immobilizing water. For APPs, the activation of transglutaminase leads to cross-link formation, but the presence of larger protein aggregates (observed later in SEM) creates voids that retain more free water. The decrease in free water proportion is critical for reducing cooking loss, as free water is easily lost during heating. These results demonstrate that APPs and CPPs modify the gel network structure to alter water distribution, with CPPs being more effective at converting free water to bound or immobilized water.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) technology can visually represent the distribution and migration of water in surimi gels. As shown in Figure 3C, the control group exhibits numerous red spots in the image, indicating the presence of free water with an uneven distribution [34]. The experimental group with added sodium chloride displays a uniform red hue, suggesting that the red signal no longer represents free water but rather bound water, immobilized and evenly distributed within the gel network, reflecting a highly homogenized water distribution. The CPP group similarly shows a broad and uniform red area, though with a slightly lower red intensity compared to the sodium chloride group. In contrast, the APP group exhibited a strong red tone similar to that of the control group, indicating a relatively high free water content. However, its structure appeared more uniform compared to the control group, with no blue spots observed. The MRI images are consistent with the low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) results, confirming that APPs can reduce the free water content in low-salt surimi gel and improve its water distribution. The red-yellow hue displayed by the CPP group indicates its superior water-holding capacity compared to the APP group, which can be attributed to its denser gel network structure. The remaining heterogeneity in CPPs and APPs may be due to uneven peptide distribution or protein aggregation during gelation. These visual and quantitative data collectively show that APPs and CPPs enhance water retention in low-salt surimi gels by modifying the water state and distribution, with CPPs exhibiting superior performance.

The correlation between water state and gel quality is evident, as the reduction in free water and the increase in bound water are directly associated with higher WHC, lower cooking loss, and better textural properties. Water mobility is closely linked to gel network structure; a dense uniform network restricts water movement, reducing free water content. For CPPs, the combination of electrostatic interactions, physical filling, and high arginine content creates a network that effectively traps water. For APPs, the Ca2+ chelation and transglutaminase activation lead to network formation, but the presence of larger aggregates limits water retention efficiency. The LF-NMR and MRI data complement the macroscopic quality results, providing a mechanistic understanding of how APPs and CPPs improve water holding capacity. These techniques reveal that the peptides modify not only the quantity but also the state of the water in surimi gels, which is critical for the development of high-quality, low-sodium surimi products.

3.4. Microstructure and Protein Secondary Structure Modifications of Surimi Gels

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to observe the microstructure of surimi gels, which directly influences their functional properties (Figure 4). The control group exhibited a rough surface with a poorly developed gel network and numerous large voids. These voids compromise water-holding capacity, as free water can easily accumulate and be lost during processing. In contrast, the addition of APPs, CPPs, or NaCl promoted the formation of a more ordered, denser network. The group with NaCl showed a porous, smooth, and uniform network with small evenly distributed pores, which is typical of high-quality surimi gels. The CPP group had a higher density of fine pores, but the distribution between large and small pores was less coordinated than in the NaCl group, resulting in overall heterogeneity. The APP group exhibited a degree of directional pore alignment with some compact regions and other loose areas, leading to inferior uniformity compared to the CPP group. These structural differences explain the variations in gel quality, with NaCl having the best microstructure, followed by CPPs and then APPs.

The microstructure differences between CPPs and APPs are attributed to their distinct charge properties and interaction mechanisms. For CPPs, the cationic peptides form strong electrostatic attractions with negatively charged surimi proteins, leading to rapid local aggregation and the formation of fine pores. The physical filling effect of CPPs’ high molecular weight fractions also contributes to network density. For APPs, the anionic peptides chelate Ca2+ from the surimi system, leading to excessive protein aggregation and precipitation. This excessive aggregation creates larger protein clusters and uneven pore distribution, reducing network uniformity. Additionally, the electrostatic repulsion between APPs and surimi proteins may hinder uniform network formation, leading to loose regions in the gel. Despite these limitations, both CPPs and APPs show significant improvements over Con, indicating that even with structural imperfections, the peptides can enhance gel microstructure compared to the low-salt control.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) (Figure 5A) spectroscopy was used to analyze protein secondary structure changes, as these are closely related to gel formation. The amide I band (1600–1700 cm−1) was deconvoluted to quantify the relative proportions of α-helix, β-sheet, β-turn, and random coil (Figure 5B). Compared to the control group, all additive groups showed a decrease in α-helix content and an increase in β-sheet proportion, indicating a conformational shift from α-helix to β-sheet. This shift is a key step in surimi gelation, as α-helices are stable structures that unfold during heating, exposing hydrophobic regions and functional groups for cross-linking. β-sheets are more flexible than α-helices, facilitating protein aggregation and network formation. The CPP group had the highest β-sheet content, followed by NaCl and the APP group, while APPs showed a greater tendency to form β-turns. The formation of β-turns in APPs is attributed to Ca2+-mediated salt bridges, which modify the secondary structure [1].

The conformational shift observed in the FTIR spectra is influenced by peptide–protein interactions. For CPPs, the electrostatic attraction between CPPs and surimi proteins induces structural rearrangement, the unfolding of α-helices, and the promotion of β-sheet formation. The high arginine content in CPPs also contributes to this shift, as arginine is known to promote the transition of myosin secondary structure from α-helix to other forms [35]. For APPs, the chelation of Ca2+ by APPs forms cross-links between protein molecules, leading to β-sheet and β-turn formation. The differences in secondary structure between CPPs and APPs explain their varying gel properties, as β-sheets contribute to network strength and flexibility [36]. The higher β-sheet content in CPPs correlates with their superior gel strength and hardness, while the higher β-turn content in APPs may limit network flexibility, leading to lower textural performance. These results demonstrate that APPs and CPPs modify protein secondary structure to promote gel formation, with CPPs inducing a more favorable conformational shift for gel quality.

The correlation between microstructure and secondary structure is evident in that the α-helix to β-sheet shift leads to improved protein aggregation and network formation. A higher β-sheet content is associated with a denser, more uniform microstructure, as observed in NaCl and CPPs. In contrast APPs’ higher β-turn content and lower β-sheet content correlate with their less uniform microstructure and lower gel quality. These structural modifications are the direct consequences of peptide–protein interactions and ionic charge effects. The combination of SEM and FTIR data provides a comprehensive understanding of how APPs and CPPs improve low-salt surimi gel quality by modifying both its macroscopic structure and molecular conformation.

3.5. Intermolecular Interactions and Protein Cross-Linking in Surimi Gels

Intermolecular interactions, including ionic bonds, hydrogen bonds, and hydrophobic interactions, play critical roles in maintaining surimi gel structure and stability [37]; see Figure 5C–E. The analysis of these interactions using solvent extraction and BCA assay showed that all additive groups had significantly higher ionic bond strength compared to the control group Con. For NaCl, it provides Na+ ions that shield repulsion between negatively charged protein groups, promoting ionic bond formation. For CPPs, the cationic peptides directly form ionic bonds, with negatively charged regions of surimi proteins enhancing interaction strength. For APPs, the chelation of Ca2+ by APPs facilitates electrostatic bridging between protein molecules, indirectly contributing to ionic bond formation. The CPP group exhibited the highest ionic bond strength, followed by NaCl and the APP group, which correlates with its superior gel quality. Ionic bonds are important for initial network formation as they bring protein molecules closer together, facilitating other interactions.

Hydrogen bonds are crucial for enhancing gel strength and water-holding capacity, as they stabilize the gel network and trap water molecules [38]. The CPP group had a hydrogen bond strength comparable to NaCl, while APPs showed a less pronounced increase. The high arginine content in CPPs allows the formation of new hydrogen bonds between peptides and myofibrillar proteins [34]. Additionally, the strong electrostatic interactions induced by CPPs alter protein conformation, exposing more backbone groups for hydrogen bonding. For APPs, the presence of arginine also promotes hydrogen bond formation, but the electrostatic repulsion between APPs and surimi proteins limits this effect. Hydrogen bonds work synergistically with ionic bonds to strengthen the gel network, explaining why CPPs and APPs with higher ionic and hydrogen bond strengths have better gel properties. The lower hydrogen bond strength in APPs contributes to their inferior water retention and textural performance.

Hydrophobic interactions were the most prominent intermolecular forces in all samples, indicating their primary role in surimi gel formation. The CPP group had the strongest hydrophobic interactions, followed by APPs and NaCl. Heat-induced protein denaturation exposes hydrophobic groups, which aggregate to form cross-links. For CPPs, the electrostatic attraction between CPPs and surimi proteins brings protein molecules into closer proximity, leading to tighter packing of hydrophobic regions and stronger interactions [6]. For NaCl, it enhances protein solubilization and unfolding, exposing more hydrophobic groups. For APPs, the chelation of Ca2+ leads to protein aggregation, but the uneven distribution of aggregates limits hydrophobic interaction strength. Hydrophobic interactions are critical for gel network cohesion as they form the backbone of the cross-linked structure. The higher hydrophobic interaction strength in CPPs explains their superior gel strength and hardness. Overall, CPPs improve gel quality primarily through electrostatic attraction + hydrogen bonding + physical filling, whereas APPs act via Ca2+-mediated transglutaminase activation, with their efficacy limited by electrostatic repulsion.

SDS-PAGE analysis revealed changes in protein composition, particularly in the myosin heavy chain (MHC) and actin bands, which are key components of surimi gelation (Figure 5F). Compared to the control group, all additive groups showed reduced intensity of MHC and actin bands, indicating increased protein cross-linking or aggregation. The NaCl group had the most pronounced band fading, followed by CPPs and then APPs. The reduction in MHC band intensity suggests that a larger proportion of MHC was incorporated into the gel network via covalent cross-links, such as disulfide bonds or transglutaminase-catalyzed bonds. MHC plays a crucial role in gelation as its tail regions entangle and head regions associate during heating to form the network. The more pronounced band fading in CPPs compared to APPs indicates higher MHC participation in cross-linking, which correlates with its superior gel quality. These results show that APPs and CPPs promote protein cross-linking, with CPPs being more effective at facilitating MHC incorporation into the gel network.

The combination of intermolecular interaction analysis and SDS-PAGE data reveals the mechanisms by which APPs and CPPs improve low-salt surimi gel quality. For CPPs, the synergy of strong ionic bonds hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions promotes extensive protein cross-linking and network formation. The high arginine and lysine content in CPPs enhances these interactions by providing additional functional groups for bonding. For APPs, the chelation of Ca2+ activates transglutaminase, leading to cross-link formation; however, electrostatic repulsion and excessive aggregation limit interaction strength and network uniformity. These mechanisms explain why CPPs outperform APPs in improving gel properties. The results also confirm that both peptides can compensate for the reduced ionic strength in low-salt surimi by enhancing intermolecular interactions and protein cross-linking, offering a sustainable strategy for sodium reduction.

4. Conclusions

This study first isolated anionic peptides (APPs) and cationic peptides (CPPs) from sheepskin collagen hydrolysate via DEAE ion-exchange chromatography, systematically elucidating their ionic charge-dependent mechanisms as effective salt substitutes for low-salt silver carp surimi gels. Both peptides significantly improved gel strength, water-holding capacity, and textural properties of low-salt surimi gels, reduced cooking loss, and yielded products with quality comparable to the NaCl-added group, with CPPs outperforming APPs. CPPs’ superiority stems from their higher arginine (46.11%) and lysine (4.64%) contents, facilitating stronger electrostatic attraction with negatively charged surimi proteins (pI = 5.5), and a predominantly high molecular weight distribution (>1300 Da, 73.90%) that exerts a physical filling effect. In contrast, APPs’ abundance of acidic amino acids and low-molecular-weight fractions induces electrostatic repulsion and excessive Ca2+ chelation, leading to uneven protein aggregation.

Analytical results from Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), SDS-PAGE, LF-NMR, MRI, and SEM confirmed that CPPs induce the conformational transition of myofibrillar proteins from α-helix to β-sheet, promote myosin heavy chain cross-linking, and strengthen intermolecular interactions, ultimately forming a dense, stable gel network with reduced free water, increased bound water, and fewer large pores for efficient water retention. Beyond these technical insights, the application of these peptides enables the high-value utilization of sheepskin (a meat processing by-product), aligns with circular economy principles, and provides a sustainable peptide-based strategy for developing low-salt surimi products, meeting consumers’ demand for nutritious, low-sodium foods.

This study has the following limitations: systematic toxicological and allergenic evaluations of APPs and CPPs were not conducted; only a single addition concentration (1% w/w) was tested, leaving the dose–effect relationship (e.g., 0.5–2.0% w/w) unclear; and their stability and efficacy in complex food matrices (e.g., surimi with fat or spices) and under long-term storage/freeze–thaw cycles remain unvalidated. Future research should optimize CPPs’ addition concentration to balance cost and efficacy, explore synergies with other salt substitutes (e.g., KCl, hydrocolloids), assess safety and stability, extend applications to other aquatic/meat gel products (e.g., fish balls, low-salt sausages), and conduct gradient concentration tests to clarify dose-dependent effects, thereby providing further guidance for peptide-based salt substitutes in low-sodium food production.
