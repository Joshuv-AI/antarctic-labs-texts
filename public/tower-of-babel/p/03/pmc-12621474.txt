Advances in natural product-based nanoparticles for the treatment of acute lung injury
Authors: Yunfeng Zheng, Yuting Zhao, Shuo Zhang, Min Wang, Xianwen Wang, Xu-Lin Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Acute lung injury (ALI) is a rapid-onset inflammatory pathology in pulmonary tissue induced by multiple causative agents. ALI usually develops rapidly within hours to days after an acute episode, and despite the development of aggressive therapeutic measures, the prognosis of patients with ALI remains poor. Currently, the clinical treatment of ALI is based on supportive therapy, and with the advancement of technology, many natural products have been used for the treatment of ALI because of their advantages, such as lower drug resistance and more abundant sources; however, shortcomings such as its lower targeting limit its further clinical application. The use of nanotechnology may be an effective way to address these issues. The combination of natural products and nanotechnology offers the possibility of solving the problems of their low bioavailability and poor water solubility, which is expected to promote their application in the field of ALI therapy. Natural product-based nanomaterials can control the progression of ALI through mechanisms such as modulating immune responses and reducing tissue inflammation. This review focuses on the roles of natural product-based nanoparticles in the treatment of ALI and further elaborates on the problems and challenges faced by natural product-based nanoparticles in the effective clinical treatment of ALI.

Introduction

ALI is an acute inflammatory pathological change in the lung that manifests as a disruption in oxygenation and an imbalance in the ventilation‒blood flow ratio [1]. ALI usually develops rapidly within a few hours to a few days after the onset of an acute episode, and patients may present with tachypnoea, hypoxemia, and other clinical symptoms [2]. Patients with ALI, especially acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), have a poor prognosis despite aggressive treatment [3]. The current clinical treatment for ALI is supportive therapy [4,5], with the main goals of improving oxygenation, reducing lung injury, controlling the inflammatory response, and preventing complications [6,7]. In the treatment of ALI, drugs are often administered both intravenously and via inhalation. When administered intravenously, whether the drug can be accurately targeted in the blood circulation to enter the lesion in the lung is a key factor affecting its efficacy. When administered via inhalation, whether the drug can penetrate the thick mucus barrier to reach the treatment site is an issue that must be considered. In addition, the toxicity of many of the drugs used to treat ALI may lead to associated toxic side effects, and these issues limit their further application.

Natural product-based nanomaterials have attracted attention in the treatment of ALI because of their excellent bioavailability, superior lesion-targeting ability, and more precise and controlled drug release. Natural products refer to relevant metabolites with specific functions and special activities that are isolated and extracted from plants and animals as well as microorganisms in nature via certain techniques. Since the discovery of penicillin, more than 20,000 natural products [8], which are widely used in the development of medicines, the treatment of diseases, the development of agro-industry, and other important fields, have been discovered. However, the low bioavailability and insufficiently defined pharmacokinetics of natural products themselves have limited their further application in medicine [9,10]. Currently, the use of nanomaterials may be an effective way to solve such problems. Since nanomaterials are characterized by their high specific surface area and good targeting properties, a series of natural product-based nanoparticles have been developed by combining natural products and nanomaterials to address the shortcomings of natural products. Combining natural products with nanoparticles in different ways can greatly improve the bioavailability, controlled release properties, and targeting of natural products [11], which greatly solves the shortcomings of natural products in practical applications.

At present, there have been a series of studies on the use of nanomaterials for the targeted treatment of ALI [12], but a systematic summary of the treatment of ALI by combining nanomaterials with natural products has not yet been reported. In this work, we first focus on the clinical manifestations and pathophysiological mechanisms of ALI, review the classification of natural product‒nanoparticle combinations and the therapeutic effects of different types of nanomaterials on ALI, and further elaborate on the lack of further applications of natural product‒based nanomaterials (Scheme 1).Scheme 1Natural product-based nanoparticles for ALI treatment.Scheme 1

ALI

Causes of ALI

ALI is a severe disease characterized by acute onset, hypoxemia, and noncardiogenic pulmonary edema. It is characterized by acute respiratory insufficiency, including shortness of breath. The etiology of ALI varies and is usually divided into two categories: direct and indirect [13,14]. Direct lung injury, such as pneumonia, inhalation of noxious gases, and injury caused by mechanical ventilation, and indirect lung injury, such as severe trauma, acute pancreatitis, infection, and sepsis, are closely related to ARDS, which is often regarded as the extreme manifestation of ALI. ARDS is a form of severe hypoxemia caused by various acute injuries that are accompanied by alveolar collapse, pulmonary edema, and fibrosis [15]. ALI and ARDS are serious diffuse lung diseases that pose considerable health burdens worldwide and have high mortality rates [[16], [17], [18]].

Mechanisms of ALI

The pathogenesis of ALI is complex and primarily involves the following aspects. When stimulated by factors such as infection, trauma, or mechanical ventilation, inflammatory mediators (e.g., cytokines and chemokines) are activated within the body, leading to damage to alveolar epithelial cells and capillary endothelial cells, increased capillary permeability, and the leakage of proteins and fluids into the alveolar space, resulting in pulmonary edema and impaired gas exchange [19,20]. Pulmonary edema and inflammatory responses cause lung tissue stiffness, leading to symptoms such as decreased lung compliance, increased respiratory rate, dyspnea, and hypoxemia. When alveoli are filled with fluid or collapse, the ratio of ventilation to blood flow becomes imbalanced, further exacerbating hypoxemia [21]. Additionally, during the course of ALI, immune system activation exhibits bidirectional effects: while the initial immune response can eliminate pathogens, excessive immune responses exacerbate inflammation and cause further lung damage [22]. Pathology of ALI

The main pathological changes associated with ALI are divided into exudative, proliferative, and fibrotic phases. During the early exudative phase, damage to the alveolar‒capillary barrier leads to fluid leakage from blood vessels into the alveolar lumen, causing pulmonary edema [23]. As tissue damage triggers neutrophil migration, activation of the inflammatory response further leads to alveolar epithelial cell damage; filling of the alveolar lumen with inflammatory cells, proteins, and fluids; and the initial development of distinctive hyaline membranes in the alveolar interstitial space, which affects oxygen exchange [24]. As a result of alveolar filling and the loss of surface-active substances, some alveoli may collapse, further leading to a decline in lung function. As the disease progresses, the diseased tissue in the lungs begins to repair itself in a regular manner and enters the proliferative phase. Regeneration of the epithelial layer helps the lungs clear edema fluid, macrophages remove intra-alveolar debris, and lung compliance is gradually restored. In contrast, some patients may still progress to the fibrotic stage, which is caused by failure of alveolar collagen clearance and cystic changes. Dense collagen fibrosis and hyalinization may occur in the alveolar walls, manifested by thickening of the alveolar septa and limited gas exchange [16].

General treatment of ALI

Traditional treatments for ALI primarily focus on symptomatic and supportive care after disease onset, including mechanical ventilation, the application of antibiotics, fluid management, and treatment of the initial injury or underlying disease [25,26]. However, existing therapeutic methods have many drawbacks. For example, while low tidal volume ventilation (LTVV) can significantly improve the prognosis of ARDS patients, it often carries risks of hyperventilation and alveolar overdistension during mechanical ventilation, potentially leading to lung injury. Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) can alleviate the oxygenation burden for critically ill patients who are unresponsive to mechanical ventilation but may increase the risk of bleeding [27]. Currently, drugs such as glucocorticoids, vasodilators, and diuretics have been explored for the treatment of ALI, but there are still significant limitations in their clinical application. For example, early application of glucocorticoids may increase the risk of systemic secondary infections; vasodilators, although improving pulmonary circulation, may disrupt ventilation-blood flow ratios and affect oxygenation efficiency; and overuse of diuretics may decrease cardiac output and further deteriorate organ perfusion. These potential side effects limit their efficacy and highlight the need to develop more precise treatment strategies. Additionally, a range of novel drugs have been developed for ALI treatment, such as biomolecular drugs composed of chemical compounds [28], novel drug delivery systems [29], and natural products [30,31]; however, these drugs have limited effects [32].

Currently, in addition to mechanical ventilation, there are no effective treatment methods. Therefore, the exploration of drugs with high bioavailability and targeted delivery has become a key focus of research.

Natural products used in the treatment of ALI

Natural products are biologically active compounds found in nature [33]. These compounds can be nonessential small-molecule compounds produced by organisms during growth and development; these compounds are not directly involved in basic metabolic processes in organisms but may have important ecological and biological functions. Many natural products are biologically active in specific ways and have different biological roles and functions. Many natural products are used in traditional folk remedies in many cultures and have been important in the history of mankind. The wide variety of sources of natural products is important for the development of new drugs, and many modern drugs are based on the optimization or modification of certain natural products with specific functions [34]. The earliest natural products of mankind were obtained from plants as early as 1575; ancient Chinese books documented the use of fermentation to extract gallic acid from the plant Schisandra chinensis, which was the world’s earliest report on the extraction of organic acids from natural products; at the beginning of the 19th century, morphine was first isolated from poppy by a German pharmacist and was successfully used in the clinic to achieve good results; and in the early 20th century, with the discovery of artemisinin and quinine, malaria, which had long plagued the world’s human beings at that time, was overcome [35]. Currently, an increasing number of natural products have been obtained and widely used in the prevention and treatment of diseases because of their excellent biocompatibility and low toxicity, including antibacterial, anticancer, and antimicrobial effects. Curcumin (Cur) is a polyphenolic compound with a variety of biological activities extracted from plants in the ginger family, such as turmeric, and experimental results have shown that it has good bactericidal effects on Escherichia coli, Bacillus subtilis, and Clostridium perfringens [36]. A polysaccharide compound extracted from poria mushrooms known as poria polysaccharide has been shown to inhibit bladder cancer progression by interfering with the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway, which in turn regulates the M1-type macrophage program [37]. Cinnamic acid can reduce the levels of related inflammatory factors, such as NLRP3 and IL-1β, in myocardial tissues after ischemia and reperfusion, attenuate the inflammatory response, and significantly improve cardiac function [38]. As the applications of natural products continue to expand, their use in the treatment of ALI is increasingly being investigated. Natural products are unique structures formed through long-term evolution in nature. They possess various complex active structures, such as the ketone conjugated skeletons with drug-like structures, rich stereochemical centers, multisubstituted aromatic ring systems, and sp3 carbon structures [39]. These diverse chemical structures that enable them to demonstrate excellent efficacy in the treatment of ALI. Natural products come from a wide range of sources and can be broadly classified into three categories according to their different sources: plant origin, animal origin and microbial origin.

Natural products of plant origin

As an important source of natural products, plants are widely distributed in nature. Their extracts are derived mainly from the roots, stems, leaves and other parts of plants. In fact, the earliest natural products were isolated from plants [40]. Many plant-derived natural products are widely used for their unique properties. For example, quercetin, a flavonoid widely distributed in the plant kingdom, is well known for its remarkable anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. It effectively improves the pathological process and prognosis of ALI by activating the Sirt1/Nrf2/Gpx4 signaling pathway [41]. In addition, resveratrol, a naturally occurring nonflavonoid polyphenolic compound, significantly ameliorated ALI symptoms in mice by inhibiting the activation of NLRP3 inflammatory vesicles, activating the Pink1/Parkin signaling pathway, and promoting mitochondrial autophagy [42]. Baicalin is the primary active flavonoid component of Scutellaria baicalensis Georgi and has significant anti-inflammatory and lung-protective effects. It reduces IL-1β and TNF-α levels in LPS-induced ALI mice and inhibits SARS-CoV-2-induced cellular damage in vitro [43]. Rb1 ginsenoside is one of the main components of ginseng. It reduces inflammation caused by Staphylococcus aureus by decreasing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines [44]. Berberine, which is isolated from Corydalis has a wide range of biological activities. In the ALI model, this compound exerts a protective effect by alleviating pulmonary edema, inhibiting inflammatory responses and neutrophil infiltration, and significantly reduces the expression levels of multiple proinflammatory factors, such as TNF-α and IL-6 [30]. In addition, traditional Chinese herbal formulas also have a positive effects on ALI. Da Cheng Qi Tang originates from the Shanghan Zabing Lun and consists of four Chinese herbs: rhubarb, zhi shi, hou po, and mang xiao. Experimental studies have shown that this formula can significantly downregulate the expression levels of key proinflammatory factors in a sepsis-induced ALI model. Additionally, by reducing the dry-to-wet weight ratio of lung tissue, it effectively improves sepsis-induced pathological damage to lung tissue [45].

Natural products of animal origin

Natural products of animal origin are usually extracted from the shells, hair and other body tissues of animals. For example, chitosan (CS) is a polysaccharide extracted from the shells of marine organisms such as shrimp and crabs. Owing to its excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability, CS is widely used [46]. It is often used as a biomaterial for wound dressings and as a hemostatic agent [47]. In addition, CS has shown promising applications in drug delivery and the ability to deliver drugs to ALI lesions, improving patient prognosis [48,49]. Naamidine J (NJ), an alkaloid derived from sponges, exerts potent anti-inflammatory effects and improves the prognosis of ALI through multiple mechanisms [50]. Taurocholic acid modulates proinflammatory cytokine secretion, the activation of the MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathways, and the activation of TLR2 in macrophages to ameliorate ALI [51].

Natural products of microbial origin

Natural products of microbial origin are bioactive substances extracted from various microorganisms, including mainly antibiotics and peptides. The extraction of antibiotics from microorganisms is one of the most effective methods for the treatment of bacterial infections. In recent decades, many antibiotics have been successfully extracted with remarkable results in the treatment of bacterial infections. For example, antibiotics such as penicillin, cephalosporin, erythromycin, and streptomycin have been widely used in clinical practice to effectively combat a wide range of bacterial infections and are widely used clinically in the treatment of ALI [[52], [53], [54]].

However, some potential challenges may limit the further utilization of natural products. For example, the low absorption of some natural products means that larger drug doses may be required to achieve the desired therapeutic effect, and the resulting reduced biosafety and associated drug side effects may be counterproductive, possibly due to factors such as their lower solubility [55]. Bioavailability is another major challenge because many natural products are rapidly metabolized in vivo, which greatly reduces their therapeutic efficacy [55]. In addition, the limited targeting of natural products results in the inability of drugs to reach the lesions in a targeted manner and eradicate the disease with precision, which greatly affects the efficacy of the drugs [56]. Since the specific mechanisms of action of many natural products have not yet been clearly investigated and their absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion characteristics, their pharmacokinetic properties are more difficult to monitor. In cases of ALI caused by different etiologies, this unpredictability makes rational drug use more complicated [57]. In addition, the combination of natural products and antibiotics may cause interactions that affect the efficacy of the drugs. Resveratrol, a natural product with antibacterial activity, may eliminate ROS produced by antibiotics through its antioxidant properties and antagonize the bactericidal effects of antibiotics in the treatment of infectious ALI [58].

These deficiencies may present challenges that affect their use as new drug candidates. Therefore, combining nanotechnology with natural products seems to be an effective solution for addressing the limitations of the physicochemistry of natural products. These materials can enhance the therapeutic potential of natural products by increasing their solubility [59], bioavailability, and pharmacokinetics. It can encapsulate hydrophobic natural products, improve their solubility and bioavailability [59,60], and lead to better dispersion in organisms.

Classification of natural product-based nanoparticles

Combinations of nanotechnology and natural products

The integration of advanced nanotechnology with traditional natural products is primarily manifested primarily in a series of drug delivery systems based on natural products. Natural products possess unique molecular structures that can form nanostructures through a series of noncovalent bonds, including electrostatic interactions, hydrophobic interactions, π-π stacking, and van der Waals forces [61]. These noncovalent assembly mechanisms exhibit distinct patterns across different types of natural products [62,63]. Flavonoids can form nanoparticles through coordination or self-assembly with related metals via their hydrophobic groups, aromatic rings, and various functional groups [39]. Some alkaloid compounds can utilize their rigid skeletons and chiral centers, combined with their various hydrophilic and hydrophobic groups, to self-assemble in aqueous systems via functional groups or fold in other media to form various forms of nanoparticles [64]. Additionally, the encapsulation of natural products using biocompatible materials such as liposomes and CS is widely applied in clinical disease treatment [65,66]. Cell membranes, as natural products derived from biological sources, have been extensively studied in the field of biomimetic nanoparticles in recent years [67]. They combine the advantages of cell membranes and synthetic nanoparticles, enabling the encapsulation of traditional nanomaterials or even drugs using various cell membranes to achieve precise targeting of specific sites [68].

Nanotechnology, as an innovative medical tool, has been widely used in clinical practice. Because of their rich natural sources, diverse mechanisms of action, and rich structural diversity, natural product-based nanomaterials have unique advantages over nanomaterials alone. Moreover, the lower bioavailability and poor targeting of natural products have been greatly improved after nanotechnology modification [69]. Currently, common natural product-based nanomaterials can be classified into the following categories.

Lipid-based nanosystems

Lipids are amphiphilic or hydrophobic molecules with a unique phospholipid bilayer structure. Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) are a new type of nanodelivery system generated by the combination of nanotechnology and lipids. LNPs can be used as an effective delivery tool to deliver active ingredients, drugs, etc., into the body and are diverse, with a wide range of manifestations to deliver the active ingredient [70,71]. LNPs have attracted increasing attention because of their unique structure and functional properties, as they can encapsulate specific active ingredients in phospholipid bilayers [72] to achieve the controlled release of drugs in the organism [73].

Liposomes

Liposomes are composed mainly of phospholipids and cholesterol, and their main structure is a spherical vesicle surrounded by a lipid bilayer [74]. Liposomes can form monolayer or multilayer encapsulated internal aqueous spaces in a self-assembling manner, and studies have shown that the size of their internal aqueous spaces ranges from 30 nm to a few micrometres [75]. In addition, liposomes synthesized via different synthetic methods can vary in size, stability, and other relevant properties [76]. Since the discovery of liposomes in the 1960s, with increasing research on them, they have now become indispensable drug delivery solutions because of their high biocompatibility, high stability, and simple synthesis methods [77]. A novel delivery system combining ginsenoside Rg3, paclitaxel (PTX), and liposomes (PTX-Rg3-lipo) has been proposed. Research results indicate that the Rg3-lipo component in this system can effectively reverse myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC) mediated immunosuppression by inhibiting glycolysis and downregulating transcription factors c-Maf and Mafb. This mechanism, combined with the direct antitumor effect of paclitaxel, ultimately significantly enhances the overall antitumor efficacy of PTX-Rg3-lipo [78].

Lipid nanoparticles

Solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) consist of a core of lipids and surface stabilizers formed by some phospholipids or bile salts, which make up a wide range of morphologies [79]. SLNs constitute the first generation of lipid nanoparticles and are characterized by their good biocompatibility and improved stability of hydrophobic drugs, which have made them ideal delivery systems for certain difficult-to-soluble drugs [80]. However, the possibility of uncontrolled release during storage, low actual drug loading, and particle size instability have also limited their development [81]. To address this potential problem, a second generation of lipid nanoparticles, known as nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs), was created. NLCs are made by combining solid lipids and liquid lipids under certain conditions, and several common lipids, such as triglycerides, fatty acids, steroids, and waxes, are used in the synthesis of NLCs [82]. Since NLCs are the product of the combination of two lipids with different traits, they are less crystalline, resulting in an imperfect lattice structure [83]. These imperfect lattice structures enable NLCs to have higher drug loading capacity, more controlled drug release, and more stable particle sizes than the previous generation of lipid nanoparticle SLNs [72]. Currently, there are three different structures of NLCs, namely, amorphous, imperfect, and multiple type, and the delivery function of NLCs varies from structure to structure [84]. Briefly, amorphous NLCs are formed as an amorphous lipid structure that controls the uncontrolled release of drugs by maintaining this amorphous state [84]. The imperfect type is an imperfect crystalline structure consisting of solid and liquid lipid phases, which allows for more drug loading, including a variety of hydrophilic and hydrophobic substances [85]. Multiple type NLCs, on the other hand, achieve better encapsulation and higher solubility of liposoluble drugs through the addition of relevant liquid regions [85]. Pioglitazone is an antidiabetic drug that may be used in the treatment of Alzheimer’s disease (AD); however, the failure of pioglitazone in clinical trials is related to its difficulty in penetrating the blood-brain barrier and peripheral adverse reactions. Therefore, a nanolipid carrier based on pioglitazone has been proposed to achieve delivery from the nasal cavity to the brain. After nasal administration, the delivery of pioglitazone-loaded NLC to the brain is enhanced, demonstrating the potential of NLC as a carrier for pioglitazone in the treatment of AD [86]. Overall, NLCs address some of the shortcomings of first-generation SLNs, provide a platform for more effective drug delivery, and promote the development of lipid-based nanosystems to some extent. NLCs have been widely used for the delivery of a wide range of drugs due to their ease of preparation, non-toxicity, ability to improve targeting efficiency, and the ability to achieve site-specific delivery through multiple routes of administration.

Niosomes

Niosomes are structurally similar to liposomes, which are self-assembled vesicles formed by nonionic surfactants, cholesterol, etc. Unlike liposomes, there is no phospholipid component in their interior, and this unique composition makes them not only more resistant to storage but also more stable [87]. Niosomes have both a hydrophilic head end and a hydrophobic tail end, and this structure can help them capture many hydrophilic groups. Niosomes have a wide range of applications in the medical field, especially in targeted drug delivery and precision medicine. Examples include pH-responsive paclitaxel-loaded niosomes [88] and magnetic niosomes designed to encapsulate anticancer drugs, for targeted treatment of breast cancer [89]. In addition, Niosomes have also been used for nasoencephalic drug delivery to provide a solution for the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases [89]. Niosomes, as advanced drug delivery tools, show great potential for improving drug efficacy, reducing side effects, and enabling precision medicine.

Polymer lipid-hybridized nanoparticles

Polymer lipid-hybridized nanoparticles (PLNs) are a new type of core‒shell-structured nanocarrier consisting mainly of a biopolymer core and an encapsulated shell formed by lipids [90]. This structure combines the advantages of both polymer nanoparticles and liposomes, which endows PLNs with good physical strength and biocompatibility [90]. PLNs represent a breakthrough in drug delivery, as they can control drug release and increase drug-carrying capacity [91]. PLNs have a wide range of uses, especially in anticancer therapy. They can be used to deliver drugs, DNA, and other related materials and as diagnostic imaging agents [92]. A paclitaxel delivery system based on PLNs has been proposed for the treatment of damaged blood vessels. It consists of a polylactic acid core conjugated with paclitaxel and a lecithin/DSPE-PEG shell, and is secondarily modified with a basement membrane-targeting peptide. In a rat model, it can specifically accumulate in the damaged blood vessels and sustain drug release for about two weeks [93]. In addition, PLNs have been used for a variety of biomedical applications, such as gene delivery, vaccine delivery, and bioimaging. The small size of PLNs allows them to evade clearance by the reticuloendothelial system, thus improving bioavailability [94].

Nanoemulsions

Nanoemulsions are kinetically stable nanodispersed systems formed from two immiscible liquids with droplet diameters of approximately 100 nm. A homogeneous and transparent colloidal dispersion system is formed by lowering the interfacial tension between oil and water through surfactants (emulsifiers) [95]. Nanoemulsions are mainly fabricated via high-energy and low-energy methods, which are widely used because of the simplicity of the synthesis steps in the low-energy method [96]. Nanoemulsions have their unique advantages in the loading of lipophilic substances, effectively preventing their degradation and greatly increasing their solubility and bioavailability [97]. A method using emulsion polymerization to cross-link alginate and quercetin to obtain a “co-constructed” water-soluble nano gel system. This greatly improved the solubility and bioavailability of quercetin. In the ALI mouse model, it significantly reduced lung inflammation and prevented subsequent pulmonary fibrosis, demonstrating excellent lung protective efficacy [98].

Protein-based nanosystems

Protein-based nanoparticles (PNPs) are nanodrug delivery systems with proteins as the main component. PNPs have potential applications in the fields of drug delivery, nanocatalysis, and protein therapeutics, and their wide range of uses depends on a series of advantages, including multifunctionality, biodegradability, and low immunogenicity [99]. PNPs can be classified according to their origin and structural features, including animal protein- and plant protein-based nanoparticles. PNPs have multiple different protein sources, including hemoglobin, transferrin, mucin, and albumin [99]. Maintaining the stability of PNPs is a challenge because of the complexity of their protein structure and sensitivity to environmental changes. Methods such as intermolecular cross-linking and the modification of covalent and noncovalent bonds can be used [99]. A simple nano-diagnosis and treatment platform, BLD NPs, which includes a peptide cage near-infrared probe (CyGbF) and the small molecule dexamethasone sodium phosphate, has been studied to accurately image the inflamed areas and perform related treatments in the early stages of inflammatory diseases, greatly alleviating the inflammatory storm [100]. Protein-based nanoparticles, as an important branch in the field of nanotechnology, have become a hot topic for research because of their unique properties and wide application prospects.

Metal-based nanosystems

The preparation of metal-modified natural product nanoparticles usually involves processes such as ligand bonding, physical adsorption, and cation‒π interactions. In this process, metal ions bind to active functional groups in natural products, such as carboxyl and amino groups, by forming ligand bonds or physisorption [101]. This modification strategy not only enhances the stability and bioactivity of natural products but also helps reduce their potential toxicity and side effects, thus greatly expanding their applications [102]. The formation of complexes between Fe2+ metal ions and Cur significantly improves its solubility in water and increases its bioavailability [103]. Compared with Cur alone, Ru2+-Cur complex can induce cell death by inhibiting the MEK/ERK signaling pathway, through its potential to interact with DNA and block the phosphorylation of MEK1 and ERK1 in A549 cancer cells, thereby enhancing antitumor activity. [104]. Flavonoids are naturally occurring polyphenols, such as quercetin and naringenin, that have a wide range of biological activities [105]. A quercetin-rhodium ligand has been shown to inhibit the proliferation of tumor cells through mechanisms such as the modulation of P53 gene expression levels and the inhibition of apoptosis, and its free radical scavenging capacity is also greater than that of quercetin alone [106]. In addition, naringenin-copper has been shown to better inhibit the proliferation of lung cancer cells by improving its ability to scavenge oxygen radicals [107]. Poppy alkaloids are commonly used alkaloidal agents in clinical practice, and experiments have shown that the complexes formed by poppy alkaloids and gold have stronger anticancer effects than poppy alkaloids alone in inhibiting breast and liver cancer cells, while their toxicity is lower than that of poppy alkaloids themselves. In addition, its anticancer activity in breast cancer cells even exceeds that of cisplatin, a commonly used chemotherapeutic drug [108]. A TiO2@CaP core-shell nanoplasmonic agent that can be activated by ultrasound in the tumor microenvironment, simultaneously generating ROS and releasing Ca2+ ions, inducing mitochondrial dysfunction and strong immunogenic cell death through synergistic effects, triggering effective systemic anti-tumor immunity and inhibiting tumor metastasis [109]. The catechol and o-benzenetriol structures in natural polyphenols can effectively form complexes with a variety of metal ions and are widely used in metal‒phenolic coatings [110]. Nanoparticles capped with Fe (III)/tannic acid can be further combined with folic acid-modified polyethylene glycol (PEG) to enhance their targeting properties for precision cancer therapy [111].

Nanocrystals

Nanocrystals are nanoscale, pure drug particles with sizes ranging from 10 to 1000 nm that have been extensively studied as a nanodrug delivery systems [112]. Unlike other nanodrug carriers, nanocrystals have good dispersibility in water, which can increase the dissolution of refractory drugs [113]. However, owing to its high surface energy and polymorphic transformation, it is prone to a series of changes such as aggregation and sedimentation during storage, resulting in poor performance [114]. To solve this problem, the addition of stabilizers such as surfactants and polymers, combined with specific preparation process parameter controls, is usually used to optimize the change in nanocrystal properties. Currently, a variety of natural product-based nanocrystals have been widely studied, such as Cur and artemisinin nanocrystals, have been widely studied [115,116].

Biomimetic nanomaterials

Biomimetic nanomaterials are advanced functional materials constructed on the basis of the structural characteristics and functional mechanisms of living organisms through precise design and controllable synthesis at the nanoscale. Biomimetic nanomaterials are widely used in the medical field because of their good biocompatibility and accurate identification capabilities, especially in the delivery system and tissue engineering of related drugs [117,118]. The main purpose of early biomimetic nanomaterials is to reduce the interaction between materials and immune cells, however, with the increase in demand, today’s biomimetic nanomaterials need not only to reduce the effect of immune cells but also to precisely target lesions [119]. At present, there are three main modification methods: (1) modifying relevant targeted ligands on the surface of nanoparticles to mimic the indicator protein of cells; (2) using natural cell membranes from living organisms to wrap nanoparticles to mimic natural cells; and (3) extracting membrane proteins from the cell membrane surface for liposome construction [119,120]. A macrophage membrane-coated delivery platform derived from ROS-responsive large balloon flower polysaccharide nanoparticles (PNPs) for loading the anti-inflammatory drug Cur. Experiments have shown that MNPs@Cur can significantly alleviate the inflammatory response and cytokine storm in mice with ALI by inhibiting the production of pro-inflammatory factors and inflammatory cell infiltration [121]. Biomimetic nanomaterials have three core characteristics: high biocompatibility, precise targeting of lesion areas, and long blood circulation times; thus, new biomimetic nanoparticles have been widely studied.

Natural product-based self-assembled nano-drug delivery systems

Self-assembled natural product-based nanomaterials refer to nanomaterials whose components spontaneously form a stable and dense structure through non-covalent interactions. This process is driven by the aforementioned weak non-covalent forces [122]. Such self-assembled drugs possess both carrier and drug functions, significantly improving drug encapsulation rates and loading capacities while reducing waste [123]. Moreover, this technology does not require exogenous nanocatalysts, greatly reducing potential toxicity or immunogenicity issues associated with carrier materials [124]. Rhein (Rhe) is an anthraquinone compound derived from the rhizomes of plants such as rhubarb. Studies have shown that this compound can self-assemble into hydrogels through π–π stacking and hydrogen bonding interactions. This hydrogel has good biostability and can achieve sustained drug release and stimulus response [125]. In addition, Rhe and Berberine can also form nanodrug delivery systems through self-assembly, showing good antibacterial ability against Staphylococcus aureus [126].

Regulation of inflammation

It is now widely accepted that inflammation of the whole body or lungs is the main pathogenesis of ALI/ARDS [127]. During ALI, inflammatory cells secrete large amounts of proinflammatory mediators. These factors interact to mediate the inflammatory response, leading to the accumulation and infiltration of large numbers of inflammatory cells into lung tissue. Moreover, they activate intracellular signaling pathways, promoting the release of more inflammatory cytokines, which further activate inflammatory cells, creating a vicious cycle that may ultimately trigger a cytokine storm. In ALI, the activation of NF-κB can promote the expression of multiple proinflammatory factors, thereby exacerbating the inflammatory response. These include signaling pathways such as ERK, JNK, and p38, which are involved in regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, and inflammatory responses [128]. Therefore, this section mainly focuses on the direct intervention in the acute inflammatory state of ALI, inhibiting the release of related inflammatory factors, alleviating the infiltration of neutrophils, and controlling the acute damage to lung tissue. A poly (citric acid-polyethylene glycol- Cur) (PCGC) nanopolymer was synthesized from citric acid, Cur, and polyethylene glycol as monomers via a simple one-pot method [129]. The highest intensity fluorescent signal was detected in the lungs 12 h after the administration of PCGC via nasal drip, indicating that the highest concentration of PCGC was present in the lungs at this time. The intensity of fluorescence in the lungs subsequently decreased at 24 h, and the fluorescence further diminished by 48 h. These results indicate that PCGC can accumulate rapidly in the lungs and that its effective concentration can be maintained for more than 24 h [129]. The significant antioxidant properties, intracellular ROS scavenging capacity, and anti-inflammatory effects of PCGC are attributed to the citrate and Cur components contained in its structure. After the administration of PCGC, hematoxylin‒eosin (H&E) staining revealed that the lung conditions of ALI model mice were significantly improved, effectively alleviating lung inflammation. Moreover, PCGC effectively attenuates inflammatory responses through two mechanisms: the suppression of inflammatory cytokine expression and the negative regulation of the NF-κB signaling pathway [129].

To further expand the utilization of Cur, iron- Cur -based nanoparticles (Fe-Cur NPs) have been investigated [130] (Fig. 3A). Elevated intracellular calcium ion (Ca2+) concentrations can disrupt mitochondrial homeostasis, induce ROS production, activate NLRP3 inflammatory vesicles, and further exacerbate the inflammatory response [130]. Therefore, lowering intracellular calcium ion concentrations may have a positive effect on maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis and reducing inflammation levels. The experimental findings revealed significant pulmonary accumulation of the Fe-Cur NPs, which effectively mitigated the inflammatory cascade through multiple mechanisms: ROS scavenging, the suppression of key inflammatory cytokine secretion, the reduction in intracellular Ca2+ mobilization, and the inhibition of both NLRP3 inflammasome activation and the NF-κB pathway [130](Fig. 3B).Fig. 3Synthesis and mechanism of action of Fe-Cur CNPs and PS-PEG. As a biologically active lipoprotein assembly secreted exclusively by type II alveolar epithelial cells (AEC II), pulmonary surfactant (PS) is fundamentally important for modulating alveolar surface tension and ensuring proper pulmonary function. Deficiency or abnormal function of PS interferes with the normal functioning of the alveoli, potentially triggering ALI. Therapeutic supplementation with exogenous pulmonary surfactant constitutes a potential treatment modality for ALI [131]. Therefore, a delivery system, PS-PEG (PLS), synthesized from PS and polyethylene glycol (PEG), was synthesized [131]. After administration, the levels of relevant ALB and inflammatory markers significantly decreased, indicating a decrease in alveolar epithelial permeability and inflammation levels [131] (Fig. 3C). In addition, PLS significantly alleviated ALI-induced tissue hypoxia, as shown by measurements of indicators such as the tissue hypoxia marker HIF-1α.

Tannic acid, a natural product derived from the plant cinquefoil, has been widely studied for its good anti-inflammatory properties. A simple one-pot method was used to synthesize nanoparticles containing tannic acid, polymyxin B, and manganese ions (TMP). Research indicates that TMP has good in vitro antibacterial and anti-inflammatory properties, effectively alleviating the cytokine storm in ALI models, significantly reducing the levels of inflammation in ALI, and improving prognosis [132].

Natural product-based nanomaterials have significant advantages in inflammation regulation. Further research is needed to improve their precision targeting of inflammatory sites and closely monitor the blood concentration of inorganic metal ions to accelerate clinical translation.

Repair of the alveolar epithelial barrier

During the disease course of ALI, increased microvascular permeability has many adverse consequences, such as pulmonary edema and lung dysfunction. The pulmonary vascular endothelial barrier is essential for maintaining proper pulmonary function [133]. Tight junction protein 1 (ZO-1) is tightly linked to the robustness of this barrier, and the upregulation of ZO-1 expression promotes the reconstruction of the barrier, thereby driving microvascular permeability back to normal levels [134]. Haukoneol (4ˊ,5-dihydroxy-3,3ˊ,7-trimethoxyflavone, Pac) is a hydrophobic flavonol with anti-inflammatory, antiapoptotic, antitumor, and immunomodulatory effects [[135], [136], [137], [138]]. However, its poor water solubility limits its further clinical application. A dipalmitoyl phosphatidylcholine (DPPC) lipid was prepared in combination with Pac (Pac-lipo), which has stable physical properties, uniform biodistribution, and good biocompatibility [139]. Pac-lips contributed to the repair of the pulmonary vascular barrier by increasing the expression level of ZO-1, which in turn reduced the permeability of lung endothelial cells to epithelial cells. Pac-lips downregulated the expression of related inflammatory factors, significantly attenuated the inflammatory response of lung tissues, and improved lung function. Pac-Lipo further alleviated the related inflammatory response by inhibiting TLR4-MyD88-NF-κB/MAPK signaling pathway activation, further attenuating the associated inflammatory response [139](Fig. 4). Inhibition of pyroptosis

The typical manifestations of pyroptosis, a specific form of cell death, include cell membrane perforation, loss of membrane integrity, cell body swelling, and leakage of cytoplasmic contents. During the process of cell death, the release of inflammatory factors such as IL-1β and IL-18 further amplifies the inflammatory cascade and activates the immune response [140]. It relatively common in ALI and triggers a strong inflammatory response. When cells undergo pyroptosis, large amounts of proinflammatory cytokines are released. These factors not only directly stimulate inflammation but also promote the production of other cytokines and chemokines, which in turn direct polymorphonuclear granulocytes toward the site of injured tissues and organs. These interrelated responses increase the level of inflammation and ultimately lead to the destruction and dysfunction of lung tissue [141]. To control pyroptosis in ALI to further control the inflammatory microenvironment of lung tissues, a nanodrug delivery tool (ARC@DPB) was developed. Extracted from the medicinal plant burdock, arctoside (ARC) is a natural xyloside compound with significant anti-inflammatory properties. However, ARC suffers from a high first-pass metabolism rate and low targeting properties. To address these issues, ARC was nanomodified with DSPE-PEG2000, resulting in the synthesis of ARC@DPB to increase its therapeutic effect [142]. Cellular pyroptosis occurs through the ASC‒NLRP3‒caspase‒GSDMD signaling axis [143], and in an ALI mouse model, ARC@DPB downregulated the expression of proteins related to cellular pyroptosis, such as GSDMD‒N, caspase, and NLRP3, and suppressed pyroptosis through the inhibition of the ERK pathway [142]. ARC@DPB reduced the level of inflammation in ALI lung tissue.

Hesperidin (HPD) is an active flavonoid glycoside that is found mainly in citrus fruits [144] and has good anti-inflammatory properties. To improve its poor water solubility and biocompatibility and expand its application, a combination of HPD, poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), and CS, HPD/NP CS nanoparticles was developed to target lung tissue for ALI treatment via nasal administration [144]. After intranasal administration, the lung histology of ALI mice was significantly improved, and HPD/NPs downregulated the expression of relevant proinflammatory factors, attenuated the cytokine storm and thus lung injury, and inhibited the pyroptosis of lung tissue cells by suppressing the expression of IL-1β and caspase 1 [144]. Future research should further strengthen the exploration of cell pyroptosis mechanisms, improve toxicity assessment standards, and promote clinical translation.

Regulation of immunity

The immune system plays an important role in the pathological process of ALI. When an organism is damaged, the immune system can respond rapidly by releasing relevant cytokines or antibodies to regulate and repair damaged tissues and prevent further aggravation of damage [145]. This section mainly focuses on the effects of natural product-based nanomaterials on the adaptive immune system of the body, such as altering macrophage polarization phenotypes (M1/M2), with the core goal of reconstructing immune homeostasis to achieve long-term efficacy. The immune system consists of a variety of organs and cells, among which macrophages play important roles in the evolution of ALI [146]. Alveolar macrophages (AMs) are distributed mainly in the alveolar lumen, which is the first line of defense in lung tissues and can secrete a variety of inflammatory mediators that mediate inflammatory responses and participate in tissue damage repair. In the early stages of ALI, alveolar macrophages initiate the inflammatory response in the lung by releasing chemokines that attract more peripheral macrophages and neutrophils to the site of inflammation. Macrophages can polarize according to different stimuli and are divided into proinflammatory M1-type macrophages and anti-inflammatory M2-type macrophages [147]. M1-type macrophages secrete many inflammatory factors that promote tissue inflammation, and M2-type macrophages secrete anti-inflammatory factors that promote the repair and regeneration of lung tissue. Therefore, regulating macrophage polarization, increasing the proportion of M2-type macrophages in the inflammatory process, and reducing tissue inflammation are undoubtedly important for alleviating the progression of ALI.

Bergapten, a type of coumarin derivative, has been extracted from the plant Ficus simplicissima Lour. A nanomedicine delivery platform, Ber-lipo, developed by integrating bergapten and DPPC liposomes, was investigated [148]. Ber-lipo can be precisely targeted to inflammatory lesions in the lungs, significantly improving lung edema, tissue damage, and lung function; downregulating the expression of relevant inflammatory factors; modulating the polarization of M1/M2-type macrophages; and inhibiting the NF-κB pathway, thereby modulating the level of inflammation [148](Fig. 5A). Natural product-derived metal nanosystems combine the active properties of natural biomolecules with the specific physicochemical properties of metal nanostructures, opening new fields of biomedical applications. Lignans (Luts) are members of the flavonoid family and have antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [149]. In addition, lignans can alleviate inflammatory responses by modulating macrophage polarization and mitigating inflammatory responses [150]. A cerium-lignans nanocomplex (CeLutNCs) has been used to treat ALI [151], where CeLutNCs exhibit superior ROS scavenging capacity compared with Lut alone and modulate macrophage polarization status by promoting the activation of M2-type macrophages. In addition, CeLutNCs reduced the decrease in the mitochondrial membrane potential and effectively inhibited apoptosis. After CeLutNC administration, edema in the lung tissues of the ALI rats was significantly alleviated, and the infiltration of inflammatory cells was significantly improved. In addition, CeLutNCs inhibited the activation of the NF-κB pathway, thereby reducing the generation of iNOS and alleviating the inflammatory response in lung tissues [151]. Dendrimers (dendritic macromolecules) are a class of monodisperse polymers with highly branched structures. Phosphorus-containing dendrimers are constructed by combining dendrimers with phosphorus-containing units, and studies have shown that these ionized phosphorus dendrimers or dendrites exhibit significant anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties [152]. A drug delivery platform based on anionic phosphorus dendritic macromolecules was developed for the treatment of ALI. The platform uses hexachlorocyclotriphosphonitrile (HCCP) as the core structure, and five different phosphorus dendritic macromolecules are successfully synthesized. After screening, the phosphodendrimer molecule AK-137 was selected for its excellent anti-inflammatory effect and protein delivery capacity [152]. Furthermore, AK-137 was combined with fibronectin (FN) to construct AK-137@FN composite nanocarriers (NCs) [152]. Upon administration, AK-137@FN NCs attenuate the inflammatory response in ALI by inhibiting the activation of the NF-κB and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways, promoting M2 macrophage polarization and downregulating inflammatory factor levels [152](Fig. 5B).Fig. 5Preparation and mechanism of action of Ber-lipo and AK-137. In summary, natural product-based nanoparticles exhibit excellent immune regulatory capabilities. In the future, their development can be further promoted by continuously optimizing the synthesis process and improving biocompatibility.

Targeted therapy

The pathological process of ALI is complex and varied, covering multiple aspects, such as inflammation and oxidative stress. Traditional systemic drug therapy often causes greater damage to normal tissues and many side effects. In contrast, targeted therapy can precisely deliver drugs to the lesion area and thus precisely intervene in the key targets of these pathological processes, thus greatly reducing damage to normal tissues. This greatly reduces damage to normal tissues.

Biomimetic nanoparticles with targeted functions

With the advancement of nanotechnology research, an increasing number of nanoparticles are being developed, and bioinspired or biomimetic nanoplatforms have been used for different biomedical applications. Biomimetic nanoparticles inspired by nature or their components have been synthesized and are now used to treat ALI [153].

Neutrophil membrane bionanoparticles

A hallmark pathological manifestation of ALI involves the pathological infiltration and hyperactivation of neutrophils within pulmonary tissues. These immune cells contribute to inflammatory pathogenesis through the excessive secretion of proteolytic enzymes, proinflammatory cytokines, and ROS, collectively potentiating alveolar epithelial damage. Neutrophils are naturally chemotactic and phagocytic in the inflammatory microenvironment, and by combining drugs with them, their intrinsic properties can be leveraged to achieve precisely targeted drug delivery. In recent years, neutrophil membranes (NMs) have been used to construct drug carriers [154]. Owing to their inflammation-targeting ability, NM-encapsulated nanoparticles have been found to have significant therapeutic efficacy in rheumatoid arthritis and acute pancreatitis [154]. By encapsulating the neutrophil membrane on the surface of the nanoparticles, targeted delivery can be achieved via the use of receptors and molecules on the neutrophil membrane. This approach endows the nanoparticles with the properties of neutrophils and allows for better enrichment in relevant lesions in vivo. Since acidic fibroblast growth factor (aFGF) was first successfully purified from the bovine brain in 1984, it has been demonstrated that aFGF has an important function in promoting tissue injury repair and neovascularization [155]. A novel liposome delivery system was developed and constructed by preparing aFGF liposomes (aFGF@Lips) and embedding NMs into them [156]. The results showed that aFGF@NMLs were able to specifically target inflammatory lung tissues where neutrophils accumulate, enhancing the therapeutic effect of aFGF. Platelet membrane biomimetic nanoparticles

Tea polyphenols (TPs) are a class of polyhydroxyphenolic compounds derived from tea. Extensive research has established the protective potential of TPs, stemming from their remarkable free radical scavenging ability and robust antioxidative properties [157]. Owing to their poor biocompatibility and on-target properties, they are unable to achieve the desired results in clinical therapy. To further utilize TPs, a biomimetic nanomaterial encapsulated with platelet membranes (PMs) was synthesized [158]. PMs are the outer membrane structures of platelets, and platelet membranes have attracted attention as novel drug delivery systems because of their biocompatibility, long blood circulation time, and in vivo targeting ability [159]. First, TP nanoparticles were prepared by encapsulating TPs into PLGA nanoparticles. Next, PM@TP-NPs were synthesized when platelet membranes (PMs) were extracted from the blood of healthy mice and encapsulated on the surface of the TP-NPs. P-selectin receptors on PMs have a strong affinity for CD44 receptors that are overexpressed at the site of inflammation, thus facilitating the active targeting of drugs [158]. In ALI, the extremely strong inflammatory response of the lung tissue makes PM@TP-NPs capable of targeting. After drug administration, PM@TP-NPs show good targeting ability and can be enriched in lung tissue and the pulmonary vascular endothelium [158]. In an ALI mouse model, PM@TP-NPs inhibited the activation of NLRP3 inflammatory vesicles and the expression of related inflammatory factors and strongly inhibited pulmonary vascular leakage, and H&E staining suggested that PM@TP-NPs improved the histological morphology of lung tissue [158] (Fig. 6B).

Lung epithelial cell-mimicking nanoparticles

A novel nanomimetic drug delivery system based on lung epithelial cells (MLE12) was developed [160]. This nanosystem is composed of Cur, an Nrf2 agonist (bardoxolone), PLGA, and MLE12. Cur has anti-inflammatory properties, and Nrf2 agonists counteract oxidative stress and inflammatory responses by increasing Nrf2 activity. To increase the water solubility and bioavailability of Cur, PLGA nanoparticles were used to encapsulate Cur, forming cur-loaded nanoparticles (PC NPs). The Nrf2 agonist was subsequently incorporated into MLE12 membranes, and owing to its chemical structural similarity to cholesterol, bardoxolone was able to specifically replace cholesterol in MLE12 membranes, resulting in the formation of engineered membrane vesicles with lung-targeting properties (MBs) [160]. Ultimately, the final nanoparticles (PC@MBs) were prepared by combining MB with PC NPs. After intravenous administration, the PC@MBs showed good lung-targeting ability due to their bionic nanocoating. The Nrf2 activator bardoxolone upregulated the expression of Nrf2-dependent antioxidant genes, which in turn promoted the binding of Nrf2 to ARE and resulted in increased antioxidant capacity [160]. Stem cell-derived biomimetic nanoparticles

Stem cell-based biomimetic nanoparticles are widely studied for their ability to effectively target sites of inflammation [161]. Stem cells are derived from a wide range of sources, including bone marrow, adipose, and other tissues, and studies have shown that stem cell membrane-functionalized nanoparticles combined with relevant drugs can exert powerful anti-inflammatory and antibacterial effects [162]. ALI often triggers cytokine storms and excessive accumulation of ROS in the lungs, and removing excessive ROS is crucial for the prevention and treatment of ALI. Naringin, a dihydroflavonoid derived from citrus peel, has diverse pharmacological effects and biological activities. To enhance the therapeutic performance of naringin, researchers have developed a novel biomimetic nanomaterial that was created via the encapsulation of bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cell (BMCS) membranes [163]. Naringin (Nar) nanoparticles were prepared by combining PLGA with Nar via the emulsion evaporation technique. The membrane layer of BMSCs subsequently covered the surface of Nar-NPs, and the Nar-NPs were modified, resulting in cell membrane-encapsulated nanoparticles (CM@Nar-NPs) [163] for targeted delivery to the inflammatory microenvironment (Fig. 7B). In an ALI mouse model, CM@Nar-NPs alleviated cytokine storm release syndrome (CRS) by scavenging excess ROS, inhibiting the expression of inflammatory factors, and modulating the polarization of M2-type macrophages. The results of the fluorescence imaging and flow cytometry analyses revealed that the CM@Nar-NPs had excellent target delivery efficiency [163] (Fig. 7C).

Natural product-based nanocarriers have demonstrated excellent ALI targeting efficiency, and their therapeutic potential is undeniable; however, further process optimization and reduction of off-target risks are still required. If a cell membrane coating strategy is adopted, potential immunogenicity must be evaluated simultaneously, and a more comprehensive toxicity evaluation system must be established to accelerate the clinical application of this cutting-edge nanotechnology.

Nanocrystals with targeted functions

AMs are categorized into resident AMs and merged AMs. During the acute phase of ALI/ARDS, resident AMs are recruited into the alveolar lumen and undergo M2‒M1 phenotypic polarization while producing multiple proinflammatory factors that exacerbate the inflammatory response [164,165]. In contrast, during the recovery phase of ALI/ARDS, the process of converting M1-type AMs to M2-type AMs is accelerated. M2-type AMs activate the anti-inflammatory response and inhibit the proinflammatory response by phagocytosing apoptotic neutrophils and regulating anti-inflammatory factor expression, which in turn promotes the repair of lung injury [146]. The modulation of the immune microenvironment by AMs in focal areas (e.g., inflammation) makes them potential inflammatory disease therapeutic targets.

Compared with other drug-carrying systems, nanocrystals are drug-carrying systems consisting mainly of drugs and stabilizers and possess higher drug-carrying capacity [166], higher solubility, and more controlled release [167,168]. Targeted targeting of M1-type macrophages can increase the efficacy of anti-inflammatory drugs. As a critical metabolic regulator, L-arginine occupies a central position in macrophage immunomodulation, where its cellular uptake machinery is predominantly expressed in classically activated M1 macrophages. Combining the properties of both nanocrystals and L-arginine, a drug delivery system using nanocrystals as carriers was proposed [169]. First, the Arg-CS complex was synthesized via a covalent binding technique, and subsequently, Cur nanocrystals modified with L-arginine (Arg-CS-Cur) were prepared by loading Cur onto the complex via sonication. To assess the targeting effect of L-arginine, CS-Cur, a nanodelivery system with unmodified L-arginine, was also synthesized. After the administration of Arg-CS-Cur to a mouse model of ALI, the affinity of Arg-CS-Cur for M1-type macrophages was increased in vitro [169]. In the rat ALI model, the Arg-CS-Cur group presented greater lung fluorescence signals in inflamed lung tissues than did the CS-Cur group, which peaked at 12 h after administration. Furthermore, the Arg-CS-Cur treatment group presented a marked reduction in the levels of pulmonary inflammatory mediators, accompanied by substantial amelioration of histopathological alterations in the lung tissue architecture. These results indicated that the nanocrystals modified with L-arginine had significant targeting ability and effectively increased the concentration of Cur in the inflammatory microenvironment by selectively targeting M1-type macrophages [169]. In the future, we should continue to optimize the process to produce nanocrystals with higher purity and more stable crystallisation, thereby promoting further development in related research.

Neutrophil targeting

Neutrophils are the body’s first line of defense against infections and tissue damage. In ALI, they are the first inflammatory cells recruited to the lungs. The precise targeting of neutrophils to allow nanoparticle drugs to promptly reach the site of infection is undoubtedly of positive significance for the treatment of ALI. Therefore, a novel drug delivery system specifically targeting certain receptors on the surface of neutrophils has been proposed. A complex called Ac-PGP-tFNA (APT) is constructed using the peptide sequence Ac-PGP, which can recognize the CXCR2 receptor on the surface of neutrophils, along with tetrahedral framework nucleic acid (tFNA). The flavonoid baicalin is encapsulated within APT to synthesize APTB. The AcPGP peptide sequence can specifically bind to receptors on the surface of neutrophils. In ALI, APTB can be delivered to the inflamed area following neutrophils, increasing the IL-10 levels at the inflammatory site while reducing the concentrations of IL-1β and TNF-α, thereby alleviating the tissue damage caused by sepsis [170].

Bacteria targeting

Bacterial infections drive the occurrence and development ofALI through direct damage to lung tissue structure and the triggering of excessive host immune responses. Lung infections caused by bacteria often lead to a poor prognosis for ALI patients. Furthermore, the deep colonization characteristics of pathogens make it difficult for traditional drug delivery systems to accurately deliver drugs to the infection sites, resulting in unsatisfactory treatment effects [171]. Therefore, accurately targeting bacteria and controlling lung infections can undoubtedly greatly improve the prognosis of ALI. Natural plant compound tannic acid-coated manganese-zinc oxide spiny spheres (MZT) achieve precise and efficient capture of bacteria through their unique spiky structure and polyphenol-mediated membrane interactions, effectively killing bacteria and demonstrating good therapeutic effects on bacterial infectious pneumonia in mice [172].

Targeting endothelial cells

Pulmonary endothelial cells play an important regulatory role in key physiological processes such as regulating vascular tone, cell transport, and angiogenesis. In the pathogenesis of ALI, endothelial dysfunction can lead to a series of serious consequences such as vascular leakage and edema. Targeting pulmonary endothelial cells and improving endothelial cell function may greatly improve the prognosis of ALI [173]. Due to the abundance of negatively charged sialic acid residues on the surface of pulmonary endothelial cells, a cationic carrier drug delivery system utilizing the characteristics of positive and negative charges has been developed. This system uses nanoparticles composed of cationic polymer polyethyleneimine-(5)-myristic acid/polyethylene glycol oleate/cholesterol (PEI600-MA5/PEG-OA/Cho), which demonstrates excellent targeting capability and delivery efficiency to pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells [174]. In addition, relevant studies have shown that the protein corona generated by nanomaterials entering the body has been proven to have a targeting function for endothelial cells. By modulating the headgroup structure of LNPs, it is possible to achieve targeting of different lung cell types, a mechanism that may be associated with the specific protein corona formed in vivo. Based on this finding, researchers developed a cationic degradable LNP-CAD9 carrier, which demonstrated excellent lung-targeting specificity, particularly enriching in pulmonary endothelial cells. To validate its editing capability, the study successfully achieved efficient knockout of the VEGFR2 gene in pulmonary endothelial cells in vivo by co-delivering Cas9 mRNA and VEGFR2-targeting sgRNA, confirming the targeting ability and therapeutic potential of this platform [175].

Mitochondrial targeting

Mitochondrial dysfunction is a central link between lung cell injury and barrier disruption in ALI. Under physiological conditions, mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation provides the energy basis for maintaining cell barrier and ion pump function; however, under the stress of ALI, the energy crisis triggered by mitochondrial dysfunction directly leads to the loss of barrier integrity and dysregulation of ion transport [176]. Therefore, targeting mitochondria to improve mitochondrial metabolism may play a positive role in the treatment of ALI. A sprayable hydrogel with both targeting and therapeutic functions has been proposed, aimed at treating ALI through precise regulation of mitochondrial functions. This system integrates nano-encapsulated astaxanthin (ASX-NPs) into the sprayable hydrogel, significantly enhancing drug bioavailability and retention in the lungs. The results indicated that ASX-NPs can efficiently target mitochondria and significantly alleviate inflammatory responses in a human lung organoid-based inflammation model by inhibiting inflammation and oxidative stress-related pathways, and promoting mitochondrial energy metabolism. In a mouse model of ALI, the sprayable hydrogel can adhere to the tracheal mucosa, releasing ASX-NPs rapidly after inhalation, effectively alleviating ALI [177].

Inhalable natural product-based nanoparticles

For disease treatment, rational drug delivery primarily aims to achieve the effective accumulation of drugs at the target site through appropriate delivery methods [178]. Currently, there are two main administration methods for the treatment of ALI: 1. intravenous administration and 2. inhalation administration. Intravenous administration, as a widely used method, delivers drugs directly into the body via injection. However, owing to first-pass metabolism in the liver, a significant portion of nanoparticles may be blocked [179]. Additionally, for ALI, intravenous administration may have limited ability to target the lungs. These objective factors significantly reduce the effective drug concentration reaching the target site. Therefore, for ALI, direct inhalation enables natural product-based nanomaterials to reach the pulmonary target site. Without first-pass metabolism by the liver, the drug concentration at the pulmonary target site rapidly increases within a short time, significantly reducing the required single-dose concentration and greatly enhancing biosafety. As primary inflammatory mediators, NE cells play a pivotal role in ALI pathophysiology, with their activation threshold and chemotactic responses directly influencing the extent of lung injury [180]. As numerous immune cells in the innate immune system, neutrophils are first mobilized and migrate to areas of inflammation, where they are activated at the onset of the inflammatory response [4]. Activated NE releases a variety of inflammatory mediators and reactive oxygen species, among other substances, which trigger lung tissue damage, thereby significantly affecting the prognosis of patients with ALI [181]. Therefore, targeting neutrophils has positive implications for improving the therapeutic outcome of ALI. During ALI pathology, neutrophils and macrophages, which are constantly recruited to lung tissue, can enhance the inflammatory state of the lungs through constant interaction. Modulation of either type of cell alone does not achieve satisfactory results. Therefore, to disrupt this type of interaction and control inflammation, an inhalable drug release platform (D-SEL) was developed [182]. D-SEL was developed by hybridization of DNase I to serum exosomes and liposomes via matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP-9), followed by encapsulation of methylprednisolone sodium succinate (MPS) on a surface (MPS/D-SEL) and synthesis [182]. Upon airway administration, MPS/D-SEL can modulate uncontrolled neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) and promote MPS release via the sustained release of DNase I, which in turn further modulates M2-type macrophage polarization to alleviate the inflammatory storm in ALI lungs [182] (Fig. 8A). Metabolic reprogramming

Glucose metabolism provides energy to immune cells and is critical for regulating their function, especially in inflammation and the immune response. Glycolysis is a key pathway of glucose metabolism through the breakdown of glucose to generate ATP. In activated immune cells, glycolysis is significantly increased to meet energy demands during inflammation [183]. This metabolic reprogramming underlies the inflammatory activation and normal function of immune cells [184]. Polarization of M1-type macrophages is also somewhat inextricably linked to glycolytic processes [185]. In addition, the release of inflammatory factors is intricately linked to the process of glycolysis [186]. In models of lung inflammation, the inhibition of glycolysis has been shown to inhibit macrophage polarization and the resulting inflammatory storm, significantly improving disease regression [187]. Thus, modulation of the glycolytic pathway may contribute to the treatment of ALI. HK2 plays crucial roles as a key enzyme in the glycolytic pathway. In addition, it plays a central regulatory role in macrophage metabolism as well as in the inflammatory response of immune cells [188] (Fig. 8B). Abnormal dysregulation of HK2 expression in macrophages is closely associated with a significant increase in the inflammatory response, suggesting that the modulation of HK2 activity may be a potential and important therapeutic target for the treatment of ALI [189,190]. CRISPR/Cas9 gene-editing technology can precisely regulate the expression of relevant proteins by modifying specific RNAs and is therefore widely used in the field of drug delivery [191]. A new generation of core-shell liposome nanoplatforms (CSN-mCas9/gHK2) incorporating CRISPR/Cas9 has been developed to address the limited potential of HK2 in the treatment of ALI because of its insufficient targeting ability [188]. In in vitro experiments, the CSN-mCas9/gHK2 system successfully downregulated HK2 expression in macrophages. In the inflammatory environment, the system was able to reprogram glycolysis in macrophages, as evidenced by the inhibition of glycolytic processes and altered mitochondrial functions. In in vivo experiments, CSN-mCas9/gHK2 was efficiently delivered to the lungs and significantly attenuated lipopolysaccharide-induced pulmonary edema, the inflammatory response, and neutrophil infiltration. In addition, this therapeutic strategy was able to reprogram the polarization state of macrophages into an anti-inflammatory phenotype [188]. Therefore, metabolic regulation is a promising research target. In the future, we should continue to explore the specific mechanisms of metabolic regulation in depth to promote the advancement of nanotechnology.

Challenges for the clinical application of natural product-based nanoparticles

To overcome the development bottleneck of natural products, which have good biocompatibility but low bioavailability, nanotechnology provides innovative solutions. Natural product-based nanomaterials possess excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and low toxicity, fundamentally enhancing the safety of clinical applications; moreover, they also offer efficient targeting capabilities, and many nanoparticles themselves have therapeutic significance, working synergistically with natural products. This strategy provides a new approach for fully leveraging the therapeutic advantages of natural products. [192]. This study delves into the etiology, pathogenesis, and pathological processes of ALI, classifies natural product-based nanomaterials on the basis of their properties, and preliminarily elucidates the mechanisms and efficacy of these materials in treating ALI. However, the use of natural product-based nanomaterials for ALI treatment remains in an early stage of development. Although traditional natural products modified via advanced nanotechnology have shown promising therapeutic effects in ALI animal models through precise targeting, regulation of inflammatory levels, and repair of lung tissue barriers, no such drugs have yet entered ALI clinical trials. Furthermore, currently developed natural product-based nanoparticles also present several limitations. LNPs entail high synthesis costs and often require low-temperature storage and transportation; their physicochemical stability poses a major challenge for large-scale applications, and their variable drug encapsulation efficiency depending on the drug type makes them non-universal carriers [193]. Protein-based nanoparticles are sensitive to environmental changes, which can lead to premature drug release, and heterologous or modified proteins may be recognized as antigens by the body, triggering unwanted immune responses [194]. Metal-based nanomaterials carry the dual risks of poor biodegradability and potential organ toxicity due to long-term retention of metal ions [195]. The large-scale production of nanocrystals remains challenging, as achieving narrow size distribution and high stability is costly, and current technologies primarily limit their application to oral and injectable routes, making formulation for other administration pathways difficult [196]. For biomimetic nanomaterials, the extraction and coating processes may cause denaturation or loss of membrane proteins, compromising functional integrity, and source cells carrying pathogens or abnormal antigens could introduce safety hazards [197]. Therefore, significant challenges remain in the application of natural product-based nanomaterials.

First, nanomedicine development for clinical applications must prioritize addressing fundamental concerns regarding biocompatibility and toxicological assessment. Although many experimental results have shown that natural product-based nanoparticles are safe and have low toxicity to organisms, their safety in the human body has yet to be assessed, and more extensive experiments need to be conducted to further evaluate them [198]. In addition, the mechanisms associated with natural products in clinical applications are not yet clear enough; therefore, the use of nanomaterials for loading or modifying natural products may be a double-edged sword, which may increase their efficacy but may also lead to other adverse effects. While nanotechnology has largely improved the deficiencies of natural products, it has also made the nature of these new materials more uncertain [199]. When nanoparticles enter the body, after exerting their therapeutic effects, they are either cleared by the organism or can be automatically degraded, especially if repeated administration is needed. If nanoparticles are not effectively removed from the body, their long-term accumulation in the body may cause toxicity and adverse effects beyond their therapeutic effects.

Currently, relevant studies are focused on the modification and assembly of nanocarriers, and the study of whether drug interactions occur in these materials is still in its infancy. Although some specific receptors can improve the targeting of nanomaterials, off-target effects still exist, and more sophisticated experiments and evaluation protocols are needed to further optimize these nanomaterials. Therefore, further investigations of the specific mechanism of action of natural product-based nanomaterials and the selection of safe nanomaterials for modification are essential to ensure therapeutic efficacy and safety [200].

Although related natural products are widely present in nature, their content in individual plants, animals, or microorganisms is often insufficient. Therefore, in clinical translation, the large-scale extraction and purification of natural products may be costly, and the extraction of membrane structures from cell-membrane-coated biomimetic nanomaterials may require many cell lines to be purified and centrifuged to achieve the quantities needed for industrial applications. Additionally, the cost and technical barriers associated with nanotechnology itself remain significant. The equipment required to process large-scale raw material-grade natural products through nanotechnology to achieve uniform morphology, particle size, stable physicochemical properties, and optimal therapeutic effects is also difficult to estimate in terms of cost. These considerations regarding cost and quality further limit the clinical translation of these nanomaterials. Currently, there are no established clinical efficacy assessment standards for the use of nanomaterials in treating ALI. The complex interactions between nanomaterials and intricate systems of the human body complicate long-term safety monitoring. Resolving these issues could accelerate the clinical translation of natural product-based nanomaterials [201].

Second, the mechanism of ALI occurrence should be further explored. Mechanisms such as scavenging ROS, inhibiting the release of inflammatory factors, and modulating macrophage polarization have been widely investigated. However, research on the pathogenesis of ALI is still incomplete. Further clarification of the pathogenesis of ALI is important for the development of novel nanomaterials. The current drug delivery methods for ALI treatment include several traditional drug delivery methods, such as intravenous drug delivery and inhalation drug delivery, which often have inherent shortcomings, such as poor bioavailability and obvious first-pass elimination; thus, new drug delivery methods should be further explored to achieve better therapeutic effects on ALI.

Finally, the development of natural product-based nanoparticle drugs faces several challenges, one of the key issues being to achieve translation from the laboratory to clinical applications. Despite significant advancements in natural product-derived nanopharmaceuticals over recent decades, few have made them the clinical front line of treatment. Paclitaxel liposomes, for example, are the only paclitaxel liposome formulations currently approved for clinical use [202]. By encapsulating paclitaxel inside liposomes, the formulation forms an innovative drug delivery system that significantly improves the solubility and stability of paclitaxel. In addition, liposomes protect paclitaxel from enzymatic degradation in the body, thereby prolonging the duration of action of the drug [202].

Conclusions

Natural product-based nanoparticles play an important role in the treatment of ALI and have the potential to optimize conventional therapeutic regimens to improve therapeutic efficacy. Although there may be some shortcomings in the current stage of research, with the continuous development of nanotechnology, the development of natural product-based drugs with better biocompatibility and more precise targeting could greatly improve the clinical survival rate of ALI patients.
