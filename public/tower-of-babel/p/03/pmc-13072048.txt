Role of Ca2+‐Dependent Epithelial‐Mesenchymal Transition in Malignant Progression of Colorectal Cancer: Special Focus on REG Iα/EDNRB
Authors: Xiao Liu, Shunyuan Wan, Lei Jian, Yi Chen, Shiyi Liang, Ziwei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is a major global health problem, ranking as the third most commonly diagnosed cancer and the second leading cause of cancer‐related deaths. This study aimed to elucidate the role and underlying molecular mechanism of the Regenerating Islet‐Derived Protein 1 Alpha (REG Iα)/Endothelin Receptor B (EDNRB) axis in CRC malignant progression.MethodsA nude mouse xenograft model and human CRC cell lines were used to investigate the REG Iα/EDNRB axis. RNA‐sequencing (RNA‐seq) was employed to identify downstream targets following REG Iα knockdown. Interventions included siRNA‐mediated knockdown of REG Iα, overexpression of EDNRB, and pharmacological inhibition with the Ca2+ chelator BAPTA‐AM. Cell proliferation, apoptosis, migration, invasion, and epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT) were assessed using Western blotting, RT‐qPCR, and Transwell assays.ResultsREG Iα was significantly upregulated in CRC tissues. Downregulation of REG Iα suppressed CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion while promoting apoptosis. Mechanistically, REG Iα silencing downregulated the expression of its target EDNRB and subsequently inhibited the Ca2+/CaMKII signaling pathway, triggering endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation to induce apoptosis. Importantly, overexpression of EDNRB rescued the malignant phenotypes suppressed by REG Iα knockdown, whereas BAPTA‐AM treatment abolished this rescue effect. The REG Iα/EDNRB axis was further demonstrated to drive the EMT process, and these findings were validated in vivo.ConclusionsThe REG Iα/EDNRB axis promotes the malignant progression of CRC by activating Ca2+‐dependent epithelial‐mesenchymal transition, suggesting that targeting this axis is a promising therapeutic strategy to inhibit CRC metastasis.

Introduction

Cancer remains a formidable global challenge, representing a complex and dynamic group of diseases characterized by the uncontrolled proliferation of cells beyond their physiological boundaries. The development and progression of diverse malignancies are intricately linked to a spectrum of physical, chemical, and biological carcinogens [1, 2, 3]. While risk assessment of human exposure to various chemicals remains a significant area of study, emerging evidence has increasingly highlighted the etiological role of infectious agents, particularly bacterial pathogens, in oncogenesis [1, 4, 5] Recent studies demonstrate that bacteria—such as Escherichia coli , Salmonella typhimurium , Helicobacter pylori , Mycoplasma hominis , and Chlamydia pneumoniae —can facilitate cancer growth by transporting proteins that target host cell organelles, including the nucleus and mitochondria [4, 6, 7]. These interactions can lead to the depletion of DNA mismatch repair (MMR) proteins, interference with programmed cell death, and disruption of fundamental processes like replication and transcription in organs such as the lungs, gallbladder, and prostate [2, 4, 8]. Specifically within the gastrointestinal tract, the interplay between microbial pathogens such as E. coli or S. typhimurium and the host's innate defense systems significantly influences the development of colorectal cancer (CRC) [2, 7, 9]. Given this multifaceted etiology, identifying novel molecular targets and understanding the influence of physiological regulators—such as the role of calcium signaling in maintaining homeostasis and its potential in combination therapy—are essential for developing effective therapeutic strategies to control CRC incidence and mortality [3].

Regenerating Islet‐Derived Protein 1 Alpha (REG Iα) is a secretory protein primarily associated with tissue regeneration and inflammation modulation [10]. As a well‐established mediator of tissue repair, REG Iα effectively suppresses inflammation, promotes cell proliferation, and inhibits apoptosis in inflammatory bowel disease [11]. Emerging evidence implicates REG Iα in the pathogenesis of various malignancies. For instance, N6‐methyladenosine modification of REG Iα promotes CRC progression through the β‐catenin/MYC/LDHA axis‐mediated glycolytic reprogramming and correlates with lymph node metastasis, advanced TNM staging, and poor prognosis in CRC patients [12]. In bladder cancer, REG Iα downregulation significantly attenuates in vitro proliferation, migration, invasion, and VEGF‐induced angiogenesis [13]. However, the involvement of REG Iα in other critical malignant processes and its comprehensive regulatory network in CRC require further investigation.

Endothelin Receptor B (EDNRB), a classical G protein‐coupled receptor (GPCR), plays pivotal roles in regulating fundamental cellular behaviors. Genetic ablation of EDNRB ameliorates bleomycin‐induced fibroblast activation and subsequent skin fibrosis in mice [14]. In triple‐negative breast cancer, EDNRB silencing via specific siRNA significantly attenuates proliferation, migration, and invasion while promoting apoptosis [15]. Aberrant activation of calcium ion (Ca2+) signaling, a crucial second messenger system, correlates with malignant phenotypes including tumor proliferation, survival, and motility [16]. Notably, EDNRB activation serves as a key upstream event triggering intracellular Ca2+ release. Mechanistically, ligand‐bound EDNRB activates phospholipase C (PLC) via GPCR signaling, generating inositol trisphosphate (IP3), which binds to receptors on the endoplasmic reticulum to induce Ca2+ release and initiate downstream signaling [17, 18]. Additionally, accumulating evidence demonstrates that altered Ca2+ levels critically influence epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT)‐related transcription factor activity, ultimately driving EMT. For instance, TRPV4 ion channel‐mediated Ca2+ signaling has been identified as essential for inducing EMT in breast cancer cells [19], while the histidine‐rich calcium‐binding protein promotes gastric cancer EMT through Ca2+‐dependent Raf/MEK/ERK pathway activation [20]. However, whether REG Iα affects CRC progression via EDNRB‐mediated Ca2+ signaling remains unknown, warranting further investigation to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms.

In this study, we initially performed REG Iα knockdown in CRC cells combined with RNA‐seq and GO/KEGG enrichment analyses to identify potential key genes within the REG Iα downstream regulatory network. Subsequently, through comprehensive in vitro and in vivo experimental models, we systematically investigated EDNRB as a critical downstream target of REG Iα in CRC progression. Our mechanistic studies revealed that EDNRB activation promoted malignant phenotypes including proliferation, migration, invasion, and apoptosis resistance via Ca2+ signaling‐mediated induction of EMT. These findings provide novel insights into the molecular network underlying CRC invasion and metastasis and establish a theoretical foundation for developing targeted therapeutic strategies against REG Iα or EDNRB to suppress tumor growth and dissemination.

Methods

Establishment of Subcutaneous Xenograft Model

BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old, 18–22 g, equal numbers of males and females) were purchased from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China) and maintained under SPF conditions (22°C ± 2°C, 50% ± 5% humidity, 12 h light/dark cycle) with free access to standard laboratory chow and sterilized water. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. All animal experiments were conducted in strict accordance with the institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University, ensuring the ethical treatment and welfare of all animals involved in this study. Furthermore, all methods were reported following the ARRIVE guidelines. After one‐week acclimatization, mice were randomly divided into five groups (n = 6/group): NC, REG Iαkd, REG Iαkd + EV, REG Iαkd + EDNRBOE, and REG Iαkd + EDNRBOE + BAPTA‐AM. For tumor inoculation, 5 × 105 LoVo cells suspended in 200 μL serum‐free medium were subcutaneously injected into the left posterior flank. Tumor size was measured every 2 days using calipers. Starting on day 7 post‐inoculation, BAPTA‐AM was administered intraperitoneally every 2 days, with control groups receiving equal volumes of vehicle at corresponding time points. At the experimental endpoint, mice were deeply anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital before being euthanized by cervical dislocation. Tumors were completely excised, weighed, and photographed before being either snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen for molecular analysis or fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological examination.

Cell Culture and Treatment

The human colorectal cancer cell lines LoVo (from a male patient; RRID: CVCL_0399) and SW620 (from a male patient; RRID: CVCL_0547) were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co. Ltd. (Wuhan, China). The cell lines were authenticated by the supplier using STR profiling. All cell lines were routinely tested and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination using a PCR‐based assay before experimentation. Upon thawing, cells were immediately cultured in RPMI‐1640 medium (ServiceBio, G4531) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (ServiceBio, G8003) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Beyotime, C0222) and maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

RNA‐Sequencing (RNA‐Seq) and Bioinformatic Analysis

To identify target genes regulated by REG Iα, LoVo cells were transfected with REG Iα siRNA or a negative control (NC) siRNA for 48 h. Total RNA was then isolated from the treated cells using TRIzol reagent (Beyotime, R0016). The subsequent RNA‐sequencing and bioinformatic analyses were performed as a professional service by Applied Protein Technology (APT, China). Briefly, RNA quality was assessed, and libraries were prepared using the KAPA Stranded RNA‐Seq Library Preparation Kit. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina platform. Bioinformatic analysis included read mapping, gene quantification, and the identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with a threshold of an adjusted P‐value (padj) < 0.05 and |Log2FoldChange| ≥ 2. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were also performed. The KEGG database (www.kegg.jp) was used as the annotation source for pathway analysis [21, 22, 23].

H&E Staining (Hematoxylin and Eosin Staining)

Tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated in graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Sections (4 μm) were cut and subjected to deparaffinization with xylene followed by rehydration through graded ethanol. The sections were stained using a hematoxylin and eosin staining kit (ServiceBio, G1005) according to the manufacturer's protocol. After staining, the sections were dehydrated through graded alcohols (70%, 85%, 95%, and 100%), cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam. Histopathological examination was performed, and images were captured using an Olympus BX53 light microscope equipped with a digital camera system.

Reverse Transcription‐Quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Beyotime, R0016), followed by cDNA synthesis from 1 μg of total RNA using a reverse transcription kit (Beyotime, D7170). The thermal conditions for reverse transcription were as follows: 25°C for 10 min, 42°C for 50 min, and 85°C for 5 min. Quantitative PCR was performed on a Bio‐Rad CFX96 system with FastSYBR Mixture (CWBio, CW2622M), using β‐actin as the endogenous control. Relative gene expression was calculated by the 2 − ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

Western Blot

Total protein was extracted from colorectal cancer tissues or cells using RIPA lysis buffer, with concentrations determined by BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, P0010S). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS‐PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% skimmed milk for 1.5 h at room temperature, membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against the following targets: REG Iα (Abcam, ab231092), EDNRB (BOSTER, PB9554), p‐CaMKII (Abcam, ab124880), GRP78(BOSTER, BA4293). CHOP (Abcam, ab317378), Cleaved‐Caspase 3 (Abcam, ab32042), Cleaved‐PARP (Cell Signaling Technology, 9541), E‐Cadherin (BOSTER, PB9561), N‐Cadherin (BOSTER, A01577‐3), and Vimentin (BOSTER, BM4029). HRP‐conjugated secondary antibody (Abcam, ab97051) was then applied for 1 h at room temperature. Protein signals were detected using an ECL kit (Beyotime, P0018S), with β‐actin (Abcam, ab8226) as the loading control.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) Staining

Tissue sections were baked at 60°C for 3 h, deparaffinized in xylene, and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating samples in citrate buffer (pH 6.0), followed by permeabilization and endogenous peroxidase blocking with 3% hydrogen peroxide. After blocking with 5% goat serum for 60 min, sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against REG Iα (Abcam, ab231092) and EDNRB (BOSTER, PB9554). The next day, sections were incubated with secondary antibody for 1 h, developed with DAB substrate, and counterstained with hematoxylin. After dehydration through graded alcohols, clearing in xylene, and mounting, the stained sections were examined microscopically.

Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8) Assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK‐8 kit (Beyotime, C0037) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Cells were seeded in 96‐well plates (1000 cells/well) and cultured for 24 h. Subsequently, 10 μL of CCK‐8 reagent was added to each well, followed by incubation for 2 h in a humidified 37°C incubator with 5% CO₂. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Bio‐Rad 680 microplate reader.

TUNEL Assay

Apoptosis was detected using a TUNEL assay kit (Beyotime, C1086). Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature, washed with PBS, and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X‐100 in PBS for 5 min. After washing, 50 μL of the TUNEL reaction mixture was added to each sample to ensure complete coverage of the cells, followed by incubation at 37°C for 60 min in the dark. The stained cells were visualized and analyzed using a Zeiss Axio Observer 7 fluorescence microscope.

Flow Cytometry

Apoptosis Assay: Cells were harvested at 70%–80% confluence, trypsinized, and resuspended in PBS. After centrifugation, cells were stained with fluorescently labeled Annexin V and propidium iodide (PI) (ServiceBio, G1511) in 1× binding buffer according to the manufacturer's instructions, followed by incubation at room temperature for 10 min in the dark. Samples were analyzed using a flow cytometer to detect apoptosis.

Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) Assay: To measure intracellular ROS levels, CRC cells were cultured in 6‐well plates to 70%–80% confluence, harvested, and washed with serum‐free medium. Cells were then incubated with 10 μM DCFH‐DA probe (Beyotime, S0033S) for 20 min at 37°C in the dark. Following incubation, cells were washed three times with serum‐free medium to remove extracellular dye. Samples were then analyzed using a flow cytometer to measure ROS levels.

5‐Ethynyl‐20‐Deoxyuridine (EdU) Incorporation Assay

Colorectal cancer (CRC) cells were incubated with medium containing 50 μM EdU (Beyotime, C0078S) for 2 h, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X‐100 for 10 min. After PBS washing, cells were incubated with the Click reaction mixture at room temperature for 30 min in the dark, followed by nuclear counterstaining with Hoechst 3,3342 for 5 min. The EdU‐positive cells (red fluorescence) were visualized under a fluorescence microscope.

Transwell Assay

For migration assays, 700 μL of complete medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower wells of 24‐well plates, while Matrigel‐precoated chambers were used for the invasion assays. Cells (2 × 104 cells/well) suspended in 200 μL of serum‐free medium were seeded in the upper chambers. After 24 h (migration) or 48 h (invasion) incubation, chambers were washed, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min. Migrated or invaded cells were counted under a microscope.

Intracellular Calcium Detection

Cytosolic Ca2+ levels were assessed using the Fluo‐4 AM fluorescent probe (Beyotime, S1060). Briefly, CRC cells were incubated with 2 μM Fluo‐4 AM for 20 min at 37°C and washed with Ca2+‐free Hank's balanced salt solution. The real‐time fluorescence intensity (excitation: 488 nm/emission 520 nm) was monitored by confocal laser scanning microscopy, with signal changes quantified to determine dynamic Ca2+ level variations.

Data Processing and Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 software and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student's t‐test, while one‐way ANOVA was used for multiple group comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, with additional thresholds of p < 0.01, p < 0.001, and p < 0.0001 as indicated. Non‐normally distributed data were analyzed using nonparametric tests. All experiments included at least three biological replicates and three technical replicates to ensure reliability and reproducibility.

Results

REG Iα Is Significantly Overexpressed in Colorectal Cancer Tissues and Associated With Poor Pathological Features

To investigate the role of REG Iα in CRC development, we collected 16 paired CRC and adjacent normal tissue samples. RT‐qPCR confirmed the upregulation of REG Iα mRNA levels in tumor tissues (Figure 1A). Western blot analysis further validated elevated REG Iα protein expression in multiple representative tumor samples (Figure 1B). These findings collectively demonstrated that REG Iα was aberrantly overexpressed in CRC and might function as an oncogenic factor.

Bioinformatics Identification of REG Iα Downstream Pathways and Key Targets

To investigate the molecular mechanisms downstream of REG Iα, LoVo cells were transfected with either si REG Iα or NC using lipofection and harvested for RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) at 48 h post‐transfection. Principal component analysis (PCA) and violin plots demonstrated clear transcriptomic separation between the REG Iαkd and control groups, confirming data reliability (Figure 2A,B). Volcano plot analysis using stringent criteria (|log2FoldChange| > 2, padj < 0.01) identified multiple differentially expressed genes, with the G protein‐coupled receptor EDNRB being among the most significantly downregulated (Figure 2C). Pathway enrichment analysis revealed that these genes were significantly enriched in key cancer‐related pathways including the calcium signaling and PI3K‐Akt signaling pathways (Figure 2D). GO analysis further supported these findings, showing significant enrichment of endothelin receptor activity, along with other biological processes such as G protein‐coupled receptor signaling and cell adhesion (Figure S1). Validation by RT‐qPCR showed strong agreement with the RNA‐seq results, with EDNRB exhibiting the most significant changes, indicating that REG Iα knockdown effectively suppressed EDNRB expression (Figure 2E). These results suggested that REG Iα might promote colorectal cancer progression by regulating its downstream target EDNRB and activating calcium signaling pathways.

REG Iα Modulates CRC Cell Proliferation, Apoptosis and Calcium Signaling Through EDNRB Regulation

To determine whether REG Iα affected cellular functions via EDNRB, we performed EDNRB overexpression in si REG Iα‐treated LoVo and SW620 cells. qPCR and Western blot analyses demonstrated that REG Iα knockdown significantly reduced both REG Iα and EDNRB expression, while EDNRB overexpression effectively restored EDNRB levels without altering REG Iα knockdown efficiency (Figure 3A,B). Furthermore, REG Iα knockdown markedly decreased the protein expression of EDNRB and downstream p‐CaMKII, which was rescued by EDNRB overexpression (Figure 3B). Fluo‐4 AM calcium imaging showed that the reduced intracellular Ca2+ levels induced by REG Iα knockdown were reversed following EDNRB overexpression (Figure 3C). The EdU incorporation, CCK‐8 and flow cytometry assays revealed that REG Iα knockdown significantly inhibited cell proliferation and increased apoptosis, whereas EDNRB overexpression restored proliferative capacity and reduced apoptosis (Figure 3D–F). These findings indicated that REG Iα likely regulated CRC cell proliferation and apoptosis by modulating EDNRB expression and subsequent calcium signaling activation.

REG Iα Promotes CRC Cell Migration, Invasion and EMT Through EDNRB Regulation

To further investigate the role of the REG Iα‐EDNRB axis in CRC cell metastasis, we assessed migration, invasion, and epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT) status. REG Iα knockdown significantly inhibited cell migration and invasion, while EDNRB overexpression restored these capabilities (Figure 4A,B). Western blot analysis of EMT markers revealed that REG Iα knockdown increased E‐cadherin expression and decreased N‐cadherin and Vimentin levels. EDNRB overexpression effectively reversed these EMT marker changes, thereby promoting mesenchymal transition (Figure 4C). These results demonstrated that the REG Iα‐EDNRB axis played a pivotal role in regulating CRC cell migration, invasion, and EMT progression.

EDNRB Regulates Malignant Phenotypes and Apoptosis via Ca2+‐Dependent ER Stress and ROS Accumulation

To verify whether calcium signaling mediated REG Iα‐EDNRB axis function, we treated cells with both REG Iα knockdown and EDNRB overexpression with the calcium chelator BAPTA‐AM. Western blot analyses confirmed that BAPTA‐AM treatment did not affect REG Iα knockdown efficiency or EDNRB overexpression levels (Figure 5A). However, BAPTA‐AM significantly inhibited the EDNRB‐induced p‐CaMKII phosphorylation (Figure 5A). Fluo‐4 AM calcium imaging demonstrated that BAPTA‐AM effectively reduced intracellular Ca2+ levels (Figure 5B). Functional assays (EdU, CCK‐8 and flow cytometry) showed that while EDNRB overexpression rescued the proliferative capacity and reduced apoptosis in cells with REG Iα knockdown, these effects were reversed by BAPTA‐AM treatment (Figure 5C–E). To further dissect the molecular mechanism linking these phenotypes to apoptosis, we investigated endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and oxidative stress. As shown in Figure 6, REG Iα knockdown significantly upregulated the expression of ER stress markers (GRP78, CHOP) and apoptotic executioners (cleaved Caspase‐3, cleaved PARP) (Figure 6A), while inducing a robust accumulation of intracellular ROS (Figure 6B). Importantly, EDNRB overexpression significantly attenuated these stress responses; however, BAPTA‐AM treatment re‐induced ER stress, ROS accumulation, and the activation of Caspase‐3/PARP. These results demonstrated that the REG Iα‐EDNRB axis promoted cell survival by maintaining calcium homeostasis to prevent ER stress‐induced apoptosis.

Inhibition of Calcium Signaling Reverses REG Iα‐EDNRB Axis‐Induced Cell Migration, Invasion and EMT

To evaluate the role of calcium signaling in REG Iα‐EDNRB axis‐regulated cell metastasis, we treated REG Iα‐knockdown, EDNRB‐overexpressing cells with the calcium signaling inhibitor BAPTA‐AM. As shown in Figure 7, compared with the REG Iαkd + EDNRBOE group, BAPTA‐AM treatment significantly inhibited cell migration and invasion capabilities (Figure 7A,B). Subsequently, we examined the expression of EMT‐related proteins by Western blot. The results showed that EDNRB overexpression promoted mesenchymal transition; however, this effect was reversed by BAPTA‐AM treatment, as evidenced by the significantly increased expression of the epithelial marker E‐cadherin and the significantly decreased expression of the mesenchymal markers N‐cadherin and Vimentin (Figure 7C). These results indicated that the promoting effects of the REG Iα‐EDNRB axis on cell migration, invasion and EMT depended on the activation of downstream calcium signaling pathways.

The REG Iα/EDNRB/Calcium Signaling Axis Drives Colorectal Cancer Growth in Vivo

To investigate the tumor‐promoting function of the REG Iα‐EDNRB‐calcium signaling axis in vivo, LoVo cells treated with NC, REG Iαkd, REG Iαkd + EV, REG Iαkd + EDNRBOE, and REG Iαkd + EDNRBOE + BAPTA‐AM were subcutaneously injected into nude mice to establish xenograft models. Compared to controls, mice in the REG Iαkd group showed significantly reduced tumor size, growth rate and final weight (Figure 8A–C). Histopathological analysis revealed decreased tumor cell density and attenuated cellular atypia (Figure 8D). Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated reduced Ki67‐positive cells and increased TUNEL‐positive cells, indicating suppressed proliferation and enhanced apoptosis (Figure 8E,F). Further experiments revealed that EDNRB overexpression reversed the tumor‐suppressive effects of REG Iαkd, while BAPTA‐AM co‐treatment counteracted the pro‐tumor effects of EDNRB overexpression (Figure 8A–F). Through Western blot and immunostaining experiments, we found that the inhibition of REG Iα expression led to a significant downregulation in the protein levels of both REG Iα and EDNRB, accompanied by a significant decrease in p‐CaMKII levels. Conversely, upon EDNRB overexpression, the expression of REG Iα remained unchanged, while EDNRB levels were significantly increased, accompanied by a concurrent significant increase in p‐CaMKII expression. Subsequently, after treatment with the inhibitor BAPTA‐AM, the expression levels of EDNRB and REG Iα were unaffected, but the level of p‐CaMKII was suppressed (Figure 8G,H). Furthermore, Western blot analysis showed that REG Iα knockdown increased E‐cadherin and decreased N‐cadherin and Vimentin expression compared to NC controls. These changes were reversed by EDNRB overexpression but blocked again by BAPTA‐AM treatment (Figure 8I). These results demonstrated that REG Iα promoted tumor cell proliferation, suppressed apoptosis and drove EMT progression through EDNRB upregulation and calcium signaling activation.

Discussion

CRC ranks among the most prevalent malignancies worldwide in terms of both incidence and mortality [24]. The uncontrolled proliferation and distant metastasis of CRC cells represent major contributors to poor patient prognosis [25]. Although therapeutic approaches for CRC, including targeted therapies and immunotherapies, continue to advance, the development of novel effective treatment strategies remains imperative due to tumor heterogeneity and drug resistance [26]. Therefore, identifying new molecular targets and elucidating their mechanisms are crucial for developing effective therapeutic approaches. In our previous study, we demonstrated that REG Iα knockdown enhanced 5‐FU sensitivity in CRC cells [27]. To investigate the specific mechanisms of REG Iα in CRC, we performed transcriptome sequencing and identified EDNRB as a significantly regulated downstream gene. Consistently, enrichment analysis revealed significant involvement of endothelin receptor activity, the G protein‐coupled receptor signaling pathway, and cell adhesion‐related biological processes. Notably, RT‐qPCR validation of selected candidate genes, including EDNRB, showed high concordance with the RNA‐seq data. These findings suggest that REG Iα may play an important role in CRC pathogenesis by regulating EDNRB expression and its associated signaling pathways.

EDNRB, a ubiquitously expressed G protein‐coupled receptor, mediates diverse physiological and pathological processes through ligand binding. In prostate cancer, EDNRB activation suppresses cell growth and migration via the cGMP‐PKG pathway [28], whereas in melanoma, it promotes cell proliferation, metastasis, and chemoresistance [28, 29]. The regulatory effect of REG Iα on EDNRB in CRC malignancy remains unclear. Our experimental data demonstrated that REG Iα knockdown significantly enhanced apoptosis while suppressing proliferation, migration, and invasion. Notably, EDNRB overexpression in REG Iα‐deficient CRC cells effectively rescued these malignant phenotypes and attenuated apoptosis. Furthermore, we observed that REG Iα silencing markedly reduced p‐CaMKII protein expression and intracellular Ca2+ levels, suggesting that REG Iα may exert its oncogenic function through the EDNRB‐mediated regulation of downstream calcium signaling pathways.

The Ca2+ signaling pathway serves as a fundamental cellular transduction system, the dysregulation of which critically impacts various human diseases. In autoimmune disorders, antibodies targeting calcium channels induce cardiac arrhythmias [30], while mitochondrial calcium overload triggers neuronal apoptosis in neurodegenerative diseases [31]. In cancer biology, aberrant Ca2+ signaling drives malignant progression through multiple mechanisms [32]. For instance, prostate, breast, and lung cancer cells frequently maintain elevated basal Ca2+ influx by remodeling the expression or activity of plasma membrane channels to promote proliferation and migration [33]. As a pivotal Ca2+ signaling node, CaMKII regulates diverse oncogenic behaviors through the ERK1/2, p38, and AKT pathways to modulate migration, apoptosis, and autophagy balance [34, 35]. Our experiments demonstrated that BAPTA‐AM treatment effectively blocked the intracellular Ca2+ elevation and CaMKII phosphorylation induced by EDNRB overexpression, without altering the expression of upstream REG Iα or EDNRB. Functionally, inhibition of calcium signaling reversed the EDNRB‐rescued malignant phenotypes in CRC cells, including proliferation, survival, migration, and EMT. These results indicate that the REG Iα/EDNRB axis exerts its oncogenic function in CRC through the regulation of downstream Ca2+/CaMKII signaling. Furthermore, our study reveals a novel molecular mechanism by which this axis regulates cell survival. We found that inhibition of the REG Iα/EDNRB axis disrupted calcium homeostasis, leading to severe endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). It is well established that calcium overload or depletion in the ER can trigger the unfolded protein response (UPR); when unresolved, this stress activates the pro‐apoptotic factor CHOP and initiates the caspase cascade [36, 37, 38]. Consistently, our data confirmed this cytotoxic cascade, as evidenced by the marked upregulation of GRP78/CHOP and the cleavage of Caspase‐3/PARP, which ultimately drove the ER stress‐induced apoptosis observed in our study.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that REG Iα was significantly upregulated in colorectal cancer (CRC) and functioned as a critical oncogenic driver by activating the EDNRB/Ca2+/CaMKII signaling axis. We provided direct molecular evidence that the inhibition of this axis triggered ER stress‐induced apoptosis, characterized by the substantial accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the subsequent biochemical activation of the executioner proteins cleaved Caspase‐3 and cleaved PARP. Furthermore, this signaling pathway promoted malignant progression by driving epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT), thereby enhancing the migratory and invasive capabilities of CRC cells. However, this study has certain limitations, particularly regarding the precise molecular mechanisms of the crosstalk between ER stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, which require further detailed investigation. These findings not only advance our understanding of the molecular crosstalk in CRC pathophysiology but also identify the REG Iα/EDNRB axis as a novel molecular target for clinical intervention, highlighting its significant translational potential.
