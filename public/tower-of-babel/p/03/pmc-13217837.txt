Bilateral traumatic testicular dislocation associated with an unstable pelvic ring injury: a rare case report and literature review
Authors: Peter V Giannoudis, Konstantinos Giatroudakis, Spyridon P Galanakos, Theodoros K Kateros, Konstantinos Kateros
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraumatic testicular dislocation is a rare complication of high-energy blunt trauma, most commonly occurring in motorcycle collisions. When associated with pelvic ring injuries, diagnosis may be delayed due to haemodynamic instability, soft-tissue swelling and distracting injuries. We report a 20-year-old man who sustained an unstable anteroposterior compression pelvic ring injury following a motorcycle accident, complicated by bilateral extra-scrotal testicular dislocation. Initial clinical assessment was limited by extensive scrotal haematoma. Cross-sectional imaging and Doppler ultrasonography demonstrated bilateral displacement of the testes into the superficial inguinal regions with reduced perfusion. The patient underwent combined orthopaedic and urological surgical management, including definitive pelvic fixation and bilateral orchidopexy. Postoperative imaging confirmed restoration of normal testicular position and blood flow. At 12-month follow-up, normal urogenital, endocrine and sexual function were preserved.Keywords: Traumatic testicular dislocation, Pelvic ring fracture, Anteroposterior compression injury, Genitourinary trauma

Introduction

Pelvic trauma represents a significant challenge in modern trauma care, particularly with the rise of high-energy mechanisms such as motorcycle and motor vehicle collisions. These injuries often involve multisystem damage, with pelvic ring disruptions associated with life-threatening hemorrhage and a considerable risk of genitourinary complications. Bladder and urethral injuries are well-recognized sequelae, occurring in up to 15% of severe pelvic fractures, but injuries involving the testes are far less commonly reported [1].

Traumatic testicular dislocation (TTD), defined as the displacement of one or both normally descended testes out of the scrotum, is an especially rare complication, with fewer than 200 cases described in the literature since the first report by Claubry in 1809 [2]. Most cases occur in young males following straddle-type or “fuel tank” impacts during motorcycle crashes, where blunt perineal trauma and reflex cremasteric contraction are combined to force the testis into ectopic positions ranging from the superficial inguinal region to abdominal and prepubic locations.

Due to significant swelling, hematoma, and associated pelvic injuries or intra-abdominal bleeding that commonly coexist, TTD is frequently being masked and underdiagnosed or missed altogether. Prompt recognition is essential, as delayed treatment increases the risk of impaired fertility, hypogonadism, or testicular loss. While careful scrotal examination is crucial, imaging -especially Doppler ultrasonography and computed tomography (CT)- is often required for accurate localization [3]. Awareness of TTD is also critical to prevent iatrogenic injury during anterior pelvic or acetabular surgery, where an unrecognized displaced testis may lie within the operative field [4].

Given the rarity of this condition and its potential long-term consequences, increasing awareness among trauma, orthopaedic, and urologic teams is crucial. This report contributes to the limited body of literature by presenting a case of bilateral TTD associated with an unstable pelvic ring injury, highlighting diagnostic challenges, multidisciplinary management, and strategies to avoid delayed or missed diagnosis.

Case presentation

A previously healthy 20-year-old man was involved in a high-energy motorcycle accident resulting in severe polytrauma, including pelvic trauma, right distal radius fracture, Gustilo-Anderson II open left distal radius fracture, and maxillofacial fractures.

On arrival at the emergency department, the patient was alert with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15, but complained of intense suprapubic and pelvic pain. Physical examination revealed multiple contusions, edematous external genitalia, and a large bilateral scrotal hematoma, which prevented reliable palpation of either testis.

Plain anteroposterior pelvic X-ray and CT under pelvic binder demonstrated a Young-Burgess anteroposterior compression type III (APC III) pelvic ring injury, with diastasis of the pubic symphysis and left sacroiliac joint, bilateral ischiopubic and sacral fractures, right acetabulum fracture and extensive soft-tissue hematoma in the lower abdomen with signs of active bleeding. Notably, both testes were absent from their normal scrotal position (Fig. 1, Fig. 2).

Coronal and axial CT images of the pelvis obtained under pelvic binder, demonstrating absence of both testes from the scrotum and abnormal displacement of the right testis through the external inguinal ring into the right superficial inguinal region (arrows), consistent with extra-scrotal testicular dislocation.

Despite pelvic binder application, the patient subsequently developed recurrent hemodynamic instability, consistent with ongoing intrapelvic hemorrhage. Consequently, an emergent pelvic external fixator with iliac crest placement was applied as a damage-control measure, achieving definitive hemodynamic stabilization.

On postoperative day one, after restoration of hemodynamic stability, repeat CT and targeted Doppler ultrasonography of the superficial inguinal regions were performed (Fig. 3). Re-evaluation of imaging revealed both testes displaced through the external inguinal rings, located within the superficial inguinal regions, consistent with bilateral extra-scrotal testicular dislocation. Subsequent Doppler ultrasonography of the superficial inguinal regions demonstrated diminished intratesticular blood flow bilaterally, raising concern for vascular compromise.

Post-stabilization via external fixator pelvic 3D CT, showing a Young-Burgess APC III injury with diastasis of the pubic symphysis and left sacroiliac joint, bilateral ischiopubic and sacral fractures.

Given the abnormal imaging findings and the progressive scrotal hematoma, the patient was scheduled for urgent definitive surgical management involving both orthopaedic and urologic teams.

Under general anesthesia, a Pfannenstiel incision was performed for management of the pubic symphysis diastasis. Surgical exploration revealed a large prepubic and inguinal hematoma, with both testes located high within the superficial inguinal region, protruding through the external inguinal rings and appearing mildly cyanotic due to compression (Fig. 4). After evacuation of the hematoma, warming and decompression, both testes regained normal coloration. Intraoperative Doppler ultrasonography confirmed restoration of normal arterial and venous blood flow, with no evidence of torsion or spermatic cord disruption.

Intraoperative view during the anterior pelvic approach, demonstrating the right testis displaced through the external inguinal ring and lying within the superficial inguinal region, directly within the surgical field.

A left ilioinguinal approach was subsequently performed. The left sacroiliac joint was anatomically reduced and stabilized with three plates, followed by definitive fixation of the pubic symphysis with two plates. The anterior column of the right acetabulum was additionally stabilized using a partially threaded cannulated screw. Intraoperative fluoroscopy confirmed anatomic reduction of the pelvic ring and satisfactory positioning of all implants.

The urology team then reassessed the testes intraoperatively. Both testes, including the tunicae albugineae and vasa deferentia, were found to be intact. Manual reduction of the testes into the scrotum was performed, followed by bilateral orchidopexy via a scrotal approach.

Postoperative scrotal Doppler ultrasonography prior to discharge demonstrated normal bilateral testicular position and preserved intratesticular blood flow (Fig. 5). The patient was discharged with a urethral catheter in situ and scheduled for close outpatient follow-up.

Two weeks postoperative scrotal Doppler ultrasonography, demonstrating both testes in normal scrotal position with preserved echotexture and normal intratesticular vascular flow.

Endocrinological evaluation at three months postoperatively demonstrated normal “hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis” function, with normal semen analysis and normal total and bioavailable testosterone levels.

At 6- and 12-month follow-up visits, the patient reported no urogenital symptoms, including no erectile dysfunction, voiding problems, or testicular pain. Radiographic evaluation demonstrated stable pelvic fixation without evidence of hardware failure (Fig. 6). Overall, the patient reported excellent urologic and sexual function, with no pain or functional limitation.

Anteroposterior pelvic radiograph 1 year post-operatively.

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of clinical data and images. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee.

Discussion

Traumatic testicular dislocation (TTD) is a rare but clinically important sequela of high-energy blunt trauma, most commonly associated with motorcycle collisions where the perineum or scrotum is impacted against the fuel tank. The underlying mechanism involves a combination of direct external force and reflex cremasteric muscle contraction, often facilitated by anatomical predispositions such as a wide superficial inguinal ring or a patent inguinal canal [5]. Although unilateral involvement is more common, bilateral dislocation has been reported in a minority of cases and is frequently associated with unstable pelvic ring injuries [3].

The superficial inguinal pouch represents the most common site of testicular displacement, accounting for nearly 50% of reported cases. Less frequent ectopic locations include the pubic region (18%), penile shaft (8%), inguinal canal (8%), intra-abdominal position (6%), perineum (4%), acetabular region (4%), and crural area (2%) [6], [7]. This wide range of potential locations contributes to the diagnostic difficulty of TTD, particularly in the setting of polytrauma.

Delayed or missed diagnosis remains a well-recognized problem. Sehrawat et al. reported a case in which a dislocated testis was identified only intraoperatively during pelvic fixation [8]. Similarly, Bernhard et al. described an APC-type pelvic ring injury in which an ectopic testis was unexpectedly encountered beneath the pubis during anterior pelvic surgery; prompt urologic intervention allowed successful orchidopexy with an excellent outcome [9]. Hsu et al. reported a case of bilateral TTD following a motor vehicle collision and reviewed the literature, noting that delayed surgical intervention does not necessarily result in pain or impaired sperm parameters, although early recognition remains preferable [3].

Both physical examination and imaging play essential roles in diagnosis. Careful inspection and palpation of the scrotum should be an integral part of the secondary survey in trauma patients, even in the presence of hemodynamic instability and distracting injuries. Doppler ultrasonography provides functional assessment of testicular perfusion, while computed tomography is particularly valuable for localizing ectopic testes in the context of complex pelvic anatomy. Lenfant et al. emphasized that TTD may be overlooked when imaging is interpreted without a high index of suspicion, underscoring the need for heightened awareness among trauma teams [10].

Management strategies vary depending on timing and associated injuries. Manual reduction has been described but demonstrates a low success rate, reported at approximately 15%, largely due to edema, hematoma, fascial disruption, and concomitant pelvic trauma. Consequently, most authors advocate early surgical exploration and orchidopexy. Open reduction permits evacuation of hematoma, direct assessment of testicular viability and spermatic cord integrity, and secure fixation of the testes, thereby minimizing the risk of recurrent displacement and preserving the blood–testis barrier [6]. Importantly, even delayed surgical repair has been associated with favorable outcomes, including preserved spermatogenesis and normal endocrine function [3], [11].

Bilateral testicular dislocation associated with pelvic ring fracture represents an exceedingly rare injury pattern. A non-systematic literature search restricted to English-language publications was conducted using the keywords “(bilateral) AND (testicular OR testes) AND dislocation” in the PubMed and Scopus databases, identifying only four previously reported cases describing this combination [3], [12], [13], [14] (Table 1).

Compared to them, the present case is distinguished by several features. First, it represents the only reported case of bilateral traumatic testicular dislocation associated with a Young–Burgess APC III pelvic ring injury, reflecting a higher-energy trauma pattern than in most published cases. To our knowledge, this is the first English-language report describing this unique combination of injuries. Second, both testes were displaced into the superficial inguinal regions and lay directly within the operative field of the anterior pelvic approach, posing a tangible risk of iatrogenic injury during fixation. Third, although initial assessment was limited by extensive scrotal haematoma, early targeted imaging and intraoperative recognition allowed prompt multidisciplinary management without diagnostic or therapeutic delay.

In the setting of pelvic ring injuries, unrecognized testicular dislocation carries an additional risk of iatrogenic injury during orthopaedic procedures. Anterior pelvic approaches, including Pfannenstiel and modified Stoppa incisions, place ectopic testes -particularly those in prepubic or superficial inguinal locations- directly within the operative field. Preoperative identification of testicular position is therefore essential, and early urologic consultation should be considered routine in patients with pelvic fractures and suspected genitourinary involvement [4].

In summary, TTD is a rare but potentially serious injury that requires a high index of suspicion in patients sustaining high-energy pelvic trauma. Thorough genital examination, appropriate imaging, multidisciplinary collaboration, and timely surgical management are critical to avoiding missed diagnosis and long-term complications. When recognized and treated appropriately, even complex cases of bilateral TTD can result in excellent urologic, endocrine, and functional outcomes.
