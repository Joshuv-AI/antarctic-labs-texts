Mesenchymal stromal cell therapy for systemic lupus erythematosus: mechanisms, clinical translation, and future directions
Authors: Shuaijie Ding, Shiyi Wu, Yunan Cui, Yixin Zhou, Sheng Qiu, Zhi Ye, Ruitian Ma, Yixuan Wang Wan, Zhenhua Ying
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSystemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE) is a complex autoimmune condition, fundamentally characterized by the loss of self-tolerance. This breakdown leads to the generation of autoantibodies and widespread inflammation that compromises multiple organs. A significant clinical challenge in SLE management is the lack of effective options for refractory disease, driving the pursuit of innovative treatments. Among these, mesenchymal stromal/stem cells (MSCs) have garnered significant attention due to their potent immunomodulatory and tissue-repair capabilities, highlighting their potential as a viable therapy. This review critically assesses the potential of MSCs in reconciling the unmet need for effective SLE therapies by examining their immunomodulatory mechanisms, clinical efficacy, and safety. MSCs reestablish immune tolerance through coordinated interactions with T and B cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells, predominantly facilitated by soluble factor secretion and extracellular vesicle release. In severe SLE, allogeneic MSC transplantation has demonstrated a capacity to induce durable remission, restore organ function, and improve survival rates, coupled with a reassuring safety profile. However, the field must still address issues of product standardization and long-term safety evaluation. Next-generation solutions-featuring engineered MSCs, iPSC-MSCs products, and vesicle-based therapies-hold the potential to usher in a new era of personalized SLE treatment. The conduct of large-scale randomized controlled trials is now imperative to definitively establish therapeutic efficacy and optimize clinical protocols.Keywords: Systemic lupus erythematosus, Mesenchymal stromal cell, Immunomodulation, Cell therapy, Cell engineering modifications, Clinical translation, Safety

Introduction

Pathological characteristics of systemic lupus erythematosus

Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disorder that exhibits a strong female predilection, particularly during the reproductive years [1]. The disease is defined by the production of pathogenic autoantibodies, primarily directed against nuclear antigens, resulting in immune complex deposition and subsequent multi-organ inflammation and damage [2]. The pathogenesis of SLE stems from a convergence of genetic susceptibility and environmental triggers such as viral infections, ultraviolet exposure, and hormonal factors which collectively disrupt immune tolerance to self-antigens [3]. This autoimmune response targets self-antigens, leading to chronic systemic inflammation and widespread tissue injury. Immune dysregulation in SLE is marked by aberrant activation of B and T lymphocytes, accompanied by a breakdown of self-tolerance mechanisms [4]. The clinical heterogeneity of SLE is driven by dysregulated autoantibody production, impaired immune complex clearance, complement activation, and aberrant cytokine signaling, resulting in a disease spectrum that ranges from mild symptoms like fatigue and arthralgia to life-threatening organ involvement [5–7]. Autoreactive T and B cell activation follows the loss of tolerance to nuclear antigens, culminating in the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and autoantibodies against nuclear components, notably anti-double-stranded DNA (dsDNA), anti-phospholipid antibodies (anti-aPL) and anti-ribonucleoprotein antibodies (anti-RNP) [8]. Immune complex deposition in small vessels and tissues activates the complement cascade, provoking local inflammation and progressive organ dysfunction. The type and severity of clinical manifestations depend on the distribution and burden of immune complex deposition, the organs affected, and cumulative disease activity. Commonly affected organs include the kidney, skin, joints, serosal surfaces, lungs, and cardiovascular system. Additional manifestations may involve hematologic cytopenias, neuropsychiatric symptoms, and ocular or endocrine dysfunction [9, 10]. Estimates derived from the US Lupus Registry and meta-analyses of epidemiological studies indicate an incidence rate of 23.2 per one hundred thousand person-years for SLE in North America [11]. The incidence of lupus is higher among African Americans, Hispanics, and Asians compared to Caucasians [12]. Although no curative therapy exists, SLE can be effectively managed with pharmacologic regimens. A longitudinal United States population-based study on SLE revealed a 24.4% overall reduction in the age-standardized mortality rate over a 46-year period [13], which may be attributable to advances in treatment and earlier diagnosis. Mortality among SLE patients remains elevated 2- to 3-fold above that of the general population. This excess risk disproportionately affects specific demographic groups, including women, ethnic minorities, residents of southern or western regions, and patients aged 65 years or older. The clinical and public health impact of SLE is significant. In China, the disease affects an estimated 20–70 per one hundred thousand people, with a prevalence of over one million cases that continues to rise each year [14]. On a global scale, this is reflected in a persistently elevated standardized mortality ratio, which varies from 2.4 to 5.9 across different regions, primarily influenced by the availability and quality of local healthcare systems [15]. Previously, the five-year survival rate for patients with SLE was approximately 50%; however, current rates have significantly improved to 91% and 89% for 5- and 8-year survival, respectively [16]. The leading causes of death in SLE remain lupus nephritis, cardiovascular disease, and infection-related complications [13, 17].

Limitations of conventional therapies

Despite the established role of glucocorticoids (GC) and immunosuppressants in enhancing long-term outcomes for SLE, a substantial clinical challenge remains in managing patients who are refractory to or cannot tolerate these conventional regimens [18]. In recent years, a variety of biological agents targeting different pathways have been evaluated in SLE patients who show inadequate response to conventional regimens. These include monoclonal antibodies targeting B or T cells, cytokine antibodies or inhibitors, and intracellular signaling inhibitors [19–21]. Among these, only a limited number of biologics, including anifrolumab (Saphnelo), belimumab, telitacicept, and low-dose IL-2, have gained regulatory approval of SLE. However, most candidate biologics have failed to meet primary efficacy endpoints in phase II/III randomized controlled trials (RCTs), often accompanied by an increased risk of serious infections compared with placebo or standard care [22, 23]. Theoretical basis for MSCs therapy

In the 1970s, Friedenstein et al. first described a plastic-adherent, fibroblast-like population in bone marrow that was distinct from hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) and later named “mesenchymal stem cells” (MSCs) [31]. Clinical-grade MSCs are routinely obtained from bone marrow, adipose tissue, umbilical cord, placenta and dental pulp; isolation from skeletal muscle, lung or liver remains experimental [32]. MSCs exhibit properties such as hematopoiesis-supporting capacity, high proliferative potential, low immunogenicity, multilineage differentiation ability, and immunomodulatory functions, rendering them attractive candidates for cell-based therapeutics across autoimmune, ischemic, and degenerative diseases [33]. Currently, MSC-based therapy has been applied in the treatment of refractory diseases such as acute and chronic graft-versus-host disease (GvHD) [34], ischemic encephalopathy [35], ischemic heart disease [36], and end-stage liver disease [37], and has also achieved breakthroughs in the management of autoimmune diseases (AID) like SLE [38]. MSCs possess several advantageous properties for the treatment of autoimmune diseases [39]. Firstly, their isolation and expansion are relatively feasible, facilitating clinical-scale production [40]. Secondly, MSCs possess migratory properties that enable them to home to sites of tissue injury and contribute to regeneration [41, 42]. A key immunological advantage is their low immunogenicity, attributed to the lack of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II molecules and costimulatory signals such as CD80, CD86, and CD40. This phenotype allows MSCs to evade T cell-mediated immune recognition, reducing the risk of rejection in allogeneic settings [43, 44].

MSCs suppress the proliferation and cytokine secretion of CD3+ T cells [45, 46], inhibit the proliferation and autoantibody production of CD19+ B cells [47], prevent the maturation of dendritic cells (DCs), and mitigate the cytotoxicity of CD56+ natural killer (NK) cells [48, 49]. MSCs exhibit heterogeneous proliferative and differentiation capacities, which are highly dependent on their tissue of origin. For example, umbilical cord-derived MSCs (UC-MSCs) demonstrate better proliferative potential and a broader multilineage differentiation capability in comparison with bone marrow-derived MSCs (BM-MSCs) [50].

In the context of severe, refractory SLE, other cellular therapies have been explored. In 1996, Marmont et al. reported the first documented successful application of autologous HSC transplantation in a patient with refractory disease [51]. Since then, over 300 cases of HSC transplantation for refractory SLE have been documented globally, with variable outcomes [52]. Current guidelines from European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology and the European Society for Blood and Marrow Transplantation limit HSC transplantation to selected cases of life-threatening, refractory SLE following failure of all approved biologics, or when indicated by concurrent hematologic malignancy [53, 54]. Nevertheless, broader adoption of HSC transplantation has been constrained by high relapse rates, non-negligible transplant-related mortality, procedure-related morbidity, and substantial economic burden.

Heterogeneity and standardization of MSCs

A pivotal barrier to the widespread clinical translation of MSC therapy is the inherent heterogeneity of MSC products, which significantly influences therapeutic potency and consistency.While MSCs from various sources share core immunomodulatory properties, significant heterogeneity exists, posing a major challenge for clinical translation and reproducibility. This heterogeneity is influenced by multiple factors, including tissue origin, donor characteristics, isolation methods, and culture conditions. For instance, UC-MSCs generally exhibit higher proliferative capacity and greater immunomodulatory potency than BM-MSCs, while adipose-derived MSCs (AD-MSCs) show distinct secretory profiles. Donor age and health status also critically impact MSC function; BM-MSCs from SLE patients display intrinsic functional impairments, such as reduced proliferation, premature senescence, and diminished immunosuppressive capacity, thereby supporting the rationale for using allogeneic, healthy donor-derived MSCs. UC-MSCs generally demonstrate superior proliferative capacity and immunomodulatory potential compared to BM-MSCs, partly due to their younger biological age and lower exposure to environmental stressors. However, variations in donor genetics, age, and health status further contribute to batch-to-batch variability.

Beyond source and donor, culture conditions, including passage number, oxygen tension, and the use of xenogeneic serum, profoundly influence MSC phenotype and potency. Extended culture can lead to replicative senescence, altered surface marker expression, and reduced immunosuppressive function. To ensure consistent therapeutic outcomes, the field is moving toward stricter standardization. The 2025 ISCT guidelines emphasize not only identity markers but also functional potency assays, such as the ability to suppress T-cell proliferation or secrete specific anti-inflammatory factors like prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) [55] or indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) [56]. Comprehensive characterization of the cell product’s secretory profile and detailed reporting of all manufacturing parameters are now considered essential for advancing reliable MSC-based therapies.

MSCs must comply with the International Society for Cellular Therapy minimal criteria (2019), the 2022 position statement, and the 2025 draft standards, which explicitly define MSCs as “mesenchymal stromal cells” rather than “mesenchymal stem cells”, unless evidence of stemness is provided [57–60]. In contrast, the previous criteria (2006) did not clearly differentiate between these two cell types, leading to longstanding confusion within the field regarding the identity of MSCs. Trilineage differentiation and plastic-adherence are no longer mandatory; however, functional assays (immunomodulation, angiocrine activity) are strongly recommended. The new standards posit that MSCs are fundamentally stromal cells, lacking the self-renewal and differentiation potential characteristic of stem cells, and that their functions are primarily mediated through cytokine secretion and modulation of the microenvironment. Function-associated genes and markers have been highlighted: the new criteria introduce five MSC-specific function-associated genes implicated in stromal activities such as vascular remodeling and cell-cell junctions, validation of which requires technologies such as single-cell transcriptomic analysis. Furthermore, detection of surface markers must adhere to rigorously defined thresholds in flow cytometry—for instance, the positivity rates for CD73, CD90, and CD105 should be ≥ 95%, while negativity for CD45 and CD34 must be ≥ 98%. Tissue origin (adipose, umbilical cord, bone marrow, etc.), donor age, sex, and processing protocol (enzyme, explant, perfusion) must be explicitly documented. Furthermore, a comprehensive characterization of MSC functional potency is now required, including rigorous evaluation of their immunomodulatory capacity and detailed profiling of their secretory profile (e.g., cytokines, chemokines, and extracellular vesicles).

Current ISCT minimal criteria focus on surface markers and differentiation potential, which do not necessarily correlate with clinical efficacy. There is an urgent need for standardized potency assays that predict in vivo immunomodulatory function (e.g., inhibition of T-cell proliferation, IDO activity, or specific cytokine secretion profiles) prior to release. Harmonizing manufacturing processes and establishing international reference standards are essential steps to ensure the reproducibility and safety of MSC-based therapies for SLE.

Molecular mechanisms of MSCs in SLE

While Ikehara et al. [70, 71] previously proposed that SLE originates from hematopoietic stem cell defects, emerging evidence indicates that mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC) dysfunction also contributes to disease pathogenesis. Studies have shown that MSCs from both lupus-prone mouse models and SLE patients display impaired proliferative and differentiation capabilities. These cells also show significantly diminished secretion of key cytokines, including transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), Interleukin-6 (IL-6), and IL-7, accompanied by a compromised ability to inhibit the proliferation of T and B lymphocytes as well as the differentiation of plasma cells [72–74]. Compared with BM-MSCs from healthy individuals, those from patients with SLE exhibit dysregulated expression of genes controlling the actin cytoskeleton, cyclins, bone morphogenetic protein / TGF-β signaling, and mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling pathways [75]. While BM-MSCs isolated and expanded from lupus-prone mice failed to ameliorate disease progression in recipient diseased mice, infusion of MSCs derived from healthy mice significantly prolonged survival, reduced proteinuria, and improved renal pathology in the recipients [76]. Clinical evidence suggests that treatment with autologous MSCs is ineffective in patients with SLE [77]. Therefore, the observed functional impairments of autologous MSCs in SLE rationalize the therapeutic strategy of using allogeneic MSCs from healthy donors to restore immunoregulatory homeostasis.

The therapeutic mechanisms underlying allogeneic MSC transplantation in SLE are currently thought to encompass the following aspects: dose-dependent suppression of lupus T cell G₁/S phase transition and proliferation [78]; indirect modulation of T cell function by promoting Tregs and inhibiting T helper (Th) 17 cells differentiation, phagocytosis of apoptotic cells by MSCs leading to immune suppression [79]; promotion of proliferation and inhibition of apoptosis of tolerogenic CD1c⁺ DCs, as observed in the peripheral blood of SLE patients [80]; suppression of B cell activation through matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-mediated cleavage and modification of autocrine C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2) [81]; enhancement of CD206 expression and phagocytic function in lupus macrophages via IL-6 secretion [82]; and increased homing of MSCs to inflammatory or damaged sites, followed by targeted differentiation and tissue repair [83].

The immunomodulatory (rather than purely immunosuppressive) potency of MSCs is a key determinant of clinical efficacy [84]. MSCs are capable of expressing PGE2 [55], TGF-β [85], nitric oxide (NO) [86], CCL2 [81], IDO [56], IL-10 [85, 87] and programmed cell death 1 ligands (PD-L1/L2) [88]. Regulation of B cell responses

In patients with SLE, aberrantly activated B cells contribute to disease mainly by producing autoantibodies targeting nuclear components including DNA (anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies [anti-dsDNA]), antinuclear antibody [ANA]), RNP particles (anti-Ro, anti-La, and anti-Sm), histones, and nonhistone chromatin proteins [89]. MSCs have been shown to suppress B cell differentiation into plasma cells and subsequent antibody production through mechanisms involving soluble mediators and direct cell contact, particularly via the PD-1/PD-L1 axis [90, 91]. Meanwhile, regulatory B cells (Bregs) contribute to immunosuppression in SLE, at least in part, by producing IL-10 and TGF-β [92, 93]. Experimental studies further indicate that MSCs can promote the expansion of Bregs and attenuate exaggerated inflammatory responses in lupus-prone mouse models [87]. Nevertheless, it remains to be elucidated whether MSCs modulate the expression of B cell costimulatory molecules and influence their cytokine secretion profiles.

Modulation of T cell subsets

Aberrant T cell activation, Th1/Th2 imbalance, and dysregulation of other T cell subsets represent key pathogenic mechanisms in SLE. Patients with SLE exhibit a significant increase in serum IL-17 levels relative to healthy individuals, which correlates positively with the SLE Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) scores [78]. A key mechanism driving disease progression in SLE involves follicular helper T (Tfh) cells. These cells play a pivotal role in enabling B cells to produce autoantibodies and form pathogenic immune complexes, which subsequently mediate tissue and organ damage, thus perpetuating a cycle of disease exacerbation [94]. By engaging in cell-cell contact and activating inducible nitric oxide synthase, MSCs curtail the differentiation of naïve CD4⁺ T cells into Tfh cells. This suppression leads to reduced IL-21 production, which in turn ameliorates lupus nephritis and enhances survival rates in lupus-prone mice [86, 95]. Similarly, an imbalance in Th1/Th2 subsets—with a bias toward Th1—is observed in SLE patients, accompanied by the concomitant release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [96]. Research has shown that MSCs modulate immune responses by suppressing pro-inflammatory Th17 cell activity while enhancing Treg differentiation. This dual regulatory function is primarily mediated through the secretion of immunomodulatory cytokines, including TGF-β and IL-10, which inhibit Th17 cell proliferation and attenuate the production of inflammatory mediators such as IL-17 [97]. The downregulation of IL-17 establishes a negative feedback loop that further restricts Th17 cell activation and clonal expansion [98]. Conversely, experimental evidence reveals significantly diminished expression levels of Th17 cells and associated pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-17 and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α). In vitro co-culture experiments with peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) derived from SLE patients demonstrated that hUC-MSCs significantly upregulate both TGF-β expression and the frequency of Tregs in a concentration-dependent manner. This dose-related immunomodulatory effect suggests a direct correlation between hUC-MSC dosage and their capacity to enhance immune tolerance through TGF-β-mediated Treg expansion in SLE pathophysiology models [78]. In animal studies, researchers have also combined MSCs with five clinical drugs used for SLE—namely, prednisone, dexamethasone, cyclosporine A, mycophenolate mofetil, and rapamycin. This combination strategy enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs, resulting in improved Treg function and reduced drug-induced cytotoxicity [99].

Reprogramming of macrophage phenotype

In SLE, macrophages display impaired phagocytic function and aberrant activation patterns, which contribute to persistent inflammation and disease progression [100]. In vitro co-culture studies with murine lupus models demonstrate that MSCs modulate macrophage polarization by upregulating anti-inflammatory mediators (e.g., IL-10, TGF-β) and downregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6) in a context-dependent manner [101]. This dual regulation not only enhances macrophage phagocytic capacity but also directly correlates with reduced disease activity in experimental lupus models [82, 101]. Beyond their role in autoimmune conditions, MSCs have been shown to facilitate repair of the damaged bone marrow microenvironment in diseases such as leukemia by reprogramming host macrophages toward a pro-regenerative phenotype [102].

Induction of tolerogenic dendritic cells

DCs serve as central regulators of adaptive immunity by orchestrating T and B cell activation [103, 104]. Human DC populations are classified into two distinct subsets: myeloid DCs (mDCs), which specialize in antigen presentation, and plasmacytoid DCs (pDCs), primarily responsible for type I interferon (IFN) production [105]. In SLE, pDCs emerge as the predominant source of IFNs that drive pathogenic T and B cell responses [106]. Concurrently, mDCs exhibit aberrant upregulation of costimulatory molecules, accelerating T cell maturation toward pro-inflammatory subsets and inducing tissue damage in vitro [103]. MSCs modulate DC function through multiple mechanisms. They inhibit DC maturation and suppress surface expression of key antigen-presenting molecules such as HLA-DR, along with costimulatory receptors CD80 and CD86 [107]. This regulatory effect extends to the induction of regulatory DCs (DCregs), which demonstrate enhanced phagocytic activity and immunosuppressive capabilities through T cell activation inhibition [108, 109]. Additionally, MSCs reprogram DC cytokine profiles by reducing TNF-α secretion while promoting IL-10 production [110]. However, the precise molecular pathways underlying these effects in SLE remain incompletely elucidated.

Clinical evidence highlights the importance of DC dysfunction in SLE pathogenesis. Analysis of SLE patients revealed a marked reduction in peripheral blood tolerogenic CD1c⁺ DC frequencies, accompanied by diminished circulating FLT3L levels [80]. Following transplantation of UC-MSCs reversed this deficit by elevating FLT3L concentrations, which in turn stimulated CD1c⁺ DC proliferation and suppressed apoptosis, ultimately ameliorating disease manifestations [80]. While MSCs have demonstrated therapeutic potential in restoring function in inflammatory conditions like Sjögren’s syndrome (SS) [55, 111]; their specific role in SLE requires further investigation.

Clinical efficacy endpoints​

Following MSC infusion therapy, a comprehensive evaluation of therapeutic efficacy is essential. This includes regular monitoring of functional parameters in affected organs and serological immunologic indicators—such as autoantibody titers, complement levels, and Treg frequencies. Given the multisystemic nature of SLE, the use of standardized assessment tools (e.g. SLEDAI, BILAG [British Isles Lupus Assessment Group], Physician Global Assessment) is recommended for holistic disease evaluation.

Patients presenting with hematologic, renal, or hepatic manifestations require systematic monitoring encompassing comprehensive hematologic parameters (complete blood cell count), hepatorenal function assessment (liver enzyme levels [alanine aminotransferase / aminotransferase], serum creatinine, and estimated glomerular filtration rate), quantitative analysis of 24-hour urinary protein excretion, and targeted organ ultrasonography to evaluate organ-specific pathology. Repeat renal biopsy may be considered between 6 months and 1 year post-treatment. In cases of cardiac or pulmonary involvement (e.g., pulmonary hypertension, interstitial lung disease, diffuse alveolar hemorrhage), follow-up echocardiography and chest CT are advised at 6 months. For those with central nervous system involvement, reevaluation via cerebrospinal fluid analysis and cranial MRI is recommended after MSC infusion. The time to improvement in organ function and clinical indicators varies after MSC therapy. Hematologic abnormalities such as cytopenia may respond within one week, whereas reduction in proteinuria and improvement in renal function typically require 1 to 3 months. If the response to MSC treatment is unsatisfactory in patients with SLE, alternative therapeutic strategies should be considered. For maintenance therapy following MSCs infusion, low-dose corticosteroids combined with tapering immunosuppressants are generally recommended.

Allogeneic MSC therapy achieves a response rate of 60% in severe refractory SLE, reducing the five-year mortality rate from 35% to 45% to 16% [112], with no serious transplantation-related adverse events reported to date. MSCs are widely accessible stem cells possessing multipotent differentiation potential and immunomodulatory capabilities. MSC-based treatment significantly enhances clinical efficacy, improves health-related quality of life metrics, and prolongs disease-free survival periods in SLE patients. The therapeutic mechanisms involve modulating the functions of many immune cells thereby restoring immune balance, inducing immune tolerance, facilitating tissue repair, and ultimately leading to functional improvement in affected organs and amelioration of disease activity.

Dosing and administration

The dosage and frequency of MSC infusions for GvHD vary considerably across studies. Typical doses range from (1–2)×10⁶ cells/kg [with reported variations of (0.3–9)×10⁶ cells/kg] [113], administered in single or multiple infusions (ranging from 1 to 21 sessions) [114]. Although meta-analyses indicate no statistically significant difference in efficacy among varying doses of MSCs [115], overall survival rates correlate with the minimum infused MSC dose across clinical studies [116]. Likewise, no meaningful variations were detected between single and multiple (e.g., three) MSC infusions in improving proteinuria, renal pathology, or immunological parameters in lupus-prone mice [117]. In clinical studies involving SLE patients receiving either one or two MSC infusions (1 × 10⁶ cells/kg per infusion) with a mean follow-up duration of 26 months, no statistically significant differences were observed in survival outcomes, clinical response rates, or relapse rates between the two groups. Furthermore, improvements in SLEDAI scores, serum albumin, complement C3, and anti-dsDNA antibody levels did not differ significantly [118]. Therefore, a single infusion of allogeneic MSCs is sufficient in SLE, with a recommended dose of (1–2)×10⁶ cells/kg per infusion.

Summary of clinical outcomes

Allogeneic MSC therapy has also demonstrated efficacy in other autoimmune diseases (e.g. SS, RA, and polymyositis/dermatomyositis). Therefore, MSC-based treatment represents a promising therapeutic strategy for SLE complicated by other autoimmune conditions. Furthermore, MSCs possess antimicrobial and antiviral properties, and suppress infection-triggered inflammatory responses by expanding the population of C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 3⁺ (CXCR3⁺) Tregs in the lungs of animal models [119–121]. Infusion of MSCs has exhibited a positive safety profile in patients with sepsis [122]. Moreover, MSC-derived treatments have been confirmed as both safe and effective in severe pneumonia induced by COVID-19, mitigating lung injury and preventing pulmonary fibrosis through suppression of cytokine storm and promotion of endogenous repair mechanisms [123, 124]. Therefore, active infection with viral, bacterial, or fungal pathogens in patients with SLE is not an absolute contraindication for allogene MSC therapy. Treatment with MSCs can be considered alongside active infection control measures.

Currently, there is a lack of prospective RCTs on MSC-based therapy for SLE. However, several clinical studies on MSC treatment for SLE have been registered on ClinicalTrials.gov. The outcomes of these RCTs are highly anticipated to further confirm the efficacy of allogeneic MSCs in SLE treatment. Safety profile

Based on aggregated clinical data from reported studies on MSC therapy for SLE and other diseases, no serious adverse events have been observed in clinical applications to date. Safety evaluations encompass both short-term and long-term assessments. The most frequently reported adverse reactions are infusion-related; a small number of patients may experience transient and mild symptoms, which are generally self-limiting and require no specific intervention. In cases where symptoms are severe, MSC infusion should be promptly discontinued, and appropriate diagnostic investigations should be conducted to identify the cause and provide symptomatic management. Any occurrence of serious adverse events must be reported to the relevant hospital regulatory authorities in accordance with established protocols.

MSCs exhibit low immunogenicity due to reduced expression of MHC class I molecules and the complete absence of MHC class II molecules. Therefore, human leukocyte antigen (HLA) matching is not required prior to clinical application. Pediatric and elderly patients are not contraindicated for MSC therapy; however, clinicians should carefully assess disease severity and cardiopulmonary function, and discuss the potential benefits and risks of MSC treatment with patients and their families to make an informed decision. MSC therapy is not recommended for patients with severe pulmonary hypertension (> 70 mmHg). It is also not advised for patients with mild to moderate pulmonary hypertension accompanied by severe cardiopulmonary dysfunction. Owing to the lack of clinical data on MSC use in pregnant and lactating patients, MSC therapy is currently not recommended for SLE patients during pregnancy or breastfeeding. MSCs exhibit antitumor effects in breast cancer, osteosarcoma, and ovarian cancer, yet some research highlights their potential to enhance tumor angiogenesis and metastasis [140]. Thus, MSC-based therapy is not currently recommended for cancer patients.

In terms of safety, MSC treatment was well tolerated. A small number of patients experienced transient infusion-related reactions such as fever, sweating, palpitations, and facial flushing. No serious adverse events or transplantation-related mortality were observed. A safety analysis of 404 SLE patients with an average follow-up of 43 ± 26 months demonstrated no significant increase in infection rates or tumorigenic events [141]. In SLE patients treated with allogeneic MSCs, no treatment-related adverse events were observed during a follow-up period of six years [142]. Nevertheless, longer-term follow-up studies are required to obtain further clinical safety evidence.

The short-to-medium-term safety of allogeneic MSC therapy is well-established, with no significant increase in infection rates or transplant-related mortality reported in follow-up studies spanning up to six years. However, a critical gap remains in our understanding of the very long-term safety profile, particularly regarding the theoretical risks of malignant transformation and tumor promotion. While MSCs are generally considered non-tumorigenic, their long-term persistence in the host, albeit at low levels, and their capacity to secrete growth factors and immunomodulatory molecules raise a theoretical concern that they could inadvertently support the growth of existing, undiagnosed malignancies or, in extremely rare cases, undergo malignant transformation themselves. More nuanced is the potential for MSCs to promote tumor growth or metastasis in patients with pre-existing malignancies via immunosuppression or angiogenesis support. Given the conflicting evidence, MSC therapy is currently contraindicated in patients with active malignancy. Rigorous long-term registries are essential to definitively rule out late-onset oncogenic events. Preclinical studies have yielded conflicting results; some suggest MSCs can inhibit tumor growth, while others indicate they may enhance tumor angiogenesis and metastasis. Given that SLE patients already have an elevated risk of certain cancers, rigorous long-term surveillance for malignancy is paramount. Current data, with a mean follow-up of 43 ± 26 months, have not shown an increased incidence of cancer [141]. Nevertheless, the field requires dedicated, prospective registries with extended follow-up (10–20 years) to definitively rule out these theoretical long-term risks, especially in patients who may receive multiple infusions or have a history of heavy immunosuppression. Although MSCs are considered hypoimmunogenic, repeated administrations could theoretically trigger alloimmune responses. Long-term monitoring for the development of SLE is recommended.

Comparison of therapeutic efficacy of MSCs from different sources

According to clinical reports, allogeneic MSC therapy resulted in complete or partial remission in 60.5% of SLE patients after one year, accompanied by significant reductions in 24-hour urinary protein and BILAG scores [143]. After four years, 50% of patients maintained clinical remission, with a 4-year survival rate of 94% and a total relapse rate of only 23% [141]. Dysfunction of MSCs contributes to the pathogenesis and progression of SLE. Bone marrow-derived MSCs from SLE patients exhibit impaired proliferative and differentiation capacity in vitro [72], along with slower growth kinetics, premature senescence, cytoskeletal abnormalities, and aberrant expression of multiple genes and proteins [73–75]. These findings indicate that autologous bone marrow MSCs from SLE patients harbor structural and functional impairments, rendering them unsuitable for autologous MSC transplantation therapy. Thus, the use of healthy allogeneic MSCs is recommended for the treatment of SLE.

Allogeneic MSCs transplantation has been demonstrated to be safe and effective for patients with refractory SLE [112, 133, 141, 144, 145]. It exhibits significant therapeutic efficacy against SLE-related complications, particularly LN, hematologic involvement, skin ulcers, and diffuse alveolar hemorrhage [146, 147]. Therefore, allogeneic MSC therapy is indicated for patients with moderate to severe SLE, especially those with active disease affecting organs such as the kidneys, blood system, lungs, and skin.

Currently, MSCs derived from bone marrow, umbilical cord, placental, and adipose tissues have been utilized in clinical therapies [148]. However, in clinical studies for the treatment of SLE, umbilical cord-derived or allogeneic bone marrow-derived MSCs are predominantly employed. Long-term efficacy is comparable between bone marrow-derived and umbilical cord-derived MSCs for the treatment of refractory and severe SLE patients [141]. To date, there have been no clinical reports on the use of adipose tissue or other tissue-derived MSCs for SLE patients. Due to sufficient supply, convenient procurement, ease of large-scale preparation, and absence of ethical concerns, umbilical cord-derived MSCs are currently recommended as the preferred cell source for clinical treatment.

Synergistic effects with conventional therapies

Stem cell transplantation, whether autologous (from self), haploidentical (HLA-partially-matched, often from a sibling), or allogeneic (donor, HLA-matched, related or unrelated), offers the possibility of treatment-free remission. These regimens initially involve conditioning with cyclophosphamide, and various protocols have been reported. Cyclophosphamide alone has long been established to benefit severe, life-threatening, and organ-threatening SLE [149, 150].

Traynor and colleagues reported myeloablative autologous stem cell transplantation in seven patients, resulting in remission of serological abnormalities and disease activity [151]. A European study [152] described 53 cases of autologous peripheral stem cell transplantation following high-dose cyclophosphamide; 66% of patients achieved remission at 6 months, although one-third subsequently relapsed, and 12 patients died in the perioperative period. A non-myeloablative autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation regimen using lower doses of cyclophosphamide was evaluated in 48 patients, showing a 5-year survival rate of 84% and a 5-year disease-free survival probability of 50%, with two reported deaths [152]. Mesenchymal stromal cell therapy has also been attempted, both with and without cyclophosphamide, demonstrating improvements in disease activity and serological abnormalities [144, 153]. Despite these promising results, these therapies have not yet been evaluated in randomized controlled trials for SLE, and significant heterogeneity exists among all reported protocols [54]. It is recommended that patients receive aspirin or clopidogrel bisulfate (75–100 mg/day) starting one day prior to allogeneic MSC infusion and continue for two weeks (with exceptions for long-term prophylactic users; use with caution in patients with bleeding tendencies) to prevent potential thrombotic events. Although glucocorticoids can inhibit the secretion of certain cytokines and homing-related molecules by MSCs, their impact on the expression of immunoregulatory factors is not significant. Other immunosuppressants such as hydroxychloroquine, cyclosporine A, mycophenolate mofetil, and tacrolimus have minimal effects on MSC function [154]. Therefore, corticosteroids and other immunosuppressive agents need not be discontinued after MSC infusion; their doses should be adjusted based on the patient’s clinical status. Cyclophosphamide, a classical cytotoxic agent, is not recommended within 48 h after MSC infusion. If continued use is deemed necessary based on clinical evaluation, cyclophosphamide may be administered 48 h or more after MSC infusion.

Frontiers and future perspectives

Engineered mesenchymal stromal cells

Genetically engineered MSCs

Xu et al. [128] genetically modified MSCs to overexpress IL-37 and evaluated their immunomodulatory effects in vitro. The therapeutic potential of these cells was further assessed following transplantation into MRL/lpr mice—a well-established model of SLE. MSCs engineered to overexpress IL-37 retained their fundamental stem cell characteristics. In vitro, these modified cells exhibited enhanced immunosuppressive capabilities, including inhibition of splenocyte proliferation, reduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-17, and IL-6), and suppression of autoantibody production (anti-dsDNA and anti-ANA). Compared to animals treated with control MSCs or IL-37 alone, MRL/lpr mice transplanted with IL-37-overexpressing MSCs demonstrated improved survival rates and ameliorated SLE manifestations, as indicated by reduced urinary protein levels, spleen weight, and renal pathology scores. These mice also showed significantly lower expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, decreased total antibody levels in serum and urine, reduced autoantibody production, and diminished T-cell infiltration in both serum and kidney tissues. The sustained elevation of serum IL-37 levels and prolonged survival of MSCs post-transplantation may be attributed to the suppression of the inflammatory microenvironment by IL-37.

MSC-derived extracellular vesicles (MSC-EVs)

It is noteworthy that intravenous infusion of MSCs is complex and may be associated with serious adverse effects, such as vascular occlusion and tumor formation. Due to these safety concerns surrounding MSC transplantation, many researchers have shifted their focus to MSC-EVs. Compared with MSCs, MSC-EVs have demonstrated similar immunosuppressive functions in multiple in vitro studies as a cell-free therapeutic alternative and have shown a favorable safety profile [155, 156]. However, the absence of standardized and efficient extraction methods for MSC-EVs remains a major challenge, leading to low yield and high heterogeneity [157]. Studies indicate that a significant portion of the immunomodulatory effects of MSCs can be attributed to their secreted EVs, which are increasingly recognized as key mediators of MSC therapy [158, 159]. As a novel cell-free approach, EVs can deliver specific molecules to target tissues or organs and exhibit immunomodulatory capacities nearly equivalent to those of MSCs [159, 160]. EVs obtained from genetically modified MSCs have been widely explored in vitro for the treatment of various diseases [158–161]. For instance, exosomes derived from miR-122-modified MSCs can enhance tumor cell sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents and improve drug efficacy in cancer treatment [158]. In a rat model of spinal cord injury, exosomes from miR-126-overexpressing MSCs promoted tissue repair and axonal regeneration [159]. Furthermore, in a rat model of acute myocardial infarction, exosomes from Akt-modified MSCs reduced cardiomyocyte apoptosis, enhanced cardiac regeneration, and improved heart function [160]. In a mouse model of RA, exosomes derived from miR-150-5p-modified MSCs inhibited synovial cell proliferation and mitigated joint destruction, suggesting a potential therapeutic strategy for RA [161]. Nevertheless, studies on MSC-EVs remain limited in mouse models and SLE patients.

Niu et al. developed a novel combinatorial engineering strategy for MSC-derived exosomes (MSC-exos) that integrates aggregate culture and genetic editing, significantly enhancing both exosome yield and treatment specificity for SLE [162]. Initially, MSCs were subjected to an aggregate culture strategy (“Agg-exo”), resulting in high yields of engineered exosomes capable of targeted delivery to immune organs. Mechanistically, these engineered Agg-exos were found to promote Treg differentiation through the Foxp1/STAT5/Foxp3 signaling axis [163–165]. Proteomic analysis revealed that Agg-exos were enriched with the transcription factor Foxp1, a member of the forkhead family essential for maintaining optimal Foxp3 expression in Tregs [166]. Upon uptake by CD4⁺ T cells, Agg-exos enhanced the phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of STAT5, a critical step for Foxp3 induction. Concurrently, Foxp1 exhibited nuclear co-localization with Foxp3, suggesting a direct cooperative interaction. Functional validation using Foxp1-silenced MSCs confirmed the pivotal role of this axis: siFoxp1 Agg-exos failed to upregulate p-STAT5 expression, reduced nuclear co-localization of STAT5 and Foxp3, and significantly diminished their capacity to induce Treg differentiation compared to control Agg-exos. Based on these mechanistic insights, the authors employed a dual-engineering strategy combining gene editing and aggregation culture to construct Foxp1high Agg-exos with enhanced immunomodulatory capacity. When systemically administered to MRL/lpr mice [167], Foxp1high Agg-exos demonstrated superior therapeutic efficacy compared to control Agg-exos. Mice treated with Foxp1high Agg-exos exhibited significantly reduced spleen weight, as well as markedly decreased serum levels of ANA, blood urea nitrogen, and dsDNA antibodies. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed substantially diminished IgG deposition in the kidneys and skin, while renal pathology staining confirmed greater histological improvement. Flow cytometry further demonstrated that Foxp1high Agg-exos had a stronger capacity to promote Treg differentiation compared to Agg-exos, with no notable changes in Th1, Th2, or Th17 cell proportions. In summary, this dual engineering approach successfully addresses key limitations of conventional MSC-exos—namely low production efficiency and nonspecific organ distribution—by enabling the high-yield generation of Foxp1high Agg-exos. This innovative strategy, which combines mechanistic understanding with advanced engineering, holds considerable potential for advancing exosome-based therapies in autoimmune diseases.

Cell-combination and drug-delivery strategies

Ma et al. [168] proposed a promising strategy based on dexamethasone liposome-integrated MSCs (Dexlip–MSCs) for treating SLE through multiple immunomodulatory pathways. This therapeutic approach extended the circulation time of dexamethasone liposomes in vivo, inhibited CD4⁺ T cell proliferation, and suppressed the release of pro-inflammatory mediators (IFN-γ and TNF-α) from CD4⁺ T cells. Furthermore, Dexlip–MSCs initiated cellular reprogramming by activating the glucocorticoid receptor (GR) signaling pathway, upregulating the expression of anti-inflammatory factors such as cysteine-rich secretory protein LCCL domain-containing 2, and downregulating pro-inflammatory factors. Additionally, Dexlip–MSCs synergistically enhanced the anti-inflammatory suppression of CD4⁺ T cells via the release of dexamethasone liposomes or dexamethasone-integrated MSC-derived exosomes (Dex–MSC–EXOs). Based on these synergistic biological effects, the authors demonstrated that Dexlip–MSCs more effectively alleviated disease progression in MRL/lpr mice compared to Dexlip or MSCs alone. These findings highlight this stem cell-based delivery strategy as a promising therapeutic approach for the clinical treatment of SLE.

MSC therapy occupies a distinct niche. Unlike HSC transplantation and CAR-T, MSCs are primarily immunomodulatory rather than immunosuppressive or ablative. They do not require HLA matching, have a favorable safety profile with no cytokine release syndrome (CRS) or immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), and can be derived from readily available allogeneic sources (e.g., umbilical cord), enabling scalable “off-the-shelf” products. While clinical responses are generally less rapid and profound than CAR-T, MSC therapy offers a more tolerable, safer, and potentially more accessible option for a broader spectrum of patients with moderate-to-severe, refractory SLE, either as a stand-alone therapy or as a bridging strategy to more intensive interventions. Future therapeutic algorithms may involve sequencing or combining these approaches to optimize efficacy while minimizing toxicity.

Comparison with other cell therapies

The application of CAR-T in SLE

Recent studies have demonstrated that chimeric antigen receptor T cell (CAR-T) therapy shows promising efficacy in the treatment of B cell malignancies and may represent a significant breakthroughs for non-malignant diseases such as SLE [169]. The integration of CAR-T therapy into the SLE treatment paradigm has opened new perspectives for autoimmune research and clinical practice. The current B cell targeted therapy strategies for SLE, such as rituximab and belimumab, have shown some efficacy; however, a subset of patients exhibits poor or incomplete responses. In contrast, CAR-T therapy offers more rapid and sustained B cell depletion, presenting a promising alternative for refractory SLE [170]. CD19 is expressed throughout all stages of B cell differentiation and is considered a valid target for CAR-T cell therapy. In SLE, CD19-targeted CAR-T cells have demonstrated potent and durable therapeutic responses [171, 172]. Kansal et al. [173] confirmed that CD19 targeting CAR-T cells exerts therapeutic effects in mouse lupus model by clearing B cells and preventing disease onset. Subsequently, Jin et al. [174] developed mouse anti-CD19 CAR-T cells incorporating either CD28 or 4-1BB co-stimulatory domains. Their findings revealed that allogeneic CD19 CAR-T cell infusion not only prevented disease development when administered prior to symptom onset but also conferred therapeutic benefits in later disease stages.

In addition to CAR-T therapy, CAR-NK cell therapy has also shown potential in treating SLE. Specifically, anti-PD-L1 CAR-NK-92 cells can recognize and eliminate PD-1–high Tfh cells, thereby inhibiting memory B cell proliferation and differentiation, reducing immunoglobulin secretion, and alleviating splenomegaly [175]. Mougiakakos et al. [176] reported the first application of autologous anti-CD19 CAR-T therapy in a patient with refractory SLE. Following treatment, the patient’s abnormal laboratory parameters normalized, and the SLEDAI decreased to 0. In subsequent studies, all five patients with refractory SLE achieved rapid and sustained remission [177]. Zhang et al. [178] described a case of a patient with both SLE and stage IV diffuse large B-cell lymphoma who achieved stable remission following anti-BCMA/CD19 CAR-T therapy. Furthermore, analysis of serum samples from six patients with refractory SLE before and after CAR-T treatment revealed decreased levels of IL-6 and TNF-α, increased levels of IL-7 and BAFF, and a significant reduction in SLE-related autoantibodies [179]. These findings collectively indicate that CAR-T therapy holds significant potential as a therapeutic strategy for SLE.

The landscape of cellular therapies for refractory SLE is rapidly evolving, with distinct approaches offering unique advantages. HSC transplantation aims to “reset” a dysregulated immune system by eliminating autoreactive clones through myeloablative conditioning, followed by reconstitution with autologous HSCs. While capable of inducing long-term remission, its use is limited by high relapse rates, transplant-related mortality (around 5–10% in experienced centers), and significant procedure-related morbidity, reserving it for only the most severe, life-threatening cases. In contrast, CAR-T cell therapy offers a targeted, potent, and rapid approach. By genetically engineering a patient’s own T cells to target and eliminate CD19-positive B cells, CAR-T therapy achieves deep and durable B-cell depletion, leading to profound and often rapid clinical responses, even in patients refractory to multiple lines of therapy. However, its application is currently limited by high cost, complex manufacturing, and significant safety concerns, including severe CRS, ICANS, and prolonged B-cell aplasia requiring immunoglobulin replacement.

Comparative landscape: MSC transplantation vs. HSC transplantation and CAR-T

To position MSC therapy within the broader spectrum of advanced treatments for refractory SLE, it is essential to compare it with HSC transplantation and CAR-T therapy.MSCs primarily exert immunomodulatory and tissue-reparative effects via paracrine signaling, restoring tolerance without ablating the host immune system. In contrast, HSC transplantation involves high-dose chemotherapy to reset the immune system, while CAR-T utilizes engineered T cells to specifically deplete pathogenic B cells. HSC transplantation is reserved for life-threatening cases due to high toxicity and mortality risks. CAR-T shows remarkable efficacy in inducing drug-free remission but carries risks of CRS and prolonged hypogammaglobulinemia. MSCs offer a favorable safety profile with minimal acute toxicity, making them suitable for a broader range of patients, including those unfit for HSC transplantation or CAR-T. MSCs represent a “middle-ground” therapy: safer than HSC transplantation and less aggressively immunodepleting than CAR-T. They are ideal for patients needing tissue repair or those who cannot tolerate intense conditioning. Future strategies may involve combining these modalities or selecting therapies based on specific patient endotypes.

iPSC-derived mesenchymal stromal cells (iPSC-MSCs)

Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) are a type of stem cell generated through genetic reprogramming technology from somatic cells, capable of multidirectional differentiation [180]. iPSCs derived from patients with SLE are considered to harbor a genetic background predisposing to the disease, making them a valuable tool for studying the pathogenesis and distinctive features of SLE [181].

Several research groups have established iPSCs derived from patients with SLE, which have been utilized to analyze SLE-specific characteristics in iPSC-based studies [182–189]. Chen et al. generated iPSCs from renal tubular cells of the distal tubules obtained from four SLE patients [185]. In a subsequent study, Tang et al. compared these SLE patient-derived iPSCs with those from healthy donors using multi-omics analysis [186]. They identified numerous differentially expressed genes, miRNAs, and proteins. Gene Ontology analysis indicated that these differentially expressed genes and proteins were primarily associated with mRNA processing and translation. Notably, they observed upregulation of AK4, a gene involved in nucleotide biosynthesis, in SLE patient-derived iPSCs [186]. The AK4 gene was identified as a target of miR-317a-5p, which was found to be downregulated in SLE patient-derived iPSCs. The authors proposed that such integrated analysis of miRNAs, mRNAs, and proteins could not only provide insight into the pathogenesis of SLE but also lead to the discovery of novel diagnostic biomarkers. However, a potential limitation of this study is the lack of prior reports demonstrating upregulation of AK4 or downregulation of miR-317a-5p in primary cells from SLE patients. It is suggested that examining immune cells differentiated from iPSCs, rather than the stem cells themselves, may offer deeper insights into the pathogenesis of lupus.

De Angelis et al. generated iPSCs from dermal fibroblasts of a patient with central nervous system lupus [187]. Using mRNA analysis, they compared these cells with iPSCs derived from healthy donors and identified differentially expressed genes involved in the Erk and Akt signaling pathways. Furthermore, they detected dysregulated miRNAs associated with oxidative stress [187]. These findings led them to conclude that iPSCs derived from SLE patients represent a valuable tool for probing the underlying molecular mechanisms of the disease.

Park et al. generated cardiomyocytes from iPSCs derived from SLE patients and observed structural and functional differences between these cells and cardiomyocytes from healthy donors [188]. Interestingly, when exposed to serum from patients with active SLE, the iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes exhibited higher rates of apoptosis, proliferation, and fibrosis. These effects were more pronounced in cardiomyocytes derived from SLE patient-specific iPSCs. Furthermore, the addition of anti-Ro antibodies exacerbated the expression of genes associated with fibrosis, hypertrophy, and apoptosis. The authors suggested that these iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes could serve as a valuable model for studying organ damage in SLE.

A recent study established iPSCs from a familial case of SLE, specifically from a sibling of an SLE patient (designated as SLE-sibling-derived iPSCs) [182]. Using a previously described protocol, these iPSCs were differentiated into CD123⁺ pDCs. We hypothesized that these pDCs could serve as a relevant model for SLE and interferonopathies, given their intrinsic capacity to produce type I interferons via cytosolic RNA-sensing mechanisms. Notably, upon exposure to dsRNA, pDCs derived from SLE-sibling-iPSCs exhibited enhanced IFN-α secretion. Whole-exome sequencing of these iPSCs revealed a rare variant in the 2’-5’-oligoadenylate synthetase-like protein (OASL) gene. Using genome editing, we corrected this OASL p.Glu202Gln (202Q) variant to the wild-type p.Glu202Arg (202R), which resulted in reduced IFN-α production. Conversely, introducing the 202Q mutation into iPSCs from healthy donors amplified dsRNA-triggered IFN-α secretion. These findings underscore the role of the OASL variant in potentiating RNA-driven proinflammatory responses, thereby contributing to SLE pathogenesis. Our results strengthen the premise that iPSCs derived from SLE patients provide a powerful platform for elucidating genetic determinants critical to disease development. Thus, iPSC-based models offer a novel and physiologically relevant system for in-depth pathological investigation.

Homing effects of MSCs

Numerous studies have indicated that modifying MSCs to enhance the expression of homing molecules and cell surface receptors—such as CXCR2 [190] and CXCR4 [191] —can improve their therapeutic efficacy. However, whether the immunomodulatory functions of MSCs require migration and homing to damaged tissues remains controversial. It is suggested that the therapeutic benefits of MSCs in local immune responses may be associated with their migratory and homing capacities. Reported evidence shows that overexpression of CCR1, CXCR2, and CXCR4 in MSCs—or in MSCs modified with biomimetic extracellular matrix and polydimethylsiloxane—can promote greater migration to injury sites, enhance the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines, and accelerate tissue healing [192]. Additionally, pretreatment of MSCs with miR-9-5p or TNF-α can also improve their migratory ability, whereas inhibition of miR-9-5p reduces MSC migration [193, 194]. Regarding the systemic immune response modulated by MSCs, numerous studies have observed that after intravenous infusion, the majority of MSCs become trapped in the lungs in mouse models [195–197]. Mechanistically, these lung-sequestered MSCs may release bioactive molecules and EVs into the bloodstream, effectively modulating systemic immune responses [198, 199]. For instance, MSCs lodged in the lungs—particularly those with high expression of the multifunctional anti-inflammatory protein TNF-α-stimulated gene 6 protein have been shown to potently regulate systemic immunity in mouse models of lung injury [198]. Meanwhile, overexpression of CCL2 in MSCs derived from SLE patients enhances their immunomodulatory capacity both in vitro and in vivo, whereas knockdown of CCL2 in normal MSCs attenuates this regulatory function [81]. These effects are also likely associated with bioactive molecules secreted by MSCs. However, the precise molecular mechanisms through which these molecules and EVs mediate systemic immunomodulation require further investigation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, mesenchymal stromal cell transplantation represents a paradigm-shifting therapeutic avenue for SLE, particularly for patients with severe, refractory disease. Its efficacy is underpinned by a multifaceted mechanism of action that includes the restoration of immune balance—through the modulation of T cells, B cells, macrophages, and DCs—and the promotion of tissue repair. Robust clinical data consistently demonstrate that allogeneic MSC infusion can induce sustained clinical remission, significantly improve organ function, and substantially enhance patient survival, all while maintaining a commendable safety record in the short-to-medium term.

Despite these promising advances, the field must address several key challenges to realize the full potential of MSC therapy. The lack of large-scale, prospective randomized controlled trials remains a significant gap, necessitating further validation of its efficacy. Standardization of critical parameters—including cell source selection, optimal dosing regimens, and long-term safety surveillance—is essential for broader clinical adoption. Looking forward, the convergence of cell therapy with precision medicine and bioengineering holds immense promise. Innovations such as genetically engineered MSCs, iPSC-MSCs, and MSC-derived extracellular vesicles are poised to enhance therapeutic precision, efficacy, and scalability. By addressing these challenges and harnessing these innovations, MSC-based therapy is positioned to evolve from an experimental intervention into a cornerstone of personalized medicine for SLE, offering renewed hope for patients.

Literature search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted to identify studies investigating mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) for the treatment of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). The search was performed across the PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Cochrane Library databases, covering the period from database inception to May 2025. To capture the most recent advances while ensuring a comprehensive historical context, we prioritized publications from the last five years (2020–2025), while also including seminal earlier studies that established foundational concepts (e.g., the discovery of MSCs, first reports of HSC transplantation in SLE). The search strategy employed combinations of the following keywords: “Mesenchymal Stromal Cells,” “Mesenchymal Stem Cells,” “MSCs,” “Systemic Lupus Erythematosus,” “SLE,” “Immunomodulation,” “Clinical Trial,” “Efficacy,” “Safety,” “Cell Therapy,” and “Extracellular Vesicles.” Additional relevant studies were identified by searching the ClinicalTrials.gov registry and by manually screening the reference lists of included articles. Studies were included if they were (1) original research articles, clinical trials, or review articles focusing on MSC-based therapies for SLE, and (2) written in English. Exclusion criteria were (1) non-English publications, (2) conference abstracts, editorials, or letters without original data, (3) duplicate publications, and (4) studies focusing exclusively on basic mechanisms without any translational or clinical relevance to SLE. Two authors (S.J.D. and S.Y.W.) independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts, with disagreements resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (Y.N.C.). For clinical studies, the quality and risk of bias were assessed using appropriate tools (e.g., the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized controlled trials, and the Joanna Briggs Institute checklist for case series), ensuring a rigorous evaluation of the available evidence. For preclinical studies, methodological quality was assessed based on randomization, blinding, and control groups. For clinical studies, we evaluated potential biases related to selection, performance, and reporting, acknowledging the current scarcity of large-scale randomized controlled trials as a limitation in the field.

Note on nomenclature

It is noted that the acronym “MSC” has historically referred to both “mesenchymal stem cells” and “mesenchymal stromal cells.” In alignment with the 2025 guidelines issued by the International Society for Cellular Therapy, the term “mesenchymal stromal cells” is adopted throughout this review unless otherwise specified.
