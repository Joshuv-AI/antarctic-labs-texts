Patterns and dynamics of conflict-related sexual violence: an insight from 54 African countries
Authors: Abisola Esther Babatope, Oluyemi Adewole Okunlola, Idowu Peter Adewumi, Damola Olanipekun Ajisafe, Titilayo Yoyinsola Ajenifuja, Temitope Felix Ojomu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConflict-related sexual violence (CRSV) remains a critical public health and human rights issue across Africa, affecting vulnerable populations including women, children, and marginalized groups. This study explores the patterns and dynamics of CRSV across 54 African countries between 2020 and 2024.MethodsSecondary, de-identified data were sourced from the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx). Descriptive statistics were conducted using IBM SPSS v27 to determine the trends in types of sexual violence and perpetrators. Pearson’s chi-square (χ²) and Fisher-Freeman-Halton tests were used to assess associations between variables. Count data panel regression using Stata 15 was applied to examine factors associated with both the frequency and mortality outcomes of CRSV.ResultsRape was the most prevalent form of sexual violence reported across the study period. Militants and national military forces were identified as leading perpetrators. Significant associations were found between types of sexual violence and perpetrator categories (χ²=208.209, p < 0.05), as well as between violence type and victim status (χ²=11.351, p = 0.040). Regression results revealed that perpetrators such as civilians (β = 0.897) and militants (β = 0.610) were more likely to be involved in multi-victim incidents. Sexual violence involving civilians was significantly associated with increased deaths (β = 1.342).ConclusionCRSV in Africa is widespread and patterned by conflict dynamics and perpetrator type. These findings call for the strengthening of survivor-centered policy responses, improved data systems, and legal accountability mechanisms to address CRSV as a barrier to peace and development.Keywords: Sexual violence, Conflict, Africa, Perpetrators

Background of the study

Conflict -related sexual violence (CRSV), a persistent and pervasive global issue, is now acknowledged as a hallmark of modern armed conflict. Rape, sexual slavery, forced prostitution, forced pregnancy, forced abortion, forced sterilization, forced marriage, and any other form of sexual violence of comparable significance committed against people during or after a conflict are all classified as CRSV [1, 2]. According to the United Nations Secretary-General’s Report on CRSV, in the past decade, verified victims and survivors of sexual violence in conflict are not less than 51,000 [3]. The rising identification of men and boys among survivors demonstrates the epidemic’s complexity, even though women and children remain the primary targets [3].

Of the total 3,622 identified cases reported by the UN in 2023, women and girls accounted for 95%, while men and boys accounted for 4%. The Global Burden of Armed Violence Report revealed that one in every five internally displaced or refugee women has experienced sexual violence [4, 5]. Sexual violence has been routinely used in fragile and conflict-affected areas, not only as a byproduct of war, but also as a weapon of war, targeting individuals and communities to cause trauma, instil fear, and undermine social cohesion [6].

In Sub-Saharan Africa, long-running wars, insufficient legal frameworks, patriarchal behaviours, and the rise of armed organizations all contribute to the prevalence of CRSV, which is often unpunished. Tens of thousands of rapes have happened in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), also known as the “rape capital of the world” [7]. According to a poll conducted in the eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo in 2011, more than 39% of women reported being sexually abused, with approximately 74% of these incidents occurring during a conflict [8]. Between 2013 and 2016, the UN recorded 1,300 rape incidents and over 1,600 other cases of sexual assault in South Sudan, the majority of which were committed by government and opposition troops [9].

CRSV is widespread in both scope and barbarity. Victims may face forced marriage, sexual servitude, group rape, or rape in a solitary setting [10]. In addition to intentional genital injuries such as electric currents applied to male genitalia or hot oil poured into women’s vaginas, there have been reports of a variety of horrific insults, including forced sexual encounters with family members or animals [1, 10]. CRSV can occur at military or refugee camps, checkpoints or detention facilities (even during interrogation), the victim’s own home, or agricultural fields [10].

Sexual assault perpetrated by civilians are more common during times of crisis [11]. This phenomenon is caused by two factors: increasing violent conduct linked to psychological stress or near anarchy in communities as a result of war [11]. Reports of sexual exploitation and abuse by UN peacekeepers and humanitarian professionals who were supposed to protect their victims have also emerged, which is concerning [10]. Even with extensive documentation of these well-known occurrences, it is becoming clear that sexual assault in conflict is far more common and diverse than previously thought. Evidence reveals that between 1990 and 2023, nearly three-quarters of African nations experienced conflict scenarios involving sexual violence, demonstrating that CRSV is not limited to a small number of war-torn states [12].

However, the characteristics of the perpetrators, prevalence, and dynamics vary according to the situation. For example, Boko Haram and other non-state actors have exploited forced marriage and kidnapping as tactics of ideological warfare in Nigeria and Mali [13]. In contrast, there have been several accusations of sexual torture and gang rape committed by both national and allied forces in Ethiopia’s Tigray conflict [14]. Anti-sexual violence legislation has been put in place in Africa countries in recent years [15]. Examples include the increased punishments for sexual violence related with conflict in Democratic Republic of the Congo in 2006 Sexual Violence Act and modified in 2022 [15, 16].

Although these reforms show political commitment, issues such as stigma, weak enforcement, and distrust of the legal system have limited its effectiveness [17]. Despite the relevance of CRSV, thorough and comparative research throughout the continent has largely been overlooked in African intellectual and policy discourse. Many of the existing publications are case-based, fragmentary, or primarily concerned with post-conflict justice [18–20]. To fill that gap, this study investigates the patterns and dynamics of sexual violence associated with conflicts in all 54 African countries for a period of five years from 2020 to 2024.

Methods

This study employed a secondary data analysis approach using de-identified, publicly available data on conflict-related sexual violence (CRSV) obtained from the Humanitarian Data Exchange (HDX) portal (https://data.humdata.org/dataset/conflict-related-sexual-violence). The HDX) is an open data platform managed by the United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA) through its Centre for Humanitarian Data. The organization is interested in the people that are affected by humanitarian crisis, their needs and the agencies that are willing in helping those who are affected. In this study, data CRSV was downloaded in CSV format and it covered CRSV cases experienced by minor and adult females across 54 African countries from 2020 to 2024. Key variables included the type of sexual violence, perpetrator identity, weapon used, victim status (adult/minor), and reported mortality.

Descriptive statistics, including frequency distributions and percentages, were computed using SPSS version 27 to identify temporal trends and perpetrator patterns. Associations between categorical variables (e.g., type of violence and perpetrator or victim status) were assessed using Pearson’s χ² and Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact tests, depending on cell size and expected values.

Two panel regressions were used to examine the relationship between (i) the frequency of sexual violence (ii), deaths resulting from CRSV, and categorical factors such as perpetrator (civilian, military, militant, police, vigilante), weapon carried (firearms, others, unspecified), victims’ status (minor, adult), and types of sexual violence (rape, sexual harassment). In each case, the base categories are police, others, minor and sexual harassment, respectively. Because the dependent variable in both regressions is count data, the study employed panel count data regression using Stata version 15. Models tested included Poisson, Negative Binomial (NEGB), Zero-Inflated Poisson (ZIP), and Zero-Inflated Negative Binomial (ZINB). Conversely, sexual harassment increased steadily over time, from 6.6% in 2020 to 18.3% in 2024. Cases of sexual organ mutilation were the least reported but showed a small upward trend.

Discussion

The study revealed that rape consistently had the highest occurrence among all types of sexual violence across the five years (2020–2024), peaking in 2021, and then slightly decreasing through 2024. In contrast, sexual organ mutilation, though least reported, gradually increased between 2021 and 2024. This trend is consistent with previous research that shows rape as a strategic weapon of war that is used to terrorize communities, sever social ties, and establish authority [21]. Although the rates are still quite high, showing ongoing impunity and systematic failure in protection, the downward trend after 2022 may be due to changes in conflict intensity or rising international activism [22]. Conversely, despite being the least reported, the rise in sexual organ mutilation from 2021 to 2024 indicates either better reporting systems or a changing violent strategy. This is consistent with previous research, which shows that when dominant forms of CRSV garner global attention, less visible forms frequently worsen [23]. To address all forms of sexual assault in conflict areas, these developments highlight the necessity for robust legal frameworks and regular monitoring in line with the UN’s Women, Peace, and Security (WPS) agenda and the African Union’s Maputo Protocol [24, 25].

The findings also revealed that the major perpetrators of sexual violence over the years are the militants, peaking in 2022, and then reducing in 2024. National military forces were the second most frequent, while vigilantes and police officers also showed a rising trend in involvement, with police rising across. The prevalence of the militants is indicative of their deliberate use of sexual violence as a means of control and fear throughout prolonged wars [26, 27]. The significant yearly variations may, however, reflect shifting conflict intensities or peacebuilding initiatives, as demonstrated in places like South Sudan, where ceasefires momentarily decreased violence [28, 29]. As observed in countries like Nigeria, the erratic increase in vigilante-related violence in 2023 points to a breakdown in formal security mechanisms, with communities turning to informal actors [30]. The growing police force engagement over time may reflect the growing impunity or militarization in the security sectors, which would undermine the legitimacy of the state [31]. However, the increase in “No information” cases points to growing difficulties in gathering data in the face of continued violence and stigmatisation, which hinders response efforts [32]. More research is necessary since the decline and recovery of national military participation, which peaked in 2024, may indicate sporadic deployments or changes in operational leadership [26]. Despite being few, foreign military offenders reinforce worries about wrongdoing by peacekeepers [33].

Furthermore, the study revealed that rape had the highest number of reported cases with an average of 2.97 over the five years, and the highest number of reported deaths. Attempted sexual assault had a relatively high death count, while transactional sex and unspecified cases reported no deaths. The fact that rape is the most commonly reported type of CRSV and is usually employed systematically in combat is consistent with the evidence currently available [26]. It is interesting to note that the findings about unidentified cases indicate that incidents are often vaguely classified, exposing shortcomings in classification and recordkeeping [1, 34]. The mortality data on rape, showing the highest number of deaths, is consistent with a study which highlights the terrible long-term effects of rape on the body and mind [35]. However, underreporting may be the cause of the lack of deaths in transactional and unidentified situations. These results highlight the necessity of enhanced survivor health care, regulated reporting procedures, and focused interventions in line with Sustainable Development Goal 16 (Peace, Justice, and Strong Institutions) of the UN [36, 37].

A significant relationship exists between the type of perpetrator and the type of violence: militants accounted for the highest events of rape cases, attempted sexual assaults, and sexual harassment cases. National military and police were also significantly involved. The strong association between sexual violence modality and perpetrator type indicates intricate dynamics influenced by actor intentions and conflict situations [38]. The prevalence of rape by militants and sexual mutilation indicates that they intentionally employ extreme violence to create fear and destabilize communities, which is in line with long-running conflicts in places like South Sudan and the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) [39]. These issues may be caused by the militarization of law enforcement and inadequate oversight, which have been more frequently documented in unstable nations since the early 2000 s [40]. The findings also show that vigilantes play a significant role, especially in attempted assaults and rape. This is probably because of security gaps when community order is enforced by informal actors [30]. While data gaps and stigma-related underreporting are indicated by the inclusion of the “No information” category in mutilation [41], these variances show how conflict stages and institutional weakness affect the chances and incentives available to offenders throughout time [38].

The finding shows a statistically significant association between the type of sexual violence and perpetrators for the years 2020, 2021, 2022, and 2023. This is in line with previous research showing that armed groups and state actors use particular types of sexual violence as instruments of control, intimidation, and political repression. These findings imply that the nature of sexual violence is not random but rather frequently patterned by the identity or role of the perpetrator [42, 43]. Policymakers must therefore prioritize early warning systems, victim protection legislation, and criminal accountability frameworks to make sure that legal definitions and interventions take into account the changing dynamics of perpetrator behaviour over time and space [44].

Furthermore, the study revealed a significant association between the type of sexual violence and the victim’s status (adult or minor). Adults made up the majority of victims in attempted assault and sexual harassment, while a large portion of rape victims were minors. This is consistent with previous research that emphasises age as a determining factor of sexual victimisation [45, 46]. In the meantime, the high percentage of children who have been raped shows a troubling trend that is in line with research in conflict areas where children are singled out because of their vulnerability [47]. Stronger laws protecting children, harsh punishments for child-targeted sexual assault, and early intervention initiatives in community and educational institutions are all priorities for governments and humanitarian organizations [48].

The findings show that incidents involving civilians, militants, military, and vigilantes are more likely to have higher victim counts. Firearm use was associated with fewer victims and deaths. Civilians were more associated with deadly outcomes. The findings are consistent with existing studies showing that both state and non-state actors significantly contribute to mass sexual violence in wars [39, 49, 50]. The greater numbers of victims associated with these groups point to planned or opportunistic mass attacks, which are in line with the deliberate use of sexual violence to intimidate communities [51]. On the other hand, research on law enforcement abuse has shown that police involvement results in fewer victims, which may indicate more isolated or targeted activities [31]. The negative correlation between firearms and victim numbers could be attributed to the lethality of firearms, which can result in immediate fatalities, fewer survivors, or fewer victim scenarios [52]. Furthermore, the higher occurrence among adult victims may be due to reporting bias or their increased exposure to conflict areas [53]. Sexual harassment may be more common but less deadly than rape, which is linked to fewer victims, showing that the two practices have different operational dynamics [54]. The high number of civilian-related death reports suggests potentially severe extrajudicial violence outside of official command structures, which is consistent with research from war areas around the world [55].

Conclusion

This study examined the patterns and dynamics of conflict-related sexual violence (CRSV) across 54 African countries from 2020 to 2024. Rape was the most reported form, with militants and national forces identified as major perpetrators. Significant associations were found between types of violence, perpetrator identity, and victim status, affecting both adults and minors. The findings emphasize CRSV as a deliberate strategy in conflict, not merely a byproduct. Addressing it requires stronger legal frameworks, survivor-centered services, and improved data systems. Effective prevention and accountability mechanisms are crucial for promoting justice, peace, and development across the continent.

Recommendations

Addressing CRSV is crucial to attaining peace, justice, and sustainable development throughout the continent and goes beyond a simple humanitarian duty. Therefore, policymakers, regional organizations, and foreign agencies need to take a coordinated approach that incorporates robust data systems, survivor-centered care, and legal accountability. Achieving this may involve bolstering early warning and response systems based on CRSV trends, particularly victim demographics and actor types. International human rights standards must be incorporated into legal frameworks, and national governments must pledge to bring charges against all parties involved, including peacekeepers and state actors. Dismantling stigma and promoting healing simultaneously require funding for trauma-informed treatment and community education.

Strengths and limitations

Strength

This study offers a comprehensive continent-wide analysis of conflict-related sexual violence (CRSV) in Africa, covering 54 countries over five years. It employs a robust mix of descriptive, inferential, and regression methods to explore correlations between perpetrators, types of violence, and victim outcomes. Using publicly accessible, standardized data enhances transparency and reproducibility, and the multi-method analytical approach adds depth to the findings.

Limitations

The study is limited by its dependence on secondary data, which may be prone to underreporting, especially in conflict zones where stigma and insecurity discourage disclosure. Some variables lack detailed contextual information. Additionally, using aggregate data restricts a more detailed analysis of individual risk factors and country-specific differences.
