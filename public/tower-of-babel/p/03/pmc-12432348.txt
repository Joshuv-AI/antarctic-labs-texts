Inter‐Hospital Spread of Fluconazole‐Resistant C. parapsilosis in Northern Italy: Insights Into Clonal Distribution, Resistance Mechanisms and Biofilm Production
Authors: Giorgia Palladini, Valentina Lepera, Serena Trubini, Gabriella Tocci, Andrea Zappavigna, Elizabeth Iskandar, Guglielmo Ferrari, Anna Prigitano, Nicola Ferraro, Roberta Schiavo, Fausto Baldanti, Caterina Cavanna, Giuliana Lo Cascio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundStarting from 2018 onwards, several outbreaks of fluconazole‐resistant C. parapsilosis have been reported in many countries worldwide.ObjectivesHere we report a retrospective study on C. parapsilosis blood isolates collected over 7 years (2018–2024) in two hospitals in Northern Italy.Patients/MethodsThe study involved 169 C. parapsilosis isolates collected from individual hospitalised patients. We assessed the antifungal susceptibility of the isolates, evaluated the presence of mutations in the ERG11 gene and performed multilocus microsatellite typing to highlight the genetic relatedness of the strains. All isolates were also tested for their ability to produce biofilm.ResultsAmong the 169 clinical isolates, 124 (73.4%) were classified as fluconazole‐resistant C. parapsilosis (FRCP) and 45 (26.6%) as fluconazole‐susceptible (FSCP). ERG11 sequencing highlighted that the most frequent mutation in FRCP is the Y132F (118/124, 95.2%). None of the FSCP carried the Y132F. Microsatellite genotyping showed five major clusters and 13 sub‐clusters, formed by isolates sharing identical genotypes. Sub‐cluster R1 included 96 FRCP carrying the Y132F substitution, isolated from 2018 to 2024 in both hospitals. Interestingly, 99.1% of the FRCP carrying the Y132F mutation were categorised as low biofilm formers, while FRCP carrying other ERG11 mutations were categorised as medium or high biofilm formers.ConclusionsOur results confirmed that Y132F may be mainly responsible for azole resistance in C. parapsilosis and inter‐hospital spread. As we found, recent clinical studies indicate that FRCP isolates responsible for severe outbreaks produce thin biofilms. Mutated and therefore resistant strains may exhibit reduced biofilm production as a protective mechanism.Keywords: biofilm, C. parapsilosis , ERG11, fluconazole resistance, Italy, Y132F

Introduction

Although C. albicans remains the most common cause of candidaemia globally, the prevalence of non‐albicans Candida species has increased in the last two decades. Among them, C. parapsilosis has emerged as a major cause of bloodstream infections (BSI), becoming the third leading cause of candidaemia worldwide and the second cause in Europe [1, 2].

In Italy, epidemiological data from 2016 to 2017 confirmed C. albicans as the most frequently isolated species responsible for candidaemia [3, 4], as for earlier years [5]. However, a notable shift occurred during the COVID‐19 pandemic (2020–2021): C. parapsilosis became predominant, accounting for 49.8% of all fungaemia cases in 10 hospitals, followed by C. albicans (35%) and C. glabrata (10%) [6].

Unlike other Candida species that are primarily endogenous, C. parapsilosis is of exogenous origin and capable of colonising human skin and implanted medical devices [7, 8]. Its capacity to form biofilm facilitates horizontal transmission within healthcare settings, playing a key role in both superficial and systemic infections [9, 10]. One of several biofilm‐based antifungal resistance mechanisms involves the retention of azoles within the glucan matrix, shielding the fungal cells from drug activity [11, 12, 13]. Conversely, lipopeptidic antifungals, such as echinocandins, display excellent anti‐biofilm activity by interfering with beta‐glucan synthesis, thus representing a valid antifungal choice in obstinate infections [14].

Several studies have reported an alarming increase in C. parapsilosis resistance to azole antifungals [12, 15], especially fluconazole, with notably high resistance rates [5]; this resistance profile, previously uncommon, has become increasingly frequent and is often accompanied by cross‐resistance to voriconazole, further limiting treatment options [16]. Since 2018, several outbreaks of fluconazole‐resistant C. parapsilosis have been reported in many countries worldwide [17]. The main fluconazole resistance mechanism involves the presence of point mutations in the ERG11 gene, which encodes lanosterol 14‐alpha‐demethylase (ERG11p), target of the azole antifungals and key enzyme in the conversion of lanosterol to ergosterol [17, 18]. Y132F is the dominant amino acid substitution in ERG11p described in C. parapsilosis [17, 18, 19], as well as in other Candida species [20]. ERG11p substitutions such as G458S, K128N and K143R have also been associated with resistance to fluconazole with variable degrees of voriconazole resistance [21, 22, 23]. Other mechanisms involve the presence of mutations in the TAC1 (transcriptional activator of CDR genes) and MRR1 (transcription factor and multidrug resistance regulator) genes, leading to CDR and MDR1 overexpression in Candida species, respectively [18, 24]. The role of silent mutations as well as ‘compensatory’ mutations, such as R398I substitution, remains unclear, as they have been described both in susceptible and resistant isolates [18, 23].

Resistance to fluconazole represents a global threat, due to its importance in the treatment of many fungal infections worldwide, in limiting the consumption of echinocandins and reducing hospital length of stay. Fluconazole resistance in C. parapsilosis complicates the management of fungal infections even more, since this species shows diminished susceptibility to echinocandins [18]. Moreover, fluconazole‐resistant isolates seem to be associated with clonal transmission and higher mortality rates [17, 21, 25].

The COVID‐19 pandemic may have played a role in the clonal spread of fluconazole‐resistant C. parapsilosis strains, due to severe overcrowding of hospitals and significant transfer of patients and health care professionals between different hospitals [26].

In this study, we investigated phenotypic and genotypic characteristics of C. parapsilosis blood isolates collected over 7 years (from 2018 to 2024) in two hospitals in Northern Italy. We assessed antifungal susceptibility, evaluated the presence of ERG11 gene mutations, performed microsatellite genotyping to highlight the genetic relatedness of the strains and explored their ability to produce biofilm.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Clinical Isolates

The study involved C. parapsilosis bloodstream isolates collected from individual patients hospitalised at Azienda Unità Sanitaria Locale di Piacenza (AUSL Piacenza, Italy), which includes the hospitals of Piacenza, Bobbio, Castel San Giovanni and Fiorenzuola, between January 2021 and March 2024 and at the Fondazione IRCCS Policlinico San Matteo (Pavia, Italy) between January 2018 and December 2021. Clinical data obtained were age, gender, ward, year and month of isolation.

Positive blood cultures were streaked on Sabouraud glucose agar (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cleveland, OH, USA) and incubated at 37°C for 24–48 h. The identification of clinical isolates was first performed using matrix‐assisted laser desorption ionisation–time of flight (MALDI‐TOF) mass spectrometry with the VITEK MS system (bioMérieux, Marcy l'Etoile, France). Before testing, all isolates were stored at −80°C.

Antifungal Susceptibility Testing

Antifungal susceptibility testing was performed by Vitek 2 AFST using the AST‐YS08 cards (bioMérieux) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The dilution range was 0.06–8 mg/L for micafungin, 0.125–8 mg/L for voriconazole and caspofungin, 0.25–16 mg/L for amphotericin B, 0.5–64 mg/L for fluconazole and 1–64 mg/L for 5‐flucytosine. EUCAST clinical breakpoints were used to define azole resistance [27]. Cards were incubated at 35°C for 24h in the Vitek 2 instrument and MICs were read automatically. In particular, for fluconazole, isolates with MIC > 4 μg/mL were defined as resistant and those with MIC ≤ 2 μg/mL as susceptible; for voriconazole, isolates with MIC > 0.25 μg/mL were defined as resistant and those with MIC ≤ 0.125 μg/mL as susceptible.

Resistance to azoles was confirmed using Sensititre Yeast One method (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For the YeastOne plate, the dilution range was 0.12–8 mg/L for amphotericin B, 0.12–256 mg/L for fluconazole, 0.008–8 mg/L for voriconazole, caspofungin and micafungin, and 0.06–64 mg/L for 5‐flucytosine. Plates were incubated at 35°C for 24 h and MICs were determined by visual examination.

MIC results were interpreted according to clinical breakpoints reported in the CLSI M27‐S4 document [28]. For fluconazole, isolates with MIC ≥ 8 μg/mL were defined as resistant and those with MIC ≤ 2 μg/mL as susceptible; for voriconazole, isolates with MIC ≥ 1 μg/mL were defined as resistant and those with MIC ≤ 0.12 μg/mL as susceptible.

MIC data for other antifungal agents were not systematically available for the entire collection and were therefore not included in the analysis.

C. parapsilosis ATCC22019 was used as quality control.

ITS and ERG11 Sequencing

Sequencing of the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) domain of the multi‐copy ribosomal RNA gene was performed to confirm species identification. The genomic DNA was extracted from single colonies using ELITe InGenius (ElitechGroup S.p.A, Torino). Amplification and sequencing were performed using universal primers ITS1 and ITS4 and conditions previously described by Souza et al. [29] (Table 1). Sequence analysis was executed with Sequencing Analysis Software v7.0. (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and sequences were aligned using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST). Nucleotide BLAST searches were then performed in GenBank, and results were considered significant with an E value of 0.0 at 98%–100% identity and with at least 90% query coverage. Species identification was based on the following well‐defined reference strain (GenBank accession number): AY939798 for C. parapsilosis ATCC 22019.

Microsatellite Typing

Multilocus microsatellite typing was performed to evaluate the genetic relatedness of C. parapsilosis clinical isolates. A panel of six short tandem repeat markers, which consisted of three trinucleotide and three hexanucleotide markers, was used for this purpose [30]. One of the amplification primers in each panel was labelled with 6‐carboxy‐fluorescein (6‐FAM), either 6‐carboxy‐tetramethylrhodamine (TAMRA) or hexachloro‐fluorescein (HEX) (Table 2). Six simplex PCRs were carried out in a final volume of 25 μL. Then, 1 μL of the amplification products was added to 1 μL of the internal size marker GeneScan 500 LIZ dye Size Standard (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 15 μL of formamide. After that, the samples were denatured at 95°C for 10 min, then cooled at 4°C for 10 min and analysed on the automatic sequencer 3500 Dx Genetic Analyser (Applied Biosystems). C. parapsilosis ATCC 22019 was included in each run as a reference strain. The number of repeats in each marker was determined using GeneMapper Software 6 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A dendrogram was constructed to visualise similarities between genotypes using DendroUPGMA available online (https://usuaris.tinet.cat/debb/UPGMA/).

Biofilm Formation and Quantification

XTT [2,3‐bis(2‐methoxy‐4‐nitro‐5‐sulfo‐phenyl)‐2H‐tetrazolium‐5‐carboxanilide]‐reduction assay was used to calculate a semiquantitative measure of biofilm formation, as described by Ramage et al. [31] Each strain, grown on Sabouraud agar, was suspended in RPMI 1640 medium to a cellular density equivalent to 1.0 × 106 CFU/mL. Biofilms were formed by transferring 100 μL of the suspension into each well of a 96‐well microtitre plate. The plates were incubated at 37°C for 24 h. After incubation, the suspension was aspirated and unattached cells were removed by washing the wells three times with sterile PBS. Then, 100 μL of XTT‐menadione solution was added to each well and the plates were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 2 h. The absorbance was measured with a spectrophotometer plate reader at 490 nm. Isolates were categorised as being low biofilm formers (LBFs), moderate biofilm formers (MBFs) or high biofilm formers (HBFs) [32]. Each strain was tested three times.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA). Associations between categorical variables were assessed using the χ 2 (chi‐squared) test. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Where appropriate, odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to quantify the strength of associations between binary variables.

Results

Clinical Data

A total of 169 clinical isolates of C. parapsilosis from blood cultures of individual patients were included in this study (n = 93 collected in Piacenza; n = 76 collected in Pavia). The collection of isolates of Pavia comprised 22 strains isolated in 2018, nine in 2019, six in 2020 and 39 in 2021. The collection of Piacenza included 39 strains isolated in 2021, 30 in 2022, 23 in 2023 and one in 2024. Overall, 59.2% (n = 100) of the patients were men and 40.8% (n = 69) were women. The median age of the patients was 71 years (range, 25–99 years). Wards of isolation are listed in Table S1.

Antifungal Susceptibility Testing

Of the 169 isolates tested, 124 (73.4%) were classified as fluconazole‐resistant (MIC > 8 mg/L) and 45 (26.6%) were classified as fluconazole‐susceptible (MIC ≤ 2 μg/mL) by Sensititre Yeast One method. In detail, of the 93 C. parapsilosis isolates collected in Piacenza, 57 (61.3%) were fluconazole‐resistant; among them, 49 (52.7%) were cross‐resistant to voriconazole (MIC ≥ 1 μg/mL), while 6 (6.5%) were intermediate (MICs, 0.25–0.5 μg/mL) and 2 (2.1%) were susceptible (MIC ≤ 0.12 μg/mL). Of the 76 isolates collected in Pavia, 67 (88.2%) were fluconazole‐resistant, of which 30 (39.5%) were cross‐resistant to voriconazole and 37 (48.7%) were intermediate. ITS and ERG11 Sequencing

ITS gene sequencing confirmed all 169 isolates as C. parapsilosis sensu stricto.

ERG11 sequencing highlighted that among the 57 fluconazole‐resistant isolates of Piacenza, 52 (91.2%) harboured the Y132F amino acid substitution alone and one (1.8%) isolate carried the Y132F in combination with the R398I substitution. Three (5.2%) isolates exhibited the R398I substitution alone and one (1.8%) isolate harboured the R398I in combination with the A114V mutation, reported only once in the literature before [33]. Among the 67 fluconazole‐resistant isolates of Pavia, 66 (98.5%) carried the Y132F substitution alone, while one isolate (1.5%) harboured Y132F in combination with the I261M mutation, which, to our knowledge, has not been previously described in the literature.

Of the 36 fluconazole‐susceptible isolates collected in Piacenza, 27 (75%) were ERG11 gene wild type while nine (25%) harboured the R398I amino acid substitution. For those from Pavia, seven (77.8%) were wild type and two (22.2%) carried the R398I substitution. None of the fluconazole‐susceptible isolates had the Y132F mutation.

Microsatellite Typing

Microsatellite genotyping identified 52 different genotypes among 169 C. parapsilosis strains isolated in the two hospitals. As shown in the dendrogram (Figure 1), these isolates were distributed into five major clusters (cluster I–V) comprising 162 isolates and 13 sub‐clusters (sub‐clusters R1–R5 for resistant isolates and S1–S8 for susceptible isolates) formed by isolates sharing identical genotypes at all loci. The dendrogram highlights the genetic relatedness of the strains, allowing visualisation of clonal groups and their distribution across hospitals.

UPGMA (unweighted pair group method with arithmetic mean) dendrogram based on microsatellite genotyping of 169 Candida parapsilosis isolates collected from two hospitals (Pavia and Piacenza). The dendrogram illustrates the distribution of isolates into five major clusters (I–V) and 13 sub‐clusters, comprising isolates with identical multilocus genotypes. R1–R5 (in red) represent sub‐clusters of fluconazole‐resistant isolates; S1–S8 (in green) represent sub‐clusters of fluconazole‐susceptible isolates. Isolates from Pavia are labelled with the prefix ‘PV’, whereas those from Piacenza are identified by sequential numbers only. Fluconazole‐resistant isolates (n = 124) are identified with the final letter ‘R’ and fluconazole‐susceptible isolates (n = 45) with ‘S’.

Cluster I included the majority of the isolates: 107 (63.3%) fluconazole‐resistant isolates and two (1.2%) fluconazole‐susceptible isolates. All fluconazole‐resistant isolates grouped into this major cluster harboured the Y132F substitution and belonged to closely related genotypes; in particular, 96 of the 107 (89.7%) showed the same allelic profile at six loci (sub‐cluster R1). These closely related strains were recovered from 2018 to 2024 in both Pavia and Piacenza (Table S1). The departments most affected by the spread of this R1 clone were the Intensive Care Unit in Pavia with 47 isolates (49.0%) as well as the Respiratory Intensive Care Unit in Piacenza with 12 isolates (12.5%). This could be due to the nature of these departments, which often deal with critically ill patients who require prolonged hospital stays, invasive procedures and mechanical ventilation. These factors increase the risk of colonisation and infection by multidrug‐resistant organisms.

Cluster II comprised three (1.8%) isolates showing unique genetic profiles: one fluconazole‐resistant carrying the Y132F in combination with the R398I substitution (49R), one resistant carrying the R398I alone (19R) and one susceptible with the R398I (13S).

Cluster III included 36 isolates (21.3%): nine resistant isolated in Pavia with the Y132F, one resistant with the R398I (46R), one resistant with the R398I in combination with the A114V (29R) and 25 susceptible.

Cluster IV included nine susceptible isolates and one resistant isolate with the Y132F mutation (16R).

Cluster V comprised five susceptible isolates, among which two pairs (isolates PV21065S and PV21066S, and isolates PV20008S and PV18020S) were found to be identical by microsatellite typing.

While cluster I represents the predominant resistant clonal lineage, cluster II exhibits greater genetic diversity and includes strains with distinct genetic patterns. This variability may indicate the independent emergence of resistance in several strains, unrelated to the clonal spread of cluster I. Cluster III, containing both resistant and susceptible strains, may indeed represent a transitional cluster. In contrast, clusters IV and V are predominantly composed of susceptible strains, suggesting that these genetic lineages have not undergone selection for resistance and may represent the wild‐type population.

Overall, compared to fluconazole‐susceptible isolates (45 isolates/27 unique genotypes), fluconazole‐resistant isolates (124 isolates/12 unique genotypes) showed a reduced genotypic variability (p‐value < 0.00001 by chi‐squared test).

Biofilm Production and Quantification

In vitro biofilm formation was assessed for 112 resistant strains and 41 susceptible strains. 105 of the 106 (99.1%) resistant isolates carrying the Y132F mutation alone were categorised as LBFs; one isolate was classified as MBF. Four resistant isolates carrying the R398I alone or in combination with the A114V were categorised as MBFs or HBFs. The two isolates harbouring both the Y132F and the R398I/I126M mutations turned out to be low biofilm formers. In fact, the presence of the mutation is significantly associated with poorer biofilm production (p‐value < 0.00001 by chi‐squared test) and strains carrying the mutation showed a markedly increased likelihood of being low biofilm formers, with an odds ratio of 856.0 (95% CI: 24.9–29,458.1). Among susceptible isolates, 24 (58.5%) were categorised as LBFs, 12 (29.3%) as MBFs and 5 (12.2%) as HBFs.

Discussion

The spread of fluconazole‐resistant C. parapsilosis represents a global concern. In recent years, outbreaks of C. parapsilosis infection due to fluconazole resistance have been described in several countries all over the world, including Brazil [15, 25, 34], South Korea [22], India [23], Mexico [35], South Africa [36], Turkey [21, 37] and Canada [38]. In Europe, the clonal dissemination of fluconazole‐resistant C. parapsilosis affected many hospitals in Spain, France, Greece and Italy [19, 26, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46]. A study conducted by Mesquida et al. [42] demonstrated that some genotypes found in Rome were clonally related to those found in Madrid, Burgos and Ávila, suggesting that the transmission of these genotypes was not limited to a single country. People travelling and migrating may significantly contribute to this phenomenon [47].

In our study, we analysed a collection of 169 C. parapsilosis strains that caused candidaemia in patients from two hospitals in northern Italy over a 7‐year period (2018–2024). We found that 73.4% (124/169) of isolates were resistant to fluconazole. A similar resistance rate (77.7%) has been reported in Spain [41]. We characterised fluconazole‐resistance mechanisms, discovering that 96.8% (120/124) of the resistant isolates harboured the Y132F amino acid substitution in the ERG11 gene. This mutation was found exclusively in fluconazole‐resistant isolates and in none of the susceptible isolates, consistent with other reports [22, 36, 48]. Our results confirmed that Y132F may be mainly responsible for azole resistance in C. parapsilosis , in line with the findings from Italy, Spain Turkey [19, 21, 41, 49]. Another substitution, R398I, was found in 4.0% (5/124) of the fluconazole‐resistant isolates, once in combination with the Y132F and once in combination with the A114V. The A114V mutation was first described by Esfahani et al. [33] in two strains of fluconazole‐resistant C. albicans isolated from vulvovaginal candidiasis. R398I was also identified in 24.4% (11/45) of the susceptible isolates. Further investigations are needed to clarify the role played by the R398I point mutation in fluconazole resistance [18, 36].

Microsatellite analysis confirmed that some fluconazole‐resistant genotypes can spread across different hospitals and persist for several years [22, 43]. Regarding this point, analysis of sub‐cluster R1 is particularly revealing. It included 96 fluconazole‐resistant isolates, all sharing identical genotype and carrying the Y132F substitution, isolated between 2018 and 2024 in both Piacenza and Pavia, suggesting a potential epidemiological link between the affected hospitals, given that the greater clonal spread mainly affected intensive care units. As reported by Choi et al. [22], these Y132F isolates exhibited a higher propensity to cause clonal transmission than other fluconazole‐resistant isolates and can persist within hospitals for years. Moreover, we observed a reduction in genetic diversity among fluconazole‐resistant strains compared to susceptible ones, which may be partly attributed to the fact that most of the susceptible strains were recovered from unrelated cases [43].

The role of biofilm formation in C. parapsilosis pathogenicity has been well established, with previous studies associating high biofilm production with increased antifungal resistance, persistence in hospital environments and higher mortality rates [50, 51]. However, our findings suggest a more complex relationship. Interestingly, we found that the presence of the Y132F mutation in fluconazole‐resistant isolates is significantly associated with lower biofilm production, which is consistent with other studies [15, 21, 46, 52]. A similar trend was observed by Sharma et al. [53], who reported that fluconazole‐susceptible isolates produced significantly more biofilm than resistant isolates. These results challenge the previously accepted notion that increased biofilm production directly correlates with higher resistance and virulence and that it serves as a primary driver of clonal outbreaks [50, 51]. Historically, biofilm formation has been considered a key factor contributing to antifungal resistance, as biofilms can act as a physical barrier, limiting drug penetration and providing a protective environment against host immune defences. However, recent clinical studies, including our own, indicate that fluconazole‐resistant C. parapsilosis isolates responsible for severe outbreaks tend to produce thin biofilms [17, 21].

One potential explanation for the observed phenomenon is that strains which have mutated and consequently developed resistance may exhibit reduced biofilm production to protect themselves, and may compensate through alternative survival strategies, such as upregulation of efflux pumps, enhanced cell wall rigidity or metabolic adaptations that increase fitness in the presence of azoles [46]. These findings underscore the complexity of C. parapsilosis pathogenicity and the need for a multifaceted approach to infection management.

Our study is subject to some limitations. The statistical analysis was primarily descriptive and did not include multivariate modelling, due to the exploratory nature of the study and limited availability of clinical covariates. We did not investigate alternative mechanisms of fluconazole resistance such as the presence of mutations in the MRR1 or TAC1 gene, which lead to the overexpression of drug transporter genes. As these pathways were not assessed in our study, the mechanistic interpretation of resistance remains incomplete. Future studies should include the analysis of transcriptional factors and efflux pump regulation to better define the molecular basis of azole resistance in C. parapsilosis . Moreover, some relevant clinical data such as the presence of a central venous catheter, the response to antifungal treatment and the patient's clinical outcome were not available for this study and were not considered in the analysis. This restricts our ability to evaluate the direct clinical consequences of fluconazole resistance and specific ERG11 mutations, notably the Y132F substitution. In the absence of outcome data, we cannot determine whether the observed phenotypic and genotypic features, such as reduced biofilm formation or clonal persistence, translate into a poorer clinical prognosis or treatment failure. Additional studies integrating microbiological and clinical data will be crucial to fully understand the impact of resistant C. parapsilosis strains on patient outcomes and to guide therapeutic decision‐making.

In conclusion, our study provides valuable insights into the phenotypic and genotypic characteristics of Candida parapsilosis strains responsible for candidaemia in two hospitals in Northern Italy. The majority of the isolates exhibited fluconazole resistance, primarily associated with the Y132F substitution, and were genetically related, suggesting the spread of a clonal complex between the two hospitals. These findings highlight the need to enforce infection control measures in order to reduce the transmission of these pathogens and enhance antifungal stewardship to prevent the development of drug resistance [54]. We believe that investigating factors such as the genetic relatedness of C. parapsilosis strains, antifungal susceptibility profile, molecular basis of fluconazole resistance and ability to produce biofilm could support improved patient management and offer crucial insights for developing targeted control strategies for these infections. A key objective will be to further explore azole resistance using next‐generation sequencing, which enables extensive and deep genome analysis.
