Borderline personality disorder and substance use disorder: a clinical and psychopathological investigation
Authors: Paola Bozzatello, Claudio Brasso, Giacomo Marin, Gabriele Lerda, Paola Rocca, Silvio Bellino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBorderline Personality Disorder (BPD) is a severe psychiatric condition frequently associated with Substance Use Disorder (SUD), with high rates of comorbidity and poor clinical outcomes. Impulsivity and emotional dysregulation are core features shared by both disorders, suggesting common underlying mechanisms. However, less is known about the role of additional factors—such as alexithymia, coping strategies, and quality of life in contributing to dual diagnosis and SUD severity in individuals with BPD. This study aims to: (1) examine the clinical and demographic variables in a sample of patients with BPD comorbid with SUD, in comparison with a group of patients with BPD alone, and (2) identify clinical and psychopathological factors related to the presence of SUD and severity of SUD symptoms in the whole sample (BPD with and without SUD).MethodsSeventy-five outpatients diagnosed with BPD were recruited and divided into two groups: BPD with comorbid SUD (n = 43) and BPD without SUD (n = 32). Diagnoses were established using SCID-5-CV and SCID-5-PD. Participants were assessed using validated instruments measuring BPD severity (BPDSI-IV), impulsivity (BIS-11), alexithymia (TAS-20), coping strategies (CISS), addiction vulnerability and severity (ABQ), quality of life (SAT-P), and functioning (SOFAS). Group differences were analyzed using chi-square and ANCOVA. Hierarchical logistic and linear regressions were conducted to identify factors associated with the SUD presence and severity.ResultsPatients with BPD and SUD were more frequently male and showed earlier BPD onset, greater symptom severity, higher impulsivity, higher vulnerability to addiction, greater alexithymia, and more use of task-oriented coping strategies. Logistic regression showed that impulsivity and vulnerability to SUD were significantly associated with comorbid SUD, while alexithymia reduced the effect of gender. Motor impulsiveness independently contributed to the presence of dual diagnosis. Linear regression analyses indicated that alexithymia and impulsivity were variables most strictly associated with SUD severity, jointly explaining approximately 60% of the variance. Specifically, difficulty describing feelings and externally oriented thinking contributed to a greater SUD severity.ConclusionsImpulsivity and alexithymia appear to be key transdiagnostic factors underlying both the presence and severity of SUD in individuals with BPD. These findings highlight the importance of targeting deficits in emotional processing and impulse control in clinical interventions to improve outcomes in this clinical population.

Background

Borderline Personality Disorder (BPD) is a severe personality disorder, with a prevalence of almost 1.6% in the general population, which can rise to 5.9% in adult primary care settings [14]. It is characterized by a pervasive pattern of instability in interpersonal relationships and self-image and typically develops in early adulthood. Additionally, patients with BPD show poor frustration tolerance and experience intense fears of abandonment, to which they may respond with several risky behaviors, including substance abuse. Among clinical features, impulsivity and emotional dysregulation are considered core dimensions of BPD and represent key mechanisms underlying high-risk behaviors, including substance use. In this regard, converging evidence suggests that these psychopathological dimensions are also central to Substance Use Disorders (SUD), supporting the hypothesis of shared underlying mechanisms between the two conditions [36].

Comorbidity between BPD and substance abuse is common and well-documented: about 75% of BPD patients have concurrent substance abuse, and about 20% of patients diagnosed with a SUD are also diagnosed with BPD [60]. This co-occurrence is associated with a more severe clinical course, including higher rates of mood disorders, particularly Major Depressive Disorder (MDD), and poorer overall prognosis [64]. The association between BPD and SUD can be considered within a framework of shared etiological vulnerability. The shared liability may be mediated by common temperamental traits such as impulsivity, emotional instability, and neuroticism [26]. In addition to this biological predisposition, there are environmental factors, including early traumatic experiences, that contribute to the development of both disorders, potentially leading individuals to turn to substance use as a maladaptive form of self-medication [53].

Available data suggest that once one of the two disorders occurs (BPD or SUD), it is likely to become chronic, with a direct and reciprocal effect in the genesis and chronicization of the other disorder [60]. Zanarini et al. highlighted that while short-term remissions from alcohol and drug use disorders are common among individuals with borderline personality disorder (BPD), long-term remissions are less frequent, and recurrences are particularly likely following brief remission periods [65].

Amounting evidence identifies impulsivity as a key factor in SUD, influencing both the onset and the course of addictive behaviors. Individuals with SUD show higher levels of impulsivity, which has also been found to predict poorer treatment outcomes [11, 17, 21, 22, 51]. Nevertheless, impulsivity alone does not fully explain the clinical complexity observed in BPD patients with comorbid SUD. In recent years, growing interest has shifted toward additional transdiagnostic factors that may contribute to this comorbidity or to the severity of addictive behaviors. Among these, alexithymia, defined as difficulty in identifying and describing feelings and a tendency toward externally oriented thinking, has emerged as a central construct. Both BPD and SUD subjects present elevated levels of alexithymia, which is associated with impaired emotional processing and maladaptive regulation strategies. Alexithymia may also account for gender-related differences in clinical presentation; for example, females with BPD show higher levels of alexithymia with a greater tendency toward despair [2]. Gender itself represents another relevant but underexplored variable. Emerging evidence suggested that gender differences play a significant role in the expression and maintenance of BPD [2, 12, 52]. In particular, males with BPD are more likely to present externalizing symptoms (e.g., aggressiveness) and comorbid disorders (e.g., substance use). At the same time, females with BPD are more likely to present internalizing symptoms (e.g., affective instability) and comorbid disorders (e.g., major depressive disorder) [52]. These differences may indicate distinct patterns that lead to SUD vulnerability.

Dysfunctional coping strategies also play a crucial role in the development, course, and treatment outcomes of severe mental disorders, including BPD and SUD [35, 40]. Several studies have observed that both BPD and SUD patients tend to use fewer adaptive coping strategies compared to the general population [16, 40, 50]. Adaptive coping strategies have been associated with lower severity of addictive behaviors, better treatment adherence, and lower relapse rates; however, evidence regarding the specific coping profiles associated with SUD in BPD remains limited [35, 40].

In the context of recovery-oriented treatment for mental illnesses, the subjective perception of quality of life represents a key marker of disease burden and an important aspect of clinical and psychosocial outcomes in both BPD [57] and SUD patients [38]. Some authors have highlighted that, among drug users, psychosis and borderline personality disorder have the greatest impact on quality of life and functioning [15]. Despite the growing body of literature, there is still a lack of studies examining the role of impulsivity, alexithymia, coping strategies, and gender (in addition to other more studied demographic and clinical variables) in detecting both the presence and severity of SUD in individuals with BPD.

To address this gap, the purpose of the present cross-sectional study is to achieve two primary objectives. It is aimed to:examine the clinical and demographic variables in a sample of patients with BPD comorbid with SUD, in comparison with a group of patients with BPD without SUD.identify clinical and psychopathological factors related to the presence of SUD and severity of SUD symptoms in the whole sample (BPD with and without SUD).

Methods

Participants

Subjects were evaluated at the outpatient service of the Center for Personality Disorders in the Department of Neuroscience of the University of Turin, Italy.

Patients were divided into two distinct groups: 1) Group 1: patients with BPD comorbid with SUD; 2) Group 2: patients diagnosed with BPD without comorbid SUD.

An expert clinician (P.B.) made the diagnosis, which was confirmed with the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM- 5 - Clinical Version and Personality Disorders (SCID-5-CV and SCID-5-PD) [27, 28].

Group 1

Inclusion criteria

Outpatients aged between 18 and 60 years with a diagnosis of Borderline Personality Disorder (BPD) and Substance Use Disorder (SUD), according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th Edition (DSM-5) criteria [1], were recruited.

All subjects included in the study were clinically stable (with no hospitalizations, no significant changes in pharmacotherapy, no admissions to the emergency room) for at least three months.

Exclusion criteria

1) comorbidity with any psychiatric disorder other than SUD and major depressive disorder (MDD); 2) patients with neurological disorders; 2) a co-occurring major depressive episode; 3) patients with symptom severity and functional impairment deemed by the clinician to be incompatible with the administration of the assessment instruments; 4) patients with intoxication and/or withdrawal symptoms in the last two weeks.

Group 2

Inclusion criteria

Outpatients aged between 18 and 60 years with a diagnosis of BPD according to DSM-5 criteria.

All subjects included in the study were clinically stable (with no hospitalizations, no significant changes in pharmacotherapy, and no emergency room admissions) for at least three months.

Exclusion criteria

1) comorbidity with any psychiatric disorder other than MDD; 2) patients with neurological disorders; 3) a co-occurring major depressive episode; 4) patients with symptom severity and functional impairment deemed by the clinician to be incompatible with the administration of the assessment instruments; 5) past and current alcohol use disorder or substance use disorder.

The trial was carried out in accordance with the recommendations of the Ethics Committee: Comitato Etico Interaziendale A.O.U. Città della Salute e della Scienza di Torino - A.O. Ordine Mauriziano - A.S.L. Città di Torino (approval code: 00483/2022). The principles of the Declaration of Helsinki were followed. Written informed consent was obtained from all subjects. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Patients included in the study were evaluated using a semi-structured interview to assess demographic and clinical features. Data collected included sociodemographic variables (age, gender, level of education); psychiatric history (age of BPD onset; comorbidity with major depressive disorder (MDD)); current psychopharmacological medications; SUD type (alcohol, cocaine, MDMA, LSD, cannabis, opioids); SUD treatment (disulfiram, acamprosate, methadone, buprenorphine); BPD and SUD psychopathology (BPD specific symptoms; vulnerability for SUD; severity of addictive behaviors); traumatic experiences; alexithymia; impulsivity; coping strategies; functional milestones (employment status, stable romantic relationship, and independent living); global functioning, and subjective quality of life.

The BPDSI is a semi-structured clinical interview assessing the frequency and severity of specific BPD symptoms. The interview consists of eight items scored on a 10-point frequency scale (0 = never; 10 = daily), including “abandonment,” “interpersonal relationships,” “impulsivity,” “parasuicidal behavior,” “affective instability,” “feelings of emptiness,” “outbursts of anger,” “dissociation and paranoid ideation,” and of one item scored on a four-point severity scale, concerning “identity.” The total score is the sum of the nine averaged criteria scores (range 0–90). The index, as well as the separate criteria, possesses adequate reliability, as well as discriminant, concurrent, and construct validity, in both the original version and the Italian translation [23].

The ABQ is the Addictive Behavior Questionnaire (ABQ), a self-assessment tool that allows the formulation of a diagnosis of substance-related disorder or addiction disorder according to DSM-5 criteria. It is a multidimensional instrument consisting of a Severity Index (SI) and a Seven Domains Addiction Scale (7DAS). These two sections explore different constructs: SI provides a measure of the intensity and frequency of addictive behaviors, while 7DAS explores the vulnerability/predisposition to addiction. The SI investigates 4 areas of Addiction (Substance, Alcohol, Gambling, and Internet), and for each of the 4 areas, there are two separate scores referring to the last 12 months. Section A of the SI assesses addictive behaviors (yes/no), and Section B measures levels of Craving (never/always). For each section, severity levels have been established, ranging from mild to severe. Section B of the SI involves responses on a Likert scale (never/rarely/sometimes/often/always) and assesses the incidence of three distinct factors in the experience of craving: obsessiveness, impulsivity, and compulsivity. As for the 7DAS scale, it explores 7 domains of vulnerability (impulsivity, compulsivity, emotional regulation, reward sensitivity, attachment, metacognitive functioning, stress sensitivity) believed to be risk factors for the development and maintenance of pathological addiction. Each scale consists of 7 items with a 5-point frequency scale (never to always). ABQ demonstrates good psychometric properties, including strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.80–0.90), a stable factorial structure, and satisfactory convergent and discriminant validity [13].

The CTQ-SF is a retrospective questionnaire to evaluate abuse and neglect experiences in childhood. It is a standardized measure to detect a positive history of trauma exposure. CTQ-SF consists of 28 items and five subscales that investigate five different types of childhood trauma: emotional abuse, physical abuse, sexual abuse, emotional neglect, and physical neglect. There is one additional scale to explore the tendency to minimize or deny. Each item is scored on a 5-point frequency scale (1 = never true; 5 = very often true). Scoring for each subscale ranges between 5 and 25. Higher scores indicate more severe exposure to traumatic events. Scoring for the scale minimization/denial ranges between 0 and 3. The scale is valid in the original version and in the Italian translation [33].

The TAS 20, an instrument used to assess alexithymia, is a questionnaire consisting of 20 items and 3 factorial scales: difficulty in identifying feelings and distinguishing between feelings and physical sensation (5 items); difficulty in describing one’s feelings to others (7 items); and externally oriented cognitive style (8 items). Patients can receive a score from 1 to 5 for each item (1 = strongly disagree; 5 = strongly agree). The total alexithymia score is the sum of responses to the 20 items. The TAS-20 score ranges from 20 to 100, with a cut-off for alexithymia corresponding to 60. Values between 52 and 60 are indicative of possible alexithymia, while values below 51 rule out the presence of alexithymia [4].

The BIS-11 is a 30-item self-report questionnaire that measures impulsivity on a 4-point Likert scale. Higher scores for each item indicate higher levels of impulsivity. Twelve items are reverse-scored in order to avoid response sets. Is it possible to identify three factors: attentive impulsiveness, motor impulsiveness, and nonplanning impulsiveness. The global score is obtained by summing these factors. The BIS-11 demonstrates adequate reliability and construct validity in both US [7] and Italian [29] samples.

The CISS is an easy-to-administer multidimensional instrument to measure coping styles (maneuver-oriented, emotion-oriented, or avoidance-oriented). The CISS is made of three scales, each consisting of 16 items. The Task-oriented (T) scale describes efforts aimed at solving a problem through cognitive restructuring. The focus of attention is on the task and design. The Emotion (E) scale describes self-oriented emotional reactions aimed at reducing stress. The scale Avoidance (A) describes cognitive actions and changes enacted to avoid a stressful situation through distraction (task-oriented), or through social diversion (person-oriented) [25].

The SOFAS is a clinician-rated scale to measure impairment in social and occupational areas. It is independent of the psychiatric diagnosis and the severity of the patient’s symptoms. The score ranges from 0 to 100. Higher scores indicate better functioning.

The SAT-P is a self-administered questionnaire consisting of 32 scales that provide a satisfaction profile in daily life and can be considered an indicator of subjective quality of life. The SAT-P considers five different factors: “psychological functioning”; “physical functioning”; “work”; “sleep, food, and free time”; “and social functioning”. The SAT-P asks the patient to evaluate his satisfaction over the last month for each of the 32 life aspects on a 10-cm analog scale ranging from “extremely dissatisfied” to “extremely satisfied”.

The assessment was performed by an investigator (G.L.) who had received training in psychometric instruments before starting the investigation.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were first conducted to characterize the socio-demographic and clinical characteristics of the two groups: (1) patients with BPD + SUD, and (2) patients with single BPD.

A chi-square test was performed to compare categorical variables between the two groups: group 1 (individuals with BPD and comorbid SUD) and group 2 (individuals with BPD without SUD), while analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed to compare continuous variables between the two groups, adjusting for gender.

In order to achieve the second aim of the study, two hierarchical regressions were conducted by entering as independent variables those that showed a significant difference in the between-group comparisons. In both hierarchical regressions, independent variables were grouped into the following nine blocks: 1): socio-demographical variables; 2) psychiatric history; 3) BPD psychopathology; 4) psychopathological vulnerability to develop SUD; 5) traumatic experiences; 6) alexithymia; 7) impulsivity; 8) coping strategies; 9) global functioning, functional milestones, and subjective quality of life. Given the large number of variables relative to the sample size, the total score was included for each psychometric scale rather than the subscale scores. The only exception is the Coping Inventory for Stressful Situations (CISS), in which the subscales describe different coping strategies that must be considered separately. We conducted a hierarchical logistic regression with the presence of SUD as the dependent variable, followed by a hierarchical linear regression with the severity of addictive behaviors as the dependent variable.

When the total score of a rating scale was significantly associated with the outcome (dependent variable) in the last step of a hierarchical regression that significantly increased the model fit, we conducted a further regression analysis entering the subscale scores of that/those rating scale(s) as the independent variables.

Statistical analyses were conducted using the IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), Version 29.0 for Windows (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

The significance level was p ≤ 0.05.

This change was significant (F of the modification –Fm– = 4.250; p = 0.043). An earlier BDP onset was associated with more severe SUD symptoms (St. β = −0.230; p = 0.043). Adding BPD psychopathology (BPDSI) in Step 3 explained an additional 5.8% of the variance in severity of SUD (ABQ-SI) (R2 = 19.9%). This change was significant (Fm = 5.176; p = 0.026). More severe BPD symptoms were associated with SUD severity (St. β = 0.247; p = 0.026). In Step 4, the psychopathological vulnerability for SUD (ABQ-7DAS) was added, resulting in a significant increase in R2 of 10.3% (Fm = 10.32; p = 0.002). With the addition of this independent variable, the association between the severity of SUD and the type and severity of BPD-specific symptoms was no longer significant. Conversely, a vulnerability to SUD was positively associated with the severity of the disorder (St. β = 0.397; p = 0.002).

In Step 5, alexithymia (TAS-20) was added, resulting in a significant increase in R2 of 18.6% (Fm = 25.13; p < 0.001). A more marked alexithymia was associated with more severe SUD symptoms (St. β = 0.620; p = <0.001). Furthermore, when alexithymia is included in the regression model, the association with vulnerability to substance use disorder is no longer significant, nor is the association with gender. With the addition of impulsivity (BIS-11) in Step 6, R2 further significantly increased (R2 = 63.9%), explaining an additional 15.1% of the variance in severity of SUD (Fm = 28.28; p < 0.001). Together, alexithymia and impulsiveness explained about 60% of the variance in the severity of SUD (Adj. R2 = 60.7%). These two symptom dimensions made the largest contributions to SUD severity (alexithymia: St. β = 0.298, p = 0.016; impulsivity: St. β = 0.571, p < 0.001). In Step 7, adding the coping strategies (CISS “Task- oriented coping subscale”) to the model did not significantly increase model fit, resulting in a non-significant difference of explained variance (Fm = 0.821; p = 0.368). Similarly, in Step 8, entering the subjective quality of life (SAT-P) into the model did not significantly increase the explained variance (Fm = 0.193; p = 0.662).

Discussion

The first aim of the present study was to evaluate socio-demographic, clinical, and psychopathological features in patients with BPD comorbid with SUD compared with patients with BPD alone. The second objective of the study was to identify which factors were associated with the presence and/or the severity of SUD within a sample of individuals with BPD.

Some recent studies have compared specific characteristics of patients with BPD and SUD with patients with other personality disorders [65], patients with single SUD [9, 30], patients with PTSD [34], and the general population [48]. The number of studies comparing patients with BPD with or without comorbid SUD is very limited [10, 37].

Overall, our findings confirmed and extended available evidence suggesting the key role of emotional dysfunction and impulsive dyscontrol as shared vulnerability dimensions underlying SUD in comorbidity with BPD [36, 59, 60].

Consistent with prior literature, patients with comorbid BPD and SUD were more frequently male and exhibited a more severe clinical profile. In particular, our results showed that subjects with comorbidity of BPD and SUD, compared with subjects with BPD alone, presented an earlier BPD onset, more severe BPD symptoms, more severe addictive behaviors, higher vulnerability to SUD, higher impulsiveness, more compromised task-oriented coping strategies, more marked alexithymia, and a lower level of subjective well-being.

In our study, gender emerged as a significant factor in the initial steps of the regression models, with female gender associated with both a lower likelihood and lower severity of SUD. This result is in line with existing literature, which indicates that males with BPD are more prone to develop SUD as a dysfunctional coping strategy, whereas females with BPD are more likely to engage in self-injurious behaviors and exhibit depressive symptoms [9, 12, 19, 42, 55, 56]. From our results, female gender could be considered as a protective factor for both the presence and the severity of SUD.

Notably, the effect of gender was no longer significant after including alexithymia in both the logistic and linear regression models. In BPD, unlike in the general population, alexithymia is more pronounced among women [2], and, at the same time, a deficit in emotional awareness is prominent in patients with SUD. Thus, adding alexithymia to the model could offset the impact of gender in having comorbidity of SUD and BPD. This interpretation is supported by evidence indicating that alexithymia plays a transdiagnostic role across psychiatric disorders and may partially account for gender-related differences in symptom expression [2, 41].

An earlier BDP onset was associated with a higher likelihood of comorbid SUD as well as a higher severity of addictive behaviors. Early onset of BPD in patients with SUD is characterized by more severe, impulsive, and treatment-resistant symptoms. This comorbid young population frequently shows higher suicide risk and faster progression to addiction [31]. Early-emerging emotional dysregulation could increase the likelihood of resorting to maladaptive coping strategies, including substance use [18, 60]. Furthermore, early-onset borderline personality disorder has been associated with a more severe and persistent clinical course, particularly in the presence of comorbid conditions [63].

Severity of BPD symptoms was associated with both SUD comorbidity and severity. When adding vulnerability to SUD to the regression models, the association between specific BPD symptom severity and SUD (comorbidity and severity) was no longer significant. Moreover, a higher psychopathological vulnerability for SUD and severity of addictive behaviors was associated, as expected, with a higher probability of co-occurring SUD. A possible explanation is that addiction-specific vulnerability represents a more proximal index of SUD outcomes than BPD symptoms severity per se [26, 53, 60] and that the instrument of evaluation used to identify substance use vulnerability (ABQ- 7DAS) appears to be more suitable for detecting comorbid conditions.

One of the most relevant findings of the present study concerns the role of alexithymia and impulse dyscontrol. In particular, when we consider alexithymia and impulsivity together, they account for a large proportion (60.7%) of the variance in SUD severity. Impulse dyscontrol also surfaced as a key factor in dual diagnosis. This finding suggested that these two psychopathological dimensions (alexithymia and impulsivity) can be considered as core transdiagnostic dimensions. In fact, both dimensions can be underpinned by fronto-limbic dysfunctions, circuits involved in emotion and behavioral regulation [44, 61]. Greater fronto-limbic dysregulation (increased amygdala activity and decreased prefrontal control) is generally associated with higher severity of both BPD and SUD symptoms [42, 43, 62].

Difficulty in identifying, processing, describing, and working with one’s own feelings could lead to maladaptive strategies, including substance abuse, to regulate emotions and relieve stress. Thus, addictive behaviors could represent a mode of coping with unprocessed emotions. This finding is in agreement with the relevant literature reporting high rates of alexithymia in individuals with substance use [32, 45, 46, 58], especially in the young population [24, 49]. In our study, among the different alexithymia constructs, difficulty describing feelings and externally oriented thinking were independently associated with SUD severity. Externally-oriented thinking, defined as a cognitive style characterized by a preference for focusing on external events and objects, may lead individuals to rely on substances as a way of regulating internal states [20]. Our findings indicate that alexithymia is not a significant factor in distinguishing between the presence and absence of SUD, but it is a key factor in explaining the intensity and frequency of addictive behaviors. With regard to impulsivity, our results are consistent with those from previous studies showing higher levels of impulse dyscontrol in BPD patients, who prefer immediate, smaller rewards to higher future benefits [6]. The authors suggested that in patients with comorbid BPD and SUD, impulsivity is the origin of the tendency to pursue short-term compensations through substance use. This powerful mechanism is particularly active at the early stages of substance use, when “positive” reward effects predominate [5, 9, 54], and seems to be a cross-cutting mechanism that contributes to both the onset and maintenance of the two conditions [60]. Moreover, impulsivity significantly contributed to the severity of SUD, confirming its role not only in the initiation but also in the maintenance of addictive behaviors [11, 22].

Among the components of impulsivity, motor impulsivity was found to be independently associated with dual diagnosis. This finding is consistent with previous studies suggesting that a lack of impulse control and a tendency to act without reflection are closely linked to substance use and the risk of relapse [17, 21, 51].

Results related to coping strategies are not easy to describe or comment on. No differences were found between the two groups (BPD + SUD patients and single BPD patients) in the use of emotion-oriented coping strategies and in avoidance-oriented coping strategies. In contrast, the use of a task-oriented coping strategy (a proactive, problem-focused strategy aimed at managing stress by directly addressing its cause) was significantly lower in patients with dual diagnosis. Moreover, difficulties in task-oriented coping strategies were associated with a higher likelihood of SUD in the hierarchical logistic regression, but not with SUD severity in the linear regression. Previous studies have reported that individuals with BPD and SUD show reduced coping flexibility and rely on maladaptive strategies regardless of the type of coping strategy [35, 40]. The fact that, among coping strategies, the impairment of task-oriented coping strategies is what determines a tendency to develop SUD in patients with BPD may be linked to both impulsivity and alexithymia. Indeed, we hypothesize that individuals with a dual diagnosis tend to focus more on external factors and engage in action-oriented behaviors without adequate emotional processing and action planning. In other words, they do not tackle the problem or the cause of stress by formulating a plan to act on it directly. However, they diverge without reflection on substance abuse as an alternative coping strategy.

In the present study, a subjectively negative perception of quality of life was significantly higher in patients with dual diagnosis than in patients with BPD alone. Dissatisfaction with several areas of one’s life may facilitate the tendency to seek detachment through substance misuse, as a maladaptive strategy aimed at temporarily alleviating psychological distress. Conversely, having a dual diagnosis can contribute to dissatisfaction with one’s quality of life. Available evidence reported a significant association between low life satisfaction and the use of substances, suggesting that dissatisfied individuals may turn to substance use as a dysfunctional coping strategy [38, 57, 66]. Subjective perception of quality of life did not significantly contribute to the regression models, indicating that the level of quality of life does not provide information beyond impulsivity, alexithymia, and task-oriented coping strategies in distinguishing between the presence and the severity of SUD for detecting patients with dual diagnosis and more severe addictive behaviors.

The present study suffers from some limitations. The main limitations of the study relate to its cross-sectional design, which does not allow for the establishment of a causal relationship between the observations and the outcomes studied—namely, the presence and severity of SUD—and to the selection of a clinically stable sample with an underrepresentation of males in the group of BPD patients without SUD. The observed results cannot be generalized to populations of patients with acute symptoms, such as, for example, BPD patients experiencing a major depressive episode or SUD patients with a recent withdrawal syndrome. Furthermore, despite the statistical tools used to account for gender imbalance, this phenomenon may still have partially influenced the results, limiting their generalizability to populations of men with DBP and without SUD. Moreover, participants were recruited from a general psychiatry outpatient setting rather than a specialized substance use unit. They were primarily drawn from BPD programs rather than substance abuse services, which may have led to the underrepresentation of individuals with a primary SUD. Finally, the sample should be enlarged to strengthen the validity and reliability of the findings and to enable stratification of BDP patients by the substance(s) used.

Several strengths should also be noted, particularly the focus on comparing two subgroups of patients with BPD with or without SUD and the use of scales and statistical methods that enabled a detailed, granular examination of the contributions of alexithymia, impulsivity, and coping strategies to the presence and severity of SUD in BPD patients.

Conclusions

Results of the present study, in substantial accordance with current literature, suggested several differences between patients with a dual diagnosis (BPD and SUD) and those with BPD alone. Patients with dual diagnosis were more frequently male and exhibited a more severe clinical profile: an earlier BPD onset, more severe BPD symptoms, more severe addictive behaviors, higher vulnerability to SUD, higher levels of alexithymia and impulsiveness, more impaired task-oriented coping strategies, and a lower level of subjective well-being.

Finally, findings highlighted the importance of considering the contribution of multiple factors, particularly impulsivity and alexithymia, to comorbidity with SUD and severity of addictive behaviors in BPD patients. These results have important clinical implications, suggesting that interventions targeting emotional awareness and impulse control might be particularly beneficial in reducing the risk and severity of substance use in BPD subjects.
