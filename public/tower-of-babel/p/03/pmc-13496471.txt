Rare Complications of Chronic Rhinosinusitis Associated With Nasal Polyps and Osseous Metaplasia
Authors: Michael Ritz, Alaina Rubenacker-O’Dell, Faith Olson, Ilya Fonarov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP) is typically a benign condition; however, osseous metaplasia (OM), skull base erosion, and intracranial extension are rare complications, especially in young patients. Reporting such cases is important to increase awareness of unusual presentations of CRSwNP and guide management. We report the case of an 18-year-old male with a history of asthma and prior polypectomy who presented with worsening frontal headaches, subtle visual changes, and right eye proptosis. Imaging revealed extensive pansinusitis with complete frontal sinus opacification, bony erosion of the anterior skull base, and evidence of intracranial and orbital extension. The patient underwent endoscopic sinus surgery, which revealed fibrotic polyps, bone expansion, and purulent intracranial collections. Cultures were obtained, and decompression of the frontal sinuses was accomplished through bilateral Draf IIb procedures. Postoperatively, the patient experienced resolution of proptosis and improvement of symptoms. This case highlights an unusual and aggressive course of CRSwNP complicated by features of OM and emphasizes the importance of early recognition, multidisciplinary management, and close follow-up in preventing neurological and ophthalmic sequelae.

Introduction

Approximately 2–4 % of the general population is affected by chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP).1 The diagnosis of CRSwNP requires symptoms consistent with chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) (including anterior or posterior rhinorrhea, nasal congestion, decreased sense of smell, and/or facial pressure or pain) persisting for at least 12 weeks, in conjunction with objective evidence of sinonasal inflammation and nasal polyps demonstrated on imaging.2 Although generally benign, CRSwNP can cause serious complications when inflammation extends beyond the sinonasal tract, including bone remodeling, skull base erosion, and orbital or intracranial involvement.3,4

Osseous metaplasia (OM) is an uncommon condition where there is the abnormal formation of mature bone in soft tissues.5 While OM is most often described in dermatologic and musculoskeletal contexts, reports of its presence in the paranasal sinuses are limited. When present, it can contribute to unusual bone remodeling in sinonasal diseases.

The combination of CRSwNP with OM carries diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, as bone formation can complicate surgery, worsen obstruction, and increase infection risk. Importantly, reviews of osteitis and bone remodeling in CRS emphasize that bone changes are increasingly recognized as part of the disease spectrum, often correlating with disease severity and recurrence.6

We present a case of CRSwNP in an adolescent complicated by suspected OM, skull base erosion, and intracranial extension. The report reflects the importance of early recognition, multidisciplinary management, and follow-up in preventing serious morbidity.

Case report

An 18-year-old male with asthma and a history of CRSwNP, status post endoscopic sinus surgery 2 years prior, presented with progressively worsening headaches, mild visual changes, and 2 months of right eye proptosis. The patient had been self-treating with over-the-counter analgesics without significant relief. After the first endoscopic sinus surgery, his mother was advised that additional surgery would be required, but the patient was lost to follow-up and had not seen an ear, nose, and throat (ENT) specialist since then.

Vital signs on presentation were unremarkable. Physical examination revealed right eye proptosis without purulent discharge or periorbital edema. Extraocular movements were intact with normal conjunctiva. Facial sensation (V1–V3) was intact bilaterally. There was pain with palpation of the frontal and maxillary sinuses. Hearing was intact bilaterally, and the remainder of the physical examination was unremarkable.

Upon admission, laboratory results included an elevated eosinophil percentage at 8.5 % (normal 0.0–5.0 %), corresponding to an absolute eosinophil count of 0.76 × 103/μL (normal 0.10–0.50 × 103/μL), consistent with underlying allergic inflammation commonly associated with CRS and asthma. The total white blood cell count was elevated at 11.8 ×103/μL (normal 4.0–10.5 × 103/μL) on admission.

Computed tomography (CT) imaging (Fig. 1) showed severe pansinusitis with complete opacification and marked expansion of the frontal sinuses, resulting in intracranial extension and mass effect on the frontal lobes. The right frontal sinus was significantly enlarged, protruding into the right orbit. Dehiscence of the right orbital rim and roof produced proptosis with displacement of the superior rectus and superior oblique muscles. There was significant bony erosion of the frontal sinus walls and destruction of the anterior skull base. Magnetic resonance imaging scan (MRI) of the head (Fig. 2) confirmed inflammatory extension into the intracranial space and orbit. MRI of the face (Fig. 3) showed bilateral maxillary and frontal sinus opacification, with associated ethmoid sinus involvement. Both frontal sinuses were enlarged with thinning of the sinus walls and deviation of the interfrontal septum.

The patient underwent urgent endoscopic sinus surgery. Intraoperative findings included bilateral nasal polyps involving the medial and lateral maxillary, ethmoid, sphenoid, and frontal sinuses. Osteoneogenesis was noted over the ethmoid air cells and frontal outflow tracts. OM was suspected based on intraoperative bone formation; histologic confirmation was not obtained. Fibrotic, vascular polyps were excised. Bilateral intracranial purulent masses were identified and decompressed gradually to reduce the risk of increased intracranial pressure, resulting in resolution of right eye proptosis. Bilateral Draf IIb frontal sinusotomies were performed (right: 10 mm lateral x 12 mm anteroposterior; left: 12 mm lateral x 16 mm anteroposterior). There was no evidence of cerebrospinal fluid leakage (CSF) with 40 mmHg Valsalva. Cultures obtained from the left frontal sinus, deep left frontal sinus, and left intracranial abscess were negative for acid-fast bacilli, yeasts, and fungi.

Postoperatively, the patient exhibited elevated neutrophil counts, likely secondary to corticosteroid administration. Total white blood cell count increased to 13.8 × 103/μL (normal 4.0–10.5 × 103/μL), likely reflecting a combination of postoperative inflammatory response and systemic inflammation. The patient was started on oral amoxicillin-clavulanate and a prednisone taper. He was discharged in stable condition and scheduled for outpatient follow-up in the ENT clinic.

Discussion

Our patient presented an aggressive case of CRSwNP complicated by OM. The presence of OM was suggested by intraoperative findings of bone growth over the ethmoid air cells and frontal outflow tracts. The abnormal bone growth, combined with chronic inflammation and polyp proliferation, contributed to progressive obstruction of the sinus ostia, which worsened sinus ventilation and drainage. Bone remodeling of the frontal sinus walls has been seen in CRS.7 In this case, imaging demonstrated remodeling and dehiscence of the frontal sinus walls and anterior skull base, likely secondary to long-standing inflammation and severe polyp growth.

While often treated separately, a close relationship exists between management of upper and lower airway disease in patients with CRSwNP and asthma. The recommended initial therapy includes topical corticosteroids, nasal saline irrigation, and occasionally antibiotics. Here, failure of medical therapies and disease severity necessitated surgical intervention. A hemi-Lothrop procedure, Draf IIb, was utilized to safely address frontal sinus involvement while preserving the natural nasal passageways.8 While surgery alleviated the immediate pathology, nasal polyps have a high recurrence rate. Patients with CRSwNP and asthma often require more surgeries than those with CRSwNP alone.9

While conservative management is often sufficient in CRSwNP, the severity and extent of disease in this case required surgical intervention to prevent further neurological and ophthalmic complications. 10 The absence of a CSF leak supported timely intervention. Continued outpatient monitoring and consideration of biologic therapy, such as dupilumab, are important given the high risk of nasal polyp recurrence in CRSwNP.11 Due to the chronic nature and severity of the condition, the patient may require additional surgeries as polyps redevelop.

In patients with CRSwNP and asthma, it is also important to consider associated conditions such as allergic rhinitis and aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease, which may influence symptom burden and recurrence risk.9 Our patient demonstrated peripheral eosinophilia, although serum IgE and additional immunologic markers were not obtained during hospitalization. In adolescents presenting with extensive polyposis and complications like skull base erosion, alternative etiologies, such as allergic fungal rhinosinusitis (AFRS), cystic fibrosis, primary ciliary dyskinesia, and immune dysfunction, should also be considered. AFRS was unlikely in this case, given negative fungal cultures.10 Furthermore, the patient lacked clinical features suggestive of genetic or immunologic disorders, making these alternative diagnoses less likely.

Conclusion

This case illustrates a rare and severe presentation of CRSwNP complicated by suspected OM, skull base erosion, and intracranial extension. It highlights the need for early recognition, comprehensive imaging, and multidisciplinary management to prevent potentially serious neurological and ophthalmic complications. Awareness of such an atypical presentation can guide timely surgical intervention and long-term follow-up in patients with aggressive sinonasal disease.
