Multifocal intracranial hemorrhage on emergency brain CT in a child with end-stage nephrotic syndrome
Authors: Amrou Baidri, Yousra Boukabous, Mohammed Taloubi, Marwane El Arabi, Mouataz Aabalou, Samia Berrichi, Houssam Bkiyar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpontaneous intracranial hemorrhage is a rare complication of pediatric nephrotic syndrome. We report a 10-year-old girl with steroid-resistant impure nephrotic syndrome complicated by end-stage renal disease on chronic hemodialysis, presenting with acute impairment of consciousness and hypertensive emergency (182/112 mmHg). Coagulation tests were entirely normal despite active hemorrhage, consistent with uremic qualitative platelet dysfunction not detected by conventional screening. Emergency noncontrast brain CT demonstrated multifocal intraparenchymal hematomas, extensive intraventricular hemorrhage with triventricular flooding, acute obstructive hydrocephalus, a 9 mm midline shift, basal cistern effacement, and posterior fossa hematoma with fourth ventricle collapse. Management included orotracheal intubation, intravenous nicardipine, hypertonic saline, and external ventricular drain placement. The patient died on day 4. This case illustrates that normal coagulation tests should not delay emergency brain CT in uremic children with acute neurological deterioration, and that the CT pattern of multifocal hemorrhage with intraventricular involvement carries a poor prognosis.Keywords: Pediatric nephrotic syndrome, Hemorrhagic stroke, End-stage renal disease, Hypertensive emergency, Uremic platelet dysfunction, Emergency computed tomography

Introduction

Nephrotic syndrome is one of the most prevalent glomerular disorders in childhood, encompassing pure and impure forms, the latter carrying a less favorable renal prognosis due to associated hematuria, hypertension, or impaired renal function [1,2]. Steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome—defined by failure to achieve remission after 4 weeks of daily corticosteroids—carries a substantial risk of progression to end-stage renal disease, with 10-year dialysis-free survival rates as low as 43% in refractory cases [3]. While nephrotic syndrome classically predisposes to thromboembolic complications through urinary loss of anticoagulants and heightened platelet reactivity [4], progression to end-stage renal disease paradoxically shifts the hemostatic balance: uremic toxin accumulation induces qualitative platelet dysfunction and endothelial injury, creating a dual prothrombotic and hemorrhagic state—further amplified by systemic anticoagulation during hemodialysis sessions—even in the presence of entirely normal conventional coagulation parameters [5,6]. Cerebrovascular events in this population predominantly manifest as ischemic stroke or cerebral venous thrombosis [4,7], while spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage remains exceptionally rare, with only isolated cases reported in the literature [8]. Its pathogenesis implicates hypertensive cerebrovascular injury, uremic hemostatic dysfunction, chronic anemia, and immunosuppressive therapy-related vascular fragility—with a landmark Mendelian randomization study published in JAMA Neurology (2022) confirming that chronic kidney disease independently doubles the risk of spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage [5,6,9]. Emergency noncontrast brain computed tomography remains the cornerstone diagnostic modality for suspected intracranial hemorrhage, given its high sensitivity for acute bleeding and critical role in guiding neurosurgical decision-making [10]. Herein, we report a fatal case of multifocal hemorrhagic stroke in a child with steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome and end-stage renal disease on chronic hemodialysis, highlighting the essential role of emergency neuroimaging and advocating for increased clinical awareness in this high-risk population.

Case presentation

A 10-year-old girl with a 6-year history of corticosteroid-resistant impure nephrotic syndrome—first diagnosed at age 4 years and complicated by multiple immunosuppressive-refractory relapses and progressive renal deterioration ultimately culminating in end-stage renal disease requiring chronic hemodialysis 3 sessions per week—was admitted to the pediatric intensive care unit for acute and rapidly progressive impairment of consciousness. The last hemodialysis session had been performed 48 hours prior to admission.

On admission, the patient presented in critical neurological condition with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 6/15 and right-sided anisocoria, raising immediate concern for intracranial hypertension. Hemodynamic evaluation documented a hypertensive emergency (182/112 mmHg) with tachycardia (130 beats/min). Pulmonary examination revealed bilateral mid-field crackles consistent with significant volume overload in the context of end-stage renal disease, with oxygen saturation maintained at 98% on nasal oxygen at 4 L/min. Multiple ecchymoses distributed over the trunk and extremities suggested underlying hemostatic impairment, consistent with uremic platelet dysfunction despite the absence of any identifiable coagulation abnormality.

Laboratory investigations summarized in Table 1 revealed a normal coagulation profile in the setting of active intracranial hemorrhage, reflecting the inability of standard hemostatic tests to detect uremic qualitative platelet dysfunction. Biochemical analysis confirmed severe azotemia (creatinine 61.07 mg/L, urea 2.38 g/L), hypoproteinemia (total protein 46 g/L), and hypoalbuminemia (albumin 28 g/L), consistent with active nephrotic syndrome and advanced uremic derangement. Blood gas analysis demonstrated severe metabolic acidosis (pH 7.21, HCO₃⁻ 14 mmol/L, base excess −12 mmol/L), consistent with uremic acidosis compounded by hemodynamic compromise.

Emergency noncontrast brain CT was performed within 45 minutes of admission, immediately following initial stabilization. Imaging revealed extensive multifocal intracranial hemorrhagic findings (Fig. 1A-C). The dominant lesion was a heterogeneous hyperdense right frontoparietal intraparenchymal hematoma measuring 39 × 26 mm with a craniocaudal extent of 43 mm, yielding an estimated volume of approximately 22 mL by the ABC/2 method, surrounded by perilesional edema and small satellite hematomas. A second hematoma was identified in the left cerebellar hemisphere, measuring 39 × 24 mm with a craniocaudal extent of 23 mm, corresponding to approximately 11 mL, also surrounded by perilesional edema. Both lesions were consistent with hypertensive small vessel rupture across multiple vascular territories. Extensive intraventricular hemorrhage with triventricular flooding—sparing the fourth ventricle, which was collapsed by the posterior fossa hematoma—resulted in acute obstructive hydrocephalus with marked dilatation of the lateral and third ventricles and transependymal resorption. The Evans index was 0.38, confirming significant ventricular enlargement. A 9 mm leftward midline shift was consistent with subfalcine herniation; diffuse cerebral edema with effacement of the cortical sulci was noted predominantly over the right hemisphere. Basal cistern effacement was present, consistent with elevated intracranial pressure. Transtentorial, uncal, and tonsillar herniation were absent on CT. The posterior fossa hematoma exerted direct mass effect on the brainstem with collapse of the fourth ventricle. Chest CT additionally demonstrated bilateral posterior basal consolidations with air bronchograms consistent with aspiration pneumonia (Fig. 2).

Immediate neurocritical care was instituted. Airway protection was secured by rapid sequence orotracheal intubation followed by mechanical ventilation with deep sedoanalgesia. Intravenous nicardipine was titrated to achieve a controlled systolic blood pressure reduction of 15%-25% within the first hour. Given the absence of residual renal function and pre-existing volume overload, hypertonic saline (3% NaCl, 2-3 mL/kg boluses over 15-20 minutes, repeatable every 4-6 hours) was selected as hyperosmolar therapy in preference to mannitol, which carries significant risks of plasma accumulation and paradoxical rebound intracranial hypertension in anuric patients. Urgent external ventricular drain placement was performed within 2 hours of CT acquisition; the EVD opening pressure was markedly elevated at 42 mmHg. One packed red blood cell unit was transfused during the hemodialysis session, with a post-transfusion hemoglobin of 8.5 g/dL. No platelet transfusion was administered. Desmopressin was unavailable at our institution; its use is recommended in analogous future cases given its established efficacy in correcting uremic platelet dysfunction. Intravenous methylprednisolone was maintained as adjunct immunosuppressive therapy, and hemodialysis was resumed per the established schedule.

Despite maximal multimodal neurocritical care, the clinical trajectory was one of unrelenting neurological deterioration with no meaningful improvement in responsiveness. On day 3, bilateral fixed and dilated pupils confirmed irreversible brainstem failure and refractory transtentorial herniation. On day 4, refractory hemodynamic instability culminated in cardiac arrest unresponsive to cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Death was attributed to multifocal hemorrhagic stroke complicated by massive intraventricular hemorrhage, acute obstructive hydrocephalus, and irreversible brain herniation.

Discussion

This case illustrates a rare neurological complication arising from the convergence of multiple hemorrhagic risk factors in a child with steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome progressing to end-stage renal disease. While cerebrovascular complications in pediatric nephrotic syndrome are predominantly thromboembolic, progression to end-stage renal disease shifts the hemostatic balance toward a hemorrhagic state: uremic toxin accumulation induces qualitative platelet dysfunction and endothelial injury, rendering advanced chronic kidney disease patients susceptible to spontaneous intracranial bleeding despite normal conventional coagulation parameters [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. A Mendelian randomization study published in JAMA Neurology (2022) confirmed that chronic kidney disease independently doubles the risk of spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage [9].

The most radiologically instructive finding in this case is the CT-laboratory dissociation: entirely normal conventional coagulation parameters in the setting of active intracranial hemorrhage. Standard coagulation tests evaluate only secondary hemostasis and are insensitive to uremic qualitative platelet dysfunction, which results from defects in platelet membrane receptors, impaired thromboxane synthesis, and storage pool depletion [6,11]. Recent thromboelastography studies have confirmed that end-stage renal disease patients harbor significant hemostatic abnormalities invisible to conventional screening [12]. A normal coagulation panel should not delay or defer emergency CT acquisition in uremic children with acute neurological deterioration. Desmopressin and red blood cell transfusion to optimize rheological conditions merit consideration in analogous future cases [6].

Several alternative etiologies of multifocal intracranial hemorrhage were considered and systematically excluded. Vascular malformation was unlikely given the multifocal bicompartmental distribution across distinct vascular territories and the absence of prior hemorrhagic episodes. Central nervous system vasculitis was excluded in the absence of fever, meningism, or systemic inflammatory signs. Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, while a recognized complication of nephrotic syndrome, characteristically produces venous infarction in a nonarterial distribution rather than the deep hemorrhagic pattern observed here [4,7]. Dialysis anticoagulation-related bleeding was excluded by the 48-hour interval since the last hemodialysis session and the entirely normal coagulation profile. Posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome was considered but excluded, as it characteristically produces symmetric posterior cortical vasogenic edema rather than multifocal deep intraparenchymal hematomas with posterior fossa involvement. Hypertensive small vessel rupture in the context of uremic microangiopathy therefore remains the most plausible unifying mechanism, supported by the severe azotemia and the multifocal CT distribution consistent with diffuse small vessel disease. Definitive confirmation would have required advanced vascular imaging, which was precluded by the severity of the clinical presentation [13].

Severe hypertension (182/112 mmHg) was a major contributing factor for hemorrhagic rupture. In children with end-stage renal disease, blood pressure dysregulation is multifactorial—driven by interdialytic volume overload, as evidenced by the bilateral crackles and the 48-hour interval since the last dialysis session, renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system activation, and endothelial dysfunction [9]. The multifocal distribution of hematomas across supratentorial and infratentorial compartments is consistent with diffuse hypertensive small vessel disease affecting multiple vascular territories simultaneously, concordant with the concept of intracranial hemorrhage as an acute manifestation of cerebral small vessel disease [13].

Emergency noncontrast brain CT was the cornerstone diagnostic tool and its radiological contribution extends beyond hemorrhage identification. The multicompartmental pattern—right frontoparietal hematoma (39 × 26 mm, ∼22 mL) and left cerebellar hematoma (39 × 24 mm, ∼11 mL) across distinct vascular territories—reflects diffuse hypertensive microangiopathy, a pattern rarely documented in pediatric nephrotic syndrome. The CT-laboratory dissociation documented in this case carries a direct clinical implication: a normal coagulation profile should not delay or defer emergency CT in uremic children with acute neurological deterioration. The overall CT severity pattern—triventricular intraventricular hemorrhage, 9 mm midline shift, Evans index 0.38, basal cistern effacement, posterior fossa hematoma with brainstem compression—directly informed critical therapeutic decisions: EVD placement within 2 hours of CT acquisition and selection of hypertonic saline over mannitol. The 2022 AHA/ASA guidelines confirm noncontrast CT as the first-line modality for suspected intracranial hemorrhage, and this case reinforces that recommendation in a rare pediatric context [10].

Despite maximal neurocritical care, the clinical course was fatal, consistent with the high mortality reported for large-volume multifocal intracranial hemorrhage with extensive intraventricular hemorrhage in end-stage renal disease [14]. A multicenter study published in 2023 confirmed that end-stage renal disease patients with intracranial hemorrhage exhibit significantly higher in-hospital mortality than nonrenal failure counterparts, attributable to chronic anemia, uremic platelet dysfunction facilitating hematoma expansion, and metabolic complications aggravating secondary cerebral edema [14]. This case advocates for proactive cerebrovascular surveillance in children with steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome and advanced renal disease: systematic interdialytic blood pressure optimization and a low threshold for emergency CT at the onset of any new neurological symptom remain key components of prevention and early diagnosis in this high-risk population [9,10].

Conclusion

This case highlights spontaneous multifocal intracranial hemorrhage as a rare complication of steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome progressing to end-stage renal disease, driven by the convergence of hypertensive cerebrovascular injury, uremic platelet dysfunction, and chronic anemia. A normal coagulation profile should not exclude intracranial hemorrhage in uremic children, as standard hemostatic tests are insensitive to uremic qualitative platelet dysfunction. Emergency noncontrast brain CT proved essential in establishing the diagnosis, quantifying the hemorrhagic burden, and guiding neurosurgical decision-making. Despite maximal neurocritical care, the outcome was fatal, underscoring the poor prognosis associated with this CT pattern in end-stage renal disease. Clinicians should maintain a low threshold for urgent neuroimaging in any child with steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome and end-stage renal disease presenting with acute neurological deterioration or hypertensive emergency. Further multicenter registries are warranted to better characterize the epidemiology and optimal management of this rare complication.

Teaching point

In children with steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome and end-stage renal disease, spontaneous multifocal intracranial hemorrhage may occur despite a normal coagulation profile, driven by the interplay of hypertensive small vessel injury and uremic platelet dysfunction. Emergency noncontrast brain CT is the first-line imaging tool for prompt diagnosis and neurosurgical guidance in any child with advanced nephrotic syndrome presenting with acute neurological deterioration and hypertensive emergency.
