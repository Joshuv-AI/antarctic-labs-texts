Application of machine learning models for predicting depression among older adults with non-communicable diseases in India
Authors: Kanchan Yadav, Dechenla Tshering Bhutia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Depression among older adults is a critical public health issue, particularly when coexisting with non-communicable diseases (NCDs). In India, where population ageing and NCDs burden are rising rapidly, scalable data-driven approaches are needed to identify at-risk individuals. Using data from the Longitudinal Ageing Study in India (LASI) Wave 1 (2017–2018; N = 58,467), the study evaluated eight supervised machine learning models including random forest, decision tree, logistic regression, SVM, KNN, naïve bayes, neural network and ridge classifier, for predicting depression among older adults. Model performance was assessed using a 70/30 train-test split and stratified 10-fold cross-validation. Performance evaluation metrics included AUROC, PR-AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1-score and interpretability via SHAP. Random forest outperformed all other models, achieving an AUROC of 0.996 and an accuracy of 95.6% with F1- score of 0.954 demonstrating excellent discrimination and calibration. Decision tree followed closely with AUROC of 0.915, accuracy of 91.5% and F1- score of 0.908. Key predictors of depression included poor sleep, age, BMI, IADL limitations, MPCE quintile, religion, smoking, education and physical inactivity. SHAP values validated the clinical plausibility of these features. A reduced-feature model using the top 12 predictors retained high accuracy, enhancing interpretability. The findings demonstrate the utility of ML models, particularly random forest, for identifying depression risk in older adults. The integration of interpretable techniques, SHAP along with Information Gain enhances clinical relevance. These results have potential implications for scalable screening strategies and policy-driven interventions in geriatric mental health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-18053-3.

Introduction

Depression is a major public health challenge, recognized in both the Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) due to its extensive impact on mental, social and physical well-being1. Globally, more than 300 million individuals suffer from depression, with older adults disproportionately affected2. Late-life depression is associated with increased mortality3, dementia4, diminished quality of life5 and economic burdens approximating US$326 billion annually6. As the population aged 60 years and above is projected to double by 2050, the mental health burden is expected to escalate, especially in low and middle-income countries (LMICs)7.

India represents a significant portion of the global mental health burden, accounting for 15% of cases, second only to China8,9. The prevalence of late-life depression is particularly high in LMICs, though it varies across countries10,11. Multiple meta-analyses have identified chronic non-communicable diseases (NCDs), functional limitations, poor social support and life stressors as common correlates of depression in older adults12,13. NCDs like diabetes, cardiovascular disease (CVD), cancer and chronic respiratory diseases are often comorbid with depression, necessitating integrated models of care14–16. WHO survey spanning 60 countries showed that up to 23% of individuals with NCDs also suffer from depression17. In India, rising NCDs prevalence, coupled with rapid population ageing, underscores the urgency for scalable, data-driven mental health interventions18.

Traditional parametric approaches often fail to account for non-linearities and high-dimensional interactions among risk factors. Machine learning (ML) models offer a promising alternative, particularly for uncovering complex dependencies and capturing the indirect influence of predictors19,20. Recent studies have applied ML techniques such as random forests, deep neural networks and gradient boosting to predict depression and identify risk factors across large cohorts. A previous study19 used random forests on UK data to identify physical illness and cognitive decline as key depression predictors and another study21 applied similar methods to over 112,000 UK adults, highlighting the role of social support and physical activity. Despite these advances, most ML-based studies have been conducted in high-income countries. In India, studies integrating ML with nationally representative mental health data are scarce. Notably, prior research has not comprehensively explored socio-relational, behavioural and functional predictors of depression among older adults with NCDs using a machine learning framework.

Recent advances in optimization and deep learning further reinforce the value of ML in healthcare. For instance, ensemble deep learning frameworks22–24 and hyperparameter tuning via metaheuristic optimization25–27 have demonstrated high accuracy in predictions. These methods, though effective, are yet to be widely applied to geriatric depression prediction in LMICs like India. Numerous international studies have investigated the relationship between mental health, NCDs and predictive modelling through machine learning. For instance, Shatte et al.28 examined ML applications in the realm of mental health, discovering that models like random forest and support vector machine effectively predict depression by utilizing clinical and sensor data. Chekroud et al.29 employed ML techniques to forecast responses to antidepressant treatments in a large dataset from the US, underscoring the significance of predictive algorithms in influencing mental health outcomes. Barak-Corren et al.30 created predictive models for suicidal behaviour based on electronic health records, revealing impressive discriminatory performance. Meanwhile, Gold et al.31 emphasized the importance of multimorbidity as a crucial predictor of depression, especially among older adults. These studies underscore the increasing interest in merging ML with comorbidity analysis to better comprehend and predict mental health risks in ageing populations.

Conversely, within Indian research, studies into mental health among elderly are expanding, yet they remain constrained by methodological innovation and the application of nationally representative data. For instance, Sahoo et al.32 utilized traditional regression models to identify predictors of depression among elderly using community-based survey data, but they did not incorporate ML models or consider multimorbidity. Arokiasamy et al.33 introduced the LASI baseline data, which showed correlation of depression, yet did not prioritize predictive modelling or ML techniques. Bhakta et al.34 delved into few ML models for depression prediction using primary data and Farooq et al.35 investigated depression prediction using a primary dataset derived from clinical questionnaires, likely based on a localized or clinical sample with a limited sample size, that lacked national representativeness and did not emphasize NCDs comorbidity. Similarly, there are other studies that shows gap with ML models along with non-representative sample, narrow focus on single models, lack of multimorbidity consideration and limited public health relevance36–39.

Despite these advances, the Indian literature remains limited in scope and methodological depth. Existing studies often rely on small, localized samples35,40 or traditional regression analyses without machine learning integration32. Moreover, most have not employed nationally representative datasets like LASI, nor have they explicitly addressed multimorbidity by modelling depression risk among older adults with multiple NCDs. From a methodological standpoint, no prior Indian study has systematically combined information gain for feature selection with SHAP-based model interpretability across a wide range of ML classifiers, moreover, this level of methodological breadth and dataset consistency is largely absent in prior Indian ML studies on mental health.

To address these gaps, this study applies multiple ML models to the LASI Wave 1 dataset to predict depression among older adults with NCDs. To enhance interpretability, this study uniquely integrates Information Gain method along with SHAP, where information gain method to assess feature importance and incorporates SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to visualize model-agnostic interpretability at both global and individual prediction levels41–43. These techniques bridge the gap between predictive accuracy and clinical applicability, while providing interpretable feature insights for policy and intervention design.

Moreover, this study pioneers the use of reduced-feature modelling by training models only on the most relevant features identified through Information Gain, thereby minimizing overfitting and enhancing generalizability, a critical factor when dealing with complex health datasets such as LASI. Robustness is ensured by benchmarking the performance (accuracy, AUROC, precision, recall and F1-score) of multiple classifiers i.e, decision tree, random forest, logistic regression, SVM, KNN, naïve bayes, ridge classifier and neural network, under a consistent evaluation framework.

This work systematically integrates multiple NCDs comorbidities, depression and various machine learning approaches using nationally representative Indian data (LASI Wave-1), covering over 72,000+ respondents across the country. It aims to develop efficient and interpretable depression risk prediction models by combining demographic, behavioural and functional predictors with feature selection and explainability techniques.

To contextualize the novelty and scope of this study, supplementary Table S1 provides a comparison of recent global and Indian studies that employed ML for depression prediction. These studies vary by country, data source, model type and most do not specifically focus on older adults with major NCDs using nationally representative dataset.

Materials and methods

Data source, sampling procedure and sample size

Data derived from the initial phase of the Longitudinal Ageing Study in India (LASI), carried out from 2017 to 2018, was utilized in the examination. The collaborative research for LASI was undertaken by the International Institute for Population Sciences (IIPS), T. H. Chan School of Public Health at Harvard University and the University of Southern California, launched under the supervision of the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, Government of India44. LASI functions as an exhaustive information source at a national level regarding the health, financial and societal dimensions, also the impacts of population ageing on individuals aged 45 years and older, along with their spouses within the same household44. This endeavour encompasses older individuals (aged 45 years and above) dwelling throughout India.

The survey methodology implemented by LASI covered a multi-stage stratified areas probability cluster sampling framework, comprising three stages in rural regions and four stages in urban areas. The initial phase entailed the selection of a Primary Sampling Unit (PSU) by the surveyor from each state/union territory (UT), succeeded by the identification of a village (in rural regions) or ward (in urban zones) in the subsequent stage. Ultimately, households were designated by the surveyor in rural environments, while in urban areas, Census Enumeration Blocks (CEB) were arbitrarily chosen from each urban area and households were subsequently identified from the selected CEB44.

A consolidated dataset, merging information from both individual and biomarker datasets, was utilized for the analysis. The individual dataset comprised data on 72,250 individuals, whereas the biomarker dataset included information on 65,900 individuals. Upon merging these datasets and concentrating specifically on older adults, the ultimate analysis retained data on 58,467 individuals aged 45 years and older. Additional insights into the sampling methodologies and sample sizes are available in the LASI India Report44.

Data accessibility and ethical considerations

The information gathered through LASI is stored in a public database at IIPS, requiring registration and authorization for valid research initiatives. The ethical clearance was granted by the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) , supplemented by further approval from the Institutional Review Board at IIPS for the research protocol. Individually, participants were informed about the aims of the survey, also the privacy and security measures established for healthcare evaluations. LASI distributed consent forms at both household and individual levels, following the protocols for safeguarding Human Subjects44.

Description of study-variables

The outcome variable considered in this research was major depression, derived using the CES-D 10-item scale. A score of ≥ 4 indicated the presence of depressive symptoms. In the dataset, depression was encoded as a binary variable where “0” denotes older adults with depression and “1” indicates those without depression. The encoding was maintained for consistency with the original LASI dataset structure. During the analysis, for all tables, model performance plots (ROC and PR curves) and interpretability analyses (e.g., SHAP), the class labels “0” represents the positive class (i.e., individuals with depression) and “1” denotes the absence of depression.

The selection of these variables was grounded in the extensive examination of self-reported depression among older individuals. The predictor (independent) variables under consideration in this research encompassed age (45 years and above), gender, place of residence, marital status, level of education, religion, MPCE quintile, four major NCDs (diabetes, cancer, chronic respiratory diseases and CVD), ADL status, mobility challenges, IADL limitations, poor sleep, self-reported health (SRH), smoking habits, alcohol consumption, physical activity, social activity status and BMI (body mass index).

Data pre-processing

Effective data pre-processing is foundational to building robust and reliable ML models, especially in healthcare analytics where raw datasets often contain noise, missing values and unstructured variables45. In the present study, data pre-processing was carried out meticulously before training the predictive models to ensure data consistency, reduce bias and enhance algorithmic performance.

The first phase involved cleaning the dataset by eliminating duplicate entries and handling missing values to maintain data quality. Subsequently, all categorical variables were encoded into numeric formats suitable for ML models. Label encoding was employed for binary and ordinal features such as gender, physical activity status and poor sleep, while nominal features with multiple levels (e.g., religion, marital status and MPCE quintile) were transformed using one-hot encoding. This step ensured that categorical attributes were represented without imposing false ordinal relationships46. To prepare numerical variables for ML models sensitive to feature scaling, such as KNN, SVM and neural networks, min-max normalization was applied to continuous features like age and body mass index (BMI). This rescaled their values to a uniform [0,1] range, thereby avoiding bias introduced by differing variable magnitudes47. Crucially, all transformation steps i.e., including encoding, normalization and oversampling were performed exclusively on the training set after partitioning the data into a train-test split. This was done to avoid data leakage and ensure that the test set remained a valid and independent benchmark for evaluating model performance48.

A major challenge encountered during model development was the pronounced class imbalance in the outcome variable. Specifically, 51,243 individuals reported no depression, while only 7,224 reported depressive symptoms, reflecting a skewed distribution that could bias classifiers toward the majority class. To mitigate this issue, the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) was employed49. SMOTE addresses imbalance by generating synthetic examples of the minority class through interpolation between feature space neighbours. For each selected instance from the minority class, its k-nearest neighbours are identified, and synthetic points are created along the lines joining these neighbours, thereby increasing minority representation without duplicating data50.

Despite its advantages, SMOTE is not without limitations. It may generate unrealistic data points that introduce class overlap or noise, particularly when minority instances are not well-separated in the feature space51. To assess its appropriateness, alternative resampling methods were considered. Cost-sensitive learning, which adjusts model training to penalize misclassification of minority instances, was deemed impractical due to the difficulty of defining exact misclassification costs in large, population-level datasets such as LASI. Focal loss, originally developed for deep neural networks, shifts learning focus to hard to classify cases by down-weighting easy samples52, but is not readily compatible with tree-based or linear models. Given these constraints and the structured nature of the dataset, SMOTE was selected as the most interpretable and model-agnostic method for balancing class distributions. Importantly, oversampling via SMOTE was confined to the training dataset to preserve the natural imbalance in the test set. This approach enabled realistic performance evaluation by simulating actual prevalence scenarios and avoiding artificial improvements in accuracy or precision due to oversampled test data53.

Model building

In this study, the process involved univariate and multivariable logistic regression, followed by the application and evaluation of eight supervised ML models to capture complex and non-linear patterns in the data. Initially, univariate logistic regression was employed to assess the crude associations between each predictor and depression, providing preliminary insights into significant risk factors. Subsequently, multivariable logistic regression was applied to adjust for confounding variables and examine the independent effects of each factor. This served as a benchmark and supported interpretability while identifying potentially relevant predictors for more advanced modelling stages.

To train and test the predictive ML models, the dataset was randomly partitioned into training (70%) and testing (30%) subsets. Importantly, the SMOTE was applied only to the training data to address the substantial class imbalance where non-depressed cases outnumbered depressed cases, while ensuring the test set remained untouched to simulate real-world conditions. This strategy mitigated overfitting and prevented inflated performance metrics arising from information leakage.

In addition to the 70/30 split, a stratified 10-fold cross-validation technique was implemented to reinforce the robustness of the findings and minimize performance bias due to a single random split. This approach ensured proportional representation of the minority class in each fold and facilitated generalizable evaluation by averaging metrics across folds. Furthermore, 95% confidence intervals were calculated for accuracy and AUROC to provide a rigorous estimation of model stability and precision.

Model development was guided by a review of prior literature54–57 on machine learning applications related to mental health prediction. Eight ML models were selected to encompass a range of model complexities and learning strategies i.e., Decision Tree, Random Forest, Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machine (SVM), K-Nearest Neighbours (KNN), Naïve Bayes, Neural Network and Ridge Classifier. There is provided a detailed rationale of selected eight ML models below:

Decision tree (DT)

The decision tree algorithm is extensively utilized within the healthcare sector for label classification. This method is considered a reliable and commonly applied classifier for calculating conditional probabilities58,59. Decision trees are transformed into binary trees to aid in classification tasks, with each end node of the tree indicating a specific class label60. Utilizing a classifier can help in identifying the fundamental patterns within data instances and in generating decision rules.

A decision tree splits the data recursively by selecting features and thresholds to maximize information gain or reduce impurity (e.g., Gini index, entropy)61,62.

Random forest (RF)

Random forest is an ensemble learning method that constructs multiple decision trees from bootstrapped samples of the dataset and aggregates their predictions via majority voting (classification) or averaging (regression)63. The prediction of depression often entails intricate interplays, a challenge that RF addresses by consolidating the results of diverse decision trees, thereby enhancing the general resilience and precision of the model. Each tree is built using a random subset of predictors at each split to reduce correlation among trees. The splitting criterion is based on Gini impurity or entropy, where the best feature at each node is selected to maximize the reduction in node impurity62,64,65. This architecture enhances generalizability and reduces overfitting, especially in high-dimensional, nonlinear datasets like this study.

Support vector machine (SVM)

The support vector machine is widely recognized for its capacity to manage high-dimensional data and ascertain the most advantageous hyperplane boundaries. SVM was selected due to its proficiency in detecting intricate correlations within the dataset and establishing distinct decision boundaries, thereby potentially capturing nonlinear associations among the predictors69.

K nearest neighbour (KNN)

The K nearest neighbour algorithm is known for its versatility in handling regression and classification tasks. When applied to predicting mental health disorders like depression, KNN leverages the shared traits among neighbouring instances to make informed decisions. This approach proves to be particularly effective in datasets featuring distinct local clusters with varying levels of depression prevalence70.

Naïve Bayes (NB)

Naive bayes is a straightforward algorithm in machine learning which relies on the Bayes theorem, under the crucial assumption that the attributes are conditionally independent given the class. The competitive classification accuracy offered by naive bayes has been well-documented71. Its widespread application can be attributed to its computational efficiency and advantageous characteristics72. Functioning as a foundational model, naïve bayes presents a basic yet efficient method for predicting mental health conditions such as depression.

Neural network (NN)

Artificial intelligence (AI), deep learning, machine learning and pattern recognition are all made possible by neural networks, which mimic the behaviour of the human brain. The input, output and one or more hidden layers are contained in the node layer of an artificial neural network (ANN)73. Nodes are interconnected and possess corresponding thresholds and weights. A node gets activated and starts sending data to the next layer of the network if its output goes above the specified threshold value.

Ridge classifier

Ridge classifier is a classification algorithm designed to handle classification problems. It is derived from ridge regression. Although the ridge classifier is a good substitute for ridge regression in situations involving categorical outcomes, ridge regression is frequently used in regression scenarios with continuous outcome variables74. For machine learning applications, the ridge classifier is a useful tool, particularly when dealing with tasks that require the classification of features that may be correlated. Because of its regularization properties, it is a reliable choice for maintaining a simple model structure and avoiding overfitting. The ridge classifier works well in scenarios where classes can be clearly distinguished from one another because of its linear decision borders.

These models were chosen to accommodate both linear and non-linear dependencies and enable performance comparison across interpretable and high-capacity models. This approach guarantees a comprehensive scrutiny of the dataset’s attributes and facilitates a deeper comprehension of the variables impacting mental health disorders like depression.

To optimize each model, hyperparameter tuning was performed exclusively on the training data using grid search cv. For random forest, the number of trees (n_estimators), maximum tree depth (max_depth) and minimum samples per split (min_samples_leaf) were tuned. SVM parameters, including kernel type, regularization constant (C) and kernel coefficient (gamma), were optimized to balance bias and variance. KNN’s performance was refined by selecting the optimal value of k (number of neighbours), while neural network tuning involved adjusting the number of hidden layers, neurons per layer and learning rate. The neural network model was implemented using Keras with TensorFlow backend. The architecture comprised an input layer with 21 features, followed by two hidden layers, first with 64 neurons and second with 32 neurons. Both hidden layers used the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function to introduce non-linearity and avoid vanishing gradient issues75. The output layer consisted of a single neuron with a sigmoid activation function to perform binary classification (depressed vs. not-depressed). The model was trained for 100 epochs with a batch size of 32. Binary cross-entropy was used as the loss function and the Adam optimizer was employed for gradient-based optimization due to its efficiency and adaptability in sparse gradients76. Early stopping with a patience of 10 epochs was used to prevent overfitting. This shallow feedforward neural network architecture was chosen to strike a balance between performance and overfitting on structured health survey data, where deeper networks tend to offer diminishing returns77. More complex architectures (e.g., deep networks or CNNs) offer minimal gains in such settings and often increase the risk of overfitting or reduced interpretability. Simpler models such as logistic regression, naïve bayes and ridge classifier were implemented using default parameters to preserve their transparency and interpretability. GridSearchCV was selected over RandomizedSearchCV to ensure thorough exploration of the relatively small and interpretable parameter space. Manual tuning was avoided to reduce subjectivity and inconsistency in model selection. All tuning was performed solely on the training set to prevent information leakage into the validation set.

To further improve both performance and interpretability, a feature selection step was included. These features were then ranked using information gain derived from a random forest model, identifying the top 12 features most predictive of depression i.e., poor sleep, BMI, age, MPCE quintile, IADL limitations, physical activity, gender, education, place of residence, smoking, religion and diabetes. These variables were used to train a second set of models to evaluate the feasibility of reduced-feature models without substantial sacrifice in accuracy.

This comprehensive model-building strategy, combining statistical models, advanced machine learning, hyperparameter tuning, SMOTE-based class balancing and embedded feature selection ensured that the predictive classifiers developed in this study are both statistically rigorous and practically deployable for identifying older adults at heightened risk of depression.

Performance evaluation

A rigorous and methodologically sound performance evaluation framework was adopted in this study which is necessary to develop an effective ML model. This section outlines the procedures and criteria used to evaluate model performance while safeguarding against data leakage, overfitting and inflated performance metrics.

Performance metrics were selected based on their relevance to imbalanced classification problems and their ability to capture different dimensions of model effectiveness. These included measures such as accuracy, sensitivity (recall), specificity, precision, F1 score, negative predictive value (NPV), the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and the area under the precision-recall curve (PR-AUC). Together, these metrics provided a holistic view of model discrimination, calibration and classification performance.

To ensure the statistical soundness of the evaluation, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for key metrics such as accuracy and AUROC. This provided an understanding of the variability around central estimates and facilitated comparison across different models. In addition, Cohen’s kappa and Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) were employed to measure the degree of agreement between predicted and actual class labels beyond chance, and to assess overall classification quality, respectively. Balanced accuracy, which averages sensitivity and specificity, was particularly emphasized to reflect the model’s ability to correctly identify both classes in the presence of data imbalance.

To further validate whether observed performance differences across models were statistically significant, McNemar’s test was employed for pairwise comparisons. This non-parametric test evaluates the significance of disagreement in misclassification patterns between two models and is especially useful in assessing whether improved metrics reflect true performance advantages rather than random variation. Significant p-values indicated robust superiority of one model over another in terms of classification outcomes.

To complement numerical performance assessments, graphical techniques such as receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and precision-recall (PR) curves were also plotted. ROC curves offered visual insight into trade-offs between true positive and false positive rates, while PR curves highlighted model behaviour under class imbalance, particularly the ability to maintain high precision across various recall thresholds. These plots served as intuitive tools for comparing classifiers in terms of both discrimination and predictive reliability.

Finally, feature importance was explored using random forest-based information gain ranking and SHAP values. SHAP provided both global and local interpretability of model predictions by quantifying the marginal contribution of each feature to individual predictions. This helped validate whether top-performing models derived their success from clinically and theoretically meaningful features and supported transparency in model decision-making.

Overall, this comprehensive performance evaluation strategy with combining multiple metrics, statistical testing, stratified cross-validation and interpretability methods ensured a robust and reliable comparison of ML models in the context of predicting depression among older adults with NCDs in India. The descriptive analysis and data preparation were executed using the STATA-17 software. The implementation of ML models was carried out utilizing Jupyter Notebook (inbuilt Python) software.

Results

Table 1 illustrates both univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses presenting the unadjusted odds ratio, adjusted odds ratio, 95% confidence interval (CI) and p-value for various factors associated with self-reported depression among individuals aged 45 years and older with NCDs. Each factor is compared to a reference category and the statistical significance of the odds ratios is determined by the p-value.

In terms of the age of participants, the adjusted odds of experiencing depression were elevated in older individuals aged 56–66, 67–77 and 78–88 years when compared to the reference category (aged 45–55 years) with a statistically significant p-value of < 0.001. In the context of gender classification, the adjusted odds of experiencing depression were 1.098 (95% CI: 1.029–1.17) for females, with a p-value of < 0.001 in comparison to males. Regarding the place of residence, there was slightly higher adjusted odds ratio which stood at 1.057 (95% CI: 0.996–1.121) for older adults living in urban areas, with a p-value of < 0.001 suggesting that self-reported depression chances are elevated in urban areas compared to rural areas. In the case of marital status among older adults, the adjusted odds ratio was 1.352 (95% CI: 1.082–1.689) for married older individuals, with a p-value of < 0.001 indicating that self-reported depression probabilities are heightened compared to the reference group. With regards to educational attainment, those who belonged to the “ever attended school” category exhibited slightly higher adjusted odds ratio was 1.081 (95% CI: 1.02–1.147), with a p-value of < 0.001, compared to reference category. The direction of association remained consistent in both univariate and adjusted models. In terms of religious affiliation, the odds of depression were higher among older adults practicing Islam and other faiths when compared to those following Hinduism. The adjusted odds ratio was 1.148 (95% CI: 1.059–1.246) for older adults adhering to Islam and 1.19 (95% CI: 1.101–1.285) for those following other religions, with a p-value of < 0.001. In relation to MPCE quintile, the odds of depression were elevated for older adults in the middle-class household category in contrast to those in the poor-class household category. The adjusted odds ratio was 1.084 (95% CI: 1.011–1.162) for older adults in the “middle” category, all with a p-value of < 0.001. For older adults diagnosed with diabetes, the (lower) adjusted odds ratio stood at 0.906 (95% CI: 0.84–0.978), exhibiting a significant p-value of < 0.001 when compared to peers without diabetes. Similarly, older adults diagnosed with cancer had lower adjusted odds ratio being 0.742 (95% CI: 0.567–0.972), and a p-value of < 0.001 compared to those without cancer. In the case of chronic respiratory diseases, older adults diagnosed with such conditions showed lower adjusted odds ratio was 0.864 (95% CI: 0.782–0.955), with a p-value of < 0.001 against those without chronic respiratory diseases. Older adults facing multiple ADL challenges had three times higher odds of depression than those without such challenges, while the adjusted odds ratio was 1.581 (95% CI: 1.233–2.028), with a significant p-value of < 0.001. Moreover, older adults dealing with mobility issues exhibited higher odds of depression, whether facing single or multiple mobility problems, in comparison to the reference category. The adjusted odds ratio was 1.216 (95% CI: 1.025–1.444) for single mobility and 1.827 (95% CI: 1.579–2.113) for multiple mobility, with a p-value of < 0.001 against older adults without mobility issues. Furthermore, for older adults experiencing challenges in IADL, the odds of depression were notably higher compared to those without such difficulties, with the adjusted odds ratio at 1.214 (95% CI: 1.147–1.284) and a p-value of < 0.001. In the realm of sleep difficulties, older adults had lower adjusted odds ratio i.e., 0.426 (95% CI: 0.402–0.451), with a p-value of < 0.001 from the reference group. When examining self-rated health among older adults, those who perceived their health as “poor” had low adjusted odds ratio was 0.801 (95% CI: 0.759–0.845), with a p-value of < 0.001. Concerning smoking habits among older adults, the odds of depression were elevated in smokers compared to non-smokers, with an adjusted odds ratio of 1.113 (95% CI: 1.048–1.182) and a p-value of < 0.001 relative to the reference category. Similarly, in the context of alcohol consumption among older adults, individuals who consumed alcohol had higher odds of depression than non-consumers. The adjusted odds ratio was 1.147 (95% CI: 1.06–1.24), with a p-value of < 0.001 compared to the reference category. Exploring the body mass index status among older adults, the lower adjusted odds ratio for underweight older adults was 0.938 (95% CI: 0.877–1.005), all with a p-value of < 0.001.

The results underscore a multifaceted relationship between demographic, health, functional and behavioural aspects linked to depression among older Indian adults. The findings highlight the importance of tailored mental health strategies that consider both clinical and socio-behavioural influences on depression in later life.

The random forest model demonstrated the highest predictive performance across almost all metrics. It achieved the highest accuracy of 95.6% (95% CI: 0.954–0.958), AUROC of 0.996 (95% CI: 0.995–0.997), F1 score of 0.954, precision of 0.994 and Cohen’s Kappa of 0.912, indicating excellent agreement beyond chance. Moreover, the PR-AUC value of 0.994 suggests superior performance in precision-recall space, important in datasets with class imbalance. The balanced accuracy was also highest i.e, 0.956, showing the model’s effectiveness in correctly classifying both positive and negative cases. These findings support the random forest as the most robust classifier in this study. Moreover, the decision tree model also performed well, with a balanced accuracy of 0.915, AUROC of 0.915 (95% CI: 0.912–0.918) and accuracy of 91.5% (95% CI: 0.912–0.918). Discussion

This study sought to identify and predict depression among older adults with non-communicable diseases, leveraging advanced machine learning techniques using data from the Longitudinal Ageing Study in India (LASI) Wave 1. The study’s findings demonstrate that both socio-demographic and health-related variables significantly affect depression risk, and ML models especially ensemble techniques are highly effective for predictive modelling in complex health datasets.

The multivariable logistic regression analysis highlighted a range of sociodemographic and health-related factors associated with self-reported depression. Age was a significant factor, with older age groups (especially 67–77 years) demonstrating increased odds of depression, consistent with findings from studies that attribute this to age-related functional decline and social isolation78,79. Females were also significantly associated with higher depression risk (AOR = 1.098), corroborating extensive literature suggesting that biological, hormonal and social determinants disproportionately affect women’s mental health in later life80,81. Urban residency, although only marginally significant, indicated elevated depression risk, which may stem from lifestyle stressors and reduced social cohesion in urban environments82. Interestingly, married individuals had higher adjusted odds of depression (AOR = 1.352), which may appear counterintuitive but aligns with emerging findings in Indian contexts where marital responsibilities, particularly caregiving or financial dependency, could increase stress in older adults83. Among religions, belief in Islam and those adhering to other religions exhibited higher odds of depression than Hindus. This may reflect socio-cultural and minority stress experiences, a well-documented phenomenon in psychiatric epidemiology84. Educational attainment, often considered a protective factor, was associated with slightly increased adjusted odds of depression (AOR = 1.081), possibly due to greater awareness and self-reporting biases among the educated ones85. Interestingly, certain non-communicable conditions, such as diabetes and cancer, showed protective effects against depression in the models, contrary to global evidence86. This inverse association might reflect coping mechanisms, support structures or underreporting of depressive symptoms in the context of severe physical illness among older adults87. While this may seem counterintuitive, similar findings have been reported in prior research. For instance, individuals diagnosed with chronic diseases may receive more frequent medical attention, structured care or social support, which can mitigate depressive symptoms88. Additionally, survivorship effects or underreporting bias among severely ill individuals may attenuate observed associations89. It is also possible that such individuals adopt lifestyle changes or coping mechanisms post-diagnosis, contributing to mental resilience. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. It may reflect unmeasured confounding, measurement limitations or cultural underreporting of mental health symptoms among physically ill patients. This study recommends that future work explore interaction terms between chronic conditions and care utilization or stratify by duration/severity of illness to uncover more nuanced patterns. However, functional impairments such as multiple ADLs, mobility limitations and IADL challenges significantly raised depression odds, consistent with the theory of disablement and its psychological toll90. Behavioural health variables such as smoking, alcohol consumption and physical inactivity were also associated with elevated depression risk. These findings are aligned with global epidemiological studies linking substance use and sedentary lifestyles to poorer mental health outcomes91,92. Notably, sleep disturbances were the most robust predictor, underscoring their bidirectional relationship with depression93. Poor self-rated health was similarly associated with increased depressive symptoms, reaffirming the predictive value of subjective health perceptions94.

To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the earliest studies to apply machine learning techniques for predicting depression among older adults with NCDs in India using nationally representative LASI Wave 1 data. While previous research has employed ML to examine mental health outcomes, this study uniquely combines population-level data, focus on depression and the intersection with chronic disease conditions. The evaluation of eight ML models across both 70/30 train-test split and stratified 10-fold cross-validation strategies consistently ranked random forest as the top performer, followed closely by decision tree and KNN. Random forest achieved outstanding AUROC (0.996 on 70/30 split; 0.955 on stratified 10-fold CV), F1 score (0.954 and 0.898), and PR-AUC (0.994 and 0.945), indicating its robustness in handling nonlinear interactions and class imbalance, a common issue in mental health datasets95.

Although the random forest model demonstrated high performance (AUROC: 0.996, Precision: 0.994) in 70/30 split, recognizing that these metrics may indicate overfitting due to SMOTE oversampling. Hence, this study conducted additional validation using stratified 10-fold cross-validation and tested on the original imbalanced test data. These validations yielded more conservative metrics (AUROC = 0.955 (95% CI: 0.953-0.956)), thus enhancing confidence in the model’s generalizability. The consistent high performance of random forest across cross-validation folds supports its robustness and predictive reliability for this application.

Decision trees in 70/30 split, while slightly less accurate, offer a balance between interpretability and predictive power. Their strong F1 score (0.908) and balanced accuracy (0.915) affirm their potential utility in clinical areas where model transparency is crucial96. KNN also demonstrated solid performance, particularly in specificity (0.929 in 70/30 split) and PR-AUC (0.878 in stratified 10-fold CV), making it suitable for reducing false positives. However, its relatively lower sensitivity suggests it may miss some true depression cases, a drawback in screening tools intended for public health use97. Conversely, models such as logistic regression, SVM, naïve Bayes and ridge classifier consistently underperformed. Their AUROC values remained under 0.65 and Cohen’s kappa values were below 0.20, indicating weak agreement beyond chance. These models struggled with specificity and failed to capture the complex, nonlinear relationships between predictors and depression, reinforcing the limitations of linear classifiers in high-dimensional health data98.

Though decision trees, random forest and logistic regression operate through algorithmic logic and layered transformations rather than explicit formulas, this study ensured interpretability by presenting feature importance plots, SHAP summary and AUROC/PR-AUC curves that visually illustrate model reasoning and performance. The mathematical representations and visual tools complement each other in conveying both model transparency and predictive strength99.

However, there is a need for a deeper theoretical discussion of the ML methodology, particularly random forest. The random forest algorithm combines the predictions of multiple decision trees using bootstrap aggregation (bagging) to improve generalization and reduce variance. Each tree is trained on a bootstrapped subset of the data, and during node splitting, features are selected based on criteria such as gini impurity or information gain (entropy) to maximize class purity64,100. In this study, the superior performance of random forest can be attributed to the model’s ability to handle nonlinear interactions and high-dimensional data with robustness against overfitting. Unlike single decision tree, the ensemble approach in random forest offers more stability in variable importance rankings, even in the presence of correlated predictors, which are common in public health datasets101.

To ascertain the accuracy and generalizability of the machine learning models, this study employed both a 70/30 train-test split and a stratified 10-fold cross-validation approach. The cross-validation framework allowed for more reliable performance estimates, particularly for an imbalanced dataset, and reinforced the consistent predictive superiority of the random forest model, as this study revealed that the random forest model exhibits superior performance relative to alternative predictive frameworks in predicting factors associated with depression102. Importantly, to confirm that random forest’s superior performance was not due to random chance or overfitting, this study performed statistical comparisons with other models. McNemar’s test revealed significant disagreement in misclassification patterns between random forest and logistic regression and further demonstrated random forest’s superior discriminative ability103.

The SHAP summary plots and random forest feature importance scores both reinforced the central role of sleep quality, BMI, age, MPCE quintile and functional limitations (ADL/IADL/mobility) in predicting depression. These variables are not only statistically significant but also actionable, making them ideal targets for screening and intervention. To further enhance interpretability, this study explored SHAP as complementary method to random forest’s built-in feature importance. SHAP values are grounded in cooperative game theory and provide local (instance-level) and global (feature-level) explanations by distributing contributions fairly across features104. Compared to model-specific methods, SHAP provides a more robust and interpretable basis for understanding model behaviour. Although LIME was considered, SHAP was chosen for its consistency and enhanced visualization capabilities, particularly in ensemble tree models such as random forest99. While random forest’s internal feature importance ranks predictors based on reduction in gini impurity, SHAP provides consistent and additive explanations, offering deeper insight into how features affect predictions across all cases. The findings from SHAP analysis largely aligned with the Information Gain rankings, reaffirming the influence of predictors such as BMI, age, poor sleep, IADL and physical activity. Furthermore, individual-level SHAP values allowed to visualize how specific feature values increased or decreased the likelihood of depression for each respondent. This theoretical augmentation not only strengthens the empirical validity of random forest’s performance but also ensures methodological transparency for health researchers and policymakers.

The top predictors identified via SHAP and Information Gain such as poor sleep, IADL limitations, BMI and self-rated health, are directly relevant to clinical and public health practice. Poor sleep is a modifiable, self-reportable symptom that has been consistently linked with late-life depression105. Functional limitations in instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) are early markers of disability and psychological distress, and can be used in routine geriatric assessments to flag those at risk106. Self-rated health, though subjective, has been shown to be a robust predictor of mortality and mental health decline, and is easily collectable in surveys or clinical encounters107. These findings align with the goals of the National Programme for Health Care of the Elderly (NPHCE) in India and suggest that low-cost, scalable tools could integrate these indicators into mental health screening protocols.

Furthermore, while comparing between model interpretability and predictive performance trade-offs, particularly random forest and decision tree where random forest yielded the highest accuracy (AUROC = 0.996), its ensemble structure can be opaque for real-time decision-making. In contrast, decision tree, though slightly less accuracy (AUROC = 0.915) offers transparent, rule-based predictions that are easier to communicate to clinicians and implement in resource-constrained settings. This highlights a trade-off between accuracy and interpretability108, where model selection should depend on the deployment context, black-box models for back-end decision support and interpretable models for real-time clinical decisions.

Moreover, there exists some assumptions and limitations associated with the interpretation of ORs, particularly in the presence of multicollinearity and confounding variables67. Odds ratios from logistic regression can be misleading if the model includes highly correlated predictors, as this may inflate standard errors and obscure the true magnitude or direction of associations109. Additionally, logistic regression assumes a log-linear relationship between independent variables and the log-odds of the outcome, which may not always hold true in complex real-world data110. In contrast, machine learning models such as random forest do not require such assumptions and are capable of capturing non-linear relationships and higher-order interactions64.

For instance, poor sleep and physical inactivity were identified as protective factors in the logistic regression model (shows in Table 1), as indicated by adjusted odds ratios less than one. However, both variables emerged as significant risk factors in the machine learning models, particularly in SHAP analysis and feature importance rankings, highlighting their strong positive influence on depression prediction. This discrepancy underscores the ability of machine learning techniques to capture complex, non-linear relationships that may be overlooked or misrepresented in traditional regression frameworks.

To improve the consistency and interpretability across methods, this study compared the directionality and strength of associations from multivariable logistic regression (i.e., adjusted ORs) with the variable importance scores derived from random forest using the Information Gain criterion. Notably, key variables such as BMI, age, MPCE quintile, IADL, physical activity and sleep quality consistently emerged as top predictors in both approaches, thereby reinforcing the robustness of the findings. While ML feature importance does not directly correspond to ORs, their convergence in identifying leading predictors lends credibility to the model outputs. This dual-method analysis enhances interpretability and enables a more nuanced understanding of the variables influencing depression risk.

Although SMOTE significantly improved model sensitivity and overall performance, it is important to acknowledge its limitations. SMOTE’s synthetic sampling process may inadvertently introduce noise or class overlap, potentially affecting generalization performance, especially in sparse feature spaces49. Alternative strategies such as cost-sensitive learning or focal loss offer promising directions, but their practical implementation in structured epidemiological datasets remains complex. Future research should explore hybrid methods that combine oversampling with cost-sensitive optimization or focal loss under ensemble frameworks to enhance predictive reliability52.

The machine learning model developed in this study holds substantial potential for integration into India’s public health infrastructure, particularly within the frameworks of the National Programme for Health Care of the Elderly (NPHCE)111,112, the National Mental Health Programme (NMHP)113 and the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission (ABDM)114. By enabling community health workers and primary care providers to identify older adults at risk for depression during routine geriatric screenings, the model directly supports NMHP’s mandate of integrating mental health into primary care and promoting early detection. The reduced-feature version, which relies on key interpretable predictors such as self-rated health, BMI, sleep patterns, IADL limitations and multimorbidity, can be embedded into mobile health (mHealth) applications or electronic health records (EHRs) to provide automated, real-time screening alerts and decision support for clinicians and care coordinators. While such platforms have demonstrated effectiveness in mental health screening globally115,116, successful deployment in low-resource settings requires consideration of infrastructure constraints, data privacy safeguards and the digital literacy of end-users. Optimizing the model for offline use, ensuring transparency and explainability and addressing potential algorithmic biases are critical to building user trust and ensuring equitable outcomes117,118. With these considerations, the model offers a scalable, technology-enabled approach to bridging the treatment gap in geriatric mental health and improving early intervention for older adults in India and other low and middle-income countries (LMICs).

In essence, future work should explore longitudinal prediction of depression, integration with electronic health records and real-world application of reduced-feature screening models in communities. Additionally, including qualitative insights or context-specific variables (e.g., caregiving burden, social networks) may further enhance model accuracy and relevance.

Strengths and limitations

The principle strength of this study is to apply and evaluate ML methods, especially ensemble techniques, for predicting depression among older adults with NCDs in India using nationally representative LASI Wave 1 data. This study systematically compared eight different ML models across multiple metrics, offering an in-depth performance evaluation. Moreover, the integration of adjusted odds ratios with ML-based feature importance and SHAP values provided a balanced view of both statistical associations and algorithmic relevance. Meanwhile, use of both 70/30 data splits and stratified 10-fold cross-validation enhanced the reliability and generalizability of the findings. The identification of key modifiable factors like sleep quality and physical activity supports the development of targeted mental health interventions in ageing populations. Because, no study is complete without its limitations, hence this study has also some limitations i.e., there might be reporting biases linked to self-disclosed chronic ailments. The data collection involved a questionnaire administered by trained interviewers, indicating that forthcoming research could benefit from utilizing clinical data or structured diagnostic interviews carried out by mental health professionals to enhance comprehension of the association between chronic conditions and depression. Moreover, few conditions like diabetes and cancer had lower depression odds, which may reflect biases in self-reporting, survivorship, or access to care rather than true protective effects. These findings highlight the need for stratified or interaction-based analyses in future studies to disentangle clinical and social modifiers of depression risk among those with chronic diseases. An important limitation of this study concerns the class imbalance inherent in the dataset. Although SMOTE was applied to mitigate this issue, the technique may introduce unrealistic synthetic samples that increase class overlap or noise, particularly when minority class observations are not well-separated in the feature space49,53. To reduce potential bias, oversampling was restricted to the training dataset, thereby preserving the natural imbalance in the test set and enabling a more realistic evaluation of model performance. Although, the LASI Wave-1 dataset is cross-sectional, which limits the ability to establish causal relationships between predictors and depression. Longitudinal data would be necessary to determine the temporal dynamics of mental health outcomes. As with all observational studies, there is a possibility of unmeasured confounding. Factors such as genetic vulnerability, environmental exposures, or antidepressant medication use were not included and may have influenced depression outcomes. Moreover, the models were trained on structured survey data, thus, their applicability to real-time clinical or EHR data remains to be explored. Lastly, the generalizability of the models in this study may be limited. The dataset represents older Indian adults and may not reflect populations in other sociocultural or clinical contexts. Further validation on international or clinical datasets is required to confirm external applicability.

Conclusion

In a nutshell, this study provides a comprehensive evaluation of depression risk among older adults with NCDs in India using a novel integration of modern machine learning methods on LASI Wave-1 data. The analysis underscores the utility of combining multivariable logistic regression with advanced ensemble models like random forest to capture both the statistical associations and complex patterns inherent in population-level health data. The study highlights key predictors such as poor sleep, BMI, self-rated health and functional limitations as consistently significant across both statistical and machine learning frameworks. Among the ML models evaluated, random forest emerged as the most reliable and generalizable approach for predicting depression risk. This model demonstrated superior performance across multiple metrics, including AUROC, F1 score and PR-AUC, validated through both 70/30 split and stratified 10-fold cross-validation. Furthermore, interpretability was enhanced through the application of SHAP values and feature importance rankings, enabling transparent insight into model decisions and variable impacts. By comparing algorithmic importance metrics with traditional odds ratios, this study bridged the gap between model accuracy and interpretability, which is crucial for implementation in clinical and public health areas. The evidence gathered not only supports the deployment of ML models for mental health risk stratification but also points to the feasibility of building parsimonious, explainable and efficient tools for large-scale screening and intervention among older adults in India.
