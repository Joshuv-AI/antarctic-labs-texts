Cytomegalovirus enteritis in a patient with AKI after type B aortic dissection surgery: a case report
Authors: Tongkai Ge, Junqiang Qiu, Zhenzhong Wang, Heng Zuo, Kan Zhou, Changjiang Yu, Huanlei Huang, Tucheng Sun, Yingkai Xiao

Background

The incidence of cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection among immunocompromised intensive care unit (ICU) patients ranges from 3.4% to 62.1%, and CMV infection or disease has been identified as an independent risk factor for in-hospital mortality [1]. Surgical trauma, stress response, and acute kidney injury can all affect the immune system, resulting in an immunosuppressive state of the body [2, 3]. The colon is the most frequently involved site of gastrointestinal CMV infection, accounting for 55%, followed by the esophagus and stomach (40% combined). Small bowel involvement is relatively rare, accounting for only 4.3% [4]. Colitis represents the most frequent clinical manifestation of gastrointestinal CMV disease, whereas surgical intervention is more commonly required in cases of GI perforation and hemorrhage secondary to CMV enteritis [4, 5]. Reported in-hospital and overall mortality rates for CMV enteritis are 27.8% and 38.9%, respectively [6]. We herein report a case of severe hemorrhage caused by cytomegalovirus enteritis complicated with acute renal failure, who underwent exploratory laparotomy, antiviral therapy combined with intravenous immunoglobulin therapy and was successfully discharged.

Case description

A 62-year-old man presented to our hospital on May 2, 2025, for management of a descending aortic dissection (originating at the T12 level with a tear width of approximately 14 mm). His past medical history included endovascular stent grafting distal to the left subclavian artery (XJZDMZ3434200, Medtronic®) performed in October 2024 due to aortic intramural hematoma. The current admission was prompted by a Type B aortic dissection (TBAD). Physical examination on admission revealed a blood pressure of 120–130/70–80 mmHg, a pulse rate of 60–80 beats/min, a body weight of 64 kg, and a height of 178 cm. Preoperative laboratory parameters were within normal limits(including negative HIV Ag/Ab, a lymphocyte count of 1.25 × 10⁹/L and a lymphocyte ratio of 23.5%), and his comorbidities were limited to hypertension and atrial fibrillation. He was neither an organ transplant recipient nor had he received immunosuppressive therapy.

On May 8, an interventional procedure was performed by the cardiology team. During the attempt to deploy a VALIANT 3,434,200 (Medtronic®) stent, incomplete deployment occurred, and the device could not be retrieved. Consequently, emergency open surgical repair was undertaken by cardiac surgeons, consisting of abdominal aortic replacement (Terumo®) and a bypass from the aortic graft to the celiac trunk, bilateral renal arteries, and superior mesenteric artery.

Approximately 20 h postoperatively, neurological assessment revealed paraplegia, with muscle strength graded MRC 0/5 in the lower limbs and hypoesthesia below the L5 level. no neurological improvement was observed thereafter.

On postoperative day (POD) 1, the patient’s serum creatinine level rose to 291.96 µmol/L, and urine output dropped to 10 ml/hour for three consecutive hours, progressing to anuria. A diagnosis of Acute Kidney Injury Stage 3 was established according to KDIGO guidelines. Continuous venovenous hemofiltration (CVVH) was initiated under nephrology guidance. Throughout CVVH, peak and trough serum creatinine levels were 742.83 µmol/L and 311.55 µmol/L, respectively. Urine output recovered to 730 mL (> 0.3 mL/kg/h) on POD 25 and 800 mL (> 0.5 mL/kg/h) on POD 26. Anticoagulation with low molecular weight heparin was started at 0.3 mL twice daily from POD 18 and increased to 0.6 mL twice daily on POD 25. The lymphocyte count was 0.57 × 10⁹/L and the lymphocyte ratio was 5.1% On POD 1. From the first to the 22nd postoperative day, the lymphocyte count and ratio remained below the lower limit of the normal range (lymphocyte count < 1.10 × 10⁹/L, lymphocyte ratio < 20%).

On POD 27, the patient was transferred to another hospital for CVVH twice per week. On POD 38, he developed recurrent hematochezia and was transferred back to our hospital on POD 39. The main symptom was hematochezia, accompanied by diarrhea, without abdominal pain, fever, or other discomfort. Laboratory studies revealed severe anemia, with a hemoglobin (Hb) level of 35 g/L and a red blood cell (RBC) count of 1.18 × 10¹²/L, accompanied by elevated serum creatinine (455 µmol/L), decreased lymphocyte count and ratio (1.0 × 10⁹/L, 14.5%), and prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT, 48.9 s). Contrast-enhanced abdominal CT demonstrated active small bowel bleeding (Fig. 1). A multidisciplinary team (MDT) was convened, including gastrointestinal surgeons, interventional radiologists, and gastroenterologists.

Image of abdominal contrast-enhanced CT. Red arrow pointed the small bowel bleeding

On POD 40, digital subtraction angiography (DSA) of the celiac artery was performed by an interventional radiologist, which showed no evidence of contrast extravasation (Supplement 1). Despite transfusion of 8 units of cryoprecipitate, 1 unit of platelets, 600 mL of fresh frozen plasma, and 7 units of RBCs, laboratory parameters remained critically deranged, with Hb 30 g/L, RBC count 0.93 × 10¹²/L, and APTT 54.8 s. Given the persistence of suspected small bowel bleeding, an emergency exploratory laparotomy was performed. Intraoperative examination revealed marked wall thickening of the ileum, approximately 110 cm proximal to the ileocecal valve. Upon enterotomy, a large volume of clotted blood was evacuated, and an actively bleeding mucosal lesion was identified and oversewn with 3 − 0 Vicryl® sutures for hemostasis (Fig. 2A and B). Double-balloon enteroscopy (DBE) was then introduced through the enterotomy site, and no additional mucosal lesions or bleeding sources were identified by this examination. The affected ileal segment was resected and sent for histopathological evaluation. On the same day, the patient received 10 units of cryoprecipitate, 1 unit of platelets, 600 mL of fresh frozen plasma, and 10 units of RBCs. Subsequent laboratory testing showed Hb 49 g/L, RBC count 1.54 × 10¹²/L, APTT 46.1 s, Total Globulin 18 g, lymphocyte count 0.50 × 10⁹/L, lymphocyte ratio 8.4%, and creatinine 413.26 µmol/L.

A-B showed the resected ileum

On POD 47, histopathological examination of the resected specimen showed that the ileal villous structure was mostly absent; the surface mucosa showed loss and erosion, accompanied by inflammatory fibrinous exudation; the stroma presented small vascular proliferation with congestion, accompanied by infiltration of numerous inflammatory cells, including lymphocytes, plasma cells, eosinophils and neutrophils. The results of special staining showed CMV positivity, and those of immunohistochemistry (IHC) were also positive for CMV (Fig.3A and B). On the same day, the patient’s blood was drawn and quantitative determination of cytomegalovirus (CMV) DNA in whole blood was performed. The report results obtained 5 days later showed a CMV-DNA level of 16,000 copies/ml. Accordingly, the MDT recommended initiating ganciclovir combined with intravenous immunoglobulin therapy, and adjusting the dose of ganciclovir according to renal function. On POD 47, the patient was administered ganciclovir 2.5 mg/kg and intravenous immunoglobulin 10 g once daily, with a serum creatinine level of 159.48 µmol/L. By POD 52, the serum creatinine decreased to 88.67 µmol/L, the dose of ganciclovir was increased to 4.5 mg/kg, and intravenous immunoglobulin was discontinued simultaneously. The patient’s condition stabilized, and he was discharged from our center on POD 54.

A-B are the images of pathological examination. They indicated the most of the villous structure of the small intestine disappeared with inflammatory exudation and much inflammatory cell infiltration in the stroma

Discussion

Cytomegalovirus (CMV) can be detected in both immunocompetent and immunocompromised individuals; however, symptomatic CMV disease occurs more frequently in immunocompromised hosts. A standardized quantitative definition of immunocompromised status remains lacking. Previous studies have commonly defined immunocompromised patients as those with AIDS, solid organ transplantation, and/or exposure to chemotherapy, systemic corticosteroids, or other immunosuppressive agents[7].

Notably, identified risk factors for CMV enteritis and colitis include chronic kidney disease and elevated serum creatinine levels [6, 8]. Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a frequent complication following Type B aortic dissection (TBAD), with reported incidences of stage 3 AKI and renal replacement therapy reaching 25.8% and 6.1%, respectively [9]. Importantly, renal impairment can induce T-cell exhaustion and dysfunction, leading to dysregulated immune homeostasis and diminished pathogen clearance [10]. In the late stage of acute kidney injury (AKI), it is characterized by immunosuppression, including elevated interleukin-10 (IL-10), decreased tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) production, impaired monocyte function, and T-cell dysfunction. Furthermore, patients with persistent AKI fail to restore immune homeostasis, while those who recover tend to correct these abnormalities [3]. Meanwhile, surgical trauma and stress response can induce activation of the sympathetic nervous system to release catecholamines, which act on immune cells through β2-adrenergic receptors, inhibit proinflammatory cytokines (such as IL-12 and IFN-γ), and increase anti-inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-10), leading to decreased immune function [2]. Therefore, patients with AKI after type B aortic dissection surgery may exist in a state of “relative immunocompromise”, potentially facilitating the progression from latent CMV infection to active clinical disease.

The risk of cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection caused by blood transfusion mainly exists in specific high-risk populations, such as individuals with immune deficiency, transplant recipients, pregnant women, and those at risk of congenital infection. Transfusion-related CMV infection is mainly derived from the reactivation of latent viruses in transfused monocytes; however, the use of leukoreduction can significantly reduce the transmission risk [11]. A meta-analysis showed that the use of CMV-seronegative components or leukoreduction could both reduce the infection risk by more than 90%, but there was no significant difference between the two in terms of symptomatic infection [12]. Although leukocyte-depleted red blood cell suspension was used in this patient, the possibility of CMV infection through the blood route cannot be excluded.

The clinical manifestations of CMV enteritis are often non-specific. In the present case, the patient presented with hematochezia, necessitating accurate localization of the bleeding source. In scenarios of obscure gastrointestinal bleeding, we initially performed contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT), which identified active small bowel bleeding. Subsequent celiac arteriography failed to demonstrate a culprit vessel. This aligns with evidence reported by Kulkarni C et al., indicating that multidetector computed tomography (MDCT) exhibits superior sensitivity to digital subtraction angiography and capsule in localizing small bowel bleeding [13]. Double-balloon enteroscopy (DBE) can reduce extensive exploration of the entire small intestine preoperatively or intraoperatively, and help surgeons accurately locate lesions by marking the lesion area [14]. Its accuracy is well established, and its intraoperative application—as utilized in this case—can offer real-time guidance superior to preoperative imaging alone. Beyond localization of bleeding, determining its underlying etiology is critical. The common causes of intestinal bleeding after type B aortic dissection surgery include non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia, reperfusion injury, application of vasoactive drugs, coagulation abnormalities, inflammation, etc. This case report belongs to a rare case. In our patient, the diagnosis of CMV enteritis was confirmed by immunohistochemistry (IHC) on postoperative day 8 following the onset of hematochezia—a significantly shorter interval than previously reported [5, 15]. Antiviral therapy with ganciclovir was initiated immediately upon diagnosis. Ganciclovir represents first-line treatment for CMV infection in patients with inflammatory bowel disease or organ transplantation [16, 17]. Prior evidence suggests that a combined intravenous (IV) and oral (PO) antiviral regimen may yield superior outcomes compared to IV or PO monotherapy in both immunocompromised and immunocompetent patients with gastrointestinal CMV disease [18]. However, given the known nephrotoxicity of ganciclovir, we administered IV therapy only, initiating at a reduced dose of 2.5 mg/kg and escalating to 4.5 mg/kg upon normalization of renal function. In specific high-risk populations (such as immunocompromised patients and those with refractory infections), combination therapy with intravenous immunoglobulin is often required to enhance antiviral immunity [19]. Since the total globulin in the blood was detected to be less than 20 g within 5 consecutive days after ileal resection, and considering that the patient might have severe immunosuppression, combination therapy with intravenous immunoglobulin was administered.

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting this case. First, as a single-case report, our findings may not be generalizable to all patients with similar presentations. Second, the causal relationship between post-surgical AKI, stress-induced immunosuppression, and CMV enteritis cannot be definitively established from this observation alone. Third, the absence of preoperative CMV serology limits our ability to determine whether this represented primary infection or reactivation of latent disease. Finally, the therapeutic approach described was tailored to this specific patient’s condition and may not be applicable to all cases of CMV enteritis. Prospective studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate these observations.

Conclusions

This case suggests that life-threatening CMV enteritis may occur in patients with relative immunosuppression following major vascular surgery, surgical stress, and acute kidney injury, even in the absence of traditional immunocompromise. Our experience indicates that post-surgical AKI and stress-induced immunosuppression may be contributing factors for CMV enteritis in this clinical context. In this patient, CT imaging was valuable for early detection of small bowel bleeding, identifying the bleeding source earlier than DSA. For CMV enteritis complicated by life-threatening gastrointestinal hemorrhage, prompt surgical intervention may be life-saving and provides essential pathological confirmation, while antiviral therapy remains the first-line treatment. However, these observations are derived from a single case and require validation in larger studies.
