Melatonin stabilizes photosynthesis and scavenges ROS to enhance salt tolerance in cucumber
Authors: Zhaoxuan Li, Zhigang Tang, Jiawei Song, Jianyu Li, Xiaozhuo Wang, Xueyan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSoil salinization severely constrains crop productivity worldwide. In cucumber (Cucumis sativus L.), salt stress induces oxidative damage and impairs photosynthesis. Melatonin (MT) improves abiotic stress tolerance across diverse crops; yet its salt tolerance mechanism in cucumber remains unclear.ResultsUnder 100 mM NaCl, foliar application of 100 µM MT increased plant height, leaf area, and biomass. Physiologically, MT enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities, increased ascorbate (ASA) and osmolyte levels, and lowered the Na⁺/K⁺ ratio. MT also elevated chlorophyll content (SPAD), indicating protection of the photosynthetic apparatus. Transcriptomics identified 1,355 melatonin-responsive DEGs, with GO/KEGG enrichment in photosynthesis, photosynthesisantenna proteins, and porphyrin–chlorophyll metabolism. WGCNA revealed a brown module strongly associated with MT and enriched in light-harvesting complex (LHC) genes; LHC genes of PSI and PSII were upregulated by MT. Consistently, MT improved photosynthetic rate and reduced H₂O₂ and O₂·⁻ accumulation.ConclusionMelatonin enhances cucumber salt tolerance by stabilizing photosynthesis—via LHC gene regulation—and mitigating ROS stress, providing a mechanistic basis for improving stress resilience in horticultural crops.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07274-3.

Background

Salt stress has emerged as a major environmental challenge threatening global food security, affecting approximately 833 million hectares of land-equivalent to 8.7% of the Earth’s terrestrial area [1]. The area of saline-alkali land is increasing at a rate of 1.5 million hectares per year due to the impact of the natural environment and agricultural cultivation [2, 3]. Extensive saline-alkali land seriously hinders soil nutrient conditions and crop production, reducing average crop yields by more than 50% [4]. With the global population rising and food demand increasing, enhancing crop tolerance to salinity has become critical for sustaining agricultural productivity [5]. Cucumber (Cucumis sativus L.), an economically important and salt-sensitive horticultural crop, is particularly susceptible to salinity stress, posing significant threat to production systems [6].

Under salt stress, cucumber plants exhibit impaired growth and development, primarily due to osmotic stress, ion toxicity and oxidative damage. Excess sodium ions (Na⁺) in saline soils significantly lowers the water potential in the rhizosphere, triggering osmotic stress in roots that restricts water uptake, leads to cellular dehydration, and reduces turgor pressure [7]. This process directly decreases stomatal conductance, impairs CO₂ assimilation, and limits photosynthetic carbon fixation, which is manifested as reduced expansion of new leaves and premature leaf senescence [8]. High concentrations of Na⁺ also disturb ion homeostasis inside and outside the cell, inhibiting the uptake of essential elements such as potassium (K⁺), calcium (Ca²⁺), and magnesium (Mg²⁺), thereby resulting in ion toxicity and nutrient deficiencies [9]. Furthermore, salt stress disrupts redox homeostasis in cucumber cells, leading to excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which initiates lipid peroxidation of the cell membranes. ROS further degrade photosynthetic pigments, denature photosynthetic proteins, and damage the electron transport chain, ultimately reducing photosynthetic efficiency and restricting plant growth [10].

Melatonin (MT) is an indole-based heterocyclic compound that is widely distributed in plants. Its biosynthesis begins with tryptophan (Trp) in chloroplasts, proceeds via conversion in the endoplasmic reticulum, and possibly involves collaboration between chloroplasts and mitochondria during the early stages of synthesis [11]. As a key regulator of plant responses to salt stress, MT enhances stress tolerance through multiple mechanisms. It directly scavenges ROS (e.g., H₂O₂) and boosts the expression of antioxidant enzymes (e.g., SOD, APX) in synergy with ascorbate accumulation, forming a combined enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant barrier that suppresses lipid peroxidation [12]. Masoumi et al. [13] reported that this mechanism significantly alleviates salt-induced oxidative damage in tomatoes. In addition, MT induces the synthesis of osmolytes (e.g., soluble proteins and soluble sugars) to counteract the osmotic imbalance triggered by salt stress [14]. MT also indirectly regulates ion homeostasis by activating H⁺-ATPase pumps that facilitate K uptake and inhibit Na accumulation [15]. Moreover, MT preserves the integrity of thylakoid membranes in sweet potatoes, reducing granum disintegration while inhibiting Mg-dechelatase activity to delay chlorophyll degradation, and markedly promotes the biosynthesis of carotenoids and anthocyanins [16]. In cucumbers, Liu et al. [17] showed that applying melatonin under salt stress effectively increased the content of ascorbic acid (ASA) and glutathione (GSH) as well as the activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and peroxidase (POD). Zhang et al. [18] suggested that melatonin’s ability to enhance plant salt tolerance is mainly related to the removal of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and that melatonin-mediated salt tolerance is associated with MAPK kinase signaling, which can significantly enhance the gene expression of MAPK3, MAPK4, and MAPK6.

Although the role of melatonin in conferring salt tolerance in many crops has been extensively studied, its mechanism of regulating reactive oxygen species scavenging and enhancing photosynthesis under salt stress in cucumber remains unclear. In this study, we analyzed the effects of exogenous melatonin on phenotypic and physiological related indices of cucumber seedlings under salt stress. The key regulatory pathways and genes for melatonin to alleviate salt stress tolerance in cucumber were identified. The dual mechanism by which melatonin regulates salt tolerance in cucumber seedlings was clarified. These findings help to provide new insights for subsequent targeting to improve crop salt tolerance.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatments

This experiment was conducted in a greenhouse at the National Science and Technology Park of Ningxia University, using cucumber (Del 100, purchased from Ningxia Bory Agricultural Technology Co., Ltd.) as the plant material. Uniform and fully developed seeds were immersed in warm water at 55 °C for 15 min, placed on moist filter paper, and allowed to germinate in the dark at 28°C. After breaking dormancy, they were sown in nutrient pots (10.5 × 7.2 cm) filled with perlite. Each pot was irrigated daily with a suitable amount of a half-strength Hoagland nutrient solution.

On the 23rd day post-sowing, we selected seedlings of uniform size, free from diseases and pests, and vigorous for subsequent experiments. The environmental parameters of the greenhouse are as follows: the temperature is 18–29℃, and the photoperiod is 16 h/8 h. Three treatments were established: (1) CK (control), irrigated with standard nutrient solution; (2) Salt, where 100 mmol·L⁻¹ NaCl was added to the nutrient solution; and (3) SMT, which included 100 µmol·L⁻¹ melatonin in addition to 100 mmol·L⁻¹ NaCl in the nutrient solution [18, 19]. Each treatment had three replicates, each containing 40 seedlings. Melatonin (purchased from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.) was foliar-sprayed at 20:00 daily for three days prior to the initiation of salt stress, using 10 mL per seedling.

Phenotypic measurements

Plant phenotypes were assessed on days 0, 4, and 8 of salt stress for each of the treatments. Plant height was measured from the stem base to the apical point using a tape measure. The single leaf area was determined following the method described by Zhang et al. [17]. Leaf chlorophyll content (SPAD value) was measured using a SPAD-520 Plus chlorophyll meter. Prior to analysis, the roots and shoots were harvested and rinsed with deionized water. Root morphology (including total root tip number and total root volume) was scanned and recorded using an Epson V700 root scanner (EPSON Expression 4990 model) equipped with the WinRHIZO software. Afterwards, roots and shoots were subjected to a heat treatment at 105 °C for 15 min and then dried to a constant weight at 80 °C; the dry weight was measured using an analytical balance.

Following the method of Kang et al. [20]. Salt damage was assessed on day 8 using a 0–5 scale, where 0 indicates no visible injury and 5 indicates complete plant death (Supplementary Table 1). A total of 30 plants were evaluated per treatment.

Measurement of physiological indices

Osmotic adjustment substances and ion content

The soluble sugar content (product No. (ml095107) and soluble protein (product No. ml076983) were measured using commercial kits (Shanghai MLBio Co., Ltd., China) at 630 nm and 562 nm with a multifunctional enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) reader (Feyond-A500). The specific operating procedures were strictly followed in accordance with the kit instructions.

In accordance with the methodology described by Kang et al. [20], the roots and leaves of the plants were dried to constant weight and ground into a fine powder. One gram of dry sample was weighed and digested in a 5:1 mixture of nitric acid and perchloric acid, followed by microwave digestion. The solution was then diluted to 25 mL in a volumetric flask, and the concentrations of Na⁺ and K⁺ in the plant were measured using inductively coupled plasma (ICP-OES Avio™ 200;USA, PerkinElmer).

Cell viability and reactive oxygen species (ROS) content

Following Baker and Mock [21], tissues were stained with Evans Blue. The second true leaves from each treatment group were sampled for electrolyte leakage, utilizing a conductivity meter (DDS-11 A, Shanghai INESA Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai), as per the protocol established by Nanjo et al. [22]. Wash the leaf surface three times with deionized water; weigh an appropriate amount of leaves and cut them into 0.5 cm pieces according to the specified method. Divide the leaves into test tubes and add 10 mL of deionized water. Vacuum the test tubes for 1 h and let them stand for 1 h. Measure the conductivity of the solution (L1) using a conductivity meter. Additionally, heat it in a 100 °C water bath for 45 min; after cooling, measure the solution conductivity again (L2). The electrolyte leakage is calculated as L1/L2 and expressed as a percentage.

Superoxide (O₂⁻) and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) concentrations were measured using commercial assay kits (product Nos. ml095218, and ml094986, respectively (Shanghai MLBio Co., Ltd.). The second true leaves from each treatment were stained with nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) and 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) to visualize ROS, following the methodologies outlined by Jabs et al. and Wei et al. [23, 24], and were subsequently decolorized with 95% ethanol. The enzymatic activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT) were measured using the corresponding assay kits (product numbers ml092620, ml095259, and ml095267, respectively, provided by Shanghai MLBio Co., Ltd.) were used to measure enzyme activity at wavelengths of 560 nm, 470 nm, and 405 nm, respectively. The specific experimental procedures were strictly followed according to the kit instructions.

Photosynthetic parameters and stomatal observations

Following Xu et al. [25] the net photosynthetic rate (Pn) was measured on the second true leaf using a LI‑6400 (LI‑COR, USA) at PPFD 800 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, reference CO₂ 390 µmol mol⁻¹, leaf temperature 25 °C, flow 500 µmol s⁻¹. For Fv/Fm was applied using an IMAGING‑PAM (Walz, Germany), leaves were dark‑adapted for 30 min and the light intensity was selected as activation light intensity, and the initial real-time fluorescence value of the leaves was adjusted to be in the range of 0.1–0.2. Leaf chlorophyll content was extracted using 95% ethanol for spectrophotometric measurements [26].

On day 8 of salt treatment, the fourth true leaves were sampled and fixed in FAA solution at room temperature for 24 h. Subsequently, the samples were dispatched to Chengdu Lilai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for scanning electron microscopy to examine stomatal morphology.

Transcriptome analysis

Leaf and root samples (0.5 g per sample) were collected on days 0, 4, and 8 of salt stress for analysis. Each treatment was performed in triplicate. Rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and transported on dry ice to Wuhan Mywett Bioscience Co., Ltd. for RNA-seq analysis. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2, applying thresholds of |log₂ (Fold Change) | ≥ 2.0 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. KEGG, GO enrichment, and weighted gene co-expression network analyses (WGCNA) of DEGs were performed using TBtools software. The raw sequencing files of transcriptomic data are now available in NCBI SRA database with accession number PRJNA1263780 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra/PRJNA1263780).

Real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted using the TaKaRa MiniBEST Plant RNA Extraction Kit (TaKaRa Biomedicals, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Scientific, USA). cDNA from cucumber seedlings was subjected to qRT-PCR using a qTOWER3G system (Analytik Jena, Germany). The PCR conditions are as follows: pre-denature at 95 °C for 1 min, then perform 40 cycles of amplification at 95 °C and 60 °C for 5 s and 30 s, respectively. After the cycles are completed, perform a melting curve analysis at 60–95°C to verify the specificity of the amplicons for each pair of primers. ACTIN2 (CsaV3_6G041900) was used as the reference gene, and the candidate core DEGs identified by WGCNA were analyzed. The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Data analysis

All data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with SPSS 20.0 software. Duncan’s multiple range test was employed to identify significant differences at P < 0.05. Figures were generated using GraphPad Prism, version 9.5.1.

Results

Exogenous melatonin alleviates salt stress damage in cucumber seedlings

To investigate the impact of exogenous melatonin on cucumber seedlings under salt stress, we measured morphological parameters, SPAD values, and salt injury scores at different time intervals. Salt stress significantly inhibited seedling growth compared to the control (Fig. 1). However, melatonin treatment partially mitigated these effects, resulting in greater plant height, leaf area, dry weight, root development, as well as reduced severity of salt damage compared to salt treatment alone (Fig. 1A–F). Notably, melatonin increased both the total number of root tips and overall root volume under salt stress (Fig. 1F–H). Since melatonin was applied via foliar spray, the relative leaf chlorophyll content was closely monitored throughout the experiment. Chlorophyll content, assessed using SPAD values, was significantly higher in melatonin-treated plants than in both salt-stressed and control plants. On the 8th day, SPAD values in the melatonin treatment group exceeded those in the control by 20.52% (Fig. 1C). This effect primarily stems from melatonin’s protective action on chloroplasts. Salt-stressed seedlings exhibited pronounced damage, with 76.7% showing grade 3–4 injury and 6.7% mortality. In contrast, melatonin-treated seedlings displayed significantly reduced damage symptoms (Fig. 1I), indicating that exogenous melatonin enhances cucumber tolerance to salt stress.

Effects of exogenous melatonin on antioxidant systems and osmotic adjustment

To explore the mechanisms behind melatonin-mediated stress tolerance, we evaluated antioxidant enzyme activities and ascorbate-related metabolism. Under salt stress treatment for 4 d and 8 d, the activities of antioxidant enzymes superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT), and ascorbate peroxidase (APX) increased to varying degrees compared with the control (Fig. 2A-C). Ascorbate (ASA) synthesis increased slightly only under salt stress for 8d. Compared with salt stress alone, melatonin significantly enhanced the activities of SOD, POD, and CAT (Fig. 2A-C). Additionally, melatonin enhanced the activity of ascorbate peroxidase (APX), which catalyzes the reduction of H₂O₂ using ascorbate (ASA) as an electron donor, thereby contributing to cellular ROS detoxification. Under salt stress, both APX activity (71.65%, p < 0.001) and dehydroascorbate (DHA) (55.69%, p = 0.0097) content were elevated; however, the accumulation of H₂O₂ led to a depletion of ASA, resulting in oxidative imbalance. In contrast, melatonin-treated plants exhibited significantly higher ASA content (55.49%, p < 0.001) and lower DHA levels (23.96%, p = 0.0381), indicating improved redox homeostasis and enhanced ROS scavenging capacity (Fig. 2D–F). Soluble sugars, and soluble proteins are important indicators of plant responses to osmotic stress. To assess the effects of melatonin on osmotic adjustment under salt stress, the concentrations of these osmolytes were measured in cucumber seedlings. Soluble sugar content in salt-stressed plants did not differ significantly from that of the control; however, melatonin treatment led to a marked increase in sugar accumulation compared to both groups (Supplement Fig. 1). As salt stress increased, soluble protein levels progressively increased in the melatonin-treated group and were significantly higher than those observed under salt stress (26.25%, p < 0.001) or control treatments (57.36%, p < 0.001) (Supplement Fig. 2).

Effects of exogenous melatonin on ion homeostasis in cucumber under salt stress

The Na⁺/K⁺ balance in plants under salt stress is a key indicator of the severity of stress. In cucumber subjected to salt stress, Na⁺ content increased dramatically—by 19.8-fold in leaves and 5-fold in roots—compared to the control. Melatonin treatment significantly reduced Na⁺ content (23.12%, 12.37%) in both tissues relative to the salt-stressed group (Fig. 3A-B). Conversely, the K⁺ content in both leaves and roots exhibited a significant decrease under salt stress relative to the control, whereas the K⁺ content in the melatonin-treated group was significantly elevated compared to that under salt stress (Fig. 3C-D). Due to the increase in Na⁺ and the reduction in K⁺ under salt stress, the Na⁺/K⁺ ratio in both the leaves and roots of cucumber increased rapidly. On day 8 of salt stress, the Na⁺/K⁺ ratio in the leaves and roots of salt-stressed plants was 15.2-fold and 4.5-fold higher than that of the control, respectively. However, in the melatonin-treated group, the Na⁺/K⁺ ratio was 8.9-fold and 2.6-fold higher than that in the control group, respectively. These findings suggest that exogenous melatonin mitigates ion toxicity induced by salt stress.

Transcriptomic analysis under different treatment conditions

RNAseq was performed on pooled leaf + root samples from CK, Salt, and Salt + MT. Biological replicates within each treatment were highly correlated (Pearson r > 0.9; Fig. 4A). DEGs were called at |log₂FC| ≥ 2.0, FDR < 0.05. As the duration of salt stress increased, the number of DEGs increased, reflecting a stronger plant response to salt damage (Fig. 4B). On day 8, 1,882 DEGs were identified in the salt treatment group compared to the control group, with 1,212 upregulated and 670 downregulated genes (Fig. 4C). Similarly, in the Salt + MT treatment, 1,963 DEGs were identified, with 993 upregulated and 970 downregulated genes compared with the Salt treatment (Fig. 4C). Venn diagram analysis revealed 1,355 common DEGs between W8_vs_S8 and S8_vs_SMT8 (co-DEG), indicating that melatonin treatment altered the gene expression profile of cucumber seedlings subjected to salt stress.

WGCNA analysis

To further elucidate the regulatory mechanism underlying melatonin-enhanced salt tolerance in cucumber, Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) was employed to identify key pathways and genes responsive to melatonin treatment. Using a soft threshold of 14, the genes were categorized into five modules (Fig. 5A). On the eighth day of salt stress, the brown module (MEbrown) exhibited a pronounced response to melatonin treatment (Fig. 5B). KEGG enrichment analysis of genes within this brown module revealed significant enrichment of the photosynthesis-antenna protein pathway (Fig. 5C). The gene interaction network demonstrated strong correlations among genes encoding light-harvesting chlorophyll a/b-binding proteins (LHC), which form essential components of photosystem I (PSI) and photosystem II (PSII) (Fig. 5D). Expression analysis of LHC genes showed that salt stress led to a downregulation of their expression on day 8, whereas melatonin treatment mitigated the downregulation induced by salt stress (Fig. 5E). To validate the transcriptome findings, qRT-PCR was conducted on selected LHC genes. The expression trends observed through qRT-PCR were highly consistent with those from RNA-seq (Fig. 6). qRT-PCR and RNA-seq both showed that LHC gene expression was significantly suppressed under salt stress, while exogenous melatonin application could alleviate the suppression caused by salt stress to a certain extent. This confirmed the accuracy of the RNA-seq data and further confirmed the role of melatonin in enhancing salt tolerance by upregulating LHC gene expression under salt stress.

Exogenous melatonin stabilizes the photosynthetic system in cucumber leaves under salt stress

Building upon transcriptomic evidence, we further evaluated the effects of melatonin on photosynthetic system stability under salt stress by assessing photosynthetic performance, chlorophyll fluorescence, ROS accumulation, and stomatal morphology in cucumber leaves. Under salt stress, the chlorophyll content, photosynthetic rate and Fv/Fm of cucumbers decreased significantly, the electrolyte permeability of cells increased significantly, and stomatal deformation occurred. Melatonin treatment effectively prevented excessive chlorophyll degradation and cellular damage under salt stress, thereby preserving photosynthetic function (Fig. 7A-B, G-K). Furthermore, melatonin maintained normal stomatal development and prevented the deformation observed under salt stress (Fig. 7C). Histochemical staining using Evans blue, NBT, and DAB further demonstrated that melatonin application suppressed ROS accumulation and electrolyte leakage. Specifically, levels of superoxide anions (O₂·⁻) and H₂O₂ were reduced in melatonin-treated leaves, indicating enhanced cellular integrity and redox homeostasis (Fig. 7D-F, L-N).

Discussion

Salt stress is a major abiotic factor limiting crop productivity, and exogenous MT has emerged as a promising regulator of stress tolerance. In this study, we systematically evaluated the ameliorative effects of MT on salt-induced growth inhibition in cucumber seedlings, integrating morphological, physiological, and transcriptomic analyses. The results demonstrate that MT enhances the salt tolerance of cucumber seedlings through coordinated regulation of growth, antioxidant defense, ion and osmotic homeostasis, and photosynthetic performance. This provides crucial phenotypic and physiological evidence for the potential application of this in saline-affected farmland.

Melatonin alleviated growth inhibition caused by salt stress, increasing plant height, leaf area, and biomass accumulation relative to untreated salt-stressed plants by 27.48%, 43.15%, and 116.56%, respectively. Although these growth parameters remained below control levels, the improvements reflect melatonin’s capacity to mitigate, rather than fully reverse salt-induced injury. Particularly notable was the restoration of root system development, with root tip number and volume approaching those of the non-stressed control. This suggests that MT may enhance root architecture, potentially through modulation of auxin transport, thereby improving water and nutrient acquisition under stress—a critical factor in supporting aboveground biomass [27, 28]. Visual salt injury scoring confirmed the protective effect of melatonin, with 90% of treated cucumber seedlings maintaining moderate damage levels (grades 2–3), indicating a substantial enhancement of stress resilience. This phenotypic evidence complements the biochemical and transcriptomic results and supports the application of melatonin for salinity mitigation in horticultural production. The generation of ROS induced by salt stress is a key factor contributing to plant mortality. Our findings demonstrate that exogenous melatonin application markedly enhanced antioxidant defense under salt stress by upregulating the activities of antioxidant enzymes (SOD, POD, and CAT) (Fig. 2). Notably, although MT increased antioxidant enzyme activities at the physiological level, classical antioxidant enzyme genes (e.g., SOD, POD, CAT) were not consistently upregulated in the MT vs. Salt comparison, suggesting post‑transcriptional regulation and/or enzyme activation contributes to the observed antioxidant responses (Fig. 4E). In particular, melatonin activates the ascorbate-glutathione (ASA-GSH) cycle, increasing ASA content by 55% and decreasing DHA accumulation by 24%, thereby reducing H₂O₂ content to near-control levels (Figs. 2D-F and 7L-N). These findings align with those of Ahammed et al. [29], but this study further revealed the priority regulation of ASA biosynthesis by melatonin. During the later stages of salt stress (8 days), the ASA content in the melatonin-treated group remained high (Fig. 2D), suggesting that melatonin may promote ASA regeneration by upregulating DHAR or MDHAR rather than relying solely on antioxidant enzymes [30]. This “enzymatic-non-enzymatic cooperative” antioxidant strategy may represent the primary mechanism by which melatonin effectively mitigates oxidative stress in the body. Furthermore, melatonin alleviates cucumber salt stress through a synergistic mechanism involving “ion screening-osmotic compensation”. Notably, although MT increased antioxidant enzyme activities at the physiological level, classical antioxidant enzyme genes (e.g., SOD, POD, CAT) were not consistently upregulated in MT vs. Salt, suggesting posttranscriptional regulation and/or enzyme activation underlies the observed antioxidant responses.

Melatonin inhibited Na⁺ translocation to above-ground tissues —leaf Na⁺ levels were reduced to just 17.8% of those in untreated salt-stressed plants—while enhancing K⁺ uptake in the roots by 135.5%. This lowered the Na+/K+ ratio in leaves from 15.2 under salt stress to 9.9, suggesting improved cellular ion selectivity. This regulatory pattern contrasts with the “Na⁺ efflux priority” strategy proposed by Sheikhalipour et al. [31], suggesting that salt tolerance mechanisms may be species specific.

Under salinity, treated seedlings exhibited a 69.3% increase in soluble sugars and a 26.5% increase in soluble proteins (Figures S1 and S2), indicating that it achieves osmotic balance through carbon metabolic redistribution rather than the accumulation of short-term stress products. This “active regulation” strategy contrasts with the passive response dominated by proline under salt stress (e.g., studies in alfalfa), highlighting the central role of melatonin in metabolic network remodeling [32].

Importantly, this study provides the first evidence in cucumber that exogenous melatonin enhances salt tolerance by specifically activating LHC gene pathway (Figs. 4E and 5C). The findings indicate that chlorophyll a consistently decreased under salt stress, whereas chlorophyll b exhibited a significant reduction only at later stages (23.46%). In contrast, melatonin-treated plants-maintained chlorophyll a and b levels comparable to the control (24.88–22.89% higher than that under salt stress), suggesting that melatonin prioritizes the stability of the PSII core pigment-protein complex (Fig. 7G, H, I). The upregulation of Lhcb family genes in the transcriptomic data (Fig. 6) suggests that melatonin alleviates chloroplast structural damage induced by salt stress by enhancing the synthesis of light-harvesting complex II (LHCII) proteins, thereby reducing chlorophyll degradation [16]. Together, these data indicate that MT primarily protects chlorophyll from degradation by stabilizing LHC proteins and limiting ROS, rather than inducing de novo chlorophyll biosynthesis under salinity.

The link between ROS metabolism and photosynthetic performance appears to be central to melatonin’s protective role. The ROS burst induced by salt stress damages the photosynthetic membrane system and leads to electrolyte leakage, whereas melatonin treatment significantly reduced the O₂⁻ and H₂O₂ levels (Fig. 7M, N). Notably, the repair effect of melatonin on the PSII reaction center (Fv/Fm), in conjunction with its reduction in ROS accumulation, may stem from its regulation of the chloroplast electron transport chain (ETC) [33]. Melatonin protects the PSII core complex from photooxidative damage by enhancing cyclic electron flow (CEF), diverting excess light energy, and reducing singlet oxygen (¹O₂) production (Fig. 8) [34]. Furthermore, scanning electron microscopy revealed that melatonin treatment preserved normal stomatal morphology and opening/closing function, preventing excessive transpiration caused by stomatal deformation under salt stress. This finding is consistent with the simultaneous enhancement of the net photosynthetic rate (Pn) (57.79% increase) and Fv/Fm ratio. These results suggest that melatonin optimizes light energy use efficiency via a dual pathway involving stomatal and non-stomatal processes while preventing excessive water loss from leaves [26].

Although this study highlights the LHC pathway as a central regulatory target of melatonin, the upstream signaling components driving Lhcb gene expression remain to be elucidated. Further research is needed to explore the crosstalk between melatonin signaling and ROS-responsive transcriptional regulators to further define the molecular framework underlying melatonin-mediated salt tolerance in cucumber and other crops.

Conclusion

Cucumber (Cucumis sativus L.), a salt-sensitive crop, is highly susceptible to yield loss under saline conditions. This study reveals that exogenous melatonin (MT) application enhances salt tolerance in cucumber seedlings through a dual mechanism involving reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging and stabilization of the photosynthetic system. At the physiological level, MT enhances antioxidant enzyme activities and promotes the ascorbate-glutathione (ASA-GSH) cycle, thereby enabling efficient ROS detoxification. Transcriptomic analysis demonstrates that MT upregulates light-harvesting complex (LHC) genes, thereby maintaining chlorophyll content and protecting photosynthetic efficiency under saline conditions. These findings elucidate a novel regulatory mechanism whereby melatonin (MT) stabilizes photosynthesis by activating light-harvesting complex (LHC) genes and mitigating oxidative stress. This discovery provides a theoretical foundation for improving salt tolerance in cucumber.
