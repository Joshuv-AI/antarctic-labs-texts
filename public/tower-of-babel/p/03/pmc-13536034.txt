Pulmonary Function and Exercise‐Based Assessments for the Detection of Pulmonary Hypertension in Fibrotic Interstitial Lung Disease
Authors: Vaida Averjanovaitė, Lina Gumbienė, Diana Zakarkaitė, Monika Laukytė‐Slėnienė, Eglė Grigonienė, Virginija Rudienė, Taida Ivanauskienė, Virginija Šileikienė
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPulmonary hypertension (PH) is a frequent complication of fibrotic interstitial lung diseases (ILDs), but its detection remains challenging due to overlapping clinical features. We aimed to evaluate the discriminatory power of physiological and exercise‐based markers that differentiate right heart catheterization (RHC)—confirmed PH from low‐probability ILD cases. In this prospective observational study of 59 patients with ILD, we evaluated pulmonary function tests (PFTs), cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET), and the 6‐min walk test (6MWT). Patients deemed at high probability for clinically relevant PH following a multidisciplinary assessment were referred for RHC. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to compare RHC‐confirmed PH cases (n = 18) with a clinically low‐probability ILD cohort (n = 19). Patients with RHC‐confirmed PH had significantly lower diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO) (43.44 ± 13.73% vs. 59.11 ± 13.50%, p = 0.002) and higher ventilatory inefficiency, as measured by the VE/VCO2 slope (47.00 ± 7.90 vs. 40.71 ± 8.48, p = 0.035). DLCO showed moderate discriminatory performance (AUC 0.799; optimal cut‐off 50.5% predicted; sensitivity 72%; specificity 72%), whereas VE/VCO2 slope showed fair discrimination (AUC 0.711; cut‐off ≈ 41.5; sensitivity 75%; specificity 59%). Although 6MWT distance did not differentiate the groups, post‐exercise oxygen desaturation and heart rate were significant indicators. In fibrotic ILD, gas exchange and ventilatory inefficiency parameters—specifically DLCO and VE/VCO2 slope—effectively discriminate RHC‐confirmed PH from clinically low‐probability cases. These markers offer a practical screening approach to identify patients who should be prioritized for further PH testing.

Introduction

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) significantly impacts survival and quality of life in patients with fibrotic interstitial lung disease (ILD) [1, 2]. Its clinical manifestation often overlaps with those of ILD, making clinical differentiation unreliable [3]. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is commonly used to screen for PH, but its accuracy is limited in patients with ILD because fibrotic lung changes may affect acoustic windows [4]. While right heart catheterization (RHC) remains the gold standard for confirming PH, its invasive nature necessitates a robust non‐invasive screening strategy [5].

Gas exchange and exercise‐derived ventilatory parameters reflect pulmonary vascular integrity. However, specific diagnostic thresholds for diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO) and the ventilatory equivalent for carbon dioxide (VE/VCO2 slope) are poorly defined in the fibrotic ILD population [6]. We evaluated the ability of PFTs, CPET, and 6MWT parameters to discriminate RHC‐confirmed PH from clinically low‐probability individuals.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

We conducted a prospective observational study at our Pulmonary Hypertension Reference Centre. Between 2023 and 2025, 59 consecutive patients with fibrotic ILD were enrolled. ILD diagnosis was established by a multidisciplinary team (MDT), based on clinical evaluation, chest computed tomography (CT) findings, and histopathology when available. Despite differing ILD subtypes, all participants had a comparable extent of pulmonary fibrosis on chest CT. At the time of PH assessment, 40 patients were receiving antifibrotic therapy, and none were on pulmonary vasodilators.

PFTs, 6MWT, CPET, and TTE were performed on room air, without supplemental oxygen, to standardize testing and because baseline hypoxemia did not exceed safety thresholds in any of the subjects. Patients were stratified into three probability groups (low, intermediate, and high) for clinically relevant PH: Low probability: tricuspid regurgitation velocity (TRV) ≤ 2.8 m/s, no secondary signs of PH on TTE or CT, and disproportionate dyspnea.Intermediate probability: TRV 2.9–3.4 m/s without secondary signs, or TRV ≤ 2.8 m/s with clear secondary signs of PH.High probability: TRV > 3.4 m/s, or TRV 2.9–3.4 m/s with secondary signs of right ventricular dysfunction, and disproportionate dyspnea. Disproportionate dyspnea was defined by multidisciplinary consensus when the severity of breathlessness exceeded that expected from the degree of restrictive impairment and gas transfer reduction, prompting suspicion of PH.

RHC was offered only for patients with high probability for clinically relevant PH (n = 27). 23 underwent the procedure and 4 did not (due to frailty or refusal). Baseline pre‐RHC screening characteristics of all 59 initially stratified subjects are documented in Supplementary Table S1. Precapillary PH hemodynamic type was confirmed in 18 (78.3%) individuals. The 4 untested high‐probability subjects, all intermediate PH probability and combined pre‐ and post‐capillary PH cases were excluded from the final analyses from the primary downstream analytical cohort, resulting in a final extreme‐group comparison between RHC‐confirmed precapillary PH (n = 18) and the clinically‐low probability cohort (n = 19). TTE was performed by a single experienced specialist following a comprehensive protocol. For this study, echocardiographic markers of PH included TRV, atrial and ventricular sizes, left ventricular systolic function, and inferior vena cava diameter and collapsibility. PH suspicion defined as TRV ≥ 2.9 m/s and/or presence of secondary echocardiographic signs indicative of elevated pulmonary pressure [1], specifically: right ventricular/left ventricular basal diameter ratio > 1.0, flattening of the interventricular septum, RV outflow tract acceleration time < 105 ms, early diastolic pulmonary regurgitation velocity > 2.2 m/s, inferior vena cava diameter > 21 mm with decreased inspiratory collapse, increased PA diameter, TAPSE/PAP ratio < 0.55 mm/mmHg, and right atrial end‐systolic area > 18 cm2.

Precapillary PH was confirmed with RHC using 2022 ESC/ERS guidelines [1]: mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) > 20 mmHg, pulmonary vascular resistance > 2 Wood units (WU) and pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP) ≤ 15 mmHg.

Testing Protocols

PFTs were performed and interpreted following ERS/ATS standards [7]. Recorded parameters included: forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), total lung capacity (TLC), vital capacity (VC), residual volume (RV), and single‐breath hemoglobin‐adjusted DLCO.6MWT was conducted by a single assessor according to ERS/ATS standards [8]. Recorded parameters included total distance, baseline, and post‐exercise oxygen saturation (SpO2), baseline and post‐exercise heart rate. The two participants receiving long‐term oxygen therapy completed the test without portable oxygen, consistent with their routine daily activity. CPET was conducted on a cycle ergometer with breath‐by‐breath gas analysis. Recorded metrics included peak oxygen uptake (VO2peak), anaerobic threshold (AT), peak work rate (Wpeak), and VE/VCO2 slope. The active exercise phase lasted 8–12 min and the test termination occurred at volitional exhaustion with evidence of maximal effort, indicated by a respiratory exchange ratio greater than 1.10, or earlier if safety criteria required termination.

Statistics

Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 27. Normality was assessed with the Shapiro‐Wilk test. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD, categorical variables as counts and percentages. Group comparisons were conducted using Student's t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate.

To determine the diagnostic utility of non‐invasive markers, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were performed. They were specifically restricted to “extreme‐group” comparison (RHC‐confirmed precapillary PH vs. clinically low‐probability patients) to minimize diagnostic uncertainty and maximize the signal‐to‐noise ratio. Optimal diagnostic cut‐offs were determined using the Youden index. No adjustment for multiple comparisons or formal sample size calculation was performed due to the exploratory design. A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics and Study Population

A total of 59 ILD patients were included in the study. The cohort was 61% male, with a mean age of 70.4 ± 10.5 years. Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis was the primary etiology, accounting for 59% of cases.

Of the 23 patients who underwent invasive assessment, RHC confirmed PH in all cases: 18 with precapillary PH and 5 with combined pre‐ and post‐capillary PH. Pulmonary Function and Gas Exchange

The study cohort exhibited a characteristic physiological profile of fibrotic ILD. The mean FEV1 was 90.76 ± 19.71% predicted, with substantial individual variability. The mean TLC of 76.66 ± 16.56% predicted and DLCO of 51.38 ± 13.72% predicted indicate a predominantly mild restrictive ventilatory pattern with moderate gas exchange impairment across the total population.

While lung volumes did not significantly differ between diagnostic groups, pulmonary gas exchange was significantly more impaired in the PH cohort, with DLCO values of 43.44 ± 13.73% versus 59.11 ± 13.50% in clinically low‐probability controls (p = 0.002). ROC analysis confirmed good discriminatory capacity of DLCO for RHC‐confirmed PH with area under the curve (AUC) of 0.799 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.653–0.945, p = 0.002) (Figure 2). The optimal balanced cut‐off for DLCO was 50.5% predicted, corresponding to a sensitivity of 72% and specificity of 72%.

Exercise and Ventilatory Efficiency

In CPET, VE/VCO2 slope was the only parameter that differentiated the groups, being significantly higher in the PH cohort (47.00 ± 7.90 vs. 40.71 ± 8.48, p = 0.035). ROC analysis for the VE/VCO2 slope showed fair discrimination with an AUC of 0.711 (95% CI 0.535–0.887, p = 0.038). A clinically balanced threshold of 41.5 provided 75% sensitivity and 59% specificity (Figure 3).

During the 6MWT, total distance did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.187). In contrast, patients with PH had significantly lower post‐exercise SpO2 (87.61 ± 10.37% vs. 94.32 ± 5.93%, p = 0.020) and a higher post‐exercise heart rate (110.83 ± 21.30 vs. 96.68 ± 8.96 bpm, p = 0.016).

Hemodynamic Phenotypes

An exploratory sub‐analysis was performed including the five patients with combined pre‐ and post‐capillary PH. No baseline non‐invasive marker from PFT, CPET, or 6MWT significantly differentiated the 18 precapillary PH patients from the five combined PH patients. This underscores that RHC remains mandatory to accurately isolate post‐capillary component of PH.

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that pulmonary vascular involvement in fibrotic ILD is better reflected by gas‐exchange impairment and ventilatory inefficiency rather than by static lung volumes. Specifically, DLCO and VE/VCO2 slope successfully discriminated RHC‐confirmed precapillary PH from clinically low‐probability ILD patients, whereas FEV1, FVC, and TLC did not.

The lack of significant difference in lung volumes between groups suggests that PH in fibrotic ILD contributes to physiological impairment independently of the restrictive defect. DLCO integrates alveolar‐capillary membrane function and pulmonary capillary blood volume, making it sensitive to pulmonary vascular loss. Our identified cut‐off of 50.5% is higher than the 40%–45% predicted threshold reported in advanced IPF cohorts [5, 9]. This difference likely reflects our inclusion of an earlier‐stage, unselected fibrotic ILD population in clinically low‐probability control. Findings reinforce DLCO as a sensitive, albeit non‐specific, early screening marker for vascular remodeling in this population [2, 10, 11].

The VE/VCO2 slope was the only CPET parameter significantly associated with PH in our cohort, reflecting ventilatory inefficiency driven by physiological dead space and ventilation‐perfusion mismatch [12, 13, 14]. The failure of other CPET variables (e.g., VO2peak) to reach significance may be attributed to our limited sample size and the confounding effects of lung restriction. Exercise testing parameters including elevated VE/VCO2 slope and reduced VO2peak have been associated with disease severity and even outcomes in ILD cohorts, reinforcing the clinical relevance of these measures [15, 16].

In our cohort, 6MWT distance did not discriminate RHC‐confirmed PH, suggesting that patient functional capacity is strongly influenced by frailty and musculoskeletal limitation [17]. Also, mechanical and ventilatory constraints of the underlying fibrotic ILD dictate submaximal walking capacity, masking the specific hemodynamically limiting effects of concomitant PH. In contrast, post‐exercise oxygen desaturation and heart rate responses better reflected cardiopulmonary reserve, consistent with findings from previous PH studies [18]. These observations align with the underlying pathophysiology of ILD‐related PH: because stroke volume is restricted by the compromised pulmonary vasculature, the cardiovascular system must rely on a compensatory increase in heart rate to maintain adequate cardiac output during exertion.

Notably, no physiological parameter differentiated precapillary PH from combined pre‐ and post‐capillary PH. RHC therefore remains essential not only for confirming PH but also for accurate hemodynamic classification [1, 19]. Furthermore, identification of occult post‐capillary disease sometimes requires fluid‐challenge or exercise testing during RHC, which we did not perform.

Beyond diagnostic classification, the clinical utility of identifying PH in our cohort was evidenced by subsequent management changes. Following RHC confirmation and PH‐MDT review, three patients were initiated on targeted pulmonary vasodilator therapy, and six patients were enrolled in a specialized PH rehabilitation program. Only three patients started receiving targeted vasodilator therapy following RHC because current international guidelines explicitly warn against non‐selective therapy in group 3 PH, primarily those with severe vascular disease (PVR > 5 WU) given the potential risk of worsening ventilation‐perfusion mismatch and hypoxemia. Furthermore, inhaled treprostinil, currently the only therapy supported by randomized clinical trial evidence for PH associated with ILD, is not yet available in Lithuania. Consequently, management of remaining patients focused on individualized optimization of supportive care, including adjustment of diuretic therapy in patients with elevated wedge pressure, referral for pulmonary rehabilitation, and assessment for lung transplantation when appropriate. These interventions underscore that physiological screening is not merely a diagnostic exercise but a critical step toward specialized management pathways.

In conclusion, while non‐invasive physiological indices like DLCO and VE/VCO2 slope cannot replace echocardiography or RHC, they provide valuable signals of underlying pulmonary vascular disease. Impaired gas exchange (DLCO < 50.5%) and ventilator inefficiency (VE/VCO2 slope > 41.5) should increase clinical suspicion and prompt dedicated cardiopulmonary evaluation for ILD‐related PH.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single‐center exploratory study with modest sample size. Diagnostic analyses used an extreme‐group design comparing RHC‐confirmed precapillary PH with clinically low‐probability patients while excluding intermediate‐risk cases. This approach likely overestimates discriminatory performance relative to an unselected ILD population, introducing verification bias and potentially inflating AUC estimates. Second, clinically low‐probability patients did not undergo RHC, so occult PH cannot be excluded, introducing reference standard bias. Third, although TTE guided initial risk stratification, final RHC referral was determined by PH‐MDT consensus using all available clinical data. Also, while CPET parameters might provide robust diagnostic utility, we have to admit that it is a specialized tool that is not universally available. Furthermore, the initial stratification into clinically low, intermediate and high‐probability groups relied partly on MDT consensus, particularly in defining concepts such as “disproportionate dyspnea”—these clinician‐defined categories are inherently subjective and may introduce selection bias. Finally, our study included a heterogeneous group of fibrotic lung diseases, and disease‐specific analyses were not feasible. No internal or external validation cohort was available.

Conclusions

In fibrotic ILD, reduced DLCO and an elevated VE/VCO2 slope are associated with RHC‐confirmed precapillary PH. Our findings suggest that these widely available physiological measures can help identify ILD patients who should be prioritized for echocardiography and invasive hemodynamic assessment for PH.
