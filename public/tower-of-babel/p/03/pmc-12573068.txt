Cross‐Contamination With Escherichia Coli, Listeria Monocytogenes, and Murine Norovirus During Potato Washing and Their Inactivation Using Peroxyacetic Acid and Aqueous Chlorine Dioxide
Authors: Hyojin Kwon, Daseul Yeo, Danbi Yoon, Seongwon Hwang, Dong Jae Lim, Songfeng Jin, Dong‐Un Lee, Changsun Choi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study evaluated the effectiveness of washing potatoes inoculated with Escherichia coli, Listeria monocytogenes, and murine norovirus (MNV) through agitation with peroxyacetic acid (PAA) and chlorine dioxide. Potential cross‐contamination during simultaneous washing of inoculated and uninoculated potatoes and the effect of water reuse were also assessed. E. coli showed a reduction of 1.86–2.51 log colony‐forming units (CFU)/potato when treated with 40 ppm PAA and 1.98–2.97 log CFU/potato when treated with 80 ppm chlorine dioxide (ClO2), both for 1–5 min. L. monocytogenes showed a reduction of 2.59 log CFU/potato when treated with 120 ppm PAA for 1 min and 2.55 log CFU/potato when treated with 80 ppm ClO2 for 5 min. MNV showed a reduction of 2.28 log CFU/potato when treated with ≥40 ppm PAA for 5 min; however, no significant reduction was noted on treatment with ClO2. During tap water washing, cross‐contamination with E. coli and L. monocytogenes occurred at 3.01 and 3.22 log CFU/potato, respectively; however, MNV was not detected because of its higher LOD. PAA completely inhibited E. coli transfer (below the LOD), whereas ClO2 allowed limited transfer (0.83–1.30 log CFU/potato). However, L. monocytogenes transfer persisted in both treatments (2.74–3.46 log CFU/potato), showing only partial mitigation compared to tap water washing. Reuse of wash water significantly increased cross‐contamination with E. coli and L. monocytogenes (p < 0.05); however, MNV transfer was not observed under any condition. For eradicating other types of microorganisms, the concentration must be determined according to the characteristics of each microorganism.Practical ApplicationsThis study revealed that washing potatoes with disinfectants like PAA or ClO2 can reduce harmful bacteria and viruses. PAA was effective even at concentrations below the regulatory limits. Hence, it may help prevent cross‐contamination during bulk washing processes in food production facilities and kitchen environments.

Introduction

The safety of agricultural products is crucial for protecting public health. In terms of human consumption, potatoes are the third most important food crop globally, following rice and wheat. Moreover, potatoes are the most widely consumed root vegetable (Devaux et al. 2014). Potato consumption is steadily rising with the increasing demand for processed potato products and the expansion of the food industry (The Business Research Company 2024). However, the presence of potentially harmful microorganisms, particularly enteric bacteria and viruses, on the surface of potatoes is a significant concern (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2022; Hashimoto et al. 2023; Tuytschaever et al. 2024). Based on data during 2016–2023 from the national outbreak reporting system of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, approximately 1109 cases of foodborne illness caused by Escherichia coli, Bacillus spp., norovirus, and Salmonella spp. were reported in relation to the consumption of potatoes, potato salads, and potato‐based sandwiches. In frozen potato processing, contamination with Listeria monocytogenes has recently been reported as a long‐term challenge, with the pathogen being recurrently detected for more than three years in and around the freezing tunnel (Tessa et al. 2025). While research is being conducted to ensure microbiological safety in various foods, there is a relative lack of research specifically focused on root vegetables.

In this study, potatoes were selected as a model root vegetable because of their high susceptibility to microbial contamination during storage and handling. Although they are typically stored under regulated temperature and humidity conditions to maintain freshness, such environments may inadvertently promote microbial proliferation (Cray et al. 2016; Liu et al. 2021). Moreover, physical characteristics of potatoes (such as the presence of cracks or bruises that occur during harvest and transport) may act as entry points for pathogen internalization (Ryser et al. 2009). As potatoes are commonly stored in bulk with limited separation, cross‐contamination between tubers via direct contact, shared moisture, or air circulation is a significant concern (Devaux et al. 2014). Hence, potatoes serve as a relevant model system for assessing disinfection strategies under practical postharvest conditions, with particular attention to the washing process. In general, postharvest washing aims to not only remove contaminants, such as dust, dirt, and pesticide residues, from fresh produce but also prevent cross‐contamination during processing (López‐Gálvez et al. 2021; Sun et al. 2022). However, produce may be contaminated by pathogenic microorganisms at both preharvest and postharvest stages, particularly when exposed to contaminated soil or irrigation water. In such cases, the pathogens may not be effectively eliminated during washing. Instead, cross‐contamination may occur (Alegbeleye et al. 2018). Moreover, during tank washing, microorganisms and viruses on the surface of produce can be released into recycled wash water, spreading contaminants to other items within the same batch (Sun et al. 2022). The washing of potatoes is recognized as one of the most water‐intensive steps in food processing. According to the best available techniques (BAT) reference document for the food, drink, and milk Industries published by the European commission, the washing stage of plant‐based raw materials, such as potatoes, consumes the highest amount of water in the sector, with the total water consumption typically ranging from 1.25 to 10 L/kg of raw potatoes (European Commission 2019; Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry 2023). The reuse of wash water has become a common practice for reducing the overall water consumption (European Commission 2019). However, this practice introduces potential risks of cross‐contamination, as pathogens can be transferred between tubers through shared water systems. Despite these risks, there is a lack of simulation‐based studies evaluating the effectiveness of washing in reducing microbial contamination, particularly in root vegetables. To address these concerns, the appropriate selection and application of disinfection methods during washing are essential for microbial control in food processing.

In agricultural processing, washing is considered a crucial step in which various nonheat disinfection methods are performed to ensure cleanliness and safety (Oms‐Oliu et al. 2010; Han 2014; Raffo and Paoletti 2022; Urango et al. 2022). Selection and application of the most suitable method for food are crucial, as different disinfection techniques have distinct advantages and disadvantages. Sodium hypochlorite (NaClO) is the most widely used product for economic purposes. It can effectively inactivate microorganisms in food processing (Elano et al. 2010; Ersoy et al. 2019; Lee and Huang 2019). However, NaClO has many limitations due to the production of chlorine by‐products (Cheng et al. 2022; Sánchez‐Montes et al. 2023). Some nations, such as the Netherlands, Switzerland, and Belgium, have prohibited the use of chlorine to ensure human and environmental safety (Chinchkar et al. 2022). PAA is a disinfectant known for its strong oxidizing properties. It is considered promising because of its low risk of producing harmful disinfection by‐products and its effectiveness in inactivating a wide range of pathogens (Bouchard et al. 2023; Lin et al. 2024). In a webinar hosted by the North American Potato Storage Organization (NAPSO), Dr. Vijay Choppakatla reported that the application of PAA to structural surfaces, equipment, and conveyors before storage could effectively suppress several common potato storage diseases, including late blight, pink rot, and Pythium leak, thereby improving the overall storability of potato tubers (NAPSO 2024). ClO2 is effective in both aqueous and gaseous forms. Given its convenient handling characteristics, it serves as a practical disinfectant in the food industry, especially if used in an aqueous solution (Luu et al. 2020; Jung et al. 2023). Although Park et al. (2008) studied the use of ClO2 and FIT produce wash for microbial control on fresh potatoes, their study was limited to a packing‐line flume system and did not simulate an actual washing process. Moreover, Luu et al. (2020) evaluated the effectiveness of aqueous ClO2 in reducing microbial contamination and preventing cross‐contamination on sweet potatoes.

Although PAA and ClO2 are widely approved and extensively studied as sanitizers for fresh produce, limited information is available regarding their effectiveness in reducing foodborne pathogens and mitigating cross‐contamination on root vegetables, such as potatoes, under laboratory‐scale washing simulations. As root vegetables, potatoes are inherently prone to microbial contamination through contact with soil and irrigation water. Therefore, the objectives of this study were to (i) evaluate the effectiveness of PAA and ClO2 in reducing E. coli, L. monocytogenes, and MNV on potatoes; (ii) assess the potential for cross‐contamination during the simultaneous washing of inoculated and uninoculated potatoes; and (iii) examine the influence of water reuse on pathogen transfer and disinfectant performance under laboratory‐scale washing conditions.

Materials and Methods

Bacterial and Viral Strains

E. coli strains (American Type Culture Collection [ATCC] 25922, ATCC 11775, and ATCC 10536) and L. monocytogenes strains (ATCC 19114 and ATCC 19115) were acquired from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA). The bacteria were acquired as stock cultures and preserved at −80°C in 1 mL aliquots of tryptic soy broth (TSB; BD Difco, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) supplemented with 20% glycerol. For the assays, the cultures were grown on TSB with aeration at 37°C for 18 h. After incubation, the bacterial cultures were collected and centrifuged at 4,000 ×g for 10 min. Subsequently, they were serially diluted 10‐fold in sterile phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS; 10 mmol/L, pH 7.4).

The RAW 264.7 cell line ATCC TIB‐71 was acquired from ATCC and cultured using Dulbecco's minimum essential medium (DMEM; Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA) and 1% antibiotic–antimycotic (penicillin–streptomycin; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) in a humidified incubator (37°C, 5% CO2). MNV was used as a surrogate for human norovirus to assess viral survival following disinfectant application. MNV (strain MNV‐1) was kindly provided by Professor Skip Virgin from the University of Washington. Viral stocks (1 × 107 PFU/mL) were prepared as per the protocol detailed by Wang (2024) and stored at −80°C until further use.

E. coli, L. monocytogenes, and MNV were selected as representative pathogens because of their frequent association with potato‐related foodborne outbreaks, their detection in potato processing environments, and their relevance as major bacterial and viral hazards that impact the safety of fresh produce (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2025; Tessa et al. 2025).

Sample Preparation and Inoculation

Potatoes with soil on their surface were purchased from local markets in Gangwon or Jeju, South Korea, to simulate industrial washing conditions. The samples were stored at 4°C for up to one week until use. Damaged potatoes were excluded, and fresh potatoes of similar size and weight (100 ± 10 g) were selected. Before inoculation, the absence of E. coli, L. monocytogenes, and MNV was confirmed as described in Sections 2.4 and 2.5. For inoculation, the potatoes were divided into three groups. A 1 mL aliquot of E. coli, L. monocytogenes, or MNV culture was applied to 15–20 spots on the surface of each potato in the respective group, according to the method of Ortiz‐Solà et al. (2020). The inoculated potatoes were then air‐dried for 2 h on a clean bench equipped with a running fan to facilitate microbial attachment (Guan et al. 2021). The initial concentrations of E. coli, L. monocytogenes, and MNV in the suspensions were measured as described in Sections 2.4 and 2.5.

Experimental Treatments

The potato washing process involved washing with fresh tap water, PAA (6%; Daesung C and S Co. Ltd., Seoul, South Korea), and aqueous ClO2 (1%; Sungchan Co. Ltd., Seoul, South Korea) at a volume equivalent to five times the weight of the potatoes (2.5 L). To simulate the vegetable washing tank conditions, shaking was performed using a shaking incubator (SI‐600R; Jeio Tech, Daejeon, Korea) operating at 60 rpm (López‐Gálvez et al. 2019).

Disinfectant Treatments

Washing treatments for potatoes included washing in the prepared potato wash water without a disinfectant (tap water; < 0.1 ppm free residual chlorine), washing in PAA at three target concentrations (40, 80, and 120 ppm), and washing in ClO2 at three target concentrations (20, 50, and 80 ppm) (Jung et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2024). The concentration of PAA in the wash and residual solutions was determined using a PAA test kit (PAA indicator strips, 0–50 and 0–500 ppm; Johnson, UK), in accordance with legal standards and the manufacturer's instructions. The ClO2 concentration was measured using a ClO2 test kit (ClO2 test strips, 0–500 ppm; LaMotte, USA) and a free chlorine test kit (Free Chlorine Water Check, 0.0–6.0 ppm; SenSafe, USA), in accordance with legal standards and the manufacturer's instructions. The experiments were independently repeated thrice, each with duplicate measurements. Data are expressed as the mean ± SD of three independent experiments.

Cross‐Contamination Simulation

Water‐mediated cross‐contamination during washing with disinfectants was evaluated through a simulation, as shown in Figure 1. In the cross‐contamination simulation, each batch underwent a 5 min washing process using tap water, 80 ppm PAA, and 80 ppm ClO2. Scenario 1 assessed whether cross‐contamination would occur from one infected potato to four uninoculated potatoes when washed in the same batch. Scenario 2 examined the amount of cross‐contamination of uninoculated potatoes from previously washed inoculated potatoes when the wash water was reused in another batch (Possas and Pérez‐Rodríguez 2023). PAA and ClO2 concentrations were monitored at each wash water sampling point using commercial test strips. When necessary, additional disinfectant was added to maintain the target concentration of 80 ppm.

Cross‐contamination scenarios during washing. Scenario 1: Cross‐contamination assessment by washing one inoculated potato and four uninoculated potatoes simultaneously for 5 min. Scenario 2: cross‐contamination assessment by repeatedly washing five inoculated potatoes and five uninoculated potatoes in the same tank up to 10 times.

Potatoes that were deliberately inoculated with the virus and bacteria were included as positive controls. These potatoes were not subjected to any washing process. Subsequently, the collected water was treated with a 10% sodium thiosulfate solution to achieve 1:1 neutralization. Potatoes that tested negative for MNV, E. coli, and L. monocytogenes were used as negative controls. Each treatment was measured in two replicates, and all experiments were independently duplicated.

Bacterial Quantification

From each treated sample and positive control (inoculated potato sample not subjected to any washing process), 25 g of potato peels were meticulously cut into 2.5 × 2.5 cm pieces. The samples were added to 225 mL of sterile saline solution (0.85% NaCl) in a sterile stomacher bag and homogenized for 2 min in a stomacher (HAPS H2‐1; Huko FS Co. Ltd., South Korea). Serial dilutions were prepared in the same diluents and plated onto EC Petrifilm (3 M, USA) and L. monocytogenes selective agar base (Oxford formulation; Oxoid, UK) for the enumeration of E. coli and L. monocytogenes, respectively. The plates were incubated at 37°C for 24‒48 h. In the cross‐contamination experiment, 100 mL of water used for washing the potatoes inoculated with bacteria was collected and analyzed using the same method. All the results were expressed as log10 CFU (log CFU) per potato or per 100 mL of water.

Detection of MNV Using a Plaque Assay

Potato samples were pretreated as per the guidelines outlined in ISO/TS 15216‐1:2019 (Wang et al. 2024). To adhere to the test protocol as closely as possible, 25 g of potato peels were meticulously cut into 2.5 × 2.5 cm pieces and then placed into a filter bag. Subsequently, 40 mL of Tris/glycine/beef extract buffer was introduced into the filter bag to initiate the elution of the virus. The eluates were incubated for 20 min at 25°C with constant shaking at 120 rpm and were then meticulously transferred into a centrifuge tube. The supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 30 min at 4°C. Its pH was subsequently adjusted to 7.0 by adding 5 M HCl. The eluted virus was concentrated by adding 0.25 volumes of 5× PEG 8000/NaCl solution, followed by incubation for 60 min at 4°C with constant shaking at 120 rpm. After centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 30 min at 4°C, the resulting pellets were resuspended in 1000 mL of PBS. In the cross‐contamination experiment, 100 mL of water used for washing the potatoes inoculated with MNV was collected and analyzed using paper filtration‐coupled ultrafiltration (Vivaspin; Sartorius, Germany) (Wang et al. 2022). Virus samples were filtered using a minimum 0.45 µm‐pore‐size filter (BioFACT, Daejeon, South Korea) to eliminate impurities for the plaque assay and stored at −80°C until use.

The MNV plaque assay was performed as described by Lee (2015), with minor modifications. In brief, RAW 264.7 cells were seeded at a density of 0.8 × 106 cells/well in 12‐well cell culture plates and incubated overnight to 37°C. Filtered virus samples were serially diluted by a factor of 10, and 400‐µL aliquots of the diluted solutions were then introduced onto 80% confluent cell monolayers. Following a 1 h incubation to facilitate the attachment of the virus to the cells, the cell monolayer was overlaid with DMEM supplemented with 0.6% agarose type II (Sigma‐Aldrich) that had been preheated to 42°C. After 36–48 h, the cells were fixed using 10% formaldehyde (Sigma‐Aldrich) for 4 h. The agarose overlay was then removed meticulously, and the cells were stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution (Invitrogen, CA, USA) for 15 min. MNV titers were quantified and expressed as log10 plaque‐forming units (log PFU) per potato or per 100 mL of water.

Statistical Analysis

The experiments described in Section 2.3.1 were independently repeated thrice on separate weeks, while those described in Section 2.3.2 were repeated twice on separate weeks. In all experiments, three serial dilutions were prepared for each condition, and two independent samples per condition were analyzed in duplicate. One‐way Analysis Of VAriance (ANOVA) was performed using SPSS Statistics 28 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) to analyze differences among group means, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene's test. The post hoc test was selected based on the results of Levene's test and sample size balance: Tukey's HSD was selected for equal variances with balanced sample sizes, Scheffé’s test for equal variances with unequal sample sizes, and Dunnett's T3 for unequal variances (Lee and Lee 2018). Bacterial counts and viral titers were expressed as log10 values.

Results and Discussion

Reduction of E. coli, L. monocytogenes, and MNV on Potatoes After Washing With PAA and ClO2

Before treatment, the initial inoculation concentrations were 6.02 ± 0.64 log CFU/potato for E. coli, 7.23 ± 0.31 log PFU/potato for L. monocytogenes, and 5.01 ± 0.41 log PFU/potato for MNV.

On washing with PAA, E. coli showed a more substantial reduction (p < 0.05) than the with tap water (1.86−2.51 vs. 1.11−1.50 log CFU/potato; Figure 2A). Treatment with 120 ppm PAA for 3 min led to a 0.68 log higher reduction than treatment with 40 ppm PAA (p < 0.05). Treatment with ClO2 resulted in a reduction of 1.46–2.97 log CFU/potato. In particular, treatment with 80 ppm ClO2 for 3–5 min resulted in a significant reduction of 2.92 ± 0.34 log CFU/potato (p < 0.05; Figure 2B). While PAA was effective even when applied at 40 ppm and for 1 min, ClO2 led to a notable reduction only when applied at concentrations over 80 ppm.

In the case of L. monocytogenes, the reduction during PAA treatment ranged from 2.14 to 2.59 log CFU/potato. In contrast, the negative control showed a relatively lower reduction, ranging from 1.34 to 2.05 log CFU/potato (Figure 2C). A significant difference (1.25 log) was noted compared to the control group on treatment with 120 ppm PAA for 1 min (p < 0.05). Treatment with ClO2 led to an overall reduction of 1.63–2.59 log CFU/potato, with no reduction noted at concentrations of 20–50 ppm compared to tap water treatment (p > 0.05; Figure 2D).

In the case of MNV, treatment with 40−120 ppm PAA for 5 min exhibited a reduction effect comparable to that of the negative control (p < 0.05). Overall, a reduction of 1.97–2.68 log PFU/potato was observed (Figure 2E). Likewise, treatment with ClO2 exhibited a reduction effect similar to that of the negative control (p > 0.05; 1.62–1.95 log PFU/potato; Figure 2F).

Significant reductions in microbial populations and inhibition of cross‐contamination were observed when PAA and ClO2 were used during the potato washing process. Both PAA and ClO2 were tested at concentrations within the regulatory limits in South Korea and at higher concentrations to explore their maximum effectiveness under laboratory conditions. In the field‐applied washing process, two to three rinse cycles are performed after the application of ClO2 to ensure the removal of residual chlorine (Food and Drug Administration 2025; Trinetta et al. 2011). Although some studies have assessed the viability of human noroviruses, they are hindered by high limits of detection (LODs) and limitations in assessing viability (Tan et al. 2023; Wales et al. 2024). In this study, saline solution was used to maintain osmotic stability and suppress bacterial growth during homogenization, facilitating quantitative enumeration. However, replacing buffered peptone water (BPW) with saline may have reduced the recovery of disinfectant‐stressed or injured cells, representing a methodological limitation. MNV was used as a surrogate for human norovirus.

PAA significantly reduced E. coli, L. monocytogenes, and MNV on potatoes even when applied at low concentrations (40 ppm) and for short durations (1 min), whereas ClO2 needed to be applied at relatively higher concentrations (≥80 ppm) to exhibit notable effects. In a previous study, when finely chopped carrots underwent a washing process involving shaking for 2 min, the concentrations of E. coli O157:H7 and L. monocytogenes were reduced by less than 0.5 log when tap water was used (Ruiz‐Cruz et al. 2007). The use of 40 ppm PAA only resulted in a reduction of 1.24 log CFU/g for E. coli O157:H7 and 0.83 log CFU/g for L. monocytogenes (Ruiz‐Cruz et al. 2007). The use of PAA in washing procedures involving shaking led to a further reduction of at least 0.5 log in our study. Luu (2020) compared the effectiveness of 5 ppm ClO2 with distilled water (DW) in reducing E. coli O157 and L. monocytogenes on sweet potatoes. They noted that ClO2 initially performed better than DW; however, after 30 min, both treatments were equally effective against E. coli O157. ClO2 significantly reduced L. monocytogenes after 30 min, whereas DW had minimal impact (Luu et al. 2020). This suggests that the effectiveness of ClO2 in reducing microbes depends on the exposure time and pathogen. Longer exposure to ClO2 slightly enhances effectiveness, especially against L. monocytogenes, aligning with previous studies that recommend longer treatment durations for achieving significant reductions (Kim et al. 2008; Vaid et al. 2010; Luu et al. 2020). Kim (2021) studied the effectiveness of PAA and ClO2 in controlling MNV on carrots and lotus roots. They noted a reduction of less than 1.0 log on treatment with 100 ppm PAA and 100 ppm ClO2 and more than 2.0 log on treatment with 400 ppm PAA and 300 ppm ClO2 (Kim et al. 2021). In our study, even in the absence of a disinfectant, more than 2.0 log reduction was observed when shaking and washing were performed. MNV was significantly reduced after washing with 40 ppm PAA for 5 min. This confirms a significant reduction effect at lower concentrations compared to previous findings (Ruiz‐Cruz et al. 2007; Kim et al. 2008; Huang and Nitin 2017; Luu et al. 2020). When washed with 80 ppm PAA for 5 min, MNV was reduced by over 3.0 log on blueberries and strawberries (Bouchard et al. 2023); however, no significant effect was observed in the case of potatoes. This suggests that the washing effect varies depending on the influence of organic matter, such as dirt and dust in the wash water, and the characteristics of the surface of the agricultural product (Mattison et al. 2007; Yuan et al. 2017; Huang et al. 2020; Zheng et al. 2021).

Cross‐Contamination Scenarios in Potato Washing Simulations

In Scenarios 1 and 2, washing with PAA and ClO2 was proven effective in preventing cross‐contamination with E. coli and MNV. Cross‐contamination can arise from three main sources: (1) contact among produce items within the same washing batch; (2) the transmission of pathogens to the surface of uncontaminated produce via water; and (3) the transfer of pathogens present in the water to the surface of uncontaminated produce (Gombas et al. 2017; Abnavi et al. 2019).

In Scenario 1, the initial inoculation concentrations were 6.13 ± 0.21 log CFU/potato for E. coli, 6.66 ± 0.12 log CFU/potato for L. monocytogenes, and 5.28 ± 0.68 log PFU/potato for MNV. When washed with tap water, cross‐contamination with E. coli and L. monocytogenes was detected at 3.01 ± 0.20 and 3.22 ± 0.38 log CFU/potato, respectively, on uninoculated potatoes (Table 1). Cross‐contamination with MNV, which has a high LOD of 2.0 log PFU per potato or per 100 mL of wash water, was not detected even when washed with tap water. When washed with PAA and ClO2, cross‐contamination with E. coli was not detected in the uninoculated potatoes (p < 0.05). Furthermore, L. monocytogenes concentrations of 3.12 ± 0.51 and 3.06 ± 0.37 log CFU/potato, after washing with PAA and ClO2, were comparable to those observed when washed with tap water (p > 0.05). When a disinfectant was used, E. coli, L. monocytogenes, and MNV were detected below the LODs in the wash water. While the spread plate method was used for both potato homogenates and wash water to enable direct quantitative comparison, it has a relatively higher LOD than membrane filtration commonly used in potable water testing. Thus, while microbial populations without disinfectants exceeded 5 log CFU/100 mL and enabled reliable quantification, this method was less sensitive for detecting low‐level cross‐contamination. This represents a limitation of our study.

In Scenario 2, washing with tap water led to cross‐contamination with E. coli, L. monocytogenes, and MNV from inoculated to uninoculated potatoes, as well as to wash water (Figure 3, 4, and 5A). Cross‐contamination with E. coli and L. monocytogenes gradually increased with the cumulative number of tap water washes (p < 0.05), whereas MNV remained constant (p > 0.05). Cross‐contamination with E. coli did not occur on uninoculated potatoes and in wash water when PAA was used (Figure 3B). However, when ClO2 was used, some cross‐contamination was confirmed (0.83–1.30 log CFU/potato) on uninoculated potatoes as the number of washes increased (Figure 3C). Cross‐contamination with L. monocytogenes occurred at 2.74–3.06 and 2.84–3.46 log CFU/potato from inoculated to uninoculated potatoes on washing with PAA and ClO2, respectively (Figure 4B). L. monocytogenes was detected at 1.04 ± 1.08 and 1.45 ± 0.88 log CFU/100 mL in wash water during the third and fifth washes with PAA, respectively; however, it was not detected when ClO2 was used (Figure 4C). Cross‐contamination with MNV was not confirmed on uninoculated potatoes or in wash water when PAA and ClO2 were used (Figure 5B and 5C).

In this study, a 10% sodium thiosulfate solution was used at a 1:1 ratio to neutralize both ClO2 and PAA, as reported in previous studies (Baert et al. 2009; Hassaballah et al. 2020). However, we acknowledge that sodium thiosulfate is more commonly recommended for chlorine‐based disinfectants, while bisulfite‐based neutralizers may be more specific to PAA. This should be considered a methodological limitation when interpreting the results. In Scenario 1, cross‐contamination with E. coli and MNV could be effectively prevented through disinfection during the washing process. In Scenario 2, MNV was effectively controlled; however, some cross‐contamination with E. coli and L. monocytogenes was detected. Huang et al. (2020) conducted a cross‐contamination simulation and confirmed that when organic matter is present in water, bacteria move more easily to the plant surface, regardless of the washing speed. The presence of organic matter, such as dirt and peels generated during the washing step, may have accelerated bacterial cross‐contamination (Huang and Nitin 2017; Bolten et al. 2020; Huang et al. 2020).

Uninoculated potatoes occasionally showed detectable levels of pathogens even when the levels in the corresponding wash water samples were below the LOD. Wang and Ryser (2020) observed this same behavior in a pilot‐scale study. They found that disinfectant treatments (80 ppm PAA, 80 ppm mixed peracid, 80 ppm chlorine at pH 6.0, and 80 ppm chlorine) reduced Salmonella in wash water to below the LOD; however, 2.34–3.33 log CFU/g of Salmonella species were still present on diced tomato surfaces. Disinfectants can suppress microbial concentrations in water to levels at or below the LODs, while detached cells may rapidly reattach to produce surfaces. Consequently, measurable loads can be recovered from products despite nondetectable counts in water (Wang and Ryser 2020). Similar outcomes have been reported in dump tank simulations. In water with 1000 ppm COD, treatment with 80 mg/L PAA reduced but did not eliminate L. monocytogenes transfer, with approximately 2.6 log CFU being retained per apple and 1.4 log CFU being retained per mL of spent water (Wang et al. 2022). Methodological aspects, sampling strategies, and limits of chemical neutralization may also contribute to these discrepancies. Therefore, nondetection in wash water should not be interpreted as the absence of cross‐contamination risk on the product surface, particularly under realistic organic loads.

PAA inhibits the production of extracellular polymeric substances in E. coli (Liu et al. 2021). In L. monocytogenes Scott A, exposure to PAA led to the downregulation of the stress‐related genes fri and kat and the upregulation of lmo0669 (Danae et al. 2022). Thus, undamaged L. monocytogenes can still retain its ability to adhere to food surfaces (Danae et al. 2022). In contrast, cross‐contamination with MNV was not observed when the disinfectants PAA and ClO2 were used. These disinfectants exhibited greater effectiveness against MNV than against bacteria. The increased number of sulfur‐containing amino acids and tryptophan in the viral capsid may result in more reaction sites and enhanced inactivation during PAA treatment because of heightened chemical reactivity (Schmitz et al. 2021). ClO2 primarily inactivates viruses through capsid protein degradation rather than genomic damage, as it reacts faster with amino acids than with nucleotides, thereby causing protein damage that leads to inactivation before significant genomic damage occurs (Sigstam et al. 2013).

This study has a few limitations. First, potatoes were purchased with visible soil, likely increasing the organic matter content during repeated wash cycles; however, chemical oxygen demand (COD) was not measured. Although PAA is effective against Listeria at COD levels up to 4000 ppm (Wang et al. 2023), complete prevention of cross‐contamination cannot be guaranteed under high organic loads. Second, only culture‐based detection was performed, which may underestimate the persistence of pathogens, as PAA and ClO2 can induce a viable but nonculturable (VBNC) state in L. monocytogenes and E. coli O157:H7 (Truchado et al. 2022). Third, while the temperature of wash water was maintained at 15°C–25°C, pH and ORP were not monitored despite their known influence on disinfectant performance and cross‐contamination dynamics. Overall, PAA and ClO2 effectively reduced pathogens on potato surfaces and limited cross‐contamination; however, their effectiveness may decline with increasing organic matter contents and water quality variations. Future studies should perform direct monitoring of COD, pH, ORP, and temperature and employ detection methods that can identify VBNC states to more accurately reflect industrial conditions and optimize disinfectant application.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that both PAA and ClO2 can effectively reduce E. coli, L. monocytogenes, and MNV on potatoes and limit cross‐contamination during the washing process. This study is among the first to simulate water reuse and pathogen transfer in a laboratory‐scale washing system specifically for root vegetables, addressing a crucial gap in food safety research. The results revealed that the effectiveness of PAA and ClO2 varies depending on the microorganism. Treatment with 40 ppm PAA led to greater inactivation of E. coli and MNV than treatment with ClO2. Cross‐contamination was more likely to occur with E. coli and L. monocytogenes than with MNV, which was used as a surrogate for human norovirus. These findings suggest that the characteristics of target microorganisms should be carefully considered when selecting the disinfectant type and concentration. Overall, our findings highlight the importance of proper disinfectant management and water control strategies to minimize microbial risks, with practical implications for the potato processing industry. While PAA and ClO2 exhibit potential for reducing contamination and preventing cross‐contamination during water reuse, further research is warranted to optimize their application and to fully address the challenges associated with recycled wash water in commercial operations.
