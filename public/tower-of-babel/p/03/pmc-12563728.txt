Prevalence of Traumatic Dental Injuries to Anterior Teeth among 14- to 60-year-Old Patients in Mysuru, India: A Hospital-Based Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Paras M. Gehlot, Annapoorna B. Mariswamy, Nandlal Bhojraj, L Krishna Prasad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective:Traumatic dental injuries (TDIs) are a major public health concern, particularly affecting anterior teeth due to their prominent position. While global prevalence ranges from 6 to 59%, limited epidemiological data exist for Mysuru district, Karnataka, India. Hence, the objective of this study was to assess the prevalence of traumatic injuries to anterior teeth and associated factors in patients reporting to a tertiary care center in Mysuru city.Methods:A cross-sectional observational study was conducted from March to July 2022 at JSS Dental College and Hospital, Mysuru. Of 3,885 patients examined, 300 patients aged 14-60 years with anterior teeth trauma were included using convenience sampling. Data collection involved validated questionnaires, clinical examinations, and radiographic assessments. TDIs were classified according to Andreasen criteria. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS with significance set at P < 0.05.Results:The prevalence of TDI was 7.72% with a patient: tooth ratio of 1:2.5. The mean age of TDI was 29.27 ± 10.74 years, and males predominated (67.7%). Two-wheeler accidents were the leading cause (24.7%). Maxillary teeth were affected 8 times more than mandibular teeth (89.4% vs 10.6%). Central incisors showed the highest injury rates. Enamel-dentin fractures were most common (35%), followed by enamel fractures (25%). Only 21.7% sought immediate treatment, with significant treatment delays observed.Conclusions:The study reveals concerning patterns of TDI prevalence and delayed treatment seeking behavior. These findings emphasize the need for targeted prevention strategies, public awareness campaigns, and immediate treatment protocols to improve outcomes in this population.

INTRODUCTION

Traumatic dental injuries (TDIs) are a major concern in dental public health due to their frequency, particularly among young individuals. These injuries often occur in the anterior teeth due to their prominent position in the mouth, making them more susceptible to trauma. Globally, it is estimated that about one billion people have experienced TDIs, with anterior teeth being the most commonly affected due to their position and function.[1]

In recent years, extensive research in etiology, diagnostics, treatment, and potential complications of TDI in permanent teeth has been conducted, and the results on the global prevalence of dental trauma are estimated at 6–59%.[2]

This variability arises from differences in study types,[23] applied classification systems, studied population,[4] geographical region, and behavioral or cultural characteristics. Although various studies have reported the prevalence of dental trauma among different age groups and districts of Karnataka, varying from 9.7, 14.85, and 15.1% in Bangalore, Mangalore, and Davangere, respectively.[567] However, there has been no epidemiological research on dental trauma in the Mysuru district of Karnataka, India.

The objective of this paper was to assess the prevalence of traumatic injuries to anterior teeth and the associated factors in patients reporting to a tertiary care center in the south Indian city of Mysuru and to compare the results in a regional, national, and international context.

METHODOLOGY

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Committee (JSSDCH Research Protocol No. 03/2020) and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants (parents/guardians for minors). This cross-sectional observational study assessed traumatic dental injury (TDI) prevalence among patients aged 14-60 years reporting to the Department of Conservative Dentistry and Endodontics, JSS Dental College and Hospital, Mysuru city.

Using convenience sampling with an estimated prevalence of 6.96-7.86%,[89] the sample size was calculated using n = Z²α/2 × p × (1-p) / d² (95% CI, 3% margin of error). With a 10% adjustment for nonresponse, the final sample size was 300. Data were collected from March to July 2022 using a validated Google form questionnaire incorporating demographic and trauma-related variables (including Andreasen classification) and clinical examination of permanent anterior teeth. Of 3885 patients examined, 300 with anterior teeth trauma were interviewed. Inclusion criteria: Patients with anterior teeth TDIs aged 14-60 years, able to provide informed consent. Exclusion criteria: Cognitive impairments, nontraumatic conditions, emergency cases unable to complete surveys, previous study participation, systemic illness/disabilities, or orthodontic treatment. A single calibrated investigator conducted face-to-face interviews with responses recorded directly into Google forms. Intraexaminer reliability was assessed by re-examining 10% of patients.

Examinations were performed under standard dental chair lighting using sterilized instruments (mouth mirror, WHO probe, explorer, cotton rolls, cheek retractor). Assessments included extraoral and intraoral examination of anterior teeth, measurement of fractures and overjet using WHO probe, pulp sensibility testing (electric/cold testing), mobility assessment, and percussion testing. TDIs were classified according to Andreasen criteria. Intraoral periapical radiographs and orthopantomograms confirmed diagnoses. Clinical photographs documented injuries when appropriate.

Data were recorded in Excel and analyzed using SPSS with significance set at P < 0.05. Descriptive statistics included means ± SD for continuous variables and frequencies/percentages for categorical variables. Chi-square tests assessed associations between categorical variables.

RESULTS

A total of 3885 patients attended the outpatient Department. Among these, 300 required treatment and care for traumatized anterior teeth, yielding a prevalence of 7.72%. The study participants presented with 754 traumatized anterior teeth, giving a patient: tooth ratio of 1:2.5.

All demographic and anthropometric variables exhibited statistically significant distributions (P<0.001) except for population type (urban vs. rural), indicating a diverse composition among most characteristics, but similar representation between urban and rural subgroups.

The distribution of Traumatic Dental Injuries by Age and Gender is shown in Table 1. The mean age of participants was 29.27 ± 10.74 years. Males comprised 67.7% (n = 203) and females 32.3% (n = 97) (P < 0.001). Rural residents predominated (55.3% vs 44.7% urban, P = 0.065). Most participants had university/degree education (35.3%), followed by high school (27.7%) and preuniversity (27%) education (P < 0.001). TDI was more prevalent in students (25.7%), compared to other occupational groups (P < 0.001). Mean height and weight were 1.61 ± 0.14 meters and 62.60 ± 14.16 kg, respectively. BMI distribution showed 46% TDI in normal weight, 28% in overweight, 15.3% in underweight, and 10.6% in obese (P < 0.001).

Trauma-associated factors revealed significant patterns (P < 0.001). The most common age at trauma was 19-30 years (36.3%). Most participants sought immediate treatment (21.7%), while 11.5% reported either within one week or between 5-10 years after trauma. About 16.3% reported after >10 years. Previous dental trauma was uncommon (6%). Trauma predominantly occurred during 11 am to 5 pm (46.3%) and the location of TDI was outdoors (69%). Two-wheeler accidents were the leading cause (24.7%), followed by home-related impacts (16.7%). Protective equipment was not applicable in most cases (58.7%), with only 7.3% using protection when applicable. Weekdays showed higher trauma incidence (47.3%) compared to weekends (21.3%) while 31.3% of the participants did not remember the day of trauma.

Most participants demonstrated adequate lip competency (77.7%, n = 233) versus inadequate competency (22.3%, n = 67) (P < 0.001). Overjet measurements showed significant variation across groups, with 48.3% cases falling within 0-3.5mm, 32% of cases with 3.5-6 mm, and 13.3% with 6-9 mm of overjet.

DISCUSSION

A comprehensive review of traumatic dental injuries over 12 years highlights that dental trauma accounts for about 5% of all injuries for which people seek treatment, highlighting the importance of understanding the distribution of traumatic dental injuries (TDIs) across different age groups.[4]

Epidemiological data of TDIs have not been reported for the population of Mysuru. The prevalence of dental trauma is in the present study is 7.72% in the present study, which is in agreement with a study by Shubham et al.[9] The global prevalence of dental trauma is estimated at 6–59%.[2] and the pooled prevalence of TDI in the Indian population as reported by Tewari et al.[10] was 13%. However, various other studies have reported a prevalence ranging from 4.15% in India, 8.4% in France, and 27.7% in the United Kingdom.[111213] The difference in prevalence might be due to different factors such as the trauma classification, the dentition studied, socioeconomic, behavioral, cultural diversities, and geographical variations of the study’s locations.[14]

In the present study the mean age of participants was 29.27 (±10.74) years, which is in agreement with previous study.[8] The highest trauma incidence in the 19-30 years range reflects the vulnerability of young adults, who are often more active and exposed to accidents, especially those involving two-wheelers, the leading cause in the present study. From these findings, it is evident that age plays a crucial role in the prevalence of dental trauma.[15]

Demographically, the predominance of males (67.7%) over females (32.3%) is a recurrent pattern in TDI research, typically attributed to higher risk-taking behaviors and greater exposure to trauma risk factors among males.[3]

The lack of a statistically significant difference between urban and rural populations suggests that TDI risk transcends residential settings, although rural predominance in the present study might reflect local demographics or healthcare access. Various studies report that the incidence of injuries to anterior teeth is significantly higher in rural areas due to activities specific to rural settings.[1617] However, contrary, various studies reported a higher incidence of dental injuries in urban than rural ones, which was attributed to greater population density, lifestyle differences, and the availability of trauma care facilities.[418]

Educational status and occupation patterns, with students being most affected, may relate to lifestyle and activity levels that increase trauma risk. Various studies have highlighted the correlation between academic performance and the prevalence of TDI, suggesting that children with lower academic achievements might be more prone to such traumas.[1920]

The BMI distribution showing higher TDI in normal weight and overweight categories could point toward activity levels or behavioral factors influencing trauma risk. A study by Sorian et al.[21] reported no statistically significant relationship between obesity and dental trauma. However, another study found that obesity, alongside socioeconomic factors, contributes to the likelihood of experiencing dental trauma.[22]

The reporting time of treatment is critical in TDI prognosis; the current study finding that only 21.7% sought immediate care while a considerable portion delayed treatment by weeks or even years, underscores a major challenge in managing dental trauma. Delayed treatment is known to adversely impact healing outcomes, increasing risks of pulp necrosis or root resorption, as noted in previous research emphasizing the importance of prompt intervention.[2324]

The 11 am to 5 pm peak (46.3%) and weekday predominance (47.3%) reflect typical activity patterns, while outdoor location predominance (69%) supports lifestyle-related trauma exposure.

The main causes for dental trauma in children are falls and injuries sustained during play, whereas in adolescents and adults, more complex traumas may occur, caused by sports accidents, fights, and road traffic accidents (RTAs). The etiological factors varied according to the age group studied, as the majority of TDI cases resulted from falls in children aged 6-10 and 11-15 years.[25] In the present study, two-wheeler accidents were the leading cause (24.7%) of trauma in adults. Chaotic traffic and poor implementation of road safety rules in developing countries contribute to the high incidence of TDIs.[26]

Most injuries involved maxillary anterior teeth, especially central incisors, following a pattern well established in the literature.[27] The majority of TDIs involved the maxillary central incisor, around 75% which is in agreement with previous studies.[2829] The maxillary lateral incisor was the second most commonly involved tooth (31%), as also verified by Rocha and Cardoso.[30] Canines were least affected by trauma.

Research indicates that conditions such as increased overjet and anterior open bite, when combined with inadequate lip coverage, significantly elevate the likelihood of dental trauma in children and adolescents. The findings in the present study are in agreement with previous studies.[3132]

Enamel fractures (EF) and enamel-dentin fractures (ED) appeared as the most common hard tissue injuries across both jaws. These findings are in agreement with previous studies.[3334] More severe injuries, involving enamel, dentin, and pulp (EDP), were reported to be 19% in maxillary teeth; however, a study reported the incidence of complicated crown fracture to vary from 2 to 13%.[35]

Soft tissue injuries had varied distributions, with avulsion being relatively frequent, with 7.17% in maxillary teeth and 3.3% in mandibular teeth; however, a study reported 3% avulsion in the south Indian population reported at a hospital.[33]

Implications and limitations

The findings can help develop preventive strategies and formulate definite clinical decisions with remedial protocols.[36] The cross-sectional study design and hospital-based sampling may introduce selection bias, and retrospective trauma history collection is subject to recall bias.

CONCLUSIONS

This study reinforces established TDI epidemiological patterns while highlighting persistent challenges in prevention and timely treatment. The multitooth involvement pattern, concerning treatment delays, and low protective equipment use underscore the need for integrated clinical and public health approaches. These findings contribute valuable regional data and emphasize the importance of targeted prevention strategies and immediate treatment protocols.
