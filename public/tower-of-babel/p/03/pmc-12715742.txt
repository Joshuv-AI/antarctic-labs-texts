Sampling Probes Microfabricated from Parylene‑C for In Vivo Neurochemical Monitoring
Authors: Thomas White, Ian Bain, Caitlin N. Cain, Pavlo Popov, Charles R. Evans, Robert T. Kennedy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Monitoring chemistry in the central nervous system in vivo is an important route to better understand chemical signals and metabolism underlying brain function. Sampling from the brain extracellular space allows for in depth and multiplexed analysis of this compartment. Miniaturized sampling devices prepared by using microfabrication are emerging as tools that allow high spatial resolution measurements with low invasiveness. In this work, we describe a microfabrication method for Parylene-C-based push–pull sampling probes. Parylene-C is an attractive material because its biocompatibility and flexibility may allow for chronic measurements. Probes with 3 mm long shanks and 45 × 166 μm cross sections were prepared and tested for suitability for in vivo experiments. Two designs, one with push–pull orifices on the probe tip and the other with the orifices recessed ∼40 mm within a sheath of Parylene-C, were investigated. The sheathed probes successfully flowed in tofu (n = 5) and ex vivo brain tissue (n = 5) for at least 3 h at 100 nL/min. In contrast, only 60% (n = 5) of the end-on probes flowed successfully in tofu. In vitro recoveries for the sheathed and design were 66 ± 2 and 91 ± 7%, respectively, in stirred solution. The sheathed probes were tested by sampling from the cortex of anesthetized rats. In 13 of 19 experiments, push–pull fractions were successfully collected. Of the six failures, all had a sheath shallower than the 30 μm design depth, suggesting that this depth is necessary for reliable sampling flow. Basal concentrations of 20 neurotransmitters and metabolites determined by liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) were comparable with concentrations recorded using microdialysis sampling in prior work. Infusion of 2.0 mM nipecotic acid through the probe resulted in a significant increase in GABA with little effect on other compounds. Untargeted metabolomics analysis by LC-MS/MS identified 141 metabolites within the push–pull perfusates. The results demonstrate the feasibility of using Parylene-C push–pull probes with a sheathed tip design for multiplexed and temporally resolved monitoring of brain chemistry in vivo.

Introduction

The brain relies on chemical signaling to coordinate vital physiological processes, cognition, and behavior. To decipher these signals, in vivo chemical measurements with intact connections to various brain regions are critical. Various measurement techniques have been developed to monitor brain chemistry in vivo. − Positron emission tomography (PET) is highly effective; , however, it requires immobilized subjects, is expensive, can be difficult to interpret, and is not well-suited for small rodents. Implantable electrochemical or optical sensors can provide excellent temporal and spatial resolution; − however, it is difficult to develop sensors with sufficient sensitivity and selectivity for use in vivo. Genetically encoded sensors provide similar benefits as implantable sensors, but with added capability of imaging. − Limits of all these methods include: (1) multiplexing is limited to no more than a few compounds, , (2) they require known targets, and (3) quantification, especially of basal concentrations, is rarely achieved.

A complementary method for studying in vivo neurochemistry is sampling, such as microdialysis. , With sampling methods, collected fractions can be analyzed by highly selective and sensitive techniques that can provide multiplexed measurements. As a result, microdialysis sampling provides the capability to quantify basal concentrations, measure many neurochemicals at once, , identify new compounds in brain extracellular space, and be used for compounds, which are beyond the reach of sensors. Indeed, microdialysis has been used for many important discoveries in neuroscience, − is routinely used in drug development, , and is used clinically, e.g., to evaluate epilepsy, glioma, and traumatic brain injury. − Limitations of microdialysis are relatively poor spatial and temporal resolution, although temporal resolution of a few seconds is possible − ,− ,− and miniaturized probes are feasible.

Alternatives to microdialysis for in vivo sampling that can provide better spatial resolution include low-flow push–pull perfusion, − electroosmotic sampling, , and direct sampling. , Of these methods, low-flow push–pull perfusion has been the most explored. In this approach, samples are pulled into a capillary channel, and fluid is replaced with flow from a push capillary. Low-flow push–pull probes for brain were originally made from capillary assemblies. , Using capillaries with an outer diameter (OD) of 90 μm and an inner diameter (ID) in the 10s of μm range enables a large improvement in spatial resolution over microdialysis, which samples from 1 to 4 mm long by 250 μm diameter membrane probes. Although the spatial resolution of sampling is high because sampling was just from the tip, the assembled capillary probes still had overall dimensions similar to microdialysis probes. Since then, several microfabricated push–pull and related sampling devices for use in the brain have been reported. ,− Microfabrication enables further miniaturization with cross sections down to 30 × 70 μm. When combined with methods for collecting and analyzing nanoliter fractions, such as droplet microfluidics, such probes enable both high temporal and spatial resolution. −

Most microfabricated sampling probes were made from Si. Although Si is a versatile substrate for microfabrication, the high stiffness of Si (190 GPa) has been suggested to reduce biocompatibility with brain tissue and limit operation time. − Work with electrophysiological neural probes has expanded to softer polymeric materials such as SU-8, polyimide, PDMS, and Parylene-C. − These materials have Young’s modulus from 200 MPa to 10 GPa, which more closely approaches the stiffness of brain tissue. Probes constructed from such materials have been shown to reduce immune responses and facilitate longer-term electrophysiological recording. − A few reports have demonstrated sampling probes fabricated from polymers including reports of SU-8 and polyimide. , While promising, SU-8 tends to absorb moisture over time and can swell, potentially delaminating from other materials, which may reduce potential for chronic measurements.

Besides the material, tip design is an important feature of the sampling probes. Microfabrication allows flexibility in design, and several configurations have been reported. ,− , Probe tips are generally pointed with the sampling inlets residing on the side, which corresponds to the top of Si wafers. One design used anodic bonding of Si and fused silica wafers to form a series of 3 μm wide sampling inlet holes (1200 μm2 total sampling area size) along the shank wall approximately 70 μm back from the pointed tip. Our initial probes also had pores on the side of probe. , In continued work with these probes, we have found variability in the in vivo success rate with reduced flow, attributed to clogging, occurring in some cases.

To expand options for in vivo sampling, we explore Parylene-C sampling probes with different inlet designs to create more robust and reliable sampling with the potential for longer-term sampling. Previously, electrophysiological probes with microfluidic channels for microinjection have been produced from Parylene-C , using photoresists as molds. Parylene-C is chemically inert, flexible, and biocompatible, which could be of benefit to chronic sampling experiments. Inlet designs that alter the interaction with tissue were tested to determine whether they allow more reliable in vivo sampling.

Experimental Section

Chemicals and Materials

LC-MS-grade solvents such as acetonitrile, water, and methanol were acquired from Fisher Scientific. Other chemicals and reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Fused silica capillaries were purchased from Molex, LLC. Si wafers and AZ 12 XT photoresist were purchased from the Lurie Nanofabrication Facility. Gastight syringes were from the Hamilton Company. Artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) consisted of 145 mM sodium chloride, 2.68 mM potassium chloride, 1.40 mM calcium chloride-dihydrate, 1.01 mM magnesium sulfate,1.55 mM sodium phosphate- dibasic-anhydrous, 0.45 mM sodium phosphate-monobasic-monohydrate, and 250 μM ascorbic acid.

Probe Design and Fabrication

Probe design was carried out using COMSOL Multiphysics, MATLAB, and L- Edit software. Figure illustrates the overall probe layout and two tip designs that were fabricated. The end-on design has push–pull orifices flush with the bottom of the probe tip. The sheathed design has the push–pull orifices recessed by 40 μm within a Parylene-C shell. The probe shank was 3 mm long, and the total probe was 6 mm long. The probe was designed to be connected with capillaries to syringe pumps and vacuum sources for fluid control as reported previously. ,

Illustration of the probe design with the inset showing two sampling tip geometries: the end-on design and the sheathed. Arrows show the direction of flow. Bottom right is a cross-section view of the shank with the dimensions of the microfluidic channels.

Figure summarizes the fabrication process. Fabrication was guided by a previous report of a microfluidic injection channel within an electrophysiological Parylene-C probe. Important changes include: (a) abandoning metal deposition between Parylene-C layers, (b) use of thicker Parylene-C layers, (c) use of an SPR 955 photoresist as a sacrificial layer for releasing probes at the end of fabrication, and (d) use of our own method for wet chemical etching of the aluminum (Al) hard mask layer.

Fabrication process employed to produce neural sampling probes produced from Parylene-C.

All fabrication was conducted at the University of Michigan Lurie Nanofabrication Facility. Photolithographic masks were created using L-Edit. Resulting GDSII files enabled laser writing of masks with a Heidelberg uPG 501 mask making tool. They were finished with wet chemical etching to strip the chrome, which left the desired patterns.

Referring to Figure , Si wafers were coated with a 900 nm film of the SPR 955 photoresist, which served as a sacrificial layer for releasing probes. Two layers of Parylene-C were molded around a 10 μm thick layer of a patterned AZ 12XT photoresist, which shaped the microfluidic channels. Top (27 μm) and bottom (18 μm) layers of Parylene-C were deposited by using a PDS 2035 deposition tool. Parylene-C deposition was characterized by using a Dektak XT profilometer. AZ 12XT was spun via a CEE 200X spin coater (thick resist recipe, 2000 rpm) and exposed in an MABA-6 mask aligner for 17 s. Prior to the deposition of the Al hard mask, Kapton tape was placed over the alignment marks to shield them from Al. The Al hard mask was deposited onto the top Parylene-C layer by an Enerjet e-beam evaporator to a thickness of 200 nm. The Kapton tape was then removed, and probe shapes were photopatterned with AZ 12 XT onto the Al directly. The Al hard mask was shaped with a Transene A wet chemical etch. Oxygen plasma etching was carried out with an STS APS DGRIE plasma tool. The remaining Al was stripped with Transene A before the release step. The probes were released by immersing the wafers overnight in acetone to dissolve the SPR 955 that held them to the wafer surface.

Probe Assembly

Once fabricated, probes were mounted into a microfabricated Si holder that could facilitate handling and connection to external fluid control as described previously and shown in SI Figure 1. The Si holder was 10 mm long × 6 mm wide × 525 μm thick, with trenches etched to accommodate the upper (broad) region of the sampling probe and the push–pull ports. Probe holders were epoxied to 2.5 cm × 0.5 cm × 1 mm glass slide sections to facilitate handling. Once mounted in the holder, a fused silica sleeve (360 μm OD × 180 μm ID × 2.5 in.) was slid over the ports. Fused silica capillaries (150 μm OD × 40 μm ID × 19 cm long) were inserted into the opposite side of the sleeve, butted to the ports, and sealed into place with epoxy. Devcon 5 min epoxy gel was used for sealing the probes and fixing the probe holders to glass slide sections. Devcon 2-Ton epoxy sealed the capillary connections and covered the final assembly to further secure the probe and connections.

In Vitro Flow Screening

For in vitro flow tests, probes were plumbed, as shown in Figure . Push flow was driven at 100 nL/min by a Chemyx syringe pump (Stafford, TX) and pull flow driven by a GAST vacuum pump (Benton Harbor, MI). Fractions were collected in 1.5 μL volume sections of fused silica capillary tubing (8.5 cm long × 360 μm OD × 180 μm ID). Each fused silica cartridge was marked at 0.5 cm intervals (Figure ). For all experiments, the meniscus of sampled fluid was observed under a magnifying lens and progress timed to measure the pull flow rate. The applied vacuum was adjusted to achieve a pull flow rate of 100 nL/min, i.e., the time for the meniscus to travel 0.5 cm was 1.27 min. For in vitro experiments, pressures of 2.71–12.7 kPa were required and depended on factors such as the sampling media and possible effects of epoxies slightly narrowing the channel/capillary opening. Probes were mounted on the arm of a Kopf stereotaxic platform and lowered into the sample to a depth of 1.9 mm over 2.5 min. During insertion, both channels of the probes were flushed at 200 nL/min. Once the final position was reached, the perfusion rate was reduced to 100 nL/min for an additional 2 min before the sampling flow.

Illustration of fluid control for push–pull sampling. A syringe pump pushed aCSF through a T-junction (made from PEEK) to a fused silica capillary connected to the probe push inlet. For in vivo sampling, a second pump was used to deliver drug through the T-junction and into the push arm of the probe. The probe is mounted on a holder (not shown). Fluid is collected from the “pull” arm via a vacuum pump connected to the probe pull outlet. A fused silica capillary “cartridge” collects 1.5 μL of sample. A photograph of the cartridge is shown. Marks are used to estimate the flow rate by observing the fluid meniscus passing the marks over time. Once the cartridge is full, it is disconnected and replaced with another for collecting multiple fractions. The union is a piece of Teflon tubing the tightly fits over the OD of the fused silica.

For flow tests, probes were inserted into 0.6% agar gel, tofu (firm grade, Simple Truth Organic), and an ex vivo brain. Agar is a commonly used brain phantom. Tofu has also been used as a brain phantom, and in preliminary work, we found it mimicked in vivo flow performance better than agar. It also has a Young’s modulus of 1–7 kPa, which is comparable to brain tissue and therefore provides a good test of penetration strength. , Follow up flow screening tests were conducted with an ex vivo brain to verify both implantation strength and flow in an environment that more closely approaches living brain.

In Vitro Sampling

To evaluate recovery in vitro, flow was generated as described above with the probe dipped into 5 mL of a solution containing a selection of neurochemicals in aCSF. Fractions (1.5 μL) were collected for analysis by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). In other experiments, probes were used to sample a 150 μM fluorescein solution. Fluorescence was measured within the capillary using a microscope equipped with a laser-induced fluorescence detector.

In Vivo Sampling

In vivo sampling was performed from isoflurane-anesthetized Sprague–Dawley rats (300–650 g) mounted on a stereotaxic apparatus. A 5 mm window was drilled into the skull to facilitate probe insertion. The probe was inserted into the premotor cortex (PM1) or medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC). Coordinates for PM1 were as follows: AP: +3.00 mm; ML: ±2.3 mm; DV: −1.2 mm. Coordinates for mPFC were AP: +3.00 mm; ML: ±0.6–1.1 mm; DV: −1.8 mm. Implantation into the brain and sampling, including flow rate measurement, followed procedures for flow screening. Probes were operated at 100 nL/min for 60 min before fraction collection began to allow neurochemical levels to stabilize. For some experiments, push flow was switched from normal aCSF to aCSF with nipecotic acid via the PEEK tee (Figure ). Unless stated otherwise, 15 min fractions, corresponding to 1.5 μL each, were collected and stored at −80 C for later analysis. At the end of the experiment, rats were euthanized. All animal procedures followed approved University of Michigan IACUC protocols.

LC-MS/MS Analysis

Unless stated otherwise, samples were analyzed using LC-MS/MS after benzoyl chloride (BzCl) derivatization as described before. , Briefly, a 1.2 μL aliquot from 1.5 μL samples was removed for analysis, and 1.2 μL of HPLC-grade filtered water was added. Each (1.2 μL) of 100 mM sodium carbonate monohydrate, 2% BzCl solution in acetonitrile, and a stable-isotope labeled internal standard mix (13C-BzCl labeled standards plus d4-Ach) was added. Samples were analyzed either using an Agilent 1290 Infinity II interfaced to an Agilent 6410 (Santa Clara, California) or a Thermo Vanquish Flex interfaced to a Thermo Quantum (Waltham, Massachusetts) triple quadrupole mass spectrometer in positive ion mode. LC analysis was performed using a Phenomenex Kinetex C18 chromatography column (150 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm, 100 Å). Mobile phase A and B consisted of water containing 10 mM ammonium formate/0.15% formic acid (v/v) and acetonitrile, respectively. The gradient used was: initial, 5% B; 0.01 min, 19% B; 0.68 min, 26% B, 1.05 min, 75% B; 1.8 min, 100% B; 2.18 min, 100% B; 2.28 min, 5% B; and 3.00 min, 5% B at 600 μL/min. The autosampler was kept at 10 C, and the column was held at 55 C with 3 μL injection volumes. The mass spectrometer was used in single reaction monitoring mode for 3-methoxytyramine (3MT), serotonin (5HT), acetylcholine (Ach), adenosine (Ado), aspartate (Asp), choline (Ch), dopamine (DA), g-amino butyric acid (GABA), glucose (Glc), glutamate (Glu), glutamine (Gln), glycine (Gly), histamine (Hist), homovanillic acid (HVA), norepinephrine (NE), normetanephrine (NM), phenylalanine (Phe), serine (Ser), taurine (Tau), and tyrosine (Tyr). For in vitro samples, a subset of these compounds were targeted. For quantification, calibration standards were prepared in aCSF and derivatized with BzCL as above. A 7-point calibration curve was run daily, prior to sample analysis. Data analysis was performed using Skyline (MacCoss Lab, University of Washington).

Metabolomics analysis was conducted using a Thermo-Fisher Vanquish Horizon LC interfaced to an ID-X Orbitrap mass spectrometer. For RPLC separations, a 20 min method was utilized. Mobile phase A consisted of water with 0.1% v/v acid, while mobile phase B consisted of methanol with 0.025% v/v formic acid. The following RPLC gradient was utilized: 0 min, 0% B; 0–10 min, 99% B; 10–17 min, 99% B; 17–17.1 min, 0% B; and 17.1–20 min, 0% B. Separations were performed on a Waters HSST3 C18 column (1.8 μm particles, 2.1 mm × 100 mm). The sample injection volumes were 3 μL for full scan (MS1) collection and 3 μL for MS/MS collection. Mass spectrometer settings were: sheath gas, 40; aux gas, 10; sweep gas, 1; ion transfer tube temp, 325 °C; vaporizer temp, 300 °C; orbitrap resolution,120,000; scan range, 70–1100 m/z; RF lens, 45%; normalized AGC target, 25%; maximum injection time, auto; microscans, 1; data type, profile; and internal mass calibration, EASY-IC. Spray voltages were set to 3200 V for positive ionization. For MS/MS methods, these settings were maintained except for full scan orbitrap resolution, which was lowered to 60,000 to maximize MS/MS spectra collection. DDA used the following settings: intensity threshold, 1.0 × 104; dynamic exclusion properties; exclusion duration, 3 s (exclude after one time with ±5 ppm); isolation mode, quadrupole; isolation window, 1.2 m/z; activation type, HCD; collision energy mode, assisted; collision energies, 20, 40, and 80%; detector type, orbitrap; orbitrap resolution, 30,000; normalized AGC target, 20%; maximum injection time, 54 ms; microscans, 1; data type, centroid; and cycle time, 1.2 s.

Metabolomics Data Analysis

MSConvertGUI was used to convert the chromatograms to.mzXML files, which were imported into Matlab 2024a (Mathworks, Inc., Natick, MA) for analysis. MS/MS spectra were matched using the NIST20 (www.nist.gov), MS-DIAL, and Massbank of North America (MONA, mona.fiehnlab.ucdavis.edu/) libraries. To filter noise, low-abundance ions at <10% base peak intensity were removed from the query MS/MS spectra. The query spectrum was then searched against the three imported libraries to find suitable candidates using a precursor ion threshold of 5 ppm. Both a dot product and spectral entropy algorithm were used to calculate the similarity between the query and library MS/MS spectra. Features were tentatively identified if both algorithms returned the same compound name, with a dot product ≥700 and entropy score ≥0.65. ClassyFire ontology was included for these identifications if an International Chemical Identifier Key (InChIKey) was available.

Results and Discussion

Probe Fabrication

Images of a completed probe, a probe with connections, and tips for both the end-on and sheathed probes are shown in Figure (see also SI Figure 1B). As shown in the images, microfabrication resulted in well-defined probes with high fidelity to the mask that are free of roughness and unwanted features. The O2-based plasma etch created an undercut of roughly 7 μm per side, resulting in probe widths of 166 μm rather than the designed 180 μm. This could be compensated for by increasing the probe outline dimensions in the mask if desired.

Variability in the depth of the sheath was observed, as shown in Figure . Some probes possessed inlets less than 20 μm deep (Figure , right), while others were closer to the designed 40 μm depth (Figure , left). This variability could stem from misalignment caused by the thickness of the top layer of Parylene-C creating more distance between the mask and the alignment marks on the bottom Parylene-C layer. Another contributing factor could be a mixture of undercut and some variability with the plasma etch rate across the surface of the wafer. An alignment issue due to thickness could be potentially remedied by splitting up the Parylene-C deposition for the top layers to include intermediate alignment marks, which would be closer to the mask and should improve the alignment. The sampling areas could also be redesigned with slightly deeper recess to accommodate the alignment issue as another possible remedy.

In Vitro Flow Testing

Si-based push–pull probes reported previously , had push–pull orifices on the side of the probe but were found to have inconsistent flow with more in vivo use. Inlets on the side of the probe would necessarily be placed directly against tissue that has been transversed by the probe tip (see SI Figure 2). We hypothesized that the proximity and potential cell debris could lead to greater chance of occlusion and therefore designed probes that had different tip geometries. The sheath and end-on designs were hypothesized to be less susceptible to flow stoppage by creating separation between the surrounding medium (tissue) and the push–pull orifices (SI Figure 2). The inlet of the sheath probe has a built-in gap from the tissue, and the end-on probes could be pulled up from the deepest penetration to create space between the probe tip and tissue.

Flow stabilities in end-on and sheathed designs were first evaluated with probes inserted into agar and tofu to gauge suitability for potential in vivo trials. All probes flowed well when inserted in the agar. In contrast, it was found that 60% of the end-on probes (3 of 5) whereas 100% of the sheathed probes (5 of 5) flowed for at least 3 h (length of test) at 100 nL/min in tofu. One probe did not flow initially, but a flush and reimplantation established flow. For the sheath design, the average flow rate for all trials was 99 ± 6 nL/min with mean pressures of 4.2 ± 2.5 kPa. The end-on design probes flowed at 101 ± 5 nL/min at an average pressure of 8.6 ± 4.5 kPa. The higher pressure requirement of the end-on probes suggests a flow restriction. The success rate of the sheathed design is evidence of the benefits of a recessed probe sampling area. All probes penetrated tofu without bending or buckling. The sheathed probes were tested in ex vivo brain tissue with a similar 100% success rate in flow (5 of 5 probes).

In Vitro Recovery

An important performance parameter of a sampling probe is relative recovery, i.e., concentration recovered over the sample concentration. We sampled a selection of neurotransmitters and metabolites at 100 nL/min from a stirred solution by using end-on design probes. Recovery was between 80 and 100% (Figure SI 3) for the different analytes. Recovery with the push arm off was comparable (SI Figure 3). These results show that recovery is high by the end-on push–pull probe and use of the push arm has relatively little effect on recovery in this configuration. The results also show that Parylene-C is suitable as a probe material, e.g., adsorption or retention of the selected analytes does not prevent high recovery; however, for some compounds, there may be some loss of analyte. With the push arm off, we expect 100% recovery but found some were as low as 80% indicative of some analyte loss in the system.

We next compared the recovery of end-on and sheathed designs for fluorescein with sampling at 100 nL/min in both a stirred and quiescent solution, as summarized in Table . The end-on probe had similar recovery for fluorescein as for the previously tested analytes in stirred solution (91%). Relative recovery was highest from the stirred solution for both probe designs due to the constant supply of analyte to the surface. The end-on design yielded substantially higher recoveries than the sheath probes in both quiescent and stirred solutions. This can be rationalized as greater dilution of analyte as it diffuses through the recessed area of a sheathed probe compared to the fully exposed pull inlet in the end-on probe. Compounding this factor is that, in a sheath probe, the push–pull flow is confined in the sheath more than in the end-on probe, so a greater fraction of the pull stream is directly from the push arm, resulting in more dilution of analyte. These rationalizations were supported by modeling, as shown in SI Figure 4.

In Vivo Experiments

Given the better flow performance of sheathed probes, they were used for in vivo tests. For a total of 19 probes tested in vivo, 13 flowed for 240–300 min (time of experiment) allowing collection of analyzable fractions. For 10 of these 13 experiments, the probes flowed continuously. For the remaining three experiments, sampling flow did not start after insertion and outward flow was reapplied for short periods until flow could be commenced. The failure rate (6 of 19 probes did not flow) was higher than that found for in vitro flow screening in tofu and ex vivo brain. An examination of probes from both failed and successful trials revealed that the probes that flowed had a sheath depth of >30 μm whereas failed probes had <20 μm sheath depth. This result suggests that a sufficiently deep sheath is important for reliable flow. The flow rate for 13 successful experiments was 100 ± 5 nL/min requiring an average pressure of 10.3 ± 9.2 kPa. Probes that flowed immediately after insertion required pressures of 1.7–5.1 kPa, which was similar to the tofu screening trials. Higher pressures were required for probes that did not flow right away, suggesting an occlusion created flow resistance in the sampling path.

Figure illustrates an extracted ion chromatogram for detected compounds from a sample collected from the mPFC. Table gives the average concentrations of 20 compounds measured during the in vivo experiments. Not all compounds were detected in all subjects, as indicated in the table. Missing values are observed mostly for low-concentration analytes, suggesting that, in some cases, recovered concentrations were below the detection limits for the limited (1.5 μL) samples collected here. For mPFC, concentrations were comparable to basal concentrations that we previously found in microdialysis samples from awake animals using the same LC-MS/MS method, although Gly, Ser, and Tau were notably lower in the current experiment. The concentrations measured in PM1 were also similar to those previously measured in that brain region. In the current study, the animals were anesthetized, which may affect the concentrations. Differences in recovery may also play a role in the observed discrepancy in the concentration. While in vitro recoveries of the sheath push–pull and microdialysis are comparable, we do not yet have a method to determine the in vivo recovery of the push–pull probes. Finally, the smaller probes may be less affected by tissue damage than the larger microdialysis probes. Given the differences in conditions (anesthesia, smaller probes), the general agreement in basal concentrations suggests that measurements made with the Parylene-C probes are reflective of the brain concentrations, like those made using more established microdialysis probes.

With some probes, we tested the ability to monitor a pharmacologically induced change in mPFC (Figure ). For these experiments, we infused 2.0 mM nipecotic acid, a GABA uptake inhibitor, through the inlet side of the probe (Figure ) for 30 min. As shown in Figure , drug delivery resulted in the expected increase in GABA with the arrival of nipecotic acid (also measured in the fractions), with little change in other compounds measured. These data highlight the potential to use the probes to monitor concentration dynamics in small regions of the living brain. Temporal resolution was limited by the need to collect 1.5 μL of sample while flowing at just 100 nL/min as well as the nonautomated fraction collection method. It may be possible to couple this probe with droplet generation, which provides a convenient way to collect fractions on the second time scale for analysis by direct MS as shown previously.

Untargeted Analysis

Recently, interest has grown in using untargeted metabolomic techniques to uncover new aspects of brain chemistry. ,− These probes provide a unique opportunity to develop a comprehensive profile of the brain extracellular space with high spatial resolution. To demonstrate the compatibility of these probes with untargeted metabolomics, an additional 6 μL of dialysate was collected from three animals. Figure A illustrates the resulting total ion chromatograms from these samples, highlighting that many compounds are recovered and potentially monitored using these probes. A total of 141 unique metabolites were tentatively identified based on matching MS/MS spectra to spectral databases (see the Supporting Information, File Parylene_Dialysate_IDs), with individual animals exhibiting an average 135 ± 9 compounds. (More detailed studies such as matching retention time with authentic standards would be required to confirm these identifications.) Among the 141 total compounds identified, the most prominent were lipids (21%), benzenoids (18%), organoheterocyclic compounds (18%), and organic acids (18%) using ClassyFire categories (Figure B). The number of metabolites is within the range of published literature results of tens to hundreds of identified compounds from microdialysis and solid-phase microextraction in vivo samples. ,− These results highlight the potential to monitor many compounds and study how the metabolome changes in vivo within small brain regions.

Comparison to Other Sampling Methods

Although we pursued push–pull probes in this work, other methods may also be explored for enhanced spatial resolution. Methods based on direct sampling of extracellular fluid using pulled capillary tips have been described but as yet are less amenable to time-resolved measurements. , Push–pull probes are also advantageous since the fluid removed is directly replaced; however, with direct sampling, care must be taken to avoid sampling faster than fluid can flow to the probe or be replaced. Evidence for an endogenous interstitial flow of 100–300 nL/g of tissue suggests that direct sampling flow must be quite low to match such levels. Electro-osmotic sampling also offers potential for high spatial resolution and continuous sampling with enhanced collection due to collecting samples by flow and diffusion; , however, the use of electric fields can complicate the experiment. It would be interesting to determine if Parylene-C could be used for making these other probe types.

Conclusions

Compared to microdialysis, microfabricated push–pull probes have much higher spatial resolution, enabling probing of brain regions not accessible by the larger probes; however, more reliable operation and identification of suitable materials and fabrication methods are desired. The results show the feasibility of fabricating a Parylene-C push–pull probe and sampling in vivo.

The probe material caused little loss of common neurochemicals, as shown by high in vitro recovery and detection of a wide range of chemicals in perfusate collected in vivo. The sheath design improves the reliability of in vivo flow performance over designs with the push–pull ports directly exposed to tissue, although the sheath did reduce recovery. Ideally, the fabrication procedure is improved to allow more consistent sheath depth since that parameter appeared to be important in achieving successful flow. The Parylene-C probe has advantages over Si-based probes, including simpler fabrication and optically clear material allowing observation of flow within the probe for testing and diagnostics. In principle, the more flexible Parylene-C probe will also be more suited for chronic operation, a feature that can be tested now that fabrication and in vivo performance have been demonstrated. Other future directions include making longer probes for deeper brain regions, incorporating droplet fraction collection for higher temporal resolution monitoring and automating the flow measurement with feedback to control pull flows. Ultimately, it may be possible to use 3D printing to fabricate flexible probes as well. , The utility of the fabrication method extends beyond just neural probes and could also be applicable to other microfluidic devices.
