Combining radiotherapy with CAR‐T cell therapy for solid tumors: Challenges, mechanisms, and future directions
Authors: Leran Qiu, Xingyu Zhou, Jiao Xue, Xiaokang Zhang, Songbing Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChimeric antigen receptor (CAR)‐T cell therapy has achieved significant success in treating hematological malignancies. However, compared to its efficacy against hematologic cancers, the application of CAR‐T cell therapy for solid tumors still faces several challenges. Radiotherapy, an important component in the treatment of solid tumors, has recently attracted widespread attention because of its immunomodulatory effects. Combined radiotherapy may overcome the limitations of CAR‐T cell therapy for solid tumors. This review discusses the theoretical foundations of combined radiation therapy and CAR‐T cell therapy, along with preclinical and clinical research progress. Additionally, this study provides an innovative exploration of key factors to be considered when combining these two therapies. These factors include radiation dose, mode, radiation type, and timing and scheduling of the combined treatment. This review offers new perspectives on future research directions and highlights critical issues that remain to be addressed.

INTRODUCTION

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)‐T cell therapy involves genetic engineering of a patient's own T cells to express synthetic chimeric antigen receptors (CARs). 1 These receptors enable T cells to specifically target tumor cells. A typical CAR consists of an extracellular antigen recognition domain, transmembrane domain, and intracellular signaling domain. Upon antigen binding, intracellular signaling domains initiate a robust antitumor immune response.

CAR T‐cell therapy has been highly successful in treating hematological malignancies. However, there are numerous challenges in the treatment of solid tumors, including the insufficient infiltration and homing ability of CAR‐T cells in tumor tissues, poor functionality and persistence, an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, tumor antigens, escape mechanisms, and heterogeneity. To address these issues, substantial innovative research has been conducted on the design of CAR‐T cells in recent years. For example, CAR‐T cells can be engineered to express chemokine receptors or matrix‐degrading enzymes to increase their homing and infiltration into tumors; inhibitory receptors can be knocked out, or “armored” cytokines can be introduced to improve their functionality and alleviate the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment; multitarget strategies can be designed to address antigen heterogeneity; CAR structures can be optimized by selecting appropriate costimulatory domains or introducing pro‐survival genes; and novel delivery strategies, such as intratumoral or regional administration, are being explored. Furthermore, the efficacy of CAR‐T cell immunotherapy can be further improved by combining it with traditional treatments, such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy or immune checkpoint inhibitors.

Radiotherapy is widely used to treat solid tumors, and has become a crucial component of comprehensive treatment strategies for these malignancies. Traditionally, radiotherapy has been regarded as a local treatment modality in which ionizing radiation induces double‐strand breaks in the DNA of tumor cells, either directly or indirectly, to eliminate cancer cells, aiming for local tumor control or elimination of distant metastases. In recent years, rapid advancements in radiobiology and tumor immunology have revolutionized the combination of radiotherapy with immunotherapy. 2 Recent research has clarified the molecular mechanisms behind radiotherapy‐induced immune responses, including: (1) induction of immunogenic cell death (ICD), which promotes the release of damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs); 3 , 4 (2) upregulation of tumor cell surface molecules that enhance antigen presentation and immune recognition; and (3) activation of the cGAS/STING pathway, which recruits immune cells and transforms the tumor microenvironment from “cold” to “hot”. 5 The emphasis in radiotherapy research has shifted from cytotoxicity to immune modulation. 6 Radiotherapy can overcome the immunosuppressive barriers of the tumor microenvironment (TME), increase immune cell infiltration, and increase antigen exposure and presentation, thereby inhibiting immune evasion. This offers promising possibilities for combining radiotherapy with immunotherapy. Therefore, radiotherapy is a promising strategy for enhancing the efficacy of CAR‐T cell immunotherapy.

Despite these advances, emerging evidence suggests that the efficacy of combining radiotherapy with CAR‐T cell therapy is highly context‐dependent. Variations in radiation dose, delivery mode, radiation type, and treatment sequencing can differentially shape the tumor immune microenvironment, resulting in divergent effects on CAR‐T cell trafficking, activation, and persistence. A systematic understanding of how these radiotherapy‐specific parameters influence the integration of radiotherapy and CAR‐T cell therapy remains limited. Accordingly, this review examines the combination of radiotherapy and CAR‐T cell therapy through radiotherapy parameter optimization with the aim of providing a design‐oriented framework relevant to radiation oncology research and clinical practice. Clinical research

Despite encouraging findings from preclinical models, the clinical translation of radiotherapy combined with CAR‐T cell therapy in solid tumors remains limited and heterogeneous. Several clinical trials have explored the use of radiotherapy combined with CAR‐T cell therapy for the treatment of solid tumors. In metastatic castration‐resistant prostate cancer, a phase Ib trial (NCT05805371) has assessed the efficacy and safety of CAR‐T cells targeting prostate stem cell antigens with radiotherapy, which is expected to be completed by 2027. Another phase I trial (NCT02664363) at Duke University explored EGFRvIII‐targeted CAR‐T cells for newly diagnosed stage IV glioblastomas. Although the trial was terminated, the data remain valuable.

Currently, clinical research on the combination of radiotherapy and CAR‐T cell therapy for solid tumors has entered the experimental phase in only a few cases. However, emerging clinical observations suggest that radiotherapy may modulate the tumor microenvironment in ways that enhance adoptive immune therapies. For example, a case reported by Patel et al. 19 demonstrated that low‐dose radiotherapy (5.6 Gy/4 in sessions) combined with adoptive T‐cell therapy (ACT) in patients with advanced melanoma resulted in long‐term stability of pulmonary nodules and an 82% reduction in liver metastases, with minimal toxicity.

A case from the MD Anderson Cancer Center (NCT03132922) 20 further supported this synergistic effect. A 71‐year‐old patient with metastatic rectal mucosal melanoma who was previously unresponsive to chemotherapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors received low‐dose radiotherapy combined with MAGE‐A4‐specific T‐cell therapy. The patient achieved partial remission, with a significantly longer remission in the irradiated liver metastasis (22.7 weeks) than in non‐irradiated areas (4 weeks). This case highlights the potential of combining radiotherapy with CAR‐T cell therapy to overcome treatment resistance by precisely modulating the tumor microenvironment.

However, compared with preclinical studies, clinical research has shown some discrepancies in effectiveness. The discrepancy between promising preclinical efficacy and limited clinical success may stem from several factors. First, the immunosuppressive complexity of the TME remains a significant barrier to CAR‐T cell persistence and function in solid tumors. Moreover, the heterogeneity of treatment protocols (e.g. radiation dose, fractionation, and CAR‐T cell infusion timing) and tumor types may contribute to inconsistent outcomes in clinical trials. Additionally, early phase clinical trials often involve limited patient cohorts and regulatory complexities, which further hinder robust efficacy data. Collectively, these early clinical experiences suggest that, while radiotherapy may enhance adoptive cellular therapies in selected settings, the clinical benefits remain variable and context‐dependent, underscoring the need for further optimization of combination strategies.

UNRESOLVED CHALLENGES IN RT–CAR‐T COMBINATIONS: A DESIGN‐ORIENTED PERSPECTIVE

Spatial limitations

From a radiotherapy design perspective, spatial accessibility represents a key, but incompletely optimized, determinant of CAR‐T efficacy in solid tumors. Several preclinical studies have reported radiotherapy‐associated modulation of the tumor vasculature and endothelial adhesion molecules, which may facilitate the entry of immune cells into tumors. Radiation therapy, particularly at low to moderate doses (5–10 Gy), promotes angiogenesis and vascular normalization by releasing nitric oxide (NO) and improving tumor blood perfusion and oxygenation. 21 , 22 Notably, Taylor et al. 23 revealed that after radiation therapy, the hypoxic and non‐perfused volume fractions of tumors were significantly reduced using multimodal imaging techniques in patient‐derived xenograft (PDX) mice. Additionally, Murty et al. 8 used intravital imaging to show that, following whole‐body radiation, GD2‐CAR‐T cells rapidly extravasated from tumor blood vessels in a preclinical glioblastoma (GBM) model and expanded in the TME.

Furthermore, radiation increases the expression of adhesion molecules such as ICAM‐1 and VCAM‐1 on tumor endothelial cells, facilitating immune cell migration into the TME. 24 , 25 , 26 A study by Mondini et al. 27 reported that a single dose of 7.5 Gy of radiotherapy combined with an anti‐human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine significantly increased pericyte coverage in a mouse model of HPV16‐positive head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), improved vascular permeability, and induced vascular normalization. This treatment also upregulated ICAM‐1 expression in tumor endothelial cells. Low‐dose radiotherapy (2 Gy) exerts multiple regulatory effects by inducing the polarization of tumor‐associated macrophages towards the iNOS + M1 phenotype. 28 Nitric oxide (NO) produced by iNOS upregulates VCAM‐1 expression and promotes vascular normalization. This is reflected by a reduction in vessel density, a decrease in vessel diameter, and a more regular vessel morphology, while simultaneously reducing the secretion of immunosuppressive factors. This regulatory mechanism was validated in murine pancreatic cancer and human melanoma xenograft models.

In addition to its vascular effects, radiotherapy has been shown to influence the chemokine signaling pathways involved in T‐cell trafficking. Radiotherapy enhances CAR‐T cell homing to tumors by inducing the production of chemokines, particularly CXCL9 and CXCL10. 29 , 30 These chemokines bind to receptors, such as CXCR3, on CAR‐T cells, guiding their migration into the tumor. The underlying mechanism involves radiotherapy‐induced stress response in tumor cells, which activates IFN‐γ signaling, increasing chemokine secretion. 31 This creates a positive feedback loop, where radiotherapy not only increases chemokine secretion but also upregulates chemokine receptors on CAR‐T cells, further enhancing their infiltration ability. 16

Additionally, radiotherapy modulates the tumor microenvironment by influencing the extracellular matrix (ECM), collagen crosslinking, and matrix metalloproteinase (MMPs). Patel et al. 19 reported that low‐dose X‐ray therapy (2 Gy) significantly reduced the number of CAFs within the tumor stroma by > 50%, thereby reducing the physical barrier hindering CAR‐T cell migration into the core tumor region in a preclinical murine model. However, increased collagen cross‐linking may harden the ECM, potentially impeding CAR‐T cell infiltration and negatively affecting therapeutic efficacy. Radiotherapy also activates MMPs (such as MMP‐2 and MMP‐9), which degrade ECM components, potentially aiding CAR‐T cell infiltration and improving antitumor efficacy. 32 On the other hand, excessive MMP activation may also pose a risk for tumor metastasis.

However, most of the available evidence is derived from heterogeneous preclinical models, and the extent to which these spatial effects can be consistently achieved across different radiotherapy parameters and tumor contexts remains insufficiently characterized, particularly with respect to the dose, fractionation, and radiotherapy delivery strategies relevant to RT–CAR‐T combinations.

Temporal mismatches

Temporal coordination represents a critical yet incompletely addressed aspect of RT‐CAR‐T cell combination strategies. Studies investigating the optimal time interval between radiation therapy and CAR‐T cell infusion have highlighted critical time‐dependent factors. In BALB/c and C57BL/6 mouse models, early infusion of CAR‐T cells 4 h after irradiation resulted in stronger antitumor effects than delayed infusion 48 h after irradiation. This could be because early infusion more effectively utilizes the window of upregulated ICAM‐1 expression induced by radiation. 15 Zhang et al. 33 further clarified the molecular mechanisms underlying this phenomenon. In a prostate cancer model, a single 10 Gy dose dynamically regulated B7‐H3 antigen expression, peaking at 1–3 days post‐radiation and returning to baseline by days 5–7. These observations underscore the importance of temporal coordination; however, treatment schedules remain heterogeneous across studies.

In parallel, T‐cell expansion and persistence in solid tumors are temporally dynamic processes, with several studies reporting transient intratumoral expansion and limited long‐term persistence. In a phase I clinical trial for recurrent glioblastoma, CAR‐T cells exhibited transient expansion in the peripheral blood but showed limited in vivo persistence, with most cells becoming undetectable within approximately 30 days after infusion. 34

Taken together, these observations underscore the importance of temporal coordination between radiotherapy‐induced effects and CAR‐T cell kinetics. However, treatment schedules, including radiation dose, fractionation, and interval between irradiation and CAR‐T infusion, remain heterogeneous across studies.

Biological trade‐offs

Dual immunomodulatory effects of radiotherapy in combination therapy

The immunosuppressive microenvironment of solid tumors is a major barrier to CAR‐T cell efficacy, which is partly driven by immunosuppressive cell infiltrates and inhibitory cytokine networks. Radiotherapy has been reported to reshape this milieu, including induction of proinflammatory signals and, in some settings, a shift towards a more “hot” phenotype.

In murine melanoma models, low‐dose radiotherapy (LDRT) promotes the polarization of tumor‐associated macrophages (TAMs) from a tumor‐promoting M2 phenotype to an antitumor M1 phenotype. 28 , 35 This enhances CAR‐T cell infiltration and antitumor efficacy. Clinical studies have further confirmed that in patients with metastatic colorectal cancer, both LDRT and high‐dose hypofractionated radiotherapy (HFRT) can modulate the local immune microenvironment by increasing the M1/M2 macrophage ratio and promoting the infiltration of CD8+ T cells. 36

Radiotherapy also enhances CD4+ T cell activation and survival, as shown in murine models and early clinical studies, evidenced by upregulation of granzyme B, MIP‐1α, and CD137. 37 , 38 Furthermore, radiotherapy increases tumor cell sensitivity to CAR‐T cell‐mediated cytotoxicity by regulating apoptosis‐related proteins, downregulating antiapoptotic Bcl‐2 and upregulating proapoptotic Bax, 9 improving CAR‐T cell killing efficiency. Collectively, these findings highlight the capacity of radiotherapy to create a more permissive immune context for CAR‐T cell activity under certain conditions.

Importantly, radiotherapy can elicit both immunostimulatory and immunosuppressive effects within the tumor microenvironment, which may coexist. It promotes the recruitment of immunosuppressive cells such as Tregs and MDSCs. Studies have shown that high‐dose radiotherapy increases Treg infiltration in tumors and activates the CCR6‒CCL20 axis, facilitating Treg migration. 39 , 40 , 41 Additionally, radiotherapy increases MDSC levels in the spleen, lungs, and peripheral blood of cancer patients. 42 The magnitude and direction of these immune changes appear context‐dependent, varying with tumor type, radiation dose, and fractionation.

The efficacy of CAR‐T cell therapy in solid tumors is often limited by the upregulation of immune checkpoint molecules, such as PD‐L1, cytotoxic T‐lymphocyte‐associated protein‐4 (CTLA‐4), lymphocyte activation gene‐3 (LAG‐3), and T‐cell immunoreceptor (TIGIT), induced by high‐dose radiotherapy. 43 The excessive expression of these molecules not only suppresses the antitumor activity of endogenous T cells but also leads to functional exhaustion of adoptively transferred CAR‐T cells, facilitating tumor immune escape. Together, these observations suggest that immune checkpoint upregulation represents an additional layer of biological constraint that may need to be considered when integrating radiotherapy with CAR‐T cell therapy.

Functional implications of radiotherapy‐modulated antigen landscapes for CAR‐T cells

One of the major challenges in solid tumor therapy is tumor antigen heterogeneity, which refers to the uneven expression of target antigens across tumor cells. This heterogeneity hinders CAR‐T cell recognition and the elimination of antigen‐negative tumor cells, severely compromising therapeutic efficacy. Radiotherapy addresses this problem through multiple molecular mechanisms.

First, double‐stranded DNA breaks and tumor cell death induced by radiotherapy not only significantly increase the release and presentation of tumor‐associated antigens (TAAs) but also upregulate the expression of target antigens on the surface of cancer cells, 44 thus making previously insufficiently expressed tumor antigens recognizable to CAR‐T cells. In preclinical studies, radiotherapy has been shown to upregulate the expression of specific tumor‐associated antigens, including MUC1 in colorectal cancer, HER2 in breast cancer, GD2 in glioma models, and B7‐H3 across multiple solid tumor settings, thereby enhancing antigen availability for CAR‐T recognition. The released antigenic material triggers an “in situ vaccine” effect, enhancing immune responses to both the primary tumor and metastatic lesions. 45

Radiotherapy also induces ICD, releasing DAMPs that promote tumor neoantigen expression and cross‐presentation to immune cells. 46 , 47 , 48 This process activates endogenous T cells to recognize newly exposed antigen epitopes. More importantly, it effectively connects the innate and adaptive immune responses, thereby expanding the scope of CAR‐T cell recognition. In terms of antigen presentation, radiotherapy has a unique regulatory effect on antigen presentation. Studies have shown that radiation significantly upregulates the expression of MHC‐I molecules on the surface of tumor cells, thereby improving antigen processing and presentation efficiency. 49 From a clinical translation perspective, this dual regulatory mechanism, enhancing both antigen expression and presentation, not only strengthens CAR‐T cell recognition of existing targets but also broadens the immune recognition spectrum, providing more comprehensive antigen coverage for the clearance of solid tumors.

Several preclinical studies confirmed the reliability of this synergistic effect. In a murine triple‐negative breast cancer (4T1) model, fractionated radiotherapy (8 Gy×3) significantly increased the expression and exposure of tumor neoantigens by upregulating the expression of antigen processing‐related genes. 50 A case report showed that combining radiotherapy with neoantigen‐reactive tumor‐infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy resulted in significant tumor shrinkage and extended progression‐free survival in hepatocellular carcinoma patients. 51 This outcome may be attributed to the ability of RT to release tumor antigens, thereby enhancing the ability of TILs to recognize tumor neoantigens.

However, enhanced antigen availability may intersect with pathways that influence CAR‐T cell durability. Antigen shedding in the tumor microenvironment is a key mechanism that contributes to CAR‐T cell exhaustion. Soluble antigens released from tumor cells can bind to CARs on the surface of CAR‐T cells, leading to the continuous activation of T cell signaling pathways. This prolonged activation ultimately results in T cell exhaustion, weakening both the antitumor efficacy and long‐term persistence.

RADIOTHERAPY PARAMETER OPTIMIZATION I: RADIATION DOSE

The radiotherapy dose critically shapes the magnitude, duration, and spatial distribution of radiation‐induced immune effects. For RT‐CAR‐T cell combinations, dose‐dependent differences in vascular modulation, antigen release, immune activation, and immunosuppression may substantially influence CAR‐T cell trafficking and functional outcomes. Therefore, understanding how the radiation dose modulates these parameters is essential for the rational integration of radiotherapy with CAR‐T cell therapy.

LDRT, typically defined as a single dose (0.5–3 Gy and a total dose of 1–20 Gy), offers unique immunomodulatory benefits compared with conventional radiotherapy and HFRT. LDRT activates antitumor immune responses, particularly in “cold” tumors. 28 , 52 , 53 Additionally, unlike ablative radiotherapy, LDRT has broader clinical applicability and is not limited by lesion location or size 10 .

LDRT offers significant advantages for reducing radiotherapy‐induced immunosuppression and enhancing immune activation. In the dual modulation of the tumor immune microenvironment by radiotherapy, immunosuppressive effects primarily arise from the radiation‐induced activation of TGF‐β. Notably, TGF‐β levels have been shown to correlate positively with radiation dose, 54 which explains why LDRT significantly reduces TGF‐β expression and the infiltration of immunosuppressive cells within the tumor microenvironment. 55 , 56

In a phase II trial, LDRT combined with PD‐L1/CTLA‐4 inhibitors showed greater immune modulation than high‐dose radiotherapy, increasing the M1/M2 macrophage ratio and CD8+ T cell infiltration. 36 Additionally, compared with high‐dose radiotherapy, LDRT may be more conducive to preserving immune effector cells within the tumor microenvironment. 57 , 58 Therefore, shorter treatment durations and lower doses can help preserve the population of immune cells that remain responsive to radiation in the tumor microenvironment. Furthermore, LDRT reduces radiotherapy‐related toxicities such as pneumonia and cardiac toxicity.

Notably, the dose–‒response relationship in radiotherapy varies significantly across different tumor types. Therefore, optimizing personalized combination treatment strategies for various tumor types is a key area of focus for future translational research.

RADIOTHERAPY PARAMETER OPTIMIZATION II: DELIVERY APPROACH AND RADIATION TYPE

Beyond the radiation dose, the delivery approach and radiation quality represent key design variables in RT–CAR‐T cell combinations. Differences in treatment techniques and radiation types can differentially influence tumor control and immune modulation. Accordingly, rational selection of radiotherapy delivery and radiation type is important for optimizing CAR‐T cell activity in solid tumors.

Delivery approach

Current research indicates that both localized precision radiotherapy and total body irradiation (TBI) can significantly increase the tumor response to CAR‐T cell therapy. 10 These two radiotherapy approaches modulates the effects of combination therapy through distinct immune regulatory mechanisms that deserve further exploration.

Stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT), a leading method in modern precision radiotherapy, delivers high doses in a few sessions (1–5) with high precision, achieving excellent local control while minimizing damage to the surrounding healthy tissue. Its biological advantage lies in the fact that, compared to conventional fractionated radiotherapy, SBRT is more effective in activating systemic antitumor immune responses. When combined with PD‐1/PD‐L1 inhibitors, SBRT can significantly enhance the abscopal effect. 59 In CAR‐T cell combination therapy, SBRT can rapidly reduce the tumor burden, potentially decreasing the required dose of CAR‐T cells and thereby lowering the risk of severe adverse events, such as CRS.

Conversely, TBI, traditionally used for immune ablation before stem cell transplantation, promotes the activation of CD4+ T cells and effector memory cells while reducing immunosuppressive cell infiltration. 60 As a “preconditioning” treatment for CAR‐T cell therapy, TBI not only exerts a lymphodepletion effect but also creates a more favorable tumor immune microenvironment.

It is important to highlight the potential risks associated with TBI. In a murine model of melanoma, Kim et al. 35 reported that 4 Gy TBI enhanced TCR‐T cell infiltration but reduced cDC1 levels and decreased T‐cell activation despite increased T‐cell numbers when combined with IL‐2. This highlights the impact of radiotherapy on both the quantity and functionality of T cells. Although CAR‐T cells do not depend on MHC antigen presentation, the cross‐presenting ability of DCs is crucial for maintaining the long‐term activity of CAR‐T cells. Thus, future research should focus on optimizing the synergy between the radiotherapy dose, fractionation scheme, and CAR‐T cell design (e.g., incorporating CD40L to activate DCs).

Beyond the general effects of radiotherapy on immune modulation, specific tumor sites offer unique challenges and opportunities. Radiotherapy offers a unique synergistic potential for central nervous system (CNS) tumors. Recent preclinical studies have demonstrated that radiotherapy can enhance the permeability of the blood‐brain barrier (BBB), creating a favorable window for the systemic delivery of CAR‐T cells. 10 , 61 These findings provide new strategic insights for applying CAR‐T cell therapy to treat brain tumors.

Radiation type

Heavy ion radiotherapy has developed rapidly in recent years as a precision radiotherapy technique, with carbon ion radiotherapy (CIRT) being the most commonly used. Compared with conventional photon radiotherapy, heavy ion beams have greater relative biological effectiveness (RBE) because of their high linear energy transfer (LET), which offers distinct therapeutic advantages, particularly against tumors with hypoxic and low‐proliferative microenvironments. Notably, compared to conventional photon radiotherapy, CIRT may improve clinical outcomes in response to radiation immunotherapy by enhancing antitumor immune responses. 62 , 63

With respect to immunogenic regulation, heavy‐ion radiotherapy induces double‐stranded DNA breaks with a higher frequency and complexity, making them more difficult to repair. 64 This unique damage pattern is more likely to trigger ICD and promote the release of DAMPs. 65 , 66 This mechanism not only enhances antitumor immunity, but also improves CAR‐T cell recognition of tumor cells.

As previously discussed, radiotherapy can upregulate the expression of ICAM‐1 and MHC‐I on tumor cell surfaces. However, studies in mice bearing NR‐S1 squamous cell carcinoma cells irradiated with carbon ions showed an increase in ICAM‐1 expression in a time‐dependent manner. 67 In an MC38 colorectal tumor model, carbon ion radiotherapy significantly outperformed photon radiotherapy in upregulating MHC I expression at the same dose. 68

In terms of regulating the tumor immune microenvironment, clinical studies have indicated that, compared with traditional photon radiotherapy, heavy ion radiotherapy not only activates antitumor immunity but also reduces the recruitment of Tregs and MDSCs 69 , 70 , 71 while lowering the expression of immunosuppressive factors such as IL‐10 and TGF‐β. 66 This characteristic minimizes the immunosuppressive side effects often seen with conventional radiotherapy, creating favorable conditions for CAR‐T cell efficacy.

From a physical standpoint, heavy ion beams exhibit a characteristic Bragg peak dose distribution, allowing for high‐dose focusing on the tumor target while minimizing damage to surrounding lymphoid tissues, bone marrow, and other immune‐related organs. 72 This precision in dose distribution not only improves treatment safety but also helps preserve the integrity of the systemic immune system.

As clinical applications of heavy ion radiotherapy continue to expand, its combined use with immune checkpoint inhibitors, Treg inhibitors, new antigen RNA‐LPX vaccines, and other immunotherapies has shown promising synergistic effects in preclinical studies. 73 Based on current evidence, heavy ion radiotherapy enhances the tumor immune microenvironment through multiple mechanisms and has the potential to become a key technology in overcoming the challenges of CAR‐T cell therapy for solid tumors, offering clinical benefits that may surpass those of traditional radiotherapy.

RADIOTHERAPY PARAMETER OPTIMIZATION III: SEQUENCING AND TIMING

In addition to spatial and biological considerations, temporal coordination represents a critical yet underexplored dimension of RT‐CAR‐T cell combinations. Emerging evidence suggests that the timing of radiotherapy relative to CAR‐T cell infusion can influence treatment efficacy by shaping the dynamic molecular and immunological landscapes within the tumor microenvironment. The infusion of B7‐H3 CAR‐T cells during the peak of radiation‐induced antigen expression resulted in optimal therapeutic effects, suggesting that radiation timing may provide a basis for selecting effective CAR‐T infusion windows.

Beyond treatment sequencing, defined by radiation‐induced molecular dynamics, broader temporal factors may also influence the immunological effects of radiotherapy. The circadian rhythm, an intrinsic clock that regulates multiple physiological processes, significantly influences the immunological effects of tumor radiotherapy. Retrospective studies have reported that radiotherapy administered within three hours before sunset improves disease control, whereas PD‐L1 inhibitor administration during this same window increases the risk. 64 These findings suggest that circadian factors may represent an additional temporal variable to consider when coordinating radiotherapy and immunotherapy.

Several mechanisms can explain this observation. Circadian rhythms can influence the radiosensitivity of tumor cells by regulating the cell cycle (e.g., G2/M phase) and activity of DNA repair enzymes. For instance, in nasopharyngeal carcinoma, the circadian clock gene BMAL1 suppresses cell proliferation and tumorigenicity while enhancing radiosensitivity. 74 Glioma cells are more likely to be arrested in the G2/M phase (the radiosensitive phase) during periods of high expression of the circadian clock gene Per2, typically from the afternoon to early evening; these core circadian clock genes regulate cyclins and DNA repair pathways, thereby affecting the efficiency of radiation‐induced damage repair. Dysregulation of clock gene expression may also impair the activation of immunogenic cell death (ICD)‐related signaling pathways, ultimately diminishing the immunostimulatory effects of radiotherapy.

These findings provide a novel perspective for the integration of immunotherapy with radiotherapy. Given that CAR‐T cell therapy is a form of immunotherapy, future strategies should consider circadian influences when radiotherapy and CAR‐T cell infusion are scheduled. Through further research, individualized and precise treatment schedules can be developed to create a tumor immune microenvironment that is more conducive to CAR‐T cell activity while maintaining a radiosensitive state.

Together, these observations suggest that temporal parameters, including treatment sequencing and circadian influences, may represent modifiable variables in RT‐CAR‐T cell combination strategies, warranting systematic investigation in future preclinical and clinical studies.

A PRACTICAL RADIOTHERAPY‐CENTERED DESIGN FRAMEWORK FOR RT–CAR‐T

Building on the design‐oriented challenges outlined above, emerging strategies have begun to move beyond single‐parameter optimization towards integrative RT‐CAR‐T design concepts. Rather than relying on RT alone to remodel the tumor microenvironment, these approaches use radiotherapy‐induced signals as actionable cues for CAR engineering and combinatorial interventions.

Innovative combination therapies based on this mechanism have been developed. In a study by Lin et al., 75 RT enhanced the tumor‐killing effects of CXCR2‐expressing CAR‐NK92 cells against hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) of mice, whereas no such effect was observed in a control group of non‐modified CXCR2 cells. This effect is attributed to specific interactions between radiotherapy‐induced chemokines and their corresponding receptors. By analyzing the profile of upregulated chemokines in the tumor microenvironment after radiotherapy, researchers can design CAR‐T cells that express specific chemokine receptors and tailor treatments. This “radiotherapy‐induced CAR response” precision strategy holds promise for improving CAR‐T cell therapy efficacy, particularly in solid tumors.

In addition to enhancing CAR‐T cell trafficking, other strategies aim to mitigate radiotherapy‐induced immunosuppressive feedback by targeting specific regulatory pathways within the tumor microenvironment. Combination therapy strategies offer promising new approaches to mitigate radiotherapy‐induced immunosuppression. A preclinical study by Zhang et al., 76 demonstrated that HFRT (5×5 Gy) can upregulate the expression of chemokine axes such as CCR2‐CCL2, promoting the migration of MDSCs to the TME. Furthermore, a CXCR2 antagonist effectively reduced MDSC infiltration into tumors while significantly enhancing the tumor‐targeting ability and antitumor activity of CAR‐T cells.

In glioblastoma models, “triple” therapy combining radiotherapy, CD39 inhibitors, and CAR‐T cell therapy significantly increased the number of TILs and improved the survival prognosis of tumor‐bearing mice. Radiotherapy activates the STAT1‐IRF1 pathway, leading to CD39 upregulation, which promotes tumor immune suppression. CD39 inhibitors (e.g., POM‐1) block this pathway, enhancing ICD and enabling CAR‐T cells to recognize additional tumor‐associated antigens through T‐cell receptors (TCRs), thus overcoming resistance due to antigen loss. 77

These findings provide valuable insights into the mechanisms of RT in combination with CAR‐T cell therapy. By studying the signaling pathways associated with radiotherapy resistance and immune suppression, the integration of additional treatments with radiotherapy and CAR‐T combination therapy could further overcome the negative effects of radiotherapy, leading to more effective treatment outcomes.

However, this negative regulatory feature does not diminish the potential benefits of combining radiotherapy with CAR‐T cell therapy for solid tumors. First, the effect of radiotherapy on immune checkpoints is dose‐dependent, suggesting that hypofractionated regimens may maximize immune activation while minimizing the inhibitory effects.

Second, the development of next‐generation CAR‐T cells provides innovative approaches for overcoming these challenges. For instance, PD1/CD28 chimeric receptor (CSR) technology, developed by researchers, successfully converts the inhibitory PD‐1 signal into an activating CD28 signal, enhancing CAR‐T‐cell functionality. 78 When combined with radiotherapy, these engineered CAR‐T cells not only resist radiotherapy‐induced immune suppression but also shift inhibitory signals towards activation signals, thereby significantly improving the antitumor response. Collectively, these studies highlight the potential of integrating radiotherapy‐responsive CAR engineering and immune checkpoint modulation as complementary components within a radiotherapy‐CAR‐T design framework.

Furthermore, several studies have confirmed significant synergistic effects between radiotherapy and CAR‐T cell therapy, or between CAR‐T cell therapy and ICI therapy, providing a solid theoretical foundation for the combination of LDRT with ICIs and CAR‐T cell therapy. 9 , 79 Liu et al. 80 provided compelling evidence to support this approach in non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).

Liang et al. 81 conducted a pioneering study using TCGA data, reporting a significant correlation between radiation‐sensitive gene signatures and CD19 expression levels. They observed that patients with gastric cancer with high CD19 expression and radiation sensitivity had a better prognosis. This finding provides a molecular biomarker to help select patients who will benefit from combination therapy and highlights the need for a multiparametric predictive model that integrates radiation sensitivity gene signatures and target antigen expression profiles, enabling truly personalized treatment plans.

CONCLUSION

CAR‐T cell therapy has achieved significant success in the treatment of hematological malignancies; however, many challenges in treating solid tumors still exist. These challenges include the unique immunosuppressive microenvironment of solid tumors, antigen heterogeneity, and treatment‐related toxicities. As a classic antitumor treatment, radiation therapy not only directly kills tumor cells, but also offers potential benefits for enhancing CAR‐T cell therapy by remodeling the TME, increasing tumor antigen exposure, promoting immune cell infiltration, and reducing immune suppression. The combination of CAR‐T cells with radiation therapy represents a significant step forward in integrating CAR‐T cell immunotherapy with traditional cancer treatments.

This review discusses the potential advantages and strategies of combining radiation therapy with CAR‐T cells for the treatment of solid tumors. At the spatial level, radiation therapy improves CAR‐T cell homing and infiltration. At the molecular level, it overcomes antigen heterogeneity, enhances T cell function, and extends the antitumor immune response at the temporal level. Although the current preclinical and clinical research is promising, many unresolved issues remain in terms of optimizing treatment protocols.

Key areas for future research include developing a precise system for regulating radiation therapy parameters (such as dose, mode, and timing), rationally designing CAR targets, and advancing the development of next‐generation CAR‐T cells. Furthermore, interdisciplinary integrated treatment models should be explored. Future studies should continue to investigate the molecular mechanisms of combination therapy and validate its safety and efficacy in multicenter clinical trials. With continued interdisciplinary collaboration and technological innovation, this combined treatment strategy could open a new era in the treatment of solid tumors, ultimately leading to significant breakthroughs in the translation of basic research into clinical applications.
