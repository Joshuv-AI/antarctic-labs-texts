Recovery of urinary continence after neoadjuvant hormonal therapy plus radical prostatectomy: A multicenter retro-prospective cohort study with propensity score matching
Authors: Zhongqing Zhang, Jiaxi Mi, Tong Lu, Yuwen Wang, Yu Chen, Shujun Liu, Jiajun Qian, Shun Zhang, Xuefeng Qiu, Yao Fu, Linfeng Xu, Shaohao Chen, Jianhao Wu, Yun Jin, Hongqian Guo, Weidong Gan, Junlong Zhuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the impact of neoadjuvant hormonal therapy (NHT) combined with robotic-assisted radical prostatectomy (RARP) on postoperative urinary continence (UC) recovery compared with RARP alone in patients with localized prostate cancer.MethodsA multicenter retro-prospective cohort study was conducted involving 2453 patients treated between November 2018 and November 2023 at four tertiary hospitals in China. After applying exclusion criteria, 1927 patients who completed 1-year follow-up were included (760 NHT plus RARP and 1167 RARP alone). Propensity score matching (1:1) was performed based on age, prostate volume, body mass index, preoperative prostate-specific antigen level, surgical approach, and pT stage, resulting in 249 matched pairs. UC was defined as using no pad or one safety pad per day and assessed via standardized questionnaires during follow-up visits.ResultsThe NHT group demonstrated significantly better UC recovery compared with the control group (p<0.001). The median time to UC recovery was 8 (interquartile range: 3–24) weeks for the NHT group and 16 (interquartile range: 4–34) weeks for the control group. Short-term continence rates (range: 2–12 weeks) were significantly higher in the NHT group (42% vs. 24%, p<0.001). Multivariate Cox regression analysis identified NHT (hazard ratio 1.273, 95% confidence interval 1.063–1.525, p=0.009) and age (hazard ratio 0.968, 95% confidence interval 0.955–0.980, p<0.001) as independent predictors of UC recovery.ConclusionPreoperative NHT was associated with significantly improved postoperative UC recovery after RARP, including faster recovery time and higher short-term continence rates. These findings suggest that NHT may provide functional benefits beyond its established oncological effects in prostate cancer treatment.Keywords: Neoadjuvant hormonal therapy, Robotic-assisted radical prostatectomy, Urinary continence, Prostate cancer, Propensity score matching

Introduction

Localized and locally advanced prostate cancer (PCa), especially high-risk cases, remains a significant health threat to elderly males worldwide. Compared with low- or intermediate-risk cases, patients with high-risk PCa undergoing robot-assisted radical prostatectomy (RARP) with extended pelvic lymph node dissection face increased risks of biochemical recurrence, metastatic progression, and cancer-specific mortality [1]. Radical prostatectomy (RP), the standard treatment for localized PCa, often leads to post-prostatectomy incontinence, with reported rates ranging from 2.9% to 87%, influenced by surgical technique, surgeon experience, and patient factors, though RARP may offer better continence outcomes (4%–31% incontinence rate at 12 months) [2].

The pursuit of functional outcomes has become as crucial as oncological control in contemporary PCa care. Validated questionnaires demonstrate that urinary continence (UC) recovery contributes more significantly to postoperative satisfaction than either oncological outcomes or sexual function restoration [3]. This clinical imperative is further underscored by health economic analyses showing that urinary incontinence affects 17% of men over 60 years old in the United States, with costs doubling since 1992; annual spending per patient is 2.4 times higher than that of unaffected individuals, which highlights the need for better prevention and management strategies amid an aging population [4].

Neoadjuvant hormonal therapy (NHT) before RP has emerged as a potential strategy to improve oncological outcomes in high-risk cases. Although its role remains controversial, several studies have demonstrated its benefits, including reduced positive surgical margins, decreased extracapsular extension, improved biochemical recurrence rates, and decreased PCa-specific mortality [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. A recent 11-year follow-up study showed that NHT results in superior perioperative outcomes, particularly in non-low-risk disease [9]. The impact of NHT on postoperative UC remains unclear. Multiple factors affecting UC recovery have been identified, including rhabdosphincter reconstruction, membranous urethral length (MUL), prostate volume (PV), and intravesical prostatic protrusion length [[10], [11], [12]]. Notably, greater preoperative MUL has been significantly associated with improved overall time to UC recovery as well as recovery at 3-, 6-, and 12-months post-RARP (odds ratio 1.09, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.05–1.15, p<0.001) [13].

Previous studies have demonstrated that NHT may exert beneficial effects on UC through multiple mechanisms. Key findings include a reduction in PV, enhanced surgical working space, shortened pneumoperitoneum duration, decreased operative time, and minimized intraoperative blood loss [14,15]. Additionally, further research has revealed that prostate collagen organization is influenced by both aging and androgen levels [16].

However, no studies have definitively established whether preoperative NHT affects postoperative UC recovery, and the definitions and assessment methods for UC remain inconsistent across studies [17,18].

Therefore, we conducted a retro-prospective study using propensity score matching to evaluate the impact of NHT on postoperative UC rates following RARP. Our multicenter study addresses these evidence gaps through three key innovations: (1) standardized intensive NHT protocols (androgen deprivation therapy [ADT] plus novel hormonal agents); (2) propensity-matched analysis of 1927 robotic procedures; (3) prospective continence assessment using International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire–Short Form (ICIQ-SF) with MRI correlation. These methodological advances enable us to test the central hypothesis that NHT enhances UC recovery through both anatomical preservation (MUL maintenance) and surgical precision (neurovascular bundle [NVB] sparing) pathways. The findings hold immediate clinical relevance for shared decision-making in high-risk localized PCa management.

Patients and methods

Study design

The hybrid design approach, sometimes called “retro-prospective”, is well-established in outcomes research when researchers prospectively collect certain endpoints in otherwise retrospective cohorts [19]. We conducted a multicenter retro-prospective cohort study with two distinct components: (a) the retrospective arm—collection of baseline characteristics and perioperative data from electronic medical records (from November 2018 to November 2023); (b) the prospective arm—standardized assessment of UC outcomes through clinic-administered ICIQ-SF questionnaires at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months post-RARP, and structured telephone interviews for non-attendees (completion rate: 92.4%).

Patients

Between November 2018 and November 2023, we conducted a multicenter retro-prospective cohort study of 2453 patients with clinically localized PCa who underwent RARP at four tertiary hospitals (Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital, The Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University Medical School; The First Affiliated Hospital, Fujian Medical University; The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University; The First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University) in China. Following this retro-prospective design, baseline clinical characteristics and perioperative data were collected retrospectively, while UC outcomes were assessed prospectively through standardized follow-up protocols. Among these patients, data from subgroups were obtained from two clinical trials (NCT04356430, registered on April 22, 2020, and NCT05406999, registered on June 7, 2022) with authorization from the principal investigators. The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committees of all participating institutions (primary site approval number: 2021-083-02, Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital). While the ethics committee waived the requirement for written informed consent for retrospective data collection, all patients provided written informed consent for prospective assessment of continence recovery outcomes, which was conducted through structured questionnaires during follow-up visits or telephone interviews according to the standardized protocol.

The inclusion criteria were: (a) histologically confirmed prostate adenocarcinoma (International Society of Urological Pathology grade ≥1); (b) clinically localized disease (cT1-3aN0M0 per American Joint Committee on Cancer 8th edition); (c) completed either direct RARP (control arm) or six-cycle NHT followed by RARP (intervention arm); (d) minimum 12-month follow-up with complete continence assessments; (e) non-nerve-sparing surgery only (no bilateral or unilateral NVB preservation).

The exclusion criteria were: (a) age >85 years (institutional review board restriction); (b) metastatic disease (confirmed by prostate-specific membrane antigen positron emission tomography/CT); (c) non-adenocarcinoma pathology (e.g., small cell, urothelial); (d) receipt of non-protocol therapies: any radiation therapy administered within 12 months before surgery, focal therapies (high-intensity focused ultrasound or cryotherapy), systemic chemotherapy regimens, or any additional hormonal therapies beyond the study's NHT protocol; (e) incomplete data (missing >30% of baseline covariates or outcome data); (f) withdrawal of consent for prospective follow-up; (g) pre-existing voiding dysfunction (e.g., urinary retention or urinary incontinence) to minimize confounding effects of baseline urinary impairment on postoperative functional outcomes.

The inclusion criteria specifically excluded patients undergoing NVB preservation because neoadjuvant therapy is typically administered to individuals with clinically advanced stage disease, where tumors often exhibit extensive invasion into surrounding vascular and neural structures. In such cases, attempting NVB preservation would significantly increase the risk of positive surgical margins, adversely affecting patient prognosis. By restricting the analysis to non-nerve-sparing cases, we ensured comparability between the control and NHT groups, eliminating confounding effects from differential surgical techniques on functional outcomes.

Clinical data, including demographic characteristics and surgery-related variables, were retrieved from electronic medical records. After applying these criteria, 1927 patients were eligible for analysis, comprising 760 patients in the NHT group and 1167 in the control group (Fig. 1).

Propensity score matching

Propensity scores were generated using logistic regression, which included the following covariates: age (continuous), PV (MRI-derived), body mass index (BMI; categorized: <25 kg/m2, 25–30 kg/m2, and >30 kg/m2), preoperative prostate-specific antigen (PSA) (log-transformed), surgical approach (Retzius-sparing RARP vs. conventional RARP), and pT stage (≤pT2 vs. ≥pT3).

The matching protocol comprised nearest neighbor 1:1 matching without replacement, a caliper width of 0.01 of the propensity score standard deviation (SD) (justified by pilot analysis showing optimal balance at this threshold) [20], and forced exact matching on surgical center.

Balance assessment

Following propensity score matching, we rigorously assessed the balance between the treatment and control groups to verify comparability. The success of the matching procedure was evaluated using two complementary approaches.

For continuous variables (including age, PV, and BMI), we calculated the standardized mean differences. Adequate balance was defined as a standardized mean difference of less than 0.1 for each variable.

For categorical variables (including PSA category, pT stage, and surgical approach), we conducted chi-square tests (or Fisher’s exact tests where appropriate). Additionally, as a supplementary check, Mann–Whitney U tests were used to assess the distribution balance for continuous variables. A p-value >0.05 was taken to indicate adequate balance between the groups.

MRI subgroup analysis

A predefined subgroup of patients underwent 3 T multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) according to the following inclusion criteria: (a) availability of a pre-NHT baseline MRI; (b) post-NHT or pre-RARP MRI performed ≤14 days before surgery; (c) adherence to a standardized imaging protocol compliant with Prostate Imaging Reporting and Data System v2.1 guidelines [21].

MUL was measured on sagittal T2-weighted magnetic resonance images at the level of the prostatic apex.

This structured approach ensured consistency in imaging acquisition and analysis, minimizing variability in anatomical assessments.

Intervention

NHT

NHT protocols were determined by treating physicians based on individual patient characteristics and institutional guidelines. The therapeutic backbone consisted of ADT utilizing luteinizing hormone-releasing hormone analogs (LHRHas), including triptorelin, goserelin, leuprolide, or degarelix, administered according to standard prescribing protocols. In conjunction with ADT, patients received one of several novel hormonal agents: abiraterone acetate (1000 mg daily with concurrent prednisolone 5 mg daily), bicalutamide (50 mg daily), flutamide (750 mg daily), enzalutamide (160 mg daily), apalutamide (240 mg daily), or darolutamide (1200 mg daily in divided doses). The neoadjuvant protocol consisted of six 28-day cycles, followed by RARP performed within 14 days of completing systemic therapy. For subgroup analysis, patients were stratified based on the efficacy of androgen receptor (AR) inhibition: the potent AR inhibitors group (n=196) comprised abiraterone (n=169), enzalutamide (n=8), apalutamide (n=13), and darolutamide (n=6), which exert profound AR pathway suppression through CYP17 inhibition (abiraterone) or next-generation AR antagonism (enzalutamide, apalutamide, and darolutamide); the first-generation antiandrogen group (n=53) included bicalutamide (n=50) and flutamide (n=3), which exhibit weaker AR blockade and potential partial agonist effects.

Surgical procedure

All enrolled patients underwent non-nerve-sparing surgery (no bilateral or unilateral NVB preservation). All surgical procedures were performed using the da Vinci Si Surgical System (Intuitive Surgical, Inc., Sunnyvale, CA, USA). The choice between Retzius-sparing RARP and conventional RARP was based on comprehensive evaluation of patient factors including age, physical condition, tumor characteristics, and requirements for erectile function preservation. Surgery was scheduled within 2 weeks of initial diagnosis for the control group or within 2 weeks of completing NHT for the treatment group.

Several established surgical techniques were employed to optimize postoperative UC recovery [22,23]: preservation of maximal MUL, meticulous dorsal venous complex ligation, and posterior reconstruction.

Postoperatively, all patients received standardized instructions for long-term Kegel exercises as part of their rehabilitation protocol.

Assessment of UC

Postoperative UC was evaluated using the validated ICIQ-SF. Continence was defined as the use of no pad or one safety pad per day [24]. Patients were classified as “no recovery” if they failed to achieve continence by study completion or were lost to follow-up without prior recovery. For temporal analysis, UC recovery was categorized into four periods: immediate continence (within 7 days after catheter removal), short-term continence (between 2 weeks and 12 weeks post-surgery), long-term continence (between 13 weeks and 52 weeks post-surgery), and no recovery (after 52 weeks post-surgery).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). Potential factors influencing UC recovery were identified as age, PV, BMI, preoperative PSA level, surgical approach (conventional vs. Retzius-sparing), and pT stage. Continuous variables were expressed as median with interquartile range (IQR) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies with percentages and compared using either the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate.

To minimize selection bias, propensity score matching was performed using the nearest neighbor method without replacement, with a caliper width of 0.01 [20]. Matching variables included age, PV, BMI, preoperative PSA level, surgical approach, and pT stage. Patients outside the matched propensity score range were excluded, resulting in 1:1 matched pairs.

Time to continence recovery was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves, and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were conducted to identify independent factors associated with UC recovery. Statistical significance was set at a p-value <0.05 for all analyses.

Availability of data and materials

The datasets generated and analyzed during the current study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The study initially included 1927 patients (760 in the NHT group and 1167 in the control group). Before propensity score matching, there were significant differences between groups in the initial PV (median [IQR]: 36.92 [27.58–52.40] mL vs. 32.64 [24.80–43.10] mL, p<0.001), preoperative PSA distribution (p<0.001), pT stage distribution (p<0.001), and surgical approach (p=0.048). No significant differences were observed in age (median [IQR]: 69.0 [64.0–73.5] years vs. 69.0 [65.0–74.0] years, p=0.7) or preoperative BMI (median [IQR]: 23.62 [20.90–26.86] kg/m2 vs. 24.38 [22.67–26.34] kg/m2, p=0.9). Standardized mean differences for all continuous variables were <0.1 (age: −0.052; PV: −0.050; BMI: 0.053), indicating excellent balance.

After 1:1 propensity score matching, 249 pairs were identified for analysis. The matched cohorts demonstrated good balance in all baseline characteristics, including age (median [IQR]: 70.0 (65.0–74.0) years vs. 69.0 (64.0–74.0) years, p=0.7), initial PV (median [IQR]: 33.82 (25.53–46.35) mL vs. 33.40 (24.30–45.10) mL, p=0.7), preoperative BMI (median [IQR]: 24.21 (21.62–27.00) kg/m2 vs. 24.22 (22.22–26.12) kg/m2, p=0.5), preoperative initial PSA distribution (p=1), postoperative pT stage (p=0.8), and surgical approach (p=0.061) (Table 1).

Analysis of UC recovery

UC rates at different time points

Analysis of UC rates during the follow-up period revealed distinct patterns between groups. In the NHT group versus the control group, the UC rates were: immediate (19% vs. 20%, p=0.8), short-term (42% vs. 24%, p<0.001), long-term (35% vs. 51%, p<0.001), and no recovery (3.6% vs. 4.8%, p=0.7) (Table 2). The median time to UC recovery was significantly shorter in the NHT group compared with the control group (8 [IQR 3–24] weeks vs.16 [IQR 4–34] weeks, p<0.001).

Survival analysis

Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated significantly better UC recovery in the NHT group compared with the control group (log-rank test, p<0.001) (Fig. 2). The difference was particularly pronounced during the short-term recovery period (2–12 weeks).

Predictive factors

In the multivariate Cox regression analysis, two independent factors were identified as significantly associated with UC recovery: NHT treatment (hazard ratio 1.273, 95% CI 1.063–1.525, p=0.009) and age (hazard ratio 0.968, 95% CI 0.955–0.980, p<0.001). Other variables, including initial PSA, initial PV, preoperative BMI, surgical approach, and pT stage, showed no significant association with UC recovery (Table 3).

Subgroup analysis by NHT regimen

Baseline characteristics

A predefined subgroup analysis was performed to compare UC recovery between patients receiving strong AR inhibitors (SAIs, n=196) and first-generation AR antagonists (FAAs, n=53). Baseline characteristics of the subgroups are presented in Supplemental Table 1. The two groups showed no significant differences in age (median [IQR]: 70.00 [65.00–74.00] years vs. 70.00 [66.50–75.50] years, p=0.13), initial PV (median [IQR]: 33.50 [25.15–44.96] mL vs. 32.65 [29.66–50.90] mL, p=0.14), preoperative BMI (median [IQR]: 24.38 [21.90–27.34] kg/m2 vs. 23.67 [21.15–26.90] kg/m2, p=0.3), preoperative PSA distribution (p=0.7), pT stage (p=0.4), or surgical approach (p=0.14) (Supplemental Table 1).

Continence recovery kinetics

Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed no significant difference in UC recovery between SAI and FAA groups (log-rank p=0.3), with median recovery times of 9 (IQR 4–23) weeks and 11 (IQR 5–27) weeks, respectively. At 52-week follow-up, 3.1% (6/196) of patients in the SAI group and 5.7% (3/53) of patients in the FAA group remained incontinent (Fig. 3).

Kaplan–Meier curves of UC recovery following different androgen receptor-targeted therapies. SAI, strong androgen receptor inhibitor; FAA, first-generation androgen receptor antagonist; UC, urinary continence.

Interval-specific outcomes

The SAI group demonstrated higher immediate continence rates (21% vs. 11%, absolute risk difference 9.6% [95% CI −2.1% to 21%], p=0.2), while short-term (42% vs. 42%, p=0.9) and long-term UC recovery rates (34% vs. 42%, p=0.3) were comparable. Persistent incontinence at 52 weeks occurred in 3.1% and 5.7% of patients in the SAI and FAA groups, respectively (p=0.4) (Supplemental Table 2).

MUL

In the MRI subgroup analysis, patients in the NHT group had a significantly longer MUL than those in the control group (median [IQR]: 14.41 [11.93–16.64] mm vs. 13.12 [11.18–15.00] mm; p=0.016).

Discussion

Thus far, a common standard for assessing UC has not been established. To evaluate the impact of UC on patients’ lives, scholars have developed several self-completed questionnaires, but most of them are specific to certain patient groups or are too lengthy for clinical application. Consequently, the International Continence Society developed the ICIQ-SF [25]. Compared with other questionnaires such as the Incontinence Impact Questions, Urinary Distress Inventory, 24-Item Large Impact Scale, and York Incontinence Perception Scale, the ICIQ-SF is more concise and widely used among postoperative PCa patients [26]. Furthermore, Chen et al. [27] proved in their study that the Chinese version of the ICIQ-SF is valid and reliable for assessing the impact of urinary incontinence on the quality of life in Chinese patients and for indicating the cause of incontinence.

Using the ICIQ-SF questionnaire, we evaluated postoperative UC recovery in patients receiving either pre-RARP ADT plus abiraterone or direct RARP. As there is no evidence-based indication for single-agent abiraterone without ADT, this preoperative regimen was not administered to PCa patients at our clinical center or included in our clinical trial. All previous neoadjuvant abiraterone studies used ADT-containing controls rather than abiraterone monotherapy [28,29]. Overall, ADT plus abiraterone resulted in earlier UC compared with prostatectomy alone, particularly in the short-term and long-term postoperative periods.

Urinary incontinence has long been a serious complication of RP. Surgery inevitably causes postoperative urinary incontinence through anatomical changes to the sphincter and surrounding support structures [30]. Various surgical techniques aim to improve the early return of UC after RP, including RARP, Retzius-sparing prostatectomy, periurethral suspension stitches, maximizing urethral length preservation, and posterior reconstruction [31]. Postoperatively, health education and pelvic floor exercises like Kegel training also improve continence [32]. However, few studies have examined the effects of preoperative hormonal therapy on continence recovery, warranting further research [33,34].

Some studies suggest that Retzius-sparing approaches particularly benefit immediate postoperative continence [35,36]. However, evidence does not demonstrate improved mid-to long-term continence with Retzius-sparing prostatectomy [[37], [38], [39]]. Our study uniquely demonstrated higher UC rates in both short-term and long-term postoperative periods, addressing a knowledge gap regarding postoperative UC recovery. Even so, further multicenter clinical trials are needed to confirm long-term outcomes.

With advancements in PCa systemic therapy, NHT has garnered increasing interest. NHT is administered prior to definitive local-regional treatment [40]. Previous studies showed that reduced androgen production or bioavailability directly causes muscle atrophy, as androgens play a major role in maintaining or restoring muscle mass [41,42]. Thus, NHT may impair pelvic floor muscle function, potentially delaying the return of UC. Adam et al. [43] reported that secondary ADT after RP exacerbates declines in urinary function, sexual potency, and quality of life. However, preoperative NHT often demonstrates different outcomes. Several studies indicate that pre-RP NHT may downstage tumors to enable easier surgical resection, reducing positive surgical margins and improving perioperative outcomes [44,45]. Additionally, Achard et al. [46] found that pre-RP NHT could alter tumor behavior and biology by changing atrophic metabolic patterns, lowering serum PSA, and reducing histopathological atrophy. These tumor responses may enable easier, more efficient prostate removal during RP, with fewer intraoperative comorbidities [47], since enlarged prostates typically exhibit greater vascularity and require wider resection margins [48]. Given these findings, NHT prior to RP has been widely adopted. However, whether preoperative NHT affects postoperative UC remains debated and lacks sufficient evidence.

Our study demonstrated that NHT prior to RARP was associated with significantly improved short-term and long-term UC recovery. Notably, patients who received NHT had longer preoperative MUL, a factor previously associated with better postoperative UC outcomes [49]. These findings suggest a potential anatomical mechanism linking NHT to UC recovery.

However, it is important to acknowledge conflicting evidence in the literature. For example, Chen et al. [27] conducted a matched cohort study of 204 patients (51 with NHT) and found no significant difference in UC rates, while reporting worse hormonal-related quality of life scores in the NHT group. This discrepancy may be attributable to differences in sample size, NHT regimen (LHRHas alone), lack of MRI-based MUL assessment, and surgical variability. In contrast, our study included a larger, propensity-score-matched cohort (n=498), used intensified androgen deprivation (e.g., abiraterone plus LHRHas), and incorporated objective anatomical measurements.

In line with our findings, Yang et al. [50] reported that NHT was an independent predictor of improved UC recovery after open RP, possibly due to elongation of the membranous urethra. Conversely, Horie et al. [51] observed modest UC recovery in a non-nerve-sparing cohort receiving NHT, likely impacted by surgical extent and older patient age. These divergent results underscore the importance of surgical technique, baseline anatomy, and NHT protocol heterogeneity in shaping functional outcomes.

Biologically, our hypothesis that NHT facilitates elongation of the membranous urethra and reduction of periurethral bulk is plausible but remains unproven. Future prospective studies should incorporate pre- and post-NHT pelvic MRI to directly quantify anatomical changes, and molecular profiling of periprostatic tissues could further elucidate the mechanistic pathways. According to previous studies, the mean MUL measurements range from approximately 10.4 mm to 14.5 mm [13,52]. For further evidence, we compared the preoperative MUL of the two groups, and results showed that patients treated with preoperative abiraterone usually have longer membranous urethra than direct-surgery patients. Therefore, we propose several hypotheses that may explain the current result. First, patients after NHT are more likely to have longer MUL, smaller PV, and shorter intravesical prostatic protrusion length before surgery, thus leading to better preservation of maximal urethral length. Second, patients after NHT may undergo more efficient reconstruction of the rhabdosphincter due to a clearer surgical field. Finally, NHT was significantly associated with shorter operative time and less blood loss, indicating better physical condition for recovery.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in our study. First, the retrospective design inherently introduces potential selection biases and limits causal inference. Second, the exclusive enrollment of Asian patients may restrict the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic populations. Third, our assessment of UC relied solely on patient-reported outcomes (pad usage and ICIQ-SF) without objective measures such as urodynamic studies, electromyography, or imaging-based assessments of pelvic floor function. While this approach is clinically pragmatic and aligns with real-world practice, it precludes mechanistic insights into whether the observed differences in short-term continence recovery reflect changes in sphincter function, anatomical preservation, or other physiological factors—a key limitation given the potential influence of androgen deprivation on pelvic floor dynamics. Fourth, the FAA subgroup’s underpowered sample size (n=53) precludes definitive conclusions about regimen-specific effects. However, the directional consistency of our findings with SAIs’ pharmacological properties suggests biological plausibility warranting prospective validation [53].

Additionally, the substantial sample size reduction after propensity score matching (from 1927 to 498 cases) may limit statistical power for detecting subtle effects, particularly in subgroup analyses such as the SAI group (n=196) and FAA regimen group (n=53), though our primary analysis maintained adequate power to detect clinically meaningful differences. Although the SAI group showed a numerically higher immediate continence rate (21% vs. 11%), this difference did not reach statistical significance (absolute risk difference 9.6%, 95% CI −2.1% to 21%; p=0.2) (Supplemental Table 2), likely due to limited sample size in the FAA subgroup. Further studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate this observation. These limitations underscore the need for future prospective studies incorporating: (1) objective functional assessments of pelvic floor recovery; (2) detailed hormonal profiling; (3) standardized rehabilitation protocols; and (4) larger, balanced cohorts receiving specific NHT agents.

We recommend that subsequent studies employ randomized controlled designs to compare various NHT regimens while incorporating both anatomical (e.g., MUL) and functional endpoints (e.g., urodynamics and electromyography). These studies should stratify outcomes by age, BMI, surgical technique, and hormonal agent type, with long-term follow-up to fully characterize recovery trajectories. Furthermore, multicenter studies stratifying by NHT regimen type are needed, particularly given emerging data on differential continence recovery profiles among next-generation antiandrogens. Only through such comprehensive investigations can we definitively elucidate the mechanisms underlying the observed differences in continence recovery patterns.

Conclusion

In this large-scale, multicenter propensity-score-matched study involving four tertiary hospitals in China, we demonstrated that NHT before RARP significantly improved postoperative UC recovery. Patients receiving NHT showed better UC outcomes, particularly during the short-term (2–12 weeks) and long-term (13–52 weeks) postoperative periods. The improvement in continence recovery was independent of other clinical factors, suggesting that NHT may be a valuable treatment strategy for optimizing functional outcomes after RARP. However, prospective, international multicenter randomized controlled trials are warranted to validate these findings across diverse populations and establish optimal protocols for preoperative hormonal therapy in the context of RARP.
