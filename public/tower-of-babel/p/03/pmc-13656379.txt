Patient-specific variables predicting triage outcomes of ear, nose, throat referrals from primary care for dysphonia
Authors: Christopher Payten, Duy Duong Nguyen, Kelly Weir, Catherine Madill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with dysphonia are referred to Speech-Language Pathologists (SLP) or Ear Nose and Throat (ENT) surgeons from General Practitioners (GPs) for specialist assessment. Accurate referral information is needed to determine the assessment-pathway and priority. This study aimed to examine which patient-specific information provided in GP referral documents predicted clinicians' triage outcomes.MethodsThis was an IRB-approved retrospective study of electronic medical records of 150 voice-disordered adults aged ≥ 18 years referred to a tertiary hospital specialist ENT department between March 1, 2019, and July 1, 2020. Data collected included demographics, voice symptoms, risk factors, and medical history. ENT clinicians triaged all patients to either SLP or ENT assessment-pathway and recommended a priority categorisation. The predictive values of documented information in the referral on assessment triage outcome were analysed using logistic regression.ResultsEighty referrals reported hoarseness as the only symptom, only eight reported three or more symptoms. Female gender, previous smoking history, and current smoking history significantly predicted the ENT-pathway for initial assessment. The same variables plus previous thyroid surgery significantly predicted triage to high-priority assessment. However, a quarter of referral letters did not include information about smoking and alcohol consumption.ConclusionGender and smoking history were significant predictors of triage outcomes. No significant association was found between referral symptoms and the triage outcomes. The exclusion of relevant lifestyle and health information likely impacted decision-making at triage. Background

Best practice guidelines for managing people with dysphonia highlight the need for early specialist assessment [1]. The typical route of referral is from the General Practitioner (GP) to Ear, Nose and Throat (ENT) specialists for initial evaluation via an ENT primary contact assessment-pathway (ENT-PC). Wait-times for specialist medical assessment in public healthcare systems worldwide are increasing due to people living longer and healthier lives [2–4]. Healthcare providers are focussed on planning efficient services whilst improving diagnostic and treatment outcomes [2, 3].

Allied Health Primary Contact models are pathways where people with specific symptoms see an Allied Health Practitioner (AHP) for assessment before or instead of a medical doctor [5]. These alternative pathways to the medical-led model have demonstrated statistically significantly reduced wait-times for specialist assessment and improved access to both surgical and non-surgical treatment services [6–8]. Previous research has demonstrated that at least 70% of patients seen in an ENT Speech-Language Pathology Primary Contact (SLP-PC) pathway could be managed by the SLP and/or discharged from ENT without surgical intervention, and by doing so reduced waiting times by a mean 277 days [8–10]. In comparison, 7% required recategorizing for more urgent ENT assessment after being seen by the SLP [10]. These findings suggest that applying the traditional ENT-PC assessment model to all patients with vocal complaints may not always be an efficient use of ENT surgical time or the most direct route to appropriate treatment. However, not all patients will benefit from being seen in the SLP-PC.

To identify those who will benefit from the SLP-PC model, suitable referrals must be carefully selected during referral triage. Queensland Health receives almost 2-million referrals to specialist outpatient services annually [11]. Referrals are triaged according to standard clinical prioritisation criteria (CPC) to determine the assessment-priority (urgency to be seen) and the most appropriate assessment-pathway (surgical or non-surgical) [12].

Research shows that up to 74% of written referrals to specialist ENT services lack essential clinical information and do not meet referral criteria [13, 14]. Furthermore, more than 50% of referrals do not contain details about clinical history and condition-specific information [15]. This leads to delays, misdirected care, and unnecessary specialist burden [15, 16]. Strategies to improve the quality of referrals have been suggested, including structured referral forms and published clinical prioritisation guidance [15]. To the authors’ knowledge, no studies have yet examined the types of information in structured written referrals to specialist ENT services that could predict specialists' triage decisions regarding assessment pathways or priorities.

Study aims

The aim of this study was to examine what information in written referrals significantly predicted (1) ENT triage decision for assessment-pathway (ENT-PC or SLP-PC), and (2) ENT decision for assessment-priority (urgent, routine).

Methods

Study design

This was an Institutional Review Board (IRB)-approved retrospective review of electronic medical records (EMR), a waiver of consent was approved prior to data collection.

Study setting

The study was conducted at a single site public tertiary referral hospital in Queensland, Australia.

Referral triage process

Referrals were received in electronic portable document format (PDF) through an electronic Smart Referrals system [17]. ENT surgeons reviewed the electronic referrals and made decisions on clinical priority and assessment pathway. The standard referral templates were semi-structured with a mix of drop-down menu options for medical history, lifestyle, and medications, and a free-text box for the referral detail. The drop-down menu options are not mandatory to complete (see Appendix 1).

Assessment protocol

Patients seen in the ENT-PC received an initial ENT consultation from either an ENT surgical trainee or an ENT surgical consultant, including a case history and flexible videolaryngoscopy. When patients were seen by a surgical trainee, the case was discussed with an ENT consultant and documented in the patient’s EMR. Patients seen in the SLP-PC received an initial voice evaluation by an advanced SLP, using the standard SLP-PC protocol previously described [10, 18]. Assessment included a case history, the Consensus Auditory Perceptual Evaluation of Voice (CAPE-V) [19], acoustic measures, and videolaryngostroboscopy. After the SLP-PC evaluation, all patients were discussed with an ENT consultant, including review of laryngeal imaging to provide a medical diagnosis, as previously described [10].

Data source

The EMRs of adults aged ≥ 18 years referred to ENT at Gold Coast Health with symptoms of hoarseness between March 1, 2019, and July 1, 2020, were examined.

Data collection

Sample size consideration and sampling method

Stratified sampling was employed to draw equal samples from triage-priority groups (category-1, urgent; category-2/category-3, non-urgent). EMRs of 150 patients were selected using random sampling methods for each of the groups. The power calculation considered a model examining referral symptoms and voice disorder type based on prevalence data from published literature. With α = 0.05 and a power of 90%, 150 was calculated as the minimum appropriate sample size for analysis [16].

Data extraction

The lead author reviewed the EMRs of patients seen for an initial assessment in an ENT-PC or SLP-PC at Gold Coast Health. Data was extracted from the referral by the lead author, and included (1) age; (2) gender (3) presenting symptoms in addition to hoarseness (e.g., dysphagia, cough, globus (non-painful sensation of a lump or foreign body in the throat), throat irritation, throat clearing, odynophagia, otalgia, weight loss); (4) circumstances of symptom onset (e.g. post-surgery; post-illness; post-intubation; (5) previous medical history, e.g., head and neck cancer, neurological diagnosis, respiratory diagnosis, gastroenterology diagnosis, psychiatric diagnosis, previous cardiac surgery, previous thyroid surgery; (6) lifestyle risk factors, e.g. smoking, alcohol, recreational drug history. These variables were selected by the primary author based on the criteria used in the Queensland Health CPC guidelines and voice disorder epidemiology literature [20–22], and their inclusion was agreed upon through consensus discussion with all co-authors.

The dependent variables extracted were (1) assessment-pathway selected for initial evaluation: ENT-PC or SLP-PC clinic; and (2) assessment-priority: category-1(urgent) or category-2/3(non-urgent). When diagnostic information was clearly documented in the patient’s EMR after the initial assessment in the ENT-PC or the SLP-PC, it was extracted and grouped into one of three categories. All patients underwent a diagnostic laryngoscopy, interpreted by an ENT, at their initial appointment. The criteria used to define the diagnostic categories were adapted from a recently published scoping review of classification frameworks for voice disorders [23]. This included: (1) non-organic voice disorder(NOVD), encompassing functional neurological voice disorder (FVD) and muscle tension voice disorder (MTVD); (2) organic voice disorder (OVD) (including vocal fold benign mucosal lesions and neurological disorders, e.g. vocal fold paralysis); and (3) laryngeal malignancy. The referral was allocated to a ‘other group’ if the diagnosis in EMR was not specified or when there was no voice symptom at the time of assessment. All data were anonymised and entered into an Excel 365 dataset stored in a secure password-protected research datastore for analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS 29 for Windows. Descriptive statistics were used to explore demographic information, range of symptoms, and lifestyle factors reported in the referral. Categorical variables were summarised as percentages. Continuous variables were summarised as mean and standard deviation (SD). Univariate logistic regression analysis was used to examine which variables significantly predicted the triage outcomes: (1) assessment-pathway (ENT-PC, SLP-PC) and (2) assessment-priority (urgent, category 1; non-urgent, category 2 or 3). Stepwise multivariate logistic regression was calculated to examine the value of the data given in the referral documents on the triage outcome for assessment-pathway and assessment-priority. The modelling was a forward selection approach, with variables added based on their p-value (p≤ 0.05). Variables considered clinically relevant in predicting the outcome (assessment-pathway and assessment-priority) based on the CPC referral criteria were added to the model to observe their combined interaction with the prediction model [12]. Multinomial logistic regression was completed to examine the relationship between (a) referral data (symptoms and lifestyle factors), (b) assessment-priority assigned at triage, and (c) assessment-pathway assigned at triage, and the voice disorder classification after initial assessment (NOVD, OVD including laryngeal malignancy, other diagnosis). The reference category selected was NOVD and samples categorised as other diagnosis were excluded from the analysis because of the low sample size of this group. In all statistical analyses, the significance level was p < 0.05.

Discussion

This retrospective review of EMRs aimed to determine which information from written referrals for individuals with vocal symptoms significantly predicted the outcome of triage decisions by ENT surgeons at a tertiary referral public hospital. Specifically, it examined the initial assessment-pathway (ENT or SLP) and the assessment-priority assigned. Additionally, this study examined associations between referral information and triage decisions, with voice disorder classification after initial assessment.

Key findings

Analysis of 150 randomly selected referrals to ENT found that only three variables predicted the triage decision outcomes for assessment-pathway (gender, previous smoking history, and current smokers) and 4 variables predicted the triage decision for assessment-priority (gender, previous smoking history, current smokers, and previous thyroid surgery). There was a statistically significant association between previous smoking history, assessment-priority and assessment-pathway assigned at the point of triage, and the classification of OVD and laryngeal malignancy after initial assessment.

Most referrals (74%) in this study represented females. It is widely acknowledged that women are more likely to present with a voice disorder due to a combination of biological, occupational, and psychosocial factors, with no single cause fully explaining this pattern [24]. However, women tend to report more frequent and intense symptoms than men, influenced not only by disease severity but also their perception and coping mechanisms [25, 26]. This may partly explain why female gender was a predictor for triage to ENT assessment and higher priority assessment. The Queensland Health CPC guidelines advise a history of smoking and excessive alcohol use, previous cardiac surgery and previous thyroid surgery is considered a high-priority for assessment of laryngeal symptoms, given the associated risks of neuromuscular/malignant conditions of the head and neck [1]. This explains why smoking history is a significant predictor variable for high-priority assessment by an ENT surgeon and a diagnosis of malignancy in our study. No other variables, including presenting symptoms, lifestyle factors and past medical history, were significant predictors of the two outcomes selected in this study.

Missing referral data

Many variables were excluded from analysis due to low sample sizes in these categories, caused by missing data in the written referrals. The quality of information within written referrals influences how medical specialists make decisions during the triage process [15]. Studies in epidemiology over the past decade have shown that a wide range of health and social factors may be linked to developing a voice disorder, including lifestyle choices (smoking and alcohol consumption), occupational voice demands, and chronic health conditions like hypertension, Type II diabetes, and respiratory diseases [20, 27]. Findings from this study are not unique; ENT referrals with inadequate information have been reported in the literature as factors contributing to long waitlists and inappropriate scheduling [28]. In 2019, a study in Queensland, Australia, found that only 1 in 5 referrals made to ENT met all CPC referral criteria. [29] Furthermore, another audit revealed that fewer than 50% of written referrals to ENT contained sufficient clinical information, and 60% of dysphonia referrals from GPs lacked condition-specific details needed for proper triage [15].

The structure of the written referrals at the time of this study may explain why information relevant to the condition was often excluded, impacting the quality of referrals available for review by the ENT specialists. The electronic referrals were a standard template used at Gold Coast Health, which included free text sections for presenting symptoms, past medical history, medications and lifestyle factors. There were no mandatory fields, and the same referral template was used for all ENT conditions; therefore, specific questions applicable to people with vocal symptoms were excluded. Information on referral criteria for specific conditions is available via the CPC website, and referrers are encouraged to review this information. However, in a Cochrane literature review on processes that influence the effectiveness and efficiency of triaging in subspecialist services, passive dissemination of referral guidelines was deemed unlikely as a useful strategy [14]. The authors recommended structured referral sheets or checklists to prompt referrers on pre-referral investigations, as a more effective alternative [14]. Therefore, adopting structured referral templates with specific referral checklists and mandatory fields specific to voice disorders could enhance the referral process to ENT for people with vocal complaints.

Clinician knowledge and experience of voice disorders

Referral information provided by GPs may be inconsistent and low-value because symptoms of voice disorders can be non-specific, common, and variably described by patients (e.g., sore throat, scratchy voice, hoarseness or fatigue). As a result, symptoms may be misinterpreted, leading to poor alignment between patient language, GP documentation, and specialist interpretation of GP suspicion for cancer, rather than precise characterisation of symptoms [30]. This highlights the need for more standardised, symptom-based risk-stratification tools that use patient-reported information to support the referral process [31].

Furthermore, research has demonstrated that triage decisions made by subspecialist clinicians can vary with their experience (e.g., surgical trainee registrars versus consultants) [32]. The triage process at Gold Coast Health at the time of data collection for this study was performed by multiple ENT trainees at different stages of their surgical training. Surgical trainees with limited exposure to a range of conditions are likely to have less knowledge of presenting symptoms, which may affect decision-making when reviewing referrals [22]. Those with less experience may require a more detailed scaffolding or support structure to build their confidence in making decisions. For example, involving more senior registrars and consultants in the triage of new referrals has been shown to reduce the number of inappropriate referrals accepted for assessment in ENT emergency clinics [33].

Strategies to support referral triage

In primary care, GPs must use their best judgment on a range of symptoms and conditions to determine the timing of referral and which service can provide the most efficient and cost-effective diagnosis [14]. This can be difficult when considering the complexity of a voice disorder, where aetiology can range from muscle tension voice disorder to vocal fold paralysis or laryngeal cancer. A recent systematic review highlighted the need for multi-component interventions to improve the quality of information in GP referrals to secondary care, rather than relying on referral algorithms and passive guideline dissemination [34]. One strategy is GP training in the assessment and management of voice disorders. Another strategy is to incorporate patient questionnaires to capture detailed symptom data to generate a risk score at the point of GP referral. In the UK, a patient-reported symptom-based risk stratification system (SYNC system) is being trialled to improve the referral efficiency of people with suspected head and neck cancer [31]. Furthermore, incorporating multidisciplinary assessment-triage models (e.g., physiotherapy assessment prior to an orthopaedic referral) may be applied to referrals of people with voice disorders [34]. The SLP-PC assessment model is a useful way to obtain quality assessment data which can be used to direct GP referrals to the most reliable subspecialist service. To our knowledge, utilising an SLP assessment in primary care has not been reported in the literature to date, but could be relevant when a reported forty to sixty percent of voice disorders do not have laryngeal pathophysiology and can be managed by an SLP [35].

Finally, GPs can directly apply the findings from this research when referring patients with vocal symptoms for specialist ENT or SLP assessment. Consistently reporting the key risk factors highlighted in these research findings (e.g., smoking and alcohol history, previous thyroid surgery, persistent pain) will help triaging clinicians to quickly identify patients who require more critical ENT assessment to rule out life-limiting disease.

Limitations

Several limitations may have impacted results from this study. Data was collected from a single location, and findings may not represent all healthcare providers. However, referral processes across public health systems appear similar, including clinician review of unstructured referrals [14, 15, 32, 36]. Therefore, the findings may facilitate generalisation to other public health services globally (e.g., Australia, Canada, Ireland, United Kingdom). The current study design, a retrospective review of EMRs, meant data collected for analysis depended on the documentation quality. Variables considered important for inclusion in the regression model (e.g., smoking history, prior thyroid or cardiac surgery, symptom onset) were frequently omitted from referral letters. A prospective design may enable the collection of higher-quality data, attaining more accurate information for analysis. Finally, the experience level of the triaging clinicians, their level of supervision, and individual clinician identifiers that would allow for comparisons were not collected and therefore could not be included in the analysis. Future studies investigating the effectiveness of triage processes should account for the triaging clinician's level of skill and knowledge during data collection and include measures of inter-rater reliability.

Conclusions

This retrospective review of 150 referrals to the ENT department at Gold Coast Health in Queensland, Australia demonstrated gender and smoking history predicted the triage decision for assessment-pathway and gender, smoking history and prior thyroid surgery predicted assessment-priority. Previous smoking history, triage to high priority and triage to ENT assessment-pathway significantly predicted a voice disorder of malignant aetiology. Most referrals did not include the relevant information to aid referral triage, which likely impacted the decision-making of subspecialist ENT surgeons. The provision of training programs for GPs on assessment and management of voice disorders may help facilitate earlier identification of the right information for triage. The use of standardised referral forms with mandatory fields tailored to individual conditions may prompt referrers to provide more detail to facilitate accurate triage. Additional information obtained through a SLP assessment model enabling thorough case history and voice assessment may provide the missing information required by ENTs to prioritise referrals, bridging the gap between primary care and sub-specialist services.
