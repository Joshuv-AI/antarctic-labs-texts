Phase Stability of Monoclinic KDP Single Crystalline Fibers Obtained in Room-Temperature Solution
Authors: Chixuan Li, Qingshan Bao, Jiewu Song, Chengqian Zhang, Yan Ren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
For a long time, it was believed that the monoclinic potassium dihydrogen phosphate (KDP) crystal could not grow directly in solution, unlike its deuterated isomer DKDP. This perception was overturned when the crystal was observed to crystallize in highly supersaturated aqueous solutions. Till now, the phase stability of the monoclinic KDP remains unknown. In this article, a comprehensive investigation is conducted on the phase stability and phase transition behavior of the monoclinic KDP crystal. The crystal transition in solution and the phase transformation to the tetragonal phase after grinding confirm the metastable nature of the monoclinic phase. Nevertheless, despite its metastability, the monoclinic crystal in the solid state maintains a structural stability in air for an extended duration of over 30 days and has no phase transition across a wide temperature range from −180 to 230 °C. These research findings enrich the KDP phase diagram and enable a deeper understanding of the nucleation, crystal growth, and thermodynamic properties of both the monoclinic and tetragonal KDP crystals.

Introduction

Potassium dihydrogen phosphate (KH2PO4, KDP) crystals are typical polymorphic crystals having an intricate structural network constructed by the interplay of hydrogen bonds and the coordination changes of K+. , To date, at least 13 polymorphs of the KDP crystal have been identified. Among them, the tetragonal and monoclinic phases have drawn considerable attention in crystallographic studies. One key reason for this keen interest is that for an extended period, it was widely thought that KDP could not form a monoclinic phase in aqueous solution under ambient conditions. This monoclinic phase, if it existed, would have a phase transition relationship with the tetragonal phase. In contrast, the tetragonal potassium dideuterium phosphate (KD2PO4, DKDP) crystal, which is the isotopic polymorph of KDP, has a corresponding monoclinic phase that can crystallize directly from aqueous solution at room temperature. , The monoclinic DKDP crystal is a stable phase, which can be obtained via the crystal transition of the metastable DKDP tetragonal phase either in solution or in solid under specific conditions. These phenomena were regarded as significant differences in the crystallization habits between the KDP and DKDP crystals.

Previous studies have shown that only under high-temperature or high-pressure conditions can KDP form the monoclinic phase (P21/c) by a phase transition from the tetragonal phase ( I4®2d ). − Typically, a plate-like crystal of the monoclinic phase was obtained under a pressure of 1.62 GPa. For crystallization in ambient solution, however, KDP monoclinic crystals cannot be obtained unless additives are added and the pH of the growth solution is adjusted. , Our research group reported the growth of the monoclinic phase of KDP by spontaneous nucleation in highly supersaturated solution under ambient conditions. Subsequently, other unusual techniques such as electrostatic levitation and rapid microfluidic approaches were adopted to study the nucleation of KDP in extremely high supersaturated solution, and achieved the monoclinic phase of KDP crystal. , However, since it was difficult to separate the crystals from the solution microbeads, their monoclinic phase finally migrated to the tetragonal phase. The phase stabilities of the monoclinic KDP crystal under varied environmental conditions, such as in solution and solid, have never been studied.

In this study, we show the crystal growth of the monoclinic KDP in solution at room temperature without doping any additives, and the crystal transition of the monoclinic phase to the tetragonal phase during the competitive growth of these two phases in solution. Then, we demonstrate the possibility of separating the monoclinic crystals from the solution and their long-term stability in the solid state at ambient conditions with certain humidity. Finally, we discuss the phase stability of the monoclinic KDP crystals in the solid state under mechanical force and at varied temperatures. This work provides direct evidence that the monoclinic phase is the corresponding metastable phase of the KDP tetragonal phase.

Experimental Section

Materials

Potassium dihydrogen phosphate with a purity of 99.9% was used, which is specially produced for the growth of research-grade crystals for high-power laser applications. The crystal growth solution was prepared by using ultrapure water provided by a Merck water purifier (Milli-Q Reference).

Preparation of KDP Monoclinic Crystals

Thirty-five grams of KDP in 100 g of water was heated at 80 °C for 48 h. After completely dissolving the solute, the solution was filtered with a 0.22 μm membrane filter and then sealed in a vial for future use. KDP monoclinic crystals were obtained by dropping the hot solution onto a clean, dry glass substrate at room temperature.

Optical Microscopy

An optical microscope (Olympus SZX16) equipped with a CCD camera (THORLABS) was employed to observe the crystal morphology and monitor the growth process in real time.

Simulation of Crystal Morphology

The morphology of the KDP monoclinic crystal was simulated by using the Bravais Friedel Donnay Harker (BFDH) method provided in Mercury software. The BFDH method uses the unit cell and space group to calculate the d-spacing for each set of Miller indices. Center-to-face distances are assumed to be proportional to the reciprocals of d-spacings, giving a rapid prediction of the morphology.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

A SEM image of monoclinic KDP SCF was taken with a Hitachi SU-8010 ultrahigh resolution field-emission scanning electron microscope at an accelerating voltage of 5.0 kV and the sample was Au-sputtered.

Powder X-ray Diffraction (PXRD)

PXRD measurements were conducted by using a Rigaku SmartLab-9KW diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å). Data over the range 10°–50° in 2θ were collected with a scanning step of 0.02° and a continuous scan rate of 20° min–1 at ambient temperature.

Second Harmonic Generation (SHG) Measurements

In the SHG response experiments, a high-energy picosecond pulse was used; Nd:YAG laser from EKSPLA (1064 nm, 30 ps pulse duration, 100 mJ pulse energy, and 10 Hz repetition rate) was used. The SHG spectrum was obtained using an optical spectrometer (10 ms Integration time, 3648 pixels, 200–1100 nm detector range).

In Situ Variable Temperature Raman Spectroscopy

Raman spectra were collected by a Jobin-Yvon Horiba LabRam HR800 spectrometer. The laser wavelength is 633 nm. Before the in situ variable temperature Raman was performed, the monoclinic SCF was adhered to a small glass slide using heat-resistant adhesive and placed in the reaction chamber. The testing temperature range was from 30 to 230 °C, with a temperature step of 10 °C and a heating rate of 5 °C min–1. Spectral data were collected after the set temperature was achieved and held constant for 5 min.

Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC)

DSC was performed by using a Netzsch DSC 214 instrument in a crimped aluminum pan with a heating rate of 10 °C min–1. The temperature range was from 30 to 300 °C.

Variable Temperature Single-Crystal X-ray Diffraction (VT SC-XRD)

The VT SC-XRD for KDP monoclinic phase was collected on a Bruker D8 VENTURE diffractometer with Cu-Kα (λ = 1.5406 Å). The crystal was mounted on a glass fiber using heat-resistant glue and then affixed to a loop base. The testing temperature range was decreased from 25 to −180 °C under a nitrogen atmosphere at a cooling rate of 6 °C min–1, with X-ray diffraction data collected over a 2θ range of 10°–70°.

Results and Discussion

Crystal Growth and Morphology

Here, a spontaneous nucleation method was adopted to grow monoclinic KDP crystals. A room-temperature highly supersaturated KDP solution (30.90% supersaturation) was directly dropped onto a clean, dry glass substrate without any surface pretreatment. The solution at the edge of the droplet usually evaporates more rapidly than the central parts, and nucleation occurs swiftly at the edge, which results in the growth of bundled KDP single crystals. The ambient conditions are typically at 25 °C with a RH from 30 to 50%. The crystal structure was confirmed to be identical to that of KDP phase IV obtained at high pressure. However, the morphology of the KDP monoclinic crystal formed in an ambient high-supersaturated solution is greatly different from the plate crystal obtained at high pressure. The monoclinic KDP crystals obtained by the spontaneous nucleation method at room temperature have a unique morphology of one-dimensional single crystal fiber (SCF) with a large length-to-diameter ratio (Figure a). Under the polarized microscope (Figure b), the SCFs labeled by the red arrow exhibit uniform color, indicating a high crystal quality. Figure c shows the simulated morphology of KDP monoclinic crystals by the BFDH method using the Mercury program. , The actual shape of the SCF is completely consistent with the calculated results (Figure d).

(a) Microscopic image of KDP monoclinic crystals obtained by the spontaneous nucleation and crystallization. (b) Microscopic image with a polarizing microscope. (c) Simulated morphology of KDP monoclinic crystals by the BFDH method. (d) SEM image of a monoclinic KDP SCF.

Crystal Transition in Solution

With crystal growth and concentration reduction of the solution, the tetragonal crystal with distinct morphology of a tetragonal dipyramid may nucleate and crystallize in the solution. We found that the two phases grow in solution in a competitive mode until the droplet dries up completely. The adjacent tetragonal crystals were growing rapidly, while the monoclinic fibers were dissolving. And in the dried solution droplet, the tetragonal phase will coexist with the monoclinic phase, not yet undissolved. It seems that the tetragonal phase has a stronger competitiveness than the monoclinic phase during growth. To explore the details of the competitive growth, an experiment was designed.

As shown in Figure a, controlling the rapid evaporation of a highly supersaturated solution initially produced only bundled monoclinic KDP SCFs. Subsequently, the growth system was disturbed with a clean needle (Figure b), which led to the formation of microcrystals in the solution (yellow circle) and on the monoclinic crystal rods (blue circle) (Figure c). Once the microcrystals appear in the solution, they compete with the existing monoclinic phase and gradually grow into block crystals with the unique tetragonal bipyramidal morphology (Figure d). As illustrated in Figure e, the monoclinic SCFs directly feed the tetragonal microcrystals on their surface, and they themselves dissolve and fracture, enabling the tetragonal crystals to grow rapidly. One can clearly see that the dissolving ends of the SCFs became pointed and receded around the tetragonal crystals (labeled with a red dotted curve) (Figure f). Finally, the crystals formed are almost all tetragonal crystals.

Microscopic pictures of the crystal transition process from monoclinic phase to tetragonal phase. (a) Monoclinic KDP SCFs were growing. (b) Growth system was subject to a disruption with a needle. (c–f) Growth competition between the two phases concluded with monoclinic dissolution and tetragonal persistence.

The same growth process was confirmed by in situ real-time XRD. As shown in Figure , as the KDP monoclinic phase nucleated and crystallized in the solution at the eighth minute after the solution was dropped on the glass slide, its XRD spectra were collected, which are characterized by peaks at 2θ ∼ 19°, 2θ ∼ 27°, and 2θ ∼ 39° (marked with red arrows). A new diffraction peak was immediately detected at 2θ ∼ 24° (marked with a purple arrow) after disturbing the growth system at the 20th minute. This is the characteristic peak of the KDP tetragonal phase, indicating the heterogeneous nucleation and rapid growth of the tetragonal crystals on the monoclinic crystal surface and in solution. Subsequently, the two crystalline phases compete for growth, resulting in the peak intensity of the monoclinic phase gradually being reduced (see the 28th min), and almost disappearing at the 36th min. By contrast, the diffraction from the crystal facet (200) of the tetragonal phase becomes stronger and stronger, which indicates that the tetragonal phase dominates the final crystals.

Real time XRD pattern of the crystal transition process of KDP showing the evolution of diffraction peaks.

This phenomenon was called “crystal transition”, which has been observed in the growth of the metastable tetragonal phase of DKDP in the presence of its stable monoclinic phase. The metastable phase will be “eaten away” by the stable phase due to the difference in solubility, as a crystal phase with higher solubility tends to be less stable. , The experimental observations of the two KDP crystalline phases in solution clearly demonstrated that the monoclinic phase exhibited a higher solubility than the tetragonal phase. Therefore, from a thermodynamic perspective, the KDP monoclinic phase should be a metastable phase under ambient conditions, while the tetragonal phase is the stable phase. This is opposite to the phase stability relationship between the monoclinic and tetragonal phases of DKDP crystals. ,

In fact, one can obtain a single product of the monoclinic phase by controlling the crystallization conditions to ensure that no tetragonal crystals are generated. Typically, high supersaturation must be maintained from the beginning until the droplet dries completely. In addition, the high-quality monoclinic SCFs have a sufficient stability to be separated from the droplet. We further found that the monoclinic KDP SCFs picked out from solution can be kept stably in the ambient conditions over extended periods. By comparing the XRD diffraction patterns (Figure S1), one can see that the monoclinic SCFs remain relatively stable without phase transition for at least one month under the ambient conditions at 25–30 °C with humidity of ∼30–50%. As the ambient humidity is lower than 10%, the monoclinic SCFs can even be kept for several months without any alteration in the crystal structure. The stability of the monoclinic KDP SCF makes further research of its phase transition properties under other conditions possible.

Grinding-Induced Phase Transition

The structural stability of the monoclinic SCFs was then studied upon applying an external mechanical force. During the experiment, the dried monoclinic crystals were ground in a mortar for 10 min at ambient conditions with humidity less than 30%. To verify the structural alteration, a 1064 nm laser was used to pump the sample before and after grinding. The acquired pictures and spectral data are shown in Figure a–c. One can see that the surface of the monoclinic crystals before grinding is dark upon excitation by a 1064 nm ps laser, and no spectral signal was detected. After the crystals were ground, they emitted a bright green light of 532 nm by laser excitation. Obviously, the second-harmonic generation (SHG) process occurred in the latter, as it was pumped by the focused IR laser. It indicates a structural transition of the crystals from a centrosymmetric to a noncentrosymmetric crystal phase. In the meantime, as shown in Figure d, after grinding, the XRD characteristic peaks of the monoclinic crystal completely transformed to those of the tetragonal crystal. The structural transition occurred on the surface or inside of the crystals due to external mechanical forces applied to the crystal during the grinding process. − Given the high brittleness and low hardness of the KDP crystal, mechanical forces during grinding generated various defects such as cracks, fragmentation, and pits. These high-density crystal defects may cause ions or groups to shift away from their original positions and result in structural rearrangement, thereby accelerating the structural transformation from the metastable monoclinic phase to the stable tetragonal phase.

Phase Stability of Monoclinic Crystals at Varied Temperatures

Temperature is a critical factor in inducing polymorphic transformations in crystalline systems. The temperature-dependent phase transitions and thermal stability of KDP crystals have been extensively investigated owing to the abundant variation of hydrogen-bonded networks and H2PO4 – tetrahedral arrangements in the crystal structure. − Here, the stability of the KDP monoclinic phase under different temperature conditions was investigated. First, Raman scattering spectroscopy was conducted on both tetragonal and monoclinic KDP SCFs. And their Raman signatures are shown in Figure S2. The characteristic peak for the tetragonal phase is located at 915 cm–1, while that for the monoclinic phase is found at 929 cm–1, which is associated with the stretching vibrations of the P–O bonds in KDP structures. , Figure a records the entire process as heating a KDP monoclinic SCF from 30 to 230 °C. It can be clearly observed that at elevated temperatures of 230 °C, the Raman signature changes. The appearance of three new peaks at 398, 681, and 1152 cm–1 indicates the effects of lattice dehydration, resulting in a replacement of hydrogen-bonded interconnection of PO4 tetrahedra with P–O–P bridging oxygen bonds, and forming dehydration products, metaphosphate (KPO3). When the crystals were cooled to 30 °C, the Raman signature remained consistent with the high-temperature state, indicating an irreversible and complete decomposition of the crystal. The DSC thermogram further proves that the endothermic peak found near 230 °C for the present monoclinic form of KDP is produced by the evolution of H2O during the thermal decomposition. Since the thermal decomposition is not a reversible reaction, the exothermic peak corresponding to the endothermic peak can not be observed on the cooling chart (Figure b). Figure c,d presents the thermally induced morphological evolution of monoclinic KDP SCF. At 30 °C, the SCF retains the intact morphology and optical transparency. Upon heating to 230 °C, the SCF exhibits marked darkening, opacity, and surface blistering. These phenomena clearly indicate the dehydration and thermal decomposition within the KDP lattice.

(a) In situ heating Raman spectra and (b) DSC thermogram of KDP monoclinic SCFs. (c, d) Pictures of a monoclinic SCF captured during variable-temperature Raman testing.

Before the heating temperature is lower than 230 °C, vibrational resonances remain consistent with the monoclinic crystalline structure of the room temperature KDP phase, which confirms that the monoclinic KDP crystals do not undergo a phase transition during heating and remain relatively stable at high temperatures up to 230 °C. Numerous studies have investigated the high-temperature phase transitions of the KDP tetragonal phase. Nevertheless, the ambiguity persists regarding whether structural changes near 180 °C correspond to phase transitions or dehydration. This is because the onset temperature of dehydration (∼180 °C) overlaps with reported phase transition ranges. ,, In this study, no thermal effects associated with high-temperature phase transitions were detected during the heating process of the KDP monoclinic phase until decomposition at ∼230 °C. Consequently, the structural disparities result in distinct thermal behaviors between the two crystalline phases of KDP (for crystal structures of the two phases, see Figure S3).

Figure displays an in situ cooling XRD spectrum of the KDP monoclinic crystal phase. Although the diffraction peaks exhibited diminished intensity during cooling (attributed to minor crystal displacement under nitrogen flow, subsequently recalibrated at −180 °C), both peak shape and position remained unchanged. This confirms the absence of phase transitions in the monoclinic phase upon cooling to −180 °C, demonstrating superior low-temperature stability compared to the tetragonal phase, which transforms into the ferroelectric orthorhombic phase with the space group Fdd2 at −152 °C. , These experimental results collectively indicate that the monoclinic phase of KDP can maintain relative stability over a wider temperature range. Such stability may expand potential applications of KDP crystals under different temperature conditions, particularly considering the previously reported efficient second harmonic generation with polarization-maintaining effect in monoclinic KDP SCFs.

Conclusions

In summary, this article reported the phase transition and structural stability of monoclinic KDP crystals obtained in solution under ambient conditions. The findings reveal that the monoclinic phase of KDP exhibits higher solubility, crystal transition, and mechanically induced transformation to the tetragonal phase, confirming that the monoclinic phase is a metastable state of KDP crystals. However, the metastable monoclinic KDP crystals obtained through spontaneous crystallization not only display a perfect one-dimensional morphology but also can maintain unprecedented long-term relative stability in the solid state, with no phase transition occurring even at elevated temperature (∼230 °C) and low temperature (∼−180 °C). The phase stability and transition relationship revealed between the monoclinic and tetragonal KDP phase enriches the information on the KDP crystal phase diagram and provides new perspectives and possibilities for studying the nucleation, crystal growth, and applications of both metastable and stable states.
