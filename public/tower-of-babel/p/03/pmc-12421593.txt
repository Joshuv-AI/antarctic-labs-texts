Allele-specific HLA LOH in solid tumors: distinct patterns by tumor type and potential prognostic relevance
Authors: Tomasz Sewastianik, Christian Roy, Michael V Gormally, Meagan Montesion, Patrick Halvey, Aastha Jindal, Hubert Lam, Adam Schoenfeld, Christopher A Klebanoff, Gregory J Opiteck, Dirk Nagorsen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractT-cell receptors (TCRs) recognize antigens derived from fragments of somatically expressed proteins that are degraded by the proteasome and presented by specific human leukocyte antigen (HLA) molecules. Recent therapeutic advances using the TCR as a tumor-targeting moiety have focused attention on HLA loss of heterozygosity (LOH) as a potential resistance mechanism. Allele-specific LOH, rather than allele-agnostic, is particularly pertinent, but rarely evaluated. Using a real-world dataset comprising 78,418 cases, we demonstrate that allele-specific LOH occurs at a relatively low frequency (<10%) across all patients with cancer. We observed a modest increase in allele-specific HLA LOH in cancers harboring associated neoantigen driver mutations (eg, KRAS or TP53 mutations), but the overall frequency remained consistently low. Furthermore, using an orthogonal dataset, we integrated clinical outcomes with HLA LOH and identified distinct impacts on overall survival in colorectal cancer (CRC) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cohorts. For instance, A*02:01-specific LOH was linked to worse survival in CRC (HR 0.5355, 95% CI 0.2991 to 0.9589, p=0.0094) but showed a trend toward improved survival in NSCLC (HR 1.249, 95% CI 0.7778 to 2.005). These findings underscore the relevance of allele-specific HLA LOH assessments and reveal nuanced differences in its clinical implications, which should be accounted for in the optimization of TCR-based immunotherapies.

Background

Immunotherapies have transformed cancer treatment, but unpredictability and limited durability of clinical response remain significant challenges to curing more solid tumors.1 Human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-restricted immunotherapies that use the exquisite sensitivity of the T-cell receptor (TCR) to home to antigens presented by the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) on the tumor cell surface have shown particular promise (including US Food and Drug Administration approval of the first-in-class adoptive TCR T-cell therapy afami-cel in 2024).24 However, it remains unclear whether alterations in epitope processing and HLA presentation pathways may affect the efficacy of these HLA-restricted approaches.5 6

The MHC class I system is comprised of three classical genes (HLA-A, HLA-B, and HLA-C) that are among the most polymorphic in the human genome. Each individual’s unique complement of these alleles is essential for presenting foreign antigens (including tumor neoantigens derived from somatic mutations) to cytotoxic T cells that then recognize and eliminate cells expressing them. Genetic loss of either of the two HLA alleles at a given locus, known as HLA loss of heterozygosity (LOH), could reduce neoantigen presentation and lead to immune escape, either during an endogenous immune response or following immunotherapy. These adaptations might impact patient outcomes.510

HLA LOH can be analyzed through two distinct approaches (online supplemental figure S1). First, allele-agnostic analysis assesses the loss of any MHC class I allele and is relevant for HLA-agnostic therapies, like immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). Allele-agnostic HLA LOH is predominantly a subclonal event, arising later in tumor evolution and shaped by neoantigen burden.9 It occurs in approximately 17% of cancers, with frequencies varying by tumor type.71012 The second approach, allele-specific analysis, focuses on the loss of particular HLA alleles. This is especially important for HLA-restricted therapies like adoptive TCR T cells and TCR T-cell engagers, which target antigens presented only by a specific HLA allele. Allele-agnostic analyses would overestimate the prevalence and relevance of HLA LOH for allele-specific treatment modalities, necessitating a more focused approach.

In addition, KRAS and TP53 mutations, prevalent in cancers with high unmet need such as colorectal cancer (CRC), non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), and pancreatic cancer (PC), modulate the tumor microenvironment and immune recognition pathways.1315 Likewise, specific HLA alleles mediate varying immune responses, and thus immune pressure, to driver mutation neoantigens.16 These interactions highlight a dynamic link between tumor signaling and immunogenicity that might affect therapeutic outcomes. Given the increasing potential of TCR-driven immunotherapies and the polymorphic nature of HLA genes,3 understanding how HLA allele-specific LOH may impact clinical outcomes, and/or interplays with driver mutations targeting KRAS and TP53, could potentially lead to improved personalized treatment strategies.

Such context-specific analyses require large, clinically-annotated datasets (eg, AACR GENIE BPC),1719 which were previously unavailable. This study analyzes allele-specific HLA LOH across a large cancer cohort, revealing distinct patterns associated with cancer type, allele heterogeneity, and mutation status.

Methods

Data sources

HLA LOH frequencies were analyzed using a dataset published by Montesion et al containing 78,418 cases germline heterozygous for one or more HLA class I locus (online supplemental table S1).7 Patient-level HLA type and LOH data were associated with ontologies and neoantigen driver mutations, and summarized for indicated combinations.

The impact of HLA LOH was assessed using subsets of AACR GENIE BPC NSCLC (V.2.0) and CRC (V.2.0) public cohorts limited to patients from the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSK) (online supplemental table S2). HLA-A typing and LOH information from the first record was exported from Bandlamudi et al (under review) and merged with AACR GENIE BPC clinical annotations using de-identified record_ids.1720

Survival analysis

Overall survival (OS) analyses from the time of diagnosis or advanced disease (subset) were performed using GraphPad Prism and the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. Hazards were compared using the log-rank method. The analysis from the time of diagnosis included early-stage and de novo advanced disease cases, while the analysis from the time of advanced disease diagnosis included cases with progression from early-stage and de novo advanced presentation. These terms refer to the starting point of survival analysis.

Statistical analysis

HLA LOH frequencies between wild-type (WT) and KRAS or TP53 mutated groups within specific ontologies were compared using two-tailed Fisher’s exact or χ2 tests based on the sample size. The difference between advanced versus diagnosis HR for common HLA-A alleles was compared using paired t-test.

Results

To characterize the patterns of allele-specific HLA LOH across cancer types, we interrogated a pan-cancer genomic dataset comprising 78,418 cases published by Montesion et al (hereafter referred to as the Foundation Medicine, Inc (FMI) dataset).7 Consistent with previous studies,7 10 allele-agnostic HLA LOH was observed in 16.6% of all samples, with notable variation across cancer indications. To explore gene-level patterns, we assessed HLA LOH among the HLA class I genes, identifying HLA-B as the most frequently lost gene, followed by HLA-C and HLA-A. This finding held both across all indications and within most tumor subtypes, including CRC, NSCLC, and PC (figure 1A and online supplemental figure S2A). Additionally, loss of any allele at a single HLA class I gene locus due to mono-allelic, bi-allelic, or tri-allelic losses was observed in ~80% of all LOH cases, with gene-specific LOH rates ranging 77%–88% (online supplemental figure S2B).

To further investigate these differences, we examined HLA class I gene germline heterogeneity within the FMI dataset, hypothesizing that greater heterogeneity—and consequently lower homozygosity rates—of HLA-B might underlie its higher LOH frequency. Notably, germline homozygous loci inherently lack heterozygosity and cannot technically be called for somatic HLA LOH. This “absence” of HLA LOH in homozygous cases is particularly relevant for HLA-restricted therapies, as at least one presenting allele remains intact, preserving potential target engagement. Additionally, these cases might be at a lower risk of developing resistance, as genetic HLA losses are typically monoallelic.7 9 Therefore, we included cases homozygous at one or two loci, which influenced LOH frequency calculations. As expected, HLA-B was the most polymorphic MHC class I gene and had the highest rate of germline heterozygosity, which may contribute to its elevated LOH frequency (figure 1B and online supplemental figure S3A).

Given the distinct peptide repertoires of each HLA allele, we investigated whether selective pressures drive the preferential loss of certain alleles. As expected, rare alleles (n<300) showed high variability in LOH frequency (online supplemental figure S3B). For common (n≥300) class I alleles, allele-specific LOH showed more consistent rates across all samples and within common cancer types and at substantially lower frequencies than allele-agnostic LOH (figure 1C, online supplemental table S3). We observed a statistically significant, though modest, negative correlation between allele-specific LOH and allele frequency across major cancer types (figure 1C).

We then focused on allele-specific LOH of the most frequent HLA-A alleles, including those presenting neoantigens from common KRAS and TP53 mutations and tumor-associated antigens from approved or investigational HLA-restricted therapies. Consistent with overall trends, loss of HLA-A*01:01, A*02:01, A*03:01, A*11:01, and A*24:02 was detected in 5.3%, 6.3%, 6.6%, 6.3%, and 6.5% of all FMI cases, respectively, compared with 16.6% for allele-agnostic loss (figure 1D and online supplemental figure S3C). While rates varied by cancer type, allele-specific LOH generally occurred at less than half the rate of allele-agnostic LOH across most indications (figure 1D and online supplemental figure S3C). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that allele-specific, as a subset of allele-agnostic, LOH occurs less frequently (~40% of allele-agnostic loss), with rates inversely correlated to locus germline homogeneity and allele frequency.

To investigate the impact of common neoantigen driver mutations, for example, KRAS (G12C, G12D, G12V) and TP53 (R175H), on allele-specific LOH, mutated cases were compared with their WT counterparts. Across all cases, A*02:01, A*03:01, and A*11:01 allele-specific LOH ranged between 5.9% and 9.7% in KRAS-mutated samples, compared with 5.9%–6.2% in KRASWT (figure 2A). However, HLA LOH rates were not statistically significantly different for any of the HLA-KRAS mutation pairs compared with KRASWT across CRC, NSCLC, and PC, except for A*11:01 in KRASG12C-positive NSCLC, where allele-specific LOH was significantly lower (5.3% for G12C vs 10.1% for KRASWT, p=0.045) (figure 2A). For TP53, both allele-agnostic and A*02:01-specific LOH were lower in WT samples than the average. The TP53R175H mutation was associated with increased A*02:01-specific LOH (4.4% for WT vs 8.1% for TP53R175H), though this trend varied by cancer type, no significant increase was observed in breast cancer and NSCLC contrary to CRC and PC (figure 2B). Overall, while a trend for increased allele-specific HLA LOH was observed with driver mutations, the frequency remained consistently low.

To evaluate the clinical relevance of HLA LOH, we analyzed OS in CRC and NSCLC, including a subset analysis of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), using an orthogonal real-world clinical dataset from AACR GENIE BPC integrated with MSK IMPACT HLA typing and LOH annotations (hereafter referred to as the GENIE/MSK dataset) (Bandlamudi et al, under review).1719 This dataset, limited to CRC and NSCLC public cohorts at submission, was selected because the FMI dataset does not include outcome annotations. Allele-agnostic HLA LOH showed no significant impact on survival probabilities from diagnosis in CRC or NSCLC (figure 3A–D), where “diagnosis” refers to the survival analysis start point. However, when evaluated from the time of advanced disease diagnosis, allele-agnostic HLA LOH remained insignificant in CRC, but was associated with significantly improved survival in NSCLC (similar trend observed in LUAD; figure 3A–D and online supplemental figure S4A–B).

We hypothesized that this shift might be partially driven by the enrichment of de novo advanced patients in the HLA LOH cohort. These patients typically receive first-line therapy for metastatic disease and have better survival outcomes than those receiving second-line therapy for recurrent disease. To investigate, we compared the proportion of de novo advanced cases in the “LOH” and “No-LOH” cohorts from diagnosis versus advanced disease survival analyses. Although the fraction of de novo advanced cases increased in the “LOH” group, the increase was higher in the “No-LOH” group (online supplemental table S4).

Interestingly, allele-specific LOH for common HLA-A alleles demonstrated even more contrasting trends between CRC and NSCLC. In CRC, allele-specific HLA LOH generally trended toward poorer survival, with A*02:01-specific LOH reaching statistical significance from diagnosis. Survival outcomes remained consistent, regardless of whether assessed from diagnosis or from advanced disease (figure 3C,E). Conversely, in NSCLC and LUAD, allele-specific LOH for common HLA-A alleles generally trended toward improved survival, particularly when analyzed from the time of advanced disease (figure 3D,F and online supplemental figure S4B–C). These findings highlight divergent patterns in the impact of HLA LOH on clinical outcomes between CRC and NSCLC. While the possibility of sampling artifacts cannot be ruled out, these trends suggest that the prognostic influence of specific HLA LOH may vary depending on cancer type, disease stage, and/or treatment context.

Discussion

Herein, we demonstrated that allele-specific HLA LOH occurs in less than 10% of cancer cases in the FMI dataset, even in the presence of neoantigen driver mutations. Given the locus’ germline heterogeneity, HLA LOH is most often detected at the HLA-B, from among all HLA class I loci. Moreover, while allele-specific HLA LOH is generally associated with a poorer prognosis in CRC, it appears to be a positive prognostic factor in NSCLC, underscoring cancer-type-specific differences.

The rate of HLA LOH, a proposed genetic immune escape mechanism, varies across studies.7912 21 Seminal work from the TRACERx Consortium, including the recent study by Puttick et al (total n=90 and n=411, respectively), identified allele-agnostic HLA LOH in 40% of NSCLC cases (58–61% in lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), 29–32% in LUAD).9 22 However, more recent large-scale studies, including ours, report lower allele-agnostic HLA LOH frequencies in NSCLC, ranging from 18% to 30%.71012 These differences could be attributed to various factors, including sample size, HLA LOH calling algorithms, and LUAD-skewed cohorts. Despite these variations, NSCLC consistently falls on the higher end of solid tumor HLA LOH frequencies. Across all samples and indications, allele-agnostic HLA LOH has a reported range of 16%–18%,7 12 suggesting it occurs in fewer than one in five patients.

TCR-based and other HLA-restricted modalities (eg, dual signaling chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cells) constitute rapidly growing therapeutic classes relying on allele-specific HLA for their mechanism of action,3 23 necessitating better understanding into absolute rates of allele-specific HLA LOH. This is particularly relevant given the HLA LOH skew—observed relative differences in allele-specific LOH frequencies associated with presented neoantigen peptides (Bandlamudi et al, under review).7 While our analysis revealed slightly higher frequencies of allele-specific LOH in neoantigen-presenting samples, the overall prevalence remained low (~40% of allele-agnostic LOH), affecting fewer than 1 in 10 patients based on the FMI dataset. While full HLA class I loss would make allele-specific LOH~50% of allele-agnostic LOH, independent allele loss (~80% per HLA gene) results in~40% allele loss. Notably, this allele-specific LOH frequency (<10%) is approximately four times lower than the often extrapolated allele-agnostic rates reported by TRACERx (~40%),9 highlighting a critical distinction for identifying potential target populations and optimizing the design of clinical studies.

Given the critical role of HLA genes in antigen presentation, LOH is typically regarded as an unfavorable alteration, leading to tumor escape and affecting clinical outcomes.5 6 However, our findings revealed more nuanced and context-dependent biology, where the impact of HLA LOH varies based on the specific allele lost (or preserved), cancer type and stage. In some instances, HLA LOH counterintuitively serves as a favorable prognostic marker.

Correspondingly, Lozac’hmeur et al demonstrated that HLA-A LOH affects survival differently in patients with metastatic NSCLC on ICIs, improving outcomes in LUAD but worsening them in LUSC.8 10 Most patients in that study were of Caucasian descent and thus HLA-A*02:01. Similarly, Zhang et al observed this trend in a Chinese cohort with prevalent HLA-A*11:01, although neither study reported allele-specific correlations.8 Moreover, given conflicting findings reported by other studies,7 variation in cohort composition, treatment context, HLA LOH detection methods, and sampling should be considered when interpreting these results.

The contrasting trends in NSCLC and CRC may reflect differences in tumor immunophenotypes and/or treatment exposures. While NSCLC and CRC both include “hot” and “cold” immune subtypes, NSCLC tends to exhibit a higher proportion of “hot” versus CRC “cold” phenotypes. In NSCLC, strong immune pressure could make HLA LOH a biomarker of an effective immune response, contributing to favorable outcomes.24 25 Conversely, in CRC, where immune pressure is lower, HLA LOH may instead reflect underlying genomic instability, associated with poorer outcomes. In line with this, biallelic deletions are more common in NSCLC versus CRC in the GENIE/MSK dataset (data not shown). However, this hypothesis does not fully explain the observed allele-specific differences in outcomes. Additionally, a larger subset of NSCLC compared to CRC, GENIE/MSK patients received ICIs, potentially influencing outcomes. However, when grouped by allele-specific LOH, small sample sizes limited analysis of treatment (or neoantigen mutations) effects on outcomes. Overall, the association between allele-specific HLA LOH and patient survival highlights its potential utility as a prognostic biomarker.

A complementary interpretation would be to consider the specific HLA allele preserved in LOH cases. The impact of HLA zygosity, where homozygosity resembles the scenario of a preserved allele in HLA LOH, and certain HLA genotypes, on outcomes of patients treated with ICIs, have been debated. Chowell et al and Naranbhai et al linked HLA homozygosity and certain HLA supertypes to poorer survival in patients treated with ICIs.26 27 Conversely, Chhibber et al found no substantial impact of HLA zygosity or supertypes on pembrolizumab-treated patient outcomes across multiple indications.28 Moreover, specific HLA alleles have been associated with distinct T-cell responses and functional phenotypes in the context of different antigens,29 30 suggesting that variability in neoantigen background and presentation may contribute to these discrepancies. Overall, these findings underscore the nuanced role of HLA in shaping therapeutic outcomes, likely influenced by allele type, treatment modality, disease stage, and tumor ontology.

Given the inherent heterogeneity of HLA class I alleles, neoantigens, treatments, and their timing, fully unraveling relevant interactions represents an opportunity for further study, necessitating even more comprehensive datasets. Future investigations are likely to explore the mechanisms behind these associations and/or integrate other HLA alterations, antigen processing, and the tumor microenvironment. This could deepen our understanding of tumor biology, advancing personalized immunotherapies.
