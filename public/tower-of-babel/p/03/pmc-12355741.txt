Viral metagenomics reveals parvovirus dark matter of herbivorous wildlife from the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau
Authors: Yue Chen, Shiyin Huang, Xiang Lu, Likai Ji, Quan Shen, Shixing Yang, Yuwei Liu, Xiaochun Wang, Ping Wu, Hongfeng Yang, Tongling Shan, Wen Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs one of the birthplaces of modern biodiversity, the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau is home to a rich variety of unique animal groups. Herbivorous wildlife constitutes a vital component of the plateau's ecosystems, yet current research on parvoviruses in these species remains limited. With the development of viral metagenomics and next-generation sequencing technology, more and more novel viruses have been detected.ResultsIn this study, we collected 741 fecal samples from herbivorous wildlife across three distinct habitats on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau and employed metagenomics analysis technology to characterize the parvovirus dark matter. We identified 32 parvoviruses by analysis, of which 13 were closely related to the members of Bocapavovirus, Dependoparvovirus and Protoparvovirus in the subfamily of Parvovirinae, 5 belonging to the Densovirinae subfamily, and 5 were classified into the newly proposed subfamily of Hamaparvovirinae. Additionally, 9 parvoviruses remain unclassified and cannot be assigned to any existing subfamily. We conducted a phylogenetic analysis based on the non-structural proteins of the viruses to explore the evolutionary relationships among parvoviruses, and found that some viruses could not be clustered with known parvoviruses.ConclusionsThese results enrich our understanding of parvovirus and viral dark matter, and reveal that these wild animals carry a large number of unknown new viruses that may have implications for the ecological balance of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04281-0.Keywords: Parvoviridae, Viral dark matter, Metagenomic, Herbivorous wildlife

Background

The Qinghai-Tibet Plateau (QTP), located in southwest China, with an average elevation of 4,000 m, is known as the"roof of the world". With an area of more than 2.5 million kilometers, it is the highest and largest plateau in the world. The region is a plateau continental climate with very complex terrain and highly variable [1], and its unique geographical location and extreme climatic conditions shape a unique and fragile ecosystem. This area is not only the source of many important rivers in Asia, but also has a rich biodiversity [2], especially the herbivorous wildlife populations. They occupy a central position in the ecosystem, and there are complex food chain relationships among different species, which provides an ideal site for studying issues such as the interactions between species and the adaptation of organisms to extreme environments. At present, the ecosystem of the QTP is facing multiple threats, including climate change [3], intensified human activities [4], and the spread of diseases. Particularly in recent years, with global warming, the melting of glaciers and the degradation of grasslands on the plateau have become increasingly severe, posing a direct threat to herbivorous wildlife that rely on specific vegetation and habitats [5]. This forces wild animals to migrate, leading to an increased overlap between their range of activities and the areas where livestock and human activities take place, which significantly raises the risk of pathogen transmission. Through in-depth research, we can understand the transmission mechanisms of viruses among different species and the co-evolutionary relationships between viruses and their hosts, thus maintaining the stability of the plateau ecosystem.

Viruses are capable of infecting cellular organisms across all domains of life and are central participants in the global biosphere [6]. It is estimated that the total number of viruses on Earth is approximately 1031 [7], and less than 1% of viral species have been identified and characterized by humans [8]. The outbreak of viruses in human populations can lead to severe consequences. It is estimated that among the 1,400 known infectious diseases affecting humans, the majority (60%) are caused by pathogens originating from animals [9]. Furthermore, most emerging infectious events in humans are zoonotic [10], with approximately 70% originating from wildlife [11]. Both wildlife and domestic animals serve as reservoirs for zoonotic pathogens, which can be transmitted to humans and, during their adaptation to new hosts, trigger significant public health crises. For instance, studies suggest that both Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus (SARS-CoV) and Middle East Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus (MERS-CoV) likely originated in bats [12]. Similarly, highly pathogenic avian influenza viruses (such as H5N1 and H7N9) have not only led to massive poultry deaths but also pose a threat to human health through cross-species transmission, resulting in severe public health and safety concerns [13].

Parvoviruses are the smallest and simplest DNA virus, nonenveloped, circular, with a linear single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) genome of 4 to 6 kb, flanked by two terminal hairpin structures [14]. Their overall genome structure is relatively conserved, primarily containing two open reading frames (ORFs). The left ORF encodes the non-structural protein (NS or Rep), while the right ORF encodes the structural protein (VP or Cap) [15]. The classification criteria for parvoviruses are primarily based on the amino acid sequence similarity of their NS1 protein. If parvoviruses share more than 85% amino acid sequence identity in their NS1 proteins and exhibit greater than 15% divergence from members of other genera, they can be classified as the same species [16]. Additionally, if parvoviruses cluster together in phylogenetic analyses based on the complete NS1 protein sequence and the SF3 helicase domain, they may be assigned to the same genus, provided their NS1 protein sequences share 35% to 40% identity with at least 80% coverage. However, these thresholds may exhibit some flexibility in practical classification [17]. Using these NS1-based criteria, the Parvoviridae family is divided into three subfamilies: Parvovirinae, which primarily infects vertebrates, and Densovirinae, which primarily infects invertebrates [18]. The newly established subfamily Hamaparvovirinae can infect both vertebrates and invertebrates [19]. Additionally, there is an unclassified genus, Metalloincertoparvovirus, which does not belong to any existing subfamily.

Parvoviruses, as widely distributed pathogens in the biological world, exhibit an extensive range of presence, encompassing nearly the entire spectrum of vertebrates and invertebrates. They exhibit remarkable resistance to environmental conditions (pH 3–9; and 60 ◦C for 1 h) [20]. Parvovirus infection may alter the health status of individual herbivorous wildlife. Among them, young individuals are particularly vulnerable to infection due to their underdeveloped immune systems, which may lead to a significant increase in the mortality rate of the young [21]. At the same time, in populations with high density or under environmental pressures (such as food shortage and climate change), parvovirus infection may exacerbate the fluctuations in population size, thus having an impact on the population dynamics of animals [22]. Simultaneously, these small yet resilient viruses exhibit remarkable host adaptability, capable of crossing species barriers to infect a diverse array of hosts, including humans [23], domestic animals [24], and wild animals [25]. At present, there have been numerous studies on parvoviruses in some domestic animals. Parvoviruses often cause severe gastroenteritis in dogs [26]and cats [27], manifesting as vomiting, diarrhea, and dehydration. However, our understanding of parvoviruses in herbivorous wildlife is rather limited. Currently, it remains unclear what specific types of parvoviruses are carried by different species of herbivorous wildlife, what their distribution ranges are, and how they spread and evolve within wild populations. This has greatly hindered our comprehensive understanding of the ecological role of parvoviruses in the wild ecosystem. It also makes it difficult for us to predict the risk of cross-species virus transmission, especially against the backdrop of increasing human activities and the growing frequency of contact between wild animals, humans, and domestic animals [28].

It is worth noting that, although herbivorous wildlife on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau play a crucial role in the ecosystem and virus dynamics, due to the complex terrain and harsh environment in the plateau area, as well as the wariness of wild animals, field sampling is full of challenges. At the same time, when collecting samples of precious and rare species, ethical issues also need to be considered, which further increases the complexity of the sampling work [29]. In addition, most of the existing studies focus on livestock that are closely related to human production and daily life, with relatively less attention paid to wild animals. Therefore, sampling of herbivorous wildlife on the QTP and virological research on them are still insufficient. Against this background, with the development of sequencing technology, an increasing number of viruses have been discovered. However, a significant portion of these viruses cannot be attributed to known viruses or, at best, exhibit only distant relationships to species already described in public sequence databases. These viruses are referred to as viral dark matter [30]. By exploring this viral dark matter, we can not only help fill the"gaps"in the evolutionary relationships of viruses, providing a more comprehensive and clear understanding of viral evolution, but also expand the known host range of viruses, deepening and broadening our knowledge of the complex interactions between viruses and their hosts [31]. In this study, we collected 741 fecal samples from herbivorous wildlife on the QTP and analyzed the composition of parvoviruses using viral metagenomics. Furthermore, we conducted phylogenetic analysis on the newly discovered parvoviruses to further investigate parvovirus dark matter, expand the existing viral database, and help predict their potential for cross-species transmission, assess the potential health risks to humans and livestock, provide a scientific basis for preventing and controlling zoonotic diseases and maintaining the ecological health of the plateau.

Methods and materials

Sample collection and processing

From September to October 2023, a total of 741 fecal samples from six different herbivorous wildlife species (bharal, goitered gazelle, tibetan gazelle, white-lipped deer, wild yak, and argalisheep) were collected across three regions of the QTP: Huaitoutala Town, Xuji Town, and Chaidanyinmaxia. Under the guidance of professionals from the Research Center for Conservation Medicine and Ecological Security, we observed the animals from a distance to avoid disrupting their normal activities. Samples were collected immediately after defecation, and the sites were thoroughly cleaned post-sampling to minimize impact on their habitats. All samples were transported to the laboratory on dry ice and stored at −80 °C. Upon removal from the freezer, the fecal samples were thawed and individually resuspended in 1 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The suspensions were then placed on a shaker for 5 min and incubated at 4 °C for 30 min before being shaken again. This process was repeated three times. The samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at low temperature to remove non-viral large particulate matter and other components. The supernatants were collected into 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes and stored at −80 °C for further use. This study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Jiangsu University and the Qinghai Institute for Endemic Disease Prevention and Control, ensuring compliance with ethical standards for sample collection and all experimental procedures.

Sample preparation and library construction

Based on the sample source and sampling site, 741 fecal samples were divided into 148 sample pools, with an average of 5 fecal samples in each sample pool. The fecal samples in the same sample pool came from the same species in the same region. See Supplementary Table 1 for details. Approximately 100 µL of supernatant was aspirated from each fecal specimen and added to the corresponding sample pool, resulting in a final volume of 500 µL per pool. The samples were first centrifuged at 12,000 × g at 4 °C for 5 min, and the liquid supernatant was collected. The supernatant was then filtered through a 0.45 µm filter (millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) and centrifuged again, yielding at least 200 µL of filtrate enriched with viral particles [32]. DNase and RNase enzymes (Turbo DNase, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA; BaselineZeroDNase, Epicentre, WI, USA; and RNase A, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were added to the filtrate, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 60 min to digest non-viral nucleic acids exposed in the filtrate [33]. Halfway through the digestion, the samples were inverted and mixed to promote nuclease activity and ensure thorough digestion. After enzymatic digestion, most of the non-viral nucleic acids in the fecal sample suspensions were eliminated. Viral nucleic acids were then extracted from the sample suspensions using the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen), following the manufacturer's instructions. Since the extracted total nucleic acids included RNA, reverse transcription was performed on the viral nucleic acids. The SuperScript III Reverse Transcription Kit was used, and a two-step RT-PCR reaction was carried out strictly according to the protocol. After completing the reverse transcription reaction, Klenow fragment polymerase (New England Biological Laboratory) was added, and the resulting product was double-stranded DNA (dsDNA). All dsDNA products can be used for library construction. The Nextera XT DNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina) was employed to build 148 libraries. Subsequently, based on the band dispersion and intensity observed in the electrophoresis gel, an appropriate amount of product was aspirated from each constructed library for purification to ensure compatibility with the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencing platform. Each library contains dual barcodes for different samples, and the sequencing results consist of 150 bp paired-end reads.

Quality control

During the experimental process, to ensure the high quality of library construction and the reliability of experimental results, all steps strictly adhered to standardized preventive measures to effectively prevent cross-contamination between samples and degradation of nucleic acids [34]. Specifically, aerosol-filter pipette tips were used in the experiments. These specially designed tips significantly reduce the generation of aerosols, thereby minimizing the risk of cross-contamination between samples. Additionally, all laboratory consumables that come into direct contact with nucleic acid samples, including microcentrifuge tubes and pipette tips, were rigorously screened and certified to be free of RNase and DNase, preventing enzymatic degradation of nucleic acids during handling. To further protect the integrity of nucleic acids, all nucleic acid samples were dissolved in diethyl pyrocarbonate (DEPC) -treated water to thoroughly inactivate any potential nuclease activity. Moreover, RNase inhibitors were added during sample processing. These comprehensive measures collectively ensured the high quality of nucleic acid samples and the accuracy of experimental data throughout the process.

Viral metagenomic analysis

After sequencing, the first step is to demultiplex the raw data from the 148 libraries using software provided by Illumina vendor. The data were processed on a 32-node Linux cluster using an internal analysis pipeline. First, we downloaded the reference genome sequences of vertebrate species from the NCBI database, including the bharal (Pseudois nayaur, GenBank no. GCA_003182575.1), white-lipped deer (Cervus albirostris, GenBank no. GCA_046563475.2), wild yak (Bos mutus, GenBank no. GCA_027580195.2), and argalisheep (Ovis aries, GenBank no. GCA_016772045.2). Subsequently, we use Bowtie2 v2.4.5 to align and remove the potential host sequences in 148 libraries [35]. Trim Galore v0.6.5 was used to remove primer sequences and low-quality reads, as well as to perform quality control on the data, with the parameters set as:–phred33–length 100–stringency 3–paired. The PRINSEQ-lite v0.20.4 was used to mark the duplicated reads generated during sequencing (parameter: -derep 1) [36]. Then, use MEGAHIT v1.2.9 to assemble the processed reads (parameter:–min-contig-len 200-t 24) [37]. The obtained contigs and singlet reads were matched against a customized viral proteome database using DIAMOND v0.9.24 for BLASTx analysis, with an E-value cutoff of < 10–5 [38]. The virus BLASTx database was constructed based on NCBI virus reference proteome (https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/refseq/release/viral/) and viral protein sequences from NCBI non-redundant (nr) fasta files (based on annotation taxonomy in the virus kingdom) [39]. The BLASTx results were imported into Megan v6.22.2 to present the annotation information of the sequences, which was then used for subsequent viral community analysis.

Viral community analysis

The compare function of Megan v6.22.2 was utilized to perform similarity analysis of the composition across the 148 libraries. Species rarefaction curves were generated within the rarefaction calculation window of Megan v6.22.2 to assess the sampling completeness of each library. Finally, the viral community composition data exported from Megan v6.22.2, combined with R v4.4.2 software, was used for statistical and visual analysis of the distribution of viral community composition within the libraries.

Viral sequence extension and annotation

Geneious Prime v2023.0.1 was used to perform de novo assemble and map to reference(s) on viral contigs with sequence lengths greater than 500 bp to obtain complete or partial parvovirus genome. The individual contig was used as reference for mapping to the raw data using the Low Sensitivity/Fastest parameter. Subsequently, the Find ORFs function in Geneious Prime v2023.0.1 was employed to predict open reading frames (ORFs) in the parvovirus sequences (with specific parameter settings: Minimum size: 400; Genetic code: Standard; Start codons: ATG). Viral contigs encoding major non-structural and structural proteins, as well as those encoding only non-structural proteins, were included in this study. The predicted ORFs were compared against the non-redundant protein database using the BLASTx function available on NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/).

Phylogenetic analysis

The NS amino acid sequences of parvoviruses obtained in this study, along with the closest matching sequences from the NCBI GenBank database based on BLASTx results and reference sequences from the Parvoviridae family, were subjected to phylogenetic analysis. The related protein sequences were aligned using the MUSCLE algorithm with default parameters in MEGA 11 software [40]. Subsequently, a Bayesian inference tree was constructed using MrBayes v3.2.7 [41]. In the MrBayes analysis, we set the substitution model as 'prset aamodelpr = mixed' for phylogenetic analysis based on amino acid sequences, which allows the program to utilize the ten built-in amino acid models. We used two concurrent runs of Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) for sampling (mcmc nchains = 2). The Markov chain was run for a maximum of 1 million generations, with sampling every 50 generations. When the standard deviation of the splitting frequency is less than 0.01, the operation is terminated, and the first 25% of the Markov chain Monte Carlo samples are discarded as burn-in. The phylogenetic tree was visualized and edited using Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) (https://itol.embl.de/) [42], Chiplot (https://www.chiplot.online/), and Adobe Illustrator 2021.

Data availability

The datasets generated during the current study are available in the National Genomics Data Center (NGDC) of China (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn), with the BioProject accession no. PRJCA037118. Please refer to Supplementary Table 1 for details. The sequences involved in this study have also been submitted to NGDC without any access restrictions, and the accession numbers are detailed in Supplementary Table 2.

Results

Overview of the virome

In this study, a total of 741 fecal samples from six species of herbivorous wildlife were collected and pooled into 148 libraries for viral metagenomic analysis. Following Illumina sequencing, reads associated with cellular organisms (archaea, bacteria, and eukaryotes) and other non-viral particles were removed, resulting in 2,772,415,048 raw reads with an average GC% of 44.77%. Among these, 44,465,235 reads were identified as virus reads.

Species rarefaction curves and species accumulation curves were used to reveal the species richness within the libraries. The species rarefaction curve indicated that the number of observed virus species in most libraries had stabilized, suggesting that the current sequencing depth was sufficient to cover all species in the samples. Therefore, further increasing the sequencing data is unlikely to significantly enhance the sample diversity (Fig. 1a). The viral species accumulation curve showed a saturation state, indicating that the sampling was adequate and reasonable. Additionally, it suggested that more than 600 different viral species might be distributed across the 148 libraries (Fig. 1b).

Viral species richness in the 148 libraries. a Species rarefaction curve generated using Megan v6.22.2, with the raw data subjected to log10 transformation. b Species accumulation curve generated using R v4.4.2, where each boxplot corresponds to the richness values of the samples, and the light blue area represents the 95% confidence interval

After removing bacteriophage-related reads, the proportions of DNA virus reads (7,289,677) and RNA virus reads (2,804,367) were 52.40% and 20.16%, respectively, while unclassified virus reads (3,816,593) accounted for 27.44% (Fig. 2a). A heatmap was constructed to display the viral family composition across the libraries, based on the viral family level, nucleic acid type, and sample species derived from the viral genomic sequences of the 148 libraries. The log-transformed results revealed that the viruses were primarily assigned to 35 virus families, including 17 DNA virus families (comprising 12 dsDNA virus families and 5 ssDNA virus families) and 18 RNA virus families (comprising 2 dsRNA virus families and 16 ssRNA virus families) (Fig. 2b). The relative abundance stacked bar chart indicated that a small portion of the gut virome composition was shared among the six herbivorous wildlife species, but significant differences were also observed (Fig. 2c). Combining the heatmap and bar chart, the Smacoviridae family was the most abundant in bharal, goitered gazelle, tibetan gazelle, and white-lipped deer, while Tombusviridae was the dominant family in wild yak, and Parvoviridae was the most prevalent in argalisheep. A total of 352,362 reads related to the Parvoviridae family were identified across the libraries. Parvoviruses were detected in the feces of five species (bharal, argalisheep, etc.), but not in white-lipped deer. Among these, the highest number of parvovirus reads was found in the argalisheep library, followed by the bharal library (Fig. 2d).

We selected reads from the Parvoviridae family for further analysis and obtained a total of 32 parvovirus contigs. Among these, 13 contigs were closely associated with infections in vertebrates and belonged to the Parvovirinae subfamily. Additionally, we identified 5 contigs belonging to the Densovirinae subfamily (1 Muscodensovirus contig, 3 Tetuambidensovirus contigs, and 1 unclassified Densovirinae contig), which primarily infect invertebrates. 5 contigs were classified under the Chaphamaparvovirus genus within the newly proposed Hamaparvovirinae subfamily. The remaining 9 contigs were classified as unclassified Parvoviridae and could not be assigned to any existing subfamily.

Identification of novel viruses of the subfamily Densovirinae

Densoviruses primarily infect invertebrates, including crustaceans and at least five orders of insects [43]. These viruses enter host cells by recognizing specific receptors on the cell surface, replicate within the host cell nucleus, and form large rectangular or circular inclusion bodies, which may ultimately lead to host cell lysis, tissue damage, or even death [44]. With the use of high-throughput sequencing, more and more densoviruses have been discovered, and their diversity is far greater than expected [45]. These new findings also highlight that our understanding of the biological characteristics and evolutionary history of densovirus is still very limited [15].

In this study, we identified a total of 5 viral contigs, with lengths ranging from 2,696 bp to 4,535 bp. Among these, four contigs contained two major ORFs, while the Bha12par01 only had an open reading frame for the non-structural protein (NS1) (Fig. 3a). To clarify the genus of these novel viruses, a phylogenetic tree was constructed based on the NS1 amino acid sequences of these viruses. These densoviruses clustered into three branches: Muscodensovirus (n = 1), Tetuambidensovirus (n = 3), and unclassified Densovirinae (n = 1) (Fig. 3b). BLASTx comparison with the NCBI database revealed that Bha12par01 showed the highest homology with the haematobia irritans densovirus (GenBank no. YP_010798259), sharing 45.35% amino acid identity and 95% coverage. Goi105par01 exhibits 45.09% identity with a bat parvovirus (GenBank no. XBS25922). The NS1 amino acid similarity of the remaining three viruses to their closest related viruses was less than 53%, all below the 85% threshold for species classification, indicating that these viruses are likely novel species.

Identification of novel viruses of the subfamily Hamaparvovirinae

Chapparvoviruses have been detected in the blood and feces of various vertebrates [46–48], as well as in some invertebrates [49]. Chapparvoviruses form a distinct lineage that cannot be classified within the existing two subfamilies, suggesting they belong to a new parvovirus subfamily. In 2019, the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) reclassified these viruses and placed them under the newly proposed Chaphamaparvovirus genus within the Hamaparvovirinae subfamily [17].

The 5 novel hamaparvoviruses identified in this study all possess both non-structural and structural proteins (Fig. 4a). A phylogenetic tree constructed based on the NS1 amino acid sequences clearly shows that the 5 newly identified viruses from the bharal libraries are classified within the Chaphamaparvovirus genus, forming a distinct cluster with a sheep parvovirus from California (GenBank no. QXO96491) (Fig. 4b). Additionally, BLASTx comparisons revealed that these 5 hamaparvoviruses share less than 60% amino acid sequence identity with their closest parvovirus relatives, indicating that they represent novel species.

Identification of novel viruses of the subfamily Parvovirinae

The Parvovirinae subfamily is a significant group within the Parvoviridae family, primarily infecting vertebrates, including mammals, birds, and reptiles. The 13 parvoviruses obtained in this study belong to the genera Bocaparvovirus (n = 4), Dependoparvovirus (n = 5) and Protoparvovirus (n = 4) of this subfamily. Among these, Bocaparvovirus is closely associated with respiratory and gastrointestinal diseases in mammals and has been identified in a wide range of hosts, including human [50], porcine [51], and canine [52]. The representative virus of Dependoparvovirus is adeno-associated virus (AAV), which relies on helper viruses (usually adenoviruses or herpesviruses) to complete its replication cycle [53]. Protoparvovirus includes viruses that infect various animals and humans, causing a wide range of pathological responses. Notable pathogens in this genus include canine parvovirus (CPV) and feline panleukopenia virus (FPV) [54].

A total of 13 viral contigs were identified in this study, and after alignment analysis, several viruses were detected as highly similar sequences in other libraries. For example, the Arg148par01 from the Argalisheep148 library was found to have similar sequences in 11 bharal libraries (with sequence similarity reaching 96.5%), indicating a high prevalence of these viruses. A phylogenetic tree based on the NS1 protein showed that the parvovirus we found clustered with three genera of the subfamily Parvovirinae (Fig. 5). In Bocaparvovirus, Arg147par01 forms a branch with 65.93% similarity to bovine parvovirus (GenBank no. YP_009259490); the Arg148par01 shares 72.96% identity to the NS1 amino acid sequence of deer-associated parvovirus (GenBank no. QTZ83139), suggesting they are novel species. Bha39par02 showed 94.55% similarity to a goat parvovirus collected in China (GenBank no. XCW88847), indicating that these two viruses may share a common ancestor or have a close evolutionary relationship. In the Dependoparvovirus genus, the four viral contigs Bha22par01, Bha39par01, Bha73par02, and Bha73par04 clustered together to form a distinct branch, with sequence identity below 55%, suggesting they may represent novel species. Bha33par02 showed 88.63% amino acid similarity to a parvovirus from the marmot (GenBank no. WRQ19904), suggesting a close phylogenetic relatedness between the two viruses. Within the Protoparvovirus genus, the Bha74par01 has the highest amino acid sequence identity with sheep parvovirus (GenBank WVW89651) at 66.88%; the Bha10par01 and its closest virus share similarity by 83.71%, suggesting these viruses may also be novel species. Bha23par01 and Bha23par02 share more than 96% NS1 amino acid sequence similarity with pigeon parvoviruses collected in Thailand, indicating the potential for cross-regional and cross-species transmission of this parvovirus, which requires further experiments to verify the hypothesis.

Identification of novel viruses among unclassified Parvoviridae

In addition to the identified members of the parvovirus subfamilies, we also discovered several viruses that could not be classified into any specific genus within the known subfamilies, thus belonging to unclassified Parvoviridae. A phylogenetic tree constructed based on the NS1 amino acid sequences of these viruses revealed that the 9 viral contigs formed relatively distinct branches separate from the Parvovirinae, Densovirinae, and Hamaparvovirinae subfamilies (Fig. 6). Among these, five viruses showed NS1 amino acid sequence homology ranging from 47.35% to 79.01% with their closest parvovirus relatives, suggesting they may represent novel species. Bha9par01 exhibited the highest homology (56.96%) with a bat parvovirus (GenBank no. DBA50301), while Bha33par03 showed 57.28% homology with a bird parvovirus (GenBank no. QKE54882). The BLASTx results for the remaining four viruses indicated that each shared greater than 98% amino acid sequence similarity with their closest relatives. Notably, Bha73par01 from the Bharal73 library showed 99.05% identity with a pigeon parvovirus (GenBank no. YP_010797210) collected in Hong Kong, suggesting they may share a common ancestor.

Discussion

Currently, our understanding of the diversity of virus remains extremely limited, and the risk of emerging infectious diseases caused by potentially unknown novel viruses is constantly increasing. Experts have pointed out that, for a considerable period in the future, the pathogens responsible for emerging infectious diseases in humans will primarily originate from unknown novel viruses carried by animals [55]. It is estimated that mammals alone may harbor nearly a million undiscovered viral dark matter species [56]. In recent years, deep sequencing of animal DNA samples has demonstrated the widespread distribution of parvoviruses in nature, yet the majority of newly discovered viruses have not been associated with any diseases. However, some variants of parvoviruses have altered their host ranges, giving rise to new epidemic or pandemic viruses [57]. Therefore, continuous and extensive research on parvovirus dark matter is essential to enhance our understanding of viruses and better prepare for the emergence and spread of new infectious diseases. In this study, we investigated the parvovirus dark matter of six herbivorous wildlife species on the QTP. The viral dark matter has significantly expanded our knowledge of parvovirus diversity.

In this study, a total of 32 parvovirus contigs were obtained through assembly. These viruses have a similar genomic structure and most of them contain two open reading frames (ORFs). One major ORF on the left side encodes a non-structural protein. This protein is cytotoxic and is crucial for the efficient replication of the virus and the generation of infectious virions [58]. Another major ORF encodes structural protein that assemble to form the capsid of the virus [59]. Among these, 5 parvoviruses belong to the Densovirinae subfamily, but their real host origins remain unclear at present. While viruses in this subfamily are generally thought to primarily infect invertebrates, this is not absolute. These newly discovered viruses may infect wildlife or could be temporarily present in the animal's gut through dietary intake without causing infection. A study has shown that differences in the microbial community composition of ruminants are largely attributed to diet [60]. However, since the samples in this study were collected from wild animals, their dietary habits cannot be inferred. Through phylogenetic analysis combined with the results of BLASTx, the similarity between the NS1 proteins of the newly identified densoviruses and hamaparvoviruses and those of their closest viruses is lower than the taxonomic criterion of 85%. It is hypothesized that they form a new species.

13 viruses belong to three genera within the Parvovirinae subfamily: Bocaparvovirus, Dependoparvovirus, and Protoparvovirus. The impacts of these viruses on hosts vary greatly. They usually cause high morbidity and mortality rates in young individuals. Since the cells of juvenile and young animals proliferate at a relatively fast rate, especially in the intestines, bone marrow, and lymphoid tissues, the viruses can replicate rapidly and damage these tissues, leading to severe disease injuries [61]. In adult individuals, however, the infections may present as subclinical or non—pathogenic [57]. Therefore, we speculate that some herbivorous wildlife may serve as long-term carriers of the virus, continuously excreting the virus during the state of asymptomatic infection, thus facilitating the transmission of the virus within a population or among different populations. Moreover, during their foraging or migratory journeys, they may promote the long-distance spread of parvoviruses, thereby influencing the epidemic patterns of the viruses in different geographical regions. Besides, comparative analysis revealed the presence of highly similar parvoviruses in libraries from different wildlife species, as well as parvoviruses with over 98% homology to known viruses. This may indicate cross-species transmission within the Parvovirinae subfamily. Further in-depth studies are needed to evaluate their potential to spill over to other species.

Although this study provides new insights into the dark matter of parvoviruses in herbivorous wildlife, the following limitations should still be acknowledged. Firstly, sample mixing may introduce biases, which can affect the tracing of the viral origin and the judgment of cross-species transmission. Secondly, due to the unique challenges of the high-altitude environment and sampling difficulties, the number of animal species and samples included in this study is limited, which may not fully represent the diversity of parvovirus dark matter in herbivorous wildlife on the QTP. The study relied solely on fecal samples, which may restrict the comprehensive discovery of viral dark matter. The parvoviruses of the subfamily Densovirinae detected in the feces of herbivores may originate from ingested plants or insects. However, the biological significance of these viruses to mammalian hosts remains unclear. Moreover, due to the lack of functional assay experiments linking viruses to hosts, our understanding of the mechanisms of virus-host interactions is still insufficient. Therefore, future research should investigate parvovirus dark matter in a broader range of herbivorous wildlife, comparing their host adaptability and transmission potential. Additionally, incorporating more sample types (such as blood, tissue, and environmental samples) will help comprehensively reveal the distribution and diversity of parvovirus dark matter. In addition, research on the functional genes of potential novel viruses and their interactions with hosts should be strengthened to deeply explore their potential impacts on host health and population dynamics.

Conclusions

In summary, in this preliminary study of parvovirus dark matter in the feces of herbivorous wildlife on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, we identified a total of 32 parvoviruses, most of which represent potential parvovirus dark matter. This reveals the diversity of parvoviruses and through genetic evolutionary analysis, it is preliminarily believed that there is a possibility of cross-regional and cross-species transmission of these viruses. Therefore, enhancing the surveillance and research of wildlife viruses not only helps uncover viral diversity and evolutionary mechanisms but also provides a scientific basis and early warning for preventing and controlling future infectious disease outbreaks.
