Controlling Fecal Incontinence With a Novel Anal Device
Authors: Sadé L. Assmann, Bjorn Winkens, Andrea Bours, Brigitte Essers, Tze Lam, Zlatan Mujagić, Stéphanie O. Breukink, Daniel Keszthelyi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionCan a novel anal insert achieve a clinically meaningful reduction in fecal incontinence (FI) severity compared to care as usual?FindingsIn this randomized clinical trial that included 73 adults, a significant reduction in FI severity occurred in 25.7% of insert participants and 18.9% of control participants, with no significant difference between groups.MeaningThis randomized clinical trial found that the novel anal insert was not beneficial on group level.

Introduction

Fecal incontinence (FI), characterized by the recurrent, uncontrolled passage of fecal material, can have detrimental impact on well-being.1,2 FI is estimated to affect approximately 8% of community-dwelling adults.3

Management of FI can be challenging due to its wide variation in causes and symptom presentation. It is recommended that patients start with conservative treatment options, progressing to more invasive (surgical) approaches if symptom reduction is insufficient.4 However, surgical approaches are associated with higher risks, costs, adverse events, and variable success rates.4 Therefore, minimally invasive solutions are particularly desirable. These include anal inserts, designed to temporarily block stool leakage. Observational studies assessing the effectiveness of anal devices have demonstrated that they are often poorly tolerated but provide symptom relief for some.5,6,7,8,9,10,11 However, to our knowledge, no randomized clinical trials (RCTs) have assessed the effectiveness of an anal device.4 Recently, a novel device has been developed to improve patient comfort and retention (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1). The device is a single-use, prelubricated, silicone, liquid-filled rectal insert with a plastic applicator, aimed at improved comfort.

This study sought to evaluate the clinical efficacy of the novel device in an RCT. The primary objective was to assess whether the device leads to a clinically meaningful reduction in FI severity during an 8-week treatment period. We hypothesized that treatment with the novel device would have superior efficacy compared with usual care. Secondary objectives included quality of life, anxiety, depression, adherence to therapy, adverse events, and FI episodes.

Method

This RCT was approved by the Maastricht University Medical Center+ ethics committee. All participants provided written informed consent. The trial protocol and statistical analysis plan are provided in Supplement 2. This study is reported following the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guideline.

Study Design, Participants, and Recruitment

We conducted a multicenter, open-label, superiority RCT with an interim sample size recalculation to compare the effects of anal inserts against usual care for patients with FI. Participants were recruited from the outpatient clinics of 2 hospitals in the Netherlands: an academic hospital offering both secondary and tertiary care (Maastricht University Medical Center+, Maastricht) and a secondary care hospital (Rijnstate Hospital, Arnhem). Additional participants were recruited via a medical device and care supplier (Medireva) and newspaper advertisements, enabling nationwide participation. Individuals aged 16 to 90 years with FI, as defined by the Rome IV criteria, were included. Other inclusion and exclusion criteria are described in the eMethods in Supplement 1.

After telephone prescreening and clinical assessment, participants completed a 14-day diary to confirm the occurrence of at least 1 FI episode within the 2-week timeframe (eFigure 2 in Supplement 1). Participants were then randomized 1:1 into either the intervention or control group. The intervention group had a 1-week accommodation period to test 2 different sizes of the insert (regular and large) and select their preferred size before starting with the 8-week treatment period, while the control group began with the treatment period immediately. Participants in both groups were offered a home visit by a continence nurse for explanations regarding continence products. The treatment phase lasted 8 weeks, followed by an end visit, either in person or via telephone, depending on patient preferences, and a 4-week follow-up in which participants were allowed to start or continue using any treatments for FI again (eFigure 3 in Supplement 1).

Randomization and Blinding

Randomization was conducted by the coordinating investigator in a secure web-based randomization system (ALEA; ALEA Clinical), applying the minimization method with stratification by inclusion center, age group (16-45, 45-65, and 65-90 years), and sex (male or female [self-reported]) to ensure balanced allocation across treatment groups. The allocation sequence was generated by the ALEA software, in which simple randomization is used when there is no difference between groups when the new participants are entered into the trial. As the study was open-label due to the nature of the intervention, investigators were aware of treatment allocation after assignment.

Treatments

Participants in both the intervention and control groups were instructed to continue using their usual treatments for FI and to refrain from starting new treatments during the study period. Participants in the intervention group were advised to use the inserts at times when they anticipated a risk of fecal leakage, such as when outside their home, or continuously throughout the day, at their own discretion. It was expected that participants would use 1 insert per day on average. Participants in the control group continued their current FI treatments.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome was change in the St Mark’s incontinence score, (range, 0-24), comparing the mean score in the 2-week baseline to the mean score during the 8-week treatment period.12 A reduction of at least 3 points was considered clinically meaningful.13 Secondary outcomes were FI-specific quality of life (FIQL), anxiety (General Anxiety Disorder-7), and depression (Patient Health Questionnaire-9). Adherence in the anal insert group was defined as using at least 1 anal insert every 2 weeks and in both groups by not initiating any new treatment for FI. Adverse events were recorded at each visit, during phone calls, and additionally via daily diary entries for the participants in the anal insert group. Additional data collection methods are described in the eMethods in Supplement 1. eFigure 3 in Supplement 1 provides the study flow and data collection timeline.

Statistical Analysis

The sample size calculation was based on detecting a clinically meaningful difference of at least 3 points in the St Mark’s incontinence score (SD, 4.2) between groups.13 Using α = .05, β = 0.8, and power of 80%, it was determined that a total of 62 participants were required in the intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis. To account for an estimated 15% dropout rate, the total target sample size was adjusted to 73 participants. Given that this trial used an adaptive design, an interim analysis was performed by a statistician (B.W.) after 50% of the intended sample had been randomized (approximately 18 participants per group) solely to determine whether the dropout rate was no greater than 15% and to adjust the sample size if not.

All analyses followed the ITT principle. The primary outcome was assessed using a χ2 test and relative risks (RRs) with corresponding 95% CIs.

A linear mixed-effects model was used to evaluate differences in disease-specific quality of life, anxiety and depression. The model included group (intervention or control), time, and their interaction and center, age, and sex as fixed factors.

A per-protocol (PP) analysis using the same method was also conducted excluding participants who withdrew from the study, began using a new FI treatment, or, for those in the anal insert group, used fewer than 1 anal insert every 2 weeks. The 2-sided significance level was set at α = .05 for all analyses. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM). Data were analyzed from August 2 to December 20, 2024.

A post hoc analysis of difference in number of FI episodes per week between groups was conducted using a linear mixed-effects model, based on the recent consensus on core outcomes in FI that was not available at the inception of the study.14 Additionally, a post hoc analysis of the St Mark’s score was conducted to assess whether there was a significant difference in the number of participants with ≥3 point reduction between those who were satisfied with the insert and intended to continue using it vs those who were dissatisfied and planned to discontinue its use.

Results

Between May 2021 and March 2024, 167 patients received study information, 73 attended screening and 72 (60 [83.3%] female; mean [SD] age, 67.3 [9.8] years]) were randomized, including 35 participants in the insert group and 37 participants in the control group (Table 1). Twelve participants withdrew during the trial, predominantly in the insert group (8 participants), due to device-related problems (falling out, leakage, or discomfort); in the control group, 3 participants dropped out due to dissatisfaction with allocation and study burden (eFigure 4 in Supplement 1). Three participants, all in the intervention group, made use of the services offered by the continence nurse after insert failure during the run-in phase. All 3 participants later discontinued participation due to continued failure. The only other additional contact time the intervention group had with trial staff was a brief phone call of a few minutes to determine preferred insert size.

Two participants in the intervention group completed questionnaires during the treatment period but discontinued anal insert use. Consequently, they were excluded from PP analyses, leaving 72 participants for ITT analyses (35 intervention and 37 control) and 59 participants for PP analysis (25 intervention and 34 control). Mean (SE) adherence to treatment was 91.4% (4.1%) in the intervention group and 100% (0%) in the control group (eTable 1 in Supplement 1).

On the days the daily diary was completed, participants used the insert on a mean (SD) of 69.0% (31.0%) of days. The mean (SD) number of inserts used per day per person was 1.01 (0.61). All participants refrained from making changes to their treatments during the trial.

A total of 9 participants (25.7%) in the insert group and 7 participants (18.9%) in the control group experienced a reduction of at least 3 points in St Mark’s score (RR, 1.36 [95% CI, 0.57-3.25]; P = .49), indicating no significant between-group difference (Table 2 and eFigure 5 in Supplement 1). In the ITT analysis, insert use was associated with greater improvement in FIQL coping (estimated mean difference [EMD], 0.19 [95% CI, 0.03 to 0.35]; P = .02) and depression (EMD, 0.15 [95% CI, 0.01 to 0.30]; P = .04), while lifestyle and embarrassment did not differ significantly (eFigures 6-9 in Supplement 1). No significant differences in FIQL subdomains were found in the PP analysis (Table 3). GAD-7 and PHQ-9 scores did not differ significantly between groups during treatment or follow-up (eFigure 10 and eFigure 11 in Supplement 1).

Post hoc exploratory analysis found that the participants in the anal insert group had significantly fewer weekly FI episodes during the treatment period compared with the control group after correction for FI episodes at baseline and stratification variables, with an EMD of −3.09 (95% CI, −4.39 to −1.75) episodes (P < .001). A similar result was seen in the follow-up period, with an EMD of −2.97 (95% CI, −4.91 to −1.03) FI episodes (P = .003) (Table 4 and Figure). Furthermore, there were significantly more people with a more than 50% reduction in FI episodes in the intervention group vs control group for both the ITT (15 participants [55.6%] vs 4 participants [11.4%]; P < .001) and PP (14 participants [56.0%] vs 4 participants [11.8%]; P < .001) analyses (Table 2).

Post hoc analysis showed that satisfaction with the insert and intention to continue use were not associated with statistically significant differences in St Mark’s score change (Table 2 and Table 4). No serious adverse events occurred. In the insert group, 20 participants (57.1%) reported at least 1 device-related event, most commonly device expulsion, discomfort, and urgency; other events were infrequent (eTable 2 in the Supplement). Remarkably, of the participants in the intervention group who wanted to continue using the insert, most had experienced 1 or more adverse events (20 of 28 participants [71.4%]).

Discussion

In this RCT comparing a novel anal insert with usual care for FI, we did not demonstrate superiority of the anal insert on the prespecified primary outcome of a reduction of at least 3 points on the St Mark’s incontinence score. As the study was powered for this end point, the absence of a significant difference indicates that overall improvement in FI severity, as captured by the St Mark’s score, was not shown. Therefore, the secondary and post hoc findings, even when positive, should be considered exploratory and hypothesis generating rather than confirmatory.

Analysis of secondary outcomes found that quality of life improvements in the coping and depression subdomains of the FIQL were significantly higher in the intervention group, suggesting that the insert can positively influence aspects of mental health and patients’ ability to manage their condition. However, anxiety and depression assessments showed no significant differences between groups. As for the post hoc secondary outcomes, the anal insert was associated with a significant reduction in the mean number of total FI episodes per week compared with the control group.14 In addition, significantly more participants experienced a more than 50% reduction in FI episodes per week in the treatment group compared with the control group.

Adverse events related to the insert, although mild in nature, were frequently reported, particularly regarding falling out of the insert, discomfort, and feelings of urgency while the insert was in place. A large proportion of participants (57.1%), including those who found the insert beneficial, reported these issues, highlighting how tolerability challenges can compromise the intervention’s potential benefits. Although most adverse events generally did not prevent continued use, discomfort and difficulties with insertion were common reasons for nonadherence to the treatment protocol, as reflected in the withdrawal rates within the intervention group. These findings are consistent with previous studies, which identified retention and comfort issues as significant barriers to the successful use of anal inserts for FI management. Observational studies have reported dropout rates ranging from 7% to 73% for other anal devices.4,5,6,7,8,9,10 Further refinements of the device to improve comfort and retention could enhance patient experiences and adherence. Interestingly, of the participants in the intervention group who reported wanting to continue using the insert, 71.4% had reported experiencing 1 or more adverse effects, demonstrating that these adverse effects were not a barrier to continue using the insert for many participants.

The discrepancy between the lack of significant difference in the primary outcome and the significant reduction in the exploratory post hoc weekly FI episodes outcome provides important and clinically meaningful insights. One potential explanation relates to the composite structure of the St Mark’s score. Although widely validated, several of its components, such as type of incontinence and use of constipating medication, may be less responsive to short-term mechanical containment. An anal insert would primarily be expected to reduce leakage episodes rather than modify established coping behaviors or medication use, which may have attenuated measurable change within the aggregate severity score. Furthermore, although it might reflect a limitation of the anal insert, it could also be explained by marked interindividual variability in treatment response. Our results also draw attention to the ongoing challenge of relying on outcome measures that are neither perfect nor universally applied in FI research.14 Although participants who wished to continue using the insert after the trial were expected to demonstrate clearer benefit, differences in FI severity between continuers and discontinuers did not reach statistical significance. This is likely due to a lack of statistical power, as the study was not designed with this comparison in mind. Similar patterns have been reported in other high-quality FI trials, including those examining percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation, where significant reductions in FI episodes have been observed despite no significant changes in FI severity scores.15 The recurrence of this finding across interventions underscores a wider limitation in current FI trial methods, in which commonly used outcome measures prioritize easily quantifiable leakage episodes while failing to capture the broader context of FI-related health impairments.

By highlighting these issues so clearly, our study adds weight to the argument for implementing a core outcome set that standardizes and refines outcome assessment in FI research. Such an approach would improve comparability across trials and allow the true therapeutic value of FI treatments to be more accurately determined.14

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The open-label design, while necessary given the nature of the intervention, may have introduced information bias for the patients, especially due to the subjective nature of outcomes assessed. Also, as the study was not blinded, a placebo response related to device use cannot be excluded, particularly as placebo response rates in FI trials have been reported to be substantial.16 The pragmatic design of this trial, while enhancing generalizability, resulted in limited phenotypic characterization; FI subtype, underlying etiology, sphincter integrity, and anorectal physiology were not systematically assessed. Given the heterogeneity of FI, this restricts meaningful comparison with prior mechanistic studies and limits identification of subgroups most likely to benefit. In addition, participants in the intervention group underwent a 1-week accommodation phase with device testing and device-specific diary recording, whereas controls proceeded directly to treatment. This differential exposure, together with the absence of a sham control and the open-label nature of the study, may have influenced results. Consequently, the observed improvements in secondary end points, including reduction in FI episodes and improvements in coping and depression score, should be interpreted with caution, as they may partly reflect effects related to patient expectations or increased clinical attention. We excluded patients with prior anorectal surgery to avoid confounding; however, since surgeries can cause FI, this may further limit the generalizability of our findings. The high rate of adverse events related to discomfort and retention underscores a significant challenge in the practical use of the anal insert. These issues may have influenced participants’ perceptions of the treatment’s effectiveness and introduced bias, potentially leading to poorer scores for other outcomes due to dissatisfaction from discomfort or other adverse events. While self-reported diaries were practical, they may have introduced recall or reporting bias, particularly in participants with discomfort. However, the 24-hour recall period likely minimized this risk.

Conclusions

Although this RCT did not demonstrate superiority on the primary outcome, the novel anal insert nevertheless showed encouraging signals of benefit in secondary and post hoc analyses. Improvements in selected quality-of-life domains, including coping and depression, together with a reduction in weekly FI episodes (a post hoc outcome), suggest that the device may meaningfully improve the lived experience of FI for some patients. Given its noninvasive nature, the generally benign profile of adverse events, and the positive response observed in a proportion of participants support the idea that individualized trials of therapy may be appropriate in practice. For selected patients, the insert could represent a meaningful addition to current management options, particularly given the scarcity of effective, low-burden interventions for FI. Future research should focus on optimizing tolerability and retention, clarifying which patient characteristics predict benefit, and evaluating the device in larger, adequately powered studies that allow subgroup analyses. With further development, anal inserts have the potential to fill an important therapeutic gap between conservative measures and more invasive, costly, and less accessible surgical interventions, thereby expanding the spectrum of options available for managing FI.
