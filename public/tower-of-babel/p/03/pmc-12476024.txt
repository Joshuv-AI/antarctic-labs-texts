A Comprehensive Review of Germination Impact on Moringa Seeds and Sprouts: Physiological and Biochemical Changes, Bioactive Compounds, Health Benefits, and Food Applications
Authors: Yi Liu, Yi Kai, Dejian Huang, Shao Quan Liu, Yuyun Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGermination is emerging as a promising bioprocess to produce edible moringa sprouts with enhanced nutritional value and health benefits. Germinated moringa seeds could be marketed as a novel food ingredient for functional food formulations. Attempts to understand the bioactive compounds and their associated health benefits of moringa seeds and sprouts would be conducive to developing functional foods and nutraceuticals. This review summarizes germination and its impact on the bioactive compounds in moringa seeds and sprouts and discusses their bioactivities, with the mechanisms of action in cell and animal models being elucidated. Some applications of moringa seeds and sprouts in functional food formulations are also provided. Germination accumulates a range of bioactive compounds, particularly phenolic compounds, glucosinolates (GSLs), and their corresponding isothiocyanates (ITCs) in moringa seeds and sprouts. Of these, glucomoringin is the most abundant GSL. The health‐promoting properties of moringa seeds and sprouts may be prominently associated with the glycosylated ITC, moringin, which is produced by the hydrolysis of glucomoringin. The reported bioactivities of moringa seeds and their extracts include antioxidation, anti‐inflammation, antidiabetes, anti‐carcinogenesis, and hepatoprotection. Multiple cell and animal studies have demonstrated the protective effects of moringa seeds and their extracts by activating antioxidant gene expression, thereby mitigating oxidative stress. Additionally, their ability to downregulate pro‐inflammatory cytokines helps alleviate inflammation‐associated chronic diseases such as diabetes and cancer. In conclusion, germination is an effective approach to enriching bioactive compounds in moringa seeds, which could be consumed as functional foods or serve as a novel bioingredient for the development of functional foods to prevent and/or alleviate chronic diseases.

Introduction

Moringa (Moringa oleifera L.) is a deciduous, perennial tree that is mainly distributed in the tropical and subtropical regions (Dhakad et al. 2019; Gopalakrishnan et al. 2016). It has been extensively used in traditional phytomedicine and provides essential nutrients against malnutrition, because almost all the plant tissues, like the leaves, flowers, barks, seeds, and roots, are edible and exhibit pharmacological properties (Arora and Arora 2021; Saa et al. 2019; Yang et al. 2023). For example, moringa leaves, as the primary edible part, have been extensively exploited as a novel food material in recent years, and their enriched nutrients, bioactive compounds, functional properties, and potential industrial applications have been well documented (Arora and Arora 2021; Dhakad et al. 2019; Hassan et al. 2021; Mehwish et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2022; Yang et al. 2023). However, studies on moringa seeds have primarily focused on oil extraction and water purification (Saa et al. 2019), whereas research on their nutritional composition and phytochemical components remains limited.

Moringa seeds are a good source of essential macronutrients, including proteins, lipids, and carbohydrates (Fejér et al. 2019; Gopalakrishnan et al. 2016; Saa et al. 2019). Notably, their proteins possess high levels of sulfur‐containing amino acids, such as methionine and cysteine, with concentrations comparable to those found in milk and egg proteins (Oliveira et al. 1999). Given that legumes are typically low in sulfur‐containing amino acids, moringa seeds can serve as a nutritional complement, helping to form a more balanced plant‐based diet that addresses this deficiency. In addition, moringa seeds are rich in unsaturated fatty acids (FAs), with oleic acid alone accounting for approximately 74% of the total FA content (Bhutada et al. 2016). They also contain relatively high levels of essential micronutrients, including vitamins A and E, calcium, phosphorus, and iron (Fejér et al. 2019; Mbah et al. 2012; Ijarotimi et al. 2013). Therefore, incorporating moringa seeds into the diet may enhance the nutritional status of pregnant women and nursing mothers, while helping to address malnutrition and iron deficiency in children (Arora and Arora 2021). Apart from their high nutritional value, moringa seeds and their extracts have been shown to be rich in phenolic compounds, glucosinolates (GSLs), and their corresponding isothiocyanates (ITCs) (Dzuvor et al. 2022; Lu et al. 2021, 2020; Singh et al. 2013). These bioactive compounds confer a range of health benefits, including antidiabetic, anticancer, and hepatoprotective effects (Al‐Malki and El Rabey 2015; Hamza 2010; Zunica et al. 2021). Among them, moringin, a major ITC produced through myrosinase‐catalyzed hydrolysis of its GSL precursor, glucomoringin, which is predominantly found in moringa seeds, is considered a key contributor to these therapeutic properties. Therefore, it is of significant interest to enhance glucomoringin and moringin levels in moringa seeds to improve their functional value.

Germination is a physiological process that commences with the absorption of water by dormant seeds and ends with the emergence of the embryonic axis, typically, the radicle through the surrounding tissues, along with complex physiological and biochemical changes, including respiration, protein biosynthesis, subcellular structural changes, cell elongation, and mobilization of storage reserves, among others (Bewley et al. 2012; Liu et al. 2022; Núñez‐Gastélum et al. 2023; Rifna et al. 2019). Numerous studies have shown that germination could improve the nutritional value and boost the bioactivities of moringa seeds (Coello et al. 2020; de la Mora‐López et al. 2018; Ijarotimi et al. 2013; Liu, Zhang, et al. 2024). It transforms the major macronutrients into smaller molecules (e.g., simple sugars, free amino acids, and organic acids), which are more easily absorbed by human body, while reducing antinutrients, such as phytate, saponins, and tannins (Ijarotimi et al. 2013; Liu et al. 2022). Besides, germination can enhance the accumulation of micronutrients and bioactive compounds in moringa seeds and sprouts, such as γ‐aminobutyric acid (GABA), riboflavin, glucomoringin, and moringin (Coello et al. 2020; Liu, Zhang, et al. 2024). A few studies have examined the effects of germination conditions, such as temperature and duration, on the GSL profile of moringa sprouts. Specifically, germination at 32°C for 24 h was found to maximize glucomoringin content, reaching approximately 235 µmol/g on a dry weight (DW) basis, whereas prolonged germination resulted in a decline in its concentration (Coello et al. 2020). In another study, the highest concentrations of glucomoringin and its corresponding ITC, moringin, were observed in sprouts germinated at either 30°C or 35°C for 96 h (with no significant difference between the two temperatures), which were further increased by 22% and 27%, respectively, under exogenous tyrosine stimulation (Liu, Zhang, et al. 2024). Small amounts of glucosinalbin and glucotropaeolin were also detected in moringa sprouts, with the former decreasing and the latter increasing during germination (Coello et al. 2020). However, the biotransformation relationships among glucomoringin, glucosinalbin, and glucotropaeolin in moringa sprouts remain unclear, and it is also uncertain whether these compounds are biosynthesized independently. Moreover, the biosynthetic mechanisms of glucomoringin and the key genes involved in its production during moringa seed germination remain underexplored, nor are the effects of germination conditions on the expression of these genes well understood. Future studies are warranted to elucidate the regulatory mechanisms of germination conditions on glucomoringin metabolism and the expression of associated genes, which could facilitate the production of moringa sprouts enriched with bioactive compounds, therefore enhancing their health benefits and being suitable for direct consumption as functional foods or serve as a novel bioingredient in functional food formulations.

Given their high nutritional value and health‐promoting properties, moringa seeds and sprouts have been widely incorporated as ingredients in the development of various functional food products, including noodles, pasta, and yoghurt (Coello et al. 2021; Dhawi et al. 2020; Liu, Zhang, et al. 2024). Their incorporation not only enhances the nutritional quality of fortified foods but also confers antioxidant and antimicrobial activities, thereby extending the shelf life of the products (Al‐Juhaimi et al. 2016; Dhawi et al. 2020). Moringa seeds and their extracts have also been explored as novel pharmaceutical agents for the treatment of chronic diseases. Various animal studies have demonstrated that oral administration of moringa seeds and their extracts can confer anti‐carcinogenic, antidiabetic, and hepatoprotective effects (Al‐Malki and El Rabey 2015; Hamza 2010; Zunica et al. 2021). Despite their promising potential in functional food and pharmaceutical applications, some controversies remain regarding their oral toxicity. For example, Oliveira et al. (1999) reported that rats fed a diet enriched with moringa seed flour (12,183 mg/kg body weight per day) exhibited loss of appetite and organ damage compared with those fed an egg white‐based diet, whereas no adverse effects were observed when moringa seed extracts were administered at doses of up to 257 mg/kg body weight per day (Kim et al. 2018). These findings highlight the need for future studies to confirm the safety thresholds for consuming moringa seed flour. Additionally, human intervention studies are warranted to further evaluate the potential oral toxicity of moringa seeds and their extracts, which would help establish safe consumption levels and provide a scientific basis for regulatory approval in functional food and nutraceutical applications.

Although the nutritional value of moringa seeds and sprouts has been reviewed (Núñez‐Gastélum et al. 2023; Saa et al. 2019), there is still a lack of a comprehensive review addressing the impact of germination on the bioactive compounds in moringa seeds, as well as discussing their health benefits demonstrated in vivo. In this review, we systematically examined the germination process of moringa seeds, detailing the associated physiological and biochemical transformation. We also provided a comprehensive overview of the principal bioactive compounds and putative metabolic pathways active in moringa sprouts, emphasizing their promising health benefits as demonstrated in animal studies. Additionally, we evaluated the effects of various processing techniques, including thermal (e.g., roasting and boiling) and biological treatment (e.g., fermentation), on the chemical composition, bioactive compounds, and organoleptic properties of moringa seeds. The nutritional profiles and functional properties of food products fortified with moringa seeds and/or sprouts were also assessed and compared. Finally, we identified and discussed key research gaps and safety concerns related to the use of moringa seeds and sprouts, highlighting areas for future investigation.

Germination Process

Seed germination is a simple, cost‐effective, green, and biologically safe bioprocess to produce edible seedlings and sprouts with enhanced nutritional and medicinal value. Germination also reduces some antinutritional components of edible seeds, while increasing digestibility. Generally, the germination process of moringa seeds involves three prominent steps, namely, sanitation, soaking, and sprouting.

Sanitation

Surface sanitation is a common practice prior to seed soaking, although it is occasionally omitted in certain studies (Dawood 2020; Salama et al. 2020). This omission may depend on the physiological status of the seeds and the intended purpose of germination, for example, concerns over food safety risks potentially associated with residual sanitizing reagents (Gan et al. 2017). Nevertheless, sanitation remains a crucial preparatory step for eliminating seed surface microorganisms, protecting seedling health, and preventing contamination. This is particularly important when seeds are cultivated on agar, in hydroponic systems, or under other sterile conditions. Additionally, when consumed as microgreens, sanitation is an essential preparatory step to avoid bacterial growth during moringa seed germination, thereby safeguarding food and human safety.

Of the various sanitizing reagents, sodium hypochlorite solution (0.02%–5.25%, w/v) is the most widely used for surface sanitation of moringa seeds (Coello et al. 2020; de la Mora‐López et al. 2018; Ijarotimi et al. 2013; Liu, Zhang, et al. 2024). However, the optimization of sanitation parameters like concentration and treatment duration for moringa seed germination remains largely underexplored, and no standardized protocol has been established, although some studies have reported the toxic effects of different surface sanitizing agents, including the type and concentration of disinfectants as well as immersion time, on seed germination (Ramakrishna et al. 1991). It is also recommended that future studies explore the effects of sanitation duration on the nutrient composition and bioactive compounds of moringa seeds and sprouts.

Soaking

Germination initiates itself with the absorption of water by the inactive seeds (imbibition). Therefore, soaking is a necessary step to activate dry, dormant mature seeds, as it enables the rapid resumption of metabolic processes, leading to the breakdown of storage macromolecules in the endosperm that subsequently provide energy and nutrients for embryo expansion and radicle penetration (Tesfay et al. 2016). Theoretically, imbibition activates growth regulators like gibberellic acid and kinetin, which stimulate enzymes responsible for starch hydrolysis. This process initiates glucose metabolism, providing energy needed for root protrusion (Núñez‐Gastélum et al. 2023). In addition to activating metabolism, soaking softens moringa seed coat, facilitating radicle protrusion and enhancing uniform seedling emergence. Generally, moringa seeds are soaked in water at ambient temperature (around 20–25°C), with soaking duration ranging from a few to 24 h. During soaking, periodically changing the soaking water is commonly applied to prevent seed fermentation, especially buildup of spoilage and pathogenic microorganisms (Salama et al. 2020).

Sprouting

Hydrated moringa seeds are normally sprouted in soil or vessels, such as in incubators or germination trays lined with wet filter papers (Coello et al. 2020; Liu, Zhang, et al. 2024; Núñez‐Gastélum et al. 2023). The sprouting process can be influenced by various factors, including seed dormancy, the testa's thickness, the type of light and light exposure period, the type of substrate, germination temperature and time, relative humidity, and oxygen, among others (Núñez‐Gastélum et al. 2023). Moringa seed sprouting is typically carried out in the dark under controlled conditions, with relative humidity maintained at 80%–90% to promote uniform germination and prevent desiccation. However, the effects of light on germination rate, as well as on the nutritional value and bioactive compound profile of moringa sprouts, remain largely underexplored. The sprouting temperature and duration for moringa seeds are commonly maintained between 25°C and 35°C for up to 10 days (Coello et al. 2020; Dawood 2020; Liu, Zhang, et al. 2024), with the optimal conditions influenced by factors such as the type and duration of sanitizing treatment, soaking time, germination substrate, and the intended purpose of sprouting. The presence of the seed husk may also affect germination. However, the impact of husk removal on sprouting remains debatable (Núñez‐Gastélum et al. 2023). Further studies are required to clarify the relationship between the seed husk and the germination process.

However, sprouting moringa seeds presents several practical and technical challenges. For example, their relatively hard seed coat may hinder uniform water absorption and radicle emergence, making optimization of the soaking process essential. In addition, moringa seeds are highly susceptible to microbial contamination during germination, particularly under warm and humid conditions such as 35°C and 90% relative humidity. Therefore, sanitation is a critical preparatory step to disinfect seeds and enhance germination rates. Furthermore, the white wings on the seed coat need to be removed prior to soaking and germination, as their high moisture absorption can lead to rancidity and promote microbial growth. However, the removal of wings without damaging the seed coat is labor‐intensive and time‐consuming, particularly in large‐scale sprouting operations. Interestingly, advanced techniques such as cold plasma treatment have been applied to enhance the germination rate of moringa seeds. For example, moringa seeds treated with cold plasma at 100 W and an argon gas pressure of 2 torr for 1 min achieved an initial germination rate of approximately 85% on day 1, which rose to nearly 100% after 2 days of germination, representing an increase of about 20%–25% compared to untreated seeds (Dawood 2020).

Physiological and Biochemical Changes of Moringa Seeds During Germination

Moringa seed germination generally comprises three phases: (1) the initial uptake of water by the seeds and DNA repair in phase I; (2) the translation or degradation of stored mRNA and substantial transcription of new genes in phase II; and (3) the radicle emergence in phase III signifying the completion of germination (Bewley et al. 2012; Núñez‐Gastélum et al. 2023). The process that extensively utilizes most stored reserves for the nascent seedling growth is not considered a part of germination but a post‐germination event (Bewley et al. 2012). During germination, plant hormones play crucial roles in regulating seed dormancy, cell cycle progression, and radicle growth and development (Miransari and Smith 2014). For example, gibberellins are known to promote seed germination, whereas abscisic acid exerts an inhibitory effect on the process (Miransari and Smith 2014). Nevertheless, the dynamic changes in key hormones, including gibberellins, abscisic acid, auxin, and ethylene, throughout moringa seed germination remain unexplored. Future studies are warranted to investigate hormonal fluctuations from imbibition to radicle emergence of moringa seeds to provide deeper insights into the physiological and biochemical processes underlying moringa seed germination.

Numerous intrinsic and extrinsic factors (e.g., morphological and physiological status of the embryo, seed nutritional reserves, seed coat characteristics, type of dormancy, maceration and time, and environmental conditions), either independently or in combination, can affect the seed germination and the final quality of sprouts (Liu et al. 2022; Núñez‐Gastélum et al. 2023). For example, during germination, the adaptive responses to either biotic or abiotic stresses (e.g., temperature fluctuation, dehydration, and osmotic pressure) generally result in changes in the multiple physiological and biochemical processes of moringa seeds, which help accumulate nutrients and phytochemicals. The accumulation of these bioactive compounds endows the germinated seeds and sprouts with versatile therapeutic potential, such as hepatoprotective, antidiabetic, anticancer, antioxidant, and anti‐inflammatory effects. Therefore, intentionally regulating the germination process through external stimuli like exogenous phytohormones is of great importance to produce moringa sprouts with enhanced health‐promoting properties.

Morphological Changes

The outer morphology of moringa seeds is shown in Figure 1A. The seeds at maturity are dicotyledonous, comprising primarily the embryo and a roughly triangular brownish husk, from which three elongated white wings, measuring 5–25 mm in length, develop (Muhl et al. 2016). The embryo encompasses the embryonic axis and a pair of cotyledons. The axis consists of the radicle (embryonic root), hypocotyl (which connects to the cotyledons), and plumule (the shoot apex, which contains the first true leaf primordia). The seed coat is composed of multiple distinct layers, including an epidermal layer, exotesta, and endotesta, which originate from the outer integument, along with remnants of the inner integument. When the seed coat is removed, the endotesta and inner integument remain tightly attached to the cotyledons, with three furrow lines corresponding to vascular bundles (Fotouo‐M et al. 2015). Interestingly, the immature moringa seeds possess endosperm of both the nuclear and cellular types. As the transformation of embryogenesis from the globular to the histo‐differentiation and to the maturation phase, the two cotyledons expand rapidly both in size and mass, consuming virtually all the endosperm at the end of the lastly developmental stage (Muhl et al. 2016). As a result, the mature moringa seeds are non‐endospermic. The anatomy of moringa seeds is shown in Figure 1B.

To germinate, dormant moringa seeds must be hydrated under appropriate conditions that support metabolic reactivation. Water uptake by the dry seeds is crucial for initiating metabolic processes and cellular activities, leading to radicle emergence and the subsequent development of the early seedling. Upon imbibition, respiration is immediately activated to provide the metabolic energy required for the germination progress (Tesfay et al. 2016). During germination, the radicle continues to elongate and gradually ruptures the integument to give rise to a primary root with positive geotropism. As the primary root develops, the cotyledonary loop forms, and the first eophylls quickly emerge (Ramos et al. 2010). Degradation of Seed Storage Reserves

After imbibition, moringa seeds mobilize their storage reserves, primarily carbohydrates, lipids, and proteins deposited in the cotyledons, to initiate germination and provide energy for the early seedling growth. The required energy is tightly associated with catalytic enzyme activities where, under suitable conditions (e.g., temperature and humidity), these stored macromolecules are hydrolyzed to simple, soluble, and mobile compounds that are utilized for growth and development. In addition to these primary reserves, phytin, although present in a relatively small quantity, is significant as it serves as a critical source of phosphate and essential mineral elements for the seeds.

Carbohydrates

Starch represents the primary form of stored carbohydrates present in moringa seeds. During germination, starch is broken down to simple sugars (e.g., glucose, sucrose, and fructose), which are readily utilized as energy sources for embryonic growth. The hydrolysis and mobilization of starch are associated with catalytic enzymes (e.g., α‐amylase), of which the higher activity can accelerate the speed of seed germination and increase the germination rate. Temperature, as a critical climatic factor, plays a pivotal role in regulating enzyme activity during the germination of moringa seeds. It is reported that the amylase activity shows a positive correlation with germination temperature. For example, the 30°C/20°C (day/night) temperature regime leads to a sharp increase of amylase catalysis of starch after 24 h seed imbibition, as compared to that at lower temperature regimes (25°C/15°C and 20°C/10°C) (Tesfay et al. 2016). Such a vigorous amylase catalytic effect provides more energy for moringa seeds to initiate their germination, achieving rapid seedling growth and high plant production. During germination, sucrose remains the dominant sugar in moringa seeds, although its concentration declines sharply after 24 h of imbibition under a 30°C/20°C regime. This could be ascribed to the fact that sucrose serves as a key substrate for seed respiration, supporting the initial emergence of the radicle (Tesfay et al. 2016).

Lipids

Storage lipids are the major energy reserves in moringa seeds, primarily existing as triacylglycerols (TAGs), which consist of a glycerol (Gly) molecule with three FAs bound to it (de la Mora‐López et al. 2018). During seed germination, TAGs are hydrolyzed by lipases to release Gly and free FAs. The glycerol is then transformed into dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP) through the action of glycerol kinase and Gly‐3‐P dehydrogenase. The latter can be metabolized in glyoxysomes through β‐oxidation and glyoxylate cycle to organic acids, which, along with DHAP, are finally converted into sucrose by gluconeogenesis (Bewley et al. 2012; de la Mora‐López et al. 2018). According to previous studies, although the lipid content experiences a substantial decline during germination, the content of polyunsaturated FAs (PUFAs) increased in moringa sprouts, particularly linoleic and α‐linolenic acids that are essential for brain function and cell growth, as well as for prevention of various diseases (de la Mora‐López et al. 2018; Salama et al. 2020). Apart from serving as major energy sources for seed germination, storage lipids are also crucial for the biosynthesis of specific structural constituents (e.g., cell membrane) in young seedlings.

Proteins

During germination, the hydrolysis of storage proteins involves the action of various proteases that release free amino acids or small polypeptides, which might be subsequently degraded by peptidases. Regarding their substrate specificities, the proteases can be classified into endopeptidases and exopeptidases (e.g., aminopeptidases and carboxypeptidases), based on their mode of action. Endopeptidases remove internal peptide bonds within the protein, producing smaller polypeptides, whereas exopeptidases sequentially remove terminal amino acid from the free amino end of the polypeptide chain (Bewley et al. 2012; de la Mora‐López et al. 2018). These proteolytic enzymes are crucial for the physiological and biochemical processes involved in moringa seed germination. It is reported that germination significantly enhances the levels of proteins and essential amino acids (e.g., lysine and methionine) in moringa sprouts, thereby improving their nutritional value (Coello et al. 2020; de la Mora‐López et al. 2018). The increase in protein content in moringa sprouts could be attributed to the depletion of lipids during germination, because the energy required for seed germination is primarily provided by lipids (de la Mora‐López et al. 2018).

Phytin

Phytin, also known as myo‐inositol 6‐phosphate, is an insoluble mixed salt formed by the combination of phytic acid with minerals like potassium, magnesium, and calcium. It serves as a source of phosphorus, inositol, and inorganic phosphate ions to be utilized during germination. However, phytic acid is considered an antinutrient in human and monogastric animal diets. The negatively charged phosphate groups can strongly chelate with divalent cations (e.g., zinc, iron, and calcium) to form insoluble complexes that cannot be dissociated for absorption, thus resulting in deficiency of some essential dietary minerals (Bewley et al. 2012; Liu et al. 2022). Phytin can also form ingestible conjugates with dietary proteins and inhibit digestive enzymes. It has been reported that germination shows the potential to reduce phytin content in germinated moringa seeds and sprouts (Matthew et al. 2022), possibly due to the increased endogenous phytase activity during germination (Zhang et al. 2022).

Bioactive Compounds in Moringa Seeds and Sprouts

Table S1 summarizes the bioactive compounds (e.g., GABA, phenolic compounds, and GSLs) in moringa seeds and/or sprouts. The accumulation of these phytochemicals endows moringa seeds and sprouts with versatile therapeutic potential, such as anti‐carcinogenic, anti‐inflammatory, and antidiabetic activities. Herein, we discuss the effect of germination on the accumulation of bioactive compounds in moringa seeds and the potential biosynthetic mechanisms involved.

GABA

GABA is a four‐carbon free amino acid commonly found in bacteria, plants, and vertebrates (Diana et al. 2014). As a stress response factor, GABA quickly accumulates in plant tissues when exposed to biotic and/or abiotic stressors, where it plays a role in regulating plant growth (Ramesh et al. 2015). In higher plants, the GABA shunt and the polyamine (PA) degradation pathway are the two primary signaling pathways involved in GABA synthesis (Gan et al. 2017; Liu et al. 2019, 2022). In the GABA shunt, GABA is synthesized through the decarboxylation reaction of l‐glutamate, a reaction catalyzed by glutamate decarboxylase (GAD) (Narayan and Nair 1990), which requires the cofactor pyridoxal‐5′‐phosphate (Diana et al. 2014). In the PAs pathway, GABA is produced through γ‐aminobutyraldehyde intermediate, where diamine oxidase (DAO) and polyamine oxidase (PAO) catalyze the conversion of PAs (e.g., putrescine, spermidine, and spermine) to 4‐aminobutyraldehyde (ABAL), which is then oxidized to GABA under the action of 4‐aminoaldehyde dehydrogenase (AMADH) (Shelp et al. 2012; Wang et al. 2019). GABA is then converted into succinate, which ultimately enters the Krebs cycle.

It is reported that germination remarkably increases the GABA content in moringa seeds (Coello et al. 2020). For example, moringa seeds germinating at 36°C for 96 h possessed the highest GABA content of 291.2 mg/100 g sample on a DW basis, with a 5.8‐fold increase as compared to that of the raw seeds (Coello et al. 2020). GABA content also increased in moringa seeds germinating at 28°C and 32°C, with a maximum increase to 197.6 mg/100 g DW (Coello et al. 2020). The remarkable accumulation of GABA in moringa seeds germinating at 36°C for 96 h could be attributed to the elevated GAD activity, whose optimal catalytic temperature is approximately 40°C. On the other hand, although few studies have explored the role of DAO in GABA accumulation in moringa sprouts, germination has been reported to improve the DAO activity in legume seeds and fava beans, which facilitates the biosynthesis of GABA during germination (Comas‐Basté et al. 2020; Yang et al. 2011). Therefore, the PAs degradation pathway may also play a role in the increased GABA content during moringa seed germination, but this merits further research. Taken together, GABA biosynthesis in moringa sprouts could be attributed to the increased activity of these endogenous enzymes (e.g., GAD and DAO) involved in its metabolism. However, more relevant studies are required to clarify the role of DAO in upregulation of GABA biosynthesis in moringa sprouts.

Phenolic Compounds

Phenolic compounds are chemically defined as a class of molecules containing one or more hydroxylated aromatic rings and are primarily biosynthesized through the shikimate and phenylpropanoid pathways (Corso et al. 2020). They are universally distributed in plants in free and/or bound forms and are involved in plant allelopathy, insect pollination, defense against herbivores and pathogens, and the regulation of plant hormones (Khanam et al. 2012). In addition to their vital physiological and ecological functions in plants, phenolic compounds exert beneficial effects on human health, offering protection against aging, cardiovascular diseases, neurodegenerative disorders, cancer, and diabetes (Corso et al. 2020; Manach et al. 2004). Moringa seeds are a rich source of phenolic compounds (Singh et al. 2013). Several individual phenolics have been identified and quantified, with gallic acid (18.1 mg/100 g moringa seed flour extracts [MSFE]), caffeic acid (3.79 mg/100 g MSFE), and vanillin (0.87 mg/100 g MSFE) more abundant in their free form, whereas catechin (749 mg/100 g MSFE), epicatechin (81.4 mg/100 g MSFE), and quercetin (1.88 mg/100 g MSFE) are predominantly present in bound form (Singh et al. 2013). Interestingly, epicatechin is also found in high concentrations in its free form, accounting for approximately 25% of the total content of the quantified free phenolic compounds. Notably, catechin and epicatechin together comprise 99.5% of the quantified bound phenolic compounds, with their concentrations in bound form being more than 1800 times and approximately 10 times higher, respectively, than that of their corresponding levels in free form (Singh et al. 2013). Chlorogenic acid has also been identified in moringa seed kernels at a concentration of 0.99 mg/100 g DW (Liu, Chin, Huang, et al. 2024). These compounds may position moringa seeds as a novel bioingredient for the development of functional foods with enhanced antioxidant activity. The molecular structures of phenolic compounds found in moringa seeds are shown in Figure S1.

Recent studies have demonstrated that germination can significantly enhance the total phenolic content (TPC) and antioxidant capacity of moringa seeds (Coello et al. 2020; de la Mora‐López et al. 2018; Liu, Zhang, et al. 2024). This effect may be attributed to the enhanced biosynthesis and biotransformation of phenolic compounds during moringa seed germination, as reported in peanut sprouts (Wu et al. 2011). Another possible explanation is that germination induces the production of enzymes capable of degrading cell wall components (e.g., cellulose, pectin, and polysaccharides) that are conjugated with phenolic compounds (Gan et al. 2017; Ti et al. 2014). This enzymatic breakdown facilitates the release of bound phenolics, thereby contributing to the increased TPC and enhanced antioxidant capacity of moringa sprouts. However, the composition of free and bound phenolic compounds in moringa sprouts has yet to be characterized, nor have their dynamic changes during germination been elucidated. Further relevant research is needed to investigate how germination impacts the dynamic changes in the composition of phenolic compounds in moringa seeds and sprouts.

GSLs and Their Corresponding ITCs

GSLs are a class of sulfur‐containing secondary metabolites invariably present in plants of the Brassicales order. They have long been recognized for anti‐carcinogenesis (Augustine and Bisht 2015; Fahey et al. 1997). The bioactivities of GSLs are primarily attributed to their corresponding ITCs, which are biologically active compounds formed by myrosinase hydrolysis (Dinkova‐Kostova and Kostov 2012). GSLs are biosynthesized from amino acids and possess a core structure comprising a β‐d‐thioglucose moiety attached to a sulfated aldoxime group, along with a variable side chain (Dong et al. 2021). On the basis of their precursor amino acids, GSLs can be categorized into three groups: aliphatic, benzenic, and indolic GSLs (Sønderby et al. 2010). The biosynthesis of GSLs consists of three stages: (1) side chain elongation of precursor amino acids, notably methionine and phenylalanine; (2) GSL core structure formation; and (3) side chain modification, such as oxidation and hydroxylation (Andini et al. 2019; Sønderby et al. 2010).

To date, over 120 GSLs have been characterized in plants, with more being continuously discovered (Blažević et al. 2020). The GSLs identified in moringa seeds and/or sprouts include glucomoringin and its acetylated derivatives, glucosoonjnain, glucotropaeolin, glucosinalbin, among others (Bennett et al. 2003; Coello et al. 2020; Fahey et al. 2018; Liu, Zhang, et al. 2024), as shown in Figure 2. Of these identified GSLs, glucomoringin is the predominant GSL found in moringa seeds and sprouts (Badgett 1964; Bennett et al. 2003; Coello et al. 2020). The presence of a rhamnose sugar moiety confers it with a solid appearance and a relatively high stability.

The content of glucomoringin in moringa sprouts fluctuates widely, depending on the germination temperature and duration (Coello et al. 2020). When germinated at 36°C, the glucomoringin content in moringa sprouts increased by 18% during the first 60 h germination, whereas it decreased by 26% during the following 36 h germination (Coello et al. 2020). Similar patterns have been observed in those germinated at 32°C, whereas those germinated at 28°C exhibited an opposite trend in the content of glucomoringin. The effects of germination temperature and time on the glucomoringin content have also been investigated in another study, which reported that higher germination temperature (35°C vs. 30°C vs. 25°C) facilitated the accumulation of glucomoringin and moringin in moringa sprouts, with the amount of approximately 149 and 129 µmol/g DW, respectively, in samples germinated at 35°C on day 4, respectively, which were significantly higher than that of samples germinated at 25°C on day 4 (125 and 106 µmol/g DW, respectively) (Liu, Zhang, et al. 2024). These results are possibly explained by that relatively higher germination temperature may facilitate the expression of certain key genes (e.g., BCA, CYP, and AOP) involved in GSL biosynthetic pathways, which activate corresponding enzymes associated with GSL metabolism during germination (Andini et al. 2019). In addition to germination temperature and time, other key factors, such as relative humidity, light exposure, and the application of exogenous elicitors, may influence the glucomoringin content in moringa sprouts. For example, the contents of glucomoringin and moringin in germinated moringa seeds stimulated with tyrosine were approximately 2.7‐ and 3.3‐fold higher, respectively, than those in ungerminated moringa seeds (Liu, Zhang, et al. 2024). However, few studies have systematically evaluated the effects of these individual or combined factors on glucomoringin accumulation during moringa seed germination. Therefore, the optimal germination conditions for maximizing glucomoringin content remain largely unexplored. More intensive studies are required to uncover the relationship between glucomoringin metabolism in moringa sprouts and germination conditions, which could offer scientific guidance for its upregulation during moringa seed germination. Moreover, the fluctuation of glucomoringin content in moringa sprouts could be due to their sensitivity to myrosinase‐catalyzed hydrolysis during extraction. Processing of edible seeds and sprouts can impact the glucomoringin content, as cellular injury allows endogenous glucomoringin to interact with myrosinase, leading to its hydrolysis into various compounds, including moringin. Therefore, it is suggested to deactivate the enzyme activity prior to glucomoringin extraction.

So far, the biosynthetic pathways of glucomoringin in moringa sprouts have yet to be well elucidated. In our previous study, we proposed that tyrosine could be the precursor amino acid for glucomoringin biosynthesis in moringa sprouts, as exogenous tyrosine remarkably stimulated the production of glucomoringin during moringa seed germination (Liu, Zhang, et al. 2024). As shown in Figure 3 (Scheme 1), its potential metabolic pathway possibly consists of oxidation, conjugation, C–S bond cleavage, glucosylation, sulfation, and rhamnosylation (Liu, Zhang, et al. 2024). In addition, Guo et al. (2025) demonstrated that glucomoringin could also be biosynthesized from phenylalanine. This pathway shares the same steps for core GSL structure formation shown in Figure 3 (Scheme 2), illustrated using methionine as an example, which involves side‐chain elongation, core structure formation, and side‐chain modification to produce aliphatic GSLs, such as 4‐methylthiobutyl GSL (Guo et al. 2025). However, the specific mechanism by which the hydroxyl group is introduced at the para position of the benzene ring remains unclear. Moreover, the specific intermediate metabolites involved in this pathway have yet to be identified and confirmed. To validate the proposed biosynthetic pathways of glucomoringin, isotope‐labeled metabolomic analysis could be used to trace intermediate metabolites involved in its formation. Additionally, transcriptomic analysis may be conducted to monitor dynamic changes in the expression of genes potentially associated with glucomoringin metabolism. Modifying the expression of these genes during moringa seed germination could provide a promising strategy to enhance glucomoringin bioaccumulation.

Health Benefits of Moringa Seeds, Sprouts, and Their Extracts

Moringa seeds have been traditionally utilized in ethnomedicine due to their diverse health benefits, including anti‐carcinogenic, anti‐inflammatory, antidiabetic, and hepatoprotective properties. Although the body of preclinical and clinical research on the health effects of moringa seeds, sprouts, and their extracts remains limited, numerous in vivo studies using animal models have consistently demonstrated their therapeutic potential (Al‐Malki and El Rabey 2015; Hamza 2010; Zunica et al. 2021). Therefore, we present a summary of the key health benefits of moringa seeds, sprouts, and their extracts as observed in vivo, with an emphasis on the proposed mechanisms of action. Antioxidant Effect

Moringa seeds, sprouts, and their extracts exhibit potent antioxidant effects in both in vitro and in vivo studies. Their in vitro antioxidant capacities have been demonstrated through free radical scavenging assays, such as 2,2‐diphenyl‐1‐picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), 2,2′‐azino‐bis (3‐ethylbenzothiazoline‐6‐sulfonic acid) (ABTS), and oxygen radical absorbance capacity (ORAC), as well as reducing power assays like ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) (Cirlini et al. 2022; Jahan et al. 2018; Liu, Zhang, et al. 2024). It has been reported that the antioxidant capacities are primarily attributed to their phenolic compounds, as evidenced by the positive correlation between in vitro antioxidant capacity and TPC (Liu, Zhnag, et al. 2024). Sprouting may further increase the antioxidant capacity of moringa seeds, likely due to the increased enzyme activities involved in phenolic biosynthesis and the activation of cell wall‐degrading enzymes, which facilitate the breakdown of cell wall components and the subsequent release of phenolic compounds (Coello et al. 2020; Liu, Zhang, et al. 2024). Apart from phenolic compounds, moringin derived from moringa seeds may also contribute to their antioxidant capacity, as demonstrated by cell studies (Rajan et al. 2016).

In animal studies, the activation of antioxidant gene expression via the nuclear factor erythroid 2‐related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathway is a key mechanism through which moringa seeds and their extracts exert various health benefits, including anticancer, hepatoprotective, and antidiabetic effects (Wen et al. 2022). The upregulated expression of antioxidant and detoxifying enzymes, such as heme oxygenase‐1 (HO‐1), glutathione S‐transferase pi 1 (GSTP1), and NAD(P)H:quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1), help mitigate oxidative stress and restore organ function. The detailed discussion of the in vivo antioxidant effects of moringa seeds and their extracts is provided in the following sections.

Anti‐Inflammatory Effect

Inflammation is a typical physiological defense response to tissue damage, driven by oxidative stress and the excessive secretion of inflammatory cytokines (Wang et al. 2022). It also plays a crucial role in tumor progression. There are numerous harmful stimuli that could cause inflammation, such as tissue damage trauma, infection, chemicals, and microbiological toxins (Rajan et al. 2016). Key biomarkers of inflammation include interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β), IL‐6, tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α), and oxidative stress mediators, particularly nitric oxide (NO), which is synthesized by inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS). The overproduction of these pro‐inflammatory mediators is closely associated with inflammation, which contributes to the development and progression of chronic diseases such as metabolic syndrome and various human cancers (Rajan et al. 2016).

Moringa seed extracts also show promising anti‐inflammatory effects in both cell and animal models. For example, cellular studies have demonstrated the anti‐inflammatory effects of moringa seed extracts by reducing NO production via mediating the iNOS pathway (Jaja‐Chimedza et al. 2017). Moringin may be the primary compound responsible for the anti‐inflammatory effects. It exerts the effects by downregulating the gene expression of iNOS, IL‐1β, and IL‐6, leading to a reduction in NO production; simultaneously, it activates Nrf2 and upregulates the expression of its downstream target genes, including NQO1, GSTP1, and HO‐1, which enhance cellular antioxidant defense mechanisms (Jaja‐Chimedza et al. 2017).

The anti‐inflammatory effects of moringa seeds and their extracts have also been confirmed in various animal studies, contributing to the alleviation of inflammation‐associated chronic diseases, such as diabetes and cancer (Jaja‐Chimedza et al. 2018). These effects are largely attributed to the modulation of pro‐inflammatory cytokines (e.g., iNOS, IL‐1β, and IL‐6) and oxidative stress pathways (e.g., Nrf2). The specific intervention mechanisms underlying the protective effects are discussed in the following sections.

Protection Against Carcinogenesis

Cancer has become a major public health threat, with risk factors including exposure to chemicals and radiation, lack of exercise, aging, and genetic predisposition. Although traditional cancer therapies, such as percutaneous ablation treatment, surgical resection, and chemotherapy, have significantly improved survival rates, the overall prognosis remains poor (Wu et al. 2021). Moreover, these treatments may cause serious side effects and, in some cases, may even be fatal. Developing anticancer drugs from natural sources, such as plants, or using food as medicine is a potential strategy to address these challenges. Moringa seeds have been widely used in traditional medicine for centuries due to their numerous health benefits, including anti‐carcinogenic activity. These health‐promoting properties of moringa seeds and their extracts may be attributed to the presence of key bioactive compounds, such as glucomoringin and its derivative moringin. The anticancer effects of moringa seeds and their extracts may involve the regulation of oxidative stress, induction of cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, as well as antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory activities. Multiple cancer cell lines have been utilized to evaluate the anti‐carcinogenic and anti‐proliferative effects of moringa seeds and/or their extracts (Das et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2020). The results suggest that moringa seed extracts inhibit cancer cell proliferation by inducing apoptosis. Various studies indicate that the cytotoxic effects of moringa seeds and their extracts may be attributed to the bioactive compound moringin, which induces cell cycle perturbation and caspase‐3 dependent apoptosis and upregulates the expression of proapoptotic proteins such as p21, p53, and Bax, leading to DNA damage and subsequent cell cycle arrest (Brunelli et al. 2010; Cirmi et al. 2019; Xie et al. 2022).

The anti‐carcinogenic effects of moringa seeds and their extracts have also been evidenced in animal studies. For example, moringa seed ethanolic extracts have been shown to reduce angiogenesis in tumors and improve metabolic health in mice with triple‐negative breast cancer (Zunica et al. 2021). Additionally, their consumption increased fasting glucose levels and raised insulin sensitivity, which may benefit individuals with metabolic disorders. In another animal study, Aldayel et al. (2023) demonstrated the anticancer activities of moringa seed oil using an Ehrlich ascites carcinoma (EAC)‐induced mice model. The study revealed that oral administration of moringa seed oil (500 mg/kg) reduced EAC cell viability, decreased pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐2, IL‐6, and TNF‐α, and elevated reduced glutathione (GSH) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activities, while reducing malondialdehyde (MDA) levels. These findings indicate that moringa seed oil has the potential for protecting against cancerous diseases through antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, and anti‐proliferative properties. Treatment with moringin (20 mg/kg), derived from glucomoringin extracted from moringa seeds, demonstrated strong antitumoral effects in a mouse model implanted with human myeloma RPMI8226 cancer cells while causing minimum toxicity to the mice (Brunelli et al. 2010). These findings align with results observed in various cancer cells, suggesting that moringin may play a key role in the anti‐carcinogenic properties of moringa seeds and their extracts.

Although the anticancer activities of moringa seeds and their extracts have been demonstrated in various cellular and animal studies, clinical evidence in humans remains scarce. Further well‐designed clinical research is necessary to evaluate their safety, efficacy, and therapeutic potential as an anticancer agent.

Protection Against Diabetes Mellitus (DM)

DM is a metabolic disorder characterized by hyperglycemia, the primary cause of diabetic complications such as retinopathy, nephropathy, and neuropathy, posing a serious health threat to populations worldwide (Al‐Malki and El Rabey 2015). Various in vivo studies have demonstrated the potential of moringa seeds as an antidiabetic agent for managing DM. Oral administration of moringa seed powder (50 and 100 mg/kg body weight per day for 4 weeks) was found to restore kidney and pancreatic function in the streptozotocin (STZ)‐induced diabetic rats by reducing lipid peroxidation, IL‐6, immunoglobulins (e.g., IgA and IgG), and glycosylated hemoglobin levels, while raising antioxidant enzyme activity (e.g., catalase, SOD, and GSH) in the serum and kidney (Al‐Malki and El Rabey 2015). The antidiabetic activity of moringa seed powder is likely attributed to its antioxidant compounds, including glucomoringin and moringin, flavonoids (e.g., quercetin and kaempferol), and phenols (e.g., chlorogenic acid), which possess free radical‐scavenging properties. Beyond oxidative stress control, moringa seed extracts may help manage diabetes by maintaining lipid homeostasis and reducing serum cholesterol and triglyceride levels (Aljazzaf et al. 2023). Moreover, urine analysis revealed that moringa seeds reduced glucosuria, creatinine, urea nitrogen, albumin, sodium, and potassium levels, which may contribute to the restoration of normal renal function (Al‐Malki and El Rabey 2015). Another potential mechanism under the renal‐protective effects of moringa seed extracts may involve the activation of glycogen synthase kinase‐3 beta (GSK‐3β) and Nrf2/HO‐1 signaling pathways (Wen et al. 2022). On one hand, intragastric administration of moringa seed extracts (50, 100, and 200 mg/kg body weight per day for 4 weeks) enhanced GSK‐3β activity in rats with diabetic nephropathy, potentially reducing renal fibrosis; on the other hand, moringa seed extracts upregulated the expression of Nrf2 and HO‐1, which may provide renal protection through its antioxidant effects (Wen et al. 2022). Additionally, moringa seed extracts improved glucose and insulin tolerance and overall metabolic health in obese mice fed a very high‐fat diet by modulating inflammatory (e.g., iNOS) and antioxidant gene expression (e.g., NQO1), as well as the gut microbiota (Jaja‐Chimedza et al. 2018).

However, human studies on the antidiabetic and hypoglycemic effects of consuming moringa seeds, sprouts, and their extracts remain scant. Although animal studies have demonstrated promising results, including improved glucose tolerance and restored renal function, well‐designed clinical trials are needed to confirm these effects on humans. Further research is required to determine their optimal dosage, long‐term safety, and underlying mechanisms of action in DM management.

Hepatoprotective Effect

Hepatic injury has emerged as a significant global health concern, driven by alcohol consumption, drug use, viral hepatitis, and metabolic disorders, which can lead to inflammation, necrosis, fibrosis, and cirrhosis (Sun et al. 2019). Several animal studies have demonstrated the hepatoprotective properties of moringa seeds, their extracts, and individual components. Moringa seed extracts exhibited hepatoprotective effects against carbon tetrachloride (CCl4)‐induced liver injury and fibrosis in rats, as evidenced by their ability to reduce serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) activities, as well as globulin levels (Hamza 2010). Additionally, moringa seed extracts reduced MDA levels while enhancing SOD activity in the liver, suggesting that their antioxidant properties may help protect against liver fibrosis in rats. The presence of important bioactive compounds, such as phenolic compounds, GSLs, ITCs, thiocarbamates, and flavonoids, likely contributes to the antioxidant capacity of moringa seed extracts (Hamza 2010; Singh et al. 2013). Moringa seed oil (administered at 0.2 and 0.4 mL/rat per day for 21 consecutive days) also exhibited potent protection against CCl4‐induced liver injury by preserving the structural integrity of the hepatocellular membrane and promoting the restoration of damaged liver cells (Al‐Said et al. 2012). Interestingly, Sun et al. (2019) successfully isolated and purified a phenolic glycoside, 1‐O‐(4‐hydroxymethylphenyl)‐α‐l‐rhamnopyranoside (MPG), from moringa seeds and confirmed its hepatoprotective effects against CCl4‐induced hepatic injury in L02 cells and mice by ameliorating the increased oxidative stress level, decreasing TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and monocyte chemotactic protein 1 (MCP‐1) levels, elevating IL‐10 release, and inhibiting the hepatic apoptosis. On the other hand, MPG (5–100 µg/mL) showed no notable cytotoxic effects on L02 cell viability. Furthermore, oral administration of MPG at doses up to 2000 mg/kg body weight in mice resulted in no mortality or observable pathological changes in major organs (e.g., heart, lung, spleen, liver, kidney, and brain) (Sun et al. 2019).

Other Health Benefits

Administration of ethanolic extracts of moringa seeds (100 mg/g body weight per day, 8 days) protected against arsenic‐induced uterine damages in rats, potentially by restoring the arsenic‐detoxified environment and upregulating liver metallothionein to mitigate oxidative stress (Jana et al. 2020). Moringa seed extracts have also demonstrated notable wound‐healing potential, as evidenced by a significant increase in the wound‐healing rate in rabbits (2 mg/wound). This effect was associated with elevated expression of transforming growth factor‐beta 1 (TGFβ1), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and Type I collagen, along with reduced levels of inflammatory biomarkers such as IL‐1β and TNF‐α (Shady et al. 2022). Additionally, moringa seed extracts have been reported to alleviate the symptoms of ulcerative colitis (UC) and reduce UC‐induced colonic pathologies in mice by downregulating pro‐inflammatory mediators (e.g., IL‐1, IL‐6, TNF‐α, and iNOS) while upregulating GSTP1, an Nrf2‐mediated phase II detoxifying enzyme (Kim et al. 2017). The cardiac protective effects of orally administered moringa seed powder (750 mg per day for 8 weeks) have been demonstrated in spontaneous hypertensive rats, leading to a reduction in nocturnal heart rate and improved cardiac diastolic function (Randriamboavonjy et al. 2016). The beneficial effects of moringa seed powder may be attributed to the upregulation of peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptors (PPAR)‐α and δ, reduced cardiac triglyceride levels, and increased plasmatic prostacyclin levels. Additionally, moringa seed extracts may exhibit neuroprotective potential against Parkinson's disease in a rotenone‐induced mice model by mitigating oxidative stress, as evidenced by improved levels of GSH, increased glutathione S‐transferase (GST), and enhanced catalase activities (Raza et al. 2024).

Impact of Processing on Chemical Composition and Bioactive Compounds of Moringa Seeds and Sprouts

Different processing techniques like thermal and non‐thermal methods can greatly impact the chemical composition and bioactivity of moringa seeds and sprouts. Although a recent review has summarized the effects of heat (e.g., roasting and cooking) and biological treatments (e.g., fermentation and germination) on the proximate composition, micronutrients, and antinutrients of moringa seeds (Saa et al. 2019), their impact on the bioactivity and organoleptic properties of moringa seeds and sprouts remains insufficiently explored. This section discusses the effects of various processing methods, including roasting, boiling, and fermentation, on the nutrients, bioactive compounds, and organoleptic properties of moringa seeds and sprouts, as shown in Table 2.

Roasting

Roasting is a key heat treatment that can induce chemical changes in moringa seeds and sprouts, which may in turn impact their nutritional composition, bioactive compounds, flavor, aroma, and sensory acceptability, among others. For example, after being roasted at 140°C for 50 min, the content of crude fibers and crude proteins in defatted moringa seed flour significantly increased from 3.7 to 4.8 g/100 g DW and from 58.7 to 61.8 g/100 g DW, respectively, whereas the amount of antinutrients, including saponins, trypsin inhibitor, oxalates, and phytates, significantly decreased (Saa et al. 2022). In another study, Salama et al. (2020) investigated the effect of different roasting conditions (e.g., temperatures of 100°C, 150°C, and 200°C; time of 10 and 20 min) on the oil profile of moringa seeds and reported that, as a comparison to unroasted moringa seeds, total saturated FAs slightly increased with the increasing roasting temperature and time, whereas PUFAs like linoleic acid and linolenic acid significantly decreased, especially for those roasted at 200°C. Additionally, roasting significantly reduced the content of α‐tocopherol by more than 50% as compared to the unroasted control (Salama et al. 2020). However, few studies have examined the effects of roasting process on the micronutrient composition of moringa seeds and sprouts, including minerals and vitamins. Therefore, more intensive research is required to examine the relationship between the nutritional composition of the seeds and sprouts and roasting degrees.

On the other hand, the effect of roasting treatment on the bioactive compounds of moringa seeds, including glucomoringin, moringin, and phenolic compounds, mainly depends on the different degrees of roasting (e.g., light, medium, and excessive roasting). Light (roasted either at 125°C and 150°C for 10 min or at 175°C for 5 min) to medium roasting (roasted at 175°C for 10 min) significantly increased the glucomoringin content from 43.7 µmol/g DW (raw moringa seed kernels) to the maximum of 127.3 µmol/g DW (roasted at 175°C for 5 min) (Liu, Chin, Huang, et al. 2024). However, excessive roasting (roasted either at 175°C for 15 and 20 min or at 200°C and 225°C for 10 min) led to a rapid thermal degradation of glucomoringin, with a trace amount of 1.8 µmol/g DW measured for those roasted at 175°C for 15 min, whereas prolonged roasting duration or further increased roasting temperature even resulted in its complete degradation (Liu, Chin, Huang, et al. 2024). The corresponding ITC moringin also experienced a sharp reduction from 52.1 µmol/g DW (raw moringa seed kernels) to a trace level of 0.39 µmol/g DW (roasted at 175°C for 10 min), or even to an undetectable level (Liu, Chin, Huang, et al. 2024). The initial increase of glucomoringin in moringa seeds was ascribed to the deactivation of myrosinase by roasting, which slowed down the transformation of glucomoringin to moringin, whereas the decrease of glucomoringin and moringin was due to their thermal degradation caused by excessive roasting. Besides, roasting significantly increased the TPC and antioxidant capacity of moringa seeds (Liu, Chin, Huang, et al. 2024). The improved antioxidant capacity was possibly due to the heat‐induced conversion of bound phenolics to their free forms, along with the formation of Maillard reaction products that exhibit strong antioxidant properties. These findings suggest that light to medium roasting could boost the bioactivity of moringa seeds, promoting the roasted seeds as a promising bioingredient for functional food formulations. However, the effect of roasting on the physicochemical and functional properties of roasted moringa seeds and sprouts remains to be investigated. For example, moringa seeds are rich in proteins, and roasting may significantly modify their structures. These changes may, in turn, markedly influence the functional properties of moringa seed proteins, such as water‐holding capacity, oil‐holding capacity, and forming properties.

Despite their high nutritional value and bioactive compounds, the strong pungent and bitter taste of moringa seeds and sprouts restricts their potential utilization either as a bioingredient for functional food formulations or as a functional food for consumption. Roasting could be a promising processing technique to improve the sensory acceptability of moringa seeds and sprouts by developing nutty and caramelic aromas. This improvement is likely attributed to multiple thermal reactions, such as Maillard reaction and caramelization, which generate complex aroma compounds that may help musk undesirable taste attributes (Liu, Chin, Sun, et al. 2024). However, excess roasting can lead to increased bitterness, burnt notes, and loss of the desirable aromas. The correlation between roasting degrees and aroma compounds produced from complex thermal reactions remains unknown, which needs more intensive studies.

Boiling

Boiling is a common processing method that has been employed to modify the nutritional composition of moringa seeds. Compared to unprocessed moringa seeds, boiling (100°C for 15 min) significantly reduced antinutrients, including tannins, phytates, oxalates, saponins, trypsin inhibitor, and cyanogenic glycoside, among which tannins were the dominant antinutrients in moringa seeds (Matthew et al. 2022). The reduction of antinutrients in moringa seeds could improve nutrient digestibility and bioavailability. Besides, moringa seeds are a rich source of vitamins and minerals; however, after boiling, the content of vitamins C and E significantly decreased from 2.33 to 0.81 mg/100 g DW and from 0.22 to 0.13 mg/100 g DW, respectively, compared to raw moringa seeds, whereas the mineral content remained stable (Matthew et al. 2022).

Apart from the loss of certain vitamins, boiling or even sub‐boiling treatment may lead to a reduction of moringin content in moringa seeds. Lu et al. (2021) investigated the thermal stability of moringin in moringa seeds when being subjected to hot water (90°C, sub‐boiling) over 60 min and reported that the content of moringin in moringa seeds experienced a sharp decrease (10‐fold) after heating for 5 min. Excessive heating treatment even resulted in the degradation of moringin to an undetectable level. Pure moringin exhibited a similar degradation pattern under the same treatment conditions but showed a slightly greater stability than that in moringa seeds (Lu et al. 2021). This could be due to its reactions with nucleophilic compounds like proteins and amino acids in moringa seeds (Hanschen et al. 2012). The instability of moringin at high temperature requires effective encapsulation strategies to protect it from thermal degradation. Another promising strategy is to incorporate moringa seed and/or sprout flour as a bioingredient in traditional staple foods, such as noodles. During cooking, the swollen starch granules may exert a protective effect on moringin (Liu et al. 2025). Moringa seed and/or sprout flour may in turn provide a valuable source of bioactive moringin when incorporated into food formulations.

Fermentation

Fermentation is an effective biological treatment that can boost the nutritional value and bioactivity of moringa seeds. For example, spontaneous fermentation at room temperature for 72 h significantly modified the proximate composition of moringa seeds, increasing carbohydrate content from 53.4% to 61.1% and protein content from 18.9% to 21.2%, whereas lipid (13.4%–14.0%) and dietary fiber (5.0%–6.2%) levels remained comparable to those of raw moringa seeds (Ijarotimi et al. 2013). Although fermentation increased amino acid content in moringa seeds, lysine and methionine remained the limiting amino acids. Similar results were reported by Aoki et al. (2023), who used two Rhizopus species (e.g., Rhizopus oligosporus and Rhizopus stolonifer) to ferment moringa seeds at 32°C for 45 and 70 h, respectively. The free amino acid content in moringa seeds fermented with R. oligosporus for 45 h (5.5 mg/g DW) was approximately three times higher than that of unfermented seeds (1.9 mg/g DW), whereas fermentation with R. stolonifer resulted in a similar free amino acid content (2.1 mg/g DW). This could be attributed to the much higher proteolytic activity of R. oligosporus compared to R. stolonifer (Baumann and Bisping 1995). Additionally, fermentation significantly reduced the monounsaturated FAs content in moringa seeds from 13.5% to 8.5%, while increasing the PUFAs content from 59.0% to 62.4%, with linoleic acid, the dominant PUFA, rising from 58.8% to 62.1% (Ijarotimi et al. 2013). Fermentation also enhanced the digestibility and palatability of moringa seeds by reducing their antinutrient content, with tannins decreasing from 241.7 to 146.7 mg/100 g DW, phytates from 78.3 to 28.3 mg/100 g DW, and saponins from 9.8 to 7.5 mg/100 g DW (Ijarotimi et al. 2013). Notably, fermentation of moringa seeds with R. stolonifer at 28°C for 72 h resulted in an even greater reduction in antinutrients, with tannins decreasing by 92.3%, phytates by 88.2%, saponins by 78.3%, and oxalates by 61.3% (Adedayo et al. 2018).

The effect of fermentation on TPC in moringa seeds varies, possibly depending on the inoculated microbial species. The phenolic content in moringa seeds sharply decreased by 42.5% after natural fermentation at room temperature for 72 h (Ijarotimi et al. 2013). However, fermentation of moringa seeds with either R. oligosporus or R. stolonifer at 32°C for 70 h resulted in approximately a 4‐fold increase in TPC, along with enhanced antioxidant capacity, compared to unfermented seeds (Aoki et al. 2023). Co‐culturing Aspergillus oryzae and Aspergillus niger (at a 1:2 ratio) at 30°C for 3 days resulted in a TPC approximately 12 times higher than that of non‐sterile, fermented moringa seed flour (Puspitasari et al. 2024). The elevated antioxidant capacity could be due to the released phenolic compounds bound to cellular structures and antioxidant peptides derived from protein hydrolyzates of moringa seeds (Liang et al. 2020).

Additionally, fermentation is an effective biotechnique to reduce the bitterness of moringa seeds by using appropriate microorganisms that produce myrosinase to degrade GSLs. For example, Puspitasari et al. (2024) investigated the effect of mono‐ and co‐cultures of A. oryzae and A. niger on the bitterness of moringa seeds after being incubated at 30°C for 3 days. It was observed that during fermentation, A. oryzae and A. niger significantly enhanced myrosinase activity from 0.198 U/g DW in non‐sterile, unfermented moringa seed flour to a maximum of 0.887 U/g DW in autoclave‐sterilized samples and 0.617 U/g DW in UV‐sterilized samples. This increased myrosinase activity corresponded to a sharp reduction in GSL content by up to 51.4% and 30.4%, respectively (Puspitasari et al. 2024). The substantial reduction in GSLs could contribute to a reduced bitter taste of moringa seeds and expand their use for development of novel food formulations.

Applications of Moringa Seeds and Sprouts in Functional Food Formulations

Germination is one of the most dynamic stages in the growth of moringa trees. As discussed, it can not only break down macromolecules (e.g., carbohydrates, proteins, and lipids) in moringa seeds into smaller substances that are more easily to be digested and absorbed by human body but also reduce antinutrient factors (e.g., phytin, tannins, and saponins) and upregulate certain bioactive compounds (e.g., GABA, phenolic compounds, and GSLs) with enhanced health‐promoting properties. These beneficial aspects may promote moringa seeds and/or sprouts as emerging dietary supplements and innovative bioingredients for functional food formulations. Moringa seeds and sprouts are commonly used in the form of flour that is incorporated into foods, serving as an alternative source of nutrients. To produce moringa seed or sprout flour, seeds are typically cleaned, dehulled, and dried, whereas sprouts are harvested after a defined germination period and then dried and milled into a fine powder (Coello et al. 2022; Liu, Zhang, et al. 2024). Of the various drying techniques available (e.g., freeze drying, oven drying, and sun drying), freeze drying is particularly recommended for preserving the nutritional and bioactive properties of moringa‐based ingredients. Moringa seed and sprout flours have both been incorporated into baked food products, including cakes, bread, and cookies. For example, partial replacement of wheat flour with moringa sprout flour can modify the pasting properties of the composite flour with reduced viscosity, making it desirable for cake preparation (Chinma et al. 2014). The cakes with up to 30% replacement of sprouted moringa flour exhibited favorable aroma and taste. Moreover, the addition of moringa sprout flour can remarkably increase the nutritional value of the manufactured cakes in terms of protein and mineral proportions, while exerting negligible influence upon color preference. Moringa seed flour has also been successfully incorporated as a nutritional ingredient into wheat flour‐based bread and cookies with acceptable organoleptic properties (Bolarinwa et al. 2019; Ogunsina et al. 2011). Interestingly, it can also be used as beef replacer in the production of meat patties (Al‐Juhaimi et al. 2016). The introduction of moringa seed flour not only improves textural properties (e.g., springiness and chewiness) of the formulated patties but also protects them against rancidity and bacterial growth during cold storage.

Apart from their applications in bakery products, the enrichment of pasta products with moringa seed and sprout flour has also been reported (Coello et al. 2021). Supplementation of pasta with moringa sprout flour enhances its nutritious quality and increases the levels of bioactive compounds, such as thiamine, riboflavin, GABA, and GSLs (Coello et al. 2021). As one of the most widely consumed food products globally, pasta fortified with moringa sprout flour may be marketed as a novel functional food to help combat malnutrition and related diseases, particularly in underdeveloped countries and regions. In another study, Liu et al. (2025) investigated the effects of incorporating moringa seed and sprout flours on the physicochemical properties of noodle doughs and the overall quality of fortified noodles. The results showed that flour supplementation favorably enhanced the dough microstructure and improved the cooking quality and textural characteristics of the noodles. Additionally, the introduction of glucomoringin and moringin contributed to enhanced bioactivity, making the fortified noodles a promising functional food (Liu et al. 2025). Moringa seed flour has also been exploited in the production of functional buffalo yoghurt with enhanced antimicrobial activity against various pathogenic bacteria, including Listeria monocytogenes, Escherichia coli, Salmonella spp., and Staphylococcus aureus (Dhawi et al. 2020). It is noteworthy that moringa seed flour acts as a fermentable substrate and benefits the growth of Lactobacillus bulgaricus and Streptococcus thermophilus in yoghurt manufacturing. The addition of moringa seed flour also improved antioxidant capacity of the fortified yoghurt with good sensory acceptance.

Moringa seeds and sprouts have also been supplemented at various concentrations for manufacturing healthy and ready‐to‐eat snack bars with acceptable texture and odor (Coello et al. 2022). Moringa sprout flour enhances the content of proteins, unsaturated FAs, micronutrients, GABA, and GSLs (e.g., glucomoringin, glucosinalbin, and glucotropaeolin) in the formulated snack bars as compared to the control group. The presence of these bioactive compounds in the snack bar formulation may make it an attractive food product because it can be consumed to momentarily satisfy hunger while providing nutritional and health benefits.

Overall, moringa seeds and sprouts are a promising bioingredient for functional food formulations. Their incorporation into food products, including breads, cakes, cookies, noodles, and snacks, not only enhances their nutritional value but also provides an alternative for vegans/vegetarians to consume sufficient proteins. In the case of meat and milk products, the addition of moringa seeds and sprouts may also improve the antioxidant and antimicrobial capacity of the products with high quality and extended shelf life. However, adding high concentrations of moringa seeds and sprouts can lead to bitter and astringent taste, negatively impacting the overall acceptance of the final products. Herein, more relevant studies are necessary to identify the taste‐evoked compounds (e.g., phenolics, GSLs, and ITCs) and elucidate their formation mechanism. Some effective strategies should be constructed, from cultivation practices to innovative food processing technologies, to reduce or even eliminate the perception of the unpleasant taste.

Opportunities and Challenges of Moringa Seeds and Sprouts

Moringa seeds and sprouts have a great nutritional value and contain a variety of bioactive compounds, particularly glucomoringin and moringin, with health‐promoting properties. These nutritional properties and beneficial health effects make moringa sprouts a promising functional food or a novel bioingredient for functional food formulations. Critically, moringin extracted and purified from moringa seeds and/or sprouts may be used as a novel chemotherapeutic agent due to its potent chemopreventive activity against a broad range of cancers, as reported in numerous in vivo studies.

However, there are still some major concerns regarding moringa seeds and sprouts that are required to be further investigated, ranging from upregulation of bioactive compounds through abiotic and biotic elicitors to validation of safety of glucomoringin and moringin in clinical studies. The challenges are listed as follows: Seed germination is a complex biological process influenced by both internal (e.g., seed viability and hormonal balance) and external factors (e.g., temperature, moisture, light exposure, and oxygen availability). These factors may affect not only germination rate but also the activation of metabolic pathways responsible for the synthesis of secondary metabolites, such as phenolic compounds and GSLs. However, how these variables influence both germination performance of moringa seeds and phytochemical accumulation during germination remains underexplored. Therefore, further research is warranted to comprehensively evaluate the impact of these internal and/or external sprouting conditions on the germination dynamics and bioactive compounds in moringa seeds and sprouts. For example, the composition of both free and bound phenolic compounds in moringa seeds and sprouts can be determined using high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). The dynamic changes in phenolic profile during germination may contribute to the enhanced antioxidant capacity of moringa sprouts, as discussed in Section 4.2. In addition to profiling phenolic compounds in moringa seeds and sprouts, the effects of germination conditions on GSLs in moringa sprouts can be evaluated using HPLC. By optimizing germination parameters, it may be possible to produce moringa sprouts enriched with both phenolic compounds and GSLs, thereby promoting their application as a novel functional food/ingredient.The precise metabolic pathways of glucomoringin in moringa sprouts remain poorly understood, and the bioavailability of moringin upon consumption has yet to be fully elucidated. Further research is needed to clarify the biosynthetic mechanism of glucomoringin, which could support strategies to enhance its accumulation in moringa sprouts by targeting key metabolic enzymes and regulatory genes involved in its biosynthesis. Integrated metabolomic and transcriptomic approaches may be used to identify the intermediate compounds and candidate genes associated with glucomoringin biosynthetic pathways. For example, ultraperformance liquid chromatography quadrupole time‐of‐flight mass spectrometry (UPLC‐QTOF MS) provides a reliable platform for the identification of plant metabolites and can be used to characterize candidate intermediates involved in glucomoringin metabolism. Isotope‐labeling techniques may further validate the proposed glucomoringin biosynthetic pathways derived from untargeted metabolomic analysis. Key genes implicated in glucomoringin biosynthesis can be identified through de novo transcriptomic analysis and subsequently validated using quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis.Both abiotic and biotic elicitors can induce physiological and biochemical changes in plants, potentially diversifying the phytochemical composition and enhancing the accumulation of bioactive compounds. Elicitors like amino acids, sugars, and slightly acidic electrolyzed water (SAEW) have been used for modulating GSL composition during edible seed germination (Baenas et al. 2014; Li et al. 2018). For example, Baenas et al. (2014) investigated the effects of several elicitors (e.g., glucose, sucrose, methionine, methyl jasmonate, jasmonic acid, and salicylic acid) on the GSL profile of GSL‐rich species, including Brassica oleraceae (broccoli), Brassica napus (rutabaga cabbage), Brassica rapa (turnip), and Raphanus sativus (China rose radish and red radish). The results indicated that methyl jasmonate, jasmonic acid, and sugars were effective in increasing the content of total GSLs in Brassicaceae sprouts (Baenas et al. 2014). Additionally, SAEW treatment markedly increased the content of glucoraphanin, which is the predominant GSL in broccoli sprouts, by promoting methionine metabolism and enhancing myrosinase activity (Li et al. 2018). However, the influence of these elicitors on GSL profile in moringa sprouts remains poorly documented. Further extensive research is warranted to systematically investigate the impact of these elicitors on GSL metabolism in moringa sprouts. These elicitors can be applied either during soaking or sprayed during germination to investigate their potential impact on the GSL composition. The dynamic changes of GSLs in moringa sprouts can be monitored through UPLC‐QTOF‐MS‐based untargeted metabolomic analysis. The potential biosynthetic pathways of GSLs stimulated by elicitors could be proposed based on the tentatively identified metabolites. The content of glucomoringin and moringin in moringa seeds and sprouts can be quantified using HPLC. Furthermore, the impact of elicitors on the myrosinase activity can be determined using glucose assays.Moringa seeds and sprouts are rich in glucomoringin and moringin with various biological activities. Although the mechanisms of moringa seeds and their extracts as a protective agent against some chronic diseases have been well evidenced, these bioactivities are mostly demonstrated based on animal models. Clinical trials assessing the efficacy of moringa seeds, sprouts, and their derivative products (e.g., extracts and formulated supplements) in treating chronic diseases remain underexplored. Therefore, further human intervention studies are needed to gain a deeper understanding of their beneficial effects on human health, as well as to assess their cytotoxicity and safety after consumption.Despite the observed nutritional improvements after germination, moringa sprouts are likely to constitute only a small portion of the average consumers’ diet and may therefore have a limited impact on overall nutrient intake. To increase their health outcomes, future work could explore the potential of fortifying staple foods with moringa sprout extracts or concentrates to enhance bioavailability and nutritional contribution without the need for large‐quantity consumption. As discussed in Section 7, the incorporation of moringa seeds and sprouts improves the nutritional value of manufactured food products and introduces bioactive compounds, including glucomoringin and moringin. However, the resulting pungent and bitter taste significantly impaired the overall sensory acceptability of fortified foods. This unfavorable taste notably restricts their commercial potential in functional food formulations. Therefore, research on mitigating or even eliminating the unfavorable attributes of moringa seeds and sprouts while preserving or enhancing nutritional quality by applying some advanced processing techniques and/or modifying the substitution ratio needs to be carried out.

Further research is needed to clarify the biosynthetic mechanism of glucomoringin, which could support strategies to enhance its accumulation in moringa sprouts by targeting key metabolic enzymes and regulatory genes involved in its biosynthesis. Integrated metabolomic and transcriptomic approaches may be used to identify the intermediate compounds and candidate genes associated with glucomoringin biosynthetic pathways. For example, ultraperformance liquid chromatography quadrupole time‐of‐flight mass spectrometry (UPLC‐QTOF MS) provides a reliable platform for the identification of plant metabolites and can be used to characterize candidate intermediates involved in glucomoringin metabolism. Both abiotic and biotic elicitors can induce physiological and biochemical changes in plants, potentially diversifying the phytochemical composition and enhancing the accumulation of bioactive compounds. Elicitors like amino acids, sugars, and slightly acidic electrolyzed water (SAEW) have been used for modulating GSL composition during edible seed germination (Baenas et al. 2014; Li et al. 2018). For example, Baenas et al. (2014) investigated the effects of several elicitors (e.g., glucose, sucrose, methionine, methyl jasmonate, jasmonic acid, and salicylic acid) on the GSL profile of GSL‐rich species, including Brassica oleraceae (broccoli), Brassica napus (rutabaga cabbage), Brassica rapa (turnip), and Raphanus sativus (China rose radish and red radish). The results indicated that methyl jasmonate, jasmonic acid, and sugars were effective in increasing the content of total GSLs in Brassicaceae sprouts (Baenas et al. 2014). Additionally, SAEW treatment markedly increased the content of glucoraphanin, which is the predominant GSL in broccoli sprouts, by promoting methionine metabolism and enhancing myrosinase activity (Li et al. 2018). However, the influence of these elicitors on GSL profile in moringa sprouts remains poorly documented. Further extensive research is warranted to systematically investigate the impact of these elicitors on GSL metabolism in moringa sprouts. These elicitors can be applied either during soaking or sprayed during germination to investigate their potential impact on the GSL composition. The potential biosynthetic pathways of GSLs stimulated by elicitors could be proposed based on the tentatively identified metabolites. Furthermore, the impact of elicitors on the myrosinase activity can be determined using glucose assays.

Moringa seeds and sprouts are rich in glucomoringin and moringin with various biological activities. Although the mechanisms of moringa seeds and their extracts as a protective agent against some chronic diseases have been well evidenced, these bioactivities are mostly demonstrated based on animal models. Clinical trials assessing the efficacy of moringa seeds, sprouts, and their derivative products (e.g., extracts and formulated supplements) in treating chronic diseases remain underexplored. Therefore, further human intervention studies are needed to gain a deeper understanding of their beneficial effects on human health, as well as to assess their cytotoxicity and safety after consumption.

To increase their health outcomes, future work could explore the potential of fortifying staple foods with moringa sprout extracts or concentrates to enhance bioavailability and nutritional contribution without the need for large‐quantity consumption. However, on the basis of the sensory evaluation results of fortified noodles (Liu et al. 2025), the substitution ratio of both ungerminated and germinated moringa seed flour for wheat flour can be adjusted to less than 5% on a DW basis to better assess its impact on noodle quality and consumer acceptability. The content of bioactive compounds, particularly glucomoringin and moringin in both raw and cooked noodles, can be determined using HPLC. Sensory evaluation can be conducted to evaluate the sensory attributes of fortified noodles, along with the overall acceptability.

Another technique that can be adopted in future studies is fermentation, which is a promising biological treatment that may reduce the GSL content. Fungal strains such as Aspergillus genus (e.g., A. oryzae and A. niger) are known to produce myrosinase, a key enzyme that hydrolyzes GSLs, thereby reducing bitterness in fermented moringa seeds (Puspitasari et al. 2024). However, the efficacy of fermentation when applied to moringa seeds and sprouts remains underexplored, necessitating further investigation. For instance, fermentation conditions such as the inoculation ratio of A. oryzae to A. niger need to be optimized to enhance myrosinase activity and GSL degradation efficiency, while simultaneously promoting the accumulation of other bioactive compounds, such as phenolics. The fermented moringa seeds and sprouts can be subsequently incorporated into traditional staple foods like noodles, and their sensory acceptability can be evaluated using quantitative descriptive analysis (QDA). The content of bioactive compounds, such as glucomoringin, moringin, and phenolic compounds, can be determined using HPLC. The impact of incorporation of fermented moringa seeds and sprouts on the viscoelastic properties of noodle doughs can be determined using a rheometer. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) can be utilized to investigate the impact of ungerminated or germinated moringa seed flour addition on the surface morphology of fortified noodles, and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) can be employed to visualize the interactions between the gluten network and starch granules. Both techniques provide valuable insights into the microstructural characteristics that influence key quality attributes such as cooking loss.

Additionally, roasting is another viable method for decreasing bitterness of moringa seeds, while simultaneously generating favorable sensory attributes such as nutty and roasted flavors (Liu, Chin, Huang, et al. 2024; Liu, Chin, Sun, et al. 2024). However, different roasting degrees may lead to either elevated or reduced levels of glucomoringin in roasted moringa seeds (Liu, Chin, Huang, et al. 2024). Therefore, roasting conditions of moringa seeds and sprouts need to be optimized for significantly reducing bitterness while enhancing the nutritional value of the seeds and sprouts. Besides, the combination of fermentation and roasting treatments may exert a synergistic effect on mitigating the bitterness of moringa seeds and sprouts. The sequence of these treatments (e.g., roasting before fermentation vs. fermentation before roasting) may influence the overall efficacy of bitterness reduction and warrants systematic investigation. Conclusion

Moringa seeds are a rich source of essential nutrients and bioactive compounds, such as phenolic compounds, glucomoringin, and moringin. These phytochemicals are closely associated with promising therapeutic effects, including anticancer, antidiabetic, and hepatoprotective activities. Germination is an effective biotechnique that can further enhance the nutritional value of moringa seeds through improving protein digestibility and bioavailability and reducing the levels of antinutrients, such as phytates, tannins, saponins, oxalates, and trypsin inhibitors. Additionally, germination stimulates the biosynthesis of these health‐promoting compounds, thereby augmenting the functional value of moringa seeds. Given their high nutritional value and health benefits, moringa seeds and sprouts enriched with bioactive compounds hold great potential for direct use as functional foods or as novel bioingredients in the development of functional food formulations.

However, several challenges still constrain the potential applications of moringa seeds, sprouts, and their extracts as functional foods and nutraceuticals. For example, although germination elevated the levels of phytochemicals, particularly GSLs, they may contribute to undesirable sensory attributes such as bitterness and astringency, potentially reducing consumer acceptance. Therefore, advanced processing techniques are required to mitigate or even eliminate these sensory drawbacks while preserving or enhancing nutritional quality. Fermentation, for example, is a promising biological treatment that may reduce the GSL content. Fungal strains from the Aspergillus genus (e.g., A. oryzae and A. niger) are known to produce myrosinase, a key enzyme that hydrolyzes GSLs, thereby reducing bitterness in fermented moringa seeds. Roasting is another viable method for decreasing bitterness while simultaneously generating favorable sensory attributes such as nutty and roasted flavors. However, the efficacy of these processing techniques when applied to moringa sprouts remains underexplored, necessitating further investigation. For example, response surface methodology can be applied to optimize the roasting parameters (e.g., temperature and duration) for maximizing glucomoringin content while enhancing the flavor of moringa sprouts. The flavor profile of roasted sprouts can be assessed using gas chromatography–MS (GC–MS), which may also help to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the formation of desirable flavors. Furthermore, the incorporation of moringa seeds and sprouts as a bioingredient into traditional staple foods like noodles, bread, and pasta offers a dual advantage of masking bitterness and improving the nutritional and functional quality of the final products. However, biochemical modifications during germination can influence the physicochemical and techno‐functional properties of moringa sprout flour. Therefore, parameters, such as pasting behavior, rheological characteristics, and textural attributes, should be thoroughly evaluated to optimize the quality and performance of moringa‐enriched food formulations. Additionally, although numerous animal studies have demonstrated the health‐promoting properties of moringa seeds and their extracts, clinical evidence in humans remains unexplored. Well‐designed human intervention studies are therefore needed to evaluate their safety and efficacy. In the case of fresh consumption as microgreens, non‐thermal treatments, like high‐pressure processing, pulsed electric fields, and cold plasma, may be applied to enhance food safety and sensory properties while preserving nutritional integrity of moringa sprouts.
