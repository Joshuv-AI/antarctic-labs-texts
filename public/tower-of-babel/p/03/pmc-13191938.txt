Post-harvest losses of perishable vegetables and their contribution to household dietary gaps in Kamonyi District, Rwanda
Authors: Gisubizo Fabien, Dushimimana Olive, Dushimimana Anicet, John Atsu Agbolosoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost-harvest losses (PHL) of perishable vegetables have been a major problem that threatens food security and nutritional outcomes in sub- Saharan Africa. Smallholder farmers also incur heavy losses during the peak harvesting seasons in Rwanda, especially of fruits with high perishability. This research paper examined the extent of post-harvest losses of tomatoes, leafy greens, and carrots, as well as the role played by these foods in the household dietary deficiencies of Kamonyi District.MethodsMeasures: The cross-sectional study was done in all of the 12 sectors of the Kamonyi District in the period between November 2024 and January 2026. The Yamane formula was used to do a systematic sampling of 384 smallholder vegetable farmers. The data were collected on physical loss data and nutritional quality deterioration data, household dietary diversity scores (HDDS), and vitamin A/C intake data. A controlled experiment was used to test the effectiveness of aloe vera-honey coating as an effective preservation.ResultsIt found out that tomatoes, leafy greens, and carrots had an average of 34.7 percent, 28.3 percent, and 19.6 percent post-harvest losses, respectively. During the high harvest period, tomato prices decreased by 67.2 percent, and the field damage amounted to 18.4 percent. The loss-making families of high PHL (>30) had a significantly lower HDDS (4.2 ± 1.3) in comparison with loss-making families of low (<15) PHL (6.8± 1.1) (p < 0.001). The deficiency in the level of vitamin A intake was established at 62.4 percent in high PHL households compared to 31.2 percent of low PHL households. Aloe vera-honey was applied to vegetable tomatoes to extend shelf life by 158 per cent (18.6 days) and preserve 89.3 per cent of vitamin C in the samples (52.1 in the untreated samples).ConclusionNutritional shortages and deficiencies in dietary habits are both significant contributors in the Kamonyi District because post-harvest losses are significant. Low costs of preservation techniques, such as coating with aloe vera-honey, have a lot of potential in terms of loss reduction and improvement of the nutritional security of small-holding farming families.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27214-6.Keywords: Dietary diversity, Vitamin A deficiency, Aloe vera coating, Smallholder farmers, Rwanda

Background

Fruits and vegetables Post-harvest losses (PHL) are one of the most severe problems of agricultural systems in sub-Saharan Africa, where fruits and vegetables are estimated to suffer post-harvest losses of 40–50% between harvest and consumption [1, 2]. These losses not only weaken the economic sustainability of smallholder farming but also contribute to the issue of food insecurity and nutritional deficit among the vulnerable groups [3, 4]. In Rwanda, where agricultural activities contribute approximately 63% of the labor force and 33% of the gross domestic product, it has been noted that reducing post-harvest losses is a national priority under the National Strategy of Transformation [5]. Vegetables that are prone to post-harvest degradation include perishable ones such as tomatoes, leafy greens, and carrots, which contain high amounts of moisture, have high metabolic rates, and are vulnerable to mechanical damage [6, 7]. In the East African countries, tomatoes (Solanum lycopersicum) suffer losses of between 25 and 42% with poor handling, and problem with transportation, among other factors, and the absence of cold chain facilities [8, 9].

The leafy crops (amaranth, Amaranthus spp., and African nightshade, Solanum scabrum) are even more susceptible, as they have been reported to have suffered losses of 30–50% in 48 h after harvest when ambient conditions were used [10, 11]. Nutritional consequences of post-harvest losses are not limited to a reduction in the quantity. The processes of deterioration lead to considerable loss of essential micronutrients, especially vitamins A and C, that are vital to the immune system, good vision, and overall health [12, 13].

Research has shown that the concentration of vitamin C in tomatoes may decrease by half or 70% in just a week of storing under non-optimal storage conditions [14, 15]. Specifically, this loss of nutrients is especially alarming in the situation when the vegetable intake is already lower than the recommended values, and it may precondition the further aggravation of the micronutrient deficiencies [12, 16]. Kamonyi District, which is situated in the Southern Province of Rwanda, is also a major vegetable production region serving the local markets and the capital city, Kigali [17, 18]. The 12 sectors in the district are vastly involved in the cultivation of vegetables, where tomatoes constitute the major crop, followed by leafy vegetables and carrots. But when the harvest times are at their highest, the farmers are confronted with a twofold threat: the market prices are at their lowest point due to excessive supply, and large amounts of produce are ruined with field damage and after-harvest damage [19, 20]. The result of this up-and-down cycle of plenty and poverty is that there is food insecurity even with high production levels. The correlation between losses after harvests and household dietary diversity has become an issue of growing interest in recent literature. A validated proxy measure of food accessibility and nutritional sufficiency, the Household Dietary Diversity Score (HDDS), has been found to have a negative relation with PHL in a number of African settings [21, 22]. When households suffer a high loss after the harvest, they tend to decrease the diversity of their consumption, increase their reliance on staple products, and abstain from using nutrient-rich non-perishable vegetables [23]. This dysfunctional habit continues to form a circle of nutritional insufficiency that disproportionately targets women and children [24, 25]. New technologies of low-cost preservation provide opportunities in the case of resource-starved smallholder farmers. Aloe vera ( Aloe barbadensis Miller ) and honey are among the natural materials that have been used to produce edible coatings that have been shown to have an extension effect on the shelf life of food products without altering their nutritional value.

The aloe vera gel has antimicrobial compounds, and it creates a semi-permeable barrier that slows down the rate of respiration and transpiration [26, 27]. The coating is used together with honey, which is naturally antibacterial and hygroscopic, giving the coating greater resistance to microbial spoilage and loss of moisture [28, 29]. In rural settings, the technologies are especially suitable since in these locations, raw materials may be available locally, it is cheap, and no specialized equipment may be needed. Although the role of post-harvest losses in Rwanda is acknowledged, there is still a lack of empirical information on the measurement of losses on a sector level and their association with household nutritional outcomes. Earlier research has been mainly centered on aggregate national estimates or value chains of particular commodities, without researching the overlaps of physical losses, loss in nutritional value, and dietary variety [30, 31].

In addition, locally-adapted preservation technologies have not systematically been considered in the Rwandan situation. The proposed study about these knowledge gaps was to: (a) quantify physical and nutritional losses of tomatoes, leafy greens, and carrots along smallholder value chains in Kamonyi District; (b) determine the relationship between the level of losses after harvest and household dietary diversity in terms of evolution and intake of nutrients; and (c) test the effectiveness of aloe vera-honey coatings in enhancing the shelf life and nutrient levels. The results can be used in evidence-based policy and intervention development to reduce post-harvest loss and improve nutrition in Rwanda and other such situations.

Methods

Study area and design

The study was a cross-sectional study that took place in Kamonyi District, located in the Southern Province of Rwanda, between November 2024 and January 2026. Kamonyi District was purposely chosen because it had an important role in the production of vegetables in Rwanda, and post-harvest losses were recorded during the peak harvest periods. The district is subdivided into 12 administrative sectors: Gacurabwenge, Karama, Kayenzi, Kayumbu, Mugina, Musambira, Ngamba, Nyamiyaga, Nyarubaka, Rugarika, Rukoma, and Runda. The 12 sectors were covered in the study to guarantee that different agro-ecological environments and farming techniques of the district were well covered.

Sampling procedure

The sampling was conducted in several stages. To begin with, every 12 sectors of Kamonyi District were covered. Second, in every sector, agricultural extension officer records were used to identify villages (umudugudu) where there was a high level of vegetable farming. Third, the allocation of the number to be sampled in each sector was done proportionally based on the relative number of vegetable farmers (Table 1). Fourth, individual farmers were selected using systematic random sampling based on lists of households in the villages, and every kth household was selected when k = N/n per sector.

Data collection instruments and procedures

A structured questionnaire [33] was used to collect the data because the participants were interviewed in person and in Kinyarwanda. The questionnaire had four modules, which included (1) socio-demographic characteristics; (2) vegetable production and post-harvest handling practices; (3) post-harvest loss assessment, and (4) household dietary diversity assessment. This questionnaire was pre-tested on 30 farmers in the adjacent Muhanga District and edited using comments. The harvest losses were measured through the load tracking method [20], where farmers reported the amount of harvest harvested, sold, consumed, gifted, and lost at every point in the value chain (field, transport, storage, and market). Physical losses were also given in percentage of the harvest. The field damage was gauged by field observation of 10 randomly chosen tomato plots per sector at the time of harvest. The quality of nutritional assessment of subsamples of tomatoes, leafy greens, and carrots was determined at harvest and storage durations of 3, 7, and 14 days. The vitamin C level was measured by the 2,6-dichlorophenolindophenol titration method [34, 35], whereas β-carotene (provitamin A) was measured by means of high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) based on AOAC guidelines [36]. Testing was done at the Rwanda Standards Board. Household Dietary Diversity Score (HDDS) was computed according to FAO guidelines, which assesses the dietary diversity of the household on the basis of the recall of the food groups eaten by the household over the past 24 h. The standardised household measures and photographic guides were used as the estimates of the size of portions when the study was conducted on the Rwandan adult population. The Rwanda Food Composition Tables were used to calculate the intakes of nutrients (RFCT, 2020 edition). The risk of vitamin A deficiency was determined as habitual intake that is less than 500 ug RAE/day, and the risk of vitamin C deficiency was less than 45 mg/day according to WHO/FAO (2004) reference levels [36, 37].

Preservation technology evaluation

The efficacy of the aloe vera-honey coating as an inexpensive preservation technology has been tested in a controlled laboratory experiment in the Food Science Laboratory, University of Rwanda, College of Agriculture, Animal Sciences and Veterinary Medicine. The Musambira sector provided fresh tomatoes of standard size and maturity (Roma type, at the stages of mature green to light red), which were randomly allocated to three treatments with the help of a simple randomization table (generated in R, set. seed(2024) (1) control (uncoated); (2) aloe-vera gel coating (100%); and (3) aloe-vera-honey coating (70:30 ratio). In both treatments, there were 60 tomatoes per replicate, and three biological replicates were used (n = 180 tomatoes per treatment) with tomatoes being randomly allocated in each replicate so that there was replicate independence. The unit of the experiment was the individual tomato; the one-way ANOVA comparisons were carried out on batch-level data. The 60 tomatoes were chosen as the sample size on a replicate decision informed by published studies on aloe vera coating post-hoc power analysis (G*Power 3.1; one-way ANOVA, a = 0.05) established the statistical power of the observed effect size (f = 0.68) to be greater than 0.99. Aloe barbadensis was used to prepare the gel of aloe vera according to the procedure of fresh leaves of the plant [26, 38]. A mixture of pure bee honey (70: 30 v/v) was added to the gel, and tomatoes were dipped in it and dried. Samples of both treatments and controls were kept at ambient temperature (2±- 2 °C) and relative humidity (65 ± 5%). At 0, 7, 14, and 21 days, the measurements of weight loss, firmness (measured with a texture analyzer), visual quality, and vitamin C content were taken.

Statistical analysis

A one-way ANOVA was considered to compare the mean post-harvest loss, HDDS, and nutrient retention across sectors and treatments, and a Tukey HSD post-hoc test was selected to compare the two variables in a one-to-one comparison. Before analysis, ANOVA assumptions (normality by the Shapiro-Wilk test, homogeneity of variance by the Levene test) were checked. The relationship between post-harvest losses and indicators of dietary diversity was determined using the Pearson correlation coefficient. They identified predictors of HDDS using multiple linear regression after adjusting for the confounding variables (age, sex, household size, income, and education). The absence of multicollinearity was acknowledged by the variance inflation factors (VIF < 5); homoscedasticity was observed with the help of residual plots. Chi-square test was used to test the comparisons between categorical variables. The = 0.05 was established as the statistical significance.

Ethical considerations

The University of Rwanda Institutional Review Board provided ethical approval (Reference: UR/IRB/2025/089). In Kinyarwanda, all participants signed informed consent following an explanation of the objectives and procedures in the study. The respondents were asked to participate voluntarily and were assured confidentiality and anonymity. The research was performed in accordance with the postulates of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Efficacy of aloe vera-honey coating

The evaluation of preservation technology is provided in Table 7. The aloe vera-honey coating greatly increased the shelf life of tomatoes by 7.2 days (control) to 18.6 days (158% increased; p < 0.001). The coating also showed better performance regarding the quality parameter than aloe vera gel individually. The percentage of weight loss was cut down by 18.4% in control samples to 6.2% in tomatoes, which were coated with aloe vera-honey after 14 days. The nutrient retention of the vitamin C at day 14 was 89.3 per cent with aloe vera-honey coating and was 52.1 per cent with controls, which is 71.4 per cent better.

Discussion

The study offers broad evidence on the extent of post-harvest losses and their effect on household nutrition in Kamonyi District, Rwanda [39, 40]. The results indicate that losses are large in all three types of vegetables under study, with tomatoes registering the highest losses (34.7%), which is in line with regional losses in East Africa [41, 42]. These differences at the sector level demonstrate the significance of interventions associated with localities since the availability of roads, storage facilities, and closeness to a market, among others, have a great impact on the pattern of losses. The situation of extreme changes in prices at times of harvest, with farmgate prices decreasing on average by 67.2%, is a paradoxical scenario when abundance at the farm level does not convert to better food security or economic outcomes for the farmers. The observation is in line with the results of Tefera [43], who reported comparable price volatility of the Ethiopian vegetable markets across seasons.

The low prices and high field damage (18.4% tomatoes) have a disincentive effect on the handling of harvests, which may increase the amount of losses in a negative feedback mechanism. A negative correlation of post-harvest loss and household dietary diversity (r = -0.67) is also the most important finding with important policy implications. Homes that had high losses showed significantly reduced HDDS (4.2 vs. 6.8 in low-loss homes) and were below the cut-off point of sufficient dietary diversity [44]. Post-harvest losses were the best independent predictor of dietary diversity (unstandardized b = -0.068, standardized b = -0.412, p = 0.001) after the fishing study compared to other socioeconomic confounding factors, indicating that every one-percentage-point increase in post-harvest losses was linked to a 0.068-point decrease in HDDS. The healthy properties of the diet do not depend on the amount of food taken, but also on the quality of the food. Our laboratory findings showed that vitamin C was lost greatly when tomatoes were kept in ambient conditions since tomatoes retained only 43.2 of their original vitamin C contents after 14 days. These results are consistent with the findings of researchers, who also found that there were similar degradation patterns of stored vegetables. Since most rural households in Rwanda rely on vegetables as the main source of vitamin C, the cumulative impact of physical losses and degradation of nutrients poses an additional threat of micronutrient deficiency. The prevalence of vitamin A deficiency risk is higher in high-loss households (62.5) than in low-loss households (31.6): this is in accordance with other sub-Saharan African settings. Similar relationships between food accessibility and micronutrient deficiency were reported in Rwanda. Our study findings add to this body of knowledge by numerically estimating the contribution of post-harvest losses to this nutritional gap, and these findings indicate that post-harvest losses can be significantly decreased without requiring more production and that these decreases correlate with a better dietary diversity of households. This cross-sectional design cannot allow causal inference, however. The fact that the aloe vera-honey coating can provide a high shelf life (158%) and a high amount of vitamin C (89.3 per cent) is a good step in the right direction to make the aloe vera-honey coating an effective solution to the resource-restrained environment of smallholder farming. These results are also similar to those found on an aloe-based coating [45, 46] and prove to be better in performance based on the synergistic behaviour of this compound that combines with honey.

Such a combination seems to offer added antimicrobial protection, as well as enhanced moisture barrier properties, based on the much lower occurrence of decay (8.4% versus 58.3% in controls). This technology has good cost implications to adopt. Raw materials ( aloe vera leaves and local honey) and local honey) are generally found in Kamonyi District, and there are initial estimates based on local input prices of the treatment that it might cost about 50 RWF/kg of tomatoes but no formal cost benefit analysis was carried out in this work and a rigorous economic analysis is to be carried out before any quantitative recommendations to policy makers. The practice scale-up will require expansion of this technology too: the variability of the quality of aloe vera gel that grows in the fields, the variability of the composition of local honey, the simplification of application protocols that can be adapted to the field, and the lack of cold chain infrastructure. The further effort must be directed towards designing application manuals that are friendly to the farmers, and the transfer of technology should be reviewed by use of the RAB extension network. Policy implications of these findings are clear, too: reduction of post-harvest losses should be officially included in the National Strategy of Transformation (NST2) of Rwanda; RAB extension services should be accompanied by the post-harvest management training; and the input aid to aloe vera planting should be taken into consideration as a low-cost intervention in the form of public health nutrition. The development of this technology will require efforts on standardizing the quality and form capacity between groups of farmers. This study has several limitations that one would know. The weakness is that, first, the cross-sectional design cannot be used to make causal conclusions on the relationship between post-harvest losses and dietary outcomes. Second, the dietary diversity data were anchored on the recall method, which is likely to be subject to reporting bias. Thirdly, the preservation technology testing was held under laboratory conditions, and in the field, it may be practiced differently. Finally, the experiment was conducted during a single harvest season, and annual variability of weather conditions might also affect the applicability of estimates of losses.

Conclusions

This Study indicates that post harvest losses of fresh agricultural commodities, especially perishable veggies, are a major cause of dietary and micronutrient deficiencies in smallholder farming families in Kamonyi District in Rwanda. Tomatoes, leafy greens, and carrots suffer a 34.7% loss, a 28.3% loss, and a 19.6% loss, respectively, leading to a high volume of nutritious food being left on the farm without reaching the consumers in an appropriate fashion, even when the levels of production are high. The effect on nutritional security is highlighted by the fact that there is a strong correlation in the negative direction between post-harvest losses and household dietary diversity. The Aloe vera-honey coating technology is very promising in terms of losses, and the nutritional value has been preserved. This is an inexpensive, locally available technology that extends the shelf life by 158% and preserves 89.3% of vitamin C, and this needs to be increased through agricultural extension activities. Reduction of post-harvest loss must be identified as a complementary measure to intensifying production towards the realization of food and nutrition security objectives. The study suggests: (1) incorporating post-harvest management training in agricultural extension programs; (2) encouraging aloe vera-honey coating and other preservation technologies; (3) endorsing collective marketing among farmers to avoid the decline in prices due to over supply; (4) investing in the installation of targeted cold chain in collection and market hub locations, and (5) full-scale trials of the low-cost preservation technologies in practice in qualitative and quantitative smallholder contexts by formal economic analysis.
