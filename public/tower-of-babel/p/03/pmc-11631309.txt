Clinical phenotypes of heart failure patients with supranormal ejection fraction
Authors: Liyan Huang, Jiayu Feng, Mei Zhai, Yan Huang, Qiong Zhou, Yuhui Zhang, Jian Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsHeart failure (HF) with supranormal ejection fraction (HFsnEF) represents a distinct clinical entity characterized by limited treatment options and an unfavourable prognosis. Revealing its phenotypic diversity is crucial for understanding disease mechanism and optimizing patient management. We aim to identify phenotypic subgroups in HFsnEF using unsupervised clustering analysis.MethodsConsecutive hospitalized patients with a diagnosis of HF and a left ventricular ejection fraction ≥65% at baseline echocardiographic evaluations were included for analysis. We conducted unsupervised hierarchical clustering analysis on principal components (HCPC) to identify HFsnEF phenogroups using mixed data variables including demographics, HF duration, vital signs, anthropometrics, smoking/drinking status, HF aetiology, comorbid diseases, laboratory tests and echocardiographic parameters. We then employed decision tree modelling to identify parameters capable of distinguishing distinct clusters. Clinical outcomes, including all‐cause death, cardiovascular (CV) death and CV readmission for different clusters, were examined.ResultsThree mutually exclusive clusters were identified from the cohort of 221 HFsnEF patients. Cluster 1 (52.5%) predominantly consisted of patients with valvular heart disease, who had larger cardiac chambers and a higher prevalence of atrial fibrillation/atrial flutter. Cluster 2 (26.2%) primarily comprised older ischaemic patients with a higher prevalence of metabolic comorbidities. Cluster 3 (21.3%) were mainly hypertrophic cardiomyopathy patients. Two clinical variables were identified that could be used to group all HFsnEF patients into one of the clusters; they were HF aetiology and comorbid diabetes. During the median follow‐up of 53.4 months, 46 (20.8%) all‐cause deaths occurred, among them 39 of CV causes. Seventy (31.7%) patients experienced CV readmissions. Three clusters showed distinct differences in mortality outcomes, with Cluster 1 exhibiting the highest risk of all‐cause mortality [Cluster 1 vs. Cluster 2: adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) = 3.32, P = 0.022; Cluster 1 vs. Cluster 3: aHR = 3.81, P = 0.036; Cluster 2 vs. Cluster 3: aHR = 1.15, P = 0.865] and CV mortality (Cluster 1 vs. Cluster 2: aHR = 3.73, P = 0.022; Cluster 1 vs. Cluster 3: aHR = 4.27, P = 0.020; Cluster 2 vs. Cluster 3: aHR = 1.15, P = 0.870). CV readmission risk was comparable among the three clusters (Cluster 1 vs. Cluster 2: aHR = 0.82, P = 0.590; Cluster 1 vs. Cluster 3: aHR = 1.04, P = 0.900; Cluster 2 vs. Cluster 3: aHR = 1.28, P = 0.580).ConclusionsIn a heterogeneous HFsnEF cohort, three clusters were identified by unsupervised HCPC with distinct clinical characteristics and outcomes.Keywords: clinical outcomes, clustering, heart failure, supranormal ejection fraction

Introduction

Recent studies in heart failure (HF) have observed a U‐shaped relationship between mortality and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). The range of 60%–65% demonstrates the lowest mortality, whereas patients with LVEF > 65% have a worse prognosis. 1 , 2 , 3 Additional studies suggest that patients with LVEF > 60%–65% exhibit differential morphologic features as compared with patients with 50% < LVEF < 60%. 4 , 5 Furthermore, among the four drug classes of guideline‐directed medical therapy (GDMT) for HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF)—renin–angiotensin system inhibitors [angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors (ARNIs), angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers (especially for patients for whom ARNI is not appropriate)], beta‐blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs) and sodium–glucose co‐transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors—many can improve clinical outcomes in patients with an LVEF up to 55%–60%. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 However, except for dapagliflozin, 12 these medications have not demonstrated efficacy in reducing major HF events among patients with LVEF > 60%–65%. Collectively, these findings underscore the need to recognize HF with supranormal ejection fraction (HFsnEF, referred to as HF patients with LVEF ≥ 65%) as a distinct group. Crudely classifying these patients into HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) would lead to a skewed perspective of patient features and inefficient management.

Considering the poor prognosis and lack of effective treatment options, the management of HFsnEF is an issue of great concern. The therapeutic effects of most GDMTs for HFrEF do not extend to HF patients with LVEF > 60%–65%, 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 possibly due to the substantial phenotypic and pathophysiological heterogeneity of the disease, rendering the conventional ‘one‐size‐fits‐all’ approach unsuitable. It is crucial to accurately identify distinct subgroups within HFsnEF, allowing for a better understanding of their possible phenotypic diversity and the exploration of any potential treatment strategies.

Unsupervised machine learning methods like hierarchical clustering can construct clusters within a set of objects without predefined labels 13 and can be used to improve the performance of subsequent supervised methods. This capability makes them widely applied in phenotypic clustering analysis. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18

In light of the above, we endeavour to (1) group the HFsnEF patients into homogeneous and mutually exclusive subgroups by applying the unsupervised machine learning method; (2) create classifiers using the most easily obtainable variables so that every individual can fall into a certain cluster; and (3) characterize the phenotypic diversity of HFsnEF and assess associations between clusters and outcomes.

Methods

Study population

Consecutive hospitalized patients admitted to the Heart Failure Center of Fuwai Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College (CAMS and PUMC), from July 2009 to December 2018 with a clinical diagnosis of HF, aged ≥18 years and with LVEF ≥ 65% at baseline echocardiographic evaluations were included. Despite their diagnosis of HF, all medical records were individually reviewed by three experienced cardiologists (MZ, YH and QZ) to validate their HF diagnosis according to the universal definition of HF, which required the presence of current or prior symptoms and/or signs caused by a structural and/or functional cardiac abnormality, as well as elevated natriuretic peptide levels or objective evidence of cardiogenic congestion. 19 The screening process was detailed in the Study Flow Diagram of the supporting information.

This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional Ethics Committee of Fuwai Hospital (approval number: 2014‐501). Informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Covariates of interest

In this study, 30 variables were considered as candidates for analysis, encompassing a range of domains including demographics, HF duration, vital signs, anthropometrics, smoking/drinking status, HF aetiology, comorbid diseases, laboratory tests and echocardiographic parameters. The pragmatic, easily obtainable and clinically relevant nature of these variables enhanced the applicability of clustering. The list of included variables, variable collection and definition, and variable selection were detailed in the Methods of the supporting information.

Follow‐up and outcomes

Patients were followed up at 1, 6 and 12 months, and every 12 months thereafter, they were followed up through outpatient visits or telephone communication. Information about the occurrence of events was retrieved from medical records and telephone contacts. The primary outcome was all‐cause mortality; secondary outcomes were cardiovascular (CV) death and readmission for CV reasons.

Hierarchical clustering on principal components

The unsupervised hierarchical clustering analysis on principal components (HCPC) 20 was chosen as our clustering method because of the following reasons: (1) HCPC integrates three multivariate data analysis methods, namely, principal component analysis, hierarchical clustering and partitioning clustering, 20 and (2) the factorial analysis on mixed data (FAMD) method was employed to extract the principal components prior to hierarchical clustering in our study. The FAMD method can be harmoniously combined with HCPC using the ‘FactoMineR’ package in R. 21

Prior to performing clustering, we imputed the missing values of our mixed dataset (Figure S1) using the principal component method. The data imputation was performed with the ‘missMDA’ package in R. Next, we performed FAMD to obtain the principal components to ensure a balanced consideration of both continuous and categorical variables in the analysis. 21 The continuous variables were scaled to unit variance, and the categorical variables were transformed into a disjunctive data table (crisp coding) and then scaled using the specific scaling of multiple correspondence analysis. We retained the first 14 principal components of FAMD based on the eigenvalue criterion (>1), which accumulatively accounted for 68.7% of the variance of the dataset (Figure S3).

Second, the retained principal components were entered into HCPC to identify different clusters. A k‐means consolidation was performed to improve the partition, and the individuals were reassigned to the nearest cluster barycentre with a k‐means algorithm, which led to more homogeneous clusters. 20 The k‐means consolidation was included in the HCPC method by default. The FAMD and HCPC were performed with the ‘FactoMineR’ package in R. 21

Construction and validation of the classification tree

The patient clusters identified with the HCPC method were then inputted into the recursive partitioning analysis to identify the key parameters able to distinguish between the created clusters. 22

Before decision tree modelling, the patient cohort was divided into a training set (65%) and a test set (35%). A balanced distribution of different clusters across both datasets was ensured. The decision tree modelling was conducted within the training set, utilizing information gain as the criterion for classification tree splitting. The tree was subsequently pruned using a complexity parameter of 0.02, with 10‐fold cross‐validation, and each subgroup was required to contain at least 20 patients. As a result, two variables were identified as capable of differentiating between three clusters. The decision tree modelling and visualization were carried out using the ‘rpart’ 22 and ‘rattle’ packages in R.

The performance was internally validated in both the training set and the test set by indexes including area under the curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV).

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were described per cluster. Continuous variables were expressed as means and standard deviations (normal distribution) or medians and inter‐quartile ranges (skewed distribution); categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. A one‐way analysis of variance test (Kruskal–Wallis test for skewed variables) was used for continuous variable comparison, and a chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test was used for categorical variable comparison. For multiple pairwise comparisons, the Bonferroni correction was used.

Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazard models were employed to determine the independent association between clusters and the primary outcome of all‐cause death. In multivariate Cox models, clinically relevant variables were adjusted, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), systolic blood pressure, log‐transformed N‐terminal pro‐brain natriuretic peptide (NT‐proBNP), haemoglobin, serum sodium, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and comorbid diabetes. The proportional hazard assumption was evaluated, and no violations were found. Results were presented as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and visualized with Kaplan–Meier curves.

For secondary outcomes, cumulative incidence function (CIF) plots were used to display the proportion of patients experiencing either the event of interest (CV death/CV readmission) or the competing event (non‐CV death/all‐cause death without CV readmission) per cluster. 23 The relationships between clusters and outcomes were assessed using univariate and multivariate Fine–Gray proportional subdistribution hazard models 24 and cause‐specific hazard models. The assumption of proportionality was tested.

All statistical tests were two‐tailed, and P < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Among the 4227 hospitalized patients admitted to the Heart Failure Center of Fuwai Hospital, CAMS and PUMC, from July 2009 to December 2018 with a clinical diagnosis of HF and aged ≥18 years, 338 patients with baseline LVEF ≥ 65% were considered for inclusion. After a thorough review of the medical records, we excluded the following patients: (1) patients lacking documented signs or symptoms of HF or patients in whom there was not clear link between their symptoms/signs and cardiac abnormalities (n = 20); (2) patients with natriuretic peptide levels within the normal range or lacking evidence indicating cardiogenic congestion (n = 13); (3) patients diagnosed with isolated right HF caused by primary pulmonary hypertension aetiologies (n = 22); (4) patients whose elevated natriuretic peptide levels were more likely attributed to recent invasive or surgical procedures involving the heart or other causes (n = 12); (5) patients with non‐cardiac conditions that could cause hyperdynamic states, including moderate to severe anaemia, hyperthyroidism, active systemic infection and arteriovenous fistula (n = 23); and (6) patients with congenital heart diseases (n = 27). Ultimately, a total of 221 HFsnEF patients were included for analysis (Study Flow Diagram of the supporting information). The mean age was 60.6 years, and 57.5% of them were male; the median length of HF duration was 1.00 years; the median NT‐proBNP level and LVEF were 1041 pg/mL and 67%, respectively (Table 1).

Phenotypic clustering of HFsnEF patients

The optimal number of clusters was three, as indicated by the increase in between‐cluster inertia, 20 the shape of the hierarchical tree, the cutting height proposed by the hierarchical tree, and considering both the total number of individuals and the interpretability of the results (Figures 1 and S4). The clusters were sequentially numbered from 1 to 3. Cluster 1 included 116 (52.5%) patients; Cluster 2 included 58 (26.2%) patients; and Cluster 3 included 47 (21.3%) patients.

Hierarchical clustering tree.

Baseline characteristics and pairwise comparisons of the study participants across three clusters are shown in Table 1. While sex, NT‐proBNP levels, LVEF and HF duration were balanced among groups, the distribution of age, BMI, aetiology, comorbidities and laboratory tests were significantly different. Cluster 1 can be classified as the ‘enlarged cavities, comorbid atrial fibrillation (AF)/atrial flutter (AFL) and valvular aetiology’ group (Figure 2). They had a higher prevalence of AF/AFL (n = 62, 53.4%) and a larger left heart [median left atrial diameter (LAD): 46.0 mm; mean left ventricular end‐diastolic diameter (LVEDD): 52.9 mm]. Valvular heart diseases (n = 103, 88.8%) and restrictive cardiomyopathies (n = 9, 7.8%) were their most common causes of HF. This cluster was also featured by lower blood pressure and higher heart rate. More patients in Cluster 1 received valve replacement therapy compared with other clusters. Cluster 2 was an ‘older, metabolic risk and ischaemic/hypertensive aetiology’ cluster (Figure 2). They had a mean age of 68.8 years and had the highest BMI (mean BMI: 26.9 kg/m2). Ischaemic aetiology (n = 39, 67.2%) and hypertensive aetiology (n = 11, 19.0%) were the most prevalent in this group. Additionally, they were more likely to be comorbid with hypertension (n = 45, 77.6%), diabetes (n = 28, 48.3%), hyperlipidaemia (n = 42, 72.4%) and renal dysfunction (indicated by the lowest eGFR). They were more frequently treated with revascularization interventions. Cluster 3 was a hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) cluster (Figure 2), with 93.6% of them being identified as having HCM aetiology. A surgical septal myectomy or alcohol septal ablation was performed on 40.4% of the patients. Additionally, they exhibited the highest levels of haemoglobin and albumin.

Construction and validation of the classification tree

Two variables were identified by supervised decision tree modelling as identifiers for specific clusters; they were HF aetiology and comorbid diabetes. According to the decision tree modelling, patients with HCM aetiology were best identified as belonging to Cluster 3; ischaemic aetiology and comorbid diabetes were the key parameters for identifying patients from Cluster 2; non‐HCM‐non‐ischaemic aetiology and absence of comorbid diabetes were the identifiers of Cluster 1 (Figure 3).

The classification performance was satisfactory in both the training and testing cohorts. A detailed overview of the model's performance is presented in Table S1.

Association of clusters with adverse outcomes

During the median follow‐up of 53.4 months (inter‐quartile range 23.1–63.5 months), 46 (20.8%) all‐cause deaths occurred, among them 39 of CV causes. Seventy (31.7%) patients experienced CV readmissions.

As shown in Figure 4, we observed that the three clusters differed markedly in all‐cause death risk. Patients in Cluster 1 had the lowest survival probability compared with patients in Cluster 2 and Cluster 3 (log‐rank test P = 0.0025). This risk stratification was further confirmed in Cox regression analysis [Cluster 1 vs. Cluster 2: adjusted HR (aHR), 3.32; 95% CI, 1.19–9.25; P = 0.022; Cluster 1 vs. Cluster 3: aHR, 3.81; 95% CI, 1.09–13.28; P = 0.036]. Cluster 2 and Cluster 3 had comparable all‐cause death risk (aHR, 1.15; 95% CI, 0.23–5.70; P = 0.865) (Table 2).

Similar patterns of association were also noted between clusters and CV death, with a graded decrease in risk profile from Cluster 1 to Cluster 3 (Figure S5). In the Fine–Gray analysis, Cluster 1 was associated with a higher CV death risk compared with Cluster 2 (aHR, 3.73; 95% CI, 1.21–11.48; P = 0.022) and Cluster 3 (aHR, 4.27; 95% CI, 1.26–14.50; P = 0.020) (Table S2). The CV readmission risk was not significantly different across the three clusters (Cluster 1 vs. Cluster 2: aHR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.39–1.70; P = 0.590; Cluster 1 vs. Cluster 3: aHR, 1.04; 95% CI, 0.53–2.07; P = 0.900; Cluster 2 vs. Cluster 3: aHR, 1.28; 95% CI, 0.54–3.02; P = 0.580) (Table S2 and Figure S5). Results from the cause‐specific regression models (Table S2) were comparable with those from the Fine–Gray models.

Discussion

By applying hierarchical clustering, we partitioned HFsnEF patients into three mutually exclusive clusters with distinct characteristics that could be distinguished by simple clinical variables. The identified clusters differed markedly in clinical outcomes.

This study highlights a considerable degree of heterogeneity within HFsnEF and represents an attempt at a detailed characterization of HFsnEF subgroups. Considering the substantial prevalence of HFsnEF (5.2% among hospitalized HF patients in our study cohort), 4 , 25 insights into patient phenotypes can inform both patient care and future research, with the ultimate goal of alleviating the disease burden of HF.

Phenotypic diversity of HFsnEF

To date, most GDMTs for HFrEF have failed to demonstrate efficacy in patients with LVEF > 60%–65%. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 The phenotypic and pathophysiological heterogeneity within the HFsnEF entity is proposed as one of the key reasons for the failure of these post hoc analyses to yield positive conclusions. Therefore, it is crucial to identify and characterize the phenotypic heterogeneity within HFsnEF for a better understanding of this disease entity.

In this phenotypic clustering study, three mutually exclusive clusters with differing clinical profiles and outcomes were identified. Cluster 1 primarily consisted of valvular aetiology patients with larger cardiac chambers and a high prevalence of AF/AFL. They exhibited the highest risk of all‐cause mortality and CV death among the three clusters.

Cluster 2 was characterized by advanced age and ischaemic/hypertensive aetiology, along with a higher prevalence of comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidaemia and renal dysfunction. Consistent with our study, a considerable number of studies on HFpEF phenotyping have identified this ‘older, metabolic risk and vascular ageing’ group as a distinct entity. 26 , 27 It is characterized by ischaemic aetiology and is frequently clustered with comorbidities such as obesity, hypertension, diabetes, chronic kidney disease (CKD) and hyperlipidaemia. 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 The interrelationships between these comorbidities explain this clustering, as they are intricately linked and collectively contribute to pathophysiological processes integral to ischaemic heart disease and HF. 32

Cluster 3 represented a group of patients with HCM aetiology who displayed overall better clinical features, such as a higher baseline haemoglobin and serum albumin. These features, along with a higher rate of aetiology‐specific treatments (93.6% of them received beta‐blockers, and 40.4% of them underwent surgical septal myectomy or alcohol septal ablation), translated to their better prognosis.

Utilizing phenotypic clustering for informing patient care and future research

Phenotypic clustering has the potential to improve patient care and inform future research in a number of ways. First, physicians can use clustering information to identify hospitalized HFsnEF patients at particular high risk of adverse outcomes. This enables them to formulate care and monitoring plans. Second, by performing clustering, we can identify homogeneous patient groups that show similarities across multiple domains. It allows for the identification of unique groups that may share underlying disease mechanisms, thereby promoting our understanding of HFsnEF pathophysiology and facilitating future mechanism research. Third, patients within a cluster are homogeneous and are therefore more likely to respond to certain treatments. Physicians can consider utilizing clustering information to tailor treatments for specific clusters. While patients in Cluster 1 and Cluster 3 are inclined to benefit more from non‐pharmacological therapies and aetiology‐targeted treatments, the clustering‐guided strategy seems applicable to patients in Cluster 2.

For patients in Cluster 2, apart from conventional aetiology‐targeted treatments such as revascularization interventions, beta‐blockers and renin–angiotensin system inhibitors, some specific medications might be appropriate considering their high prevalence of comorbid obesity, diabetes and renal dysfunction. For example, SGLT2 inhibitors seem advisable for patients with comorbid diabetes, given their potential to reduce hospitalizations for HF (HHF), major CV events, end‐stage renal dysfunction and CV death. 33 They are also recommended for patients with obesity and renal dysfunction, given their extra potential to lose weight and protect renal function. 34 , 35 , 36 The selective, non‐steroidal MRA finerenone represents a promising option worthy of anticipation among patients with CKD and diabetes. 37 The ongoing FINEARTS‐HF trial (NCT04435626) is investigating the effect of finerenone on HHF/CV death in HF patients with mildly reduced and preserved ejection fraction, which might provide insights into the potential efficacy of finerenone in HFsnEF. Additionally, in this subgroup with high BMI, lifestyle modification and glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists appear to be promising approaches given their capability to alleviate symptoms and improve exercise function. 38 , 39 Specifically, the pooled analysis of two randomized trials of semaglutide in HFpEF revealed a consistent efficacy of semaglutide in improving HF‐related symptoms among patients with LVEF ≥ 60%. 39 However, due to the absence of concrete evidence, the above discussions could only be deemed hypothesis‐generating.

After the accumulation of sophisticated phenotypic data from larger cohorts, we can anticipate a more precise understanding of HFsnEF heterogeneity and more interpretable risk stratification tools readily available for HFsnEF management. 40 Future clinical trials, guided by these clusters derived from deep phenotyping, are expected to offer insights into targeted therapeutic approaches for specific HFsnEF subgroups.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, it is a retrospective, single‐centre study, which limits the generalizability of our results. Second, misclassification concerns exist due to the limited accuracy, reproducibility, and intra‐observer and inter‐observer variations of LVEF measurements. Third, our study was limited by the lack of available data on magnetic resonance imaging, haemodynamics, genetics and cardiorespiratory fitness, which restricted us from performing more sophisticated phenotypic clustering. Fourth, because of the real‐world nature of these data, we cannot ensure that a systematic screening of all potential aetiologies was performed in all of our patients, notably in those presenting with the most severe forms of HF and a rapidly unfavourable prognosis. Fifth, phenotype studies are data‐driven; therefore, the results are highly dependent on the data cohort. Future larger cohorts are anticipated to externally validate the results, and the real value of identified clusters has to be proven in their applicability in trial design. What is more, in our study, digoxin was prescribed to 30.2% of patients in Cluster 1, suggesting that in some cases, it may be inappropriately utilized for symptom relief or heart rate control. Last, this study was conducted before the widespread utilization of ARNI and SGLT2 inhibitors, so the relevant data are absent.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that by using unsupervised clustering analysis, HFsnEF patients can be partitioned into three clusters with distinct characteristics and outcomes, which can be distinguished by simple clinical variables. The phenotypic clustering signifies the heterogeneity within HFsnEF and provides insights into risk stratification and potential phenotype‐specific management.
