Accrual of thromboembolic events and antiphospholipid syndrome in new-onset systemic lupus erythematosus: a population-based inception cohort study
Authors: Sigrid Reppe Moe, Hilde Haukeland, Cathrine Brunborg, Antonela Botea, Nenad Damjanic, Gro Årthun Wivestad, Heidi Kverneggen Øvreås, Thea Bjerkestrand Bøe, Anniken Orre, Garen Torhild, Sella Aarrestad Provan, Øyvind Molberg, Karoline Lerang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis population-based study aimed to determine timing and incidence of arterial and venous thromboembolic events (TE) and antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) relative to systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) onset and assess relationships between TE, APS and anti-phospholipid antibodies (aPL) during follow-up.MethodsWe included all medical-record confirmed new-onset SLE patients in Southeast Norway (population 2.9 million) 2000–2017 who fulfilled the 2019 European Alliance of Rheumatology Associations/American College of Rheumatology classification criteria. APS was defined by the 2006 Sydney classification criteria, and aPL positivity was determined following international guidelines. Key outcomes were APS, TE and death. We estimated outcome-free survival using Kaplan-Meier methods.ResultsAmong 700 new-onset SLE patients followed for a mean of 8 years (SD 5.0), 13% (89/700) experienced a new TE. TE incidence peaked at 59 per 100 person-years (95% CI 38 to 87) in the first year of SLE among aPL positive patients diagnosed with APS, falling to 12 (95% CI 6.2 to 21) in the subsequent 4 years. In patients without APS, corresponding TE incidences were 2.6 (95% CI 1.4 to 4.3) and 0.9 (95% CI 0.5 to 1.4), respectively. The lowest TE incidence was in aPL-negative patients aged <50 years, with 1-year TE-free survival of 0.99 (95% CI 0.97 to 1.0). Beyond the first year, TE-free survival rates did not differ between SLE patients positive and negative for aPL. Standardised mortality rate in patients with and without APS was 4.7 (95% CI 1.8 to 10.7 and 1.7 (95% CI 1.2 to 2.3).ConclusionsThis population-level study reveals high risk of TE, particularly for aPL positive patients around the time of SLE diagnosis. The elevated TE risk requires attention and early preventive strategies in newly diagnosed SLE.

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a heterogeneous autoimmune disease affecting multiple organs. SLE has a clear female predominance, and peak age at onset is around 39 years.1 The course of SLE is chronic, with fluctuating disease activity, substantial risk of organ damage accrual, disease complications, including thromboembolic events (TEs) on both arterial and venous sides and premature death.24

Specific serum auto-antibodies are central in SLE, highlighted by the 2019 European Alliance of Rheumatology Associations/American College of Rheumatology (ACR/EULAR) classification criteria for SLE requiring anti-nuclear antibodies for entry and using three sets of autoantibodies (anti-double stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA), anti-Smith and anti-phospholipid antibodies (aPLs)) for classification.5 As the aPLs are tested for by three assays: Anti-cardiolipin (aCL), anti-b2 glycoprotein (anti-β2GPI) and lupus anticoagulant (LA), individuals with aPL might be single-positive, double-positive or triple-positive.

In SLE, the testing for aPL occurs in two widely different clinical settings. The first is in the context of TE and/or pregnancy morbidity, where repeated aPL testing defines presence or absence of an antiphospholipid syndrome (APS).6 The second is aPL testing in routine work-up of new-onset or established SLE. While the aPL in this setting have no apparent clinical correlates, previous studies indicate that positive test results, in certain circumstances, associate with increased risk for pregnancy-related events or TE.7 8 There are, however, major knowledge gaps on how, when and in whom positive aPL confer risk for these events.

Estimates on the prevalence of APS in SLE vary greatly, ranging from 8% to 50%.914 Previous referral cohort studies indicated higher disease activity, more severe organ damage and decreased survival in SLE patients with APS.121418 There are, however, few population-based studies on APS in SLE,1923 and, to our knowledge, only one comparing phenotypes and disease outcome in SLE patients with and without APS.19

As indicated, there is increased risk of premature arterial TE (ATE) and venous TE (VTE) in SLE.3 What is less clear is how the ATE and VTE risk in SLE relates to aPL and APS, especially at population level.222429 In fact, to our knowledge, no population-based study has stratified risk and timing of TE in new-onset SLE in the context of aPL and APS. Understanding how, when and in whom APS and TE develops in SLE is of major importance as it may facilitate risk stratification, early treatment and even preventive strategies.

Norway is among the countries where societal factors, including universal healthcare, provide possibilities for long-term prospective observation of complete disease cohorts at population level with minimal loss to follow-up. Taking advantage of this, we recently set up the Nor-SLE cohort project. We identified and characterised all new-onset SLE patients in Southeast Norway (background population 2.9 mill) during 1999–2017 to construct this complete population-based new-onset SLE cohort and initiated long-term prospective follow-up from time of diagnosis.1

The main objective of this study was to (1) determine timing and incidence of APS and TE relative to onset of SLE. In addition, we aimed to (2) investigate how aPL status at SLE onset influences incidence of TE and APS across time, sex and age groups and (3) assess the impact of aPL and/or APS on SLE phenotype and short-term disease outcomes.

Methods

SLE study cohort

The Nor-SLE cohort is a recently established population-based SLE cohort. It includes all new-onset SLE patients confirmed in medical records and diagnosed in Southeast Norway from 1 January 1999 to 31 December 2017, all captured within 1 year of SLE diagnosis (online supplemental figure S1).1 In 2017, Southeast Norway had a background population of 2.9 million, representing 56% of Norway’s total population.

In setting up the Nor-SLE cohort, we used a rigorous two-step method to identify SLE patients.1 First, all patients residing in the study area during the study period, or those who moved there within 1 year of diagnosis, were identified by SLE-specific International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes (M32.1, M32.8, M32.9). Subsequently, a team of nine research-physicians performed individual-level medical record review to either confirm or reject a SLE diagnosis in each patient coded as SLE. To confirm SLE diagnosis, we applied the principles of Fries and Holman which necessitate evidence of multisystem disease, typical SLE manifestations in at least two organ systems, and immunological disturbance consistent with SLE.30

Due to incomplete data on patients diagnosed in 1999, we excluded patients diagnosed in that year. Consequently, this current observational inception cohort study includes all new-onset SLE patients diagnosed from 2000 to 2017 (online supplemental figure S1).

Data collection

By individual-level medical record review, the team of research-physicians retrospectively recorded clinical data in predefined data-extraction study forms. The research physicians received training and ongoing guidance from the cohort coordinators (SRM, HH, ØM and KL) throughout the data collection period. Additionally, we performed random data quality checks to ensure accuracy and reliability.

Data collected from individual-level medical record review included the 1997 ACR and 2019 EULAR/ACR criteria, aPL status, use of immunosuppression, smoking habits, occurrence and type of TE, and the presence of APS. Clinical data were recorded at two time points: (1) at the time of SLE diagnosis and (2) at the last follow-up (as of 1 January 2018 or the last recorded visit). We recorded the year of the event for TE and APS.

We obtained patient demographics from Statistics Norway, including sex (defined by the 11-digit Norwegian national ID number) and geographic origin (classified by parents’ birth countries). Geographic origin was categorised into two groups: European (Europe, including Russia) and non-European (Asia, including Turkey, Africa, and South and Central America). Medication and smoking habits were categorised as ever/never, with ever indicating treatment lasting six or more months.

Definition of aPL positivity, aPL carrier and APS

For aPL assessment, we evaluated LA assays, aCL and anti-β2GPI titres individually and classified them as positive or negative according to international guidelines.31 32 We recorded the presence of APS if both of the following criteria were met: (1) a physician diagnosed APS based on a medical record review and (2) the patient met the APS classification criteria as defined by Miyakis et al.31 The year of APS referred to the year the patient received the APS diagnosis. Patients with positive aPL, but who did not have APS were defined as aPL carriers.

Data sources

The main data source was medical record review, which included data on the main outcome measures; APS, aPL and TE. For information on vital status, we included data from The National Cause of Death Register (NCoDR). NCoDR is based on death certificates and includes all deaths in Norway. To ensure we captured patients that experienced TE as cause of death, we included TE-related deaths recorded in NCoDR before 1 January 2018.

Outcome measures and follow-up time

Main outcomes included APS, fatal/non-fatal TE or death. We defined TE as either ATE and/or VTE, identified through individual-level medical record review. For deceased individuals, we also included TE information from NCoDR, identified using ICD-10 codes, as detailed below.

ATE was defined based on information in medical records, including: (1) ischaemic stroke including cerebral infarction, confirmed by computer tomography or MRI, (2) myocardial infarction confirmed by relevant laboratory findings and one of the following: symptoms of ischaemia, relevant electrocardiograph findings or new regional wall motion abnormality, (3) transient focal symptoms from the brain or retina with a maximum duration of 24 hours, (4) angina pectoris confirmed by exercise stress test. Additionally, we defined ATE if a patient was registered with an ICD-10 code (G45–46, I63 excluding I63.6, I20–I22, R96) in NCoDR.

VTE was defined as a syndrome caused by occlusion of major venous vessels. These included calf and femoral vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, retinal vein occlusion, hepatic vein and sinus venous thrombosis documented either by: (1) clinical findings and relevant diagnostic imaging recorded in medical records or (2) an ICD-10 code of I26, I63.6, 180.2, I80.3, 181, I82, 022.3, O87.1, G08 registered in NCoDR.

Each patient’s first outcome event was considered, hence a patient with an ATE could still be at risk for a VTE and vice versa (online supplemental figure S3). In the TE outcome analyses, patients with both ATE and VTE were counted once using the earliest event date. Follow-up spanned from the year of SLE diagnosis to the year of the first outcome event or, if none occurred, the last available visit until 1 January 2018. Patients with ATE or VTE preceding SLE diagnosis contributed follow-up time for ATE or VTE, respectively (online supplemental figure S3).

We had available data on vital status from NCoDR for both patients and a matched control group until October 2022 from a previously Nor-SLE study.4 Here, we applied these data to assess the impact of APS on all-cause survival and mortality in SLE, extending the follow-up until October 2022, death or the year of emigration from Norway. The control population consisted of randomly selected individuals from the National Population Registry at a ratio of 15 controls per SLE patient, matched by year of birth, sex, geographical origin and residency, as previously described.4 Patients and controls were included at the time of SLE diagnosis, or if APS diagnosis was later than SLE diagnosis, at the time of APS diagnosis.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were made in Stata V.17 (StataCorp). Data are presented as means (SD), medians (IQR) or frequencies (%) and compared by appropriate bivariate methods. All tests were two-sided with 5% significance level. We calculated TE incidence rates as the number of events divided by the number of person-years at risk (reported as 100 person-years at risk). Corresponding 95% CI were estimated using Poisson distribution.

We performed univariable and multivariable Cox regression to investigate factors associated with new-onset TE, excluding patients who had TE prior to SLE diagnosis. The selected variables were based on prior literature-derived knowledge. We performed multivariable analyses for each factor separately, adjusting for sex, geographical origin and age. We tested the proportional hazard assumptions by Schoenfeld tests. Harrell’s c-index was used to evaluate the factors’ discriminatory capability when adjusting for covariates.

We tested overall, APS and TE-free survival by Kaplan-Meier estimates and differences by log-rank test. Standardised mortality rate (SMR) was calculated by expected number of deaths referring to the number of deaths for the matched controls. The 95% CI of SMR was calculated with Mid-P exact test.

Results

Of 744 new-onset SLE patients diagnosed from 2000 to 2017, 94% (700/744) fulfilled the 2019 EULAR/ACR criteria and 84% (628/744) fulfilled the 1997 ACR criteria (online supplemental figure S1). In this study, we focus on the 700 patients meeting the 2019 EULAR/ACR criteria where the mean age at diagnosis was 39 years (SD 17) and 85% (595/700) were female (table 1).

Cumulative incidence of APS

At study end, after mean 8 years (SD 5.0) of follow-up, 11% (79/700) of new-onset SLE patients had been diagnosed with APS (table 1). More than 1/3 of the APS (28/79; 35%) occurred within 1 year of SLE diagnosis. Among the APS patients, 59% (48/79) experienced VTE as their primary event, 26% (21/79) had ATE, and 13% (10/79) had pregnancy-related events. 25 patients had been diagnosed with APS prior to onset of SLE, while 54 developed APS after the SLE diagnosis, giving an incidence of APS in new-onset SLE of 8% (54/675).

Cumulative incidence of TE

By study end, 23% (156/700) of the new-onset SLE patients had experienced at least one TE, 70 had first TE prior to SLE, while 89 had first TE after SLE diagnosis. As expected, the prevalence of TE was higher in SLE patients with APS (87% (69/79) than in those without APS (14% (87/621). Median age at first TE was lower in SLE patients with APS than those without (37 years, IQR 26–49 vs 59 years, IQR 48–66; p<0.001). Among the APS patients, 11% (9/79) had experienced both ATE and VTE, while in SLE patients without APS, only 1% (7/621) had both.

Timing and incidence of TE in relation to SLE diagnosis

We found that TE accumulated around SLE diagnosis (figure 1) and peaked in incidence within 1 year of SLE diagnosis. The peak was most pronounced in the aPL positive patients who were diagnosed with APS. In these patients, TE incidence per 100 person years was 59 (95% CI 38 to 87) in the first year of SLE and fell to 12 (95% CI 6.2 to 21) in the subsequent 4 years. In the new-onset SLE patients without APS, the corresponding incidences of TE were 2.6 (95% CI 1.4 to 4.3) and 0.9 (95% CI 0.5 to 1.4), respectively (table 2). Subset analyses showed peaking incidences within 1 year for both ATE and VTE. Stratification by age showed lower TE incidence in patients diagnosed with SLE before the age of 50 (table 2). The 5-year TE incidence was higher in males than females (4.4, 95% CI 2.5 to 7.2 vs 2.3, 95% CI 1.7 to 3.0).

We estimated TE-free survival in the 630 SLE patients who did not have TE prior to SLE diagnosis. During follow-up, 14% (89/630) experienced a new TE, with 71% (63/89) occurring within 5 years and 93% (83/89) within ten years from SLE diagnosis. TE-free survival was 94% (95% CI 0.92% to 0.96%) at 1 year, 90% (95% CI 0.87% to 0.92%) at 5 years, and 83% (95% CI 0.80% to 0.87%) at 10 years following SLE diagnosis. Among the cases with TE in the same year as SLE diagnosis, 64% (23/36) had TE related to new-onset APS (figure 1a).

Cumulative incidences of positive aPL tests

At end of follow-up, 94% (655/700) of SLE patients had available aPL test results, while aPL status remained unknown throughout the follow-up period for 6% (45/700) of the patients. The patients with unknown aPL status were older at SLE diagnosis, but apart from that, they did not differ from patients with available data according to bivariate analysis (online supplemental table S1). Among patients with available aPL tests, 36% (287/655) tested positive for aPL during a mean follow-up of 8 years (SD 5.0). Of these, 29% (79/287) were diagnosed with APS, while 72% (208/287) did not and were defined as aPL carriers.

Phenotypes of SLE in APS and aPL carriers

At the time of SLE diagnosis, APS patients more often had thrombocytopenia, neuropsychiatric lupus features, anti-dsDNA antibodies, hypocomplementaemia, and as expected, were more likely to have single, double or triple positive aPL than non-APS patients (table 1).

The aPL carriers (n=208) had more often anti-dsDNA antibodies (p-value<0.001) and hypocomplementaemia (p<0.001) than the SLE patients negative for aPL (n=368) (online supplemental table S1). Compared with patients with APS, aPL carriers were less often positive for LA (p-value<0.001) and less often triple positive for aPL tests (p=0.036) (online supplemental table S1).

Relationship between aPL status at time of SLE diagnosis and APS development

We excluded 25 patients with pre-existing primary APS from the APS risk analysis (online supplemental figure S2). Among the remaining 675 SLE patients, 54 patients were diagnosed with APS during follow-up.

APS developed during follow-up in 21% (45/217) of the patients who were aPL positive at SLE diagnosis. We observed that some of the 358 patients who were aPL negative at SLE diagnosis had been subjected to repeat aPL testing. By this retesting, 32 seroconverted to be aPL positive during follow-up, out of which 5 developed APS. Hence, only 1% (5/358) of patients negative for aPL at SLE diagnosis developed APS during follow-up. Of patients with unknown aPL status at SLE diagnosis, 4% (4/100) developed APS.

Evidently, the probability of not developing APS within 5 years after SLE diagnosis differed between patients who were aPL negative and aPL positive at SLE onset (0.99, 95% CI 0.97 to 1.00 vs 0.80, 95% CI 0.74 to 0.85, p-value<0.001). The APS-free survival rate did not differ between aPL positive patients diagnosed with SLE before versus after the age of 50 (figure 2).

Risk of first TE in new-onset SLE by aPL status at time of diagnosis

In these analyses, we excluded the 74 patients who had TE and/or APS prior to SLE diagnosis (online supplemental figure S2). Stratified by aPL status at time of diagnosis, frequency of TE during follow-up was 22% (45/206) in aPL positive patients, 9% (31/332) in aPL negatives and 15% (13/88) in those with unknown aPL status.

Overall, the probability of remaining TE-free was lower in aPL positive patients compared with aPL negative patients already 1 year after SLE diagnosis (0.88, 95% CI 0.83 to 0.92 vs 0.98, 95% CI 0.96 to 0.99, p<0.001) (figure 2, online supplemental table S2). Stratified by age, in patients aged ≥50 years at SLE diagnosis, the TE-free survival rates decreased gradually during follow-up, both in the aPL negative and aPL positive subsets. This contrasted the findings in patients <50 years at diagnosis, where aPL positive patients had a significant fall in TE-free survival rates during the first year after SLE diagnosis, before the curve flattened out, while the aPL negative patients had persistently high TE-free survival across the whole follow-up period (figure 2 and online supplemental table S2).

Risk factors at the time of SLE diagnosis for TE development

We excluded 70 patients with TE prior to SLE diagnosis from the multivariable analyses (online supplemental figure S2). These excluded patients were older and more often male (online supplemental table S3).

When adjusting for sex, geographical origin and age, presence of LA positivity at time of SLE diagnosis, more than one positive aPL and older age were the individual factors with highest HRs for TE development. Additional factors individually associated with increased risk of future TE were male sex, presence of haemolytic anaemia, neuropsychiatric disease, smoking status (current/former) and aPL positivity. Hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) use at SLE diagnosis showed a protective effect against TE, though this was not statistically significant (table 3).

Impact of APS on all-cause survival and mortality

Extending follow-up to 2022, 13% (93/700) new-onset SLE patients died during the study period. Among patients with APS, 9/79 (11%) died. Follow-up time was comparable in patients with and without APS (mean 13 vs 12 years, p=0.145). Overall, all-cause survival was significantly lower for patients compared with controls for both subsets of patients with and without APS (figure 3, online supplemental table S4). Mortality was high in patients with APS (SMR 4.7, 95% CI 1.8 to 10.7), but due to the wide CI, not significantly different from patients without APS (SMR 1.7, 95% CI 1.2 to 2.3).

Discussion

Major knowledge gaps exist on the relationships between APS, TE and aPL. In this comprehensive study of all new-onset SLE patients in Southeast Norway, the key findings were: (1) TE accumulated around time of SLE diagnosis, particularly in the context of APS, (2) at time of diagnosis, SLE patients were at high risk for APS, (3) beyond the first year after SLE diagnosis, the risk of APS fell, and, most notably, aPL lost significance as risk factor for TE, especially among those <50 years at SLE onset and (4) APS emerged as risk factor for mortality in SLE at population level.

11% of the new-onset SLE patients developed APS within a mean of 8 years of follow-up from SLE diagnosis. This is lower than previous population-based estimates from Denmark and the USA, equal to smaller-scaled data from Norway and Sweden2023 and higher than from Asia,19 with differences possibly relating to follow-up times, aPL testing practices and population genetics. In our cohort, 4% of the patients were diagnosed with APS before the onset of SLE. This prevalence greatly exceeds the prevalence of primary APS in the general population and aligns with a recent estimate of 80-fold increased risk for SLE in patients with primary APS.6 33 Hence, high awareness of SLE appears warranted in primary APS populations.

We observed a high incidence of TE in general, and VTE in particular, within 1 year of SLE diagnosis, before a significant drop over the subsequent 4 years, as previously reported.2629 3436 Accordingly, the incidence of ATE and VTE within 1 year of SLE was far above European reference data (ATE: 2.6 vs 0.4 and VTE: 4.2 vs 0.3).37 Most notably, we saw that the peaking TE incidence in early SLE associated strongly with APS, with 64% of TE in the year of SLE diagnosis relating to new-onset APS. Correspondingly, we observed peaking risk of APS around the time of SLE diagnosis, with 54% of all new APS occurring within 2 years of SLE diagnosis.

Consistent with some previous studies, although not all, we found that the new-onset SLE patients with APS presented with more thrombocytopaenia, hypocomplementaemia and neuropsychiatric disease, higher anti-dsDNA, but they did not differ from patients without APS regarding age, sex or lupus nephritis.101316 38 Reported risk for APS and/or TE in aPL carriers varies widely.7 39 40 While the 5-year risk of developing APS was higher in patients positive for aPL at the time of SLE diagnosis in our study, the risk of APS fell beyond the first year of SLE diagnosis. Given that active systemic inflammation may induce local and systemic imbalances in the coagulation system, new-onset SLE could represent the ‘second hit’ necessary for APS development in aPL positive patients.41 Our results underscore that attention to APS-related manifestations in newly diagnosed SLE is critical.

Rates of TE-free survival in this new-onset SLE cohort were in concordance with previous studies,8 35 36 and similarly to others, we identified older age, LA or double aPL positivity as the baseline factors most strongly associated with TE.84244 Although almost all SLE patients had used HCQ by the end of the follow-up period, only 75% were on HCQ at SLE diagnosis. This may reflect the capture of early disease in your inception cohort or less established guidelines for HCQ treatment in the beginning of the study period. Multiple studies have suggested a potential protective role of HCQ against future TE.84547 While this study showed a trend towards a protective effect of HCQ against TE, it was not statistically significant. Stratifying risk of TE by age and aPL status, we revealed that older patients continued to be susceptible to TE throughout the follow-up period, while in the young, TE risk associated with aPL peaked around the time of SLE diagnosis. The results suggest that, particularly in the young, aPL serve as markers for TE risk around SLE diagnosis, but not later in the disease course. Reassuringly, short-term risk of TE in young patients without aPL was low.

In total, our results align with a model where risk of TE early in the course of SLE is driven by two distinct processes: (1) a dynamic risk largely attributed to APS, where systemic inflammation and endothelial activation likely contribute or (2) an accumulating risk related to age, sex and smoking. Our risk analysis captures elements of both processes, without fully differentiating between them. Considering the significantly elevated TE risk during the initial year of SLE, it appears that comprehensive TE risk assessment at the time of SLE diagnosis is warranted, with prophylactic treatment as indicated.48

To provide mortality analysis at high resolution, we benchmarked the outcome of each new-onset SLE patient against 15 carefully matched controls from the general population. We found that survival in APS patients diverges from their matched peers. Accordingly, the mortality rate in APS was high, with an SMR of 4.7, reinforcing previous findings that APS associates with poor outcome.12 15 17 19 49

The strength of this study is the population-based approach with a rigorous patient verification by medical experts and the early capture of SLE patients at diagnosis. This limits selection bias and allows for identification of prognostic factors from the initial phase of the disease.

The main limitation of this study is the lack of information on traditional TE risk factors for both ATE and VTE. This includes data on antithrombotic agents, cumulative glucocorticoid dose, the use of oestrogen therapy and disease activity indices during follow-up, which would have enhanced the depth of understanding in this study. Additionally, as this study was conducted in a real-life setting, we did not have aPL profiles for all patients, particularly for anti-β2GPI antibodies. Furthermore, due to the use of different aPL test systems across hospital laboratories during the study period, it was not feasible to apply aPL titres directly; instead, we could only report them as positive or negative. As we had not recorded data on certain clinical criteria like cardiac valve involvement, it was not possible to apply the new ACR/EULAR criteria for APS in this study cohort.50 We also cannot rule out residual detection bias in our study. Moreover, the timing of TE and SLE diagnosis was only available at a year-level resolution. Most SLE patients were of European ancestry, limiting generalisation due to varying SLE incidence across geographical origin. Finally, the cohort study design allows us to demonstrate associations but not infer causality.

In conclusion, we provide novel data that shed light on the clinical implications of APS co-occurrence and short-term outcome in SLE. We identify a high risk of thrombosis, around SLE diagnosis, particularly in aPL positive patients, indicating suboptimal management of thromboembolic risk in this population. Heightened awareness of TE at the time of SLE diagnosis appears crucial, underscoring the need for early risk assessment and targeted prevention strategies.
