Impact of UGT1A9 Expression on Analgesic Efficacy and Safety of Postoperative Ketorolac in Thyroid Cancer Surgery: A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Xitong Zhang, Xiunan Jia, Dongmei Fu, Dongxin Wang, Xiaoyu Zhang, Dongxue Li, Shuyi Yang, Zongming Liu, Shiqi Diao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesKetorolac tromethamine is a key opioid-sparing analgesic in Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols for thyroid cancer surgery; however, clinically significant interindividual variability in its analgesic efficacy and adverse-event profile is frequently observed. Because UDP-glucuronosyltransferase 1A9 (UGT1A9) plays a central role in the metabolic clearance of ketorolac, we hypothesized that preoperative UGT1A9 expression levels would be associated with differences in postoperative analgesic outcomes.MethodsThis prospective observational cohort study enrolled 150 ASA I–II patients undergoing elective total thyroidectomy with central lymph node dissection. Preoperative peripheral blood UGT1A9 protein expression was quantified by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA; optical density, OD). Patients were stratified by median OD into a high-expression cohort (Group I, n = 75) and a low-expression cohort (Group II, n = 75). All received 30 mg intravenous ketorolac at wound closure; no patient-controlled analgesia was used. Primary outcome: resting VAS pain scores at 4 h (T1), 6 h (T2), 8 h (T3), and 12 h (T4). Secondary outcomes: serum IL-6 and CRP at 12 h, adverse events, and length of hospital stay (LOS).ResultsBaseline characteristics were comparable (all p > 0.05). From T2 onward, Group II (low expression) demonstrated lower VAS scores than Group I (e.g. T3: 1.47 ± 0.55 vs. 2.00 ± 0.90; p < 0.001; Cohen’s d = 0.70). The incidence of mild dizziness was higher in Group II (9.3% vs. 1.3%; p = 0.029, Fisher’s exact test).ConclusionThese preliminary findings suggest that preoperative UGT1A9 expression may be associated with postoperative ketorolac analgesic response in thyroid cancer surgery. This study provides hypothesis-generating evidence for future pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic validation studies exploring pharmacogenomic individualization of perioperative NSAID analgesia.Keywords: UGT1A9, thyroid cancer, ketorolac tromethamine, ERAS, pharmacogenomics, NSAIDs, postoperative analgesia

Introduction

Thyroid cancer is among the most rapidly increasing malignancies worldwide.1–7 Surgery remains the cornerstone of treatment; however, postoperative pain and inflammation significantly affect patient comfort, impede early mobilization, and prolong recovery. Postoperative pain after thyroidectomy with lymph node dissection is characteristically mild-to-moderate in intensity but is particularly sensitive to swallowing and neck movement, driving a strong clinical preference for opioid-sparing multimodal analgesia to minimize postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) and facilitate rapid recovery within Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) pathways.8–11

Ketorolac tromethamine, a potent nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID), is a central component of multimodal ERAS analgesia in thyroid surgery. It provides effective prophylactic analgesia and anti-inflammatory effects via cyclooxygenase inhibition and prostaglandin synthesis reduction, with established opioid-sparing properties.12–17 However, clinically significant interindividual variability in ketorolac analgesic efficacy and adverse-event profiles is observed even at standard doses. Understanding the sources of this variability is an important clinical problem.

Ketorolac undergoes dual metabolic elimination: hepatic glucuronidation (principally via UDP-glucuronosyltransferase 1A9 [UGT1A9], with contributions from UGT1A6 and UGT2B7) accounts for approximately 40% of drug clearance, while renal excretion of unchanged drug accounts for approximately 60%.18 UGT1A9 is a key Phase II metabolic enzyme responsible for the glucuronidation of multiple endogenous substances and exogenous drugs. Individual variation in UGT1A9 expression and activity may therefore contribute to differences in ketorolac clearance, plasma drug exposure, and ultimately analgesic response. It is important to acknowledge, however, that postoperative analgesic variability is multifactorial, influenced not only by drug-metabolizing enzymes but also by age, sex, body composition, renal function, inflammatory state, concurrent medication use, psychosocial factors, and other genetic polymorphisms (eg., OPRM1, COMT).19–23

Pharmacogenomic approaches to perioperative analgesia individualization have demonstrated clinical utility for opioids,24,25 yet comparable evidence for NSAIDs—and specifically for UGT1A9 expression in relation to ketorolac response—remains limited. The current study was designed as an exploratory investigation to evaluate whether preoperative peripheral blood UGT1A9 expression levels are associated with postoperative ketorolac analgesic efficacy and safety outcomes following thyroid cancer surgery. We selected thyroid cancer surgery because of its procedural standardization (limiting operative confounders), mild-to-moderate pain profile, short operative duration, limited tissue trauma, and established opioid-sparing NSAID reliance—factors that collectively support its use as a pharmacogenomic model.

We note an important conceptual limitation of our approach: peripheral blood UGT1A9 expression was used as a surrogate measure for hepatic metabolic capacity. While some pharmacogenomic studies suggest concordance between peripheral and hepatic enzyme expression phenotypes, this relationship was not directly validated in the current study and should be interpreted with caution. We hypothesized, in an exploratory capacity, that preoperative UGT1A9 expression levels would be associated with differences in postoperative ketorolac analgesic response. The findings are intended to generate hypotheses for future pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) validation studies rather than to establish definitive clinical guidance.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

This prospective observational cohort study was conducted at Jilin Province Cancer Hospital in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision). The study protocol was approved by the Jilin Province Cancer Hospital Institutional Review Board for Science and Technology Ethics Committee (Approval No.: 202409-102-01), and written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their families prior to enrollment. The clinical trial was prospectively registered with the Jilin Provincial Health Commission of China (Registration No.: 2024A126). This study strictly adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.26

A total of 178 patients were assessed for eligibility during the enrollment period (October 2025 to March 2026). Of these, 28 were excluded: 12 met exclusion criteria (significant comorbidities), 7 did not meet ASA status criteria, 5 declined participation, and 4 had incomplete baseline blood samples. The remaining 150 patients were consecutively enrolled and all 150 completed the study with complete outcome data. Patients were enrolled in sequence of surgical scheduling to minimize selection bias (Figure 1).

The primary inclusion criteria were: (1) age 30–70 years; (2) ASA Physical Status I or II; (3) cardiac function classified as New York Heart Association (NYHA) Functional Class I or II (ie., NYHA Class < III); (4) blood pressure consistent with Grade 1 or Grade 2 hypertension per the WHO/International Society of Hypertension classification (systolic BP < 180 mmHg and diastolic BP < 110 mmHg at baseline); and (5) normal hepatic, renal, and coagulation function. Patients were excluded if they had: NYHA Class ≥ III cardiac dysfunction or atrioventricular block; WHO/ISH Grade ≥ 3 (severe) hypertension; a history of diabetes mellitus, hypothyroidism, morbid obesity (BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2), or psychiatric disorders; or known allergies to study drugs. Pregnant or lactating women, patients undergoing emergency surgery, and those who requested withdrawal were also excluded.

All enrolled patients underwent total thyroidectomy with central compartment lymph node dissection as the standardized institutional procedure. This procedural uniformity was an intentional design choice to minimize surgical extent as a confounding variable.

UGT1A9 Expression Measurement

Before surgery, a 5 mL peripheral venous blood sample was collected from each patient. UGT1A9 protein expression was quantified by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) using a validated commercial kit (CUSABIO, Wuhan, China; Catalog No. CSB-E13422h), measured as optical density (OD) at 450 nm using a calibrated microplate reader with manufacturer-provided standards. Intra-assay coefficient of variation was < 8%; inter-assay coefficient of variation was < 10%, consistent with kit specifications. All measurements were performed by laboratory technicians blinded to clinical group allocation and outcomes, prior to patient stratification.

Based on the median OD value of the entire cohort, the 150 patients were stratified into two groups: a high-expression group (Group I, n = 75; OD ≥ median) and a low-expression group (Group II, n = 75; OD < median). This dichotomization was selected for clinical interpretability; however, we acknowledge that median splitting reduces statistical power and may obscure dose–response relationships. A supplementary continuous regression analysis treating OD as a continuous variable is included to complement the primary group-based analysis.

Anesthesia and Analgesia Protocol

Upon arrival in the operating room, two peripheral intravenous lines were established in the lower extremities for lactated Ringer’s solution infusion. Continuous physiological monitoring was initiated using a Dräger anesthesia workstation, including 5-lead ECG, heart rate (HR), systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP/DBP), pulse oximetry (SpO2), bispectral index (BIS), end-tidal sevoflurane concentration, core body temperature, and neuromuscular blockade monitoring.

All patients received a standardized general anesthesia protocol. Induction combined propofol (1.0–1.2 mg/kg) and etomidate (0.15–0.20 mg/kg), a protocol used at our institution to balance the hemodynamic stability benefits of etomidate against propofol’s favorable myoclonus profile; this combination has been previously described in Chinese anesthesia practice to reduce both hemodynamic instability and induction-phase myoclonic movements when using standard induction doses of either agent alone. Sufentanil (0.2–0.3 μg/kg) and cisatracurium besylate (0.15–0.2 mg/kg; 3–4× ED95) were additionally administered. Following achievement of target BIS (40–60), endotracheal intubation was performed using a video laryngoscope with positive pressure mechanical ventilation.

Anesthesia maintenance employed a combined intravenous-inhalation technique: target-controlled infusion (TCI) of remifentanil at an effect-site concentration (Ce) of 0.5–1.0 ng/mL combined with continuous inhalation of sevoflurane (1.0–1.2 MAC). This low remifentanil Ce is consistent with balanced anesthesia using a primary volatile anesthetic in a procedure of limited tissue trauma. Additional sufentanil and cisatracurium besylate were administered as required, guided by BIS and neuromuscular monitoring. Mechanical ventilation was maintained with tidal volume 6–8 mL/kg, respiratory rate 10–12 breaths/min, targeting end-tidal CO2 35–45 mmHg and SpO2 > 95%.

Toward the end of surgery, preemptive analgesia was administered as intravenous ketorolac tromethamine 30 mg (fixed dose). No patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) was utilized postoperatively. A pre-specified rescue analgesic protocol was established (intravenous tramadol 1 mg/kg for VAS > 4 sustained beyond 15 minutes); no patient in either group required rescue analgesia during the 12-hour observation period.

Patients emerged naturally at surgery end. Extubation was performed once consciousness and protective airway reflexes were fully restored (spontaneous respiratory rate 10–12 breaths/min, tidal volume 6–8 mL/kg, bilateral clear breath sounds). Stable patients were transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU).

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome was the intensity of resting incisional pain assessed by visual analog scale (VAS; 0 = no pain, 10 = worst imaginable pain) at 4 h (T1), 6 h (T2), 8 h (T3), and 12 h (T4) postoperatively. VAS assessments were performed by designated ward nurses blinded to patients’ UGT1A9 group allocation. Patients received standardized VAS education preoperatively to ensure consistent reporting. Pain was assessed at rest only.

Secondary outcomes were: (1) Inflammatory markers: peripheral venous blood (5 mL) collected preoperatively and at 12 h postoperatively for serum IL-6 and C-reactive protein (CRP) quantification. We acknowledge that 12 h may not represent peak values for all markers (IL-6 peaks earlier, CRP later at 24–48 h), and this is noted as a limitation. (2) Adverse events: nausea (reported sensation of unease/urge to vomit without gastric expulsion), vomiting (forceful expulsion of gastric contents), and dizziness (patient-reported lightheadedness or disequilibrium) were monitored at each evaluation time point and graded as mild (no intervention), moderate (non-pharmacological intervention), or severe (pharmacological intervention). Only mild events were recorded in this cohort. (3) Length of hospital stay (LOS, days): discharge criteria included VAS ≤ 3 at rest, tolerance of oral intake, absence of surgical complications (hypocalcemia, hematoma, recurrent laryngeal nerve injury), acceptable drain output, and physician clearance.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics v21.0. Continuous variables were assessed for normality by the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data are expressed as mean ± SD, compared by independent samples t-test; non-normally distributed data as median (IQR), compared by Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables are expressed as frequency (n, %) compared by Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate (Fisher’s exact test applied when expected cell count < 5). Repeated-measures ANOVA (RM-ANOVA) was used for serial VAS scores; sphericity was assessed by Mauchly’s test and the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied where violated. Pairwise post-hoc comparisons used Bonferroni correction. Effect sizes are reported as Cohen’s d (continuous outcomes) or Cramér’s V (categorical). 95% confidence intervals are reported for primary comparisons. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. A supplementary analysis treated UGT1A9 OD as a continuous predictor in linear regression with VAS at T3 as the primary outcome to complement the median-split primary analysis.

Postoperative Analgesic Efficacy

Postoperative VAS scores at multiple time points are presented in Table 3 and Figure 2. At T1 (4 h), VAS scores were comparable between groups (Group I: 2.37 ± 1.06; Group II: 2.40 ± 1.10; p = 0.880). From T2 (6 h) onward, a statistically significant divergence emerged: Group II demonstrated lower VAS scores at T2 (95% CI for difference: 0.20–0.73; p = 0.001), T3 (0.29–0.77; p < 0.001; Cohen’s d = 0.70), and T4 (0.11–0.63; p = 0.007). The RM-ANOVA confirmed a significant group × time interaction (F(3,148) = 8.42; p < 0.001; ηp2 = 0.15), indicating a differential time course of pain scores. While statistically significant from T2 onward, the absolute between-group differences (approximately 0.4–0.5 VAS points at most time points) were modest and likely fall below the conventional minimum clinically important difference (MCID) of approximately 1.3–1.5 points for postoperative VAS pain scores. The proportion of patients with VAS > 6 at any time point was low in both groups (Group I: 3/75 [4.0%]; Group II: 0/75 [0%]).

Postoperative Inflammatory Markers

Serum IL-6 and CRP levels are presented in Tables 4 and 5. Preoperative values were comparable between groups (IL-6: p = 0.879; CRP: p = 0.991). At 12 h postoperatively, both markers were significantly lower in Group II than Group I (IL-6: 9.07 ± 0.36 vs. 10.51 ± 0.62 pg/mL; p < 0.001; CRP: 6.85 ± 1.66 vs. 12.44 ± 3.39 mg/L; p < 0.001). The between-group IL-6 difference of approximately 1.4 pg/mL, while statistically significant, represents a modest absolute difference whose clinical relevance remains uncertain in the absence of established MCID thresholds for postoperative IL-6. The observed 12-h time point may not represent peak concentrations for all markers, given that IL-6 peaks at approximately 4–6 h and CRP at 24–48 h postoperatively; these findings should therefore be interpreted within these kinetic constraints.

Adverse Events

Postoperative adverse event incidence is summarized in Table 6. The occurrence of nausea (Group I: 12.0%; Group II: 10.7%; p = 0.798) and vomiting (6.7% vs. 5.3%; p = 0.733) were comparable between cohorts. The incidence of mild dizziness was significantly higher in Group II (9.3% vs. 1.3%; Fisher’s exact test p = 0.029). All dizziness events were graded mild (no pharmacological intervention required) and resolved spontaneously. This finding is consistent with the hypothesis of altered drug clearance in low-expressers, but alternative explanations—including residual anesthetic effects, hemodynamic variability, or random variation—cannot be excluded in the absence of pharmacokinetic data.

Length of Hospital Stay

Mean LOS was significantly shorter in Group II (7.19 ± 1.44 days) compared to Group I (8.69 ± 1.85 days; t = 5.56; p < 0.001; Table 7). This approximately 1.5-day difference is consistent with the pattern of better analgesic outcomes in Group II; however, we note that LOS at our institution reflects institutional protocols for post-thyroidectomy monitoring (hypocalcemia surveillance, drain management, wound assessment) rather than ERAS-based early discharge criteria, and may therefore be influenced by factors beyond analgesic efficacy. Institutional discharge practices were not adjusted for in the analysis, limiting causal inference.

Discussion

This prospective observational cohort study explored the hypothesis that preoperative UGT1A9 expression levels are associated with postoperative analgesic response to ketorolac tromethamine in thyroid cancer surgery. The results suggest that patients in the low-expression cohort (Group II) demonstrated lower postoperative VAS scores from 6 h onward, lower 12-h inflammatory markers, and shorter LOS, with a modestly higher rate of mild dizziness. These findings provide preliminary associative evidence consistent with the broader pharmacogenomic hypothesis that UGT enzyme expression may modulate NSAID analgesic response. It is important to note, however, that this is an observational study without pharmacokinetic measurement, and the findings cannot establish a causal relationship.

The pharmacogenomic rationale is scientifically plausible. UGT1A9 is a major contributor to ketorolac glucuronidation, and lower enzymatic activity is expected to result in slower clearance and potentially higher sustained drug exposure. This might explain why Group II patients demonstrated better analgesic coverage over the 6–12 h observation window, consistent with—but not proven by—the pharmacokinetic hypothesis. We must acknowledge, however, that peripheral blood UGT1A9 expression is used as a surrogate measure for hepatic metabolic capacity, and the validity of this surrogate was not independently confirmed. Whether OD values from peripheral leukocytes accurately reflect hepatic UGT1A9 activity and functionally predict drug clearance requires direct pharmacokinetic validation. Furthermore, ketorolac metabolism involves not only UGT1A9 but also UGT1A6, UGT2B7, and significant renal excretion of unchanged drug. The current approach therefore captures a part—but not the entirety—of metabolic variability.

Importantly, UGT1A9 expression is a relatively stable individual phenotype under normal physiological conditions, not subject to rapid short-term fluctuations. Published pharmacogenomic literature on related UGT enzymes suggests that single preoperative measurements can provide a reproducible reflection of the patient’s metabolic baseline, supporting the clinical rationale for a one-time preoperative test. However, longer-term stability of UGT1A9 expression across different physiological states (illness, hepatic dysfunction, drug interactions) should be considered in future studies.

The observed inflammatory marker differences require cautious interpretation. We propose the physiologically plausible hypothesis that inadequate analgesia in high expressers leads to greater neuroendocrine activation, contributing to higher IL-6 and CRP levels via stress-mediated inflammatory pathways. Additionally, lower ketorolac bioavailability in high expressers may reduce the drug’s inherent anti-inflammatory activity. However, this cascade was not directly measured in this study—stress hormone levels, pharmacokinetic exposure, and inflammatory markers were not analyzed conjointly—and the mechanism remains speculative. The absolute IL-6 difference (~1.4 pg/mL) was modest, and the CRP data are constrained by the single 12-h time point. Alternative explanations for inflammatory differences, including unmeasured confounders such as surgical stress variability or individual inflammatory responsiveness, cannot be excluded.

The higher incidence of mild dizziness in Group II is consistent with the pharmacokinetic hypothesis of prolonged drug exposure, but we emphasize that this explanation remains speculative in the absence of plasma concentration data. Anesthesia residual effects, hemodynamic factors, and random variation are plausible alternative explanations for this small difference (1 vs. 7 events). The small event numbers limit statistical robustness. Future studies should include comprehensive pharmacokinetic monitoring and more systematic adverse-event capture.

The LOS difference of approximately 1.5 days may have health economic implications, but we caution against definitive conclusions regarding cost-effectiveness. At our institution, a tertiary cancer center in China, LOS after total thyroidectomy with central lymph node dissection reflects extended monitoring requirements (hypocalcemia surveillance, drain management), and is substantially longer than ERAS early-discharge benchmarks reported in many Western centers (24–48 hours). Institutional discharge practices may therefore exert a confounding influence on LOS independent of analgesic efficacy. Formal cost-effectiveness analysis was beyond the scope of this exploratory study.

We also wish to acknowledge limitations regarding the assumption of UGT1A9 expression stability as a confound: inflammatory states themselves can modulate UGT enzyme expression, creating a potential bidirectional relationship that complicates interpretation. Additionally, other UGT isoforms and non-UGT pharmacogenomic factors (such as renal function, which determines approximately 60% of ketorolac elimination) likely contribute to the observed variability in ways not captured by UGT1A9 expression alone.

This study must be considered in the context of its limitations. This was a single-center, exploratory cohort study without pharmacokinetic measurement or genetic polymorphism analysis, limiting mechanistic inference. The median-split dichotomization reduces statistical power and may obscure dose–response relationships. No a priori power calculation was performed. The purely univariable statistical approach—without multivariable adjustment for potential confounders such as sex, renal function, or intraoperative drug exposure—limits the strength of causal claims. The 12-h postoperative observation window is relatively short for comprehensive safety evaluation. The restrictive exclusion criteria limit external generalizability to broader thyroid surgery populations. The LOS is confounded by institutional practice. A supplementary continuous regression analysis (OD vs. VAS at T3: β = –0.64; 95% CI: –0.91 to –0.37; p < 0.001) supports the directional association but cannot substitute for a rigorous multivariable model.

Future studies should address these limitations through: multicenter designs with diverse demographic populations; direct pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic modeling;27 multivariable regression including established confounders; analysis of UGT1A9 expression as a continuous predictor; inclusion of UGT1A9 genetic polymorphism data for mechanistic comparison; extended follow-up; and formal cost-effectiveness analysis. Should robust PK/PD evidence accumulate, preoperative UGT1A9 phenotyping could represent a candidate biomarker for individualized NSAID dosing within ERAS frameworks—but this potential requires rigorous validation before clinical implementation can be recommended.

Conclusions

This exploratory prospective cohort study provides preliminary evidence suggesting an association between preoperative UGT1A9 expression levels and postoperative analgesic outcomes of ketorolac tromethamine in thyroid cancer surgery. Patients in the low-expression cohort demonstrated lower VAS pain scores from 6 h onward, lower 12-h inflammatory markers, and shorter LOS, with a modestly higher incidence of mild dizziness. These findings are hypothesis-generating and consistent with the pharmacogenomic framework of enzyme expression–dependent drug metabolism, but do not establish causation and require replication in multicenter studies with direct pharmacokinetic validation before any clinical recommendations can be made. Individualized metabolic phenotyping represents a promising direction for precision analgesia within perioperative ERAS protocols.
