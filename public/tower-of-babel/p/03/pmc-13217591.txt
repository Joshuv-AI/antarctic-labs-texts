Efficacy of White Mineral Trioxide Aggregate in Regenerative Endodontic Procedures and Apexification for Immature Permanent Teeth: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Zizhan Li, Yue Xu, Songlin An, Hui Liu, Li Liu, Yingzi Pan, Taiyi Sun, Shiyun Kang, Rui Liu, Lin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study systematically reviews the efficacy of bioceramic-based treatments for non-vital immature permanent teeth in children. A systematic search retrieved 229 records, ultimately including 15 studies, focused on White Mineral Trioxide Aggregate (WMTA) or other bioceramics used in apexification or Regenerative Endodontic Procedures (REP). A meta-analysis was conducted on WMTA’s radiographic success and the comparative efficacy of platelet concentrates (PC) versus blood clot (BC) scaffolds. Data regarding clinical success, pulp sensibility, root development, and discolouration, were extracted. Risk of Bias (RoB) was evaluated using the RoB2 tool for RCTs. Evidence certainty was assessed using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation) approach. The meta-analysis of radiographic success included 6 studies, revealing a pooled proportion of 0.99 (95% CI: 0.96-1.00), though GRADE rated evidence certainty as very low due to serious risk of bias and imprecision. Comparative analysis of PC versus BC scaffolds (4 studies, 145 observations) showed a non-significant trend favouring PC (OR = 3.56, 95% CI: 0.87-14.52), but very low certainty due to indirectness and sparse data. Comparison of WMTA versus alternative bioceramics revealed 100% clinical success, though non-significant (OR = 0.18, 95% CI: 0.02-1.68), with moderate certainty downgraded only for imprecision. All analyses demonstrated no heterogeneity (I² = 0.0%). PC favoured root lengthening compared to BC scaffolds that promoted dentinal wall thickening, alternative bioceramics exhibited a significantly lower discolouration risk than WMTA (7.69% vs 58.33%, P = .01), and pulp sensibility restoration was achieved in 60% to 86% of cases across regenerative treatments. Bioceramic-based treatments achieve high success for non-vital immature permanent teeth in children, though evidence certainty ranges from very low to moderate. While therapeutic efficacy is comparable, alternative bioceramics reduce discolouration risk compared to WMTA, and scaffold selection influences root maturation patterns.Keywords: Mineral Trioxide Aggregate, Regenerative endodontics, Apexification, Platelet-rich plasma, Root development, Tooth discolouration

Introduction

Regenerative endodontics has made remarkable progress in the management of immature permanent teeth with necrotic pulps, particularly in paediatric patients, where preserving tooth vitality and promoting root maturation are critical for long-term oral health. Emerging bioactive materials, including platelet concentrates (PC) and alternative bioceramics, have demonstrated promise in pulp regeneration. Recent studies highlight their efficacy in achieving periapical healing and sustained root development. For instance, Bartaw et al.1 conducted a randomised clinical trial (RCT) in children aged 8 to 14 years with necrotic immature maxillary incisors, reporting that Totalfill bioceramic putty achieved clinical success rates comparable to White Mineral Trioxide Aggregate (WMTA), with the notable reduction in discolouration risk. Similarly, Aly et al.2 found Biodentine and WMTA were equally effective as direct pulp capping agents in promoting healing and root development in young permanent molars. These studies underscore the potential of regenerative protocols to achieve periapical healing and sustained root growth, as further illustrated in case series such as that by Ulusoy et al.,3 which evaluated the use of platelet-rich fibrin (PRF), plasma, and injectable variants for soft tissue and osseous regeneration.

Despite the advancements, earlier literature has predominantly focused on broad comparisons of scaffolds or general outcomes in regenerative endodontics, often neglecting the specific role of WMTA as a coronal barrier in paediatric pulp regeneration treatments. While case reports, such as Sachdeva et al.4 provide preliminary evidence of success using platelet rich plasma (PRP) with WMTA in immature necrotic teeth, these findings remain largely anecdotal and lack a comprehensive quantitative synthesis. Furthermore, systematic works like Kahler et al.,5 fail to differentiate the influence of WMTA from other procedural variables when measuring success against American Association of Endodontists (AAE) standards. Consequently, the isolated effect of WMTA on paediatric regenerative outcomes remains insufficiently explored.

While Abdellatif et al.6 partially addressed the existing gap by synthesising data from 30 studies, their findings specifically examined WMTA and other bioactive materials in pulp regeneration for permanent teeth in paediatric subjects. Their meta-analysis reported an overall success rate of 91.20%, demonstrating WMTA's strong association with high success rates under AAE standards and its role in promoting root development in immature teeth. Nevertheless, while their analysis confirmed the material's general efficacy, there remains a critical need for a detailed evaluation of WMTA's performance specifically as a coronal barrier, a critical application with known risks related to tooth discolouration.

The present systematic review and meta-analysis specifically aimed to quantify the risk-benefit profile of WMTA when utilised exclusively as a coronal barrier in regenerative endodontics. This study aims to advance beyond the previously reported overall success rates by separately isolating the impact of WMTA as a coronal barrier on both clinical and radiographic success rates and the incidence of postoperative tooth discolouration, thereby providing refined, evidence-based guidelines for material selection in paediatric regenerative protocols.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines7 and the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (http://www.training.cochrane.org/handbook). The protocol for this systematic review has been registered in the PROSPERO database (Registration number: CRD420261337704).

Selection criteria

Inclusion criteria were designed to identify studies evaluating WMTA in pulp regeneration for immature or necrotic permanent teeth. Priority was given to registered clinical trials (e.g., ClinicalTrials.gov), RCTs, and prospective cohort studies. To ensure a more comprehensive understanding of the topic, non-RCTs and case reports/series were also considered. Given the limited availability of RCTs in the field of regenerative endodontic treatment for immature permanent teeth, excluding case reports would restrict the external validity and generalisability of the findings. The inclusion of case reports enabled the capture of long-term outcomes that are typically underreported in short-term clinical trials. However, owing to the inherent risk of bias associated with these study designs, results derived from case reports should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Studies were included if they assessed WMTA as an apical plug in apexification or coronal barrier in REP with scaffolds (e.g., PRP, PRF, PP (platelet-poor plasma) and BC).

Data extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted data using a standardised form, resolving discrepancies through consensus or the involvement of a third reviewer. The extracted variables encompassed study characteristics (country, period and design), sample size, disease types (e.g., pulp necrosis, apical periodontitis), intervention methods (e.g., WMTA as an apical plug or coronal seal with scaffolds), intervention duration, grouping (e.g., WMTA vs Biodentine, WMTA + PRP vs WMTA + BC), control interventions, primary endpoints (e.g., clinical/radiographic success), secondary endpoints (e.g., root growth, sensibility, discolouration), inclusion/exclusion criteria, and follow-up periods. The outcomes extracted included clinical success (absence of symptoms/pathology), radiographic success (periapical healing/stability), pulp sensibility (electronic pulp testing/cold response), root development (length, wall thickness, relative root area/root canal area), and discolouration rates.

Risk of bias assessment

RoB was assessed using appropriate tools. RCTs were evaluated with the Cochrane RoB2 tool, focusing on aspects such as randomisation, intervention deviations, missing data, outcome measurement, and result selection. Non-RCTs were assessed using the ROBINS-I tool, which addressed confounding, selection bias, and additional domains. Case reports/series followed case report guideline guidelines to ensure methodological rigor. The RoB was visualised through traffic light and summary bar plots for RCTs.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using R with the meta package.8 The included studies exhibited significant clinical and methodological differences regarding study design, scaffold types, population characteristics, and follow-up durations. Therefore, based on conceptual heterogeneity, the random-effects model was pre-specified as the primary analytical approach for all meta-analyses. This model was employed to pool proportions and odds ratios (ORs) with their corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) for both single-arm and comparative analyses. For proportions approaching boundary values (e.g., success rates nearing 100.00%), the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation and generalised linear mixed models were applied to stabilise variances and prevent CI from exceeding the (0, 1) rang.8 Pooled effect estimates and their 95% CI derived from transformed data were subsequently back-transformed to report results on the original proportion scale. Subgroup analyses were performed stratified by scaffold type (PRP + WMTA, PRF + WMTA, PP + WMTA, and apexification + WMTA), with between-subgroup differences assessed using the Q statistic. Heterogeneity was comprehensively evaluated using the τ² statistic, the I² metric with its 95% CI, and Cochran's Q test (with P < .10 indicating statistically significant heterogeneity). Sources of heterogeneity were explored through sensitivity analyses, which involved: (1) excluding non-randomised studies and case series/reports; and (2) restricting analyses to studies judged to have low risk of bias. Publication bias was assessed via visual inspection of funnel plot symmetry for all meta-analyses. Given that the number of studies included in each meta-analysis was ≤ 6, formal statistical tests for publication bias (e.g., Egger's regression test or Begg's rank correlation test) were not performed, as these tests are underpowered and potentially misleading with sparse data. Statistical significance was set at P < .05. The certainty of evidence for each outcome was evaluated using the GRADE approach (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation).9 Two reviewers independently assessed these domains, that is, risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. When a specific domain was rated as having serious or very serious limitations, the corresponding evidence was downgraded accordingly. Each outcome was assigned an overall certainty rating of high, moderate, low, or very low. Any discrepancies in domain-level judgments between reviewers were resolved through discussion and, if necessary, consultation with a third reviewer. Further details regarding the GRADE methodology and implementation guidance are available at the official GRADE Working Group website (https://www.gradeworkinggroup.org).

Results

The initial literature search identified 229 records (n = 183 from primary databases and n = 46 from secondary sources). After the removal of 32 duplicates, 197 unique records remained for screening. The review of titles and abstracts led to the exclusion of 164 records, resulting in 33 full-text articles being assessed for eligibility. During the full-text evaluation, 18 articles were excluded due to unsuitable study designs (n = 13) or because they were reviews/meta-analyses (n = 5) (Supplementary Table 1). Ultimately, 15 studies met the inclusion criteria, comprising 4 RCTs, 2 cohort studies, and 9 case reports/series (Figure 1).

Included study characteristics

The included literature comprised a mix of 6 RCTs/comparative cohorts and 9 case reports/series predominantly from India, Egypt, and Turkey. The studies focused on treating non-vital immature permanent teeth using bioceramic cements (primarily WMTA, comparisons to Biodentine and Totalfill) either for apexification or as a coronal seal in REP. Primary outcomes varied, ranging from binary clinical/radiographic success and changes in root dimension in comparative trials (sample sizes between 26 and 88) to regaining sensibility in smaller cohorts and long-term observations of root development in case reports (Table 1).

Comparative review of overall clinical success rate

Clinical success, defined by the absence of signs, symptoms, and soft tissue pathology (e.g., swelling or fistula), was notably high across most studies. Annamalai et al.10 reported a 100.00% clinical success rate at 12 months. Similarly, the two comparative regenerative studies evaluating alternative bioceramics against WMTA reported high success rates: Aly et al.2 found a 100.00% clinical success rate for Biodentine and 91.66% for MTA, while Bartaw et al.1 reported 100.00% success for Totalfill bioceramic putty and 85.70% for WMTA. In both comparative studies, the differences were not significant (P > .05). Ulusoy et al.,3 comparing various PC (PRP, PRF, PP) to a BC scaffold, also observed that success rates were comparable across all groups, with only two failures among 88 teeth.12 Nageh et al.12 reported that all 15 cases were clinically successful, with symptom resolution. These findings strongly suggest that the elimination of disease is consistently achievable, irrespective of the specific bioceramic or scaffold employed.

Radiographic success rate of WMTA-based treatments

Radiographic success was defined as complete periapical healing (disappearance of periapical radiolucency), reduction in the size of existing lesions, or stable apical barrier formation with no evidence of pathology on follow-up radiographs, in accordance with AAE criteria for regenerative procedures and apexification. Of note, radiographic success rate was the percentage of cases that achieve radiographic success within a studied cohort. The meta-analysis concerning radiographic success, which included 6 studies with 112 observations and 110 events, revealed an exceptionally high overall efficacy. The pooled radiographic success proportion was 0.99 (95% CI: 0.96-1.00) using Freeman–Tukey transformation (Figure 3) and 0.96 (95% CI: 0.90-0.99) via generalised linear mixed model analysis (Supplementary Figure 1), indicating that nearly all treated teeth achieved radiographic healing or stability. Importantly, the analysis indicated no evidence of heterogeneity among the included studies, with τ2 < 0.0001, I2 = 0.00%, and a non-significant test of heterogeneity (Q = 0.01, P = 1.00). Subgroup analysis, based on the type of scaffold used (PC vs Apexification), obtained consistently high success rates. Notably, the PRP + MTA subgroup had a proportion of 0.98 (95% CI: 0.87-1.00), which was nearly identical to that of the PRF + MTA subgroup at 0.98 (95% CI: 0.88-1.00). The test for subgroup differences yielded a Q statistic of 0.00 with a P-value of 1.00, suggesting that the clinical approach used, whether PC or traditional MTA apexification, did not significantly affect the final radiographic success rate of the treatment.

Comparative efficacy of PC versus BC scaffold on radiographic success

The meta-analysis comparing the use of various PC scaffolds (PRP, PRF, or PP) in conjunction with WMTA against the standard BC scaffold plus WMTA for radiographic success included 4 comparative studies comprising a total of 145 observations. As shown in Figure 4, the random effects model revealed a trend towards greater efficacy for the intervention group when comparing all platelet-based methods to the BC control, with a pooled OR of 3.56 (95% CI: 0.87-14.52). Although the point estimate favoured the intervention, statistical significance was not reached as the CI crossed the null value. Importantly, no heterogeneity was detected across the studies (I2 = 0.00%, P = .52). Subgroup analysis showed a significant effect for PRP + MTA group (OR = 7.49; 95% CI: 1.16-48.22), while the PRF + MTA subgroup and the PP + MTA subgroup did not reach significance. The test for subgroup differences was non-significant (P = .43), indicating that the relative benefits of PRP, PRF, and PP were not statistically distinguishable from one another in enhancing radiographic outcomes relative to BC.

Forest plot of odds ratios for radiographic success comparing PC scaffolds (PRP, PRF, and PP) with BC scaffold, both applied with WMTA in REP.

Comparative clinical success of WMTA versus alternative bioceramic cements

The comparative meta-analysis evaluating the clinical success rate of WMTA versus alternative bioceramic cements (Totalfill Putty and Biodentine) included 2 studies with a total of 67 observations and 63 clinical success events. While the alternative bioceramic groups achieved a 100.00% clinical success rate, numerically outperforming WMTA, the difference was not statistically significant. As shown in Figure 5, the overall result, assessed by the random effects model, remained statistically non-significant, with an OR of 0.18 (95% CI: 0.02-1.68) crossing the null threshold. Furthermore, there was no evidence of heterogeneity between the two studies (I² = 0.00%, χ² = 0.13, P = .71).

Radiographic success and periapical healing

Radiographic success, commonly defined by the reduction or complete healing of periapical radiolucency, has shown remarkable efficacy in various studies. Annamalai et al.10 reported a 100.00% radiographic success rate for MTA apexification at 12 months. In the comparative study by Alagl et al.,11 all 30 teeth showed reduction in the size of the periapical lesion and increased bone density.10 Both the PRP and BC groups demonstrated significant reductions in lesion size (e.g., PRP: from 3.92 ± 1.46 mm→ 1.50 ± 1.08 mm) and significant increases in bone density (e.g., PRP: 120.56 ± 61.47→ 485.88 ± 154.15 HU), with no significant differences between the two groups (P > .10). Bartaw et al.1 observed a comparable 90.50% radiographic success rate for both Totalfill bioceramic putty and WMTA. Similarly, Nageh et al.12 noted healing of periapical lesions in all cases. The consistent healing observed across various methods underscores the reliability of bioceramic materials in the treatment of necrotic young permanent teeth.

Pulp sensibility regained

The restoration of pulp sensibility, evaluated through positive responses to cold or EPT, serves as a crucial indicator of potential re-innervation and the development of functional pulp-like tissue. Nageh et al.,12 utilising a PRF scaffold in mature teeth, documented a significant increase in EPT sensitivity over 12 months, increasing from baseline to 60.00% (9/15) at 12 months (P < .0001). Alagl et al.11 reported that 76.70% (23/30) of all teeth showed a delayed positive response to cold/EPT at 5 months. The study by Ulusoy et al.3 reported an overall 86.00% positive response to sensitivity testing across the PRP, PRF, PP, and BC groups, and there were no significant differences in the initial response time between the groups (P > .05). The high rates of sensibility restoration observed in multiple regenerative studies support the notion of responsive tissue formation following these treatment protocols.

Root development (length and width)

Root development increases in root length and dentinal wall thickness, representing a primary objective of true regenerative endodontics. Annamalai et al.10 reported a relatively low root growth rate of 30.00% in the MTA apexification study. In contrast, regenerative studies frequently demonstrate higher rates of success. Alagl et al.11 indicated that 73.00% of teeth exhibited continued root development. Notably, the PRP group experienced a significantly greater increase in root length compared to the BC group (P = .004). Aly et al.2 found no significant differences in root length increases between the Biodentine and MTA groups, with both achieving an average increase of 0.70 mm (P = .897). Ulusoy et al.3 reported no significant differences in root length or width increases among the PRP, PRF, PP, and BC groups (P > .05). However, they did find that the BC group exhibited significantly greater increases in radiographic root area and radiographic canal area compared to most platelet concentrate groups (P < .05), suggesting better overall dentinal wall thickening with the simpler BC technique.3

Tooth discolouration

Tooth discolouration poses a significant aesthetic complication associated with bioceramic cements, particularly WMTA. Aly et al.2 reported a statistically significant difference in discolouration rates between the two materials: 58.33% for the MTA group versus only 7.69% for the Biodentine group (P = .01). Bartaw et al.1 also reported differences, with 14.30% of the WMTA group exhibiting discolouration compared to 0.00% for the Totalfill bioceramic putty group, although the difference was not statistically significant. Collectively, these findings highlight the significantly higher risk of discolouration associated with WMTA compared to novel alternative bioceramic materials when employed as a coronal seal.

Synthesis of clinical and radiographic outcomes in bioceramic endodontic treatment

Evidence from prospective clinical studies and detailed case reports on bioceramic-based treatments underscores both the considerable promise and specific challenges associated with these protocols, particularly in terms of long-term follow-up. Clinical success was overwhelmingly positive across regenerative endodontic treatments (RET) and vital pulp therapy, often achieving a 100.00% success rate.13,14 Radiographically, successful periapical healing was consistently observed, with high resolution rates (e.g., 90.30%5) and complete healing documented in numerous cases over follow-up periods extending up to 5 years15 and even 12 years.18 Notably, while case reports provide valuable insights into long-term stability, such study designs are inherently susceptible to publication and selection bias; thus, they serve to generate hypotheses for future rigorous clinical trials rather than to establish definitive efficacy. Functional outcomes, a hallmark of RET, are evidenced by the restoration of pulp sensibility, confirmed by a delayed positive EPT response in multiple case reports.4,15 However, the pattern of root maturation remains highly variable and often unpredictable: while some cases achieved ideal outcomes with 100.00% root development and apex closure.13,18 other studies reported a wide range of changes (e.g., root length change from −2.70% to 25.30%; dentin thickness change from −1.90% to 72.60%).5 Some case series specifically noted successful healing without any increase in root length or thickness despite the use of potent scaffolds like PRF,17,18 indicating that successful healing does not necessarily guarantee continued development. Long-term complications are a notable concern: WMTA used as a coronal barrier frequently resulted in tooth discolouration in a majority of cases, with rates ranging from 62.50% in one study5 to prominent gray-blue staining reported in several others,14,15,17 often necessitating subsequent internal bleaching to manage aesthetic outcomes.14,15 Furthermore, rare late-stage failures have been documented, such as a case that developed late periapical pathology requiring conventional root canal treatment at 3 years,19 highlighting the importance of prolonged monitoring (e.g., 36 months or longer) to confirm true stability (Table 2).

Publication bias assessment

Potential publication bias was assessed by visual inspection of funnel plots for all meta-analyses. The funnel plots for the radiographic success rate meta-analysis, the comparison of PC versus BC scaffolds, and the comparison of alternative bioceramics versus WMTA all demonstrated symmetrical distribution (Supplementary Figure 2).

Certainty of evidence

As detailed in supplementary table 3, the GRADE assessment revealed that the certainty of evidence for radiographic success was rated as very low, downgraded for serious risk of bias due to the inclusion of non-randomised studies, which are inherently susceptible to confounding, selection bias, and lack of allocation concealment. Additionally, serious imprecision was identified, attributable to the limited total sample size and wide 95% CI, thereby reducing the reliability of the pooled estimate. Similarly, the certainty for PC versus BC scaffolds comparison was also very low, downgraded for serious indirectness, reflecting substantial clinical heterogeneity in PC preparation protocols (e.g., PRP, PRF, and PP differ in centrifugation parameters, platelet activation methods, and growth factor release kinetics) and very serious imprecision due to sparse data and wide CI in subgroup analyses. In contrast, the comparison of WMTA versus alternative bioceramics for clinical success was rated moderate, downgraded only for imprecision stemming from the small number of included trials and limited event numbers. Collectively, these ratings underscore that further high-quality, adequately powered RCTs are very likely to alter the confidence for most outcomes.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis integrated data from prospective clinical trials, RCTs, and case series to evaluate WMTA and alternative bioceramics in REP and apexification for necrotic immature permanent teeth. Here, our findings underscore the substantial clinical and radiographic success of these protocols, with WMTA-based treatments achieving a pooled success proportion of nearly 98.00%. Nevertheless, nuanced distinctions were observed when comparing scaffold types (e.g., PC versus BC) and material compositions (e.g., WMTA versus Biodentine or Totalfill), particularly concerning secondary outcomes such as root maturation, pulp sensibility restoration, and tooth discolouration.

REP represents a paradigm shift in the management of necrotic immature teeth,20 functioning as a biological procedure designed to replace damaged structures, including dentin, root, and cells of the pulp-dentin complex.21 These procedures frequently utilise PC, such as PRP and PRF, which have shown promise in promoting apical maturation.22 WMTA remains the predominant barrier material owing to its superior biocompatibility, bioactivity, and ability to protect underlying scaffolds.23 Furthermore, this material facilitates apical barrier formation and periapical healing within months, thereby enhancing overall treatment success rates.24

Low heterogeneity was observed across analyses of WMTA radiographic success, PC versus BC scaffolds, and WMTA versus alternative bioceramics. Potential contributors likely contributed to this consistency: (1) methodological consistency, as all studies adhered to standardised disinfection protocols and WMTA coronal sealing, minimising procedural variability. This standardised sealing technique is critical for maintaining material stability, aligning with findings in other dental regeneration fields, such as alveolar ridge preservation, regarding barrier membrane applications25; (2) consistent follow-up durations and outcome definitions, which minimised misclassification bias; (3) homogeneous patient populations focused on young patients (8-18 years) with immature permanent teeth. This demographic similarity likely stabilises success rates,26,27 as younger teeth possess wider apical foramina and mesenchymal stem cells with higher proliferative and differentiation capacities, facilitating tissue ingrowth28; and (4) a “ceiling effect” resulting from high baseline success rates (approaching 100.00%), which limits the statistical power to detect heterogeneity, a limitation further exacerbated by small sample sizes.

However, these findings warrant cautious interpretation. Wide CI for I² suggest potential underestimation due to the limited number of studies, and publication bias favouring positive outcomes may exclude heterogeneous failures. According to the GRADE framework, the certainty of evidence was rated as very low for radiographic success and PC versus BC comparisons, whereas it was rated as moderate for WMTA versus alternative bioceramics. These ratings indicate that future high-quality RCTs are likely to influence the confidence in effect estimates for most outcomes.

Clinical cohort studies and RCTs (n = 6) provided more consistent and quantifiable evidence than case reports (n = 9). While both evidence types indicated high success rates (95.00%-100.00% vs 90.00%-100.00%), case reports exhibited greater qualitative variability and lower statistical rigour. This discrepancy is inherent to the study designs, that is, clinical cohorts and RCTs provide powered comparative evidence with predefined endpoints, yielding reliable pooled estimates. In contrast, while case reports offer valuable long-term insights, they are highly susceptible to selection bias, which may inflate perceptions of success.

A significant drawback of WMTA is the risk of tooth discolouration,29 potentially caused by metal oxides (e.g., bismuth, aluminium, magnesium, or iron) or contamination from blood and bioactive materials.30 Clinical studies indicated a significantly higher discolouration risk for WMTA compared to alternative bioceramics, a finding corroborated by case reports. Conversely, meta-analyses on minimally invasive pulp surgery utilising novel bioceramics versus WMTA for apical sealing reported no significant difference in the number of successful or failed teeth.31 Furthermore, recent animal histological evidence suggests that MTA may exhibit inferior pulp biocompatibility and dentin bridge quality compared to bioactive glass-loaded hydrogels.32

In conclusion, this systematic review highlights critical gaps. Firstly, there is a scarcity of high-quality RCTs with long-term follow-up, limiting the assessment of long-term efficacy. Secondly, direct comparative evidence between WMTA and emerging biomaterials is insufficient. Future research should prioritise head-to-head comparisons between WMTA and novel alternative bioceramics or functional hydrogels to optimise biocompatibility and clinical outcomes in REP.

Limitations

This meta-analysis and systematic review have several limitations. Firstly, the limited number of studies included and their modest sample sizes may restrict statistical power and generalisability, potentially obscuring subtle differences or heterogeneity.1,2 Secondly, the prevalence of short to medium-term follow-ups (12-36 months) may fail to capture long-term complications, such as late periapical pathology or resorption, as highlighted in case reports. Thirdly, there is a possibility of publication bias toward positive outcomes, which could lead to an overestimation of success rates, as studies with negative or inconclusive results are less likely to be published. Lastly, the overall low certainty of evidence for most outcomes, as indicated by the GRADE assessment, underscores the preliminary nature of current conclusions and highlights the need for more rigorous primary studies. Future studies should prioritise addressing these gaps through larger, standardised RCTs with extended follow-up periods to validate these results.

Conclusion

Bioceramic-based treatments achieve high clinical and radiographic success for non-vital immature permanent teeth in children. However, the low certainty of evidence warrants cautious interpretation. The choice of material influences discolouration risk, and scaffold selection may affect root maturation patterns. Future high-quality RCTs with standardised protocols are needed to strengthen these findings.
