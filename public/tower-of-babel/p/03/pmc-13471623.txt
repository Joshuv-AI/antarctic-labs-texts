Predictors of a successful external cephalic version: a population-based cohort study
Authors: Shan Liu, Lingyan Chen, Guilian Liao, Danling Cheng, Yuyan Huang, Jinying Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExternal cephalic version (ECV) corrects breech presentation and improves vaginal delivery success rate.ObjectiveTo investigate factors influencing ECV success in breech-presenting pregnant women in southern China.MethodsA total of 484 pregnant women who underwent ECV from August 2021 to August 2023 were enrolled. Logistic regression analyzed factors associated with ECV success, and restricted cubic spline (RCS) evaluated relationships of amniotic fluid index (AFI) and gestational weight gain with ECV success.ResultsIn all ECV, the success rate was 75.41%. Logistic analysis showed that pregnancy weight gain was a risk factor for ECV, and the success rate decreased with weight increase, OR = 1.05(1.01, 1.09). Shallow into the basin of the fetal breech was a protective factor for ECV, OR (95%CI) = 0.10(0.03, 0.33) compared with not into the basin. Single nuchal cord can increase the probability of ECV success compared with no nuchal cord (OR (95%CI) = 0.43(0.20, 0.91)). The success gradually increases with AFI’s rise, OR (95%CI) = 0.06 (0.01, 0.23). RCS analysis revealed non-linear relationships between ECV success and AFI, as well as between ECV success and pregnancy weight gain. ECV success rate increased with AFI within the range of 13.2–17.0 cm. And for pregnancy weight gain, the success rate increased within 5.5–12.0 kg but decreased when weight gain was 12.0–20.0 kg.ConclusionExcessive pregnancy weight gain can reduce the success rate of ECV, while AFI, shallow into the basin, and single nuchal cord are protective factors. Non-linear relationships exist between gestational weight gain, AFI, and ECV success. Clinically, these findings advise women to monitor AFI around 13.2–17.0 cm and control weight gain within 5.5–12.0 kg after 28weeks to improve ECV success.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09448-9.

Background

Breech is one of the most common abnormal fetal positions in obstetrics. The incidence of breech during full-term pregnancy is 3%-4% [1]. Natural delivery in breech has a high risk of fetal asphyxia and intrauterine accidents. Existing clinical study had confirmed that vaginal breech delivery is associated with increased perinatal mortality and short-term neonatal morbidity [2]. Specifically, the perinatal mortality rate of breech delivery is 3 times higher than that of cephalic delivery, and the incidence of various functional disorders caused by birth trauma is 12 times higher [3]. Consistent with these findings, current obstetric guidelines recommend cesarean section as the preferred mode of delivery for full-term breech fetuses. Clinically, we have also observed a gradual increase in the proportion of planned cesarean sections for full-term breech presentation in recent years [2].

However, cesarean section is associated with a series of adverse maternal outcomes, including prolonged hospital stay, postoperative fever, surgical site infection, and increased postpartum hemorrhage [4]. More importantly, 80%-96% of women with a primary cesarean section opt for repeat cesarean section in subsequent pregnancies to avoid uterine rupture risk related to vaginal birth after cesarean (VBAC) [5]. Repeat cesarean sections further elevate the risk of severe maternal complications such as placenta accreta, uterine scar defects, and adhesive intestinal obstruction, which affect immediate maternal recovery and pose long-term threats to reproductive health and quality of life [6–8]. Dahlquist K et al. pointed out that planned cesarean section has a higher risk of short-term maternal complications than planned vaginal delivery [9], so vaginal delivery is a better choice for both mothers and newborns.

External cephalic version (ECV) is a conventional manual intervention in obstetrics, which involves manipulating the fetus through the maternal abdominal wall to convert non-cephalic positions (breech, transverse lie) into cephalic presentation, thereby facilitating vaginal delivery. As a safe and minimally invasive procedure, ECV can effectively reduce the incidence of full-term breech presentation and the corresponding cesarean section rate [10, 11], thus mitigating dual risks of breech vaginal delivery and cesarean section for mothers and neonates. Previous study had identified several potential predictors of ECV success, including amniotic fluid index (AFI), absence of placenta previa, favorable pelvic conditions, and body mass index (BMI) < 25 [12]. Additionally, clinical study had confirmed that preterm ECV is more effective in reducing non-cephalic delivery than no ECV attempt [13], providing evidence for the optimal timing of ECV in clinical practice.

However current ECV success rates vary widely [14], and existing studies have explored the predictive factors of ECV success in other populations and regions, there are significant differences in the utilization and promotion of ECV among different regions [15–17], which may leading to unnecessary procedures for low-probability candidates. As a mega city with strong population mobility, Shenzhen has distinct population characteristics compared to other regions in China, such as a younger age structure of pregnant women, a higher first childbirth rate, a large number of floating population, and a unique clinical practice [18]. The unique characteristics of these regions may lead to differences in ECV success rates and influencing factor spectra.

Therefore, by identifying reliable predictive factors, we can classify patients into risk classes, thereby avoiding surgery with low success rates and reducing the occurrence of iatrogenic complications. This investigation holds particular clinical significance for optimizing ECV implementation through three key mechanisms: (1) Enhanced patient selection improves procedural cost-effectiveness by targeting high-success-probability candidates, thereby minimizing physical and psychological burdens on unsuitable pregnant women; (2) Modifiable factors can be systematically optimized through targeted interventions to elevate regional ECV success rates; (3) The establishment of localized, evidence-based guidelines will standardize clinical practice, ultimately reducing breech-related cesarean deliveries and improving perinatal outcomes across the region.

Methods

Study population and design

This is a retrospective cohort study conducted in the obstetrics department of a tertiary hospital that supervises approximately 15,000 deliveries per year. Ethic approval was obtained from Institutional Review Board of Maternity & Child Healthcare Hospital of Longgang District, Shenzhen City (LGFYKYXMLL-2024-07). 549 women age ≥ 18 years, with singleton pregnancies, who underwent ECV from August 2021 to August 2023 were identified. Referring to previous studies on the success factors of ECV, assuming a moderate effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5), a significance level (α) of 0.05, and a power of statistics (1- β) of 0.90, the estimated minimum sample size required is 385. Considering the possibility of a 15% loss due to follow-up or missing data, the initial sample size was expanded to 450. Among the 549 women, 65 were excluded due to missing key clinical data, including 28 cases with incomplete prenatal ultrasound records (unrecorded placental location or fetal presentation), 25 cases lacking data on pregnancy weight gain, 12 cases with missing intraoperative monitoring indicators (duration of ECV procedure or fetal heart rate changes. There was no significant difference in baseline characteristics between women with missing data and those included, P > 0.05. A total of 484 women were finally included in this study, exceeding the estimated minimum requirement.

ECVs were performed in accordance with a standardized hospital protocol. Women with singleton pregnancies diagnosed with breech presentation at antenatal clinics after 37 weeks of gestation were counseled on ECV by attending residents if there were no contraindications to vaginal delivery. Contraindications for ECV included placenta previa, fetal macrosomia, history of ≥ 2 cesarean sections, uterine malformation, premature rupture of membranes, and multiple pregnancies. Eligible women were admitted to the hospital for further evaluation. Pre-procedure ultrasound was performed to confirm fetal presentation, placental location, and fetal heart rate. Subcutaneous injection of 0.25 mg terbutaline sulfate was administered to inhibit uterine contractions if there were no contraindications to the drug.

After the patient signs a written informed consent form, ECV operation will be performed in the operating room by 2 senior obstetricians with more than 5 years of clinical experience in ECV operation, aiming to minimize differences in force application, manipulation angle, and duration control. Not all subjects receiving ECV receive routine anesthesia. Provide selective anesthesia based on the following criteria: (1) women with a history of pelvic surgery or suspected pelvic adhesions (to alleviate pain during the procedure); (2) women who experience severe pain during preoperative evaluation refuse to continue surgery without anesthesia; (3) ultrasound shows cases where the fetal position is tense and requires prolonged operation. For eligible cases, spinal anesthesia is performed by anesthesiologists to ensure maternal comfort while maintaining fetal safety. Women without the above indications received ECV without anesthesia and continued psychological counseling to alleviate anxiety.

During the operation, continuously record fetal heart rate, maternal blood pressure, and pulse. During the operation, slight adjustments were made based on the individual characteristics of the pregnant woman. For women with a history of cesarean section or thin uterine wall (assessed by preoperative ultrasound), the intensity of the procedure was appropriately reduced. For women with excessive uterine contractions despite subcutaneous injection of 0.25 mg terbutaline sulfate, the operation was temporarily stopped. If complications such as placental abruption, umbilical cord prolapse, and fetal distress occur, emergency cesarean section should be performed. Ultrasound is used to monitor the surgical process and repeat examinations after operation to confirm the success of the operation. After ECV, fetal heart rate monitoring (FHM) was performed immediately, and the patient was transferred to the obstetric ward for further observation, including at least two rounds of FHM to ensure fetal stability. These adjustments are consistent with the standardization plan and do not introduce any additional procedural deviations.

Data collection

Data were extracted from electronic medical records and paper medical charts. Collected variables included age, height, education level (college or above, below college), pregnancy weight gain (admission weight-pre-pregnancy weight), gestational age, operation time, parity (nulliparous, multiparous), number of gestations (included both the number of births, stillbirths, and miscarriages) (0,1,≥2), delivery status, amniotic fluid index (AFI), fetal presentation ( difficulty in fetal head palpation), placental location (anterior, posterior, other), nuchal cord (0, 1, ≥ 2), breech type (single breech, mixed breech, transverse) and fetal pelvic entry status (not into the basin, shallow into the basin, deep basin). Not into the basin means the fetal breech above the pelvic inlet, movable above the pubic symphysis. Shallow into the basin means the breech partially descended into the pelvic inlet but not fixed. And the deep basin means the breech fully past the pelvic inlet, at/below the ischial spine, and fixed [19].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis is carried out under statistical methods after access is granted by statistical members. Stata 15.0 and R 4.2.2 software were used to sort out the data and carry out statistical description and analysis. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) if normally distributed, or median (interquartile range, IQR) if abnormally distributed. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Univariate analyses were performed using the Chi-square test (for categorical variables), t-test (for normally distributed continuous variables), and analysis of variance (ANOVA) (for multiple groups of normally distributed continuous variables). Multivariate regression analysis was conducted using Stata 15.0.

Multivariable logistic regression model was employed to examine the associations. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was performed using R 4.2.2 software. RCS is used to explore the nonlinear relationship between continuous variables and ECV success rate. The model used 4 knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of each continuous variable to capture non-linear trends while avoiding overfitting. We further performed interaction analyses using restricted cubic spline regression to evaluate the potential effect modification between AFI and pregnancy weight gain on ECV success. The Wald test was used to examine overall interaction and nonlinear interaction terms.

Sensitivity validation of this study was conducted through stratified analysis of nulliparous and multiparous women. The population included in this study have complete datasets with no missing variables, so there is no need to handle missing values.

Results

Overview of the study population

A total of 484 pregnant women who underwent external cephalic version (ECV) in southern China from August 2021 to August 2023 were included in this study, with 365 successful cases (success rate = 75.41%) and 119 failed cases (failure rate = 24.59%). As shown in Table 1, the success rate for the operation time > 2 min group was only 36.87% (66/182), compared to 97.34% (183/188) for = 2 min group and 99.15% (116/117) for the <2 min group. Univariate analysis indicated that the ECV success rate was significantly associated with maternal age (P = 0.018), pregnancy weight gain (P = 0.003), AFI (P < 0.001), parity (P = 0.003) operation time (P < 0.001), fetal pelvic entry status (P < 0.001), fetal presentation (P < 0.001), and anesthesia use (P < 0.001). In contrast, maternal number of pregnancies (P = 0.104), loops of nuchal cord (P = 0.967), placenta location (P = 0.945), gestational age (P = 0.065), and education level (P = 0.077) had no statistically significant effect on the ECV success rate.

Multiple regression model for identifying factors influencing ECV success

Multiple regression model revealed that operation time, fetal pelvic entry status, loops of nuchal cord, AFI, and pregnancy weight gain were the main independent factors affecting ECV success (Table 2). After controlling for potential confounding variables such as maternal age, anesthesia use, parity, placental position, and breech type in multivariate model, operation time > 2 min was a strong risk factor for ECV success compared to operation time = 2 min (OR (95%CI) = 95.7 (30.91, 296.01), P < 0.001). Compared with not into the basin, shallow into the basin was a protective factor for ECV success (OR(95%CI) = 0.10(0.03,0.33), P < 0.001), indicating that fetuses in shallow into the basin had a significantly lower risk of ECV failure, and deep into the basin showed no significant difference compared with not into the basin (OR(95%CI) = 0.62(0.27,1.41), P = 0.256). Compared with 0 loop, single nuchal cord reduced the risk of ECV success (OR (95%CI) = 0.43(0.20,0.91), P = 0.028), while ≥ 2 loops of nuchal cord had no significant effect on ECV (OR = 0.62(0.27,1.41), P = 0.674). The ECV success rate increased with the increase of AFI, OR (95%CI) = 0.06(0.01,0.23), P < 0.001), indicating that higher AFI is a protective factor for ECV success. The ECV success rate decreased as pregnancy weight gain increased, with each additional 1 kg of weight gain associated with a 5% decrease in the odds of ECV success, OR (95%CI) = 1.05(1.01,1.09), P = 0.019. However, in this multiple regression model, there was no statistically significant correlation between placental position, maternal age, and number of pregnancies and ECV success (P > 0.05).

RCS analysis of the associations between AFI, pregnancy weight gain, and ECV outcomes

RCS analysis was used to further analyze the nonlinear relationship between AFI, pregnancy weight gain, and ECV success. The results confirmed a non-linear “S” - shaped relationship between ECV success rate and AFI, the ECV success rate increased steadily within the clinically optimal range of 13.2–17.0 cm, after which the success rate plateaued or slightly declined (Fig. 1A). For pregnancy weight gain, ECV success increased within the optimal range of 5.5–12.0 kg, but success decreased significantly when weight gain exceeded 12.0 kg (Fig. 1B). These nonlinear patterns indicate that the relationships of AFI and pregnancy weight gain with ECV success are not linear. The optimal ranges for ECV success are 13.2–17.0 cm for AFI and 5.5–12.0 kg for pregnancy weight gain, and clinical management should target these ranges to maximize ECV success.

Formal interaction analysis showed that the overall interaction between pregnancy weight gain and AFI was not statistically significant (P = 0.0783). A marginally significant nonlinear interaction was observed (P = 0.0509). Further decomposition showed that the nonlinear modifying effect of AFI on the association between pregnancy weight gain and ECV success was statistically significant (P = 0.0361), while the nonlinear modifying effect of pregnancy weight gain on the AFI-outcome association was not statistically significant (P = 0.5479) (Additional Table 1).

Sensitivity analysis

In this study, pregnant women were divided into nulliparous and multiparous groups for logical analysis. The results indicate that operation time, fetal pelvic entry status, and AFI are important factors for the success of nulliparous and multiparous ECV. Age and delivery status mainly affect the success rate of nulliparous, while loops of nuchal cord, placental localization, and pregnancy weight gain mainly affect the success rate of multiparous (Additional Table 2).

Discussion

A total of 484 pregnant women who underwent ECV in southern China from August 2021 to August 2023 were included, with a success rate of 75.41%. The results showed that ECV success was associated with nuchal cord loop count, fetal pelvic entry status, amniotic fluid index (AFI), pregnancy weight gain, and operation time. Among them, after adjusting the potential confounding factors in the multivariable model, single nuchal cord and shallow into the basin were promoting factors for ECV success, while operation time > 2 min was an independent risk factor for ECV success and its odds ratio was still very high (OR (95%CI) = 95.7(30.91,296.01) (P < 0.001)). RCS results showed a non-linear relationship between AFI, pregnancy weight gain and ECV success. To our knowledge, we are the first to identify a protective effect of single nuchal cord on ECV success, and the first to explore the association between pregnancy weight gain and ECV success.

Nuchal cord is a very common in obstetric practice during late pregnancy. incomplete statistics show its incidence is as high as 10–29%, and the incidence increases with advancing gestational age [20]. Our study found for the first time that single nuchal cord is conducive to ECV completion, and these finding challenges conventional clinical wisdom, which generally holds that any nuchal cord restricts fetal mobility and hinders ECV. However, we observed moderated amniotic fluid volume in fetuses with a single nuchal cord, which is consistent with previous studies [21, 22]. Moderate amniotic fluid not only loosens the nuchal cord to avoid tight compression on the fetal neck but also expands intrauterine space, enhancing fetal mobility which is the key physiological basis for facilitating ECV. ECV relies on gentle external manipulation to guide fetal position rotation, and successful version is largely dependent on the coordination of active fetal movement. In the setting of a loose single nuchal cord and adequate amniotic fluid, the fetus maintains good mobility, allowing ECV to be completed more smoothly, even within 2 min. However, multiple nuchal cords (≥ 2) limit fetal intrauterine activity due to increased cord tension, which elevates ECV difficulty. Moreover, multiple nuchal cords are prone to causing fetal heart rate deceleration during manipulation, forcing repeated attempts that prolong operation time and ultimately reduce ECV success rates.

Our study once again highlights the critical role of AFI in ECV success, which is consistent with previous studies [23–25]. The sufficient amount of AFI can better allow the fetus to float, cushion the umbilical cord from compression and protect the fetus [26], and creates favorable conditions for clinicians to perform ECV efficiently without prolonged manipulation [27]. RCS results showed that excessive AFI decreased the success rate of ECV. In prospective studies, it was found that hyperamniotic fluid led to an increased risk of fetal respiratory distress [28], as well as an overexpansion of the uterus and increased uterine sensitivity [29], and these changes elevate abdominal wall tension, limit fetal rotation, and complicate manual manipulation. And often prolonging operation time as clinicians attempt to achieve successful ECV. Notably, clinical study had confirmed that both oligohydramnios and polyhydramnios are contraindications for ECV due to increased procedural risk and reduced success rate [30]. This further supports our conclusion that maintaining AFI within the normal range is pivotal for ECV. We also found that the optimal AFI range for ECV is 13.2–17.0 cm, which aligns with the appropriate AFI reference range for the third trimester [31, 32]. This suggests maintaining normal amniotic fluid volume is not only key to ECV success but also helps control operation time within the favorable ≤ 2 min.

Our study is the first to show a non-linear relationship between pregnancy weight gain and ECV success. Most previous studies have focused on gestational BMI changes, with inconsistent findings regarding the association between BMI and ECV success [33–35]. From an obstetric perspective, optimal pregnancy weight gain maintains maternal physical health, prevents excessive obesity, and ensures fetal well-being [36]. More importantly, it keeps the maternal abdominal wall thin and elastic, allowing clinicians to clearly identify fetal anatomical landmarks and apply precise rotational force during ECV and facilitating both procedural success and completion within 2 min. Population and experimental studies have shown that obese pregnant women have increased uterine contraction activity and reduced myometrial sensitivity [37, 38]. Excessive pregnancy weight gain is likely to lead to fat accumulation in the abdomen, and increase maternal uterine contraction, which not only increase ECV difficulty but also prolong operation time due to repeated manipulation attempts, and reflecting inherent maternal factors that hinder fetal position transition. Our study revealed that the optimal pregnancy weight gain range for ECV is 5.5–12.0 kg, gains exceeding 12.0 kg decrease ECV success rates. This pregnancy weight gain range is also found in most studies to be the optimal pregnancy weight gain during pregnancy [39–41] and is within the range recommended by the Institute of Medicine 2009. Moreover, recent international study emphasized that scientific weight management during pregnancy reduces maternal complications and optimizes obstetric outcomes [42], which further validates the clinical value of our proposed weight gain range for ECV. Thus, maintaining reasonable weight gain not only benefits maternal-fetal health but also facilitates ECV success, reduces prolonged operation time, and lowers cesarean section rates. In this study, we found that AFI could significantly modify the nonlinear association between pregnancy weight gain and ECV success, which suggests that the effect of pregnancy weight gain on ECV outcome may vary with different amniotic fluid levels. However, we did not find a significant modifying effect of pregnancy weight gain on the association between AFI and ECV success, which may be due to the relatively narrow range of pregnancy weight gain in our study population, and this finding needs to be confirmed in larger studies.

From a clinical perspective, the nonlinear patterns identified in this study have important practical implications. The spline analyses revealed clear, actionable thresholds that can directly guide clinical decision-making. AFI between 13.2 and 17.0 cm provides sufficient intrauterine space for fetal rotation while avoiding the technical difficulties associated with either oligohydramnios or polyhydramnios. Pregnancy weight gain between 5.5 and 11.5 kg is associated with the highest ECV success rates, likely by maintaining optimal maternal abdominal wall compliance and fetal mobility. These ranges are consistent with routine clinical standards and can be used to counsel women prenatally to optimize conditions before ECV attempt. Notably, the confidence intervals for the spline estimates became quite wide at the lower and upper extremes of both pregnancy weight gain and AFI distributions. This was primarily due to sparse observations in these tails. Fewer than 5% of patients had pregnancy weight gain outside the range of 0–20 kg, and AFI values below 6 cm or above 19 cm. The limited sample size in these extreme ranges reduces the precision of the non-linear trend estimates, and these findings should be interpreted with caution.

We found a strong correlation between operation time > 2 min and ECV success. The success rate of operation time < 2 min was 99.15% (116/117), while = 2 min was 97.34% (183/188), but > 2 min was only 36.87% (66/182). This significant gradient confirms a clear dose-response relationship between operation time and ECV success. After further controlling for potential confounding factors in the multivariate model, the association between operation time > 2 min and ECV success remained statistically significant (P < 0.001). In fact, prolonged operation time is not the direct cause of ECV failure, but rather a manifestation of inherent maternal-fetal factors that may increase procedural difficulty or hinder fetal position conversion. Relevant studies have shown that factors such as fetal overgrowth and abnormal uterine tension can affect the feasibility of ECV [43, 44]. These inherent factors not only elevate the technical complexity of ECV, necessitating prolonged operation time, but also directly reduce the success rate of the procedure. Additionally, prolonged operation may lead to increased tension in the maternal abdominal wall or repeated umbilical cord traction, which further raises the risk of fetal heart rate abnormalities and results in surgical termination [44]. This forms a vicious cycle that strengthens the link between prolonged operation time and ECV failure. Collectively, these findings align with the understanding that ECV success is influenced by multiple maternal-fetal and procedural factors [43], and further support our conclusion that prolonged operation time reflects underlying procedural difficulties rather than being a mere confounding variable.

Strength and limitation

Our study is the first to identify that single nuchal cord and pregnancy weight gain are the most influential factors for ECV success. Moreover, restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves were used to explore the linear and nonlinear relationships between AFI, pregnancy weight gain, and ECV success rate, thereby better clarifying the correlation between these factors and ECV outcomes as well as their associated interval ranges.

However, this study has several limitations. Firstly, the research subjects were limited to a single center, and southern Chinese women, whose dietary habits, weight characteristics, and local ECV plans may differ from those of other ethnic or regional populations, limiting external validity. Even if we adopt consistent operating standards, single center data may overestimate the association between specific factors and ECV success. Secondly, participants with missing key clinical data were excluded from the final analysis. This exclusion may introduce selection bias, reduce the representativeness of the study population, and limit the external validity and generalizability of our findings. Thirdly, although we performed interaction analyses between pregnancy weight gain and AFI, the overall interaction was not statistically significant. Thus, the combined effect of these two variables needs to be confirmed in larger studies. Finally, estimates at the extreme lower and upper ranges of AFI and pregnancy weight gain had relatively wide confidence intervals due to sparse data; these findings should be interpreted with caution. Future studies could expand to multi-center data collection, optimize data completeness, and incorporate interaction analyses between key nonlinear factors to provide more robust evidence for clinical decision-making.

Conclusion

This study identified the key factors affecting the success of external cephalic version (ECV), namely the protective effect of single nuchal cord, while operation time > 2 min is an independent risk factor. It also confirmed the non-linear relationship between amniotic fluid index and pregnancy weight gain and ECV results, which is the first report on the protective effect of single nuchal cord and the association between AFI/weight gain and ECV success. These specific optimal ranges (AFI 13.2–17.0 cm, pregnancy weight gain 5.5–12.0 kg) provide clear, evidence-based targets for clinical practice. After 28 weeks of pregnancy, if the fetus in a breech position may require ECV, doctors need to provide nutritional and exercise guidance to control weight gain during pregnancy below 12.0 kg, and emphasize that weight gain exceeding 12.0 kg will reduce the probability of successful ECV. Before ECV, doctors should evaluate the status of the nuchal cord to avoid ruling out single nuchal cord with normal AFI. At the same time, AFI needs to be evaluated. Oral liquid should be supplemented when AFI < 13.2 cm, and the cause of polyhydramnios should be investigated when AFI > 17.0 cm. During ECV, the operation should be strictly limited to 2 min to reduce the risk of failure and complications. If the operation time exceeds 2 min, it is necessary to promptly communicate with the mother and family again that this ECV may fail. Overall, these results provide actionable evidence for improving ECV success rates, reducing unnecessary cesarean sections, and enhancing obstetric management of late-stage breech pregnancies.
