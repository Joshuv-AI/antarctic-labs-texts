Dual-Nozzle Electrospinning for Janus Membranes in Membrane Distillation of Highly Saline and Oil-Contaminated Waters
Authors: Michaela Olisha Lobregas, Ratthapol Rangkupan, Hsiu-Po Kuo, Yu-Ming Tu, Chalida Klaysom
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Electrospun nanofibrous membranes are highly valued for membrane distillation (MD) due to their inherent porosity and tunable structure. This study introduces a novel dual-nozzle electrospinning method to fabricate a multilayered Janus membrane, combining an omniphobic layer of PVDF fibers and fluorinated TiO2-PVDF microclusters with a hydrophilic layer of hydrolyzed PMA fibers. The resulting membrane demonstrated exceptional performance and durability. In a 21 h MD operation against oily saline solutions, it retained 80% of its initial flux while producing high-purity water (conductivity <5 μS/cm). Notably, the membrane exhibited complete underwater oil repellence, preventing fouling from oil droplet adhesion. Furthermore, in a 7 h test with highly saline feed, the membrane maintained 99% of its initial flux and permeate conductivity below 60 μS/cm. The success of the membrane is attributed to the dual-nozzle setup, which provides both improved processing efficiency and strong interlayer adhesion, enhancing structural integrity during prolonged MD operations and cleaning cycles. This work presents a robust and scalable method for fabricating high-performance Janus membranes, offering a significant advancement for treating challenging, oil-contaminated water via membrane distillation.

Introduction

Membrane distillation (MD) is a thermally driven separation process that uses a hydrophobic membrane to allow only water vapor to pass through, while retaining nonvolatile contaminants. The process is driven by a vapor pressure difference created by a temperature gradient across the membrane. MD holds particular promise for treating hypersaline brines and complex wastewaters scenarios where pressure-driven technologies like reverse osmosis (RO) become ineffective due to high osmotic pressures or susceptibility to fouling. Unlike RO, MD can handle elevated salinity levels and tolerate a broader range of feed compositions, making it suitable for applications such as RO concentrate treatment, produced water from oil and gas operations, and industrial wastewaters containing organics and emulsified oils. −

However, MD still faces challenges in real-world applications, particularly membrane wetting and fouling when processing oily or surfactant-laden solutions. , These issues limit long-term performance and reliability. Therefore, developing robust membranes with tailored surface properties is essential to fully realize the potential of MD in these demanding applications.

Electrospun nanofibrous membranes are gaining attention in MD applications due to their high porosity and tunable pore size. , For MD membranes to effectively separate and prevent wetting from both feed and permeate liquids, they must possess a hydrophobic nature. However, hydrophobic membranes are susceptible to oil fouling due to the hydrophobic–hydrophobic interaction between the membrane surface and the oil in underwater conditions. Oil droplets are strongly attracted to the membrane surface, where they spread and eventually block the pores, leading to a reduction in vapor flux. To tackle this fouling challenge, surface modification of hydrophobic membranes can enhance their properties, transforming them into omniphobic membranes. These omniphobic membranes feature unique surface architectures and low surface energy, which can be achieved through the incorporation of fluoro-alkyl-silane-modified nanoparticles such as TiO2, , SiO2 and ZnO. As a result, these membranes demonstrate high resistance to a diverse range of liquids and contaminants. A study by Kharraz et al. (2022) showed that the omniphobic membrane exhibited superior oil resistance underwater compared to hydrophobic membranes, successfully preventing oil droplets from spreading and leading to total pore blockage. Nonetheless, it is important to note that while these oil droplets adhered to the surface, they still caused fouling and a decline in performance.

The development of electrospun Janus membranesmembranes with asymmetric wettabilityhas garnered significant attention due to their potential to address oil fouling and capabilities in the separation of oil and water. ,, Janus membranes possess distinct properties on either side, typically exhibiting hydrophobic (nonwetting) and hydrophilic (wetting) characteristics. Both the nonwetting and wetting layers play active roles in the MD process. The hydrophilic wetting layer draws in the aqueous feed while resisting oil foulants, whereas the nonwetting hydrophobic layer prevents the aqueous feed from entering the pores while allowing water vapors to pass through. , Thus, the dual asymmetric layers of Janus membranes provide advantages in achieving high separation efficiency and excellent fouling resistance in MD desalination processes. The Janus membrane design, integrating an omniphobic substrate with a hydrophilic coating, strategically enhances membrane performance by minimizing wetting and fouling, particularly in oil-contaminated environments, while optimizing desalination efficiency.

The fabrication of electrospun Janus membranes remains complex and labor-intensive, involving sequential electrospinning and/or electrospraying of the nonwetting and wetting layers. − Membranes produced through this process are prone to delamination due to the lack of proper intercalation in the fibrous network. This delamination can compromise the stability and durability of the membranes during both the operation and cleaning processes. Therefore, studies on innovative approaches to membrane fabrication are essential to achieve robust and reliable electrospun MD membranes with a layered structure and opposite wettability.

This study presents a novel and facile electrospinning fabrication method for creating multilayered electrospun Janus membranes, designed as a robust solution for desalinating challenging saline waters and treating oily saline feeds. By employing a dual-nozzle electrospinning setup, this method enables the concurrent fabrication of layers with contrasting wetting properties at their interface, significantly reducing fabrication time compared to conventional sequential electrospinning processes. The resulting membranes exhibit an intertwined structural arrangement and enhanced interfacial adhesion. This streamlined fabrication process simplifies membrane production while ensuring the durability of the layered structure under the harsh conditions of prolonged desalination operations and membrane cleaning procedures.

The performance of the fabricated electrospun Janus membrane was evaluated in direct contact membrane distillation (DCMD) processes of different saline, brine, and oily saline feed solutions. Additionally, the study compared the performance of the electrospun Janus membrane with control membranes having symmetric wettability, assessing their fouling resistance, flux retention, and water quality output. Furthermore, the research investigates the potential of the developed membrane as a reliable and effective anti-oil fouling solution in long-duration DCMD operations, specifically over 21 h. To ensure rigorous testing, the membrane underwent alkali cleaning between 7 h runs, demonstrating its durability and ability to withstand harsh DCMD operating conditions. This study aims to contribute to the development of advanced membrane technologies that can meet the demands of modern desalination and water treatment processes, supporting the achievement of sustainable and clean water management.

Materials and Methods

Materials and Chemicals

Poly­(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF, MW = 275 000 g/mol) was sourced from Sigma-Aldrich. Poly­(acrylonitrile-co-methyl acrylate) (PMA, MW = 150 000 g/mol) was acquired from Haihang Industry, China. The solvents N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF, AR 99.8%) and acetone (AR 99.5%) were obtained from RCI Labscan. Lithium chloride (LiCl, RPE) and sodium chloride (NaCl, RPE) were supplied by Carlo Erba Reagents. Titanium dioxide powder (TiO2, > 95%) was purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. 1H,1H,2H,2H-Perfluorodecyltriethoxysilane (FAS, 97%) and mineral oil (light) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. A commercial PVDF membrane (pore size: 0.45 μm) was procured from CNW Technologies.

Membrane Fabrication

Janus membranes, composed of a thin hydrophilic layer atop an omniphobic substrate, were fabricated using a dual-needle spinneret system. The omniphobic layer was fabricated by electrospraying fluorinated TiO2–PVDF microclusters, a method adapted from our previous work. This technique imparts robust liquid repellency by creating a surface with both high roughness and low surface energy, which is essential for achieving the membrane’s omniphobic properties. Schematic illustration of the layer-by-layer fabrication of the membranes. The top surfaces of the membranes are depicted as follows: The Omn membrane primarily consists of PVDF. The Hb-Omn membrane is composed of PMA. The Hb-Omn membrane consists of hydrolyzed PMA.

The membrane fabrication involved three solutions and six sequential segments. Solution 1 was loaded into a 10 mL Luer Lock syringe with a 24G stainless steel blunt needle and electrospun horizontally onto a grounded, aluminum foil-covered drum collector rotating at 200 rpm. The needle was positioned 15 cm from the collector, with an applied voltage of 25 kV and a flow rate of 1.0 mL/h, maintained for 4 h (Segment 1). Solution 2 was introduced via a 5 mL syringe with a 21G blunt needle positioned vertically 8 cm above the collector. Electrospraying was conducted at 18 kV and 0.75 mL/h for 1.5 h, concurrently with electrospinning of Solution 1 to form an intertwined interface (Segment 2). Electrospraying of Solution 2 then continued alone for 0.5 h (Segment 3). The membrane obtained after Segment 3 exhibited omniphobic properties and is referred to as the ″Omn″ layer.

In the next stage, Solution 3 was electrospun from a 5 mL syringe with a 24G blunt needle positioned 12 cm from the collector. Electrospraying of Solution 2 and electrospinning of Solution 3 were carried out simultaneously for 5 min (Segment 4), followed by electrospinning of Solution 3 alone for another 5 min (Segment 5), forming a thin PMA layer atop the Omn layer. The concurrent processes in Segments 2 and 4 helped form well-integrated interfaces, minimizing delamination risk.

After fabrication, the membrane was air-dried for 12 h, then hydrolyzed in 1 mM NaOH at 60 °C for 1.5 h (Segment 6). It was rinsed with deionized water to neutral pH and dried in an oven at 60 °C for 1 h. Control membranes obtained after Segments 3 and 5, referred to as Omn and Hb-Omn, were used for comparison with the final hydrolyzed Janus membrane, Hl-Omn.

Membrane Characterization

The morphology of the membranes was evaluated using scanning electron microscopy (Hitachi S-3400N). The images were analyzed with ImageJ software (version 1.53k; https://imagej.net/ij/) to determine the mean fiber diameter and surface porosity, as described in the literature. The surface roughness of the membranes was analyzed using a 3D measuring laser microscope (LEXT OLS5100). The membrane thickness was measured using two methods: (1) using a digital micrometer (Mitutoyo 293-340-30) and (2) measurement from the CT scan images acquired using SRXTM with a synchrotron radiation source of 2.2 T multipole wiggler and filtered polychromatic X-ray beam with energy of 12 keV. The functional groups of the membranes were assessed using ATR-FTIR (Bruker Alpha II), with transmittance spectra obtained within the 600–4000 cm–1 wavenumber range, using 32 scans and a resolution of 2 cm–1.

The liquid contact angle of the prepared membranes was measured at room temperature using an optical contact angle system (Data Physics OCA-200) in sessile drop mode. The testing liquids were deionized water, glycerol, diiodomethane, ethylene glycol, 0.1 mM Triton X, 0.1 mM sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), 0.1 mM cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB), and silicone oil. A 5 μL drop of liquid was placed on the surface at a dosing rate of 1 μL/s, and the contact angle was measured after 1 min of contact with the surface. An average of five measurements was reported. The underwater–oil contact angle was measured using a similar optical contact angle system in captive bubble mode. Membrane samples were placed upside down inside a deionized water-filled transparent container with a U-shaped needle at the bottom. A 2 μL drop of silicone oil was injected onto the membrane surface at a dosing rate of 1 μL/s, with an average of three measurements reported.

The mean pore size and pore size distribution of the prepared membranes were measured using a capillary flow porometer (Porolux 1000), with Porefil125 (surface tension = 16 mN/m) used as the wetting liquid agent. The liquid entry pressure (LEP) was estimated using eq . 1 LEP=−2γcos⁡θr where γ is the surface tension of water (72 mN/m), θ is the water contact angle, and r is the pore size radius (μm).

The porosity of the membranes was determined by gravimetric analysis using isopropanol as the wetting liquid. Porosity, ε, was calculated using eq . 2 ε=(Ww−WD)ρb(Ww−WDρb)+(WDρPVDF)+(WDρPMA) where W w and W D are the weights of wet and dry membranes, respectively (g); ρ b is the density of isopropanol (0.79 g/cm3), ρ PVDF is the density of PVDF (1.78 g/cm3), and ρ PMA is the density of PMA (1.15 g/cm3).

The robustness of the PMA top coating was evaluated by subjecting the Hl-Omn membrane to ultrasonication, followed by contact angle measurements based on the method described by Wu et al. The Hl-Omn membrane was immersed in water and sonicated for one hour at 60 °C. After ultrasonication, the membrane was dried in an oven at 60 °C for an additional hour. Subsequently, the contact angles of both water and oil underwater on the sonicated Hl-Omn membrane were measured.

Membrane Performance Evaluation

Performance against Challenging Saline Solutions

The membranes were used in a lab-scale direct contact membrane distillation process for desalination of three types of feed solutions: (1) saline (3.5% NaCl), (2) brine (25% NaCl), and (3) oily saline (3.5% NaCl with 1000 ppm mineral oil). The membrane was secured in a customized acrylic membrane cell with an effective area of 3.14 cm2. The feed solution was heated to approximately 60 °C, while deionized water was used as the cold stream, chilled to 20 °C using a chiller (Deng Yng D-620). Both the feed and permeate were circulated using a double-channel peristaltic pump (Longer BT100–3J) set to 100 mL/min. The increase in the mass of the permeate was recorded and used to calculate the water vapor flux, J (kg m–2 h–1), as expressed in eq . 3 J=ΔmA×t where Δm is the mass increase of permeate (kg), A is the effective membrane area (m2), and t is the time duration (h). The normalized flux was calculated using eq , 4 Normalizedflux=JJo where J o and J are the flux at initial and different times, respectively.

The permeate tank conductivity was measured using a calibrated conductivity meter (Suntex SC2300). The instantaneous salt rejection was calculated using a mass balance approach to correct for the dilution effect in the permeate tank. The detailed methodology and equation are provided in the Supporting Information (Section S2).

The surface morphology and elemental composition of the membranes subjected to brine testing were characterized through SEM-EDS (FEI Nova NanoSEM 230).

Performance Stability in Oily Saline Solution

The membranes were subjected to a three-cycle DCMD operation in an oily saline solution, with each cycle lasting 7 h. Between cycles, an in situ alkaline cleaning was performed. The module was emptied of feed and permeate liquids, and the feed solution was replaced with 1 mM NaOH, heated to 60 °C. The alkaline cleaning solution was circulated at a flow rate of 115 mL/min, in contact with the membrane for 30 min. Subsequently, the membrane was rinsed with water at the same temperature and flow rate until a neutral pH was achieved. Following the cleaning and rinsing step, the module was refilled with a fresh oily saline solution to begin the next cycle. The surface of the membranes used in the third cycle was evaluated using biological microscope (Whited WM-100) and SEM (Hitachi S-3400N). To evaluate the process productivity, the per-cycle water recovery rate was also determined, with the detailed calculation provided in the Supporting Information (Section S3).

Results and Discussion

Morphology and Chemical Characteristics

In this study, an omniphobic substrate was used as the base for electrospinning nanofibrous PMA. Initially, a hydrophobic PMA layer was electrospun onto the omniphobic substrate, producing a membrane referred to as “Hb-Omn”. This configuration reduced the surface hydrophobicity compared to the uncoated substrate. To achieve the desired Janus membrane structure, the PMA layer was further modified to become hydrophilic through hydrolysis, resulting in a hydrophilic coating on the omniphobic substrate, denoted as “Hl-Omn”. Figure a presents a schematic of the fabrication process, while Figure b displays the surface morphologies of the resulting membranes.

(a) Schematic illustrations, (b) SEM images at 10,000× magnifications, (c) laser-scanned 3D images, and (d) CT-scan and reconstructed 3D images of (1) Omn, (2) Hb-Omn, and (3) Hl-Omn membranes.

The Omn membrane (Figure b1), fabricated via simultaneous electrospinning and electrospraying, exhibited a mixed structure of nanofibers and beads. Electrospinning and electrospraying are electrohydrodynamic processes operating under distinct fluid property regimes, governed by polymer chain entanglement and viscosity. In electrospraying, dilute polymer solutions with limited chain entanglement undergo electrostatic stress, causing charged fluid jets to break into droplets. As droplet size decreases, surface tension becomes dominant, stabilizing the droplet’s spherical morphology. Conversely, electrospinning occurs at higher polymer concentrations with sufficient chain entanglement, where the charged jet undergoes rapid elongation via an electrically driven bending instability mechanism. The synergistic combination of these techniques resulted in the membrane’s characteristic mixed morphology.

The Hb-Omn membrane (Figure b2), coated with electrospun PMA fibers, showed a loosely packed network of thicker, bead-free fibers, indicating that the PMA layer effectively masked the bead-containing surface of the underlying Omn layer. After hydrolysis, the Hl-Omn membrane (Figure b3) displayed a more compact fiber structure due to the fusion of hydrolyzed PMA nanofibers, consistent with observations from earlier studies. ,, The average fiber diameters increased across the three membranes: 163 ± 44 nm (Omn), 217 ± 63 nm (Hb-Omn), and 242 ± 86 nm (Hl-Omn). As shown in Figure d, adding the PMA layer increased membrane thickness from 96 ± 26 μm (Omn) to 120 ± 7 μm (Hb-Omn). However, following hydrolysis, the Hl-Omn membrane showed a reduced thickness of 106 ± 12 μm, attributed to enhanced hydrogen bonding between hydrolyzed fibers. Furthermore, the capillary forces generated by the evaporation of the aqueous solvent during the drying of the membrane result in the contraction of the fibers, leading to the overall shrinkage of the membrane. , Surface porosity also decreased progressively from 21.8% (Omn) to 14.6% (Hb-Omn) and 10.5% (Hl-Omn), as presented in Figure S1. This reduction correlates with the increase in fiber diameter and confirms that the PMA layer effectively covered the Omn substrate. Due to challenges in cross-sectional sample preparation, direct observation of fiber interpenetration at the interface was limited in this study. However, our previous work under similar fabrication conditions provides visual evidence (Figure S2) supporting the proposed structural integration.

Figure c presents 3D scanned images of the membrane surface. The Omn membrane demonstrates a relatively uniform distribution of electrosprayed microclusters and electrospun fibers, as evidenced by the peaks and valleys with moderate color variations. In contrast, the Hb-Omn membrane surface exhibits a more fibrous structure due to the presence of larger PMA fibers, resulting in an increased Ra value. The Hl-Omn membrane displays intricate surface features with significant color contrast, indicating sharp peaks and deep valleys caused by the fusion and nonfusion of thick PMA fibers.

Figure a shows the FTIR spectra of the surface of the prepared membranes. The Omn membrane exhibited characteristic peaks at 842 and 1406 cm–1, indicating the presence of α-phase, β-phase, and γ-phase of PVDF. , It showed peaks at 874 and 1071 cm–1, representing the α-phase of PVDF. Additionally, the peaks at 842, 1280, and 1435 cm–1 corresponded to the β-phase, while the peak at 1238 cm–1 signified the γ-phase of PVDF. A small absorption peak at 760 cm–1 suggested the presence of TiO2, and overlapping peaks between 1100 and 1300 cm–1 were associated with FAS. The successful incorporation of PMA into the Hb-Omn membrane is indicated by the FTIR absorption bands at 1739 cm–1 and 2240 cm–1, which are associated with the ester and nitrile functional groups, respectively. Following hydrolysis, these ester groups are converted into carboxylic acids. , This chemical transformation in the Hl-Omn membrane is evidenced by a broader absorption peak near 3300 cm–1, corresponding to the hydroxyl (−OH) stretching vibration, and a peak at 1724 cm–1, representing the carbonyl (CO) stretching of the carboxylic acid group, confirming the formation of hydrolyzed PMA. The successful hydrolysis of PMA is further validated by the XPS analysis, as shown in Figures b–d.

The XPS survey scan of the top and bottom surfaces of the Hl-Omn membrane, illustrated in Figure b, further confirms the Janus design of the membrane. The top surface is rich in O, C, and N, consistent with the chemical composition of hydrolyzed PMA. In contrast, the bottom surface predominantly contains F and C, signifying the presence of PVDF. The C 1s narrow scan (Figure c) reveals characteristic signals from both unhydrolyzed PMA (e.g., N–CN bonds) and hydrolyzed PMA (e.g., OC–NH2 bonds), further supporting the chemical transformation. Additionally, small F 1s peak at 685.1 eV in Figure b, along with a minor C–F2 peak at 290.5 eV in Figure c, can be attributed to exposed PVDF fibers, suggesting not full coverage of the Omn layer by the PMA porous layer. The C–F2 peak aligns with the peak at 289.8 eV observed in the C 1s narrow scan of the bottom PVDF surface shown in Figure S3. This finding is further supported by the notable similarities in the FTIR spectra of both the Omn and Hl-Omn membranes. Moreover, the O 1s narrow scan presented in Figure d reveals a carbonyl peak at 530.9 eV for the unhydrolyzed PMA, as well as carbamide peak at 530.3 eV and carboxyl peak at 531.7 eV for the hydrolyzed PMA fibers, confirming the partial hydrolysis of the PMA fibers.

Further insight is provided by the O 1s narrow scan (Figure d), which shows distinct peaks corresponding to carboxyl groups in unhydrolyzed PMA, as well as peaks associated with carbamide and carboxyl functionalities in the hydrolyzed PMA. These results confirm that hydrolysis occurred to a significant extent but may have been incomplete, resulting in a partially hydrolyzed PMA coating.

Wettability Property

Liquid contact angle, surface energy, porosity, and liquid entry pressure are key parameters that determine a membrane’s wettability. As shown in Figure a, the Omn membrane exhibits high contact angles exceeding 100° for water and various low-surface-tension liquids, including glycerol, diiodomethane, ethylene glycol, and surfactant solutions (Triton X, SDS, and CTAB), indicating its omniphobic nature. This property is attributed to the electrosprayed microclusters consist of fluorinated TiO2 composites. In contrast, as illustrated in Figure b, the PMA-coated Omn membrane (Hb-Omn) displays increased water affinity, with a reduced contact angle of 118°. Despite PMA’s inherently hydrophilic nature, the coated surface appears hydrophobic, with a contact angle exceeding 90°. This unexpected result is attributed to the loosely packed, porous structure of the PMA nanofibrous layer (illustrated in Figure b2), which does not completely cover the underlying Omn substrate. After hydrolysis, the introduction of hydroxyl groups and the denser, more uniform arrangement of PMA fibers lead to a smoother surface and significantly improved hydrophilicity. The Hl-Omn membrane exhibits the lowest water contact angle of 64°, reflecting the highest water affinity among the tested membranes. This clear contrast in wettability between the Omn and Hl-Omn surfaces highlights the asymmetric nature of the Janus membrane design.

The interaction between membrane surfaces and silicone oil was evaluated under air and underwater conditions. In air, all membranes exhibited good affinity to oil, with contact angles below 90°. The Hl-Omn membrane showed the highest oil affinity, likely due to the relatively lower surface tension of silicone oil compared to the high surface energy of the hydrophilic membrane. Despite the high viscosity of silicone oil that slows the spreading rate, its low surface tension plays a dominant role in in ensuring effective spreading, resulting in the lowest contact angle. Underwater, oil contact angles increased for all membranes except Hl-Omn, which effectively repelled oil. Despite this increase, most membranes remained oleophilic. As shown in Figure c, the Omn membrane retained strong oil affinity underwater, likely due to hydrophobic–hydrophobic interactions, a behavior also observed in commercial hydrophobic membranes. The Hb-Omn membrane displayed heterogeneous behavior, with varied contact angles across its surface. Some regions showed oleophilic behavior (Figure b, d), while others repelled oil droplets completely (Figure c), indicating surface irregularities. Among all samples, only Hl-Omn consistently repelled oil, as confirmed by the spherical shape of the oil droplet during underwater contact angle testing. This behavior is attributed to the hydrophilic surface forming a hydrogen-bonded hydration layer, which acts as a barrier against oil. The surface tension from cohesive forces within the oil droplet maintains its spherical shape, resulting in underwater superoleophobicity.

Figure d shows visual changes in the membranes after underwater–oil testing. All membranes were initially opaque white (Figure S4). After oil contact, the Omn membrane became transparent, indicating strong oil adhesion and spreading. The Hb-Omn turned semitransparent, suggesting partial oil resistance, while Hl-Omn membrane remained opaque and clean, demonstrating excellent oil resistance. The Hl-Omn membrane also resisted water wetting, due to the hydrophobicity of its underlying layer. This dual resistance to oil and waterits Janus characteristicmakes Hl-Omn highly suitable for antifouling applications in oil-contaminated environments. Notably, it outperformed commercial hydrophobic membranes, which turned semitransparent upon oil contact. Despite the higher underwater–oil contact angle of the Omn membrane compared to the commercial membrane, the latter appeared less transparent post-test due to its thicker structure, supported by a PTFE backing layer. This backing layer hindered oil from spreading across the membrane, resulting in a less transparent appearance. In contrast, the thin dimensions of the Omn membrane facilitated the spreading of oil across the membrane, leading to a transparent appearance.

Figure illustrates the impact of ultrasonication on the water and oil underwater resistance of the Hl-Omn membrane. As seen in Figure a, the top surface of the membrane remained hydrophilic, while the bottom surface maintained its hydrophobicity. This observation demonstrates that the asymmetric surface wettability of the Hl-Omn membrane was preserved even after extended sonication. Figure b further confirms that the top surface maintained its underwater oleophobicity. The fibrous morphology and fiber size distribution of the sonicated membrane also remained consistent with those of the pristine Hl-Omn membrane, as shown in Figure S5. These results demonstrate the membrane’s structural integrity and durability under mechanical stress.

The liquid entry pressure (LEP) values follow a similar downward trend. A high positive LEP indicates that greater pressure is needed for water to penetrate the membrane, reflecting a nonwettable surface. In contrast, a negative LEP suggests that water is spontaneously drawn into the membrane, indicating strong surface wettability. This is consistent with the behavior of the Hl-Omn membrane, where water spreads easily across the top surface, as supported by the contact angle shown in Figure b.

It is important to highlight that LEP in this study is influenced by both pore size and surface wettability. Hydrophilic surfaces typically exhibit negative LEP values. In Janus membranes, water is pulled across the hydrophilic layer due to capillary action, but movement is hindered when it encounters the hydrophobic bottom layer. For the Hl-Omn membrane, while water is absorbed at the hydrophilic top, it is effectively blocked by the underlying hydrophobic Omn substrate, which resists liquid intrusion. This phenomenon highlights the distinct ability of Janus membranes to regulate water penetration while preventing unwanted liquid entry. Similar to the observations in the study of Wang et al., the spontaneous penetration of water droplets depends on the thermodynamic free energy barrier, where the hydrophobic bottom layer, characterized by its positive Gibbs free energy, acts as a barrier that effectively prevents water intrusion. As a result, despite having a negative LEP, the Hl-Omn membrane effectively prevents feedwater breakthrough and maintains high performance in MD processes, as demonstrated by its strong salt rejection during DCMD testing with saline feed.

Performance against Challenging Saline Feed Solutions

The fabricated membranes were evaluated using complex saline feed solutions to assess their efficiency in DCMD processes, which often involve challenging feed conditions. A commercial PVDF membrane, prepared via phase inversion, was tested under identical conditions as a benchmark. In comparison to the commercial membrane, all three fabricated membranes exhibited superior performance, as evidenced by their sustained flux and consistently low permeate conductivity. This result highlights the omniphobic properties of the Omn substrate membrane. The incorporation of electrosprayed microclusters composed of fluorinated TiO2 composites effectively introduced the necessary surface roughness and low surface energy for reducing salt deposition on the surface and preventing pore blockage, thereby ensuring stable and high flux throughout the process.

Figure a shows the membrane performance in saline feed. The corresponding actual flux data over the entire operational period is available in the Supporting Information (Figure S6). The uniformly low conductivity of the fabricated membranes suggests that the presence of a hydrophilic top layer does not compromise salt rejection. In the Hl-Omn membrane, although the saline feedwater infiltrates the hydrophilic surface layer, it is successfully blocked by the underlying Omn substrate, which acts as a secondary barrier to ensure separation performance. This illustrates the distinct working principle of Janus membranes in MD systems. However, water absorption into the hydrophilic surface can reduce fluid mixing near the Omn interface, which may worsen temperature polarization and lower the vapor pressure gradient. , This effect likely contributes to the lower flux observed in Hl-Omn compared to the Omn membrane.

In Figure b, when exposed to brine, the Hl-Omn membrane showed an increase in permeate conductivity, indicating partial ion penetration. The highly saline feed is absorbed by the hydrophilic top surface, but it encounters resistance at the hydrophobic Omn interface below. This interface serves as the main vaporization site, where the feed evaporates, allowing the MD process to proceed. Over time, the high salt concentration near the surface leads to concentration polarization and salt crystal deposition, as evidenced by Na and Cl detected in the used membranes (inset, Figure ). The continuous pressure at the feed–membrane interface forces salt crystals toward the Omn layer, mimicking scale-induced wettingwhere salt accumulates within the membrane structure, allowing some ion passage. Despite this, the Omn layer remains resilient, preventing complete salt breakthrough and keeping permeate conductivity within acceptable limits for drinking water. The fabricated nanofibrous membranes (Omn, Hb-Omn, and Hl-Omn) maintained stable flux and produced permeate with low conductivity, indicating their high effectiveness. In contrast, the commercial membrane exhibited a significant increase in normalized flux. This apparent flux enhancement is directly correlated with a sharp rise in permeate conductivity to over 150 μS/cm, which indicates membrane failure via pore wetting and subsequent liquid leakage, rather than improved performance.

SEM images and EDS elemental analysis (inset) of Omn, Hb-Omn, and Hl-Omn membranes after a seven-hour DCMD operation in the brine feed solution.

Overall, the results highlight the potential of electrospun Janus membranes for high-salinity water treatment. As shown in Figure , all membranes retained their fibrous morphology after brine exposure, confirming their structural stability under harsh conditions.

When tested with oily saline feed (Figure c), the Omn membrane experienced a significant flux declinedropping to 75% of its original value after 7 h of continuous DCMD operation. This reduction is attributed to oil fouling, resulting from the membrane’s underwater oleophilic nature, as indicated by its oil contact angle in water. Oil droplets adhered to the surface and gradually clogged the pores, impeding vapor transport over time. In contrast, the Hl-Omn membrane maintained its initial flux and consistently low permeate conductivity throughout the test. A slight enhancement in its initial flux was also observed. This minor flux increase, occurring in the absence of any rise in conductivity, is attributed to nondestructive structural relaxation of the polymer matrix under operational conditions. This subtle change can slightly enhance vapor transport without compromising the membrane’s excellent barrier integrity, highlighting its structural resilience. The robust performance of Hl-Omn is due to its underwater oleophobic top layer, which prevents oil from attaching to the membrane surface. The hydrophilic coating keeps the pores accessible for water vapor passage during prolonged DCMD operation, ensuring stable performance. The results highlight a clear contrast between the two membranes: Omn offers higher initial flux but suffers rapid performance loss, while Hl-Omn delivers lower but stable flux with excellent oil-fouling resistance. This illustrates a common trade-off in membrane designbalancing high permeability with fouling resistance. Despite its lower initial flux, the long-term performance of Hl-Omn is advantageous, as it minimizes fouling-related downtime and reduces the frequency of membrane replacement, leading to more efficient and sustainable operation.

Performance Stability in Prolonged Desalination of Oily Saline Water

The long-term performance of the membranes was assessed through repeated DCMD cycles using oily saline feed. As shown in Figure a, each membrane underwent three 7-h DCMD runs, with diluted alkali cleaning between cycles. Both Omn and Hb-Omn membranes showed a steady decrease in flux after the first cycle, suggesting progressive oil fouling. This was visually confirmed by the presence of stains on the membrane surfaces (Figures b–c). Their underwater oleophilic nature led to oil droplet adhesion and pore penetration, causing pore blockage and partial wetting. This partial wetting compromised membrane hydrophobicity, increasing susceptibility to liquid breakthrough. As a result, saline feed entered the membrane structure, elevating the permeate conductivity. The cleaning method, which was limited to the surface of the membrane, was ineffective in removing foulants embedded within the pores, limiting its ability to restore membrane performance. Similar limitations of surface cleaning were reported in previous studies involving membranes exposed to oily saline feed. Furthermore, as these tests were run in a nonsteady-state batch mode, the increasing feed concentration throughout each cycle also contributed to the flux decline by reducing the transmembrane vapor pressure gradient and increasing osmotic effects. ,

(a) Membrane performance in a three cycle-DCMD against oily saline, (b) digital images of the membranes after each cycle, and (c) optical micrographs of membranes after the third cycle in DCMD against oily saline. The red squares represent the areas tested in optical microscopy.

In contrast, the Hl-Omn membrane demonstrated superior operational stability. This superior fouling resistance translates directly to a more stable and higher water recovery. The Hl-Omn maintained a recovery rate between 15.2% and 17.9% per cycle, whereas the conventional membranes’ performance dropped significantly after the first cycle (Figure S7a). Its hydrophilic top surface, with low oil affinity under water, effectively resisted oil adhesion and pore clogging. This is supported by the absence of staining on the Hl-Omn membrane shown in Figures b–c. Among the tested membranes, Hl-Omn demonstrated the highest resistance to oil fouling in DCMD operations, retaining 80% of its initial flux after 21 h of nonsteady-state testing. Additionally, the results affirm the membrane’s structural robustness and strong interfacial bonding between layers, as it withstood both extended operation and repeated cleaning without signs of delamination.

To provide a more accurate assessment of separation efficiency, the instantaneous salt rejection was calculated and is presented in Figure S7b. Initially, all membranes exhibited excellent performance, with salt rejection values greater than 99.8%, which is characteristic of a stable MD process. However, as the experiment progressed, clear distinctions emerged. The Hl-Omn membrane, despite its superior flux stability, consistently showed a slightly lower salt rejection compared to the Omn and Hb-Omn membranes. This behavior represents the trade-off inherent in the Janus membrane design; while the hydrophilic top layer provided excellent antifouling properties, it also made the membrane interface slightly more susceptible to partial wetting, resulting in higher salt passage. The steady decline in rejection for all membranes across the three cycles confirms that progressive pore wetting occurred, which directly corresponds to the rise in permeate conductivity shown in Figure a. Ultimately, the Hl-Omn demonstrates a successful trade-off, sacrificing a minor degree of salt rejection for a significant gain in long-term operational stability against a challenging oily saline feed.

The morphological changes in the used membranes after three cycles of DCMD operation against an oily saline feed (Figure b) reveal a more compact fiber structure and a loss of distinct boundaries compared to fresh membranes (Figure a). To investigate these changes, two experiments were performed on fresh membranes: (1) dropping oil onto the membrane surface, and (2) applying alkali cleaning treatment twice on the membrane, as shown in Figures c and d, respectively.

Dropping oil onto the membrane caused the oil to spread and infiltrate the pores, which interfered with SEM imaging and resulted in charging effects, particularly at higher magnifications. As a result, the image of the oil-wetted membranes in Figure c was taken at a lower magnification compared to other images in Figure . However, the notable transformations observed in Figure c did not match the morphological changes seen in Figure b, indicating that oil infiltration is not the primary cause of the structural alterations.

Meanwhile, the two alkali treatments slightly fused the membrane fibers (Figure d), leading to an increase in fiber sizes (Figure S8). But, the membranes still preserved their fibrous and porous structures, which suggests that the treatment was not the main factor behind the changes in Figure b. However, it should be noted that the treatment in Figure d was performed on fresh membranes, which do not fully replicate the conditions of the used membranes in Figure b. The fiber fusion effect is inferred to be more pronounced in used membranes than in fresh ones. These results suggest that the structural transformations in Figure b are caused by the cumulative physical and chemical stresses during prolonged and repeated DCMD operations and cleaning treatments.

The variations in surface hydrophilicity were quantified in Figure S9, which displays the changes in water contact angles for each membrane before and after the first DCMD operation cycle. Notably, the Omn and Hb-Omn membranes experienced significant decreases in water contact angle following one cycle of DCMD, while the Hl-Omn membrane exhibited minimal changes in surface properties, attributed to its anti-oil fouling capabilities. Consequently, the Hl-Omn membrane demonstrated stable performance in subsequent cycles. Despite the denser structure after the DCMD cycles and cleaning treatments, the Hl-Omn membrane maintained nearly constant vapor flux and consistently low permeate conductivity. This indicates its ability to sustain efficient vapor transport while effectively rejecting saline feed and oil contaminants. The enhanced performance is attributed to the improved hydrophilicity of the hydrolyzed PMA surface, induced by alkali cleaning treatments, which mimicked the hydrolysis reaction from segment 6 of the membrane fabrication process.

Additionally, the alkali treatments increased the hydrophilicity of the Hb-Omn membrane between operation cycles, contributing to its denser structure and superior performance compared to the Omn membrane. This ensured long-term operational efficiency under challenging conditions. While our current study provides valuable insights, future research could further elucidate the membrane’s performance through extended experiments, including comprehensive long-term DCMD investigations against complex feed solutions, detailed fouling layer characterization, and systematic optimization of cleaning protocols. Such follow-up studies would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the membrane’s durability and performance mechanisms under varied operational conditions.

Conclusion

This study introduced a novel dual-nozzle electrospinning technique for fabricating multilayered Janus membranes with asymmetric wettability and strong interfacial adhesion. Two distinct wetting layers were simultaneously formed: a thin hydrophilic layer composed of hydrolyzed PMA nanofibers and an underlying omniphobic substrate. The resulting Janus membrane demonstrated excellent performance in DCMD, particularly under harsh conditions involving high salinity and oil contamination. The hydrophilic top layer effectively prevented oil adhesion and pore blockage, while the omniphobic sublayer provided strong resistance to liquid intrusion. Although the Janus membrane exhibited slightly lower initial flux than the control (omniphobic) membrane, it consistently produced high-quality permeate with conductivity below 5 μS/cm and retained up to 80% of its flux after three DCMD cycles with oily saline feed. In contrast, the control membrane showed significant flux decline due to oil fouling and partial pore wetting.

The Janus membrane also preserved its fibrous structure and asymmetric wettability after extended operation and cleaning, confirming its structural integrity and long-term durability. These findings underscore the effectiveness of the dual-nozzle electrospinning method in producing robust, multilayered membranes with improved operational stability. Therefore, the electrospun Janus membrane presents a promising and reliable solution for antioil fouling applications in DCMD processes treating oil-contaminated saline water.
