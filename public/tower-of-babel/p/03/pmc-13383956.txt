Blue light during rearing: Does it impact the health and production of leghorn pullets and hens?
Authors: Zoe Uhren, Tory Shynkaruk, Carolin A.B. Adler, Trever G. Crowe, Karen Schwean-Lardner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aggression and cannibalism in table egg laying birds are major welfare concerns. Use of blue light has been shown to have a calming effect on broilers. However, because of its short wavelength, blue light may delay sexual maturation in reproductive birds. An objective of this study was to assess how blue light, with intensity corrected for avian vision, provided during the pullet phase, impacted the production of Lohmann pullets and hens. The 3 light treatments studied were white light to 17 wk (W17), blue light (peak 455 nm) to 17 wk (B17), or blue light to 15 wk followed by 2 wk of white light (B15). Body weight, uniformity, feed disappearance, mortality, and ocular measurements were assessed during the pullet phase (0-17 wk) and body weight, feed intake and efficiency, egg production and quality, and mortality were measured during the hen phase (17-53 wk). Two Lohmann strains, LSL-Lite and Brown-Lite, were studied (n = 1,800 per strain). Two replicate trials using a 3 × 2 factorial arrangement in a randomized complete block design were performed. Each trial included 6 rooms with 6 pens/room (3 pens/strain). Data were analyzed using Proc Mixed (SAS 9.4), and significance was declared when P ≤ 0.05. The B15 and B17 treatments were combined into one treatment (Blue), before being separated at 15 wk when B15 rooms switched to white light for the remaining 2 weeks. In the hen phase, only white light was provided. Pullets were heavier under Blue at 4 wk (P < 0.01). Pullets in the W17 treatment had greater feed disappearance from 4 to 8 wk (P < 0.01). Light did not affect any eye parameters; however, eye weight and anterior-posterior diameter were increased in the LSL-Lite strain. Light had no effect on age at first egg, egg production or quality, or hen mortality. To conclude, blue light during pullet rearing had no lasting impacts on body weight, feed intake, ocular measures, or overall mortality.

Introduction

The Canadian table-egg industry is transitioning from conventional cage housing to enriched or free-run housing by 2036 (NFACC, 2017). These housing systems have increased complexity compared to conventional cages, enabling birds to perform a broader range of behaviours (Lay et al., 2011). As vision is the bird’s dominant sense, lighting plays an important role in their ability to navigate their environment (Jones et al., 2007). The Canadian Codes of Practice for the Care and Handling of Pullet and Laying Hens currently requires a light intensity of ≥10 lux for laying hens, with no guidelines for light wavelength (NFACC, 2017). Additionally, when studying colored lighting, it is important that the different spectral sensitivity of poultry be considered when interpreting results (Prescott, 1999). The unit, lux, has been the standard unit of measurement for light intensity in poultry houses, and it is a function of the spectral power output of the light and the sensitivity of the human eye (Nuboer et al., 1992).

Humans can see light in the visible range from 380 to 750 nanometers (nm), whereas chickens have different spectral sensitivities and can also perceive ultraviolet light extending their range to 315-750 nm (Soliman and El-Sabrout, 2020). This enhanced visual ability is due to chickens’ being tetrachromatic, meaning they have four types of cone photoreceptors (Prescott, 1999). Their unique ocular anatomy also results in an underestimation of light intensity under blue and red wavelengths (Nuboer et al., 1992). Therefore, when conducting light wavelength research with poultry, it is important to account for the spectral sensitivity of avian vision and the spectral intensity of the illumination to avoid the confounding effects of light intensity and wavelength (Lewis and Morris, 2000). This can be achieved by equalizing light intensity between treatments, using gallilux instead of lux, as gallilux is adjusted for the bird’s unique spectral sensitivity (Lewis and Morris, 2000).

Blue light (435-500 nm) consists of short wavelengths, meaning it does not penetrate the skull, and is therefore classified as ‘non-stimulatory’ (Lewis and Morris, 2006), and can potentially impact growth and feed intake (Cao et al., 2008), mortality (Cave, 1990), ocular growth (Nickla et al., 2022), and sexual maturity (Bissonnette, 1932; Min et al., 2012). A recent broiler study found that blue light (peak 455 nm, intensity equalized using gallilux) had minimal effect on body weight in early life compared to green (510 nm) or white light (Remonato Franco et al., 2022b). In experiments where the light intensity is balanced using measurements of lux, the light intensity, as perceived by the birds, is not consistent between treatments. For example, in studies where light intensity is balanced using measurements of lux, birds will actually perceive blue-lit environments as being brighter than other treatments, and this interaction between spectral sensitivity and spectral output from lamps could explain the increased feed consumption and body weight gain in broilers reared under blue light (Cao et al., 2008). To our knowledge, there is currently no scientific evidence of the effects of light wavelengths on the health and production of leghorn pullets when intensity is adjusted for avian vision.

Because blue light has been demonstrated to have no impact on broiler body weight or feed intake when intensity was balanced for avian visual capacity, we hypothesized that it would not affect pullet or hen body weight or feed intake regardless of strain. We also hypothesized that blue light could impact sexual maturation, but that switching from blue to white light prior to sexual maturity could mitigate this effect. Therefore, the objectives of this study were to examine the effects of white and blue light during rearing on pullet performance, mortality, and ocular measurements, as well as hen production and mortality of two Lohmann strains. Data from this study have been separated into two manuscripts – one focusing on health and production and the second on behaviour, stress, and fear.

Materials and methods

The experimental procedures for this research were approved by the University of Saskatchewan Animal Care Committee (AUP#20230019), and all birds were cared for as specified in the Guide to the Care and the Use of Experimental Animals by the Canadian Code of Animal Care (2009). The primary objective of this experiment focused on the effects of providing white or blue light during pullet rearing on body weight, feed disappearance, mortality, uniformity, and ocular growth of 2 pullet strains (Lohmann Brown-Lite and LSL-Lite). The secondary objective was to evaluate any carryover effects of light wavelength during the rearing phase on laying hen performance when housed under white light. Two 53 wk experiments were conducted from May 2023 to May 2024 and July 2024 to August 2025. The pullet phase of each trial occurred from 0 to 17 wk, followed by the hen phase from 17 to 53 wk.

Housing and management

Each trial housed 900 Brown-Lite and 900 LSL-Lite pullets (N = 3,600) in randomly assigned floor pens (4.0 m x 2.3 m). Pullets were placed on day-of-hatch in 6 environmentally controlled, light-tight rooms with six pens per room (3 pens per strain). Stocking density was 5.4 birds/m2 (50 pullets per pen), in accordance with the Lohmann Management Guide (Lohmann Tierzucht, 2018). Each pen was bedded with 7-10 cm of wood shavings and contained one wooden perch (2.18 m x 1.16 m x 0.56 m) with angled edges. Two tube feeders and one drinker line with six nipples (Lubing Systems LP, Cleveland, TN, USA) were provided in each pen and offered continuous access to commercial feed and water. Pullets were infra-red beak-treated and vaccinated for Marek’s Rispens, HVT-IBD, and Poulvac ST at the hatchery (Clark’s Hatchery, Brandon, MB), and were vaccinated against Salmonella typhimurium, Newcastle Bronchitis, and Salmonella enteriditis during rearing.

Two rooms per trial were assigned to each light treatment: white light to 17 wk (W17), blue light to 17 wk (B17), and blue light to 15 wk followed by white light for 2 wk (B15). Prior to 15 wk, both the B15 and B17 treatments were identical so they were combined into one treatment (Blue) for analyses. Light was provided via ten 11-W LED lamps (AgriLamp 11 W ES26/27; Greengage Lighting Ltd., Edinburgh, UK) in each room. Initially, 4 rooms per trial were outfitted with blue bulbs and 2 rooms were outfitted with white bulbs. Two of the rooms with blue bulbs were transitioned to white bulbs when the pullets reached 15 wk (B15 treatment), while the remaining two rooms continued using blue bulbs to 17 wk (B17 treatment). Light intensity was set to 15 gallilux, and every 2 wk intensity was measured at bird height in the middle of the pen with a gallilux meter (ExTech LT300, ExTech Instruments, Montreal, Quebec, Canada for Trial 1, and HATO ONE, HATO Agricultural Lighting, Sittard, Limburg, Netherlands for Trial 2) and adjusted if necessary. From d 0-9, pullets were reared under 22 hours of light and 2 hours of dark (22L:2D) per day, then the photoperiod gradually decreased each week until 7 wk when it was maintained at 8L:16D for the remainder of the trial (NFACC, 2017). Dawn and dusk periods (15 min each) were simulated daily.

At 17 wk, pullets were transferred from the rearing barn to the production barn. A total of 360 birds remained on trial during the hen phase from 17 to 53 wk. These birds were randomly selected and balanced by room, pen, and strain for a representative sample (120 birds/light treatment, 180 birds/strain, and 60 birds/light treatment/strain). Birds were housed in 30 conventional cages (5 replicates/treatment) at a floor stocking density of 500 cm2/bird. Each cage was equipped with a trough feeder and 4 nipple drinkers. Hens were fed commercial layer diets, appropriate for each phase of egg production. Light was provided by white LED bulbs at an intensity of 14 gallilux (10 lux) at the middle of the 3-tier cage system. The photoperiod began at 8L:16D and was gradually increased until 16L:8D was reached at 23 wk and this photoperiod was maintained for the remainder of the trial.

Data collection

Pullet Phase. Birds were weighed on a pen basis at 0, 4, 8, 12, and 17 wk to assess body weight changes. A subset of 516 birds per trial (172 birds per strain per light treatment, N = 1,032) were weighed individually at 17 wk to assess flock uniformity. At 4, 8, 12, and 17 wk, residual feed was weighed and feed disappearance (feed consumed and spillage) per pen from 0 to 4, 4-8, 8-12, and 12-17 wk was calculated. Feed disappearance was the term selected in place of feed intake due to pullet activity at the tube feeders resulting in spillage of some feed into the litter. During Trial 2 at 16 wk, one bird per pen (6 birds per strain per light treatment; N = 36) was euthanized using manual cervical dislocation and the right eye was enucleated and weighed. Three measurements were taken via digital calipers (Mastercraft Digital Caliper 6-in, Canadian Tire Corporation, Toronto, ON): anterior-posterior diameter, medio-lateral diameter, and dorso-ventral diameter. These measurements were used to determine ocular morphology and growth under the different light treatments (Schwean-Lardner et al., 2013; Vermette et al., 2016). Birds were monitored daily and those found dead or culled due to illness were sent for necropsy and diagnoses (Prairie Diagnostic Services, Saskatoon, SK, Canada). As no mortalities occurred after 15 wk, mortalities were analyzed according to 2 light treatments: Blue and W17.

Hen Phase. At 17 and 53 wk, all birds on trial during the hen phase were individually weighed to assess body weight. Feed intake (no spillage from feed trough) was measured on a cage basis every 4 wk and feed efficiency was calculated. Egg production was recorded daily on a cage basis, allowing for the determination of age at first egg. The number of double-yolked, cracked, broken, soft-shelled, and abnormal eggs was also recorded daily. Every 8 wk, egg weights and specific gravity were assessed on 1 day for all eggs laid within a 24 h period.

Statistical analyses

The experiment was designed as a 2 (light) x 2 (strain) factorial arrangement in a complete randomized block design prior to 15 wk, then as a 3 (light) x 2 (strain) factorial arrangement from 15 to 17 wk. Light was nested within room and trial was the blocking factor, with room as the replicate unit for light and pen as the replicate unit for strain in the pullet phase. In the hen phase, cage was the replicate unit. Before 15 wk, there were 4 replicates for the W17 treatment and 8 replicates for the Blue treatment. After 15 wk, there were 4 replicates for each light treatment (W17, B17, B15). Data were checked for normality using the Univariate procedure of SAS 9.4 and were normalized using log transformation (log +1), if necessary. Data were analyzed using the Mixed procedure, and a Tukey’s range test was used to separate means, with significance declared when P ≤ 0.05.

All mortalities in the pullet phase occurred prior to 15 wk, so data were analyzed for the W17 and Blue treatments. Light treatment had no effect on overall mortality (0.32 vs. 0.16 for W17 and Blue, respectively; P = 0.17), nor did strain (0.30 vs 0.13 for Brown-Lite and LSL-Lite, respectively; P = 0.09).

Hen Phase. Lighting treatment did not impact body weight at 17 wk, however, at 53 wk, hens that were reared under the B15 treatment were heavier than hens reared entirely under the W17 or B17 treatments (2.05 kg vs. 2.00 and 2.00 kg respectively; P = 0.02). Brown-Lite hens were heavier than LSL-Lite hens at both ages (17 wk = 1.44 vs 1.22 kg; P < 0.01 and 53 wk = 2.17 vs 1.87 kg; P < 0.01). Overall feed intake was higher in the B15 treatment compared to the B17 and W17 treatments (111.19 vs 109.48 and 109.40 g/bird/d, respectively; P = 0.04). Brown-Lite hens consumed more feed than LSL-Lite hens (112.54 vs 107.51 g/bird/d; P < 0.01). Overall feed efficiency was unaffected by both lighting treatment and strain when expressed both as total feed per egg mass and total feed per dozen eggs (Supplementary Table 2).

Lighting treatment had no impact on age of first egg (130, 131, and 131 d for the W17, B17, and B15 treatments, respectively; P = 0.78.) Brown-Lite hens came into production earlier than LSL-Lite hens (128 vs 133 d; P < 0.01). Lighting treatment had no impact on overall egg production from 17 to 53 wk (hen day or hen housed) or the percentage of unsaleable, double yolk, soft shelled, cracked, broken, or abnormal eggs (Table 4). Additionally, there was no impact of light treatment on any of the above parameters over the first 8 wk of the hen phase (17-24 wk; Supplementary Table 3). The LSL-Lite hens had higher egg production (P < 0.01) and lower percentages of unsaleable, double yolk, soft shelled, cracked, broken, and abnormal eggs (P < 0.01). Discussion

The objectives of this study were to determine the effects of blue vs white light on the performance and health of Lohmann Brown-Lite and LSL-Lite pullets reared in floor pens to 17 wk and to identify any carryover effects during the production period where hens were reared under white light only.

Pullets in the Blue treatment were heavier at 4 wk compared to those in the W17 treatment, which could be due to reduced activity, and subsequent reduced energy expenditure, as reported in broilers reared under blue light by Khaliq et al. (2018) and Remonato Franco et al. (2022c). This inactivity could be due to decreased fear and stress under blue light (Mohamed et al., 2017; Remonato Franco et al., 2022c). However, in a study with leghorn pullets, Lewis et al. (2007) saw decreased body weight under blue light compared to white light at 6 wk, with no effect of light color at older ages. In this study, hens in the B15 treatment were heavier at 54 wk, however the difference was negligible (50 g), which likely had no biological relevance.

The term “feed disappearance” was used as pullets were observed dustbathing and playing in feeders, wasting an unknown amount of feed and making it difficult to quantify the amount of feed consumed. During the 4-8 wk period, pullets in the W17 treatment had increased feed disappearance compared to those in the Blue treatment. In a preference test, 50 d old pullets exhibited increased feed intake under white light compared to red light, with blue and green light as intermediates, without adjusting light intensity for avian sensitivity (Li et al., 2019). In previous studies when light intensity was adjusted for avian spectral sensitivity, no differences in feed intake were observed between broilers reared under blue, green, or white light (Remonato Franco et al., 2022b), or between layers reared under green or white light (Lewis et al., 2007). The small increase in body weight observed for the B15 treatment during the hen phase was due to a higher feed intake in these birds over the production period. Again however, the difference between values was small, less than 2 g/bird/d and lighting treatment had no effect on hen feed efficiency.

Based on the results of the present study, light color did not affect ocular measurements. This agrees with a study by Remonato Franco et al. (2022a) who did not see any difference in medio-lateral diameter, dorso-ventral diameter, anterior-posterior diameter, or eye weight in broilers reared under blue, green, or white light. However, ocular measures were only assessed during the second trial using a limited number of randomly selected birds, which should be taken into account when interpreting these results.

The lighting treatments evaluated in this study did not negatively affect the age at which hens came into lay, as indicated by the lack of response in the age at first egg. Additionally, there were no impacts of light treatment on egg production, the percentage of unsaleable or double yolk eggs, egg weight, or specific gravity during the first 8 weeks of lay or for the duration of the trial. This indicated no long-term negative impacts of rearing pullets under blue light on hen performance (when hens were housed under white light during production).

No effect of light treatment was observed on overall mortality, nor when separated by necropsy diagnoses during the pullet or hen phases. Similarly, Wabeck and Skoglund (1974) found no differences in mortality of broilers reared under blue, green, yellow, or red light. However, when light intensity was adjusted for the enhanced visual ability of poultry, broilers reared under white light had higher mortality from 28 to 35 d compared to blue light (Remonato Franco et al., 2022b), although these results may differ from the current study due to the different genetic selection targets for meat versus egg production birds.

The effects of genetic strain on the performance parameters assessed in this study were as expected based on the Lohmann management guidelines (Lohmann Tierzucht, 2018). Furthermore, no interactions existed, which suggests that birds from both the strains reacted similarly to the white and blue light treatments that were studied. However, the ocular measurements demonstrated a strain effect, with heavier eye weights (relative to body weight) and a larger anterior-posterior diameter in LSL-Lite pullets compared to the Brown-Lite strain. The increased anterior-posterior diameter observed in LSL-Lite pullets in the current study could suggest increased visual acuity (Land and Nilsson, 2002), but this requires further investigation.

One of the limiting factors in this research was the inability to continue the lighting treatments during the production period. Future work evaluating the use of blue light in the hen barn is needed. Additionally, evaluating the impact of strain and housing system in future work would be beneficial. Welfare outcomes associated with the treatments in the current study were assessed and will be reported in a subsequent manuscript.

In conclusion, rearing egg-type pullets under blue light did not negatively impact pullet or hen performance, with no effect on age at first egg, egg production, or egg quality and only minor impacts on body weight and feed intake. Ocular measures and mortality were unaffected by light treatment, suggesting no negative health implications of rearing egg production pullets under the blue light conditions utilized in this study.
