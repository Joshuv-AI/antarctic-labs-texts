Protocol for HIV-1 latency reversal using engineered bacteriophage T4 particles
Authors: Himanshu Batra, Venigalla B. Rao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryA major barrier to curing HIV-1 is the latent viral reservoir in resting CD4+ T cells. Here, we present a protocol for preparing CD4-DARPin (designed ankyrin repeat protein)-T4 nanoparticles and their application to HIV-1 latency reversal in a model of HIV-1 latency. We describe steps for producing T4 capsid nanoparticles in E. coli and their decoration with the CD4-targeting DARPin ligand fused to the T4 outer capsid protein Hoc. We then detail procedures for assessing proviral reactivation using fluorescence readouts.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Batra et al.1

Before you begin

The protocol describes the steps for the assembly of CD4-receptor-targeted phage T4 nanoparticles (T4-NPs) and their use to reverse HIV-1 latency in an in vitro cell model, J-Lat 10.6 cells containing a full-length proviral genome. Experience in phage biology and cell culture techniques is required. The protocol is performed in BSL2-like laboratory environment. The phages and head particles used in the protocol are not infectious to humans. The J-Lat-10.6 cell line containing HIV-1 proviral genome has a deletion of nef and env genes, hence it is replication-incompetent and non-infectious.

Innovation

This protocol describes an innovative strategy that uses bacteriophage T42 as a virus-mimetic, immune-active nanoparticle for targeted HIV-1 latency reversal. The T4 capsid is engineered to display CD4-binding ligands on its outer capsid protein,3 enabling selective engagement of CD4+ T cells, the major reservoir of latent HIV-1. In parallel, the phage’s intrinsic pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), including its highly ordered capsid structure, may provide innate immune stimulation that promotes proviral reactivation.

This approach is innovative because it integrates receptor-specific targeting and innate immune signaling within a single, non-replicating biological scaffold. Existing latency-reversing agents are mainly small molecules that act systemically and often trigger off-target effects. In contrast, the T4-NPs enable targeted activation by confining stimulatory signals to CD4+ T cells. Moreover, unlike synthetic nanoparticles, the T4-NPs offer, in addition to structural stability, a high capacity and tunable surface display platform, and control over ligand density.

Overall, this protocol establishes bacteriophages as programmable immunomodulatory platforms and provides a new alternative for HIV-1 latency reversal with potential applicability to other targeted immunotherapy strategies.

This sub-subsection describes the purification of T4 capsid heads lacking Hoc (highly antigenic outer capsid protein) and Soc (small outer capsid protein), derived from the 10-amber13-amber hoc-del soc-del (10am13am-hoc.del-soc.del) T4 phage mutant. The released DNA is degraded by DNase I.4 These head particles are suitable for in vitro packaging of externally added DNA(s).5 The head particles also lack the outer capsid proteins Hoc and Soc, hence suitable for display of targeting molecules on the surface. Expected outcomes

This protocol is expected to yield uniformly assembled bacteriophage T4-NPs displaying CD4-binding DARPin ligands on the capsid surface. Purified nanoparticles should be structurally intact and homogeneous, supporting reproducible surface functionalization. Efficient display of Hoc-DARPin fusion protein is expected, as confirmed by particle-associated protein detected by SDS–PAGE. The non-displayed head controls, no band is visible at the same position.

In latency reversal assays, CD4-DARPin-T4-NPs are expected to selectively bind CD4+ cells and induce HIV-1 latency reversal in J-Lat reporter cells. Reactivation should be detectable as increased GFP expression relative to untreated cells or cells treated with non-targeted T4 nanoparticles, confirming CD4-dependent targeting and functional latency reversal.

Limitations

This protocol evaluates HIV-1 latency reversal using CD4-targeted T4-NPs exclusively in an in vitro cell line model, J-Lat full-length cells. While J-Lat cells are well established and experimentally tractable, they do not fully capture the cellular heterogeneity, epigenetic regulation, and microenvironmental cues present in primary CD4+ T cells in vivo. In addition, the efficiency of CD4-DARPin display on T4 capsids can vary between preparations and requires careful optimization and quality control. The molecular mechanisms underlying T4-NPs-mediated proviral reactivation are not fully defined within the scope of this protocol, and potential off-target effects, immunogenicity, and in vivo delivery parameters are not addressed.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Low yield of T4 heads after phage lysate preparation (Step 6).

Possible reason: Incomplete host cell lysis or suboptimal infection conditions.

Potential solution

Ensure that E. coli cells are infected at the appropriate growth phase and that complete lysis is achieved before clarification. Extend incubation time if necessary and confirm lysis by a drop in optical density and microscopic examination.

Problem 2

Poor separation or low recovery of T4 heads after CsCl gradient purification (Step 13).

Possible reason: Incorrect gradient formation or improper identification of the head band.

Potential solution

Prepare fresh CsCl gradients with accurate density ranges and mark tube orientation prior to centrifugation. Collect only the well-defined band corresponding to heads and avoid additional material that might contain cell debris.

Problem 3

Low display of CD4-DARPin-Hoc on T4 capsids (Step 30).

Possible reason: Improper protein folding, suboptimal protein-to-capsid ratio, or insufficient incubation time.

Potential solution

Use freshly purified, monomeric CD4-DARPin-Hoc protein and confirm its purity and integrity by SDS–PAGE. Optimize the Hoc-fusion protein concentration and ensure incubation at 4°C for sufficient time.

Problem 4

Weak or variable HIV-1 reactivation in J-Lat cells (Step 35).

Possible reason: Suboptimal nanoparticle dose, cell density, or stimulation conditions.

Potential solution

Optimize nanoparticle concentration and ensure consistent cell seeding density. Include positive control (PMA stimulation) in each experiment to confirm assay responsiveness. Extend incubation time if necessary to capture delayed reactivation kinetics.
