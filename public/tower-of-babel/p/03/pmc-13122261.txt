National Trends in Utilization of Speech‐Language Pathology and Otolaryngology Services for Dysphonia
Authors: Vicki Liu, Kyle Cook, Dori Smith, Helena Yip
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveDysphonia affects a growing number of individuals in the United States, but many do not receive targeted care. This study explores trends in speech‐language pathology (SLP) and otolaryngology utilization in a large, national cohort of individuals with dysphonia.MethodsOf 287,012 participants with EHR data who enrolled in the NIH All of Us Research Program between May 2018 and July 2022, those with dysphonia were identified using ICD codes. SLP and otolaryngology utilization rates were determined using CPT codes for relevant diagnostic and therapeutic interventions. Multivariate logistic regression analyses evaluated associations between sociodemographic characteristics and specialist care.ResultsOf 8308 individuals with dysphonia, 4136 (49.8%) received specialist care, with 1740 (20.9%) having utilized SLP services and 3973 (47.8%) having utilized otolaryngology services. In adjusted analyses, older age, female sex, and lower education were associated with decreased SLP utilization, while female sex and lower income were associated with decreased otolaryngology utilization. Higher rates of otolaryngology utilization were seen in individuals identifying as Black or Other versus Non‐Hispanic White. Insurance status and rural location did not independently correlate with specialist utilization.Conclusion49.8% of individuals with dysphonia accessed specialist care, with 20.9% of the individuals with dysphonia receiving SLP services and 47.8% receiving otolaryngology services. Utilization rates varied significantly across age, sex, race and ethnicity, education level, and annual income, but not insurance status or geographical setting.Level of Evidence3.

Introduction

Dysphonia affects one in 8.5 adults, with a total prevalence of nearly 30 million in the United States [1, 2]. Changes in voice quality, pitch, loudness, or effort often result in functional impairments, leading to altered self‐identity, decreased work productivity, increased depression and anxiety, and reduced quality of life [3, 4, 5, 6]. In fact, one study suggests the perceived negative impact of moderate dysphonia is comparable to that of monocular blindness [7]. While dysphonia is frequently caused by benign or self‐limited conditions, it may be indicative of serious underlying diseases, such as head and neck cancers or neurological conditions [4]. Thus, clinical practice guidelines from the American Academy of Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery Foundation support prompt evaluation of dysphonia [8]. Besides a comprehensive history and physical examination, diagnostic laryngoscopy should be pursued if dysphonia persists beyond 4 weeks or a serious etiology is suspected. Antibiotics, anti‐reflux medications, and corticosteroids should not be routinely prescribed prior to laryngeal visualization. Depending on the etiology, recommended treatments include voice therapy, botulinum toxin injections, or surgical intervention.

Despite the prevalence and burden of voice disorders, a minority of patients seek care [2]. In a primary care study, only 22.1% of individuals with dysphonia received treatment [5]. Contrary to clinical guidelines, this was often in the form of proton‐pump inhibitors, antihistamines, antibiotics, and only rarely speech‐language pathology (SLP) evaluation. Major deterrents patients cited included absence of physician questioning about voice problems and lack of awareness surrounding treatment options [5]. In concordance with this, a survey demonstrated that only 36.5% of internal and family medicine physicians routinely inquire about voice problems [9]. A qualitative analysis of individuals seeking voice therapy revealed themes of difficulty navigating therapeutic options and desire for further support from the healthcare system [10].

In addition to shortcomings in patient education and provider screening, broader inequities in voice care have been identified across age, race and ethnicity, sex and gender, insurance status, income, and geography [11, 12, 13]. However, current research on voice care utilization is relatively limited and yields inconsistent results, largely due to clinical and methodological heterogeneity between studies. Notably, there is no established methodology for accurately collecting and defining demographic and socioeconomic characteristics of participants [14]. Most existing studies were also performed at single medical centers, which may fail to represent the broader patient population. To address these caveats, this study explores associations between self‐reported sociodemographic parameters and utilization of SLP and otolaryngology services in a large, national cohort of individuals with dysphonia.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

Participants in the All of Us Research Program were included. The All of Us Research Program is an ongoing National Institutes of Health (NIH) effort to create a large, publicly accessible biomedical database that is representative of the diverse US population [15]. Since launching in May 2018, eligible adults who opted in were asked to provide survey responses, physical measurements, electronic health records (EHRs), and biological samples. For this study, de‐identified demographic, socioeconomic, EHR, and survey information from version 7 of the data repository, containing data collected until July 1, 2022 and released in April 2023, was accessed. This study received exemption from the University of Arizona Institutional Review Board.

A total of 287,012 participants had available EHR data. Individuals with dysphonia were then identified using International Classification of Diseases, Ninth (ICD‐9) and Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes, as detailed in Table 1.

Variables of Interest

Variables of interest were self‐reported age, sex at birth, race and ethnicity, highest education level, annual household income, health insurance coverage, and geographical location. Race and ethnicity responses were categorized as Non‐Hispanic White, Black or African American, Asian, Hispanic, and Other. Other encompassed Middle Eastern or North African, Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander, more than one population, or an unlisted race. Education level was categorized as no college, some college, and college or advanced degree. Annual household income was grouped into ranges of under $50,000, between $50,000–$100,000, and over $100,000. Health insurance status was coded as the binary presence or absence of coverage. Geographical location was rural or non‐rural. Since these survey prompts were optional, only individuals with a recorded response were included in respective analyses.

The primary outcomes were utilization of SLP and otolaryngology services. Rates of each were based on the presence or absence of at least one current procedural terminology (CPT) code corresponding to relevant interventions performed by SLPs and otolaryngologists. Specifically, speech therapy and evaluations of laryngeal function, voice, and resonance constituted SLP care. SLP interventions primarily centered around stuttering, fluency, dysarthria, and aphasia were not included as these are not commonly or routinely used to target dysphonia. Otolaryngology care encompassed diagnostic laryngoscopy, laryngoscopy with stroboscopy, laryngoscopy with injection, operative laryngoscopy with biopsy, operative laryngoscopy with excision or laser ablation, medialization laryngoplasty, and other laryngeal procedures. Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed using R software version 4.1.0 in the All of Us Researcher Workbench [16]. Percent frequencies described categorical variables, while mean and standard deviation described age. Chi‐squared tests for categorical variables and a t‐test for age compared individuals who received any specialist care, including SLP and otolaryngology services, versus those who did not.

Unadjusted and adjusted logistic regression analyses evaluated associations between variables of interest and SLP utilization as well as otolaryngology utilization. Adjusted models controlled for age, sex, race and ethnicity, educational level, annual income, insurance, and geography. Unadjusted odds ratios (OR), adjusted odds ratios (aOR), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and p‐values were reported. p‐values were deemed statistically significant at 0.05.

Results

Of 287,012 individuals with available EHR data in the All of Us database, 8308 (2.9%) were diagnosed with dysphonia and included in this study. 4136 (49.8%) of these individuals had at least one recorded instance of accessing SLP or otolaryngology services and were thus included in the “Specialist Care” group. Within this group, 1740 (20.9% of total cohort) utilized SLP services and 3973 (47.8% of total cohort) utilized otolaryngology services, with 1577 individuals utilizing both. The remaining 4172 (50.2%) individuals did not utilize either and constituted the “No Specialist Care” group.

The mean age of the total cohort was 59.4 ± 14.5 and did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.16). The No Specialist Care group had a higher proportion of females at 71.4% compared to the Specialist Care group at 64.8% (p < 0.001). In addition, a greater percentage of the No Specialist Care group identified as Non‐Hispanic White compared to the increased representation of other race and ethnicity categories in the Specialist Care group (p < 0.001). The No Specialist Care group reported lower overall education levels, with 48.4% of individuals obtaining a college or advanced degree compared to 51.5% in the Specialist Care group (p = 0.02). Annual household income was also lower in the No Specialist Care group, as 47.6% of individuals earned under $50,000 compared to 44.5% in the Specialist Care group (p = 0.02). The difference in health care coverage trended toward significance between groups (p = 0.09), with a slightly higher incidence of coverage in the Specialist Care group (98.8%) than in the No Specialist Care group (98.3%). The rural versus non‐rural geographic distribution was similar across groups (p = 0.90). Characteristics of the cohort are detailed in Table 2.

Logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify potential correlations between sociodemographic factors and SLP utilization. Older age correlated with decreased SLP services in unadjusted (OR = 0.996, 95% CI = 0.992–0.999, p = 0.03) and adjusted (aOR = 0.993, 95% CI = 0.987–0.999, p = 0.01) analyses. Compared to male sex, female sex was associated with decreased SLP utilization based on unadjusted (OR = 0.81, 95% CI = 0.73–0.91, p < 0.001) and adjusted (aOR = 0.77, 95% CI = 0.65–0.91, p = 0.002) analyses. Identifying as Hispanic versus Non‐Hispanic White correlated with increased SLP utilization in unadjusted analysis (OR = 1.36, 95% CI = 1.16–1.58, p < 0.001), but this was no longer significant after adjusting for covariates (aOR = 1.03, 95% CI = 0.74–1.45, p = 0.85). Identifying as Black, Asian, or Other as opposed to Non‐Hispanic White did not influence SLP utilization. In analyses of education, decreased odds of SLP utilization were seen in those who completed no college (unadjusted: OR = 0.61, 95% CI = 0.53–0.71, p < 0.001; adjusted: aOR = 0.42, 95% CI = 0.30–0.59, p < 0.001) or some college (unadjusted: OR = 0.78, 95% CI = 0.69–0.89, p < 0.001; adjusted: aOR = 0.77, 95% CI = 0.63–0.94, p = 0.01) compared to those with a college or advanced degree. For annual household income, earning under $50,000 was associated with decreased SLP utilization compared to earning over $100,000 in unadjusted analysis (OR = 0.73, 95% CI = 0.64–0.84, p < 0.001), but this did not hold true in adjusted analysis (aOR = 0.90, 95% CI = 0.73–1.11, p = 0.33). Absence of insurance correlated with decreased SLP utilization in unadjusted analyses (OR = 0.49, 95% CI = 0.28–0.86, p = 0.01), but this was not significant after adjusting (aOR = 0.63, 95% CI = 0.24–1.67, p = 0.35). Lastly, living in a rural versus non‐rural location did not correlate with SLP utilization in unadjusted (OR = 1.01, 95% CI = 0.68–1.49, p = 0.98) or adjusted (aOR = 0.95, 95% CI = 0.59–1.50, p = 0.81) models. Similar logistic regression analyses explored the relationship between sociodemographic characteristics and otolaryngology utilization. Age did not correlate with otolaryngology utilization in unadjusted (OR = 1.00, 95% CI = 1.00–1.01, p = 0.08) or adjusted (aOR = 1.00, 95% CI = 1.00–1.01, p = 0.55) models. As with SLP services, female sex predicted decreased otolaryngology utilization based on unadjusted (OR = 0.78, 95% CI = 0.71–0.85, p < 0.001) and adjusted (aOR = 0.68, 95% CI = 0.58–0.78, p < 0.001) analyses. Identifying as Black versus Non‐Hispanic White was associated with increased otolaryngology utilization (unadjusted: OR = 1.35, 95% CI = 1.20–1.53, p < 0.001; adjusted: aOR = 2.00, 95% CI = 1.56–2.57, p < 0.001). In adjusted analysis, identifying as Other was similarly associated with increased otolaryngology utilization (aOR = 1.57, 95% CI = 1.07–2.30, p = 0.02). Identifying as Hispanic also positively correlated with utilization in unadjusted analyses (OR = 1.42, 95% CI = 1.24–1.62, p < 0.001), but this was no longer significant after adjusting for covariates (aOR = 1.22, 95% CI = 0.93–1.62, p = 0.15). Identifying as Asian did not correlate with odds of otolaryngology utilization. In terms of education, some college as opposed to a college or advanced degree was predictive of decreased otolaryngology utilization in unadjusted (OR = 0.87, 95% CI = 0.78–0.96, p = 0.008) but not adjusted (aOR = 0.93, 95% CI = 0.79–1.09, p = 0.37) models. In adjusted analyses, earning under $50,000 or between $50,000 and $100,000 was associated with decreased otolaryngology utilization compared to earning over $100,000 (under $50,000: aOR = 0.73, 95% CI = 0.61–0.87, p < 0.001; $50,000 to $100,000: aOR = 0.79, 95% CI = 0.67–0.94, p = 0.006). Insurance status was not predictive of otolaryngology utilization (unadjusted: OR = 0.73, 95% CI = 0.50–1.05, p = 0.09; adjusted: aOR = 0.65, 95% CI = 0.31–1.37, p = 0.26). Likewise, comparable otolaryngology utilization rates were observed across rural versus non‐rural location based on unadjusted (OR = 1.04, 95% CI = 0.75–1.44, p = 0.82) and adjusted (aOR = 1.06, 95% CI = 0.73–1.54, p = 0.75) analyses. Discussion

Using the NIH All of Us Research Program, this study assessed sociodemographic trends in utilization of SLP and otolaryngology services in a large, national cohort of individuals with dysphonia. Here, the prevalence of dysphonia was 2.9% (8308 of 287,012 individuals), compared to previous national survey estimates of 7.62% in 2012 and 11.71% in 2022 [1]. Of 8308 individuals with dysphonia, 4136 (49.8%) had at least one documented instance of utilizing SLP or otolaryngology care, while the remaining 4172 (50.2%) did not. Among those with specialist care, 1740 (20.9% of total cohort) utilized SLP services and 3973 (47.8% of total cohort) utilized otolaryngology services, with 1577 individuals receiving both. These access rates are higher than the 22.1% reported by Cohen et al., which may partially reflect the inclusion of diagnostic services as well as treatment modalities in this study. Moreover, the All of Us Research Program tends to recruit participants who are engaged in healthcare systems, potentially resulting in an overestimation of utilization.

To explore trends in utilization, logistic regression models analyzed the associations between various demographic and socioeconomic factors and specialist care. After adjusting for covariates, older age, female sex, and lower education level were associated with decreased odds of SLP utilization. In terms of otolaryngology services, female sex and lower annual income correlated with decreased utilization. These trends largely align with the results of Hur et al. [12] and White et al. [4], indicating those with lower levels of educational attainment and lower income have decreased rates of voice care for dysphonia. A scoping review by Feit et al. suggests that women may be less likely to be referred for laryngology services compared to men [11], which could underlie the decreased utilization of both SLP and otolaryngology interventions observed in females in this cohort. While the cause remains unclear, this study suggests that older age, female sex, and lower socioeconomic status may be associated with less utilization of targeted dysphonia interventions.

On the other hand, increased odds of otolaryngology utilization were observed among individuals who identified as Black or Other as opposed to Non‐Hispanic White. This finding is relatively unexpected, as racial and ethnic minorities tend to face greater delays in care and increased barriers in access across the broader healthcare system [17]. For instance, another study reported a significantly lower rate of voice therapy adherence in Hispanic versus Non‐Hispanic individuals [18]. However, a trend similar to this study's was seen among patients of a tertiary laryngology clinic, where identifying as Black showed a positive correlation with utilization of services [4]. Because this study is limited to correlational data, it was impossible to ascertain an underlying reason for this trend, whether that be due to differences in dysphonia presentation, referral patterns [19], access to care, and/or other confounding variables. As previous research presents conflicting results, additional studies are necessary to understand the impact that race and ethnicity may have on dysphonia care and outcomes.

After adjusting for covariates, this study did not find any significant associations between specialist care utilization and health insurance status or rural geography despite previous studies indicating possible relationships [4, 11, 20, 21]. This may be partially attributable to selection bias, as the database may disproportionately capture individuals with higher healthcare utilization than the population at large. Another potential reason for the negligible impact of insurance coverage may be that the specific type of insurance requires consideration. Previous studies have reported differences across public versus private plans [4, 12, 20], suggesting that particular benefits and policies could be more important in determining access compared to the presence or absence of insurance itself. The decreased incidence of SLP utilization with increasing age seen in this study could reflect this, as older adults overwhelmingly possess public insurance plans such as Medicaid and Medicare as opposed to other private plans. Furthermore, these findings may be partly due to relatively small proportions of the cohort lacking insurance (1.5% of responders) and residing in a rural region (3.4% of responders). Overall, given these inconsistencies, future studies should explore the impact of insurance coverage and geography on SLP and otolaryngology care for dysphonia.

There are several limitations inherent to the design and methodology of this study. Firstly, due to its observational nature, no causal relationships could be inferred. As a cross‐sectional study, long‐term clinical outcomes for individuals with dysphonia were not followed. Utilization of speech and otolaryngology care were considered binary outcomes, but future research should incorporate nuanced measures such as timeliness of intervention, treatment outcomes, and patient‐reported satisfaction. In addition, because diagnosed dysphonia and care utilization were strictly defined based on ICD and CPT codes respectively, recorded health information may be incomplete or inaccurate. As this study utilized ICD codes broadly capturing dysphonia rather than selecting for specific etiologies, it is possible that SLP or otolaryngology intervention was not indicated for some cases, such as dysphonia that is self‐limited or not amenable to certain treatment modalities. For these reasons, no conclusions can be drawn on how national utilization rates compare to clinical need. CPT codes did not have associated indications, so some recorded instances of SLP or otolaryngology intervention may have been for reasons other than dysphonia. This is particularly likely for the 163 patients who utilized SLP services only, without prior otolaryngological evaluation. Also, while the total cohort was large, certain populations, such as those without insurance and living in a rural region, were underrepresented. Future research should strive to identify factors contributing to varying utilization rates in dysphonia care across sociodemographic groups.

Altogether, in the NIH All of Us database, 49.8% of individuals with dysphonia utilized SLP or otolaryngology care, with 20.9% of individuals with dysphonia accessing SLP services and 47.8% of the same cohort accessing otolaryngology services. Older age, female sex, and less education served as negative predictors of SLP utilization, while female sex and lower income correlated with decreased otolaryngology utilization. Higher rates of otolaryngology utilization were observed among individuals identifying as Black or Other compared to Non‐Hispanic White. While additional research should be done to understand the complex interplay between sociodemographic characteristics and dysphonia care, this study highlights that differences in utilization rates may exist across age, sex, race and ethnicity, education, and income on a national scale.

Conclusion

In the NIH All of Us database, 49.8% of individuals with dysphonia utilized specialist care, with 20.9% of individuals with dysphonia receiving SLP services and 47.8% receiving otolaryngology services. Utilization rates differed significantly across age, sex, race and ethnicity, education level, and annual income but not insurance status or rural location. Further research is necessary to determine the reasons underlying these trends.
