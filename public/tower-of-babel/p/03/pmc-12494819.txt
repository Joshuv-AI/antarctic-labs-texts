Galectin-3 Is Associated With Risk of Cardiovascular and Kidney Outcomes in Ambulatory Veterans
Authors: Shreya Banerjee, Pranav S. Garimella, Kimberly N. Hong, Alexander L. Bullen, Lori B. Daniels, Nicholas Wettersten
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Rationale & ObjectiveCardiovascular and kidney disease are highly prevalent comorbid conditions, and each is a risk factor for the other condition. We evaluated whether Galectin-3 (Gal-3), a biomarker of organ fibrosis, is associated with cardiovascular and kidney events in ambulatory Veterans.Study DesignAn observational cohort study.Setting & ParticipantsAmbulatory Veterans presenting for an outpatient echocardiogram at the San Diego Veterans Affairs between 2010 and 2013.PredictorBlood Gal-3 levels.OutcomesThe primary cardiovascular outcome was major adverse cardiac events (MACEs: acute coronary syndrome [ACS], stroke, heart failure [HF] hospitalization, or cardiovascular death). The primary kidney outcome was major adverse kidney events (MAKEs: ≥40% estimated glomerular filtration rate decrease or renal replacement therapy). Secondary outcomes were cardiovascular death, incident HF, ACS, and all-cause mortality.Analytical ApproachCox proportional hazard models adjusting for cardiovascular and kidney disease risk factors.ResultsA total of 882 Veterans with an average age of 66 ± 12 years, 97% were men, average eGFR of 78 ± 20 mL/min/1.73 m2, 40% prevalence of coronary artery disease, and 17% prevalence of HF. Higher Gal-3 was associated with a greater risk of MACEs (hazard ratio [HR], 1.54 per 2-fold higher Gal-3; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.02-2.32; P = 0.04), cardiovascular death (HR, 3.41; 95% CI, 1.75-6.66; P < 0.001), and all-cause mortality (HR, 1.81; 95% CI, 1.41-2.33; P < 0.001), but not incident HF or ACS. Higher Gal-3 was associated with a greater risk of MAKEs (HR, 1.92; 95% CI, 1.20-3.08; P = 0.004). Higher Gal-3 was associated with >350% greater risk of experiencing both MACEs and MAKEs during follow-up (HR, 3.57; 95% CI, 1.63-7.84; P = 0.002).LimitationsSelect population of Veterans presenting for an echocardiogram who were primarily older men with multiple comorbid conditions.ConclusionsGal-3 is associated with risk of cardiovascular and kidney outcomes among ambulatory Veterans.

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) are both significant causes of morbidity and mortality. They have shared risk factors such as hypertension and diabetes that frequently occur in the same individual, and each disease is associated with higher risk for and progression of the other disease.1 Individuals with CVD are at greater risk for an accelerated decline in kidney function, and individuals with CKD are at higher risk of major adverse cardiac events (MACEs).2,3 Additionally, CVD is the most common cause of death among patients with CKD.4 With their closely intertwined physiology, the bidirectional interaction between the heart and kidney is thought to be a result of multiple factors including the renin–angiotensin aldosterone system activation, changes in hemodynamic function, and inflammatory changes mediated by vascular and kidney damage.5 Because outcomes are worse when there is concomitant CVD and CKD, there is a need for tools to identify individuals at risk for both conditions.

Galectin-3 (Gal-3) is a soluble member of the lectin family that is ubiquitously expressed and is a marker of organ fibrosis.6 Prior studies have reported associations between Gal-3 and both cardiovascular and kidney outcomes. Higher levels of Gal-3 are associated with greater risk of adverse outcomes in patients with heart failure (HF), death after myocardial infarction, and incident HF and CVD in cohort-based studies.7, 8, 9, 10, 11 Furthermore, plasma Gal-3 is associated with risk of incident CKD and adverse kidney events and death after intensive care unit admission.12,13 These studies suggest Gal-3 may be a marker of risk for both cardiovascular and kidney outcomes; however, these findings require confirmation in other populations with varied health backgrounds. Furthermore, no study has evaluated both cardiovascular and kidney outcomes in the same analysis.

In this study, we evaluated whether Gal-3 levels were associated with cardiovascular and kidney outcomes in ambulatory Veterans. We hypothesized that Gal-3 would be prognostic for both cardiovascular and kidney events, independent of risk factors for cardiovascular and kidney outcomes.

Methods

Study Population

This analysis used data from a prospective observational study of ambulatory Veterans undergoing echocardiography for clinical indications at the San Diego Veterans Affairs Medical Center (SDVAMC) which began enrollment in 2010 and completed in 2017. This study recruited consecutive ambulatory Veterans presenting to the SDVAMC for an outpatient echocardiogram for participation unless the echocardiogram was ordered solely for the exclusion of intracardiac thrombus or vegetation. After obtaining consent, a blood specimen was collected, processed, and stored at –80 °C, and the participant completed their echocardiogram. From this cohort, a subgroup of 886 Veterans recruited between 2010 and 2013 had Gal-3 levels measured. We excluded 4 individuals who had end-stage kidney disease receiving dialysis, leaving 882 individuals for our analytic cohort. The original study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) at the SDVAMC and Veterans provided written consent for participation. This subsequent analysis was approved by the IRB at the SDVAMC (IRB # H210144).

Data Collection

Participant demographics, medical history, medications, and laboratory values at the time of study enrollment were abstracted from the medical record. The most recent laboratory value at or before the time of enrollment was used. We extracted annual serum creatinine measurements from the medical record for up to 10 years from study enrollment. Serum creatinine values closest to and within 3 months of the annual date that were not associated with a hospitalization were extracted. If no outpatient creatinine measurement was available within 3 months of the anniversary of enrollment, no value was recorded.

Outcomes

The primary cardiac outcome was a composite of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) defined as cardiovascular death, stroke, acute coronary syndrome (ACS), and HF hospitalization. The primary kidney outcome was a composite of major adverse kidney events (MAKEs) defined as initiation of renal replacement therapy or ≥40% decrease in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) from study enrollment. We also evaluated risk in individuals who experienced both a MACEs and MAKEs event during follow-up censored at time of the first event. Secondary outcomes were the individual cardiovascular outcomes of death from cardiovascular cause, new-onset HF, ACS, and all-cause mortality. Individuals with a history of HF (n = 151) were excluded from evaluation of new-onset HF.

Outcomes were determined based on chart review through November 2023. Cause of death was determined using discharge summaries, documentation of cause of death by a physician, or death certificates, when available. Only those with confirmed cardiac causes of death were marked as dying from cardiovascular causes. Prior studies have found >95% agreement between VA records and National Death Index (NDI) for date of death.14

Biomarker Measurement

Blood specimens were collected from participants before undergoing echocardiography. Specimens were processed and stored at −80 °C until measurement. Gal-3 levels were measured once using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (BG Medicine). The assay limit of detection was 1.13 ng/mL with coefficient of variation <10%. Measurements were performed in 2014 with stored specimens after undergoing either one or 2 freeze-thaw cycles. Prior studies have shown Gal-3 levels are stable over 6 freeze-thaw cycles for up to 1 year stored at −70 °C.15,16

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics of the study population were reported by quartiles of Gal-3. Differences in means, medians and counts of characteristics in each quartile were compared with analysis of variance for normally distributed variables, Mann-Whitney U test for nonnormally distributed variables, and χ2 test for categorical variables.

The distribution of Gal-3 was right skewed, and values were log2 transformed for continuous analyses to achieve a more normal distribution and such that associations are interpreted as per 2-fold higher biomarker value. Less than 5% of data were missing and imputed with a total of 10 imputed data sets with results pooled according to Rubin’s rule.17

We evaluated the association between Gal-3 and outcomes using Cox regression in sequential multivariable models. First, the unadjusted association of Gal-3 with an outcome was evaluated. Next, we adjusted for age, sex, race, body mass index, systolic blood pressure, history of diabetes mellitus, history of hypertension, history of hyperlipidemia, history of coronary artery disease (CAD), history of HF, blood pressure medication use, statin use and eGFR at enrollment calculated with the 2021 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) race free equation.18 We further evaluated Gal-3 as quartiles to assess its functional form and as a restricted cubic spline with 3 knots to assess for nonlinear associations. In a sensitivity analysis, we performed a competing risks analysis for outcomes that did not include all-cause mortality, using methods of Fine-Gray to obtain the subdistribution hazard ratio (sHR).19 We also evaluated for an effect modification of associations based on Veterans having or not having a history of CVD (defined as history of HF, CAD, or stroke) by testing the Gal-3∗CVD interaction.

For all analyses, a 2-sided P value < 0.05 was considered significant. All analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (version 4.2.2, R Core Team [2022]. R: A language and environment for statistical computing. R Foundation for Statistical Computing. https://www.R-project.org/).

Association of Gal-3 and Cardiovascular Outcomes

Over a median follow-up time of 13 years, 432 (49%) participants died, 172 (20%) experienced MACEs, 62 (7%) died of CVD, 53 (6%) had an ACS event, and 73 (8%) had a new diagnosis of HF (Table 2). Each 2-fold higher Gal-3 level was associated with more than double the risk of MACEs in unadjusted analysis (Table 3; hazard ratio [HR], 2.31; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.68-3.17; P < 0.001). After adjusting for confounders, the association was attenuated but remained significant (HR, 1.54; 95% CI, 1.02-2.32; P = 0.04). When evaluated by quartiles, risk increased stepwise with higher quartiles with individuals in the fourth quartile having 71% higher risk of MACE compared with the first quartile. When modeled as a restricted cubic spline, no significant nonlinear association was found (P = 0.95 spline). The associations did not differ between those with versus without a history of CVD (P interaction = 0.34). Higher Gal-3 levels were also associated with higher risk of death from cardiovascular causes (HR, 6.28; 95% CI, 3.76-10.47; P < 0.001, Table 4). This association attenuated with adjustment for confounders but remained significantly elevated with more than three-fold higher risk of death from CVD per doubling of Gal-3 (HR, 3.41; 95% CI, 1.75-6.66; P < 0.001). Evaluation by quartiles showed an increase in risk from the second to third quartile then minimal increase in risk from the third to fourth quartile, but when Gal-3 was modeled as a restricted cubic spline, no significant nonlinear association was found (P = 0.09 spline). There was no difference in association by history of CVD (P interaction = 0.70). In competing risk analysis, the risk was partly attenuated compared with the Cox model but remained elevated and statistically significant (sHR, 2.54; 95% CI, 1.35-4.79; P < 0.01; Table S1).

Gal-3 was associated with higher risk of incident HF in unadjusted analysis (HR, 2.27; 95% CI, 1.34-3.84; P < 0.001, Table 4), but not after adjusting for confounders (HR, 1.48; 95% CI, 0.78-2.81; P = 0.23). When evaluated by quartiles, risk appeared similar in the first 3 quartiles and only became higher in the fourth quartile, though Gal-3 was still not significantly associated with incident HF in the fourth quartile. There was no significant nonlinear association between Gal-3 and risk of incident HF (P = 0.58 spline). Findings were the same in competing risks analysis (Table S1).

Gal-3 was not associated with risk of ACS in the unadjusted model, fully adjusted model or competing risk analysis (Table 4, Table S1). Risk was flat across quartiles with no significant nonlinear association found (P = 0.83 spline). There was no interaction with history of CVD (P interaction = 0.42).

Association of Gal-3 and Kidney Outcomes

There were 119 Veterans (13%) who experienced the composite MAKE outcome. Gal-3 was associated with MAKE in the unadjusted analysis (HR, 2.52; 95% CI, 1.72-3.68; P < 0.001, Table 5) and after adjusting for confounders (HR, 1.92; 95% CI, 1.20-3.08; P < 0.001). When evaluated by quartiles, risk increased from the first to third quartile, but the risk estimate in the fourth quartile was similar to the third quartile. This was also seen when Gal-3 was evaluated with a restricted cubic spline (P = 0.03 spline) in which risk increased from lowest values to the median then remained elevated but flat after the median (Fig 1). Association of Gal-3 with Developing MACEs and MAKEs

There were 51 Veterans (6%) who experienced both a MACE and MAKE event during follow-up. Higher Gal-3 was associated with greater risk of an event in these individuals in unadjusted (HR, 5.27; 95% CI, 2.96-9.40; P < 0.01) and adjusted analysis (HR, 3.57; 95% CI, 1.63-7.84; P < 0.001). Risk increased stepwise across quartiles, and no significant nonlinear association was found (P = 0.80 spline). The association did not differ by history of CVD (P interaction= 0.33). The association was attenuated but still elevated and statistically significant in competing risks analysis (sHR, 2.42; 95% CI, 1.15-5.09; P = 0.02).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the association between plasma levels of Gal-3 with incident cardiac and kidney outcomes in a prospective cohort of ambulatory Veterans with more than 13 years of follow-up. We found that higher levels of Gal-3 were associated with greater risk of both cardiovascular and kidney events including MACEs, cardiovascular death, and MAKEs. Furthermore, each doubling of Gal-3 was associated with more than 3 times higher risk of having both MACEs and MAKEs during follow-up. Beyond cardiovascular and kidney outcomes, Gal-3 also captured general mortality risk, as higher levels were associated with greater risk of all-cause mortality. Conversely, Gal-3 was not associated with specific cardiovascular diseases such as incident HF or ACS. Overall, these findings further support plasma Gal-3 as a marker of risk for cardiovascular and kidney disease regardless of comorbid conditions.

Other studies have also reported an association between Gal-3 and incident kidney and cardiovascular events.7, 8, 9, 10, 11 Of these, 3 analyses from the Framingham Offspring study most closely resembled ours because they also evaluated the association of plasma Gal-3 with incident cardiovascular and kidney events in an observational cohort of ambulatory individuals with and without CVD at baseline.9,10,12 These analyses found higher levels of Gal-3 measured were associated with 28% greater risk of incident HF and 15% greater risk of all-cause mortality over 11-years follow-up; however, the association with incident HF was attenuated and no longer significant in sensitivity analyses that excluded participants with prevalent CKD or those who developed incident CKD.10 When Gal-3 levels were measured again 10 years after this first measurement, they found that an increase in Gal-3 during this time frame was associated with 39% greater risk of incident HF, 29% greater risk of CVD, and 30% greater risk of all-cause mortality.9 Furthermore, higher Gal-3 levels measured in 2,450 individuals from the Framingham Offspring study were associated with 49% greater risk of rapid decline in kidney function, defined as eGFR loss of >3 mL/min per 1.73 m2 per year, over 10 years of follow-up.12 Similar to studies from Framingham, we found higher Gal-3 was associated with greater risk of all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, and decline in kidney function; however, we examined both cardiovascular and kidney outcomes concurrently in the same cohort as well as Gal-3’s association with risk of developing both MACE and MAKE events in an individual. These findings are further supported by a recent meta-analysis, which showed an association between Gal-3 and cardiovascular death, HF and kidney events.20 Although our analysis and these studies support Gal-3 as a biomarker for identifying individuals at greater risk for rapid declines in kidney function and cardiovascular mortality, our analyses differ from these others on Gal-3’s association with incident HF. This difference may reflect unique features of our Veteran population and highlights the need to further study Gal-3’s role in predicting HF in other populations.

Studies of Gal-3 in different patient populations also support its use as a risk marker for cardiovascular and kidney events. In a study of 790 individuals admitted with acute heart failure, higher plasma Gal-3 levels were associated with greater risk of adverse cardiovascular and kidney outcomes.21 In a study of 8,445 ambulatory patients, individuals who had the combination of metabolic syndrome, diabetes, and elevated Gal-3 had a 4-fold higher risk of incident HF compared with individuals with neither diabetes nor metabolic syndrome with Gal-3 in the lowest quartile.22 These studies suggest Gal-3 may serve as a biomarker for risk assessment across the spectrum of stages of cardio-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome.23

Beyond blood levels of Gal-3, urine Gal-3 may also be a useful biomarker. Urinary Gal-3 has been associated with histological kidney fibrosis in a study of 280 patients who underwent kidney biopsy.24 Additionally, a study of 566 ambulatory patients with HF found that higher urine Gal-3 was associated with higher mortality but only in individuals with low eGFR, suggesting that urine Gal-3 may be associated with fibrotic changes in these patients.25 In total, these studies and ours add mounting evidence for Gal-3 as a biomarker of global risk for both cardiovascular and kidney events. Notably, we found no interaction between Gal-3 and prevalent CVD suggesting Gal-3 is prognostic across a spectrum of cardiovascular diseases and health statuses and supporting Gal-3 as a risk marker across the spectrum of stages of CKM. This may be especially important in the early stages of CKM given that earlier initiation of therapies like sodium glucose cotransport 2 inhibitors or therapies that antagonize the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system may mitigate risk. Although we were unable to assess urinary Gal-3 in this study, this is a promising avenue for future research.

Whether Gal-3 is simply a marker of cardiorenal disease or mediates pathology still needs to be elucidated. As a mediator of fibrosis and inflammation, which occur in both kidney and cardiovascular disease, increased Gal-3 expression likely reflects tissue injury and adverse remodeling in both organs. Translational studies have helped shed some light on the mechanisms by which Gal-3 may promote kidney and cardiovascular disease. In a mouse model, acute kidney injury (AKI) was shown to directly increase plasma Gal-3 levels. This increase in Gal-3 led to direct cardiac injury, which was prevented when Gal-3 was knocked out.26 These findings support the hypothesis that elevated Gal-3 may reflect underlying kidney injury that contributes to cardiac fibrosis and dysfunction.26 However, the directionality of this relationship requires further study because it is also plausible that cardiac injury could lead to greater Gal-3 levels and subsequent kidney inflammation and injury. Human studies have indicated that patients with HF have decreased clearance of Gal-3 compared with healthy controls, suggesting that Gal-3 may be a marker of kidney injury in patients with cardiac disease.27 Further studies are needed in which direct myocardial injury clearly occurs before kidney injury to determine whether this leads to increases in Gal-3 and subsequent AKI supporting Gal-3 has bidirectional effects on cardiorenal health.

Strengths of our study include an ambulatory population with extended follow-up. Our study also has important limitations. Although the prevalence of women in the Veteran population is increasing, our study primarily enrolled men. Because Veterans were specifically referred for echocardiography, this may have introduced selection bias for a higher risk population. Outcomes were abstracted from the medical record based on documentation and not adjudicated by a central committee; however, with a large integrated health system, the Veterans Affairs can capture many health outcomes. Additionally, though the VA database and National Death Index have been shown to be highly concordant, some mortality outcomes may have been missed because of the lack of direct linkage to the National Death Index.14 Measurements of kidney biomarkers beyond creatinine, such as cystatin C and urine albumin, that may have attenuated Gal-3’s association with MAKE were unavailable. Similarly cardiac biomarkers like natriuretic peptides or troponin were lacking, which may have attenuated associations with MACEs. We did not have complete annual creatinine data for all participants, with an average of 25% of values missing during follow-up. This may have biased our results, though likely toward the null, because declines in kidney function may have gone undetected. Lastly, as a single-center study within the Veterans Affairs Healthcare System, the results also may not be relevant to the wider population.

In conclusion, higher levels of plasma Gal-3 were associated with greater risk of all cause and cardiovascular death, MACE, and adverse kidney events, supporting Gal-3 as an important biomarker for capturing risk for kidney and cardiovascular outcomes.
