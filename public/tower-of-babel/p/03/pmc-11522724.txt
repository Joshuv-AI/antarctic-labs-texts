Vasospasm-Induced Myocardial Infarction and Syncope Manifesting as Recurrent Chest Pain
Authors: Hebah Hassan, Paramvir Singh, Vittorio Terrigno, Arthur Rusovici
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present a case of coronary vasospasm that presented as an acute ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction following a syncopal event, which was preceded by an episode of crushing chest pain. This report discusses proper diagnosis and treatment of cardiogenic syncope and recurrent chest pain secondary to uncontrolled coronary vasospasm.Key Words: myocardial infarction, myocardial ischemia, syncope, vasospasm, vasospastic angina

History of Presentation

A 67-year-old man with a history intermittent chest pain presented after a witnessed syncopal episode in the setting of sudden severe chest pain. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation was initiated by family for concerns of no pulse and unconsciousness. He regained consciousness seconds later. Upon arrival to the hospital, he was found to have T-wave inversions in leads V2 to V6 and leads I and aVL (Figure 1A). The patient reported a similar episode of chest pain 3 months prior and underwent coronary angiography in China. Previous reports (electrocardiogram [ECG] and angiography) were unavailable to staff but reportedly revealed no abnormalities.

(D) Severe coronary spasm of the left anterior descending coronary artery and distal left circumflex artery is seen in during coronary angiography that (D) resolves with intracoronary nitroglycerin injection.

Initially, the patient was resting comfortably with no complaints. Soon after, he developed crushing chest pain and diaphoresis. ECG revealed a ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (Figure 1B). Given this abrupt change in clinical presentation, dynamic ECG changes, and anterior wall motion abnormalities on bedside echocardiogram, he taken emergently for catheterization.

Investigations

Upon selective injection of the left coronary system, there was severe spasm of the left anterior descending coronary artery and distal left circumflex artery (Figure 1C). Intracoronary nitroglycerin was administered, resulting in complete resolution and TIMIflow grade 3 in both vessels (Figure 1D). Left ventriculogram revealed normal left ventricular systolic function despite previously noted wall motion abnormalities.

Management

While in the cardiac care unit, he continued to have episodes of chest pain accompanied by periodic ST-segment elevations (Figure 1B). These episodes resolved with sublingual nitroglycerin. In addition, he was started on isosorbide mononitrate 90 mg and nifedipine 30 mg, resulting in complete resolution of vasospastic episodes.

Discussion

Coronary artery vasospasm is an overlooked mechanism contributing to the development of acute coronary syndrome.1 Typically, this phenomenon manifests with comorbid conditions like hyperlipidemia, hypertension, or a history of smoking. This case is distinctive because the patient presented without a discernible triggering event or underlying comorbidity.

Stenosis of the coronary arteries during vasospastic episodes can lead to transient myocardial ischemia. If spasms persist, they can cause myocardial infarction. Although rare, severe coronary vasospasm can cause chest pain, cardiac arrest, or syncopal episodes.

When diagnosis is unclear and ST-segment changes are present, catheterization is necessary. Intracoronary nitroglycerin is a relatively safe and easy tool to aid in differentiating vasospasm from stenosis, as seen in our case.

Medical therapy involves medications that promote vasodilation, such as calcium-channel blockers and nitrates. If patients do not respond to pharmacologic interventions and have focal vasospasms, percutaneous stent placement may be considered.2

In some instances, patients may develop life-threatening arrhythmias and be at risk for sudden cardiac death. Current guidelines recommend insertion of an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (Class IIa) for this patient population.3

Conclusions

Coronary vasospasm is an infrequently encountered mechanism contributing to the development of acute coronary syndrome. Prompt identification of vasospastic angina is vital because it has the potential to lead to myocardial infarction. This condition encompasses symptoms ranging from asymptomatic presentations to syncope and, in severe cases, cardiac arrest.1 Consideration of vasospasm is crucial to prevent unnecessary stenting. This case report highlights a rare discovery of ST-segment elevation in a patient presenting with angina following an episode of cardiogenic syncope.
