Fusarium wilt disease induced changes in the composition and function of the rhizosphere metabolome and microbiome in tobacco plants
Authors: Lin Yang, Wenjuan Yang, Honghao Zhang, Lingfeng Li, Jiaxin Liu, Mei Li, Rubing Xu, Haibo Xiang, Yanyan Li, Yong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTobacco Fusarium wilt (TFW), caused by Fusarium spp., is a destructive disease affecting tobacco crops and resulting in significant economic losses. The current chemical fungicide-based management strategy poses a risk of resistance development in pathogens. In order to identify key factors in the tobacco rhizosphere for sustainable TFW control, untargeted metabolomics and microbiomics were conducted to analyze the key metabolites and microbes between healthy and diseased rhizosphere soils. The results revealed a total of 65 differential metabolites (DMs), with 40 upregulated and 25 downregulated were identified in the diseased group. Among these, Aesculin, 8-Deoxy-11-hydroxy-13-chlorogrosheimin, and N-Gluconyl ethanolamine phosphate were identified as key DMs exhibiting a strong negative correlation with healthy soil status. The microbiome analysis revealed that bacterial diversity (OTUs: 3278 vs. 3039; Shannon: 6.72 vs. 6.65) and fungal diversity (OTUs: 919 vs. 701; Shannon: 3.91 vs. 3.44) were significantly higher in healthy soils. Furthermore, co-occurrence networks in healthy soils were larger and more stable, with bacteria comprising 431 nodes compared to 405 in diseased soils, and fungi comprising 112 nodes compared to 101 in diseased soils. Additionally, the analysis of the microbial community assembly process revealed that deterministic processes dominated microbial assembly in healthy soils during the first 75 d, subsequently shifting toward stochastic processes. In contrast, diseased soils exhibited a consistently deterministic assembly. Furthermore, we observed that variations in soil types influenced the distribution of key microbial groups. Chloroflexi, Bryobacter, Bacillus, Preussia, and Tausonia, were enriched in healthy soils while Lysobacter, Arthrobacter, Fusarium, and Lectera were dominated in diseased soils. The combined analysis results indicated that the three key DMs correlated positively with disease-enriched microbes but negatively with health-associated taxa. This study provides insights into the changes in and microbe-metabolite interactions within the tobacco rhizosphere under pathogen stress and supports the future development of TFW control strategies based on key metabolites and microbes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40653-w.Keywords: Fusarium wilt, Diseased-healthy, Soil type, Metabolome, MicrobiomeSubject terms: Ecology, Microbiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Fusarium wilt is a destructive fungal disease caused by Fusarium spp. that resides in the soil and significantly reduces the quality and yield of various crops, including tobacco1,2. Its occurrence can lead to leaf wilting, root decay, soil deterioration, the proliferation of harmful microorganisms, and localized outbreaks of the disease3.

Tobacco is a significant economic crop in China, with a planting area exceeding two million hectares4. However, it is highly susceptible to Fusarium wilt which can have detrimental effects on leaf yield and quality, resulting in substantial economic losses. Among the various soil-borne diseases, tobacco Fusarium wilt (TFW) is particularly concerning as it poses a major threat capable of causing significant crop reduction or even total crop failure5. Therefore, it is imperative to explore effective methods for controlling TFW in order to promote sustainable development in the tobacco industry.

Currently, the main method to control TFW is the use of chemical fungicides6. However, long-term use of this approach may lead to the emergence of drug resistance in pathogenic fungi, which in turn can complicate disease control measures and diminish the efficacy of chemical treatments7.

In recent years, researchers have utilized multi-omics analysis to identify crucial antagonistic factors against pathogens in different crops, including Fritillaria ussuriensis, soybean, and Panax notoginseng8–10. These comprehensive analyses have significantly contributed to disease prevention and crop improvement across various agricultural sectors. To address the challenge of controlling TFW more effectively, safely, and sustainably, there is an urgent need for integrated multi-omics analysis.

The occurrence of diseases in tobacco is primarily influenced by the deterioration of soil ecology, imbalance of microbial communities, and reduction of plant resistance, all of which result from disruptions to the soil-microbe-crop system11. The rhizosphere, defined as the area influenced by root exudates and colonized by microorganisms12, is considered one of the most intricate ecosystems on Earth13,14. This microenvironment in the rhizosphere plays a crucial role in influencing the growth and development of plants and their progeny15. Additionally, rhizosphere metabolites play a crucial role in governing the interactions between plants and microorganisms16. They shape the structure of microbial communities and attract beneficial microorganisms to combat plant pathogens, thus contributing to disease prevention17,18. By analyzing the microbiome and metabolome of tobacco’s rhizosphere, we can effectively identify key factors for disease prevention.

Healthy soils are characterized by their resistance to pathogen colonization and ability to display mild disease symptoms19. These soils exhibit remarkable disease suppression even in environments conducive to pathogen spread20. They can foster specific microbial species that outcompete pathogens for ecological niches21–23 or produce specific metabolites that disrupt nutritional systems favorable to pathogens24,25, thereby contributing to disease suppression. Thus, healthy soils play a crucial role in identifying antagonistic factors that can inhibit TFW disease.

Over the past decade, the compositions and functions of metabolites and microbiomes in the rhizosphere of healthy and TFW-diseased tobacco plants, have been characterized using high-throughput sequencing (HTS) and metabolomics methods26–28. One study investigated rhizosphere soil with a severe occurrence of TFW, identifying beneficial microbial microbes such as Lysobacter, Streptomyces, Mortierella, and Penicillium, as well as harmful microbes including Alternaria, Fusarium, and Filobasidium27. Previously, we employed untargeted metabolomics to identify differentially accumulated metabolites between TFW-diseased soil and healthy soil, and natural antifungal metabolites such as Linustatin, scopoletin and phenylpropiolic acid were screened out28. These studies primarily focus on a single technical level and lack the systematic integration of microbiome and metabolome analyses to reveal the key microecological mechanisms by which healthy soil suppress TFW29. In particular, there remains a significant gap in the systematic understanding of how interactions between key microbes and metabolites in healthy versus diseased soils collectively affect the rhizosphere microenvironment, thereby driving the transition of soil from a healthy to a diseased state.

This study utilizes HTS and liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS) to analyze tobacco Fusarium healthy and diseased soils, aiming to identify key factors in Fusarium healthy soil and their potential in controlling Fusarium. The research investigates the impact of healthy soil on the tobacco rhizosphere microbiome and metabolome. The study aims to examine the distribution patterns of metabolites and microbes in the rhizosphere of both healthy and diseased soils. Additionally, it seeks to identify the essential factors contributing to soil Fusarium spp. suppressiveness properties and to investigate the specific bacterial genera that play significant roles in shaping microbial communities.

Results

Changes in the rhizosphere soil metabolome and functional analysis of DMs

To investigate the differences in rhizosphere metabolites between healthy (referred to as the healthy group) and TFW-diseased soils (referred to as the diseased group), we employed untargeted metabolomics to analyze the collected rhizosphere soil samples. Principal component analysis (PCA) based on the Bray-Curtis distance matrix of metabolites from two groups revealed that PC1 and PC2 accounted for 24.04% and 10.67% of the sample variation, respectively (Fig. 1A). Additionally, the samples from the healthy and diseased groups showed a significant separation trend, indicating good reproducibility of the experiment. A total of 553 metabolites were identified in the healthy and the diseased soil (Supplementary Table S1), most of these compounds belong to the categories of Fatty acids, Prenol lipids, Carboxylic acids and derivatives, Organooxygen compounds, Carboxylic acids and derivatives, and Benzene and substituted derivatives (Supplementary Figure S1). Notably, the types of metabolites in the healthy group were significantly higher than those in the diseased group (Fig. 1B, p < 0.05). In comparison to the healthy group, a total of 65 differential metabolites (DMs) were identified in the diseased group, comprising 40 upregulated and 25 downregulated DMs (Fig. 1C, |Fold Change| > 1.2, p < 0.05, VIP > 1). Among these, 8-Deoxy-11-hydroxy-13-chlorogrosheimin (Prenol lipids, C15H19ClO4), N-Gluconyl ethanolamine phosphate (Organic phosphoric acids and derivatives, C8H18NO10P), and Aesculin (Coumarins and derivatives, C15H16O9) with VIP > 2 were identified as key DMs (Fig. 1D). KEGG analysis revealed that these metabolites were mainly involved in metabolic pathways such as Purine metabolism, Pyrimidine metabolism, Alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, Nitrogen metabolism, and Glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism (Supplementary Figure S2).

Bacterial diversity and community structure in rhizosphere soil

A total of 4,497,808 high quality raw sequences were obtained from rhizosphere soil samples following quality filtering. To assess the diversity and richness of microbes across different soil types and sampling times, the observed operational taxonomic units (OTUs) and Shannon index were employed as evaluation indicators. The number of OTUs in all samples varied from 2530 to 3854, with the healthy group exhibiting a higher number of OTUs compared to the diseased group (3278 vs. 3039, p = 0.045) (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Table S2). Furthermore, the Shannon index for the healthy group was significantly greater than that of the diseased group (6.72 vs. 6.65, p = 0.040) (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Table S2). In contrast, sampling time had a minor influence on bacterial diversity between these two groups (Supplementary Figure S3A and B; Richness: p = 0.004, Shannon: p = 0.05).

(D) Composition and structure of the five most abundant bacterial phyla. The values in the figure represent the significance levels obtained from ANOVA and PERMANOVA analysis. ns, nonsignificant; *, p < 0.05; **, p < 0.01.

Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis distance algorithm at the OTU level showed that PC1 and PC2 explained 38.22% and 26.02% of bacterial variation, respectively (Fig. 2C). Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was used to assess the relative contributions of soil type (healthy and diseased) and sampling time (Day_045, Day_075, and Day_105) to the rhizosphere microbial community structure. The results showed the samples collected during the same tobacco developmental stages within one group showed close distances, but distribution among different groups was relatively discrete, and the samples taken at different times were also clearly separated in two groups (PERMANOVA, soil type: R2 = 0.73, p = 0.001; sampling time: R2 = 0.20, p = 0.149). This indicated that soil types had important impact on the formation of rhizosphere bacterial community.

At the phylum level, the five most abundant categories were analyzed, and significant differences in bacterial communities were observed between different soil types (Fig. 2D). The results showed that Acidobacteriota dominated in both soil types (healthy: 32.2%; diseased: 33%). Additionally, Chloroflexi and Firmicutes exhibited significant differences in enrichment across different soil types (Chloroflexi: p = 0.011; Firmicutes: p = 0.009). On the basis of compositional structure analysis, we further conducted LEfSe analysis on the rhizosphere bacterial communities of healthy and diseased groups (Supplementary Figure S4; Supplementary Table S3). The results indicated that in the healthy group, Chloroflexi (LDA = 4.02), Bacillus (LDA = 3.29), and Bryobacter (LDA = 3.15) were significantly enriched, while Methylomirabilota (LDA = 3.55), Lysobacter (LDA = 3.14), Massilia (LDA = 3.22), and Arthrobacter (LDA = 3.65) were enriched in the diseased group, suggesting these bacteria may play an important role in rhizosphere soil healthy state.

Bacterial community stability, symbiotic network, and assembly process analysis

We further assessed the microbial community stability using the average variation degree (AVD) index, where lower AVD values indicated higher community stability. The AVD value for average bacterial variability in healthy soil was significantly lower than that in diseased soil (p < 0.001, Supplementary Figure S6A, Supplementary Table S2), suggesting a greater stability of bacterial communities in healthy soil.

A molecular ecological network was employed to investigate the degree of aggregation among microbial communities, providing additional insights into the composition of rhizosphere microbes (Fig. 3A, B). In bacterial co-occurrence networks, different nodes represent distinct species. After filtering out inactive nodes, the bacterial communities in healthy soil consisted of 431 nodes and 60,466 edges, while those in diseased soil comprised 405 nodes and 35,003 edges. This indicated that the network size of the bacterial community in healthy soil was larger, reflecting higher stability (Supplementary Table S4), which aligned with the AVD results. The topological properties of the network were analyzed through inter-module and intra-module connectivity. Consistent with the LEfSe analysis results, in healthy soil, OTU_1256 (Vicinamibacteraceae) and OTU_1980 (Bryobacter) exhibited high connectivity between modules. In diseased soil, OTU_2204 (Lysobacter) demonstrated high connectivity within a module, while OTU_2318 (Lysobacter) displayed high connectivity between modules (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Table S4).

Microbial communities are constructed by both stochastic and deterministic ecosystem processes. To evaluate the assembly process of rhizosphere bacterial communities, we calculated the beta nearest taxon index (βNTI) to quantify the phylogenetic distances between community compositions, thereby comparing the influence of soil type on community structure. The results indicated that there was no significant difference in βNTI between the two soil types before 75 days of sampling (with both βNTI values ranging between 2 and 4). However, when the sampling time reached 105 d, the βNTI of different soil types was significantly separated. The βNTI for diseased soil exceeded 2 at all sampling intervals, indicating that the construction of bacterial communities in diseased soil was predominantly governed by deterministic processes. Conversely, the βNTI for healthy soil was less than 2 and approached 0 at 105 days (Fig. 3D), suggesting that stochastic processes predominantly influenced the phylogenetic turnover in the healthy rhizosphere microbiome.

Fungal diversity and community structure in rhizosphere soil

A total of 5,498,374 sequences were obtained through optimization, with an average sequence length of 233 base pairs. The richness values and Shannon indices were used to assess soil rhizosphere microbes across different soil types and sampling times. Alpha diversity indices (observed OTUs and Shannon) indicated that changes in soil type significantly affected both observed OTUs and Shannon indices (Fig. 4A, B, Observed OTUs: 799 vs. 722, p = 0.011; Shannon index: 3.65 vs. 3.58, p = 0.001), while the impact of different sampling times on richness and Shannon was minimal (Supplementary Figure S3C, D, Observed OTUs: p = 0.052; Shannon index: p = 0.318). These findings suggested that diseased soil exhibits lower fungal richness and diversity indices within the tobacco rhizosphere.

Similar to the bacterial communities, the composition of fungal communities also exhibited significant variation between the healthy and diseased groups. PCoA result showed that PC1 and PC2 accounted for 24.23% and 19.35% of the fungal data variation, respectively (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Figure S2). PERMANOVA analysis revealed that soil type had a primary effect on fungal community data (R2 = 0.43, p = 0.0002), while the impact of sampling time was less pronounced (R2 = 0.24, p = 0.0008).

At the phylum level, the five most abundant categories were Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, Mortierellomycota, Chytridiomycota, and Olpidiomycota (Fig. 4D). Among these, Ascomycota was predominant in both soil types (healthy: 80.6%; diseased: 79.7%), while only Olpidiomycota exhibited significant differences in enrichment across different soil types (p = 7.82E-10). Based on compositional structure analysis, we further conducted LEfSe analysis on the rhizosphere fungal communities of healthy and diseased soils (Supplementary Figure S5; Supplementary Table S3). The results indicated that in the healthy group, Preussia (LDA = 4.20), Tausonia (LDA = 4.17), and Thelebolus (LDA = 3.88) were significantly enriched. In contrast, Plectosphaerella (LDA = 4.24), Fusarium (LDA = 4.17), Gibberella (LDA = 3.78), and Lectera (LDA = 3.10) were enriched in the diseased group, suggesting that these fungi may play an important role in maintaining a healthy rhizosphere soil state.

Fungal community stability, symbiotic network, and assembly process analysis

The average value of AVD in healthy rhizosphere soil was significantly lower than that in diseased rhizosphere soil (Supplementary Figure S6B, Supplementary Table S2), indicating that the stability of fungal communities in healthy soil is greater than in diseased soil. Further fungal co-occurrence network analysis revealed that the network structure in healthy group contained 2,919 edges and 112 nodes, whereas the network in diseased group contained 2,411 edges and 101 nodes (Fig. 5A, B; Supplementary Table S4). This suggested that the stability of the fungal community in healthy soil is superior, exhibiting a stronger capacity to resist external disturbances. Based on the calculation of the Zi and Pi values, three significant nodes were identified: OTU_4079 (Clonostachys), OTU_486 (Holtermanniella), and OTU_4433 (Lectera) (Fig. 5C), which partially aligned with the LEFSE results.

Similar to the bacterial assembly process, the βNTI values of the fungal community in both groups ranged from 0 to 6, with all βNTI values exceeding 2 in both diseased and healthy soils before 75 d. At the 105 d, the βNTI values of different soil types were significantly distinct, with diseased soil exhibiting βNTI values greater than 2, while healthy soil displayed values between 0 and 2 (Fig. 5D). These findings suggested that the assembly process of fungal communities in healthy soil was predominantly influenced by deterministic factors during the first 75 days, transitioning to a primarily stochastic process thereafter. In contrast, the assembly process of fungal communities in diseased soil was consistently governed by deterministic factors throughout the entire cultivation period.

Multi-omics reveals key metabolites and microbes in diseased soil

We investigated the correlation between the detected DMs and the abundant microbial genera in two distinct groups. In bacterial community, the relative relative abundance of Aesculin, 8-Deoxy-11-hydroxy-13-chlorogrosheimin and N-Gluconyl ethanolamine phosphate were all positively correlated with the relative abundance of Arthrobacter, Lysobacter, Massilia and Terrisporobacter, while negatively correlated with the relative abundance of Bryobacter and Mycobacterium (Fig. 6A, Supplementary Table S5). In the case of fungal genera, Monocillium, Lophiotrema, Preussia, Tausonia, and Cephalotrichum exhibited strong negative correlations (r < −0.6) with these three DMs, whereas Olpidium, Stachybotrys, and Lectera demonstrated strong positive correlations (r > 0.6) with these three DMs (Fig. 6B, Supplementary Table S6).

Discussion

In this study, we integrated LC-MS/MS data with high-throughput sequencing data from TFW rhizosphere-associated soil samples to identify key metabolites and microbial community characteristics linked to disease. Soil metabolites predominantly derive from plant roots and microbes. The composition of root exudates is influenced by factors such as plant species, genotype, and environmental stress30,31. Alterations in the composition and levels of soil metabolites can provide insights into the immediate or past reactions of soil microbes to soil nutrients32. Furthermore, specific metabolites in root exudates may influence the assembly process of rhizosphere microbial communities, leading to a “susceptible” microbiome under pathogen attack29. Our study revealed significant alterations in the distribution of metabolites within the rhizosphere microbiome associated with healthy and diseased roots affected by Fusarium. Notably, 8-Deoxy-11-hydroxy-13-chlorogrosheimin, N-Gluconyl ethanolamine phosphate, and Aesculin demonstrated a strong correlation with diseased soil, suggesting their potential significance in disease development. Aesculin is a well-known coumarin glycoside that possesses various therapeutic properties, including anti-inflammatory, anti-tumor, anti-oxidative, anti-depressive, anti-diabetic, anti-amyloidogenic, antithrombotic, antibacterial, antifungal, and antiviral activities33,34. Although Aesculin has demonstrated antibiosis effects against a variety of fungi, including Trichophyton interdigitale, Trichophyton mentagrophyte, Trichophyton rubrum, Trichophyton soudanens, Trichophyton tonsurans, Microsporum canis, Aspergillus fumigatus, and Scopulariopsis brevicaulis, there have been no reports of its inhibitory effects on Fusarium33. In contrast, its aglycone, Aesculetin, has been reported to exhibit antifungal activity against 84 fungi isolated from human superficial mycoses, as well as Fusarium35–37. Aesculin can be enzymatically hydrolyzed to esculetin by β-glucosidases or certain microbes35. The hydrolysis of Aesculin to Aesculetin has been suggested to reduce the survival of specific pathogens in anaerobic gut habitats38. Our results indicated that the content of Aesculin in healthy soil is lower than that in diseased soil, suggesting that Aesculin may be transformed into esculetin to exert an anti-Fusarium effect. However, we did not identify Aesculetin in either group and further quantitative analysis of Aesculetin is needed.

The composition of the rhizosphere soil microbial community plays a critical role in determining plant health and immune responses. This community is essential for inhibiting soilborne pathogens39. This study demonstrated that the alpha diversity index of both bacterial and fungal communities in healthy soil was significantly higher than that in diseased soil, indicating that lower microbial community diversity was associated with disease, which aligned with earlier findings40. Network analyses revealed that a lower number of bacterial and fungal connections were associated with the diseased network, reflecting less robust microbe-microbe interactions within the community. This finding was consistent with the observation that the number of network edges decreased in a Fusarium wilt-diseased microbial network in bananas, with more connections present in networks associated with healthy rhizosphere soils compared to diseased samples41. This study demonstrates that the transition from healthy soil to diseased soil caused by Fusarium affects the structural distribution of microbial communities, reduces community stability, and significantly impacts microbial network characteristics and community evolution processes.

The assembly of plant rhizosphere microbiome is influenced by various biotic and abiotic factors, with pathogen invasion being one of the most significant biotic stresses affecting this assembly42. Numerous studies on wheat43, sugar beet44, and Arabidopsis thaliana45 have demonstrated that the roots of pathogen-infected plants can attract special microbes, thereby facilitating rescue or protection for future generations, a phenomenon referred to as the “cry for help” strategy. Specifically, alterations in soil conditions can impact the distribution of key microbes within microbial communities46. In this study, Lysobacter, which is enriched in diseased soil, along with Bryobacter and Bacillus, which are enriched in healthy soil, played crucial roles in the networks of bacterial communities. These bacteria responded rapidly to changes in soil conditions and possessed the potential to prevent diseases as well as promoted disease occurrence. Bacillus has been widely used in the control of many pathogens, such as Fusarium47, Ralstonia solanacearum48, and Alternaria solani49, making it an ideal biocontrol agent50. The strong correlation of Bacillus with Healthy soil (LDA > 3) further supported this conclusion. Although Bryobacter had rarely been reported in the control of pathogens, it is generally considered a genus sensitive to environmental changes, including pH51 and soil52. Additionally, it was recognized as a beneficial bacterium involved in the control of Fusarium root rot in Angelica sinensis. The Gammaproteobacterial genus Lysobacter represents a group of antifungal biocontrol agents that exert their antifungal effects through the secretion of antibiotics and growth-inhibiting enzymes53. This genus is classified as a beneficial microbe and has shown a notable decreasing trend when the rhizosphere microbial community of diseased tobacco plants shifts towards a pathological composition27. This finding contradicted our observations, which indicated that Lysobacter is positively correlated with diseased soils. The discrepancy may be attributed to differences in sampling locations (southern China vs. central China). Additionally, when plants are infected by pathogens, they will cry for help to recruit beneficial microorganisms that accumulate in the rhizosphere. Lysobacter is one such beneficial bacterium that can be recruited, leading to an increased presence in the disease soil54. Similarly, Gao et al.42 reported that, compared with healthy plants, the relative abundance of potential beneficial bacteria in FWD pepper significantly increased.

Fungi also play a crucial role in maintaining soil health55. Diseased soil significantly impacts the structure and assembly characteristics of the soil fungal network. The distribution pattern of communities was closely related to their functional roles56. This discrepancy may be attributed to the presence of Fusarium, as indicated in an early study57. Consistent with the distribution pattern of bacteria, diseased soil led to a decrease in fungal community richness and evenness. Furthermore, the assembly process of the community tended to be influenced by deterministic factors, which reduced the functional redundancy of fungal communities due to soil disease58. Soil disease affected the distribution characteristics of key fungal communities, with 20 significantly different fungal genera identified through LefSe analysis. Notably, potential pathogenic fungal such as Fusarium, Olpidium, Plectosphaerella, Stachybotrys, and Gibellulopsis significantly enriched in diseased soil. This suggested that TFW may result from the combined action of multiple harmful microorganisms59, with the phenotypic manifestation in this study being the invasion of Fusarium. Arthrographis, Cephalotrichum, Lophiotrema, Monocillium, Preussia, Ramophialophora, Tausonia were significantly enriched in healthy soil and exhibit a strong negative correlation with three metabolites, indicating their potential in controlling Fusarium or serving as indicators of soil health status.

This study found that changes in the types and abundances of key rhizosphere metabolites and microbes, as well as changes in their interactions, played important roles in the transition from healthy soils to diseased soils. However, the soil type transition driven by these key metabolites and microbes required further verification. Next, we will integrate chemical biology and synthetic microbial community techniques to further validate the functional roles of key metabolites and microbes, as well as to elucidate the specific mechanisms by which they control TFW.

Conclusion

Integrated metabolome and microbiome analysis of the healthy soil and TFW-diseased soil revealed three key metabolites—Aesculin, 8-Deoxy-11-hydroxy-13-chlorogrosheimin, and N-Gluconyl ethanolamine phosphate showed a strong negative correlation with soil health. Healthy soils had higher bacterial/fungal diversity, larger and more stable microbial co-occurrence networks, and their community assembly shifted from deterministic to stochastic processes, while diseased soils showed consistent determinism. Key microbial taxa differed between the two soil types, with key metabolites positively correlating with disease-enriched microbes and negatively with health-associated ones. This study significantly enhances our understanding of the relationship between changes in rhizosphere microecology and soil health. Furthermore, it provides potential strategies for manipulating the plant metabolome and microbiome to control TFW and promote sustainable tobacco production.

Materials and methods

Experimental site and field treatments

The sampling site, located in Xuan’en County (29.99◦N, 109.44◦E), Hubei Province, China, was characterized by field soil classified as a specific type of yellow-brown soil with a moderate level of fertility and situated at an elevation of 500 meters. The tobacco fields in this area had been continuously cropped with tobacco for over 15 years, with a severe incidence of TFW disease. The tobacco variety ‘Yunyan-87’ was cultivated. Samples of healthy soil (referred to as the Healthy group) and TFW-diseased soil (referred to as the Diseased group) were collected at 45, 75, and 105 days after tobacco transplanting (named as Day_045, Day_075, and Day_105). To ensure data reliability, each group had rhizosphere soil samples from three different fields. In each field, three replicate samples were collected using the five-point sampling method. The required rhizosphere soil samples were obtained by brushing the surface of the roots with a brush to remove the adhering materials. The soil samples were mixed evenly, placed in dry ice, and brought back to the laboratory, where they were stored at −80 °C for DNA and metabolite extraction.

Untargeted metabolomics analysis

Untargeted metabolomics was carried out with the method described previously60. Briefly, soil samples were resuspended with 80% methanol and the extracted metabolites were separated on an HSS T3 column using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) coupled with Fourier transform mass spectrometry (FTMS) at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% aqueous formic acid solution (Phase A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile solution (Phase B). Mass spectrometry data were acquired in both positive and negative ion scanning modes, with a mass scan range of 50–5000 m/z.

Metabolomics data processing was performed using Compound Discoverer 3.3 (CD) software. Metabolites were identified by matching the AcquireX data with the KEGG and HMDB databases, with a mass tolerance of 10 ppm. The repeatability of the samples was assessed using the orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) in the online software MetaboAnalyst 5.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca). The identified metabolites were classified based on the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/).

DNA extraction and high throughput sequencing

The DNA extraction was carried out with the method described by Liu et al.25 with minor revision. Briefly, total soil DNA was extracted using the FastDNA Spin Kit (MP Biomedicals, USA). The V4 region of 16 S rDNA was amplified with primers 515 F (GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA) and 806R (GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT), while the fungal ITS1 region was amplified with primers ITS5-1737 F (GGAAGTAAAAGTCGTAACAAGG) and ITS2-2043R (GCTGCGTTCTTCATC GATGC). The reaction protocol was as follows: 98 °C for 1 min; 98 °C for 10 s, 50 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 30 s (30 cycles); and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min (Biorad T100 gradient PCR machine). The PCR products were purified with QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (QIAGEN, Germany), and the concentration and purity of DNA were determined using the Nanodrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Nanodrop Technologies, Wilmington, DE, USA). Subsequently, the soil microbial diversity was sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform by Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Company in China.

Microbial community analysis

Microbial community structure and diversity analysis

For all samples, the raw sequencing reads were quality-controlled and analyzed according to the method described by Yang et al.27. Briefly, clean reads were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) via Uparse software (Uparse v7.0.1001) (http://drive5.com/uparse/) with a 97% sequence identity cutoff value. Unique sequences with ≥ 97% sequence similarity were assigned to the same OTUs. The Alpha diversity indices, including richness and Shannon, were fully assessed using the vegan package in R software (v4.3.1). Beta diversity was calculated using the Bray-Curtis method to create a sample distance matrix, and Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) was performed using the vegan package to determine the distribution patterns of microbial communities25. Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was performed with 999 permutations through the adonis function implemented in vegan package to analyse the significant differences in community structure between groups. Species analysis highlighting significant differences between groups was performed using LEfSe software, with a default LDA score threshold of 3.5.

Assessment of Community Assembly Processes

The dynamic assembly process of the microbial community was understood using the beta nearest taxon index (βNTI)29, where |βNTI| < 2 indicates that the assembly of the community is influenced by stochastic processes. βNTI > 2 suggests that variable selection plays a dominant role in community assembly, while βNTI < −2 indicates that homogeneous selection is the primary driver.

Molecular Ecological Network Analysis Based on Random Matrix Theory

For molecular ecological network analysis, network visualization was performed using Gephi software. Additionally, topological parameters of the network (such as average path length, network diameter, average degree, graph density, clustering coefficient, and modularity) were calculated using the ggClusterNet package in R software32. Nodes were classified into four categories based on inter-module (Pi) and intra-module (Zi) connectivity values: When Zi < 2.50 and Pi < 0.62, the node was a peripheral node; when Zi < 2.50 and Pi ≥ 0.62, the node was called a connector; when Zi ≥ 2.50 and Pi < 0.62, the node was a module hub; When Zi ≥ 2.50 and Pi ≥ 0.62, the node was called a network hub61. Only network hubs containing key species were used in the symbiotic network.

Statistical Analysis and Testing

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (v 4.3.1). Intergroup statistical tests were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Spearman’s method was employed to calculate the correlations between OTUs and metabolites, and the correlation was considered significant at padj values below 0.05.
