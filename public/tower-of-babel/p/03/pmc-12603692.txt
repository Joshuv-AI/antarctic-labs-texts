Spray drift in arboriculture: A dataset to analyse the influence of spray application techniques, anti-drift nets, and their combinations on the reduction of sedimentary drift, aerial drift and exposure of bystanders
Authors: Yoan Hudebine, Florence Verpont, Adrien Vergès, Sébastien Codis, Benjamin Perriot, David Pasquier, Jean-Paul Douzals, Carole Bedos, Sonia Grimbuhler, Marianne Sellam, Olivier Naud
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In 2021 and 2022, the national and cross-sector project CAPRIV funded by the French Agriculture Ministry, made it possible assess the influence of application techniques associated or not with a hedge, or an anti-drift net on spray drift and bystander exposure. The acronym CAPRIV stands for “Concilier l’application des PPP et la protection des riverains” (Reconciling the use of PPPs and the protection of residents), within the orchard, viticulture, and field crop sectors. This specific data article focuses on arboriculture, especially on trellised pome fruit. Over the two years, sprayers with contrasting performance (axial airblast sprayer with and without anti-drift nozzles, pneumatic sprayer with directional flow) were used to measure the drift potential with and without a net. The solid set canopy delivery system “PulVefix” and border row management techniques were also evaluated. Spray drift has been measured according to a common protocol harmonised between cropping sectors within the project. Three different types of passive drift collectors were used: Petri dishes for measuring sedimentary drift, PVC wires placed between two masts for measuring airborne drift and cotton Long sleeve shirts placed on manikins to assess potential dermal exposure of bystanders. The sprayed mix contained a fluorescent dye and water. The mass of dye was measured using a classical technique with dilution and concentration evaluation. Two fluorescent dyes were successively used, Brillant Sulfaflavine and Sulforhodamine B. A total amount of 3573 collectors were analysed individually. The data set provides a drift index for each collector expressed as the quantity of dye recovered per unit area of collectors on the quantity of dye applied per unit area on the sprayed field multiplied by 100.

Value of the Data

•The presented data, which are part of the data accessible in [1], are about spray drift in the vicinity of an apple tree field being sprayed with commonly used spraying technologies and drift mitigation measures such as using air-induction nozzles or using an anti-drift net at the edge of the field. They are complementary to data presented in [2] and [3], as these previously published data papers deal with wheat and with viticulture.•Spray drift was evaluated with a specific collecting method for each drift notion considered: Petri dishes on the ground for sedimentary drift, PVC wires (diameter 2 mm) arranged on a vertical plane for airborne drift, and cotton cloth arranged on torso and arms of flat human shaped manikins to represent bystander dermal exposure. For this study on orchards, sedimentary drift was collected between 2 m and 20–25 m from the field, the vertical plane for collecting air drift was placed at 5 m from the field, and the manikins were placed between 3 m and 20 m.•Spray drift was evaluated with regards to physical effects that are not product specific by using a spray mixture of water and tracer. The three types of spray drift data were simultaneously acquired: sedimentary drift, airborne drift and drift deposition on bystanders (simulated by manikins).•The diversity of drift sampling measures that were simultaneously collected should contribute to the development of new data, new knowledge and new models within an applied physics approach. This drift sampling scheme was applied within the CAPRIV project to three crop types: trellised grapevine, trellised fruit crops and field crops. The experimental protocol was harmonised between these three crop types with a diversity of spraying modalities. A harmonised database [1] was built and its contents for orchards (trellised apple trees) are presented in this data paper.•The drift data can be compared according to spray technologies and drift mitigation measures for supporting decision-making at various levels: growers, specifications for production, regulations by public authorities, incentives for sustainable production methods.•The drift data can be exploited to develop and enhance risk assessment methods and databases related to the use and homologation of phytosanitary products used in trellised apple production. In particular, such risk assessment methods might embed contrasted technologies, settings and drift mitigation means and methods and eventually support differentiations between production cases in decisions and regulations that fit the reality of farming practices.

Background

Drift of pesticides during spraying in field crops and perennial crops is an important issue that has motivated a number of studies and research worldwide. One major concern for European authorities has been the protection of freshwater within the framework of the EU Directive 2000/60/EC on water quality. This motivated many studies on drift deposited on the ground downwind of the treated field referred hereafter as “sedimentary drift”. One other major concern is the protection of people in the vicinity of crops, which are referred to hereafter as “bystanders” and “residents” [4]. Studies related to exposure of bystanders as a consequence of spray drift have recently often been conducted on manikins to assess dermal exposure [[5], [6], [7]]. Finally, the study of the airborne fraction of spray drift is often done with specific collectors in a vertical plane in order to minimize flow disruption [8].

The motivation of the CAPRIV project was to combine the evaluation of these three different spray drift effects in a common protocol and three types of collectors [2,3]. This data paper presents a dataset obtained with trials carried out in orchards.

Data Description

The data in this article is distributed in two main csv files (separator semi-colon), CAPRIV_Orchards.csv with 3573 lines containing, for the orchards sector, the data from individual collectors for a set of trials which are individually identified, and a table SAMC_Orchards_CAPRIV.csv that details the parameters of spraying methods which were evaluated. Two complementary tables that are common to data of the whole CAPRIV project complement the information, one (units_maintables.xlsx) details the signification of column names for CAPRIV_Orchards.csv and provides information on units, and one (units_samctables.xlsx) provides similar information for SAMC_Orchards_CAPRIV.csv. These tables are provided in the ‘data’ directory within the dataset [1]. A directory named ‘weather’ contains raw weather data for traceability.

In the following tables, the column name has a format N-XX, where N designates the table (number, 1 for CAPRIV_Orchards.csv, 2 for SAMC_Orchards_CAPRIV.csv) and XX designates the column in their order of appearance. Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Field tests implementation and conditions

To measure the drift of different sprayers at field level, the standardized methodology “Methods for field measurement of spray drift” described in ISO 22,866:2005 was used as a reference. Performing tests under the exact conditions described in this standard and in natural wind conditions is a challenging task. This is why the conformity to the standard and the discussion on validity of weather conditions is documented in the data. All the trials for which the data are provided in this datapaper were carried out in the framework of the CAPRIV project to investigate solutions to reduce spray drift. The dataset provides here all the results obtained, including those obtained in meteorological conditions that do not meet the requirements of the standard. Many of these have been validated because the weather conditions were relatively close to the criteria of the standard (expert validation documented in “weather_comment” column). The columns “weather_conformity” and “weather_valid” of the database respectively identify if a test is in strict conformity to the standard and if it is considered valid by the data producers.

The tests were carried in three orchards described below. Two of these were on opposite sides of a plot with short grass on which collectors could be placed. Thus, the tests could be carried out in the event of a West or East wind (Fig. 1). The choice of the number and positioning of the collectors for the sedimentary and airborne drift was made following the recommendations given by the ISO 22,866:2005 standard and based on work carried out previously [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12]]. Meteorological conditions were recorded during trials according to the requirements of ISO 22,866:2005. In 2021, the weather station used (WMR300 model; Oregon Scientific, Inc., Tualatin, OR, USA) made it possible to record the horizontal component of the wind, its speed, the temperature, and the hygrometry at a frequency of one measurement per minute. The weather station was positioned 2.40 m above the ground, 12 m behind the last line of collectors. In 2022 another weather station (150WX model; Milford, NH, USA) was used and made it possible to record the wind direction and speed vectors as well as temperature and hygrometry at a frequency between 0.37 Hz and 3.3 Hz (differing between trials but constant in a trial). The weather station was positioned 5 m above the ground, 12 m behind the last line of collectors.Fig. 1Location of the experimental plots.Fig 1

The wind direction that is measured by the weather station is the direction of origin of the wind (wind from the North has value 0°). All the orchards consisted of apple trees. The first orchard (Fig. 2a) was 4 m high (total height of the trees). The planting distances were 4 m for inter-row distance and 1.25 m for inter-plant distance within the row. The variety was Gala Brookfield and it was planted in winter 2004. The second orchard (Fig. 2b) at the opposite side of the grass plot was 4 m high. The planting distances were 4 m for inter-row distance and 1 m for inter-plant distance within the row. The variety was Braeburn and it was planted in winter 2004. The third orchard (Fig. 2c) was 3.60 m high. The planting distances were 3.5 m for inter-row distance and 1 m for inter-plant distance within the row. The variety was Rosy glow and it was planted in winter 2013. The Pulvefix solid set canopy delivery system and its ability to reduce drift were evaluated in this third orchard. The system used makes it possible to treat ten rows of 50 m long in 30 s using microsprinklers. A schematic representation of the drift test set-up is provided in Figs. 3 (without anti-drift net) and 4 (with anti-drift net). In Fig. 4 where the anti-drift net is deployed, the manikins on the 3 m line were not placed for most of the tests. The zero point is positioned at the axis of the last row of the orchard. This drift sampling protocol was harmonized within the CAPRIV project between three production sectors: field crops, vines and fruit tree crops. Collectors for drift sampling

The collectors were the same as described in [2], applying the common protocol. Their placement and numbers could nevertheless be adapted to handle situations met in the trial orchards. All data collection information is summarised here for quick reference.

The diameter of Petri dishes was 14 cm (Fig. 5) and their collecting surface area was 0.0154 m². They were placed at 2, 3, 5, 10 and 20 m from the last treated row of the orchard. Ten petri dishes, labeled from a to j were placed at each distance, with a space between each replicate of 2 m, and on the bare ground. In 2022 for the tests with the anti-drift net, additional petri dishes were also positioned at 15 m and 25 m. Once collected, the Petri dishes were closed and placed by groups of 10 in the dark.Fig. 5Petri dishes used for the sedimentary drift collection.Fig 5

Petri dishes used for the sedimentary drift collection.

The wires used to quantify airborne drift were entirely made of PVC and stretched between two masts (Fig. 6) placed at 5 m from the last row treated, meeting requirements of ISO 22,866. Preliminary tests demonstrated the benefits of collecting aerial drift at 5 m rather than at longer distances such as 10, 20 or 30 m. The PVC wires had a diameter of 2 mm. Laboratory analysis

a) General procedure

The principles for analysis were the same for all production sectors in the CAPRIV project and explained in the article dedicated to field crops measurements [2].

The principle is to extract the dry tracer deposits present on the collector with a known volume of waterThen an aliquot of the solution is sampled, analyzed in a spectrofluorometer and compared to the initial concentration of the tank mixture. b) Extraction methods by collector type

The extraction method consists in redissolving the deposits by adding 20 to 140 mL of deionised water for Petri dishes, 10 to 30 mL for wires, 200 to 350 mL for cotton arms (clothes of manikins) and 700 to 900 mL for cotton -trunks (clothes of manikins). For the Petri dishes and the wires boxes, a manual shake is done to ensure that all the water passed over the collection surface. For the clothes boxes, a shaker tray is used (20 min at 150 rpm) and the samples are leaved to soak for additional 1h30. c) Spectrofluorometer settings

For trials 2021 using Brilliant Sulfaflavine (BSF) as fluorescent tracer, the setting of the spectrofluorometer used for trials in orchards (Hidex Sense microplate reader) was: λ excitation = 460 nm (slit 20), λ emission = 535 nm (slit 20). For trials 2022 using Sulforhodamine B (SRB) as fluorescent tracer, the setting of the spectrofluorimeter was: λ excitation = 575 nm (slit 20), λ emission = 620 nm (slit 10). The tracer used and the regression coefficient resulting from spectrofluorometer calibration on a tank mixture are documented in the data (columns tracer and reg_coeff respectively). d) Blank correction

All RFU measurement made in the orchards trials were made using a “blank” sample introduced in the spectrofluorometer before introducing the sample to be measured. As blank correction was always made inside the machine, the column named “blank” in the CAPRIV data for orchards is always filled with zero (see [2] for exceptions in CAPRIV data for other crops). For calibration and details on extraction process, please refer to [2].

Expression of the results

Considering the field names provided in Table 1, and consistently with calculations made in [2,3], the normalized drift index di was obtained according to the formula:di=(rfu−blankB)×Vdilb×s×V×N×103where blank is zero, where di and rfu are as from these columns in the data, and where Vdil is ‘dilution_vol’, b is ‘reg_coeff’, s is ‘collector_area’, V is ‘vol_ha_applied’ and N is ‘nb_pass’.

Limitations

For traceability reasons, lines with Nan (not a number) values for RFU can be found in the main table, when an incident on a given sample was encountered, (e.g. overturned Petri dish) and thus no measurement could be provided. There are between 104 and 136 data lines (1 line is 1 value for a collector) per trial in the main table for orchards.

Due to quick wind changes during trials, there are many situations in which the conditions of the ISO 22,866:2005 norm are not met. Yet, these data may have some use for making comparisons. The column ‘weather_valid’ is an indication provided by expertise that data have some interest for evaluation of drift. When ‘no’ is indicated for ‘weather_valid’, the data user is advised to check corresponding raw weather data before any interpretation.

For Airmar 150WX weather station used in 2022, frequencies of acquisition were not the same for all trials, due to some inappropriate settings affecting digital communication between sensor and computer. This does not affect the quality and regularity of measurements in each trial.

As mentioned in [2,3], users of the data are advised to treat with caution the data from manikins’ samples when tracer is BSF (due to difficulties in extracting BSF as documented in [2]).

Users of data related to deposits on manikins are advised that only torso and arms were considered in sampling, not the lower parts. Manikins used were flat (surface interpretation of the body).
