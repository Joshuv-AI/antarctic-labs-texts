Re: Abdelmotaal et al: Artificial Intelligence—Derived Biomechanical Index for Early Corneal Ectasia Detection: Advancing Beyond Tomography
Authors: Abhijit Sinha Roy
Published: 2026

To The Editor:

This study presented a new artificial intelligence (AI) model using custom-derived image analytics parameters from the deformed cornea images captured by Corvis-ST.1 The objective of this study was to differentiate between healthy, very asymmetric ectasia-normal topography (VAE-NT) and mild keratoconus (MKC) corneas.1 The study initially derived 94 features from custom image analytics of corneal deformation. However, only a 10-feature subset was used in the final AI models (Table S1 in the original article).1 In binary models using the 10 features independently, Figure S8 (in the original article) showed that the maximum area under the curve achieved was 0.87 for the mean above-threshold correlation for dynamics, whereas the other features achieved an area under the curve ranging from 0.5 to 0.6 (barely better than a coin toss).1 A natural conclusion would be that the 10 features would not be very effective in distinguishing between normal and VAE-NT corneas. However, the AI model combining the 10 features achieved an area under the curve, with sensitivity and specificity of 1, equal to 100%, and 100%, respectively (Table 3 in the original article).1 This final result was very surprising, and because the paper did not provide any specifications of the AI model settings, we were unable to assess overfitting of the data.

In the paper, all VAE-NT corneas were identified as ectasia along with the MKC corneas by the AI model.1 This implied that there was no unilateral KC in the patient population of the study. It appeared improbable to us that a data set would include all the patients with VAE-NT incidentally bilaterally affected by disease. Table 3 (in the original article) showed that tomographic biomechanical index and corneal biomechanical index identified a few of the VAE-NT corneas as nonectatic, which appeared reasonable.1 Brillouin modulus claims that the VAE-NT has a distinct profile from KC corneas.2 Further, there is no known data on the repeatability of the 10 features (Table S1 in the original article), which was another limitation of the study. Another fundamental issue was that the methods were dependent on 2-dimensional digital image correlation.3 The actual deformation of the cornea occurs 3-dimensionally when applanated by an air-puff or any other mechanical perturbation. Hence, the cross-section imaged at the start of the applanation may differ significantly from the cross-section at the maximum applanation time point due to out-of-plane motion. The Corvis-ST can image only along a fixed location. Perhaps the image analysis1 was a significant oversimplification of the applanation deformation mechanics. Tables 1 and 2 (in the original article) showed that stiffness parameter at 1st applanation point differed significantly between normal, VAE-NT, and MKC (P < 0.05).1 The index, stiffness parameter at 1st applanation point, is a measure of stiffness of the cornea at first applanation point. Thus, the biomechanical stiffness did differ between the 3 groups.1 However, the outcome (Figure 4 in the original article) of the AI model essentially implied that the stiffness of the VAE-NT corneas was similar to the MKC corneas. In summary, the AI model was not effective in identifying unique differential features of VAE-NT corneas compared with normal and MKC corneas. This may be a concern for screening corneas for refractive or therapeutic surgery, as the general population includes several patients with bilaterally thin corneas and normal topography, for example, the demographics in Table 1 (in the original article) showed significant overlap in maximum anterior curvature and central corneal thickness ranges between normal and VAE-NT. Thus, several patients may become ineligible for surgery if screened using the proposed AI model.
