Species-specific brain transcriptomic responses to clothianidin and sulfoxaflor in pollinators
Authors: Alicja Witwicka, Federico López-Osorio, Courtney May, Yeahji Jeong, Yannick Wurm
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryInsects that pollinate essential crops are frequently exposed to neurotoxic insecticides. Although most pollinators belong to insect lineages that diverged hundreds of millions of years ago, insecticide safety tests focus on a few model bee species. Here, we used comparative whole-brain transcriptomics to test whether sulfoxaflor and clothianidin elicit similar or distinct responses across four phylogenetically diverse pollinators: Bombus terrestris, Osmia bicornis, Lucilia sericata, and Vanessa cardui. Within each species, both insecticides produce broadly similar gene regulatory profiles, with more than 70% of differentially expressed genes shared. By contrast, transcriptional responses differed sharply across species, with no shared genes or pathways consistently disrupted. Unexpectedly, sulfoxaflor, promoted as a “bee-safe” alternative, provokes broader gene regulatory changes in non-bee species than clothianidin, a restricted neonicotinoid. Our findings reveal extensive transcriptional divergence in insecticide responses across pollinators, suggesting that risk assessments would benefit from greater phylogenetic diversity and novel molecular approaches.

Introduction

Insects pollinate plants as they forage for nectar and pollen, enhancing wild plant biodiversity and supporting production of fruits, vegetables, and nuts.1,2,3,4,5 These ecological and agricultural benefits have come under pressure due to multiple anthropogenic hazards, such as neurotoxic insecticides linked to pollinator losses.6,7,8,9,10,11,12,13 For example, a growing body of research on bees indicates that insecticides impair their ability to memorize, navigate, reproduce, and survive.8,12,14,15,16,17,18,19,20,21 In response, policymakers have banned certain insecticides in some world regions and approved others as alternatives.22 Yet, these new agrochemicals often carry the same risks.18,23,24,25 These insights raise a central question: why do insecticides approved as “safe” still harm pollinators?

Despite mounting reports of indiscriminate pollinator harm, insecticide toxicity testing continues to have a narrow taxonomic scope. In particular, the western honeybee Apis mellifera remains the primary model for assessing the unintended damage of insecticides on pollinators.26,27,28,29 With a history spanning over 479 million years,30,31 insects have evolved diverse behaviors, metabolic pathways, neural architectures, and adaptations for metabolizing foreign chemicals of natural or synthetic origin. Therefore, non-Apis bees and other critical pollinating insects, such as flies and butterflies, differ substantially from the honeybee and one another, and may vary in their responses to insecticide exposure.2,28,32,33,34,35,36,37,38

While most insecticides act on conserved neuronal receptors, the molecular processes driving species-specific responses remain poorly understood, particularly across diverse pollinator lineages. Cholinergic insecticides such as neonicotinoids and sulfoximines activate nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs) that mediate fast excitatory synaptic signaling in the insect central nervous system, primarily in the brain.39,40 Although all pollinators express nAChRs, insecticide susceptibility can vary due to differences in nAChR subtypes, detoxifying enzymes, and downstream pathways.22,41,42,43 Yet, we still know little about genome-wide molecular responses to insecticides beyond bees, leaving the broader mechanistic basis of pollinator damage unresolved.

Here, we examine whole-brain transcriptomic profiles of pollinating insect species to test whether insecticides trigger conserved or lineage-specific responses. Our study examines four species from three insect orders: a widely distributed butterfly species, the painted lady (Vanessa cardui); the agriculturally significant green bottle fly (Lucilia sericata); the solitary red mason bee (Osmia bicornis); and the social buff-tailed bumble bee (Bombus terrestris). These species span distinct evolutionary lineages, life histories, and ecological roles, providing insight into broader patterns of species-specific responses to insecticides. Each species received a field-realistic dose (4.4 ppb) of two insecticides administered separately, the neonicotinoid clothianidin and the sulfoximine sulfoxaflor, with exposure lengths scaled by adult lifespan. We analyze transcriptomic responses across 120 samples, including individual brain samples for solitary species and pooled brain samples for the social bumble bee, by identifying differentially expressed genes, screening for co-expressed gene modules, mapping orthologous genes, and comparing enriched biological pathways. We show that while insecticides act on conserved molecular pathways within species, the downstream molecular responses are highly species-specific. Our results challenge the validity of single-species surrogacy in insecticide risk assessments and the practice of classification of insecticides based on their chemical structure rather than functional qualities.

Results

Sulfoxaflor and clothianidin cause contrasting intensity of effects in different species

The two insecticides affected transcriptomes in contrasting ways across species: clothianidin impacted thousands of genes in bees, whereas sulfoxaflor caused larger-scale responses in butterflies and flies. Clothianidin triggered differential expression in 2,248 genes (Wald test’s false discovery rate [FDR] <0.05) in the bumble bee and 759 genes in the solitary bee, while sulfoxaflor had almost no effect. By contrast, sulfoxaflor induced larger transcriptomic responses than clothianidin in both non-bee species, disrupting 12 times more genes in butterflies and eight times more genes in flies (Figure 1; Tables S1 and S2). Different insecticides affect overlapping gene sets within species

We observed overlaps among differentially expressed genes in response to clothianidin and sulfoxaflor within species. Clothianidin and sulfoxaflor induced largely shared sets of differentially expressed genes: 85% of those in the fly and 81% in the butterfly (hypergeometric test p values <10−11 for both). The direction and amplitude of expression changes in these genes were correlated between treatments in flies and butterflies (Figures 2A and 2B), suggesting that both insecticides perturb co-regulated or interacting genes in these species. To further investigate this pattern, we screened for gene modules with coordinated expression shifts under both treatments. We hypothesizsed that genes showing overlapping responses to both insecticides would belong to the same co-expressed gene modules. We did not detect any modules significantly associated with exposure to sulfoxaflor (the weaker treatment) in the bee species, further showing that sulfoxaflor does not cause major disruptions to the brain transcriptome in bees. In flies and butterflies, two gene modules showed correlated responses to both insecticides (Tables S3 and S4). We checked if genes in these modules overlapped with previously detected differentially expressed genes. Notably, 68% of genes in flies and 33% in butterflies significantly differentially expressed only under sulfoxaflor were part of these modules, exhibiting correlated shifts under both insecticides. Under clothianidin, these genes showed weaker expression changes that did not reach statistical significance (Figures 2C and 2D). Because sulfoxaflor induced minimal transcriptomic changes in bees, we could not robustly compare its effects with those of clothianidin. To test whether different insecticides cause overlapping responses in red mason bees, we exposed this species to acetamiprid, another neonicotinoid insecticide. We detected 27 differentially expressed genes, 71.5% of which overlapped with those affected by clothianidin exposure, exhibiting expression changes with correlated direction and amplitude across both treatments (hypergeometric test p value <10−22, Figure S1). We further compared the genes differentially expressed in bumble bees exposed to clothianidin with those in a study of imidacloprid exposure in the same species44 to expand the number of compounds tested. We detected a 36.5% overlap in differentially expressed genes with those affected by clothianidin (hypergeometric test, p value <10−12). This overlap persisted despite variations in exposure duration and concentration that determine the molecular effects of exposure more than the compound type itself45 (Figure S1).

The effects of insecticides differ between species

In each species, 23%–29% of genes affected by the more impactful treatment (clothianidin for bees, sulfoxaflor for butterflies and flies) were among the conserved one-to-one orthologs shared across all four species (2,956 genes), suggesting that exposure affected some conserved pathways. However, pairwise comparisons between species revealed minimal, non-significant overlap among these genes (Table S5). We found only one statistically significant overlap between the two bee species (hypergeometric test, p value <10−5; Figures 3A and 3B), but most of the genes exhibited opposing directions of expression change (Figures 3C and 3D). Only one gene, tafazzin, a putative acyl transferase associated with altered metabolism of the mitochondrial phospholipid cardiolipin,46 was affected in all four species. However, it was downregulated in flies and butterflies under sulfoxaflor exposure but upregulated in both bee species under clothianidin exposure, further showing the differences in responses between species (Figures 3C and 3D). In all species, the proportion of enriched Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways unique to a single species ranged from 25% in the red mason bee to 62% in the painted lady butterfly. We detected no KEGG pathways enriched across all four species (Figure 3E). For example, oxidative phosphorylation was disrupted in bumble bees and flies but remained entirely unchanged in butterflies. Likewise, immunity pathways were affected in both bees and flies, but the nature of these changes differed: bumble bees showed downregulation of antimicrobial peptides, whereas flies showed upregulation (Figure 3F). To strengthen the validity of our findings, we extended our analysis to include transcriptomic profiles from hind-femur and Malpighian tubule samples from unexposed bumble bees originating from the same experimental setup. The goal was to demonstrate that the observed differences in insecticide responses across species reflect genuine biological signals rather than experimental or technical artifacts. To this end, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) on control brain samples from all four species together with the two additional bumble bee tissues. The first principal component (PC1) accounted for 38.3% of the variance, while the second principal component (PC2) accounted for 25.5%. As anticipated, brain samples from all species, including bumble bees, clustered together, indicating a conserved neural-specific transcriptomic profile across species regardless of phylogenetic distance. In contrast, bumble bee samples from other tissues (hind-femur and Malpighian tubules) formed distinct clusters, highlighting tissue-specific transcriptomic differences within a single species. This clustering pattern is the expected outcome if the data are of high quality and free of technical bias. Had brain samples from different species failed to cluster together, this would have suggested that the between-species differences we detect reflect technical artifacts rather than robust biological responses to insecticide exposure (Figure S2).

Transcriptomic changes reflect insecticide effects independently of mortality

Survival rates varied markedly across species and treatments, yet the extent of transcriptomic disruption did not fully correspond to mortality outcomes. Flies experienced the highest mortality under both insecticides, with only 23% surviving sulfoxaflor exposure and 26% surviving clothianidin exposure (p value <0.001 for both). In bees, clothianidin caused moderate mortality, with 71% survival in bumble bees (p value <0.001) and 78% in solitary bees (p value <0.01), while sulfoxaflor caused virtually no mortality, with 98% and 100% survival, respectively (p value >0.05). All butterfly individuals survived exposure to both treatments (Figure S3). Notably, although flies showed similar final survival rates under both insecticides, sulfoxaflor caused earlier mortality, with only 43% of flies surviving after four days compared to 94% under clothianidin, suggesting that the two compounds differ in the timing of their lethal effects even when overall mortality outcomes are comparable. This difference in mortality dynamics was reflected in a borderline violation of the proportional hazards assumption for the fly clothianidin model (Schoenfeld residuals test, p value = 0.048), consistent with the time-varying nature of clothianidin’s lethal effects in this species. No violations were detected in the remaining models (all p values >0.05).

Variation in statistical power across species influences differentially expressed gene detection

The power to detect differentially expressed genes depends on the agreement between the biological replicates within treatment groups and the similarities in genetic background of individuals between groups. To test whether controlling for genetic background improves detection power in bumble bees, we randomly sampled control and clothianidin-exposed individuals from either matched or unmatched source colonies across 100 iterations. Accounting for the source colony more than doubled the median number of differentially expressed genes (543 vs. 225; Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, D = 0.21, p value = 0.02). The distribution when the colony was accounted for was also less positively skewed (skewness = 1.99) compared to when colony was not accounted for (skewness = 0.53; Figure S4). This finding confirms that controlling for genetic background increases sensitivity. Thus, the fewer differentially expressed genes observed in butterflies and solitary bees may reflect limited statistical power due to inter-individual genetic variation, rather than lower insecticide toxicity.

We next measured the agreement between biological replicates within species. The rationale was to determine whether in species in which individuals had more dissimilar responses to the exposure, the power to detect differentially expressed genes was lower. Fly samples showed significantly greater inter-individual variability in transcriptomic profiles than the other three species (all p values <0.001). Both insecticides nonetheless induced numerous differentially expressed genes in flies, indicating that the transcriptomic impact of exposure was sufficiently strong to overcome this background variability. Given the previous observations, we do not use the number of differentially expressed genes to compare the insecticide toxicity levels between species; instead, we provide a quantitative measure of which insecticide affects each species more strongly.

Discussion

Our findings reveal substantial variation in how different pollinator species respond to insecticide exposure. While clothianidin caused relatively more transcriptomic changes in both bee species compared to sulfoxaflor, this pattern was reversed in non-bee species, where sulfoxaflor induced broader transcriptomic disruption. We also found no consistent responses at the gene or pathway level across species. Despite these differences, gene expression responses to different insecticides within each species were highly consistent, with both compounds affecting largely overlapping gene sets in the same direction and disrupting shared biological pathways, differing primarily in magnitude.

Brain tissue shares fundamental molecular characteristics across species47,48,49 (Figure S2), providing a conserved cellular context within which insecticides act. Activation or blockage of nAChRs by insecticides triggers Ca2+ influx, disrupting neuronal signaling. However, different insecticides bind to receptors with varying affinities due to differences in subunit specificity.50 Because of interspecific differences in nAChR composition, compounds may activate the nAChRs at different rates, thereby determining overall response dissimilarities across species and contrasting effects of the two compounds tested.51,52

The shared gene sets affected within a species across different compounds suggest that insecticides targeting the same receptor type elicit similar biological mechanisms and downstream effects, differing primarily in intensity despite their varying affinities to nAChR subunits within a species.50 We previously showed similar overlapping effects of acetamiprid and clothianidin in the bumble bee brain. Specifically, both insecticides triggered stress-response mechanisms at high concentration and short-duration exposure, while downregulating antimicrobial peptide genes at low concentration and chronic exposure.45 It is plausible that nAChR activation triggers a limited set of neuronal responses within a species but that the intensity with which these responses are expressed varies between compounds and is driven by their affinity to specific subunits; this remains an important open question that warrants direct empirical investigation.

Beyond receptor-level differences, variation in detoxification capacity represents another key determinant of divergent insecticide responses across species. Similar detoxification mechanisms are employed to initially break down the toxic compound across species. However, species-specific expansions and gene loss of detoxification gene families, driven by differences in occupied habitats and subsequent evolutionary pressures, likely determine the inherited capacity to cope with the exposure.42,53,54,55,56 The size of the P450 family can vary considerably across arthropod species, ranging from as few as 21 to over 200.54 However, the mere number of P450 genes is not the sole determinant of “insects” detoxification capabilities.57 Instead, the effectiveness of detoxification hinges critically on the presence and activity levels of specific functionally relevant P450 genes.55,56,58 We detected altered expression of Clan 3 cytochrome P450 genes, known to detoxify foreign compounds, across all four species: 11 in flies under sulfoxaflor exposure, two in butterflies under sulfoxaflor exposure, two in bumble bees under clothianidin exposure, and one in solitary bees under clothianidin exposure (Table S6). However, studies of bees have found that constitutive rather than induced expression of P450 determines their role in detoxifying insecticides.59,60 Therefore, non-differentially expressed CYPs in our transcriptomic snapshots could still play an essential role in metabolizing insecticides. Interestingly, for recognized subfamilies in Hymenoptera (CYP336)61 and bees (CYP9Q)59 that detoxify foreign plant alkaloids and neonicotinoids, respectively, we did not observe up-regulation of these genes in the brain. Rather, one putative CYP336 gene (LOC114876409) was downregulated in the solitary bee. Consistent with observations in Malpighian tubules where detoxification enzyme activity was similarly reduced upon insecticide exposure,42,60 this suggests that prolonged exposure may compromise rather than enhance detoxification capacity.

Changes in gene expression may reflect protective62,63,64 and toxicity-driven45,65,66 responses to environmental stressors. Across species, we observe exposure-driven effects ranging from negligible to severe transcriptomic disruption. At the lower end of this gradient, the brains of bumble bees and solitary bees tolerated sulfoxaflor exposure without exhibiting many transcriptomic changes. Other tissues in bumble bees showed the same pattern.67 When insecticide exposure does not reach a sufficient threshold of molecular disturbance, it appears to neither activate protective mechanisms nor dysregulate core pathways. In bumble bees exposed to clothianidin, activation of nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism pathways suggests some degree of active cellular response to the compound. However, we also detected dysregulation of energy metabolism pathways (Figure 3E) coupled with 29% of individuals not surviving the exposure in this species (Figure S3), indicating a disruption of metabolic activity beyond simply coping with the toxic compound. Butterflies, similarly to bees exposed to sulfoxaflor, survived the exposure yet exhibited substantial transcriptomic changes. In this species, altered pathways involved central carbon, amino acid, and nucleic acid metabolism and biosynthesis, as well as signaling pathways (Figure 3E). These metabolic shifts likely signal a toxicity-driven stress response in which homeostatic maintenance pathways still actively cope with chemical pressure. Flies exhibited the most severe responses, with downregulated ribosome biogenesis, DNA replication, and nucleotide metabolism, alongside enrichment of mitophagy and apoptosis (Figure 3E), consistent with a classical toxicological damage response reflected in high mortality rates under both treatments. Notably, transcriptomic responses distinguished between the two insecticides in flies more precisely than survival rates alone, with sulfoxaflor inducing earlier mortality and broader gene regulatory disruption despite similar final survival rates to clothianidin (Figures 1 and S3). Together, these cross-species profiles suggest that field-level exposure triggers transcriptional responses driven primarily by toxicity rather than protective mechanisms, ranging from no detectable change through active metabolic stress to systemic cellular damage, depending on each species intrinsic sensitivity to the compounds tested. These molecular changes likely underlie phenotypic and behavioral alterations.14,68,69 The extent of transcriptomic disruption, reflected in the number and intensity of differentially expressed genes and the nature of affected pathways, therefore provides a measure of relative insecticide toxicity. The fact that these signatures are detectable even in species that survive exposure entirely demonstrates that comparative transcriptomics captures a dimension of exposure impact that mortality-based assessments cannot.

The use of comparative analyses across taxonomically diverse pollinator species is especially relevant when evaluating novel insecticides developed as alternatives to restricted compounds. Sulfoxaflor is a relatively novel insecticide, developed and introduced after criticisms of neonicotinoid insecticides and their detected high toxicity to bees.25 Importantly, although it targets the same neural receptors, sulfoxaflor is not classified as a neonicotinoid insecticide. After its legislation, sulfoxaflor has been implicated in reproductive effects in bumble bees,18 leading to its restriction by the European Commission. However, subsequent studies have yielded inconsistent findings on its impacts on bees.70,71,72 Data on non-bee species remain scarce; in other insects, sulfoxaflor has been linked to reduced fertility and oviposition in Harmonia axyridis,73 mitochondrial dysfunction in Chironomus kiinensis,74 and toxicity comparable to imidacloprid in Locusta migratoria.75 Our data show that at the molecular level, sulfoxaflor causes more pronounced effects than a commonly used neonicotinoid, clothianidin, in at least some non-bee species. Sulfoxaflor also induces more immediate toxic effects in flies than clothianidin, leading to earlier mortality (Figure S3). This result suggests that the timing and mode of action of these insecticides may differ, even when overall mortality outcomes appear similar. Collectively, these findings highlight that the development of new pesticides must be taken with caution, and that assessments of insecticide safety should incorporate a broader range of species that reflect the phylogenetic diversity of pollinating insects before legislation.

Our results underscore a central point: insecticides cause molecular disruptions at concentrations that do not cause high mortality, and these disruptions differ markedly across species. Both dimensions of this finding would be invisible to traditional mortality-based assessments, which focus on a small number of model species and do not capture sublethal effects at field-realistic concentrations.28,76,77 Our findings highlight the inadequacy of current regulatory frameworks for capturing the true breadth of insecticide impacts across the diversity of pollinating insects. Addressing these challenges will ultimately require a transition toward integrated regulatory approaches that account for the taxonomic and ecological diversity of pollinating insects. Since insecticide applications, regardless of refinement, will always pose some challenges to biodiversity,78,79 the most effective long-term strategy is to reduce dependency on chemical controls in favor of integrated practices that work with nature, prioritizing biodiversity as a cornerstone of sustainable food production.

Limitations of the study

In this study, we focused on field-realistic sublethal exposure, reflecting the concentration pollinators are likely to encounter in agricultural landscapes. Rather than asking how lethal a compound is to a given species, we asked whether different insect species show conserved or divergent molecular responses when exposed to a sublethal, ecologically relevant concentration they are likely to encounter in their natural environment. Our data show that exposure to insecticides can cause significant transcriptomic changes across species, even when survival rates remain relatively high (Figure S3). However, the use of a single field-realistic concentration across all species and compounds, while reflecting ecologically valid exposure conditions, is a limitation of this study because individual insecticide intake was not measured. Therefore, species experiencing higher effective toxicity may show transcriptomic responses that partly reflect dose-specific responses rather than compound-specific molecular effects.45 Published LD50 values for clothianidin and sulfoxaflor are unavailable for L. sericata and V. cardui (Table S7).80,81,82,83,84 Toxicity-level matched exposures, calibrated to species-specific LD50 values, would be a valuable complementary approach to disentangle the downstream molecular programmes affected by exposure across insect species. However, the consistent within-species overlaps across compounds combined with the contrasting between-species responses to clothianidin and sulfoxaflor, suggest that the patterns we report reflect more than simple toxicity scaling.

Our study focuses only on four species and two insecticides. The consistent patterns of divergence in responses, both in survival rates and at the gene expression level, suggest that biological characteristics of each species play a stronger role in shaping responses to exposure than compound chemical structure alone. Expanding this approach to a wider selection of insecticides and species spanning multiple phylogenetic distances and ecological contexts would allow a more comprehensive characterization of the factors that determine insecticide sensitivity across pollinating insects and help establish whether this variability correlates with phylogeny or ecological roles within ecosystems. The rapid emergence of reference genomes for non-model species makes this an increasingly tractable goal.85,86

Experimental model and study participant details

All exposure experiments took place in the same exposure room. All insect species were subject to 8 h of darkness and 16 h of light. Insects were handled under red light to minimise flight activity during feeding. All insects were kept at 24 °C (± 1 °C). All feeding happened between 11:00 am and 1:00 pm to minimise the effects of circadian rhythms on gene activity. Treatment groups were assigned using a sequential rotational allocation; individuals were assigned to treatments in a rotating order (Control, Clothianidin, Sulfoxaflor, Control...) as they emerged daily. This approach was chosen to ensure that emergence date, which could influence baseline gene expression, was evenly distributed across all treatment groups, and to maintain balanced group sizes throughout the experiment. All sampling took place between 1:00 pm and 2:00 pm. Upon the end of exposure, insects were placed in 2 ml screwcap cryo-tubes, rapidly submerged in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 °C for further processing. We dissected brains from all individuals. For solitary bees, butterflies, and flies, each individual brain constituted a single replicate. For bumble bees, we pooled brains from three workers originating from the same microcolony to represent colony-level exposure, reflecting the social nature of this species and minimising the influence of inter-individual variation within a colony. We obtained 10 replicates for each species and treatment group combination.

Osmia bicornis

Female cocoons were obtained from a commercial breeder (Mauerbienen Shop, Germany). Individuals were visually examined upon emergence to confirm sex. Individual adult bees were kept in food-grade plastic boxes (height 10 cm, radius 6cm). Each bee was provided with ground honey bee collected pollen (General Food Merchants LTD) ad libitum as a source of protein and a single 5 ml syringe with either exposure or control feeding solution. Pollen was provided solely as a protein source and was not used as a vehicle for insecticide delivery. As a certified organic product, this pollen is subject to regulatory controls prohibiting the use of synthetic pesticides in its production. Although formal chemical analysis was not performed, any trace contamination would be negligible relative to the administered dose and would have been present equally across all treatment groups including controls. Each bee was provided with an unbleached paper ball to hide. Paper balls were prepared from unbleached paper cut into approximately 7x7 cm pieces and scrunched to approximately 1.5 - 2cm radius. Pollen and feeding solution were changed every other day. In addition to clothianidin, sulfoxaflor, and the control, we exposed this species to acetamiprid under the same conditions.

Lucilia sericata

Fly cocoons were obtained from a commercial breeder (Koppert, UK). Females were identified upon emergence from cocoons by the presence of a larger gap between their eyes in females, compared to the males. After sampling, sex was confirmed by checking for the presence of ovaries. Individual flies were kept in plastic boxes (height 10 cm, radius 6cm). Each adult fly was provided with pure sucrose ad libitum and a yellow cellulose sponge soaked with either exposure or control feeding solution. All sponges were cut to approximately 1x1cm squares prior to soaking; the sponges expanded upon absorption of the feeding solution. Sucrose and exposure solution were changed every other day.

Vanessa cardui

Butterfly chrysalides were obtained from a commercial breeder (Worldwide Butterflies, UK). Females were recognised in the chrysalis stage by the presence of a distinctive indented line running from the middle of the anal segment to the second segment. After sampling, sex was confirmed by checking for the presence of ovaries. Five adult butterflies assigned to the same treatment group were marked using water-based markers (POSCA, UK) and kept together in mesh cages (40 cm long, 40 cm wide, 30 cm deep). We assigned the control treatment or one of the two insecticide treatments to all cages. Since adult butterflies require artificial feeder training, individuals were hand-fed for 1 minute every day using artificial flowers made from 1.5 ml Eppendorf tubes and yellow or purple vinyl petals. All hand feeding was performed by a single researcher, eliminating inter-observer variability as a source of bias. Artificial flowers of both colours and feeding solution-soaked cellulose sponges on petri dishes were always present in the exposure cages and changed every other day. We observed butterflies in all treatments and cages to feed independently after the adjustment phase.

Bombus terrestris

We acquired ten source colonies of Bombus terrestris audax from a commercial breeder (Agralan Growers UK). We transferred the queen, existing brood and 20 workers to wooden boxes (30cm long, 20cm wide, 15cm deep) separated into two equal-sized chambers (foraging and nesting area). We provided each colony with ad libitum 30% sucrose solution and organic honey bee-collected pollen (General Food Merchants LTD). We marked all twenty workers using water-based markers (POSCA, UK) and screened the colonies daily for the emergence of new workers. All source colonies were two weeks old when we started setting up the microcolonies. Six callow workers that emerged within 24 h were used to assemble microcolonies. We obtained three microcolonies from each source colony. Each microcolony was kept in a single-chamber wooden box (12cm long, 12cm wide, 10cm deep) and provided with organic pollen ad libitum and a single dose of nest substrate (5 parts organic pollen to 1 part 30% sucrose solution in a 2.5 cm Petri dish). We assigned the control treatment or one of the two insecticide treatments to all microcolonies. Each microcolony received two 5 mL syringes with either exposure or control feeding solution. We changed pollen and sugar solution every other day.

Method details

Experimental design

Adult females of bumble bees, solitary bees and painted lady butterflies were treated for 14 days. For the first two days of adjustment period, all individuals were fed control treatments. Subsequently, for the remaining 12 days, individuals were fed 4.4 ppb insecticide solution or control treatment. Flies, because of shorter adult lifespan and considerably higher mortality upon exposure (> 95% mortality after exposure over 12 days to both sulfoxaflor and clothianidin) were subject to one day of adjustment period followed by 8 days of insecticide exposure to collect a sufficiently large sample size to measure sub-lethal effects of exposure on the transcriptome. Exposure solutions were provided ad libitum and changed every other day.

Insecticide treatment preparation

All insects were exposed to two insecticides that target nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs): clothianidin or sulfoxaflor (Sigma Aldrich, UK). In addition, red mason bees were exposed to acetamiprid (Sigma Aldrich, UK). The use of clothianidin is restricted in the European Union,96 but it remains widely used worldwide.97 Sulfoxaflor’s use has been increasing18,25,98 despite recent restrictions within the EU and reports of its high toxicity to bees when applied during flowering.99 We used the same concentrations for each compound to allow for direct comparisons. We established the concentration at 4.4 ppb, which is at the lower end of the concentrations found in pollen and nectar17,18,100,101,102 because we aimed to test whether exposure to small concentrations can be detected in the transcriptomic profiles.

We prepared insecticide stock solutions by adding each compound in powder form to a fixed volume of acetone to a final concentration of 0.5 mg/mL, and storing them in darkness at -20 °C. Subsequently, we used the stock solution to prepare treatments for each species. Both compounds were dissolved in equal volumes of acetone, and since the same volume of stock solution was added to feeding solutions, the final acetone concentration was identical across all treatments. The same volume of acetone was added to control feeding solutions. We fed B. terrestris, O. bicornis, and V. cardui using 30% sucrose solutions as control treatments. For the exposure treatments, we diluted the stock solutions using 30% sucrose solution to 4.4 ppb feeding solutions. Since individuals of L. sericata were not attracted to the feeding solution containing only sugar, and because young females require a protein-rich diet, we prepared amino-acid-rich solutions to feed this species. We prepared 30% solution using sucrose, 10 essential amino acids, 11 non-essential amino acids, and cholesterol (Table S8), and added the stock solutions to obtain the 4.4 ppb feeding solutions. To prevent insecticide degradation, we stored feeding solutions in the dark at 4 °C.

The feeding solutions were made available to the insects ad libitum throughout the experiment. Due to biological differences such as body size, metabolic rates, feeding behaviour, and nutrient requirements, each species consumed varying amounts of the feeding solutions. This approach of allowing insects to feed ad libitum, rather than providing a standardised amount per individual across species, reflects how species naturally consume resources in their environments. By mirroring their natural feeding behaviours, this method provides more realistic and ecologically valid results, rather than imposing artificial constraints on how much we think insects should consume or be exposed to. Notably, no preference for any treatment was observed among individuals across exposure groups.

Brain tissue dissection, sample processing and sequencing

Bumble bee brains were dissected on dry ice due to their larger size. In the absence of a queen, one bumble bee worker can become dominant, which induces the development of bigger ovaries. We examined abdomens to assess ovarian development and excluded dominant workers from subsequent steps, as significant ovarian development may impact gene expression patterns. We randomly selected three non-dominant workers per microcolony and pooled dissected brains for further steps to represent microcolony-level exposure in this social species. We did not pool all six workers per microcolony due to high mortality rates under exposure to clothianidin. All other insects were dissected in RNAlater solution. Dissected brains were placed in homogenisation tubes with ceramic beads and 400 μl of TRIzol. Samples were placed in -80 °C until homogenisation and RNA extraction. We homogenised the dissected brains in TRIzol using FastPrep96 (45 seconds at 1800 RPM for bumble bees; 35 seconds at 1800 RPM for solitary bees and butterflies; 20 seconds at 1800 RPM for flies due to variation in tissue size and structure). We isolated RNA using chloroform and purified it with Genaxxon RNA Mini Spin Kit, applying DNase I on-column digestion. We prepared cDNA libraries using the NEBNext® Ultra™ II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina. We altered the standard protocol using one-third volumes of enzymes and buffers and 20-200 ng of total RNA input, depending on the species, with 14 PCR cycles. The library size was quantified using TapeStation 2200 (Agilent, UK) and Qubit 2.0 fluorometer. Libraries were sequenced on NextSeq 500. We obtained the following ranges of 40 bp paired-end reads per sample per species: Bumble bees mean of 36 million (from 22.8 million to 52.2 million reads), solitary bees mean of 22.6 million (from 15.5 million to 30.8 million reads), butterflies mean of 24.3 million (from 17.9 million to 32.8 million reads), flies mean of 32.9 million (from 24.8 million to 39.3 million reads, Table S12).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Differential gene expression analysis

We assessed the quality of raw reads using FastQC v0.11.9.87 To evaluate alignment qualities, we aligned RNA-seq samples to the reference genomes using STAR v2.7.88 We used the following reference genomes: Bter_1.0 (PRJNA45869), iOsmBic2.1 (PRJEB44455), ilVanCard2.1 (PRJEB42869), and ASM1558622v1 (PRJNA602506). We processed STAR alignments using the RNA-seq module of Qualimap v2.2.1.89 We summarised the output BAM files from STAR using the featureCounts function in the Rsubread v1.22.2 package in R.90 Each species and exposure group were analysed separately against its corresponding control to enhance statistical power by reducing the complexity of the statistical design relative to the number of replicates per group. This approach also minimises the risk of batch effects and avoids confounding biological differences that could arise from analyzing all species together in a single model. We filtered out genes with relatively low expression levels for each dataset, using a cut-off of at least 10 counts in half of the replicates per treatment group. To detect differentially expressed genes between exposure treatments and the control, we applied a Wald test on median-of-ratios normalised counts in DESeq2 v1.32.0,91 with betaPrior = FALSE. We included treatment as a factor in all the model designs. For the bumble bees, we also included the source colony as a factor. Initially, we included cage as a factor in the butterfly model; however, no significant effect of cage was detected, and the factor was subsequently removed to maximise statistical power. We report genes as differentially expressed using a significance cut-off of 0.05 after false discovery rate adjustment of the Wald test P-values (FDR).

Correlation of gene expression changes between treatments

We built regression models to evaluate if the directions and intensities of expression changes in the affected genes are comparable between the treatments: 1. Genes recognised as differentially expressed in both treatments; 2. Genes recognised as differentially expressed only under the stronger treatment; 3. A random set of genes not recognised as differentially expressed under any of the treatments. We used the same number of genes in the random set of genes as the number of the overlapping differentially expressed genes between treatments. The rationale was to check whether the direction of the gene expression change and the intensity were similar between the two treatments. We used log2-fold change values estimated by DESeq2 for all the genes used in the analysis. We applied Pearson correlations and a generalised linear model with a Gaussian distribution and identity link function to assess the correlation strength, slope, and statistical significance of the correlations (Table S9).

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

After obtaining strong correlation values for the overlapping gene sets in both species, we wanted to test if these genes may belong to the same functional gene groups. While the correlation analysis demonstrated that genes differentially expressed under the stronger treatment (sulfoxaflor in flies and butterflies) showed correlated fold-change directions under the weaker treatment (clothianidin), many of these genes did not reach statistical significance under clothianidin exposure. The weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) allowed us to test whether these genes nonetheless belong to co-regulated gene networks that respond coherently to both treatments. We constructed weighted gene co-expression networks separately for all species. We wanted to detect modules that experience expression shifts under both treatments within each species. Since we did not detect any modules significantly associated with sulfoxaflor exposure in bee species, we proceeded only with flies and butterflies.

To build the gene co-expression network, we first applied VST as implemented in the DESeq2 package to pre-filtered DESeq2 objects containing all active genes for each species. We used the WGCNA v.1-72 package.92 We applied a soft-thresholding power of 10 for butterflies and 11 for flies to the correlation matrix of a signed co-expression network, based on the scale-free topology fit index of 0.8 and the minimal corresponding mean connectivity of the network value. The mergeCutHeight parameter used in module identification to control the merging of closely related gene clusters was set to 0.25. The minimum module size was the WGCNA default of 20 genes, and a random seed of 1234 was set for reproducibility. After constructing the network on all expressed genes in both species, we tested for correlation with exposure treatment: exposed vs. control for each treatment separately using eigengenes and applying Pearson correlation and Benjamini-Hochberg P-value adjustment for multiple testing. We consider modules to be significantly associated with the exposure treatment, applying a cut-off of adjusted P-value of 0.05.

Orthology search

Using predicted genes from the reference genomes, we assessed the completeness of the longest isoform protein sequences for each of the four species with BUSCO, using the Endopterygota lineage dataset (odb10). The motivation was to demonstrate that, despite deep divergence times among species, genome completeness at the Endopterygota lineage level was sufficient to identify one-to-one orthologs (Table S10). Using OrthoFinder v.2.5.5 and Diamond v.2.1.8 we searched for one-to-one orthologs between all species tested and between pairs of species (Table S11).

KEGG rank-based enrichment analysis

We tested KEGG pathway enrichment using information from KEGG database and the clusterProfiler library in R.93 We performed this analysis separately for each species and treatment. We excluded both bumble bees and solitary bees exposed to sulfoxaflor because the treatment caused minimal differential expression in both species, with FDR values approaching 1 for most genes, indicating no statistically detectable differential expression.

We sorted all genes by FDR values and performed a rank-based test applying a custom function based on the enrichKEGG function from clusterProfiler. We applied a P-value cut-off of 0.05 to select pathways with multiple genes affected after exposure and compared the detected pathways across species.

Comparison to other bumble bee tissues

We extended our analysis to include eight hind leg and eight Malpighian tubule samples from bumble bees originating from the same experimental setup (PRJNA1076820). These samples were processed identically to the brain samples, ensuring consistency. Only unexposed samples were included in the analysis. Surrogate Variable Analysis (SVA) was applied to the VST-normalised data to identify hidden batch effects. The detected batch effect was subsequently removed using the removeBatchEffect function in the limma v.35.4.2 package.95 Principal component analysis (PCA) was then performed on the entire dataset.

Survival analysis

We collected data on the survival of individual insects throughout the experiments. We fitted Cox proportional hazards regression models to conduct pairwise comparisons of survival between each treatment and the control, utilising the coxph and survfit functions from the survival v.3.5-7 R library. We performed the test individually for each treatment-species pair. The proportional hazards assumption was tested for all models in which mortality occurred using Schoenfeld residuals with the cox.zph function.

Variation in testing power between insect species

Because we controlled for the genetic background of the bumble bees by using a microcolony-based experimental design, in which replicates across all treatments came from the same ten source colonies and were therefore genetically related, we wanted to test the impact of this adjustment on the detection power of the DESeq2 pipeline. We randomly selected control samples from five source colonies and clothianidin samples from the remaining five source colonies. We iterated this process over 100 different selection combinations and built a distribution of the number of differentially expressed genes detected. Next, we repeated this process, but we randomly selected control and clothianidin samples from the same five source colonies. We compared the two distributions and tested whether obtaining a higher number of differentially expressed genes is more likely when controlling for the source-colony effect. We applied the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test to compare the distributions.

To quantify whether high variability in responses to exposure among individuals within a species affects the power to detect differentially expressed genes, we built a generalised linear model using control samples from all species. We used the standard deviation of genes as the response variable and species as the explanatory variable. To address issues related to heteroscedasticity and non-normality, we applied a variance stabilising transformation (VST) before calculating the standard deviation of gene expression. The use of VST helped mitigate the influence of extreme values observed when the standard deviation was calculated from un-normalised counts, which resulted in extremely positively skewed residual distributions and hindered our ability to fit a generalised linear model. The standard deviation of gene expression values after VST is more consistent across genes, as VST reduces the dependence of variance on mean expression. Therefore, if we still see differences in standard deviation between species calculated using VST-normalized counts, the differences in standard deviation between genes in non-normalised counts should be greater. To ensure the best model fit for continuous data with a positive skew, we used the gamma distribution.
