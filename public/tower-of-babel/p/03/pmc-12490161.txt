Salt-sensitive hypertension promotes neuronal mitochondrial stress and neurodegenerative alterations via neuro-vascular metabolic reprogramming and local RAS signaling
Authors: Hanxue Wu, Zihan Qiu, Junya Mu, Yi Wang, Jiawei Wang, Yue Han, Ruijie Yang, Shenglan Yuan, Miao Yuan, Rui Yang, Xingjuan Chen, Qi Sun, Fanni Li, Lei Xiao, Ming Zhang, Jiaxi Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hypertension increases risks for cognitive impairment and Alzheimer’s disease (AD). In renal patients with both hypertension and cognitive decline, via rest-state fMRI, their cerebral cortical region showed maintained cerebral blood flow (CBF), but reduced signals of blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD). In mice, although CBF was unchanged, deoxycorticosterone acetate (DOCA)-salt treatment markedly reduced cerebrovascular reactivity, with altered transcriptomic pattern in cortical endothelial cells (ECs) and astrocytes, showing downregulated expression of glucose transport 1 (GluT1) but upregulated metabolic reprogramming. Lipidomic analysis using prefrontal cortex (PFC) further revealed enhanced catabolism of glycerophospholipids and accumulation of free fatty acids. In the PFC of hypertensive mice, neurodegenerative alterations were observed, including reduced number of neuronal dendritic spines and more expression of phosphorylated Tau (p-Tau). Via both morphological and molecular tests, we identified that DOCA-salt hypertension was associated with significant mitochondrial injury and upregulated lysine succinylation in the PFC neurons. Upregulated lysine succinylation was largely mitochondria-located, and they were functionally enriched in gluconeogenesis-related energy metabolic pathways, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, oxidative stress, and neurodegenerative diseases. In hypertensive mice, angiotensinogen (Agt) expression was markedly upregulated in most astrocytes, together with neuronal expression of Agtr1a. In cultured neuronal cells, angiotensin II (ang II) elevated mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP biosynthesis. In mice with neuronal AT1aR knockout (AT1N), DOCA-salt failed to induce cognitive impairment. Additionally, DOCA-salt-associated reduction of acetylcholine, accumulation of p-Tau, and upregulation of lysine succinylation were not observed in AT1N mice. Direct anti-hypertensive treatment did not abolish DOCA-salt-related pathological phenotypes, and enhanced lysine succinylation was not detected in hypertension models induced by norepinephrine or L-NAME. Our data provide evidence that hypertension induced metabolic rearrangement (enhanced energy metabolism from non-glucose source and upregulated mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation) in the neuro-vascular unit, due to downregulated glucose uptake in ECs. Increased neuronal energy consumption, via local ang II/AT1R signaling, further exacerbated mitochondrial stress and neurodegenerative alterations. Together, by multi-omics analysis, this study provided novel insights regarding how hypertension increases the risk for age-related cognitive impairment. Introduction

Hypertension represents a major risk factor for age-related cognitive impairment and pathogenesis of Alzheimer’s disease (AD), with emerging evidence demonstrating its dual role in driving both cerebrovascular pathology and neurodegenerative processes [1–3]. Chronic hypertension accelerates cerebrovascular injury, manifesting as microinfarcts, hemorrhages, white matter hyperintensities, and amyloid-β deposition, while simultaneously inducing neurovascular unit dysfunction [1, 4]. Although how hypertension effect vascular structures and functions have been well-characterized, outside of the vasculature what happen to the brain cells is still unclear.

The brain is a high energy-demanding organ with constant high consumption of glucose and oxygen. Hypertension-induced by angiotensin (ang) II infusion or deoxycorticosterone acetate (DOCA)-salt treatment has been associated with dysfunction of neuro-vascular coupling (NVC), leading to reduced functional hyperemia in the arterioles [5, 6]. This suggests that brain cells with high energy-demand could be vulnerable to hypertension-induced vascular dysfunction. Within a neuro-vascular unit, glucose is actively taken up by endothelial cells (ECs) and transported into astrocytes via cell-specific glucose transporters [7]. By astrocytes, glucose is metabolized into lactate, which is further exported to neurons to serve as the primary fuel supporting aerobic respiration [8]. In neurons, glucose starvation or lactate deficiency has been shown to limit mitochondrial ATP production [9, 10], exacerbating energy crisis during hypoglycemia or ischemia. Disrupted energy metabolism in brain neurons (e.g. mitochondrial stress and gluconeogenesis) is related to the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases [11], like Parkinson’s disease (PD) and AD [12], whose risks have been linked to hypertension. Via NVC, increase of neuronal activity leads to elevation in local cerebral blood flow (CBF). In this process, glucose and oxygen in the blood are usually over-supplied, more than the neuronal energy demand. In mild hypertension, with no significant infarcts or bleeds in the cerebral vessels [5, 13], whether and how it would induce metabolic reprogramming in the neuro-vascular unit are unknown either.

For hypertension-induced vascular remodeling and dysfunction, locally generated ang II and its type 1 receptor (AT1R) have been demonstrated as essential players. In postmortem patients of AD, accumulation of ang II-related proteins, like angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE) and AT1R, was observed in the cortex [14]; and ACE2, the enzyme that degrades ang II, was found to be reduced in the postmortem AD brains [15], suggesting an increased activity of ang II and its canonical downstream signaling. In AD, brain metabolic changes are associated with detrimental protein deposits, which are then involved in the formation of plaques and tangles [16, 17]. Whether brain renin-angiotensin system (RAS) overactivation could disturb the normal pattern of energy metabolism in neurons could be an important link between hypertension and etiology of AD.

DOCA-salt treatment induces a low-renin type of hypertension with elevated ang II levels selectively in the brain while reduced in the peripheral. DOCA-salt hypertension can lead to impaired endothelial (nitric oxide-dependent) vasodilation and functional hyperemia, inward remodeling of parenchymal arterioles, and cognitive declines in rodent models [5, 13]. In this study, we evaluated the effects of DOCA-salt hypertension on the cerebral cortex of mice by utilizing multi-omics study and a series of validation tests, and showed that DOCA-salt hypertension induced energy shortage, mitochondrial stress and neurodegenerative alterations in the cerebral cortical astrocytes and neurons. On one hand, we identified that ang II increased neuronal energy metabolism, and expression of AT1aR in the cortical neurons was upregulated by DOCA-salt treatment. On the other, targeting neuron-expressing AT1aR significantly blunted DOCA-salt-associated metabolic stress and neurodegenerative alterations, and abolished cognitive impairment in mice with DOCA-salt treatment. Notably, significant metabolic stress was not observed in hypertension models induced by L-NAME or NE, but maintained in DOCA-salt models with co-treatment of direct anti-hypertensive drug, suggesting its dependence on the brain RAS.

Methods and materials

Ethical approval

Human study

All research procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of the Medical College at Xi’an Jiaotong University and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This prospective study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03191409, https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT03191409). All subjects provided written, informed consent after the experimental procedures had been fully explained.

From July 1, 2018, through June 3, 2021, patients with hypertension were enrolled in the First Affiliated Hospital of the Medical College at Xi’an Jiaotong University. Hypertension was defined by a measured average systolic BP ≥ 140 mmHg or average diastolic BP ≥ 90 mmHg. The enrolled patients have a combination of hypertension and kidney disease. The underlying cause of kidney disease was mesangiocapillary glomerulonephritis. All patients had a GFR < 15 ml/min/1.73 m2 and required renal replacement therapy (> 12 months). The exclusion criteria for all subjects were as follows: (a) macroscopic brain T2-visible lesions (regardless of size) on MRI scans, (b) existence of a neurological disease, (c) physical deformities, (d) alcohol, nicotine, or drug abuse, (e) diabetic nephropathy, or (f) claustrophobia. A group of age-, education-, and sex-matched, right-handed, healthy controls (HCs) were recruited from the local community. The inclusion criteria for HCs were age ≥ 18 years, declared not having relevant medical history of hypertension or a prior use of any antihypertensive. The exclusion criteria used for the patients were also used for the HCs.

Patients’ CSF samples

Excess of CSF collected for diagnostic purposes was obtained from patients undergoing neurosurgery or seeking neurological treatment at The First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University (IRB#XJTU1AF2023LSK-492). Informed consents were obtained prior to experiments and all procedure conform to the principles outlined in Declaration of Helsinki.

Animal study

All procedures were approved by the IACUC of Xi’an Jiaotong University (XJTU2022-67), in agreement with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Institutes of Health Publication No. 86 − 23, revised 1996).

Human fMRI study

Cognitive function assessment

The cognitive function assessment was conducted in a quiet room by two clinical psychologists with 10 years of experience in the field. The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) was used to detect the cognitive function among all subjects.

Magnetic resonance image acquisition

All subjects were scanned on a 3.0 Tesla GE Excite scanner using an eight-channel coil (GE Medical Systems, Milwaukee, WI). During the magnetic resonance image acquisition, all subjects were placed in a standard head coil to reduce head movement; at the same time the subjects were told to remain still with their eyes closed and to not think about anything.

The rs-fMRI acquisition parameters were as follows: 30 axial slices; repetition time (TR) = 2,000 ms; echo time (TE) = 30 ms; flip angle = 90°; slice thickness = 5 mm; no gap; matrix = 64 × 64; and field of view (FOV) = 240 × 240 mm2.

Whole-brain cerebral blood flow (CBF) measurements were obtained using the 3D pseudo continuous arterial spin labeling (ASL) technique. The acquisition parameters were as follows: TR = 5046 ms; TE = 11ms; slices = 50; post-label delay = 2025 ms; flip angle = 111°; slice thickness = 3 mm; matrix = 128 × 128; and FOV = 256 × 256 mm2.

Magnetic resonance image preprocessing and analysis

The rs-fMRI data preprocessing was conducted by using the Data Processing Assistant for Resting-State fMRI (DPARSF, http://www.restfmri.net/forum/DPARSF) toolbox. Preprocessing steps included: (a) removal of the first ten volumes; (c) slice timing correction; (d) realignment to correct for spatial displacements due to head motion; (e) spatial normalization; (f) removal of linear trend; (g) temporal band-pass filtering (0.01 ~ 0.08 Hz); and (h) regression of nuisance covariates including Friston 24 head movement parameters, cerebrospinal fluid signals and white matter signals from the fMRI data. Amplitude of low frequency fluctuation (ALFF) map, fractional ALFF (fALFF) map, degree centrality (DC) map, and regional homogeneity (ReHo) map were calculated, Z-score transformed and smoothed after preprocessing procedures.

ALFF is defined as the total power within the low-frequency range (between 0.01 and 0.1 Hz), while fALFF is defined as the relative contribution of specific low-frequency (0.01–0.1 Hz) fluctuation to the entire detectable frequency range. Both the ALFF and the fALFF of the BOLD signal measure the magnitude of the regional activity amplitude and reflect the intensity of spontaneous neural activity. ReHo measures Kendall’s coefficient concordance in neighboring voxels to reflect the coherence of the BOLD signal amplitude between a single voxel and its nearest neighbors. It has been suggested as a measure of localized connectivity, providing information about local alterations in brain function. DC, takes a given region’s relationship with the entire functional connectome into account. Examinations of DC have focused on the identification of “functional hubs” in whole-brain, which reflect the corresponding blood supply and metabolism of brain hubs. All the metrics have the potential to evaluate abnormalities of certain brain regions at the whole-brain level.

For the ASL data, corresponding CBF images were obtained by using an automated image postprocessing tool in the ADW work station. The processing procedure of CBF maps included normalization, Z-score transformation and smoothing.

Statistical analysis and correlation analysis

SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical analysis. Group differences in the subjects’ demographic information (age, years of education, BMI) were analyzed using independent two-sample t-tests. Sex differences were evaluated by using the χ2 test. Independent two-sample t-tests was used to investigate group differences of cognitive function (MoCA) between HCs and hypertension patients. Significant levels were set at P < 0.05.

Because the subjects in this study were combination of hypertension and kidney disease, to study the relationship between cognitive function score and BP, a stepwise regression model was used to calculate the odds ratio of the renal function (Urea, Creatinine), subjects’ demographic information, and BP for hypertension patients’ cognitive function score. The mean value of ALFF, fALFF, Reho, DC, and CBF were calculated in 246 independent regions, respectively. Then, the independent samples t-test was used to detect the significant difference between hypertension patients and HCs in ALFF, fALFF, Reho, DC, and CBF.

Animal models

Transgenic mice and animal husbandry

All the experiments were performed in adult (20–22 weeks, 25–30 g) male or female wildtype mice (WT), neuronal AT1aR knock-down mice (AT1N), and their control littermates (AT1fl/fl or fl/0, Cre0/0), all on a C57BL/6 background. WT mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co. The J20 AD model mice were purchased from the Institute of Laboratory Animal Science, CAMS&PUMC. The AT1N mice were obtained by breeding AT1aR floxed mice (T009458, GemPharmatech Co., China) with Syn1-cre recombinase animals (003966, The Jackson Laboratory, USA). AT1N mice appear fertile and devoid of gross anatomy defects. All animals were housed with a 12:12-hour light-dark cycle, at a constant temperature of 24 °C, and received standard mouse chow.

Mice were assigned to each treatment group via simple randomization. A power analysis using the PASS-15 software (version 15.0.3, USA) based on this estimate indicates 8 animals per group, for blood pressure and hemodynamic parameters, will be needed in order to attain a statistical power of 0.80 with an alpha error of 0.05. BP were recorded for priori inclusion and exclusion criteria of data.

DOCA-salt treatment

Before surgery, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (2%) in an oxygen flow (1 L/min) and placed on a heating pad (RWD, China) to maintain normal body temperature. Mice first underwent unilateral right nephrectomy via flank incision. After 1 week of recovery, they were implanted subcutaneously with a DOCA-silicone sheet containing 1 mg of DOCA/g body weight or sham. Animals with DOCA-pellets received 1% NaCl in their drinking water. After 28 days, all mice were euthanized, and tissues were harvested for experiments described below.

After 2-week of DOCA-salt treatment, a subset of mice received hydralazine via drinking solution (75 mg/L in 1% NaCl) for 2-week.

Systemic infusion of norepinephrine (NE)

WT mice were implanted subcutaneously with an osmotic minipump (Alzet, USA) containing norepinephrine (3.8 µg/kg/min) or vehicle (saline).

Treatment of N (ω)-nitro-L-arginine Methyl ester (L-NAME)

L-NAME (3 mg/ml/day, Aladdin Biochemical Technology, N109211, China) was provided to the WT mice by oral administration in their drinking water. All drinking solutions for mice were freshly prepared daily.

Tail-cuff blood pressure measurement

After 3 weeks of DOCA-salt treatment, systolic blood pressure was recorded via noninvasive tail-cuff technique (CODA system, Kent Scientific Corp, USA). The mice were pretrained by measuring blood pressure daily for 5 days before the experiments to minimize stress-induced fluctuations, followed by a test session consisting of 25 blood pressure measurements. All operations were performed by the same tester at the same time of day.

Behavioral studies

All behavioral studies were conducted by trained testers who did not obtain information regarding the treatments, experimental grouping, or genotypes of any tested mice.

Novel object recognition (NOR) test

The NOR test was conducted under dim light in a lidless plastic box (L×W×H: 50 × 50 × 40 cm). The recognized objects were plastic objects that varied in color and shape but were similar in size. The test consists of a habituation phase, acquisition phase, and testing phase. During the habituation phase the mice were acclimated to the testing room and chamber for 3 d. Then, in the acquisition phase the mice were allowed to explore two identical objects for 10 min in the same chamber. After an intersession interval of 24 h, a testing phase occurred. During this period, mice were placed in the same chamber and allowed to explored the familiar object and a novel object for 10 min. Exploration of an object was defined as the mouse sniffing or touching the object while looking at it, but sitting or climbing on it was not considered as an exploration. Between the trials the arena and the objects were cleaned with 70% ethanol to remove residual mouse odor. For data collection and analysis SMART V3.0 software (Panlab, Harvard Apparatus, USA) was used. A percent of time spent exploring the novel object relative to the total time spent exploring both objects was used as a measure of novel object recognition. Novel (%) = (Tnovel)/(Tnovel+Told) × 100%.

Barnes maze test

The Barnes maze apparatus (RWD, China) consisted of a circular open surface with 92 cm in diameter, located 77.5 cm above the floor. There were 20 circular holes (5 cm in diameter) equally spaced around the perimeter and one of the holes had a detachable opaque trapezoid box, which was the escape box. A light and buzzer were used as aversive stimuli. Extra-maze visual cues were all around the room as reference cues to learn the position of the escape hole. For data collection and analysis SMART V3.0 software (Panlab) was used. Before and between trials, mice were placed into cages in a dark room adjacent to the test room for at least 30 min. The acquisition phase consisted of three consecutive training days with three trials per day with the escape hole located at the same location across trials and days. Before starting each trial, a mouse was covered by an opaque box in the center of the maze. Once the box was raised, and the buzzer was turned on until the mouse entered the escape hole. In each trial mouse was given 3 min to locate the escape hole. If they failed to enter the escape hole, they were guided to the escape hole or placed directly into the escape box. After each trial, the mice remained in the escape box for 1 min and then returned to their cage. On the day-4 and − 5, the location of the escape hole was moved 180° from its previous location (reverse learning) and three trials per day were performed. Between trials the maze floor was cleaned with 70% ethanol to minimize olfactory cues.

Nesting test

The nesting behavior was conducted at night in the home cages of mice (L×W×H: 28 × 17 × 11.5 cm). Two sheets of two-ply paper towel were cut into 1 × 1 cm pieces (about 250 pieces), and then were laid flat on the bottom of the cage. After 14 h, the nesting behavior of each mouse was recorded. The behavior was scored using the following parameters: the bottom of the mouse cage is divided into 6 equal areas. 0: paper scattered in 6 areas; 1: paper scattered in 5 areas; 2: paper distributed in 4 areas; 3: paper distributed in 3 areas; 4: paper concentrated in 2 areas; 5: paper concentrated in 1 area.

Measurement of CBF via laser speckle imaging system

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (3% induction and 2% maintenance) in an oxygen flow (0.5 L/min), immobilized and placed on a stereotaxic frame (Stoeling, USA). For CBF measurements, the scalp was pulled aside. The exposed pial surface vessels were kept moist with saline prewarmed to 37 °C. Blood perfusion continuously was measured by imaging the pial arteries in the regions of interest with Laser Speckle Imaging System (RWD, China). After blood perfusion stabilized, isoflurane was discontinued, and the baseline was recorded for 30 s. Then the whisker-barrel cortex was activated for 30 s by stimulating the whisker pad, and the evoked changes in CBF were recorded. Changes in CBF were used to assess the functional hyperemia.

MRI cerebral perfusion image acquisition of mice

The MRI cerebral perfusion quantification was performed on a Bruker 7.0T scanner (Bruker, PharmaScan 70/16, Germany), using a volume coil with an inner diameter of over 23 mm for transmitting and a 4-channel phase-array coil for receiving. After a localizer scan for mouse positioning, a global shimming was performed based on a pre-scanned B0 field map, and the shimming results were confirmed by checking the field map again. Two pseudo-continuous arterial spin labeling (pCASL) sequences were scanned to cover the whole brain with the labeling pulse perpendicular to the vertebral and carotid arteries. The first pCASL sequence was scanned with multiple phases to calibrate the label and control phase. Anatomical images were acquired using a T2-weighted TurboRARE sequence with TE 35 ms, TR 2500 ms, FOV 15 × 15 mm2, matrix size 256 × 256, slice thickness 1 mm, 15 slices, and 2 averages. Then, the second pCASL sequence with the optimal phase was performed to measure the CBF signal. The parameters in the pCASL sequence were TE 12.32 ms, TR 3000ms, matrix size 96 × 96, in-plane resolution 0.156 × 0.156 mm, slice thickness 1.0 mm, 15 slices, label duration 2038 ms, post labeling delay 200 ms, and 40 pairs of label/control volumes.

Mitochondrial isolation

Mitochondria of cortex were isolated using Tissue Mitochondria Isolation Kit (Beyotime, C3606, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, freshly isolated cortical tissues were mixed and homogenized with the mitochondrial extraction reagent. Following differential centrifugation, the mitochondria and cytoplasm were isolated. The protein concentration of mitochondria and cytoplasm was quantified using a BCA assay kit (Thermo Fisher, 23227, USA).

Western blotting

Cortex (∼20–30 mg) and hippocampus (∼20 mg) were homogenized and lysed in Urea lysis buffer, respectively. The protein concentration was determined by a BCA assay kit (Thermo Fisher, 23227, USA). Equal mass proteins were mixed with LDS sample buffer (Thermo Fisher, NP0007, USA), boiled, and loaded into 4–12% NuPAGE™ Bis-Tris gels (Thermo Fisher, NP0335BOX, USA) or 4–20% FuturePAGE™ gels (ACE, ET12420Gel, China). Proteins were transferred to PVDF or nitrocellulose membranes. After blocking with 5% milk in TBS/0.1% Tween-20 (TBST), membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti-NDUFS1 antibody (Cell Signaling, 70264T, 1:1000, USA), rabbit anti-cytochrome c antibody (Cell Signaling, 11940T, 1:1000, USA), rabbit anti-COX IV antibody (Cell Signaling, 4850T, 1:1000, USA), monoclonal mouse anti-phospho-tau antibody (Ser202, Thr205) (Thermo Scientific, MN1020, 1:800, USA), mouse anti-tau46 antibody (Cell Signaling, 4019s, 1:800, USA), mouse pan anti-succinyl-lysine antibody (PTM Biolab, PTM-419, 1:1000, China), and rabbit anti-GADPH antibody (Cell Signaling, 2118S, 1:1500, USA). Membranes were washed in TBST and incubated with species-specific horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (antibody to mouse, 31430; antibody to rabbit, 31460; Thermo Scientific, 1:5000, USA) for 2 h at room temperature. After washing, proteins were visualized to enhanced chemiluminescence detection and the signals were developed using Vilber Fusion SoloS Imaging System (Vilber, France).

Co-immunoprecipitation assay

The tissue homogenates of the mouse cortex and human CSF were processed for pre-enrichment of succinyl-proteins. Pan anti-succinyl-Lysine antibody (PTM Biolab, PTM-401, China) and IgG (Proteintech, B900610, China) were incubated with magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher, 88802, USA) overnight at 4℃, as indicated. Then the beads were incubated with protein lysates at 4 ℃ overnight. After magnetic separation, the immune complexes were washed and eluted for western blot analysis.

Fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH)

Mice were euthanized, and their brains were rapidly dissected and snap-frozen in cold isobutane on dry ice. Brains were cryostat-sectioned to 10 μm and directly mounted onto chilled microscope slides. Sections were then processed to detect mouse Agtr1a mRNA, ChAT mRNA using an RNAscope® fluorescent multiplex reagent kit (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, 320851, USA). Images were captured using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica, TCS SP8 STED 3X, Germany).

Immunofluorescence

Mice were euthanized and transcardially perfused with saline, followed by perfusion with 4% PFA. The brain was then removed, fixed overnight in 4% PFA and submerged in 30% sucrose at 4 °C before sectioning. Brains embedded and sectioned (20 μm) using a Leica CM1950 cryostat. Then, brain sections were incubated in permeabilization buffer (0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 10 min, blocked at room temperature for 2 h with 10% normal goat serum in PBS, and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Dilutions of primary antibodies are as follow: rabbit anti-GFAP antibody (Cell Signaling, 80788, 1:200, USA), rabbit pan anti-succinyl-Lysine antibody (PTM Biolab, PTM-401, 1:100, China), mouse anti-AT8 antibody (Thermo Scientific, MN1020, 1:500, USA), mouse anti-tau46 antibody (Cell Signaling, 4019s, 1:500, USA), rabbit anti-GLUT1 antibody (Proteintech, 21829-1-AP, 1:200, China), rabbit anti-CD31 antibody (Cell Signaling, 77699, 1:200, USA). After washing with PBS, sections were incubated with different fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher, A32731, A11001, 1:500, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. After staining with 4ʹ,6-Diamidino-2-Phenylindole, Dihydrochloride (DAPI), sections were visualized with a fluorescence microscope (Leica DMi8, Germany). Quantitative analysis was determined using Image J software (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Nile red staining

Frozen brain sections (20 μm) were stained with Nile Red (MCE, HY-D0718, 1ug/mL, USA) in the dark for 20 min at room temperature. After washing with PBS for three times, sections were stained with DAPI for 5 min. The images were observed and captured under a fluorescence microscope (Leica DMi8, Germany) or a confocal microscope (Olympus, FV3000).

Golgi staining

The experiment method was performed according to a previously published protocol [18]. Briefly, mouse brains were rapidly dissected, then immersed in Golgi-Cox solution in the dark at room temperature. After 24 h of immersion in Golgi-Cox solution, the solution of all the brains was refreshed with Golgi-Cox solution and left for 7–10 days at room temperature. Brains were transferred from Golgi-Cox solution to tissue-protectant solution, and the tissue protective solution was changed once after 24 h to wash away the residual Golgi-Cox solution. Brains were cryostat-sectioned to 100 μm, air-dried for 2–3 days in dark, developed with 3:1 ammonia solution and 5% sodium thiosulfate, dehydrated through 70, 95 and 100% ethanol, cleared in xylene and cover-slipped. Using a Leica microscope (Leica DMi8, Germany), bright-field images of pyramidal neurons in the cortex were taken.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Mice were exsanguinated by per-fusion with saline followed by a mixture of 4% PFA and 2.5% glutaraldehyde in phosphate buffer (0.1 M, pH 7.4). Following dissection of the whole brain, samples (1 × 1 × 1 mm) were taken from hippocampus and cortex, fixed in the same fixative, washed in phosphate buffer for 30 min, and incubated in 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h. After washing, samples were dehydrated in graded ethanol series before being transitioned in propylene oxide and embedded in Epon812 epoxy resin. Thick Sects. (1–2 μm) were cut and stained into methylene blue. Thin sections were cut at 50–70 nm by an ultramicrotome (Leica, UC7), and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, visualized with an electron microscope (HITACHI, H-7650).

Acetylcholine quantification

Cortical and basal forebrain tissues were sonicated for 30 s on ice in cell lysis buffer and centrifuged at 10,000 g for 5 min at 4 °C. Acetylcholine was then analyzed with by commercially available ELISA kits (Abcam, ab287812, UK) according to manufacturer’s instructions. The acetylcholine concentrations were determined by comparison with the standard curve.

Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)

The prefrontal cortex was finely minced in a drop of PBS-T before transfer to an Eppendorf tube. Following trituration in cold PBS-T, supernatants were combined and filtered through a 70 μm mesh prior to antibody staining. Cells were then incubated with monoclonal rabbit anti-Neun antibody (Abcam, ab177487, 1:500, UK) at 4 °C overnight. After washing with PBS-T, the cells were incubated with Alexa-fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher, A32731, 1:300, USA) at 4 °C for 1 h. The neurons were sorted on a BD FACSAria Fusion (BD Biosciences) and collected for further analysis.

RNA extraction and quantitative real Time-PCR (qRT-PCR)

RNA samples from cortex were isolated using Direct-zol RNA Miniprep Plus Kits (Zymo Research, R2070, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Total RNA was extracted from sorted neurons using Trizol (Thermo Fisher, 15596018, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and quality were detected using the NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher, USA) system. After controlling the total RNA to 900 ng, a Reverse Transcription kit (Vazyme, R433-01, China) was used to obtain cDNA. SYBR Green (Vazyme, Q712-02, China) was used for qRT-PCR to quantify Agtr1a, Bdnf, Pi4k3b, Nrf2, and Slc2a1 mRNA levels. The primer sequences are listed in the supplementary material (Table S3). Gene expression was normalized to β-Actin or 18 s and quantified using the comparative cycle threshold (Ct) method (2−ΔΔCt).

Cell culture

The CATH.a cells (a neuron cell line) were cultured in DMEM complete medium (Gibco, C11995500BT, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, 10270106, USA), and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Mishubio, MI00614, China), under the condition of 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) measurement

Mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using the tetramethylrhodamine ethyl ester perchlorate (TMRE) Assay Kit (Beyotime, C2001S, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The CATH.a cells were placed in 12-well plates (1 × 105 cells per well) and then treated with 100 nM ang II (Sigma Aldrich, 05-23-0101, USA) or vehicle for 3 h. Carbonyl cyanide 3-chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP, 10 µM) was used as a positive control for the induction of mitochondrial membrane depolarization. The cells were washed posttreatment with PBS and incubated with TMRE staining solution for 20 min at 37 °C in the dark, followed by flow cytometry analysis (Beckman Coulter, CytoFLEX, USA).

Measurement of ATP by probes

pCMV-Mito-AT1.03 (a tool plasmid for mitochondrial ATP fluorescent probe) was purchased from Beyotime (D2604). The CATH.a cells were plated onto 12-well plates in a concentration of 1 × 105 cells per well, followed by transient transfected with pCMV-Mito-AT1.03 using Lipofectamine 8000 (Beyotime, C0533, China) for 48 h. Then cells were treated with 100 nM ang II (Sigma Aldrich, 05-23-0101, USA) alone for 0.5–3 h, or pretreated with 10 µM losartan (TOCRIS, 3798, Australia) for 30 min. Under each treatment condition, fluorescence images were obtained from several randomly chosen fields under a fluorescence microscope (Leica DMi8, Germany), and quantification of the fluorescence intensity was achieved by using ImageJ software.

Measurement of released ATP

The CATH.a cells were plated onto 12-well plates (1 × 105 cells per well), followed by treated with 100 nM ang II (Sigma Aldrich, 05-23-0101, USA), 10 µM losartan, 10 µM losartan (pretreated for 30 min) plus 100 nM ang II or vehicle for 3 h. Then the culture media were collected and the ATP content was measured using the Enhanced ATP Assay Kit (Beyotime, S0027, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The luminescence signals were determined using the BioTek Synergy Neo2 Hybrid Multimode Reader (Agilent, USA).

Single cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq)

Single-cell RNA-Seq was performed to profile the cortical tissues of adult male mice after DOCA-salt or sham treatment (3 mice per sample pool). After harvested, tissues were washed in ice-cold RPMI-1640 and dissociated using Adult Brain Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-107-677, USA) as instructions. DNase-I (Sigma Aldrich, 9003-98-9, USA) treatment was optional according to the viscosity of the homogenate. Cell count and viability were estimated using a fluorescence Cell Analyzer (Countstar® Rigel S2, China) with AO/PI reagent after removal erythrocytes (Solarbio, R1010, China). Finally fresh cells were washed twice in the RPMI-1640 and then resuspended at 1 × 106 cells per mL in 1×PBS and 0.04% bovine serum albumin.

Single-cell RNA-Seq libraries were prepared using SeekOne® Digital Droplet Single Cell 3’library preparation kit (SeekGene, K00202, China). Briefly, appropriate number of cells were mixed with reverse transcription reagent and then added to the sample well in SeekOne® DD Chip S3. Subsequently Barcoded Hydrogel Beads (BHBs) and partitioning oil were dispensed into corresponding wells separately in chip S3. After emulsion droplet generation reverse transcription was performed at 42℃ for 90 minutes and inactivated at 80℃ for 15 minutes. Next, cDNA was purified from broken droplet and amplified in PCR reaction. The amplified cDNA product was then cleaned, fragmented, end-repaired, A-tailed and ligated to sequencing adaptor. Finally, the indexed PCR was performed to amplify the DNA representing 3’ polyA part of expressing genes which also contained Cell Barcode and Unique Molecular Index. The indexed sequencing libraries were cleanup with SPRI beads, quantified by quantitative PCR (KAPA Biosystems KK4824), and then sequenced on illumina NovaSeq 6000 with PE150 read length or DNBSEQ-T7 platform with PE100 read length.

The raw sequencing data was processed by Fastp (version 0.23.1) to trim primer sequences and low-quality bases to collect basic statistics. Next, SeekSoul Tools pipeline (version 1.0.0, SeekGene) was used to process the cleaned reads and generate the transcript expression matrix. For downstream analysis, the Seurat package (v4.0.5 or v4.3.0) was used, and expression matrices were filtered to remove cells with a number of unique gene counts less than 300. Cells with more than 10% of mitochondrial gene counts were also filtered. From the remaining cells a global-scaling normalization method “LogNormalize” was carried out to normalize the feature expression measurements for each cell by the total expression, multiply this by a scale factor of 10,000, and log-transform the result. Next, the top 2000 variable genes were selected for dimensionality reduction analysis. Principal components analysis (PCA) was conducted and the top 30 principal components were selected for downstream analysis. Batch correction was done using Harmony, and clusters were generated by Louvain clustering method. The cell clusters were visualized using the uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE). FindAllMarkers were used to identify all differentially expressed markers between clusters. Genes were tested for significance if they showed a minimum log fold change greater than 0.25 and were expressed in a minimum of 10% of cells belonging to the cluster of interest. The annotation of each cell cluster was confirmed by the expression of canonical marker genes.

Nontargeted lipidomic analysis

Cortical tissues of adult male mice after DOCA-salt or sham treatment (n = 5–6 mice per group) were harvested, placed in liquid nitrogen for 10 s, and stored at − 80 °C. Briefly, Lipid compositions from the tissues were extracted with methyl tert-butyl ether/methanol (5:1 ratio). The samples were homogenized and centrifuged, then the supernatants were dried using a vacuum concentrator. The resulting pellet was resuspended in dichloromethane/methanol (1:1 ratio). The mixed solution was centrifuged, and the supernatant was used for liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry analysis.

Proteomics analysis and 4D quantitative succinylation proteomics

For proteomics analysis, the cortical tissues of adult male mice after DOCA-salt or sham treatment (n = 6 mice per group, 3 batches) were collected. 4D quantitative succinylation proteomics was performed by PTM Biolab. The experimental strategy and workflow are present in Fig. 4A. Samples of three DOCA-salt-treated mice and three controls analyzed in parallel for 4D-FastDIA-based comparative proteomics and quantitation of their succinylome after enrichment of peptides with succinylation.

Protein extraction

The sample was grinded with liquid nitrogen, followed by sonication three times on ice using a high intensity ultrasonic processor (Scientz) in lysis buffer (8 M urea, 1% protease inhibitor cocktail). After centrifugation at 12,000 g at 4 °C for 10 min, the supernatant was collected and the protein concentration was determined with a BCA kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Tryptic digestion

After being reduced with 5 mM dithiothreitol for 30 min at 56 °C and being alkylated with 11 mM iodoacetamide for 15 min at room temperature in the dark, the protein solution was diluted to a concentration < 2 M by adding 100 mM TEAB to the mixture. Then, trypsin was added at 1:50 and 1:100 trypsin-to-protein mass ratio, respectively, for the first and second digestion. Finally, the peptides were desalted by C18 SPE column.

Enrichment of Post-Translational modifications​ (for Succinylome)

To enrich modified peptides, tryptic peptides dissolved in NETN buffer (100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 50 mM Tris-HCl, 0.5% NP-40, pH 8.0) were incubated with pre-washed antibody beads (PTM Biolab, PTM402) at 4 °C overnight with gentle shaking. Then the beads were washed for four times with NETN buffer and twice with H2O. The bound peptides were eluted from the beads with 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid. Finally, the eluted fractions were combined and vacuum-dried. For LC-MS/MS analysis, the resulting peptides were desalted with C18 ZipTips (Millipore) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

LC-MS/MS analyses for proteomics

The tryptic peptides were dissolved in solvent A and subsequently loaded onto a homemade reversed-phase analytical column (25 cm length, 75/100 µm i.d.). The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (0.1% formic acid, 2% acetonitrile/in water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile). Peptides were separated with the following gradient: 0–8 min, 9–24% B; 8–12 min, 24–35% B; 12–16 min, 35–80% B; 16–20 min, 80% B, and all at a constant flow rate of 450 nL/min on a nanoElute UHPLC system (Bruker Daltonics). The peptides were then subjected to capillary source followed by the timsTOF Pro 2 (Bruker Daltonics) mass spectrometry. The electrospray voltage applied was 1.60 kV. Precursors and fragments were analyzed at the TOF detector. The timsTOF Pro was operated in data-independent acquisition parallel accumulation serial fragmentation (dia-PASEF) mode, with the following parameters: MS1 scan range of 100–1700 m/z, 10 PASEF-MS/MS acquisitions triggered after each full MS1 scan, and MS2 scans covering 400–1200 m/z divided into sequential 25 m/z windows for fragmentation.

LC-MS/MS analyses for succinylation proteomics

The tryptic peptides were dissolved in solvent A and subsequently loaded onto a homemade reversed-phase analytical column (25 cm length, 75/100 µm i.d.). The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (0.1% formic acid, 2% acetonitrile/in water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile). Peptides were separated with the following gradient: 0–18 min, 6–22% B; 18–22 min, 22–30% B; 22–26 min, 30–80% B; 26–20 min, 80% B, and all at a constant flow rate of 450 nL/min on a nanoElute UHPLC system (Bruker Daltonics). The peptides were then subjected to capillary source followed by the timsTOF Pro 2 (Bruker Daltonics) mass spectrometry. The electrospray voltage applied was 1.65 kV. Precursors and fragments were analyzed at the TOF detector. The timsTOF Pro was operated in dia-PASEF mode, with the following parameters: MS1 scan range of 100–1700 m/z, 10 PASEF-MS/MS acquisitions triggered after each full MS1 scan, and MS2 scans covering 400–1200 m/z divided into sequential 25 m/z windows for fragmentation.

Database search

The resulting MS/MS data were processed using MaxQuant search engine (v.1.6.15.0). Tandem mass spectra were searched against the human SwissProt database (20422 entries) concatenated with reverse decoy database. Trypsin/P was specified as cleavage enzyme allowing up to 2 missing cleavages. The mass tolerance for precursor ions was set as 20 ppm in first search and 5 ppm in main search, and the mass tolerance for fragment ions was set as 0.02 Da. Carbamidomethyl on Cys was specified as fixed modification, and acetylation on protein N-terminal and oxidation on Met were specified as variable modifications. False discovery rate (FDR) was adjusted to < 1% and minimum score for peptides was set > 40.

Data analysis

MRI cerebral perfusion data analysis

The cortex regions of interest (ROI) were manually delineated using ITK-SNAP software (version 3.8.0, http://www.itksnap.org) based on the CBF images, and the regional CBF was calculated by averaging signals in the ROIs. Then the normalized CBF ratio was calculated as the signal within the ROI divided by the mean signal of the whole-brain.

Analysis of differential expression

For scRNA-seq data, FindMarkers function was applied to the integrated samples to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the cell subsets of interest. DEGs were defined by FDR < 0.05 and absolute log2 fold change (Log2FC) > 0.25 or 0.4. The DEGs were showed in Volcano plots with the R package.

For lipidomics data, the significantly changed lipids were considered by VIP > 1, |Log2FC|≥ 0.5, and P < 0.05. Cytoscape software v3.10.3 was used to create lipid network according to the results of statistical analyses.

For proteomics data, the batch effect was evaluated and corrected through an online tool, Omics Batch Correct (OBC). OBC was available at https://omia.untangledbio.com/obc/. The differentially expressed proteins were identified by the Limma R package with a criterion of |Log2FC|≥1 and P < 0.05.

Functional enrichment analysis

For scRNA-seq data and proteomics data, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the marker genes was implemented by the ClusterProfiler R package. GO analysis includes the biological process, molecular function, and cellular component categories. The calculated P-value was corrected by FDR, and P-value < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway was calculated in the same way as GO. For lipidomics data, KEGG pathway analysis was carried out in the online tool (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/). To gain further insight into biological implications, the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was carried out based on the MSigDB database by msigdbr R package. The significantly enriched gene sets were selected with a criterion of |NES|>1, p-value < 0.05.

Subcellular distribution analysis

Subcellular distribution of the identified candidates was determined using Cytoscape (version 3.10.3) and stringAPP (version 2.2.0) software. All the parameters were set to the default values, but only the highest compartment score as the high confidence localization were kept.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as means ± S.E.M. Student’s t-test was used to analyze differences between the two groups. For multiple comparisons, data were analyzed by one- or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey post-hoc test for one-way ANOVA and Bonferroni post-hoc test for two-way ANOVA. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v.9.0). Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Data Availability

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. All source data, including sequencing reads and single-cell expression matrices, are available from the Gene Expression Omnibus under accession code GSE255082. The raw proteomics data generated in this study has been deposited in iProX database by ProteomeXchange (PXD063476).

Hypertension-associated transcriptomic alterations indicate disorder of energy uptake and metabolism in endothelial cells and astrocytes

To further study the neuro-vascular unit, cortical tissue from both DOCA-salt- and sham-treated mice were collected and processed for single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) obtained in the endothelial cells (ECs) were subjected to Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) for KEGG, to acquire functional differences, such as immune responses and metabolic reprogramming, between hypertension and normal cortex (Fig.1D). Positive normalized enrichment scores (NES) indicated upregulation of these pathways, highlighting a hypoxia condition of ECs during hypertension, accompanied by significantly increased energy metabolism (cholesterol homeostasis, Kras signaling, and glycolysis), and inflammatory response (TNFα signaling via NFκB). Among the DEGs of ECs (Fig. 1E), we noticed that expression of Slc2a1, which encodes glucose transport 1 (GluT1), was dramatically downregulated in the ECs of hypertensive mice (Fig. 1F), so as its protein level (Fig. 1G), suggesting that ECs’ metabolic changes could be compensative result of reduced glucose uptake.

As an important party of the neuro-vascular unit, astrocytes are essential for transfer energy metabolites from the ECs to the neurons. Astrocytes from both control and hypertension cortex were re-populated into 5 sub-groups (Fig. 1H), in which A0 and A1 constitute most astrocytes. GO functional enrichment analysis of DEGs in each astrocyte sub-group further confirmed metabolic changes-induced by hypertension (Fig.1I). In specific, DEGs in A0 (including Slc2a4, Serpina3n, Adcy8, Agt, Tnf, Sod3, Hif3a, Noct, and Foxo1) were mainly related to hypoxia, response to oxygen level, and response to hormonal peptide; DEGs in A1 (including Slc2a4, Serpina3n, Adcy8, Agt, Tnf, Apoc1, Cacna1d, and Foxo1) were related to glucose homeostasis, phospholipid/lipid metabolism, and response to hormonal peptide; DEGs in A3 were mainly related to regulation of synaptic function and cognition. Of note, expression of Slc2a4 was significantly upregulated in both A0 and A1, suggesting that astrocyte might increase glucose uptake to compensate ECs’ dysfunction. In general, transcriptomic alterations in ECs and astrocytes indicate reduced glucose uptake, upregulated energy metabolism/utilization, oxidative stress and inflammation during hypertension.

Hypertension is associated with metabolic stress and lipid catabolism in the cerebral cortex

To further identify hypertension-associated metabolic reprograming and its potential link to neurodegeneration, the prefrontal cortex (PFC) were collected for lipidomic analysis on phospholipids. In the PFC, multiple types of phospholipids’ contents were significantly altered by hypertension. On one hand, as shown in Fig. 2A-B, the contents of multiple free fatty acids (FFA) were markedly increased in the PFC of hypertensive mice. On the other, most TAG (triacylglycerol), DAG (diacylglycerol), PC (phosphatidylcholine), PE (phosphatidylethanolamine), PS (phosphatidylserine), and PI (phosphatidylinositol) were dramatically reduced following hypertension. This pattern demonstrated a significant remodeling of the lipidome, characterized by membrane phospholipid metabolic rearrangement and accumulation of FFAs. Based on this hypertension-associated lipidomic remodeling, functional enrichment analysis was performed via KEGG (Fig. 2C), and it was significantly enriched in the pathways of glycerophospholipid metabolism, biosynthesis of UFAs, GPI-anchor biosynthesis, and linoleic acid metabolism. Hypertension induces neurodegenerative alterations and mitochondrial stress

To further confirm the above results, we then conducted multiple morphological and molecular validations. In the PFC of hypertensive mice, accumulation of lipid-droplets (LDs) was found to be significantly increased (Fig. 3A, B). In contrast, number of neuronal dendritic spines was markedly reduced (Fig. 3C, D), further suggesting that hypertension-related metabolic disorder could lead to neurodegeneration. Moreover, we observed marked damage of mitochondria via transmission electron microscopy (TEM), featured by “white spots” (defined as vacuoles), elongated shape, and broken membranes in mitochondria of the PFC neurons following hypertension modeling (Fig. 3E, F). Via Western Blotting, we further identified increased level of NADH dehydrogenase Fe-S protein 1 (NDUFS1) expression in the cytosol, and reduced expression of cytochrome c (Cyto C) in the cellular mitochondrial fraction (Fig. 3G, H). NDUFS1 and Cyto C are core components of the mitochondrial respiratory chain (electron transport chain, ETC). These alterations suggest possible permeabilization of mitochondrial membrane and ETC dysfunction in the cerebral cortical cells following hypertension. Blocking ETC pharmacologically has been demonstrated to induce tau phosphorylation and alter the pattern of lysine succinylation (SL) [17, 20], a protein post-transcription modification that closely correlates to mitochondrial function and tauopathy [17]. Accordingly, accumulation of phosphorylated Tau (AT8/Tau), and the level of SL, were measured using PFC tissue from control and hypertensive mice (Fig. 3I, J). DOCA-salt hypertension dramatically increased expression ratios of AT8/Tau, indicating abnormal accumulation of p-Tau in hypertensive brain. Upregulated SL reveals enhanced mitochondrial energy metabolism and metabolic stress in neurons during hypertension

To further identify the specific impact of DOCA-salt hypertension to mitochondrial function, lysine succinylomes was then performed to collect detailed information regarding those SL-proteins, using PFC tissues (Fig. 4A). Similar as the result of pan-SL, DOCA-salt hypertension was associated with an upregulation of SL in both numbers of proteins and sites (Fig. 4B, C). As revealed by further analysis, we found that proteins/sites with increased succinylation were more located in the cellular mitochondria (Fig. 4D), and these mitochondrial proteins were functionally enriched in gluconeogenesis-related metabolic pathway and energy metabolism/utilization, including the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, degradation of vital amino acids, 2-oxocarboxylic acid metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, and ATP biosynthesis (Fig. 4F, upper panel: GO-biological process; lower panel: KEGG). As to all SL-upregulated proteins, in addition to gluconeogenesis-related metabolic pathway and energy metabolism, they were also related to the biological processes of nucleotide metabolism, function of cellular vesicles, and they were inevitably involved in the pathways of multiple neurodegenerative diseases, including AD (Fig. 4E, upper panel: GO-biological process; lower panel: KEGG). Notably, in the PFC, most pan-SL were detected in neurons (Fig. 4G), co-labeled by Tau and AT8 (p-Tau), indicating hypertension-related SL mainly happened in this type of brain cells. In mouse PFC and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples from in-patients, we further confirmed lysine succinylation of tau, using protein pre-enrichment with immunoprecipitation (Fig. 4H). Meanwhile, other proteins, which are (a) within the detected hyper-succinylation range (40 ~ 70 kDa) and (b) contain multiple succinylation sites similar as tau, were listed separately in Table 2. Their predicted subcellular locations, functions and involved pathways further confirmed that DOCA-salt treatment massively altered mitochondrial function and cellular energy metabolism in the PFC. Selective deletion of AT1R in neurons meliorates DOCA-salt-induced neuronal metabolic disorder and cognitive impairment

Utilization of a mouse line with selective knockout of neuron-expressing AT1aR (AT1N), we then determined its potential impacts on DOCA-salt hypertension-associated metabolic stress (SL upregulation and p-Tau accumulation), neurodegenerative alterations, and cognitive impairment. Firstly, in the last week of sham/DOCA-salt treatment, mice were subjected to behavioral tests, including novel object recognition (NOR) test, Barnes maze test, and nesting test, for assessment of their text and space learning/memory, as well as overall health of cognitive function. Lower NOR preference, longer latency time to locate the shelter in maze, and reduced nesting scores, indicated hypertension-associated cognitive decline. Unlike the non-transgenic controls (NT), in the AT1N mice, DOCA-salt treatment failed to induced significant decline in cognitive function, evaluated by the NOR test (Fig. 6A), nesting scores (Fig. 6B), and Barnes maze test (Fig. 6C). Then, in the PFC of these mice, DOCA-salt-associated increase of pan-SL was not detected in the AT1N mice (Fig. 6D, E), and p-Tau accumulation (AT8/Tau) was also markedly blunted (Fig. 6F, G). Expression of markers for neuronal function, Bdnf and Pi4k3b [23], was measure using brain cortical tissues, showing significantly reduction in the NT controls post DOCA-salt treatment, while their levels were preserved in the AT1N mice; as to expression of Nrf2, which regulates cellular glucose uptake/metabolism and protection against oxidative stress [24, 25], was dramatically reduced by DOCA-salt treatment, but it was not affected in the mice of AT1N (Fig. 6H). Additionally, we measured the tissue level of acetylcholine (ACh), because studies have suggested that ang II may affect the cholinergic system via AT1R [26, 27], and cholinergic inputs from the basal forebrain to the cortex were critical to multiple neurological functions, such as sensory processing and memory [28]. The level of ACh content was significantly lower in the cortex of control mice with DOCA-salt hypertension, and DOCA-salt had no marked effect on ACh level within the basal forebrain, which is a core for cholinergic neuronal soma. Nevertheless, DOCA-salt modeling did not induce decrease in cortical ACh level in the AT1N mice (Fig. 6I). To further evaluate the effect of AT1N on DOCA-salt-related metabolic stress, in the brains of both NT and AT1N mice, morphological changes of astrocytes were studied (Fig. 6J). A larger cellular area and higher branching density of astrocyte indicate a stronger contribution to glucose uptake, lactate shuttling, and blood-brain barrier maintenance, and importantly enhanced metabolic support for neurons [29]. In NT controls, DOCA-salt modeling was associated with significantly smaller astrocyte area, shorter process length, and lower branching density, while these indications were not detected in the AT1N mice (Fig. 6K), supporting that mice with neuronal AT1aR deletion might have less severe energy crisis upon hypertension modeling. DOCA-salt-induced neuronal metabolic reprogramming is driven by the activation of local RAS, not high BP

To validate whether high BP has direct role in hypertension-related neuronal metabolic reprogramming, another two sets of WT mice were given norepinephrine (NE) or N (ω)-nitro-L-arginine methyl ester (L-NAME) to induce hypertension. Both NE and L-NAME significantly elevated BP (Fig. 7A), but only L-NAME induced cognitive impairment (Fig. 7B) and significantly impaired vascular reactivity, determined by whisker stimulation-induced functional hyperemia in pial arterioles (Fig. 7C). However, neither hypertension modeling was able to increase lysine succinylation (pan-SL) (Fig. 7D, E), in a manner similar as DOCA-salt hypertension. To further confirm the independence of high BP, hydralazine, a direct vasodilator, was provided to DOCA-salt-treated mice on the 3rd week of the protocol (day-15) as a co-treatment. At the end the protocol, co-treatment of hydralazine markedly blunted hypertension development in DOCA-salt-treated mice (Fig. 7F), however, this anti-hypertensive treatment showed no protection against DOCA-salt-induced impairment in either cognitive function or vascular reactivity (Fig. 7G, H). Further analysis using their PFC tissue indicated that both pan-SL and p-Tau accumulation (AT8/Tau) were still significantly increased in DOCA-salt-treated mice, regardless of their BP (Fig. 7I, J, K, L). Notably, NE and L-NAME induce hypertension through mechanisms that is independent on the brain RAS, while hydralazine blunts hypertension development via direct vasodilation. Discussion

In the current study, via multi-omics analysis, we studied how hypertension affect energy metabolism in the neuro-vascular unit of cerebral cortex. Hypertension is known to induce cerebrovascular dysfunction, which impairs functional hyperemia upon neuronal energy demand. In mice with hypertension, when decreased vascular reactivity leads to reduced glucose uptake and hypoxia in the ECs. Surprisingly, rather than being downregulated or maintained through compensatory mechanisms, energy metabolism, including lipid metabolism, gluconeogenesis, and oxidative phosphorylation, were markedly upregulated in the neurons. This uncoupling between vascular energy intake and neuronal metabolic demand contributes to mitochondrial stress in neurons, which further supported abnormal protein succinylation, p-Tau accumulation, and neurodegenerative alterations.

As summarized in Table 3, we firstly observed the upregulated pathways of cholesterol metabolism, glycolysis, Kras signaling, then we identified significantly downregulated expression of GluT1, the transporter for glucose uptake by ECs. This indicates that DOCA-salt hypertension leads to metabolic changes in the gatekeeper of the brain. Outside of the vasculature, astrocytes can serve as an energy conduit for neurons. Functional enrichment analysis of hypertension-associated DEGs also suggested energy metabolic reprogramming in the cortical astrocytes. Further morphological analysis of these astrocytes showed a potential reduction in their energy supply to neurons. By lipidomics, dramatically reduced levels of TAG and DAG were observed in the PFC of hypertensive mice. Combining the downregulation of GluT1 in ECs and metabolic rearrangement in astrocytes, this result of lipid analysis in the PFC indicated enhanced conversion of non-carbohydrate substances (particularly glycerol) to glucose (the primary energy substrate for the brain) in the hypertensive brain. Increased adipogenesis and elevated FFA levels further supported this statement. On one hand, gluconeogenesis produces energy metabolites and consumes ATP; on the other, activation of AT1R by ang II significantly upregulates consumption of energy metabolites and enhances ATP biosynthesis. This feedforward interaction among multiple pathways is likely to further increase the expenditure of limited energy substrates, augment ATP biosynthesis, and elevate the burden on the mitochondrial ETC. Because SL relies on succinyl-CoA from the TCA cycle, which directly supports oxidative phosphorylation, so we think that the increased level of pan-SL and the enriched pathways of hypertension-associated succinylomes did provide evidence for this hypothesis. Consequently, in the hypertensive brain, the metabolic stress for neuronal mitochondria is inevitably elevated. Indeed, by rest-state fMRI, lower BOLD signals were detected in patients’ group in many functional brain regions, where the CBF signals were not changed or even elevated. In our scan setting, the CBF signals were recorded simultaneously with BOLD in a rest-state, therefore the BOLD signals only reflected the level of blood oxygen, rather than neuronal activity. Combing the result of multi-omics analysis in DOCA-salt-treated mice, lower level of blood oxygen in maintained CBF could be the consequence of enhanced energy metabolism, especially the oxidative phosphorylation, in the neurons of patients. Notably, for fMRI tests, healthy controls and advanced chronic kidney disease patients with concurrent hypertension and cognitive impairment were recruited, because hypertension alone (as a single risk factor) does not induce significant cognitive decline in human subjects [19].

In consistence with the previous findings that hypertension contributes to cerebrovascular dysfunction, scRNA-seq analysis on the cortical ECs indeed pointed to hypoxia and downregulated expression of GluT1, which is the main transporter for the brain’s glucose uptake [7]. But considering the upregulated energy metabolism/utilization in astrocytes and neurons, it is unlikely that the top reason for neuronal metabolic stress and neurodegenerative alterations is lack of oxygen. This was also supported by the opposite trend of pan-SL in DOCA-salt hypertension and L-NAME hypertension, with endothelial dilation mostly abolished in the latter. In the brain, deletion of ECs’ GluT1 inhibited EC-derived lactate production [30], highlighting its important role in supporting neuronal energy supply. The enhanced lipid metabolism and gluconeogenesis also pointed that lacking glucose uptake should be the primary driving force for the neuronal metabolic reprogramming during hypertension. In fact, downregulation of GluT1 in hippocampal microvasculature has been demonstrated to contribute to post-operative cognitive dysfunction in aged mice [31]. Lowered expression of GluT1 was also observed in patients with AD [7], showing a potential link between hypertension and the etiology of AD. Therefore, improving ECs’ glucose uptake could be considered for treatment of neurodegenerative disorders associated with cerebral small vessel disease. ​It is still unknown how DOCA-salt hypertension reduced GluT1 expression in the brain ECs. But in HUEVC, mRNA expression of GluT1 was found to be downregulated by oscillatory shear stress (supplementary Figure-S2C), supporting the previous findings that endothelial glucose uptake can be regulated by hemodynamics [32, 33]. So we speculate that the cortical ECs might downregulate GluT1 expression upon DOCA-salt-induced hemodynamic and/or vascular reactivity changes. Unlike GluT1, the expression of GluT4 (Slc2a4), the subtype of glucose transporter in astrocytes, was upregulated. In astrocytes, GluT4 facilitates glucose uptake upon insulin-signaling [34], indirectly supports neuronal energy demand during hypoglycemia. Moreover, we found that Foxo1 was downregulated in astrocytes (Fig. 1I), and inhibition of FOXO1 in astrocytes has been shown to shift metabolism from glycolysis to oxidative phosphorylation, enhancing ATP synthesis, and decreasing astrocytic export of lactate [35], indicating that astrocytes might increase their own utilization for energy but decrease energy supply for nearby neurons. As evidence, smaller area and less processes of astrocytes were observed in mice with DOCA-salt hypertension (Fig. 6 J, K). Taken together, this “selfish” metabolic changes in the astrocytes could also be consequence of the compromised glucose uptake via ECs. Expression of FOXO1 in astrocytes can be affected by various mechanisms, such as inflammation and metabolic stress [36, 37], and further investigation into the molecular mechanisms for astrocyte activation during hypertension is warranted.

In the PFC, the major sources for phospholipids are oligodendrocytes, neurons, and astrocytes. We did not observe marked demyelination or reduction in sphingomyelin contents following DOCA-salt hypertension, so the hypertension-associated lipidomics pattern indicated lipid metabolism in term of energy metabolism, not demyelination, and this was further confirmed by the analysis of lipidomic networking and KEGG. Increased lipid metabolism was also confirmed by GESA of proteomics, showing upregulated expression pattern for proteins in the pathway of adipogenesis, and in the pathway of peroxisome, a multifunctional organelle that plays essential role in lipid metabolism, including cholesterol biosynthesis [38]. A recent work identified the fundamental relationship between upregulated adipogenesis and aging [39]. Moreover, peroxisomes and mitochondria have a strong interconnection, as they show complementary activities and exchange many metabolites [40]. As the evidence for upregulated lipid metabolism, we observed increased number of LDs in the PFC. LDs are important storages for energy lipid (such as TAG) in neurons and astrocytes. Increased formation of LDs has been shown as a protective response to ER stress and mitochondrial damage. LDs accumulation has been associated with aging, neuroinflammation, and development of AD [41].

Mitochondrial damage closely links to the mechanisms underlying neurodegenerative alterations in neurons. Mitochondria serve as the primary site for lysine succinylation, because of its dependence on succinyl-CoA. Normally, lysine succinylation is taken place largely in the mitochondria [42]. It has been shown in several pathological conditions that impaired mitochondria redistributes succinyl-transferases from the mitochondria to cytosol, thus increasing succinylation of nonmitochondrial proteins [17, 43, 44]. In AD, impaired mitochondrial function is found to promote this “off-target” succinylation, which serves as an important player in p-tau and APP accumulations [17]. Moreover, succinylation levels were significantly increased both during aging and upon lipopolysaccharide-induced neuroinflammation [45]. In the current study, we also confirmed tau succinylation in the PFC of mice, using lysine succinylomes, co-immunoprecipitation, and immunofluorescence co-labeling. Further analysis of lysine succinylomes revealed that succinylation for the proteins in energy metabolism pathways, including gluconeogenesis, the TCA cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation, were upregulated, confirming enhanced metabolic activities in or close to the mitochondria. Considering that ECs’ glucose uptake was limited, two possibilities arise for this enhanced energy utilization: (a) neurons might require more ATP consumption to maintain high energy metabolism; (b) there could be other factors that elevate the energy demand in neurons during hypertension. These data indicated again that hypertension-associated brain metabolic stress is highly possibly due to limited glucose uptake, rather than reduced oxygen supply.

Hypertension and hypertension-induced endothelial dysfunction are mediated by different but interconnected pathways. Notably, hypertension induced by NE-mediated vasoconstriction did not affect vascular reactivity, cognitive function, or pan-SL in mice; hypertension induced by L-NAME-mediated blockade nitric oxide biosynthesis did not upregulate pan-SL, while direct anti-hypertensive treatment via vasodilation failed to reverse DOCA-salt-induced pathologies, supporting that the potential shared mechanisms are less BP-dependent. Although we previously showed that neuronal AT1R knockout alleviates DOCA-salt-induced hypertension, targeting neuronal AT1R should not void ang II’s action on the vessels. Ang II/AT1R signaling in vascular cells, including endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, and perivascular macrophages, has been shown to mediate endothelial dysfunction and vascular remodeling independently of BP [1, 6, 13]. On ex vivo cerebral arterial preparations, co-treatment with losartan, a competitive antagonist of AT1R, reversed DOCA-salt-associated endothelial dysfunction, providing strong evidence for the accumulation of brain tissue-local ang II in this model [13]. In rats, minimum dose of 4.78 pmol ang II can completely block the induction of long-term potentiation (LTP) in the hippocampus, while intraperitoneal injection of losartan or its metabolite that crosses the BBB (DuP 753) reversed this inhibition of LTP induction induced by ang II administration directly onto hippocampal Sects. [46, 47]. These findings provide evidence that it is the hyper-activity of tissue-local RAS, rather than its pressor response, drives the development of end-organ damage of DOCA-salt hypertension. Therefore, in mice lacking neuronal AT1R, abolished upregulation of pan-SL and reduced level of p-tau, imply a possibility that beyond the effects of ang II/AT1R downstream signaling per se on neuro-vascular cells, its overall impact on metabolic stress may also contribute to neurodegenerative alterations and cognitive decline in DOCA-salt hypertension.

In general, our data suggest that DOCA-salt hypertension induced metabolic reprogramming in the neuro-vascular unit, due to downregulated glucose uptake in ECs. Increased neuronal energy consumption, via local ang II/AT1R signaling, further exacerbated metabolic stress and neurodegenerative alterations. These findings provide novel insights regarding how hypertension increases the risk for age-related cognitive impairment, especially for AD.

Limitations

The purpose of using DOCA-salt model in this study is to selectively activate the brain RAS. It has been suggested this experimental model mimics salt-sensitive hypertension. In human, salt-sensitive individuals exhibit a higher prevalence among African Americans in the United States, elderly individuals, and hypertensive patients [48]. The detection rate ranges from 28.57% to 58% respectively in normotensive and hypertensive populations in the Northern China [49]. In addition, DOCA-salt model also exhibits key characters of volume-dependent hypertension (water-sodium retention), chronic kidney disease-related hypertension (nephron loss, RAS over-activation, and water-sodium retention), and Conn’s syndrome (primary aldosteronism), which are associated with more severe damage to the end-organs [50–52]. This might explain why L-NAME treatment or NE infusion could not increase lysine succinylation in a similar manner to DOCA-salt, and why the therapeutic effect of hydralazine was poor. Although DOCA-salt indeed induce rather mild BP elevation, its neural and humoral elements contribute to more significant impact on the target organs (i.e. brain), and this could limit the extrapolation of our finding in the current study to patients with salt-independent essential hypertension, or secondary hypertension induced by other mechanisms. The mechanisms underlying human hypertension and related vascular injury are rather complex, thus increasing the difficulty of preventing hypertension-related cognitive impairment. While most preclinical studies support the use of angiotensin receptor blockers to prevent AD and vascular dementia [53], the optimal type, dosage, and duration of antihypertensive medication for preventing cognitive disorders in humans remain unclear.
