Study of plasma exosomal miRNAs as novel biomarkers for differentiating primary central nervous system lymphoma and glioblastoma
Authors: Shan Lu, Lixia Xu, Yu Lan, Chen Wang, Lidong Mo, Hua Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPreoperative diagnosis of the nature of PCNSL is crucial for distinguishing it from commonly confused intracranial gliomas, such as glioblastoma multiforme (GBM). The present research aims to sequence peripheral blood exosomal miRNAs from PCNSL and GBM patients, and to identify the discriminative miRNAs with high feasibility. Plasma exosomal RNAs were extracted, and miRNA sequencing was performed. The miRNAs with significant differences were validated by RT-qPCR. A total of 67 miRNAs were significantly different between the PCNSL and GBM groups. Twenty-seven PCNSL and 27 GBM patients were enrolled for the RT-qPCR validation of 10 selected differentially expressed miRNAs. The expression levels of plasma exosomal hsa-miR-148a-3p, hsa-let-7f-5p, hsa-miR-345-5p and hsa-miR-4433b-5p were upregulated in the PCNSL group compared with those in the GBM group (P = 0.019, P = 0.036, P = 0.009, and P = 0.001, respectively). The combined panel comprising hsa-miR-148a-3p, hsa-miR-345-5p, and hsa-miR-4433b-5p demonstrated significantly enhanced diagnostic performance, with an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.791. Immunohistochemistry analysis revealed that the expression level of EGFR in PCNSL was significantly lower than that in GBM. Western blot and RT-qPCR analysis of EGFR expression levels in LN229 cells revealed that miR-148a-3p and miR-4433b-5p downregulated EGFR expression. The results of luciferase reporter assay showed that the relative luciferase activity of HEK293T cells transfected with EGFR-WT was notably suppressed by miR-4433b-5p (P < 0.001). In conclusion, the plasma exosomal hsa-miR-148a-3p, hsa-miR-345-5p, and hsa-miR-4433b-5p might be identified as novel biomarkers for differentiating PCNSL and GBM. The increased expression level of EGFR in GBM may be achieved by the negative regulatory effects of miR-4433b-5p. Introduction

Primary central nervous system lymphoma (PCNSL) is an extranodal and highly malignant non-Hodgkin lymphoma that originates from the central nervous system without the involvement of other organs, including the eye, brain, spinal cord, meninges, or cranial nerves. The main subtype is diffuse large B-cell lymphoma [1]. In recent years, the prevalence of PCNSL has increased, but the underlying pathogenesis remains ambiguous, and research on the associated diagnostic and therapeutic approaches has progressed slowly. Surgical resection of the tumor alone is not sufficient to cure PCNSL or improve the survival rate of patients. The main value of surgery lies in obtaining pathological results. A combination of radiation therapy and chemotherapy (methotrexate, etc.) as the mainstay of comprehensive treatment is currently recommended to improve the survival rate of patients [2–4]. Hence, a preoperative diagnosis of the nature of PCNSL is crucial for distinguishing it from its commonly confused intracranial gliomas, such as glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), and providing guidance for effective follow-up treatment.

It is usually straightforward to distinguish typical PCNSL from GBM with conventional MR imaging, but the differential diagnosis of atypical cases is often difficult. For instance, GBM without necrosis and with substantive enhancement may be similar to PCNSL, whereas PCNSL exhibiting necrosis, haemorrhage, or irregular margin enhancement may resemble GBM [5].

Liquid biopsy is an important advancement in solid tumor research and clinical application. It is a technique for the diagnosis and evaluation of disease mainly through the detection of circulating tumor cells (CTCs), circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), or other markers (e.g., exosomes) released into the peripheral blood from the primary or metastatic tumors [6]. Compared with other solid tumors, PCNSL is more likely to infiltrate bone marrow and tumor cells to enter the blood circulation [7]. Thus, liquid biopsy technology is advantageous for auxiliary diagnosis of PCNSL.

Exosomes are cellular exocytotic vesicles of 30 ~ 100 nm in size with a lipid bilayer membrane structure. They are important for cell-to-cell communication. The small particle size and high permeability of exosomes facilitate access to a wide range of bodily fluids, which is a crucial prerequisite for liquid biopsy. The lipid membranes of exosomes serve as protective structures for the contained RNA, maintaining and resulting in intact sequence structure and biological activity [8–10]. This facilitates the discovery of tumor-specific biomarkers at the molecular level of RNA, which holds promising prospects for the advancement of precision medicine.

The present research aims to sequence peripheral blood exosomal miRNAs from PCNSL and GBM patients, and to identify discriminative miRNAs with high feasibility. When conventional preoperative diagnostic methods fail to identify PCNSL and GBM, exosomal miRNA indicators provide neurosurgeons with rational guidance for precise intraoperative surgical approaches and postoperative treatment options.

Materials and methods

Human samples

Patients who were diagnosed with PCNSL or GBM at Tianjin Huanhu Hospital were included in this study. The inclusion criteria for PCNSL patients were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) pathologically diagnosed with PCNSL for the first time; and (3) no lymphoma detected outside the central nervous system, including the brain, meninges, eyes, spinal cord, or nerve roots. The exclusion criteria for PCNSL patients were as follows: (1) age < 18 years; (2) adult immunodeficiency syndrome-associated or Epstein-Barr virus-associated PCNSL; and (3) received any intracranial interventions, including surgery, chemotherapy or radiation therapy. The inclusion criteria for GBM patients were as follows: (1) age ≥18 years; (2) pathological diagnosed of GBM for the first time; and (3) no history of other malignant tumors. The exclusion criteria for GBM patients were as follows: (1) age < 18 years; (2) recurrent GBM; (3) history of other malignant tumors; and (4) received any intracranial interventions, including surgery, chemotherapy, or radiation therapy.

The plasma and tumor tissue samples of the patients were retrieved from the Neurological Disease Sample Repository of Tianjin Huanhu Hospital. The plasma samples of 28 PCNSL patients and 28 GBM patients were retrieved. Two groups of patients were matched by sex and age (percentage of males: 42.3% vs. 38.5%; age: 56.7 ± 15.6 vs. 57.0 ± 12.1; both P < 0.05).

We selected samples with plasma volumes > 3 ml for both miRNA sequencing and subsequent quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) validation experiments. Ultimately, samples from 6 PCNSL patients and 9 GBM patients were sequenced. After sequencing, the plasma sample of 1 PCNSL patient was insufficient for subsequent RT-qPCR experiments; thus, the sample size for RT-qPCR experiments was 54 (PCNSL: 27 and GBM: 27). The current study was carried out with the approval of the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Huanhu Hospital (No: 2021 − 142) and strictly followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Exosome extraction

The sample repository staff prepared plasma samples according to the following standardized methods: 5 ~ 10 ml of blood was collected from each patient using an ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) anticoagulant tube. All the blood samples were centrifuged at 1500×g for 10 min at room temperature. The supernatant plasma was transferred into a clean 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube and stored at −80 ◦C before use.

Exosomes were extracted using an Exosome Isolation and Purification Kit (Umibio; UR52151; Shanghai; China). In accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol, 400 µl of precooled solution A was added to 500 µl of each plasma sample and mixed thoroughly for 30 s. The mixed samples were subsequently centrifuged at 12,000×g for 20 min at 4 ◦C to remove heteroproteins. The supernatant was transferred to a new 1.5 mL centrifuge tube. Then, 120 µl of solution B was added, and the sample was centrifuged at 12,000×g for 15 min at 4 ◦C. The collected precipitate was dissolved in PBS. The suspension was transferred to the an exosome purification filter (EPF) column and centrifuged at 3,000×g for 10 min at 4 ◦C. After centrifugation, the purified exosomes at the bottom of the EPF column were collected and stored at −80 ◦C for subsequent analysis.

Exosome identification

The exosomes were identified using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA). First, 10 µl of exosomes was added onto a copper mesh and incubated for 2 min. Subsequently, 10 µl of 0.3% phosphotungstic acid (pH = 7) was added onto the copper mesh and stained for 2 min. After drying at room temperature, the sample was imaged under a transmission electron microscope.

To measure the exosome particle size and concentration, we performed NTA with Zetaview-PMX120-Z (Particle Metrix, Meerbusch, Germany) and the corresponding software ZetaView (version 8.05.14 SP7). Isolated exosome samples were appropriately diluted with PBS to measure the particle size and concentration. NTA measurements were recorded and analyzed at 11 positions. The ZetaView system was calibrated using 110 nm polystyrene particles. The temperature was maintained at approximately 23 ◦C and 30 ◦C.

RNA extraction and miRNA sequencing

Total RNA from plasma exosomes was extracted by the TRIzol method. Two hundred microliters of the exosome suspension was added to 800 ul of TRIzol reagent, mixed thoroughly, and incubated for 10 min at room temperature, followed by mixing with 200 µl of chloroform. After being incubated for 10 min at room temperature, the sample was centrifuged at 12,000×g for 15 min at 4 ◦C. The supernatant was transferred to a new 1.5 mL RNA-free centrifuge tube and then mixed with an equal volume (500 µl) of isopropanol. The mixture was left at −20 ◦C for 1 h and centrifuged at 12,000×g for 15 min at 4 ◦C. The supernatant was discarded, and 1 mL of precooled 75% alcohol was added to rinse the RNA precipitate, followed by centrifugation at 7,500×g for 5 min at 4 ◦C. The above washing step was repeated, and the sample was air-dried for approximately 2 min until the RNA precipitated. Twenty microliters of diethyl pyrocarbonate (DEPC) water was added to dissolve the RNA, which was subsequently stored at −80 ◦C until use. The RNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop 1000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Approximately 10 ng of RNA from each sample was used for the preparation of an sRNA sequencing library according to the manufacturer’s instructions for the TruSeq Small RNA Sample Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, USA). After library preparation, the constructed libraries were sequenced using an Illumina HiSeq 2000/2500, and the sequencing read length was 50 single-end bp. Data analysis of the miRNAs was performed using ACGT101-miR (version 4.2), and the analysis process was as follows: 1) the 3’ adapter and junk sequences were removed to obtain clean data; 2) sRNA length screening was performed as follows: the retention length ranged from 18 to 26 nt; 3) comparison and analysis of various RNA databases were performed as follows: the remaining sequences were compared to mRNA, RFam and Repbase databases (without miRNA) and filtered; 4) miRNA identification was performed as follows: the reads were obtained after length filtering, RFam, and other database filtering, and the precursors and genomes for miRNA identification were compared; 5) differential expression analysis of the miRNAs was performed as follows: the input data were normalized (norm values). Significant differences between two groups were analyzed using Student’s t test. Owing to the small sample size for sequencing and the low abundance of miRNAs in exosomes, we identified differentially expressed miRNAs according to the following criteria: (I) a fold change > 1.5 (upregulated miRNAs) or a fold change < 0.67 (downregulated miRNAs); (II) both P < 0.05 and an FDR-adjusted P value < 0.05; and 6) target gene prediction: TargetScan (version 5.0) and miRanda (version 3.3a) were used to predict the target genes of the differentially expressed miRNAs [11, 12]. The intersection of these two software programs was taken as the final target genes (TargetScan_score ≥ 50 and miRanda_ Energy < −10).

The procedure of candidate miRNA screening was as follows: (1) cDNA was optimized at a 20-fold dilution on the basis of the qPCR preamplification efficiency, indicating a validation limit of 10 miRNAs; (2) on the basis of the proportion of upregulated to downregulated miRNAs identified by sequencing, the validation cohort was stratified to include 3 upregulated and 7 downregulated candidates; (3) miRNA candidates were prioritized according to the following criteria: (I) selection of miRNAs located in the outer edges of the upper quadrants of the volcano plot, indicating high confidence in differential expression; and (II) miRNAs exhibiting an average raw read count > 1,000 across both PCNSL and GBM cohorts in sequencing data.

Reverse transcription and RT-qPCR

RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using a miRNA 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme; MR201; Nanjing; China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RT-qPCR was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme; Q711; Nanjing; China). The total reaction volume was 20 µl, which included 9.2 µl of cDNA dilution solution, 10 µl of 2×qPCR Master Mix, 0.4 µl of forward primers, and 0.4 µl of reverse primers. The thermal cycling program for PCR was as follows: initial denaturation at 95 ◦C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 ◦C for 10 s and then one cycle of 60 ◦C for 1 min. Each experiment was repeated 3 times on a Light Cycler 480II (Roche, Switzerland), and the relative expression level of the miRNAs was calculated by the 2−∆∆CT formula. MiR-16-5p was used as the endogenous control [13]. The forward primer sequence for miR-16-5p was 5’-CCGCTAGCAGCACGTAAATATTGGCG-3’, and the forward primer sequences of the 10 candidate miRNAs are listed in Table 1. The universal reverse primer in the kit was used as the reverse primer. For the RT-qPCR analysis of EGFR expression, the forward primer sequence for GAPDH was 5’-GGCGTGAACCACGAGAAGTAT-3’, and the reverse primer sequences for GAPDH was 5’-CCTCCACGATGCCAAGTG-3’.

Immunohistochemical analysis of target genes

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded samples from 11 PCNSL patients (median age, 62 years; age range, 51 ~ 68 years) and 13 GBM patients (median age, 63 years; age range, 50 ~ 70 years) were used. The paraffin-embedded brain tissues were cut into slides, and immunohistochemistry was performed on a YN-05MY Automatic immunohistochemical staining system (YongNian, China) with an anti-EGFR antibody (Cat No. ab52894; Abcam, Cambridge, London, UK).

Cell culture and transfection of differentially expressed miRNAs

LN229 cells were purchased from iCell Bioscience, Inc., and incubated in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) in a humidified incubator at 37 ◦C with 5% CO2. NC mimics, miR-4433b-5p mimics, and miR-148a-3p mimics were transfected into LN229 cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

Western blot analysis and luciferase reporter assay

Proteins from transfected cells were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with PMSF (Solarbio Co., Beijing, China). The proteins were then separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). After blocking with 5% nonfat milk, the membranes were incubated with antibodies against EGFR (Cat No. ab52894; Abcam, Cambridge, London, UK) or β-Actin (Cat No. 66009-1-Ig; Proteintech, Chicago, USA). Afterwards, the membranes were incubated with goat anti-rabbit IgG HRP (Cat No. abs20040; Absin, Shanghai, China) for 90 min at room temperature and treated with enhanced chemiluminescence reagents for visualization.

We commissioned Hanbio Biotechnology Co., Ltd. to perform the dual luciferase reporter assay.

HEK293T cells were transiently transfected with the pSI-Check2 plasmids with the wild-type (WT) or mutant (MUT) 3’-UTR fragment of EGFR, together with miR-4433b-5p or miR-148a-3p. After 48 h of cell transfection, the luciferase reporter gene activity was measured using a dual luciferase reporter assay system (Promega, Madison, WI, USA).

Statistical analysis

Quantitative variables are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs) or medians (P25 ~ P75). Qualitative variables are presented as proportions. Quantitative data that fit a normal distribution were compared using Student’s t test; otherwise, the Mann-Whitney test was used. Group comparisons for qualitative data were compared using the chi-square test. A receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC curve) and area under the curve (AUC) were used to evaluate the actual diagnosis of differential miRNAs. Statistical data were analyzed with SPSS software (SPSS Inc., version 23.0). P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Bioinformatics analysis was performed by R software (version 4.3.3).

Validation of differentially expressed miRNAs by RT-qPCR

In total, 27 PCNSL patients and 27 GBM patients were included in the validation experiment. As shown in Fig. 3, the expression levels of plasma exosomal hsa-miR-148a-3p (Fig. 3A) and hsa-let-7f-5p (Fig. 3B) were upregulated in the PCNSL group compared with those in the GBM group (P = 0.019 and P = 0.036, respectively), which was consistent with the results of miRNA sequencing. The expression levels of hsa-miR-345-5p (Fig. 3D) and hsa-miR-4433b-5p (Fig. 3J) were also significantly higher in the PCNSL group than in the GBM group (P = 0.009 and P = 0.001, respectively). The expression levels of the other 6 miRNAs were not significantly different.

Evaluation of the diagnostic effects of differentially expressed miRNAs

To test the clinical diagnostic effectiveness of the differentially expressed miRNAs validated by RT-qPCR, we performed ROC curve analysis. As shown in Fig. 4, the separate AUCs for hsa-miR-148a-3p, hsa-let-7f-5p, hsa-miR-345-5p, and hsa-miR-4433b-5p were 0.686, 0.667, 0.706, and 0.765, respectively. The highest AUC value among the two-miRNA combinations was achieved by the pair of hsa-miR-148a-3p and hsa-miR-345-5p (AUC = 0.789; Fig. 4F). For the three-miRNA combinations, the optimal performance was observed for the combination of hsa-miR-148a-3p, hsa-miR-345-5p, and hsa-miR-4433b-5p, with a superior AUC of 0.791 (Fig. 4M).

Target gene prediction and validation analysis

TargetScan and miRanda were used to predict the target genes of the differentially expressed miRNAs. The details of the target gene prediction of the identified miRNAs are displayed in Table S1. When the target genes of the above 4 differentially expressed miRNAs were analyzed, we discovered that both hsa-miR-148a-3p and hsa-miR-4433b-5p possessed binding sites with the EGFR gene, and the TargetScan score value of the EGFR gene as the target gene of hsa-miR-4433b-5p was 95.

Next, we performed immunohistochemistry analysis to detect the expression of EGFR in PCNSL and GBM (Fig. 5A-B; Table 2). We found that the expression level of EGFR in PCNSL was significantly lower than that in GBM. The distribution of the EGFR expression level significantly differed between the 2 groups (P = 0.006) (Table 2). The proportions of strongly expressed (+++, > 90%) and moderately expressed (++, 30%−90%) genes in the PCNSL group were lower than those in the GBM group (9.1% vs. 46.2%, and 18.2% vs. 46.2%, respectively). The details of the immunohistochemical staining slices and the expression level of EGFR for each sample are shown in Fig. S1.

Expression level of EGFR in PCNSL and GBM

Western blot analysis and RT-qPCR analysis of EGFR expression levels in LN229 cells revealed that miR-4433b-5p and miR-148a-3p downregulated EGFR expression (Fig. 5C-E). The binding relationship between the above two MiRNAs and EGFR was verified by luciferase reporter assay, in which the 3’-UTR of EGFR was fused with a gene encoding luciferase. The results showed that the relative luciferase activity of HEK293T cells transfected with EGFR-WT was notably suppressed by miR-4433b-5p (P < 0.001), suggesting that EGFR serves as a potential target of miR-4433b-5p (Fig. 5F). Compared with the NC group, miR-148a-3p failed to downregulate the expression of EGFR-WT luciferase (P > 0.05).

Discussion

It is crucial to distinguish PCNSL from GBM before surgical treatment is performed. The aim of our research was to identify discriminative exosomal miRNAs in the peripheral blood of PCNSL patients and GBM patients by sequencing. When conventional preoperative diagnostic methods fail to differentiate PCNSL and GBM, exosomal miRNA indicators provide neurosurgeons with rational guidance for precise intraoperative surgical approaches and postoperative treatment options.

MicroRNAs are small noncoding RNAs involved in many biological processes such as cell growth and development and the regulation of many cellular functions [14, 15]. Previous studies have discussed the diagnostic value of miRNAs from tissues, cells, cerebrospinal fluid, and blood in differentiating PCNSL from glioma and GBM. They reported that miR-21, miR-16, miR-205, miR-15b and miR-301 could aid in discriminating between glioma, especially GBM, and PCNSL [16–20]. However, studies differentiating PCNSL and GBM using exosomal miRNAs have not yet been effectively reported. Exosomes are cellular exocytotic vesicles of 30 ~ 100 nm in size with a lipid bilayer membrane structure. Studies have shown that exosomes are secreted into the extracellular space in a selective manner and that exosomal miRNAs are more sensitive and specific than total cellular miRNAs or free miRNAs are [21, 22]. In addition, the lipid membrane of exosomes provides a favorable structure for the preservation of the contained miRNA, ensuring its intact sequence structure and biological activity. We also considered that PCNSL is more likely to enter the blood circulation than other solid tumors are and blood samples are easier to access and less invasive than tissue samples are; therefore, we chose exosomal miRNAs isolated from plasma as our target indicator.

The results from TEM and NTA indicated that the exosomes we extracted from plasma were fairly pure. Subsequent miRNA sequencing revealed a considerable number of differentially expressed miRNAs. According to our RT-qPCR findings, plasma exosomal hsa-miR-148a-3p was significantly upregulated in the PCNSL group compared with the GBM group. To the best of our knowledge, previous studies have not reported any differences in the expression level of miR-148a between PCNSL and GBM plasma exosomes. However, the expression of miR-148a-3p was higher in the tumor tissues of patients with GBM than in nonneoplastic brain tissues, and miR-148a-3p expression was significantly higher in glioma cell line-derived exosomes than in human primary astrocyte-derived exosomes. Both GBM cellderived miR-148a and circulating exosomal miR-148a from GBM patients can promote tumor cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and metastasis by targeting different signaling pathways, such as the EGFR/MAPK and CADM1/STAT3 signaling pathways [23–25]. With respect to other differentially expressed miRNAs that were upregulated in the PCNSL group compared with the GBM group, as validated by both sequencing and RT-qPCR, let-7f-5p has not yet been mentioned in PCNSL- and glioma- related studies. However, let-7f-5p may be a robust plasma biomarker for the diagnosis of sporadic amyotrophic lateral sclerosis disease and mild cognitive impairment [26, 27]. More evidence is needed to validate the diagnostic value of let-7f-5p for PCNSL and GBM.

According to the sequencing results, the expression levels of miR-345-5p and miR-4433b-5p were significantly lower in the PCNSL group than in the GBM group. Interestingly, the expression pattern of these genes was inversely upregulated according to RT-qPCR. We subsequently performed a subgroup analysis of miR-345-5p and miR-4433b-5p expression in the miRNA-seq cohort. The results were in accordance with the qPCR validation results from the overall study population (Fig. S3). While a perfect concordance between sequencing and qPCR is ideal, technical variations between the two platforms are well-documented. Quantitative PCR is widely regarded as the gold standard for validating differential expression due to its superior sensitivity and quantitative accuracy [28, 29]. Following rigorous validation of the following qPCR experimental parameters: primer specificity, stability of endogenous control genes, amplification efficiency, and reproducibility, the results are therefore considered the definitive findings for these miRNAs. The initial sequencing data served its purpose effectively in generating hypotheses and identifying candidate miRNAs.

To test the clinical diagnostic effectiveness of the differentially expressed miRNAs validated by RT-qPCR, we performed ROC curve analysis. The diagnostic utility of hsa-miR-148a-3p, hsa-let-7f-5p, and hsa-miR-345-5p appeared to be limited, and the diagnostic value of hsa-miR-4433b-5p was relatively high (AUC = 0.765). The combined use of multiple miRNAs, especially the combination of hsa-miR-148a-3p, hsa-miR-345-5p, and hsa-miR-4433b-5p, significantly enhanced diagnostic performance, achieving an AUC value of 0.791. This combinatorial panel showed promising clinical applicability as a biomarker for differentiating PCNSL from GBM.

We discovered that miR-148a-3p and miR-4433b-5p possessed binding sites for the EGFR gene during target gene prediction. The immunohistochemistry analysis also revealed that the expression level of EGFR in PCNSL was significantly lower than that in GBM. Therefore, we hypothesized that the increased expression level of EGFR in GBM may result from the negative regulatory effects of miR-148a-3p and miR-4433b-5p. Combining the results of western blot and luciferase reporter assay, the binding relationship between miR-4433b-5p and EGFR was verified. These data suggest that the higher expression level of miR-4433b-5p in the plasma exosomes of PCNSL than GBM may be achieved by its negative regulating effects on EGFR expression. EGFR can be used as a potential biomarker for distinguishing PCNSL from GBM.

The present study still has several limitations: (1) Owing to the lower prevalence and the difficulty in obtaining PCNSL samples in comparison to those of GBM, the sample size for sequencing and validation experiments was relatively limited. Nevertheless, basic demographic characteristics were maintained between the two groups. (2) Few previous studies have compared the expression levels of plasma exosomal miRNAs in PCNSL and GBM, and we may not discuss this topic thoroughly. (3) Prior to practical clinical application, the identified miRNA biomarkers still require further validation in independent external cohorts and more in-depth mechanistic investigations.

Future research should comprehensively explore the multidimensional value of plasma exosomal miRNAs in differentiating PCNSL from GBM, monitoring treatment response, and developing interventions. For instance, their early diagnostic performance should be validated in large prospective cohorts, dynamic changes should be tracked over time to enable personalized assessment of therapeutic efficacy, and the pathogenic mechanisms of critical miRNAs should be elucidated. However, translating these promising biomarkers to clinical practice still requires the following critical challenges: implementing standardized protocols across the entire workflow from sample processing to data analysis and confirming clinical robustness through multicenter studies.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that plasma exosomal hsa-miR-148a-3p, hsa-miR-345-5p, and hsa-miR-4433b-5p were significantly upregulated in the PCNSL group compared with the GBM group, and that the combined panel showed promising clinical applicability, suggesting that these genes might be novel biomarkers for differentiating PCNSL and GBM. The increased expression level of EGFR in GBM may be achieved by the negative regulatory effects of miR-4433b-5p. The diagnostic performance of the miRNA biomarkers still needs to be further verified in larger sample size cohorts.
