Distinct correlates of post-traumatic stress disorder and subthreshold post-traumatic stress disorder considering a broad range of adverse experiences
Authors: Celeste Minn Tan, Vanessa Ai Ling Seet, Rachel Hsiao Shen Tan, Sherilyn Shi Hui Chang, Yoke Boon Tan, Phern-Chern Tor, Charmaine Yu Zheng Tang, Yu Wei Lee, Nisha Chandwani, Swapna Verma, Mythily Subramaniam, Jianlin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: While not all trauma-exposed individuals develop post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), many experience severe and enduring emotional effects. In Singapore, no study has investigated the prevalence and correlates of subthreshold PTSD and PTSD using gold-standard clinical interviews.Objectives: This study was the first to examine the prevalence and correlates of PTSD, subthreshold PTSD, and correlates associated while exploring a broad range of adverse experiences, including Criterion A (e.g. physical and sexual abuse) and non-Criterion A (e.g. emotional abuse) events.Method: We recruited 500 outpatients, aged 21–65, with mood or schizophrenia spectrum disorders from Singapore’s only tertiary psychiatric hospital. Self-report measures assessed emotion dysregulation, suppression, and total trauma events. Clinical interviews, like the Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale (CAPS-5), measured dissociation and PTSD, while the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale measured negative affect. Among trauma-exposed individuals (n = 461), multinomial regression examined differences between subthreshold PTSD, PTSD and the no-PTSD reference group, with analyses conducted separately for Criterion A and non-Criterion A events.Results: In the overall sample, PTSD prevalence was 15.0% and subthreshold PTSD was 18.4% among individuals with an index event classified as Criterion A. PTSD following an index event classified as Criterion A was associated with a broader range of correlates than index events classified as non-Criterion A. Conversely, among individuals exposed to events classified as non-Criterion A, PTSD was primarily associated with emotional correlates when subthreshold PTSD was defined as ‘two of four criteria.’ PTSD is also associated with a broader set of correlates than subthreshold PTSD across exposure types and subthreshold PTSD definitions. Correlates of subthreshold PTSD vary depending on exposure type. Negative affect was most frequently associated with multiple definitions of subthreshold PTSD and PTSD across trauma types.Conclusion: Findings suggest distinct symptom profiles by trauma type and emphasise the clinical relevance of non-Criterion A events and subthreshold PTSD.KEYWORDS: Subthreshold PTSD, prevalence, PTSD correlates, non-Criterion A, CAPS-5

Introduction

According to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR), a formal diagnosis of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), requires exposure to a Criterion A trauma, defined as directly experiencing, witnessing, or repeatedly being exposed to events that involve actual or threatened death, serious injury or sexual injury (American Psychiatric Association, 2022). However, clinically significant post-traumatic stress symptoms are commonly observed in clinical settings among individuals exposed to adverse experiences that do not qualify as Criterion A traumas, such as emotional abuse and the death of a loved one due to illness (Marx et al., 2024). This discrepancy between diagnostic criteria and clinical presentation raises important questions regarding individuals who experience clinically meaningful distress but fall outside diagnostic boundaries and remain untreated for traumatic stress. Compounding this issue, a growing body of research has demonstrated that individuals with subthreshold PTSD often remain undetected even though they experience clinical levels of distress, impairment and have higher suicidality (Kim et al., 2020; McLaughlin et al., 2015). Subthreshold PTSD has been conceptualised as exposure to a traumatic event, with associated distress and functional impairment from PTSD symptoms, but not meeting full diagnostic criteria (Biscoe et al., 2025). The subthreshold PTSD presentation might be potentially meaningful in cases where stressors do not meet DSM-5-TR Criterion A definitions. Such individuals might experience clinically consequential post-traumatic stress symptoms even though they may fall short of PTSD diagnostic criteria. Thus far, limited research has investigated the intersection between subthreshold PTSD and non-Criterion A stressors.

Although subthreshold PTSD is broadly understood as not having met full PTSD diagnostic criteria, there is currently no consensus in the trauma literature on a standard definition for measurement. The four commonly recognised subthreshold PTSD definitions based on the DSM-5-TR criteria with prevalence estimates include: fulfilling Criteria B (re-experiencing) and C (avoidance) along with either D (negative alterations in cognition and mood) or E (hyperarousal) (4.8%), meeting three of four PTSD criteria (12.0%), meeting two of four criteria (17.1%) and the presence of any six PTSD symptoms across the four criteria (13.4%) (Klein et al., 2024). Of which, meeting ‘two of four criteria’ and ‘three of four criteria’ are the most widely empirically supported definitions as seen from population-based data across 13 countries collected by the World Health Organisation (McLaughlin et al., 2015). McLaughlin’s research (2015) revealed that for both definitions of subthreshold PTSD, symptom severity is lower than PTSD, which is expected given the fewer number of PTSD criteria met. Both definitions also have their merits. For one, the ‘two of four criteria’ definition consistently identifies a larger group of trauma-exposed people with clinically meaningful trauma-related distress and impairment relative to the other three definitions (Kim et al., 2020; McLaughlin et al., 2015). In contrast, the ‘three of four criteria’ definition approximates PTSD more closely and is associated with higher suicidality than the ‘two of four criteria’ definition (Klein et al., 2024). No studies have examined the prevalence or correlates of any subthreshold PTSD definition in Singapore.

In considering individuals whose symptoms fall short of a PTSD diagnosis, it is necessary to study the nature of the precipitating stressor, particularly the distinction between Criterion A and non-Criterion A. The clinical relevance of non-Criterion A events remains an unresolved controversy, inviting intense debate even in recent conceptual PTSD reviews (Brewin et al., 2025; Marx et al., 2024). Criterion A was revised in the DSM-5 to remove the subjective response element to improve PTSD diagnosis reliability, thereby narrowing the range of eligible traumatic events (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). However, a considerable body of research has repeatedly documented that PTSD symptoms can develop following non-Criterion A stressors, including receiving a serious medical diagnosis (Swartzman et al., 2017) and psychosis (Buswell et al., 2021). A clinical implication of adhering strictly to Criterion A is that individuals who experience these serious events will not qualify for a formal PTSD diagnosis and might not receive trauma-focused treatment even if they experience substantial impairment (Howard et al., 2024). Conversely, proponents of the current boundaries of Criterion A have argued that it constitutes the basis of a PTSD diagnosis and without a qualifying event, core symptoms such as intrusion and avoidance are difficult to contextualise (Weathers & Keane, 2007). Meta-analytic evidence also suggests that Criterion A events are associated with slightly higher PTSD symptom severity than non-Criterion A events, supporting meaningful differences between exposure types (Larsen & Pacella, 2016).

These debates are especially relevant in Singapore, as emotional neglect and abuse are the nation’s most prevalent adverse childhood experiences (ACES) and highly associated with mental disorders (Subramaniam et al., 2020). Singapore’s context of high societal safety with low crime and violence (Institute for Economics & Peace, 2025) may heighten the prominence of non-Criterion A stressors relative to other settings. Despite its clinical relevance, no study has examined how correlates of PTSD and subthreshold PTSD differ for Criterion A and non-Criterion A events. A review also highlighted the need for methodologically rigorous comparisons investigating how PTSD presents following more objective and subjective traumatic events (Brewin et al., 2025). Existing studies examining a direct comparison between both trauma types have relied on self-report measures instead of clinical interviews to determine clinical cut-offs for PTSD diagnosis and severity (Marx et al., 2024). This is a major limitation in current research as clinical interviews are important to detect participants' reporting inconsistencies and misunderstandings.

As such, the study’s first goal was to assess the prevalence of PTSD and subthreshold PTSD using a gold-standard clinical interview – the Clinician Administered PTSD Scale for DSM-5 (CAPS-5) (Weathers et al., 2018) in a clinical sample. To estimate subthreshold PTSD prevalence, we applied four of the most commonly used definitions: (1) two of four criteria, (2) three of four criteria, (3) any six symptoms, and (4) Criteria B + C + (D or E) (Klein et al., 2024). We also explored prevalence estimates across events classified as Criterion A and non-Criterion A under clinical supervision. We hypothesised that PTSD and subthreshold PTSD prevalence would be similar across both trauma types. Secondly, we investigated correlates of PTSD, subthreshold PTSD, and trauma-exposed individuals without either PTSD or subthreshold PTSD condition, and we also explored patterns based on both trauma types separately. We used a dimensional approach to study PTSD by diagnostic status and a continuous approach to examine PTSD symptom severity to detect variables that differentiate discrete groups and explain variations in severity, respectively. For the dimensional approach, the two most widely supported definitions of subthreshold PTSD, ‘two of four criteria’ and ‘three of four criteria’ (McLaughlin et al., 2015), were used in separate analyses. As there remains no consensus on which definition should be used, our current study presents both results. We hypothesised distinct differences in the number of correlates between PTSD and subthreshold PTSD groups and in the Criterion A and non-Criterion A analysis.

Methods

Participants

Participants were recruited from the outpatient clinic of the Institute of Mental Health, Singapore’s sole tertiary psychiatric institution, from 2022–2024. The study included patients with a primary diagnosis of mood disorder (i.e. depression or bipolar disorder) or schizophrenia-spectrum disorder; were Singapore residents (Singapore Citizens and Permanent Residents); aged 21–65 years old; and able to speak and understand English. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board and the Domain Specific Review Board of the National Healthcare Group of Singapore (2022/00759). We recruited 500 participants with an 88.5% response rate, comprising 250 mood disorder and 250 schizophrenia patients. The same number of patients were recruited in both groups to ensure equal representation and prevent findings from being disproportionately driven by one diagnostic group.

Procedure

All participants provided written informed consent prior to study commencement. Sociodemographic variables like age, sex, ethnicity, education, marital status and employment, and clinical variables like emotion dysregulation, worry, rumination, and emotional suppression were first measured via self-report. Following that, all participants were clinically interviewed by trained interviewers to assess negative affectivity using the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale-Expanded (Lukoff et al., 1986; BPRS-E). Lifetime exposure to trauma and total number of trauma events were then self-reported using the Stressful Life Events Screening Questionnaire (Goodman et al., 1998; SLESQ) to lower barriers to disclosing sensitive information. If participants experienced at least one event, interviewers would verbally verify which event was perceived as their worst, and this was designated as their index event for subsequent PTSD assessment. This portion of the questionnaire was verbally administered rather than self-reported so interviewers could personally confirm the worst event. Individuals were then classified into the Criterion A or non-Criterion A sample based on their index event. This classification was done post-assessment in consultation with the study’s clinical supervisor, a doctoral level psychologist. Only participants exposed to an adverse event were interviewed to assess PTSD severity and diagnostic status. Finally, psychiatric and mental comorbidities were extracted from participants' medical records after the interview.

To determine interrater reliability for all clinical interviews, the study team’s clinical supervisor, sat in with each interviewer and independently assessed each participant for up to 10 sessions. To strengthen and corroborate our assessment results, attending clinicians independent of the study team were consulted to provide an independent assessment of trauma and PTSD symptoms of the participants. We also conducted ongoing supervision and calibration meetings to prevent rater drift.

Measures

The BPRS-E and SLESQ have been used among patients with mental disorders in Singapore (Liu et al., 2021). This is the first study in Singapore to utilise the CAPS-5 for research; although this has been done in Asia (Kim et al., 2019).

Emotion dysregulation

Global emotion dysregulation was measured with the 18-item Difficulties in Emotion Dysregulation Scale – Short Form (Kaufman et al., 2016) with responses rated from 1 (almost never) to 5 (almost always). We did not use any of the subscales in this scale. Items 1, 4, and 6 were reverse coded. Total scores were computed (range = 1 to 90), and higher scores indicated greater emotion dysregulation. Internal consistency was excellent in this sample (α = 0.91).

Worry

Habitual worry was measured using the 8-item Penn State Worry Questionnaire-Abbreviated (Hopko et al., 2003) using a 5-point scale from 1 (not at all typical of me) to 5 (very typical of me). Worry was computed as a total score (range = 1 to 25). In the present sample, Cronbach alpha was excellent (α = 0.95).

Emotional suppression

The 4-item subscale of the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (Gross & John, 2003) was used to measure emotional suppression on a 7-point scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). The total score for emotional suppression was taken (range = 1 to 28). Internal reliability was acceptable for this scale (α = 0.78).

Rumination

The 10-item Rumination Response Scale Short-form (Treynor et al., 2003) measured habitual rumination from 1 (almost never) to 4 (almost always). Rumination was calculated as a total score (range = 1 to 40). Rumination had a good reliability index (α = 0.84).

Negative affect

The negative affect subscale on the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale-Expanded (Lukoff et al., 1986; BPRS-E) was administered via clinical interview by trained interviewers. This subscale assessed somatic concerns, anxiety, depression and guilt on a 7-point scale from 1 (absent) to 7 (extremely severe). Item scores of 5 and above were indicative of functional impairment and total scores reflected severity. The total score was taken for the negative affect subscale (range = 1 to 28). Interrater reliability among 6 raters was excellent (ICC = 0.94).

Total trauma events, index event and age of index event

Lifetime exposure to 12 potentially traumatic events (e.g. life-threatening accident, emotional abuse) was assessed using the 13-item Stressful Life Events Screening Questionnaire (Goodman et al., 1998). Item 13 was an open-ended question allowing participants to report exposure to events they felt extremely frightened, horrified or helpless, with additional contextual details requested to verify event type if endorsed. If participants experienced at least one event, they were verbally asked to identify the worst event, which would function as their index event. Individuals were classified into the Criterion A or non-Criterion A sample based on their index event in consultation with the team’s clinical supervisor. This questionnaire was selected over other common measures like the Life Events Checklist for DSM-5 (Weathers et al., 2013) because it captures both Criterion A and non-Criterion A events. However, formal index trauma negotiation procedures were not conducted, and classifications of index trauma should be interpreted cautiously. Clinical variables, like total trauma events, were calculated as the total number of events experienced, and age of index trauma was derived from the self-reported age of their index event in the SLESQ.

Subthreshold PTSD, PTSD diagnosis and PTSD symptom severity

If participants were exposed to any adverse event, participants were additionally interviewed with the gold-standard clinical interview, the CAPS-5, to measure PTSD severity and diagnosis. All 6 interviewers were formally trained online and certified by the National Center for PTSD with excellent interrater reliability (ICC = 0.95). To reach PTSD threshold in this study, participants had to have one symptom from Criterium B (re-experiencing), one from Criterium C (avoidance), two from Criterium D (negative alteration in cognition and mood), two from Criterium E (hyperarousal), and fulfil Criteria F (≥ 1 month duration) and G (impairment), regardless of Criterion A. There were 30 interview items. Items measuring criteria B, C, D, E and G were rated from 0 (absent) to 4 (extreme/incapacitating). Severity scores were based on the intensity of the distress and frequency. A severity score of 2 or more indicates that criteria was met for that symptom. We measured subthreshold PTSD based on four common definitions. In addition to diagnostic thresholds, we calculated PTSD symptom severity by totalling symptoms in the 4 main clusters from CAPS-5 items 1 to 20 (Criteria B to E). PTSD symptom severity scores ranged from 0 to 80.

Dissociation

Dissociation was measured using CAPS-5 items 29 and 30 for depersonalisation and derealisation respectively. A score of 2 or more on either of these items was necessary to qualify for PTSD dissociative subtype. The two items were summed to calculate symptom severity for dissociation (range = 0 to 8). Higher scores indicated higher severity.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted with IBM SPSS statistics (version 29.0.1.0). Preliminary analysis included ANOVA, chi-square tests and correlations on all variables. Frequencies estimated the prevalence of PTSD, subthreshold PTSD, trauma-exposed individuals with no PTSD/subthreshold PTSD, and those who were not trauma-exposed. Among the trauma-exposed participants (n = 461), multinomial logistic regression examined clinical and sociodemographic correlates of those in the PTSD, subthreshold PTSD and no PTSD/subthreshold PTSD group. The ‘two of four criteria’ and the ‘three of four criteria’ subthreshold definitions (Klein et al., 2024) were used in separate multinomial regressions. Multilinear regression identified variables associated with PTSD severity, measured as a continuous outcome, instead of discrete severity categories (i.e. subthreshold, no PTSD). Results were reported separately for Criterion A and non-Criterion A index events for all main analyses.

Results

Sociodemographic

The sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of participants (n = 500) are reported in Supplementary Table 1. The mean age of participants was 35.8 (s.d = 11.1) years. Among them, 290 (58.0%) were female; 348 (69.6%) were Chinese, 82 (16.4%) were Malay, 47 (9.4%) were Indian and 23 (4.6%) belonged to other ethnic groups.

Preliminary analysis

Correlation analyses were conducted separately for participants with index events classified as Criterion A (n = 250) and non-Criterion A index events (n = 211) and presented in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3 respectively. ANOVA, post-hoc comparisons and chi-square analyses examining sociodemographic and clinical variables by PTSD status are shown in Supplementary Tables 4–9. In both trauma groups, PTSD status was positively associated with emotion dysregulation, dissociation and total trauma events.

Prevalence

Table 1 shows the prevalence of PTSD and subthreshold PTSD according to the four definitions. In the overall sample, PTSD prevalence was 15.0% and subthreshold PTSD ranged from 4.0% to 18.4% among individuals with index trauma classified as Criterion A. PTSD prevalence was lower at 8.6% and subthreshold PTSD ranged from 4.0% to 14.8% among individuals with index events classified as non-Criterion A. For both trauma types, the definition with the lowest prevalence was ‘Criteria B + C + (D or E)’ and the highest prevalence was the ‘two of four criteria’ definition. Within the whole sample, 23.6% met criteria for PTSD and 7.8% of the sample were not exposed to any trauma.

Multilinear regression

Table 4 summarises the multiple regression results with PTSD symptom severity as the outcome. Criterion A and non-Criterion A events were examined separately. For the non-Criterion A analysis, PTSD symptom severity was associated with greater emotion dysregulation (β = 0.18, p < .01), more dissociation (β = 0.27, p < .001), greater number of trauma events (β = 0.11, p = .02), and more negative affect (β = 0.42, p < .001). These variables were similarly associated with PTSD symptom severity in individuals with index trauma classified as Criterion A. However, additional correlates included younger age (β = −0.18, p < .001), non-Chinese ethnicity (β = 0.08, p = .02), greater emotional suppression (β = 0.10, p = .01), and less worry (β = –0.14, p = .01).

Discussion

This is the first study to examine the prevalence and correlates of subthreshold PTSD and PTSD using a gold-standard clinical interview according to whether the index event was classified as Criterion A and non-Criterion A. PTSD prevalence was higher among those with index events classified as Criterion A (15.0%) compared to non-Criterion A events (8.6%), aligning with a recent umbrella review reporting 23.95% considering both trauma types (Schincariol et al., 2024). The highest subthreshold PTSD prevalence was observed when utilising the ‘two of four criteria’ definition (18.4%) and lowest with ‘Criteria B + C + (D or E)’ (4.0%), consistent with other studies (Kim et al., 2020; Klein et al., 2024). These rates were alarmingly similar to findings in a trauma-exposed veterans sample, with 17.1% meeting ‘two of four criteria’ and 4.8% meeting ‘Criteria B + C + (D or E)’ definitions (Klein et al., 2024). While participants in our sample were not exposed to war, the comparable distress and impairment highlight the significant clinical burden of subthreshold PTSD and the need to study these symptoms in clinical populations. These prevalence estimates should also be interpreted within the context of this psychiatric sample, as existing psychiatric conditions may influence trauma reporting and PTSD symptom presentation due to possible symptom overlap with negative mood and dissociative symptoms. We expect that prevalence estimates will be lower in the general population and higher in inpatient samples. Nonetheless, given subthreshold prevalence estimates of 8.0–33.2% across trauma types in outpatients, our findings highlight the importance of subthreshold PTSD in this setting. Taken together, these results underscore the clinical relevance of subthreshold PTSD screening and provision of targeted trauma informed care within psychiatric outpatient settings.

PTSD was also associated with a broader range of correlates than subthreshold PTSD for both trauma types and both subthreshold PTSD definitions. This is in line with previous studies that have consistently replicated distinct differences in terms of functional outcomes between PTSD and subthreshold PTSD considering Criterion A index trauma (Kim et al., 2020; Klein et al., 2024). Models investigating those with index events classified as Criterion A identified a broader range of correlates for PTSD status and associations with PTSD severity than non-Criterion A models in all multinomial and multiple regression analyses. A similar pattern was observed for both definitions of subthreshold PTSD, where a broader set of correlates was observed for those with index events classified as Criterion A compared to non-Criterion A. These results should be interpreted cautiously given the limitation in Criterion A classification procedures. However, these results are also consistent with previous studies indicating that although non-Criterion A events can elicit PTSD symptoms, Criterion A events are associated with higher post-traumatic stress symptoms (Larsen & Pacella, 2016). The presence of a greater number of correlates for PTSD and subthreshold PTSD among participants with events classified as Criterion A compared to non-Criterion A, highlights the possibility that there is increased psychological complexity and a broader range of treatment targets for the Criterion A group.

Our findings may suggest that emotional correlates were more prominent among participants with PTSD following index events classified as non-Criterion A when the subthreshold PTSD definition was relaxed to ‘two of four criteria’. From the multinomial regression with subthreshold PTSD defined as ‘two of four criteria’, associations with PTSD among those with an index event classified as non-Criterion A, were with fewer and mainly emotional factors such as negative affect and emotion dysregulation, providing preliminary evidence for affective processes driving this relationship. One possible reason emotional correlates are especially salient in the non-Criterion A sample is that such events can cause overwhelming emotional strain leading to associated difficulties with regulating emotions over time (Cloitre et al., 2019). While non-Criterion A events might not be immediately life-threatening, negative emotional reactions elicited by these events could contribute to PTSD symptoms (Georgescu et al., 2025). This aligns with Cognitive Models of PTSD, which emphasise that reactions to the event rather than the event itself, determine the onset and maintenance of PTSD (Ehlers & Clark, 2000). Longitudinal research could clarify if an emotional pathway underlies PTSD in non-Criterion A cases. Taken together, the multinomial regression findings suggest that different patterns of correlates can be observed across index events classified as Criterion A and non-Criterion A. Further research using more rigorous Criterion A assessments is needed to establish the role of both Criterion A and non-Criterion A index events in the prognosis of PTSD.

Regarding clinical correlates, when subthreshold PTSD is defined as ‘two of four criteria’, emotion dysregulation, negative affect, dissociation, and total trauma events are significant subthreshold PTSD correlates for those in the Criterion A group. However, when the definition was tightened to ‘three of four criteria’, only dissociation and total trauma events remained significant. For those with index trauma classified as Criterion A, dissociation was a consistent correlate for subthreshold PTSD across both definitions, and for PTSD. Dissociation was also one of the most strongly associated variables with PTSD severity for both trauma groups. Additionally, for those who experienced index events classified as non-Criterion A, negative affect was a significant correlate for both definitions of subthreshold PTSD. Negative affect was also consistently associated with PTSD in the multinomial regression and had the strongest influence on PTSD symptom severity in the multilinear regression for both trauma types. Given that negative affect and dissociation were significantly associated with both measurements of PTSD as symptom severity and across PTSD thresholds, regardless of trauma type, this strengthens evidence that they are important transdiagnostic risk factors in psychopathology development (Díaz-García et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2025). As such, screening and interventions targeting negative affectivity and dissociation could be useful for individuals with a trauma history in preventing worsening PTSD symptoms. For example, trauma-specific skill-based sequential interventions like Skills Training in Affective and Interpersonal Regulation (STAIR) may be beneficial for addressing chronic emotional coping difficulties and dissociation through techniques like distress tolerance and grounding (Cavicchioli et al., 2021; Cloitre & Schmidt, 2015).

Ethnicity was the strongest correlate of PTSD diagnosis among participants with events classified as Criterion A, with non-Chinese ethnicity associated with four times higher odds of PTSD, as shown in Table 2. National data from Singapore revealed that non-Chinese ethnicity was associated with more ACEs, such as physical neglect, emotional neglect and growing up with household members with mental illnesses during childhood (Subramaniam et al., 2020). Given the established association between ACEs and mental health outcomes including PTSD (Subramaniam et al., 2020; Umar et al., 2025), differences in early life adverse experiences may contribute to the observed patterns. In a multi-ethnic population like Singapore’s, it is particularly crucial to consider culturally informed assessments and treatments since cultural factors can substantially influence PTSD development (Bernardi et al., 2019).

This study has several limitations that should be considered. Firstly, the study involved a clinical population with mood and schizophrenia-spectrum disorders, and results may not be generalisable to non-clinical populations or clinical populations with other primary mental disorders. Secondly, as this was a cross-sectional study, causality cannot be determined. Results should be interpreted cautiously as some significant 95% confidence intervals in the multinomial regression were close to 1.00 (e.g. emotion dysregulation: lower bound = 1.004; Table 2). Nonetheless, the findings offer valuable direction for future longitudinal research on relevant PTSD and subthreshold PTSD correlates. Additionally, the study did not include formal procedures to negotiate the index trauma, in line with best-practice standards, which can affect the accuracy of Criterion A determination. As a result, it is possible that more ambiguous events may have been misclassified with respect to Criterion A and this study’s results provide preliminary evidence of differences between exposure types. Moving forward, more in-depth interviews allowing for the negotiation of the index event such as the index event form (IEF) (Bovin et al., 2026) can be used to increase the accuracy of identifying the worst event and Criterion A determination. This measure has not been tested in Asia and is a valuable way to advance PTSD research quality. However, this paper has several strengths, such as using a gold-standard clinical interview to measure PTSD in a large clinical sample in an outpatient setting. This paper is also the first to identify correlates of multiple definitions of subthreshold PTSD, exploring events classified as Criterion A and non-Criterion A according to clinical guidelines under clinical supervision. Furthermore, we assessed for PTSD symptom severity and diagnosis to conduct Singapore’s first comprehensive PTSD study.

In conclusion, the prevalence of PTSD and subthreshold PTSD is high among Singapore’s outpatient mental health population underscoring the significant burden of trauma-related distress and PTSD’s relevance in non-war contexts. This study carries important implications for mental healthcare systems, highlighting the value of integrating trauma-informed care and providing preliminary evidence on the clinical impact of non-Criterion A events. Lastly, in line with Singapore’s national shift towards preventive care (Ministry of Health Singapore, 2023), early intervention for subthreshold PTSD and research on prognosis can be prioritised.
