Association of modified 5C scale and influenza vaccine hesitancy among the elderly population in Ningbo, China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Zhanghang Zhu, Hongbo Shi, Xingqiang Pan, Jianmei Wang, Danjie Jiang, Xuefei Zhao, Yan Zhang, Dandan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSeasonal influenza imposes disproportionate morbidity and mortality burdens on elderly populations globally, with suboptimal vaccination coverage persisting as a critical public health challenge. Despite policy advancements including China’s 2025 elderly influenza vaccination target, persistent gaps necessitate deeper understanding of influenza vaccine hesitancy determinants in high-risk cohorts.MethodsThis cross-sectional study assessed influenza vaccine hesitancy among 2,288 community-dwelling adults aged ≥ 60 years in Ningbo, China (February–March 2025). Validated 5C psychological antecedent scales (Confidence, Complacency, Convenience, Calculation, Collective Responsibility) were administered alongside sociodemographic and cognitive assessments. Multivariable logistic regression identified determinants of hesitancy (defined as vaccine refusal/delay), reporting adjusted Odds Ratios (ORs) with 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs). We calculated the proportion of hesitant respondents indicating a potential change in vaccination intention for each motivator.ResultsThe prevalence of influenza vaccine hesitancy among the elderly population in Ningbo was 32.4% (741/2288). We found that female (OR = 1.30, 95%CI: 1.00–1.70) was significantly associated with higher odds of hesitancy and urban residence (OR = 0.74, 95%CI: 0.56–0.98) and influenza-vaccine-related cognition (OR = 0.66, 95%CI: 0.50–0.86) were significantly associated with lower odds of hesitancy. The results showed that having no history of influenza vaccination (OR = 12.01, 95% CI:9.36–15.50) was most strongly associated with higher odds of influenza vaccine hesitancy. Lower confidence (OR = 0.82, 95%CI: 0.79–0.85) and higher complacency (OR = 1.14, 95%CI: 1.11–1.17) was associated with higher odds of influenza vaccine hesitancy. Among influenza vaccine hesitators, a relative/decedent’s recommendation would convince 26.7% to be vaccinated, far exceeding a physician’s advice (18.9%). Personal infection fear and perceived high exposure persuaded 22.4% and 19.6%, respectively.ConclusionNearly one-third of elderly exhibit influenza influenza vaccine hesitancy, driven by intersecting demographic, cultural, and policy factors. The counterintuitive role of collective responsibility highlights the need for culturally adapted interventions. We propose family-inclusive education, age-targeted digital outreach, and health system integration to overcome barriers. Introduction

Seasonal influenza remains one of the most underestimated yet formidable threats to global health. Each year, the World Health Organization (WHO) attributes 3–5 million cases of severe illness and 290,000–650,000 respiratory deaths to the virus—a significant number of deaths and disabilities each year [1]. The burden may lead to severe illness or even death in the elderly imposing a heavy burden on individuals, families, and society by immunosenescence and cardiorespiratory comorbidities [2]. Vaccination remains the primary defense against these outcomes and is crucial for reducing the overall public health impact of seasonal epidemics [3]. Globally, and notably in China, vaccination prevalence among seniors consistently lag behind targets, highlighting significant gaps in implementation and uptake that demand urgent attention [4].

China’s three-decade struggle with influenza immunisation illustrates the chasm between policy ambition and ground-level reality. Pilot programmes in the 1990 s focused almost exclusively on children [5], only in 2007 did national guidelines formally elevate adults ≥ 60 years to priority status [6]. Despite post-H1N1 pandemic expansions (e.g., Beijing’s free elderly program reaching 43% coverage by 2011 [7], national rates stagnated below 10% pre-2018 due to fragmented funding and supply constraints [8]. The 2018 pivot to quadrivalent and adjuvanted high-dose formulations, framed within the “Healthy China 2030” vision of 30% elderly coverage by 2025, promised a new chapter [9]. Yet systemic inertia prevailed: by 2023, only twelve of thirty-one provinces offered no-cost vaccination, and national coverage crawled to 24.1%—one-third of the WHO target [10]. This long-standing gap in influenza vaccination coverage has significant human and social impacts, especially in winter.

Influenza vaccine hesitancy is an important factor affecting vaccination coverage—characterized by delayed acceptance or refusal of available vaccines [11] —affects 23–38% of older adults globally [12], with distinct drivers in China. Seniors exhibit pervasive misconceptions, compounded by socio-cultural barriers: historical medical mistrust [13], Confucian fatalism [14], and digital exclusion limiting health information access [15]. Critically, family dynamics mediate decisions, with adult children overriding parental preferences in 41% of cases [16]. Paradoxically, recent policy advances—free urban programs (2023–2025) and novel mRNA vaccines (2024)—exacerbated equity concerns and safety uncertainties [17]. Current frameworks fail to capture these Chinese geriatric contexts, particularly the interplay between intergenerational influence and policy evolution. This study addresses this gap by quantifying family decision-making roles and policy-triggered hesitancy shifts in elderly cohorts.

In winter–spring 2023–2024, the influenza nucleic-acid positivity prevalence in Ningbo sentinel hospitals reached 25.78%, indicating a high-intensity epidemic with the positive rate above the epidemic threshold (≥ 25%) [18, 19].People’s hesitation about vaccines has greatly reduced vaccine uptake and coverage, and the influencing factors are unknown. Therefore, this cross-sectional study aims to: (1) Assess the cognitive terrain—what older adults in Ningbo actually know, misunderstand, and fear—about influenza and its vaccines; (2) Evaluate current vaccination uptake, analyze multidimensional hesitancy patterns, and determine contextual influences on future vaccination intent; (3) Identify key hesitancy determinants to inform public health strategies and optimize Ningbo’s 2025 free elderly influenza vaccination program.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This research was conducted from 1 Feberuary to 15 March 2025 in 4 districts (Haishu, Yinzhou, Yuyao and Ninghai) in Ningbo City (Fig. 1). Among them, Haishu District and Yinzhou District represented urban survey sites, while Yuyao City and Ninghai County represented rural survey sites.

Participants

The subjects of this survey were elderly people aged 60 years and above. They were required to give informed consent to the research content and be able to answer questions independently. In addition to age (≥ 60 years old) and informed consent, the eligibility criteria also include: (1) permanent residents of the four study areas (Haishu, Yinzhou, Yuyao, Ninghai) for more than 6 months; (2) no cognitive impairment (able to complete the questionnaire independently or with the assistance of researchers without communication barriers); (3) no history of severe mental illness. In each of the four study areas (Haishu, Yinzhou, Yuyao, Ninghai), we selected 3–5 elderly gathering places (including 1 vaccine clinic, 1 hospital, 1 physical examination center, and 1–2 community activity centers) through convenience sampling. Investigation content and methods

Questionnaire design

The questionnaire comprised the following sections: informed consent, sociodemographic information, vaccination status and awareness, attitudes and willingness towards vaccination, and vaccine hesitancy including its psychological antecedents. To assess the psychological antecedents of influenza vaccine hesitancy among participants, this study employed the 5 C psychological antecedents scale. Influenza vaccine hesitancy is recognized as complex in its determinants [11]. In 2011, the WHO Strategic Advisory Group of Experts on Immunization (SAGE) conceptualized this complexity through the 3 C model, encompassing Confidence, Complacency, and Convenience [11]. To better understand the factors influencing vaccination decisions, subsequent research expanded this model to incorporate psychological characteristics [20]. Specifically, Betsch et al. (2018) augmented the 3 C framework by adding two dimensions—Calculation and Collective responsibility—resulting in the comprehensive 5 C scale [21]. Integrating and extending previous approaches, the 5 C scale was widely regarded as one of the most complete models for assessing the psychological antecedents of vaccine hesitancy [22]. The scale comprised five dimensions with 21 total items: Confidence (4 items); Complacency (5 items); Convenience (4 items); Calculation (4 items); Collective responsibility (4 items). A five-level scoring system was adopted (1 = disagree, 2 = somewhat disagree, 3 = neutral, 4 = somewhat agree, 5 = agree). Responses are recorded on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = neutral, 4 = agree, 5 = strongly agree). Items 1 and 4 of the collective responsibility dimension were reverse-scored. Dimension scores are calculated as mean values, with higher scores indicating stronger endorsement of the construct. The questionnaire was based on the modified 5 C scale [20] and supplementary items designed for this study; the English version of the questionnaire is available as Supplementary. History of influenza vaccination refers to whether the participant has received 2024/2025 seasonal influenza vaccine.

Definition of vaccine status

Vaccine status refers to the dichotomy acceptance vs. hesitancy. In light of the definition of vaccine acceptance and hesitancy, this study defined willingness to receive the 2025/2026 seasonal influenza vaccine as influenza vaccine acceptance and unwillingness or uncertainty about receiving the 2025/2026 seasonal influenza vaccine as influenza vaccine hesitancy [12].

Influenza-vaccine-related cognition status

Influenza-vaccine-related cognition was assessed with an eight-item battery developed for this survey (Supplementary Material). Each item was answered with “correct”, “incorrect”, “do not know”, or “not sure”. The battery was developed for the present study on the basis of previous Chinese population surveys; content validity was reviewed by public-health experts. Each correct response was assigned one point, with incorrect answers receiving zero points, yielding a total score range of 0–8. Influenza-vaccine-related cognition status was defined as achieving a threshold score of ≥ 5 points (60% of the maximum possible score).

Covariates

We considered the following baseline characteristics (all categorical unless stated otherwise) as potential confounders: age (continuous, years), sex (male, female), marital status (married, other), education (primary school and below, junior high school, others), occupation before retirement (unemployed, agriculture, government agency, private enterprise, worker, others), annual income per person (≤ 25 000, 25 000–50 000, > 50 000 yuan), residence (town, village), religious belief (no, Buddhism, others), history of seasonal influenza vaccination in 2024/2025 (yes, no), and presence of a medical worker in the family (yes, no). For modelling purposes, the following variables were dichotomized: (1) Education level: divided into primary school and below and junior high school and above; (2) Annual income per person: divided into ≤ 25,000 yuan and > 25,000; (3) Occupation: divided into unemployed or agriculture and others; (4) religion: divided into yes and no.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics included frequencies (%) for categorical variables and mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables. Group comparisons used Chi-square tests (categorical) and t-tests or ANOVA (continuous). Multivariable logistic regression identified factors associated with influenza vaccine hesitancy, including demographics, vaccination history, and 5 C scale dimensions (analyzed as continuous scores). Candidate confounders were selected based on the following criteria: (1) variables with statistical significance (P < 0.05) in the univariate analysis; (2) variables that have been reported to be associated with vaccine hesitancy in previous studies (e.g., age, gender, and education level). In addition, this study focuses on the 5 C scale, so all items of the 5 C scale were included in the logistic regression. Before constructing the model, multicollinearity among candidate predictors was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with a VIF value < 10 (ranging from 1.10 to 1.39) indicating no significant multicollinearity [23]. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test was used to evaluate the goodness-of-fit of model, and the resulting P value of 0.43 (P > 0.05) indicates that the model fits well. Crude and adjusted Odds Ratios (OR) with 95% Confidence Intervals (CI) were reported. The proportion of hesitant respondents indicating a potential change in vaccination intention was calculated for each influencing factors of influenza vaccination intention (e.g., recommendation from relatives, friends, or descendants). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 (two-tailed). Data were double-entered using EpiData 3.1 and analyzed using R software (version 4.1.2).

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 2,288 participants were included in this study, among which 1,547 (67.61%) were vaccine acceptors and 741 (32.39%) were vaccine hesitators. Table 1 provides the distribution of sociodemographic factors for 2,288 participants. Participants who accepted vaccination were slightly older than those who hesitated (70.08 ± 6.74 vs. 68.73 ± 6.63 years, P < 0.001). Male were more likely to accept vaccination than female (70.24% vs. 65.71%, P = 0.025). Education level was significantly associated with vaccine acceptance (P = 0.015), with participants having primary school education or below showing the highest acceptance rate (69.9%), followed by junior school (65.5%) and other education levels (62.4%). Occupation was also significantly associated with vaccine acceptance (P = 0.003), with government agency workers having the highest acceptance rate (75.2%), followed by agriculture workers (70.8%), unemployed individuals (67.9%), private enterprise employees (65.4%), workers (63.8%), and others (58.6%). Annual income was significantly associated with vaccine acceptance (P < 0.001), with participants earning ≤ 25,000 yuan showing the highest acceptance (71.5%), followed by those earning 25,000–50,000 yuan (67.2%) and > 50,000 yuan (60.3%). Residential status was another strong determinant: living in a village rather than a town was associated with increased acceptance (74.28% vs. 59.50%, P < 0.001). History of influenza vaccination was significantly associated with current vaccine acceptance (P < 0.001), with participants having previous influenza vaccination history showing lower acceptance of the current vaccine (18.55%) compared to those without such history (32.29%). Having medical workers within the family showed a marginally non-significant association with vaccine acceptance (71.12% vs. 66.59%, P = 0.060).

Influenza-vaccine-related cognition status and vaccine disposition

In 2024, a total of 1,431 respondents accepted the 2024/2025 seasonal influenza vaccine, accounting for 62.54%. The average score of the respondents was 4.58 points. The results showed that there were statistically significant differences in the distribution of influenza-vaccine-related cognition scores among the elderly with different educational backgrounds, occupations, and annual income per person (P < 0.001). The proportion of elderly people with higher educational backgrounds, higher annual income per person, and those working outside had a higher influenza-vaccine-related cognition score of > = 5 points, as shown in Table 2. The proportion of people with a high influenza-vaccine-related cognitive score among those with family members in the healthcare system was higher compared to those without (72.48% vs. 49.78%, P < 0.001). However, the differences in cognitive status of influenza vaccines among different ages and between urban and rural areas did not show significant differences (P > 0.05).

Determinants of hesitancy: 5 C psychological scales

Scores on the 5 C psychological antecedents scale differed significantly between the two groups (Table 3). Participants who accepted vaccination reported higher confidence in vaccine safety and effectiveness than those who hesitated (P < 0.001). Conversely, hesitant respondents displayed higher complacency and collective responsiblity than acceptors (P < 0.05). We did not observe any significant differences in convenience and calculation between the two groups.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis

Using the recipient group as the reference, variables significant in univariate analysis (P < 0.05) were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model. The model included age, gender, education level, occupation, annual per capita income, type of residence, religious belief, and the five “5C” psychological antecedents of vaccination (confidence, complacency, convenience, calculation, and collective responsibility) (Table 4). Odds of vaccine hesitancy were greater among female (OR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.00–1.70) and town residents (OR = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.56–0.98) than males and village residents. Exhibiting influenza-vaccine-related cognition was associated with lower odds of influenza vaccine hesitancy (OR = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.50–0.86). For each 1 point increase in confidence, odds of hesitance decrease 18% (95% CI: 15–21); For each 1 point increase in complacency, odds of hesitance increase 14% (95% CI: 11–17); For each 1 point increase in convenience, odds of hesitance decrease 5% (95% CI: 4–9). Calculation (OR = 1.01, 95% CI: 0.98–1.04) and collective responsibility (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: 0.99–1.08) is not statistically significant.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study in Ningbo, China, assessed influenza vaccine hesitancy among 2,288 older adults (≥ 60 years) during February–March 2025 using the validated 5 C psychological antecedents scale. We identified a hesitancy prevalence of 32.4% (741/2288) and uncovered key demographic, cognitive, and psychological determinants of hesitancy through robust multivariate logistic regression. Our findings align with global patterns of suboptimal elderly influenza vaccine uptake but highlight unique socio-cultural and policy-driven barriers within the Chinese context.

The influenza vaccine hesitancy prevalence among older adults (≥ 60 years) in Ningbo was 32.4%, positioning it within the global mid-range but revealing critical regional variations. Compared to industrialized nations, Ningbo’s hesitancy prevalence was higher than reported in Germany (18.2%, ≥ 65 years;), Switzerland (21.5%, ≥ 65 years;), and Japan (25.1%, ≥ 65 years; —countries whose seniors enjoy decades-long immunisation traditions and primary-care integration [12]. In contrast to developing economies, this prevalence was lower than those observed in rural India [24] (52.8%, ≥ 55 years; and urban Brazil [25] (44.3%, ≥ 60 years). Within mainland China, Ningbo’s hesitancy exceeded prevalence documented in Beijing [26] (26.7%≥60 years;) and Shanghai [27] (28.9%≥60 years;), suggesting localized barriers during its 2025/2026 free-vaccine policy transition. The difference might be due to primary care, health literacy [28], leading vaccine research and development and vaccine policy.

Our regression model confirmed the 5 C scale’s utility in quantifying influenza vaccine hesitancy drivers among older adults. Low Confidence (OR = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.79–0.85) and High Complacency (OR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.11–1.17) were significant drivers of hesitancy [21]. This aligns with global influenza vaccine studies in elderly populations, such as Schwarzinger et al.’s (2020) cohort analysis in France (OR = 0.75, p < 0.001) [29] and Wagner et al.’s (2022) multi-country survey (β = 0.28, P = 0.002), where confidence deficits and perceived low risk were consistently primary barriers [30].

Collective responsibility showed no significant association with hesitancy (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: 0.99–1.08). This contrasts with influenza-specific studies including Wong’s community trial in Hong Kong (OR = 0.61, 95%CI: 0.53–0.70) [31] and Schmid et al.’s (2023) Swiss case-control analysis (aOR = 0.82, 95%CI: 0.77–0.88) [12], where higher collective responsibility was associated with reduced hesitancy. We interpret this discrepancy through the lens of “graded collectivism” [Triandis, 1995] and “family-group primacy” [Ho, 1998]: mainland-Chinese seniors operate within a Confucian moral hierarchy in which filial piety (xiao) is the primary imperative, whereas the more abstract civic duty (gong) is secondary [32, 33]. Our finding may reflect cultural specificity within mainland China: Confucian values emphasizing familial duty over societal obligations [34] could lead seniors to prioritize family reservations about vaccination over public health imperatives. In vaccination decisions this kin primacy translates into a “protect-the-grandchildren” frame that is activated only when messages explicitly reference inter-generational benefit; the generic 5 C item of collective responsibility lacks that cue and therefore fails to predict uptake. Our finding thus aligns with prior work showing that standardized prosocial scales lose predictive power in high in-group collectivist settings unless they specify the reference group [35]. Consequently, geriatric vaccination instruments in Confucian contexts should supplement the 5 C with a “family responsibility” sub-domain and intervention messages should foreground inter-generational reciprocity—e.g., “vaccinate to safeguard your grandchildren’s schooling”—to convert cultural values into vaccine acceptance [36].

Our findings underscore the primacy of social and risk-perception factors over structural or material incentives in motivating influenza-vaccine uptake among individuals who initially declare themselves hesitant to be vaccinated. The observation that a recommendation from relatives, friends, or descendants catalysed the greatest shift in intention (26.72%) aligns with Social Norms Theory and the literature on vaccine decision-making, which repeatedly identifies interpersonal trust and perceived“injunctive norms”within close-knit networks as stronger predictors of behavioural change than information delivered by formal authorities [12]. The significant impact of perceived susceptibility to infection—either through self-assessed poor immunity (22.40%) or elevated environmental exposure (19.57%)—supports the Health Belief Model’s proposition that perceived threat is a necessary, if not sufficient, condition for preventive action [37, 38]. This implies that targeted risk-communication campaigns emphasising personal vulnerability and the transmissibility of influenza within households or workplaces could be more effective than generic educational messages. Physicians, while still influential (18.89%), ranked below recommendation from relatives, friends, or descendants and below fear-based cues, suggesting that the credibility of medical expertise may be moderated by relational proximity in this highly hesitant subgroup [39]. Conversely, convenience-oriented strategies (door-to-door delivery, choice of vaccine brand, small gifts) exerted minimal influence, each converting < 10% of refusers. This contrasts with studies in low-hesitancy populations where reducing logistical barriers has yielded measurable increases in uptake [40, 41]. The discrepancy suggests that among individuals who are explicitly hesitant about vaccination, attitudinal resistance outweighs practical obstacles. Material incentives may therefore function partially as facilitators for the already-motivated rather than as catalysts for behavioural change among the resolutely hesitant [42]. The notably weak effect of community-cadre endorsement (7.56%) further illustrates the limited reach of authority figures who lack intimate social ties or medical credentials [43]. This has practical implications for public-health programs that rely heavily on grassroots mobilization: resource allocation might be optimized by focusing on family-centered or peer-mediated interventions rather than broad community campaigns [44]. Taken together, our results indicate that tackling deep-seated vaccine refusal requires strategies that leverage trusted social networks and heighten perceived personal risk, rather than those that merely simplify access or provide token rewards. Future intervention studies should test family-focused counselling modules and personalized risk-messaging delivered through credible lay influencers to quantify their additive impact on actual vaccination prevalence among high-hesitancy cohorts.

There are several limitations need to be noted. First, the observational nature of this survey precludes establishing causal inferences and capturing dynamic trends in influenza vaccine hesitancy over time. Secondly, this study adopted convenience sampling to recruit participants from elderly gathering places such as vaccine clinics, hospitals, and community activity centers. This may lead to sampling bias, as participants may be more likely to have health-seeking behaviors (including vaccination), resulting in an underestimation of the actual influenza vaccine hesitancy prevalence. Thirdly, time frame for defining vaccine status could also affect the consistency of research results. By assessing willingness to receive the 2025/2026 seasonal vaccine in February–March 2025, we captured time-point intentions rather than stable hesitancy patterns. This advanced assessment may fail to predict actual behavior because: (a) participants’ health status and risk perception may change substantially before the vaccination season; (b) 2025/2026 vaccine characteristics remain unknown; and (c) “uncertain” responses may reflect information deficit rather than true hesitancy. Consequently, hesitancy likely comprises a mixture of stable hesitancy, situational uncertainty, and rational postponement, limiting identification of true patterns of hesitancy. Fourthly, unmeasured confounders such as the economic status of participants and the vaccination history of family members may also affect the study results. Finally. findings should be interpreted with caution beyond Ningbo’s unique high-intensity epidemic setting during the 2024–2025 influenza season.

Conclusion

This study identifies an influenza vaccine hesitancy prevalence of 32.4% among elderly residents (≥ 60 years) in Ningbo, China. Key determinants include younger age, female gender, urban residence, low confidence, and high complacency. To mitigate these barriers, we advocate family-inclusive interventions engaging adult children as decision gatekeepers; age-targeted digital outreach via social media for younger seniors; and systematic integration of vaccination advocacy into routine geriatric care. Future research must validate culturally adapted hesitancy scales accounting for collectivist dynamics and longitudinally track policy maturation effects.
