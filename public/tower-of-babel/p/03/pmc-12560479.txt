Soluble CSF1R promotes microglial activation and amyloid clearance in alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Lianshuai Zhang, Kai Chen, Ruicheng Zeng, Guojun Bu, Xiao-Fen Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor (CSF1R) is a receptor tyrosine kinase essential for microglial development and homeostasis. While dysregulated CSF1R signaling has been implicated in Alzheimer’s disease (AD), the biological function of its soluble ectodomain (sCSF1R)—generated by a disintegrin and metalloproteinase 17 (ADAM17)-mediated cleavage—remains poorly understood in neurodegeneration.MethodsWe quantified sCSF1R levels in the brain and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of 5×FAD transgenic mice and wild-type controls using ELISA and immunoblotting, and reanalyzed publicly available CSF proteomic datasets from three independent, clinically characterized AD cohorts. Functional studies were performed in primary microglial cultures and through hippocampal delivery of recombinant sCSF1R into 5×FAD mice to evaluate its effects on microglial activity and amyloid pathology.ResultsIn this study, we identify sCSF1R as a previously unrecognized, functionally active modulator of microglial responses in AD. Analysis of three independent clinical cohorts revealed significantly elevated sCSF1R levels in the CSF of AD patients, a finding recapitulated in both the brain and CSF of 5×FAD transgenic mice. Importantly, sCSF1R concentrations showed positive correlations with core AD biomarkers—including total tau, phosphorylated tau, and β-amyloid 1–42 (Aβ42)—and with measures of cognitive performance, highlighting its clinical significance and suggesting that sCSF1R may serve as a marker of disease-associated microglial responses. Functionally, recombinant sCSF1R enhanced microglial survival, migration, proinflammatory signaling, and Aβ phagocytosis in vitro. In vivo, administration of sCSF1R promoted microglial clustering around amyloid plaques, reduced Aβ deposition, and attenuated plaque-associated neuritic dystrophy in 5×FAD mice. Finally, we found that soluble TREM2 (sTREM2)—a CSF biomarker and potent activator of microglia—stimulates ADAM17-dependent cleavage of membrane-bound CSF1R, thereby driving the generation of sCSF1R. Collectively, these findings establish sCSF1R as a novel regulator of microglial function in AD and reveal a regulatory axis linking sTREM2, ADAM17 activity, and CSF1R shedding with potential implications for disease modulation.ConclusionsThese findings identify sCSF1R as a novel component of the neuroimmune signaling network in AD and highlight its dual potential as a CSF biomarker of beneficial microglial activation and a candidate therapeutic modulator of neuroinflammation and amyloid pathology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-025-03558-5.Keywords: CSF1R, Alzheimer’s disease, Amyloid-β, Microglia

Introduction

The colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor (CSF1R) is a receptor tyrosine kinase predominantly expressed in microglia, the resident immune cells of the central nervous system (CNS) [1]. CSF1R plays a pivotal role in microglial development, maintenance, and function. Genetic ablation of Csf1r in mice results in near-complete depletion of microglia throughout the brain, highlighting its essential role in microglial survival and homeostasis [2, 3]. In the adult CNS, CSF1R signaling also governs microglial responses to injury and disease, modulating microglial proliferation, migration, and the expression of inflammatory genes [4, 5]. Loss-of-function mutations in CSF1R lead to adult-onset leukoencephalopathy with axonal spheroids and pigmented glia (ALSP), a rapidly progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by white matter degeneration, cognitive decline, behavioral disturbances, and motor dysfunction [6–8]. These findings emphasize the critical role of CSF1R signaling in preserving CNS immune integrity.

In addition to its involvement in rare hereditary white matter disorders, CSF1R has garnered increasing attention as a key modulator of microglial function in Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Several common CSF1R genetic variants have been associated with increased AD risk [9–12], and functional studies suggest that CSF1R interacts with key AD-related pathways, particularly those involving triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2) and apolipoprotein E (APOE) [13]. In mouse models of AD, pharmacological inhibition or conditional deletion of Csf1r has led to context-dependent therapeutic outcomes, including reductions in amyloid plaque burden, attenuation of tau pathology, preservation of neuronal integrity, and improvements in cognitive function [14–19]. Additionally, recent studies indicate that microglial repopulation following CSF1R inhibition may promote brain resilience. For example, microglial repopulation in aged mice restores spatial memory performance to levels similar to young controls [20], while repopulation has also been associated with more compact amyloid plaques and improved synaptic function [21, 22]. Collectively, these findings position CSF1R as a promising therapeutic target in AD, offering opportunities for modulating disease-associated microglial states through either transient depletion or selective repopulation strategies.

CSF1R is activated by two primary ligands—colony-stimulating factor 1 (CSF1) and interleukin-34 (IL-34)—which initiate intracellular signaling cascades, including the PI3K-Akt and ERK1/2 pathways, to support microglial proliferation, differentiation, and survival [1, 23]. In addition to its canonical signaling functions, CSF1R undergoes regulated intramembrane proteolysis (RIP)—a two-step proteolytic processing also observed in other neurodegeneration-associated transmembrane proteins such as amyloid precursor protein (APP) and TREM2 [24–26]. Sequential cleavage by a disintegrin and metalloproteinase 17 (ADAM17) at the juxtamembrane domain releases the CSF1R ectodomain as a soluble fragment (sCSF1R), followed by γ-secretase-mediated cleavage of the intracellular domain [27–30]. While sCSF1R has been detected in peripheral circulation and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) [31–33], its regulatory mechanisms and functional consequences—particularly in the CNS—remain poorly understood.

In this study, we demonstrate that sCSF1R is markedly elevated in both the brain and CSF of 5×FAD mice, as well as in the CSF of individuals with AD across three independent clinical cohorts. The sCSF1R levels in CSF show strong positive correlations with core AD biomarkers—including total tau, phosphorylated tau, and β-amyloid 1–42 (Aβ42)—and with cognitive performance measured by the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). We further identify sCSF1R as a previously unrecognized and functionally active modulator of microglial responses to AD pathology. In response to pathological stimuli, microglia upregulate sCSF1R production, which in turn enhances microglial activation, survival, migration and phagocytosis. In vivo, direct hippocampal delivery of sCSF1R in 5×FAD mice promotes microglial clustering around amyloid plaques, accelerates Aβ clearance, and reduces neuritic dystrophy. We further demonstrate that sTREM2 promotes sCSF1R generation through ADAM17-mediated cleavage of CSF1R, likely by engaging microglial signaling pathways. Collectively, these findings position sCSF1R as a novel effector within the microglial regulatory network in AD, with potential utility both as a biomarker of microglial activation and as a therapeutic target for neurodegenerative disease.

Materials and methods

Mice

The 5×FAD amyloidosis mouse model mice (stock #034848), Trem2–/– mice (stock #027197) and C57BL/6J wild-type mice (stock #000664) were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Xiamen University and conducted in strict accordance with institutional and national guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions, maintained on a standard chow diet, and kept on a 12-hour light/dark cycle. For all experimental studies, both male and female mice were included and were randomly assigned to different experimental groups. To minimize bias, experimenters were blinded to both the genotype and treatment status of each mouse. The identities of the mice were revealed only after the data analysis was completed.

Analysis of clinical data

Clinical data on sCSF1R protein levels were obtained from two independent CSF proteomic datasets. The first dataset, generated by Ali et al. [34] and publicly available through an interactive browser (https://proteomics.wustl.edu/csf/), includes two cohorts. Cohort 1 comprises 1,170 individuals from the Knight Alzheimer Disease Research Center (Knight ADRC) and the Fundació ACE Alzheimer Center Barcelona (FACE), consisting of 680 A−T− and 490 A+T+ participants. Cohort 2 comprises 593 individuals from the Alzheimer Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) and Barcelona-1 cohorts, including 235 A−T− and 358 A+T+ participants. Individuals with high levels of Aβ42 and low levels of phosphorylated tau at threonine 181 (pTau181) in CSF were defined as controls (A−T−), indicating a low presence of amyloid plaques and tau tangles in the brain. In contrast, individuals exhibiting low CSF Aβ42 levels together with elevated pTau181 levels were classified as amyloid plaque/pTau positive (A+T+), reflecting a high burden of amyloid plaques and tau tangles in the brain. The sCSF1R levels in CSF were quantified using the SomaLogic aptamer-based SomaScan platform (SomaScan 7k). The second dataset was derived from the CSF proteomic analysis conducted by Dammer et al. [35], accessible via Synapse (Project SynID: syn52888250; https://synapse.org/Emory300ATX). In this dataset, the sCSF1R levels in CSF were measured using either tandem mass tag-based mass spectrometry (TMT-MS) or the SomaScan platform. Additional AD-related biomarkers were extracted, including total tau (tTau), pTau181, Aβ42, and cognitive performance assessed by MoCA score. Pearson correlation analyses were performed to assess the relationships between the sCSF1R levels in CSF and AD-related biomarkers as well as cognitive scores. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.2; GraphPad Software, USA).

Brain stereotaxic injection

Stereotaxic injection was performed as described previously [36]. Briefly, mice were anesthetized using isoflurane in an induction chamber and placed in a stereotaxic frame (RWD Life Science, China). Anesthesia was maintained with 1–2% isoflurane inhalation. After a midline scalp incision, small holes were drilled at coordinates ± 2.0 mm lateral and − 2.0 mm posterior to bregma. A Hamilton syringe was used to bilaterally inject 5 µg of protein diluted in 2 µL PBS into each hemisphere at a depth of 2.0 mm, at a rate of 0.20 µL/min. The syringe was left in place for 5 min after injection before being slowly withdrawn. After 7 days, mice were re-anesthetized and perfused transcardially with cold PBS. Brains were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4 °C, and transferred to 30% sucrose for cryoprotection for 48 h. The brains were then embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound and sectioned using a cryostat.

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) injection

Both male and female wild-type mice at 7 months of age were Weighed and intraperitoneally injected with 5 mg/kg LPS (Sigma, L2654) dissolved in 300 µL of sterile, abiotic PBS per animal. The brains were subsequently harvested 18 h post-injection for downstream biochemical analysis and histological examination.

CSF collection

CSF was collected from mice as previously described [37, 38]. Briefly, mice were anesthetized and placed in a prone position on a platform under a dissection microscope. The head was gently flexed to form an approximate 135° angle with the body to expose the cisterna magna. The overlying skin and muscles at the posterior base of the skull were carefully separated using blunt dissection with fine forceps. A glass capillary tube was then gently inserted through the dura mater into the cisterna magna, allowing CSF to flow passively into the tube. After collection, the capillary tube was slowly withdrawn, and the CSF samples were transferred into pre-labeled 0.2 mL tubes. Samples were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

Aβ Preparation and oligomerization

Oligomeric Aβ42 (oAβ42) and fibrillar Aβ42 (fAβ42) were prepared as previously described, with minor modifications [39–41]. Briefly, Aβ42 peptides (AnaSpec, AS-20276) were first dissolved in DMSO to a concentration of 2.5 mM. For oligomer formation, a portion of the peptides was diluted to 100 µM in cold, phenol-free F-12 culture medium (Thermo Scientific, 21041025) and incubated at 22 °C for 16 h, followed by 24 h at 4 °C. For fibril formation, another portion was diluted to 100 µM in 10 mM HCl and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. After incubation, samples were centrifuged at 16,000 g for 15 min, and the resulting supernatants were collected and used as oAβ42 and fAβ42, respectively.

Soluble CSF1R ELISA

The sCSF1R levels in CSF from different mouse models were measured using the Mouse CSF1R ELISA Kit (Abcam, ab213858) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, all reagents, samples, and standards were prepared as directed. Fifty microliters of each standard or sample were added to wells pre-coated with the capture antibody and incubated at 37 °C for 90 min. After removing the contents, 100 µL of biotinylated detection antibody was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min. Wells were then washed three times with 300 µL of 10 mM PBS. Subsequently, 100 µL of ABC working solution was added and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by five washes with 300 µL of 10 mM PBS. Next, 90 µL of TMB substrate solution (Sigma, T8665) was added and incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 25 min. The reaction was terminated by adding 100 µL of stop solution, and optical density was measured at 450 nm within 30 min.

Immunohistochemistry

Mice were anesthetized and transcardially perfused with cold PBS. Brains were removed and dissected. For biochemical analysis, the hippocampus and cortex were carefully isolated, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for subsequent protein and mRNA extraction. For histological analysis, whole brains were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C overnight, then transferred to 30% sucrose solution for cryoprotection over 48 h. The brains were subsequently embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound and sectioned using a cryostat.

Coronal brain sections were cut, mounted, and air-dried overnight at 37 °C. Sections were washed in PBS for 15 min and blocked in PBS containing 5% bovine serum albumin and 0.5% Triton X-100 for 1 h at room temperature. They were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature in the dark. After a final PBS wash, sections were mounted with anti-fade mounting medium (Thermo Scientific, P36935) for imaging using confocal microscopy. For Thioflavin-S (Thio-S) staining, coronal brain sections were incubated in 1 mM Thio-S in 50% ethanol for 15 min at room temperature. Sections were then washed sequentially with 80% ethanol and PBS, and mounted onto slides for imaging.

The following primary antibodies and dyes were used: Aβ plaques, anti-Aβ monoclonal antibody (MOAB-2) (Abcam, ab126649, 1:1000) or Thio-S (Sigma, T1892, 1 mM); microglia, anti-Iba1 polyclonal antibody (Wako, 019-19741, 1:500); microglial activation and phagocytosis, anti-CD68 monoclonal antibody (BioLegend, 137001, 1:800); homeostatic microglial marker, anti-P2ry12 antibody (AnaSpec, AS-55043 A, 1:500); disease-associated microglial marker, anti-Clec7a monoclonal antibody (InvivoGen, mabg-mdect-2, 1:500); dystrophic neurites, anti-LAMP1 antibody (BD Biosciences, 553792, 1:800); cytoskeleton, anti-β-actin recombinant antibody (Proteintech, 81115-1-RR, 1:500); nuclei, DAPI (Sigma, D9542, 1:500).

Protein purification

The extracellular domains of human TREM2 (sTREM2; amino acids 1-157) and CSF1R (sCSF1R; amino acids 21–502) were cloned into the pFUSE-hIgG1-Fc1 (InvivoGen, pfuse-hg1fc1) plasmid and pFUSE-hIgG1-Fc2 (InvivoGen, pfuse-hg1fc2) plasmid containing the IL2 signal sequence, respectively. Recombinant sTREM2 and sCSF1R proteins were expressed and purified from HEK293T cells, which are human-derived and do not produce endotoxins. Expression and purification of the sTREM2 and sCSF1R fusion proteins were carried out as previously described [42]. To generate Fc-free sTREM2 and sCSF1R proteins, a TEV protease cleavage site was inserted between the extracellular domain and the Fc tag. The resulting fusion proteins (sTREM2-TEV-Fc and sCSF1R-TEV-Fc) were purified from culture supernatants using rProtein G Agarose Resin (YEASEN, 36405ES25). The bound proteins were eluted and incubated with TEV protease (Promega, V6101), which contains an N-terminal His-tag, to remove the Fc domain. Following cleavage, the TEV protease was removed using cOmplete His-Tag Purification Resin (Roche, 51122800). The final protein preparations were dialyzed against PBS, filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane, and stored at appropriate conditions until use. For heat inactivation, purified sCSF1R protein was incubated at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by immediate cooling on ice.

Isolation and culture of mouse primary microglia

Primary microglia were prepared as previously described [36, 42]. Briefly, brains were dissected from postnatal day 1–2 mouse pups, and the meninges were carefully removed. Mixed glial cultures were established in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and maintained at 37 °C in a Humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. After 2 days, the medium was replaced with fresh DMEM containing 10% FBS and 25 ng/mL granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) (R&D Systems, 415-ML-010/CF) to promote microglial proliferation. On day 7, microglia were harvested by vigorous shaking and collected from the supernatant. This process was repeated every 3 days to obtain additional microglial populations.

For treatment experiments, primary microglia were incubated with the indicated proteins or inhibitors for specified time periods. After treatment, cells were washed with cold PBS and lysed in RIPA buffer (Boster, AR0105-100) supplemented with protease (Ivdshow, K1008) and phosphatase (Ivdshow, K1014) inhibitor cocktails. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, 23225) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

To collect sCSF1R protein from the microglial culture medium, the medium was first centrifuged at 10,000 g for 5 min at 4 °C to remove debris, and the supernatant was collected. One-ninth volume of 100% trichloroacetic acid (TCA; Aladdin, T104261) was then added to the supernatant, and the mixture was gently inverted to mix before incubating overnight at 4 °C to allow protein precipitation. After precipitation, the mixture was centrifuged at 15,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C to pellet the proteins. The resulting pellet was washed with 300 µL of pre-cooled acetone, gently mixed by inversion, and centrifuged again at 15,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain the final protein pellet.

Western blotting

Cells or tissue samples were lysed in RIPA buffer (Boster, AR0105-100) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Ivdshow, K1008) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Ivdshow, K1014). Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, 23225) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal amounts of total protein were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto 0.45 μm polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in PBST and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: CSF1R (Abcam, ab37858, 1:2000), sCSF1R (Sino Biological, 50059-RP01, 1:2000), Iba1 (Cell Signaling Technology, 17198 S, 1:1000), β-actin (Cell Signaling Technology, 4970, 1:10000), ADAM17 (Cell Signaling Technology, 61048, 1:2000). After washing, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1.5 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were detected using the Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (Millipore, WBKLS0500) and imaged with a chemiluminescence detection system. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH).

Microglial migration

Microglial migration was assessed using a transwell system with 8.0 μm pore size polycarbonate membrane inserts (Costar, 3422). Primary microglia suspended in 100 µL of DMEM were seeded into the upper chamber, while 600 µL of serum-free DMEM was added to the lower chamber. After 1 h of equilibration, the medium in the lower chamber was replaced with DMEM containing 40 nM sCSF1R protein. Following a 24-hour incubation at 37 °C, migrated cells on the underside of the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. The total number of cells and the number of migrated microglia were quantified using ImageJ software. Migration efficiency was calculated as the ratio of migrated microglia to the total number of seeded microglia.

Microglial phagocytosis

Primary microglia were plated in 12-well or 24-well plates for flow cytometry or immunohistochemistry analysis, respectively. Cells were pre-incubated with 40 nM sCSF1R in serum-free DMEM for 24 h, followed by treatment with 200 nM FAM-labeled oAβ42 (AnaSpec, AS-23526-01) in serum-free DMEM for 3 h at 37 °C. After incubation, cells were collected, washed thoroughly with cold PBS, and analyzed by flow cytometry or immunohistochemistry to assess FAM fluorescence as a measure of Aβ uptake.

Microglial survival

Primary microglia were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with 40 nM sCSF1R protein diluted in serum-free DMEM for 24 h. Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 Cell Proliferation and Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Solarbio, CA1210) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

TUNEL staining

Cell apoptosis was assessed using the Colorimetric TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit (Beyotime, C1089) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Primary microglia were cultured in 24-well plates and treated with 40 nM sCSF1R for 24 h. Cells were then harvested, washed with PBS, and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature. After a 5-minute incubation in 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS, cells were incubated with TUNEL detection solution in the dark at 37 °C for 60 min. Finally, cells were washed with PBS, mounted with anti-fade medium, and examined under a fluorescence microscope.

Detection of ADAM17 activity

ADAM17 activity was measured using the TACE (α-Secretase) Activity Assay Kit (AnaSpec, AS-72085) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Primary microglia were cultured in 12-well plates and treated with 40 nM sCSF1R for 1 h. Cells were then harvested, washed with PBS, and lysed in assay buffer containing 0.1% Triton X-100 at 4 °C for 10 min. Lysates were centrifuged at 2,500 g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Subsequently, 50 µL of each supernatant was mixed with the reaction substrate in a 96-well plate, protected from light, and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min. Fluorescence intensity was measured at Ex/Em = 490/520 nm using a Thermo Scientific Varioskan Flash plate reader.

Image analysis

Panoramic imaging of amyloid plaques was performed using a fully automated digital slide scanner (Zeiss AxioScan 7), with laser and detector settings kept constant across all experiments. Images were analyzed in ImageJ to quantify amyloid plaque burden within the hippocampal region. Confocal microscopy was conducted on a Nikon A1R laser confocal microscope under identical acquisition settings across experimental groups. To assess amyloid plaques, microglia, CD68, LAMP1, Clec7a, and P2ry12, 10-µm z-stacks were acquired using 20× or 60× objectives and processed into maximum intensity projections. Resulting images were analyzed in ImageJ to quantify signal levels of the respective targets. Microglial phagocytic activity toward amyloid plaques was evaluated by acquiring 10-µm z-stacks with a 60× objective, followed by 3D reconstruction using Imaris software (Bitplane). Plaque compaction was assessed using Thio-S staining. Based on Thio-S labeling patterns, plaques were classified into three subtypes: dense-core plaques, exhibiting strong Thio-S positivity with compact morphology; diffuse plaques, displaying weak Thio-S signal with irregular borders; and mixed plaques, showing features of both dense-core and diffuse types. All image analyses were performed by observers blinded to the experimental groups.

Statistical analysis

Details regarding sample size (n), statistical tests used, and p-values are provided in the corresponding figure legends. For all in vivo experiments, “n” refers to the number of animals used. For all in vitro experiments, “n” indicates the total number of samples analyzed, derived from at least three independent biological replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v8.0.2 (GraphPad Software, USA). Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired Student’s t-test (two-tailed). For multi-group comparisons involving a single variable, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied. Comparisons between DMSO- and inhibitor-treated groups were analyzed using two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Paired Student’s t-tests (two-tailed) were used for experiments involving sTREM2 and sCSF1R protein injections. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was defined as follows: ns, not significant; *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001.

Results

The sCSF1R levels in CSF is elevated in AD and positively correlates with AD biomarkers and cognition

To determine whether sCSF1R levels are altered in AD, we reanalyzed publicly available CSF proteomic datasets reported by Ali et al. [34]. This study employed mass spectrometry-based proteomic analysis across two cohorts to identify disease-associated protein changes in CSF. In cohort 1, which included 1,170 individuals (680 A−T− and 490 A+T+), and cohort 2, comprising 593 individuals (235 A−T− and 358 A+T+), we found a significant increase in sCSF1R levels in the CSF of A+T+ AD patients compared to A−T− controls in both cohorts (Fig. 1a-b). Consistent results were obtained from an independent cohort analyzed by Dammer et al. [35], in which two complementary proteomic platforms—tandem mass tag (TMT)-based mass spectrometry and the SomaScan aptamer-based assay—both revealed elevated sCSF1R levels in AD patients relative to controls (140 controls and 160 AD patients; Fig. 1c-d).

[34] revealed significantly increased levels of sCSF1R in A+T+ AD patients compared to A−T− controls. c-d Independent validation using a dataset from Dammer et al. [35] confirmed that sCSF1R levels in the CSF were elevated in AD patients compared with controls. sCSF1R protein abundance was quantified using tandem mass tag-based mass spectrometry (TMT-MS) (c) and the SomaScan platform (d). e-h Correlation analyses between the sCSF1R in CSF (measured by TMT-MS) and AD-related biomarkers, including total tau (tTau; e), phosphorylated tau at threonine 181 (pTau181; f), β-amyloid 1–42 (Aβ42; g), and cognitive performance assessed by the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score (h) (Pearson correlation coefficient test). i-l Correlation analyses between the sCSF1R in CSF (measured by SomaScan) and tTau (i), pTau181 (j), Aβ42 (k), and MoCA score (l) (Pearson correlation coefficient test). **p < 0.01; ****p < 0.0001

To further examine the clinical relevance of sCSF1R elevation in AD, we performed correlation analyses using data from the Dammer et al. cohort [35], in which AD endophenotypes such as tTau, pTau181, and Aβ42 in CSF were measured by ELISA, and cognitive performance was assessed using MoCA score. The sCSF1R levels in CSF showed significant positive correlations with tTau and pTau181 (Fig. 1e-f, i-j), suggesting that sCSF1R is upregulated in response to tau-related neurodegeneration. Moreover, sCSF1R levels were positively associated with Aβ42 concentrations (Fig. 1g, k), which may reflect less severe amyloid pathology or more efficient Aβ clearance in individuals with higher sCSF1R levels. Importantly, sCSF1R levels were also positively correlated with MoCA scores (Fig. 1h, l), indicating a potential link between higher sCSF1R levels and better-preserved cognitive function. These findings suggest that increased sCSF1R in AD CSF may represent a protective or compensatory response during disease progression.

Pathological microglial activation drives sCSF1R elevation

To investigate the cellular origin of sCSF1R and its regulation under pathological conditions, we examined microglial activation in an amyloidosis mouse model (5×FAD) and in a model of acute inflammation induced by LPS. Both models exhibited robust microgliosis and microglial activation, as evidenced by Iba1 and CD68 immunostaining, with Clec7a further assessed in 5×FAD mice (Fig. 2a-e). ELISA analysis revealed significantly elevated sCSF1R levels in the CSF of both 5×FAD and LPS-treated wild-type mice compared with untreated controls (Fig. 2f). Western blotting indicated an increase in sCSF1R abundance in brain lysates (Fig. 2g-i); however, because Western blot signals are inherently non-linear, the normalized values should be interpreted with caution. Consistent with these findings, both oligomeric and fibrillar Aβ42 significantly increased sCSF1R production from microglia in vitro, supporting a direct link between amyloid pathology and sCSF1R release (Fig. 2j-l).

Both treatments markedly increased sCSF1R release into the culture medium (Fig. 2m-o), supporting a causal role for microglial activation in CSF1R production. Collectively, these results indicate that microglial activation, whether induced by AD pathology or inflammatory stimuli, is a key driver of sCSF1R production.

sTREM2 promotes sCSF1R production via ADAM17-mediated cleavage

Previous studies have shown that activated macrophages generate sCSF1R through ADAM17-mediated cleavage of membrane-bound CSF1R [30]. However, whether activated microglia employ a similar mechanism remains unclear. TREM2 itself is cleaved by ADAM family proteases to release a soluble ectodomain (sTREM2), which is elevated in the CSF of AD patients and has been shown to activate microglia [25, 26, 42–45]. We therefore used sTREM2 as a model to investigate how microglial activation drives sCSF1R production. Recombinant human sTREM2 protein was purified and applied to primary microglial cultures to confirm its biological activity (Fig. S1a-d). Notably, sTREM2 treatment reduced membrane-bound CSF1R while concurrently increasing sCSF1R levels, indicative of enhanced CSF1R shedding (Fig. 3a, d, and g). To determine whether this effect is mediated by ADAM17, primary microglia were treated with sTREM2 in the presence of inhibitors targeting the proteasome, lysosome, or ADAM17. Strikingly, only the ADAM17 inhibitor TAPI-1 effectively blocked sTREM2-induced CSF1R cleavage and sCSF1R production (Fig. 3a-f). To further confirm specificity, a more selective ADAM17 inhibitor, DPC 333, was tested and similarly prevented sTREM2-induced CSF1R cleavage and sCSF1R production (Fig. 3g-i). Finally, we assessed ADAM17 at the transcript, protein, and enzymatic activity levels, and found that sTREM2 significantly enhances ADAM17 enzymatic activity without affecting its expression (Fig. 3j-l). While these results strongly suggest that ADAM17 mediates sTREM2-induced CSF1R cleavage, further studies employing ADAM17 knockout or knockdown approaches are necessary to confirm the specificity of the observed effects. Together, these findings indicate that sTREM2 promotes CSF1R cleavage and sCSF1R production primarily by stimulating ADAM17 enzymatic activity.

To assess in vivo relevance, we injected sTREM2 into the hippocampus of wild-type mice. sTREM2 treatment significantly activated microglia, as evidenced by pronounced microgliosis, downregulation of homeostatic markers, and upregulation of activation markers (Fig. 3m-s). sCSF1R modulates microglial functions in vitro

To investigate the functional role of sCSF1R in microglia, we purified the extracellular domain (D1-D5) of human CSF1R (Fig. 4a). The resulting recombinant protein exhibited a molecular weight comparable to endogenous sCSF1R detected in brain tissue and in the culture medium of primary microglia, and was thus used as a surrogate for the naturally occurring soluble form (Figs. 2g, j and m and 3a, d and g). Treatment with sCSF1R robustly activated primary microglia, as evidenced by the downregulation of homeostatic markers (Cx3cr1 and P2ry12; Fig. 4b-c) and the upregulation of disease-associated microglial (DAM) markers (Cst7 and Clec7a; Fig. 4d-e). To assess its effect on inflammatory responses, we measured the mRNA levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines following sCSF1R exposure. Notably, sCSF1R significantly increased the expression of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α (Fig. 4f-h).

Transwell assays revealed that sCSF1R significantly enhanced microglial migration compared to controls (Fig. 4i-j). Moreover, sCSF1R treatment promoted microglial survival and inhibited microglial apoptosis (Fig. 4k-m). To evaluate phagocytic capacity, microglia were co-incubated with fluorescently labeled Aβ42 (FAM-Aβ42) and sCSF1R, followed by immunofluorescence analysis. sCSF1R-treated microglia exhibited greater intracellular accumulation of FAM-Aβ42 (Fig. 4n-o), a finding further corroborated by flow cytometry, which showed a higher proportion of FAM-Aβ42-positive microglia in the sCSF1R group (Fig. 4p-q). Together, these results demonstrate that sCSF1R promotes microglial activation and modulates key microglial functions, including inflammatory signaling, migration, survival, and phagocytosis in vitro.

sCSF1R regulates microglial functions independently of TREM2

TREM2 is a key innate immune receptor expressed on microglia and is critically involved in regulating microglial survival, migration, and phagocytosis in the context of neurodegenerative diseases, including AD [26, 46–48]. Interestingly, CSF1R has been reported to interact with TREM2, raising the possibility of crosstalk between these signaling pathways [49]. Given their overlapping functions and physical association, we sought to determine whether the effects of sCSF1R are dependent on TREM2 signaling. To this end, we treated primary microglia derived from Trem2 knockout mice with sCSF1R and assessed key functional outcomes. Notably, sCSF1R treatment significantly enhanced microglial inflammatory responses (Fig. S2a-c), migration (Fig. S2d-e), and survival (Fig. S2f), reduced apoptosis (Fig. S2g-h), and promoted phagocytosis of FAM-labeled Aβ42, as shown by both immunofluorescence and flow cytometry analyses (Fig. S2i-l). These results indicate that sCSF1R exerts its effects on microglial function through a TREM2-independent mechanism, suggesting that sCSF1R may serve as an alternative regulatory pathway for microglial activation, particularly in Trem2-deficient or dysfunctional contexts with potential therapeutic relevance.

sCSF1R activates microglia in vivo

To determine whether sCSF1R can induce microglial activation in vivo, we stereotactically injected purified sCSF1R protein into the right hippocampus and heat-inactivated sCSF1R protein (Ctrl) into the contralateral hippocampus of adult wild-type mice (Fig. 5a). Seven days post-injection, immunofluorescence staining for Iba1 revealed a marked increase in microgliosis in the sCSF1R-injected hemisphere compared to the contralateral control side (Fig. 5b-d). Morphological analysis further showed that microglia in the sCSF1R-injected region exhibited reduced branching complexity—a hallmark of activated microglia (Fig. 5e-f) [50]. To further assess microglial activation, we performed immunostaining for CD68, a lysosomal marker upregulated in activated microglia. Both the total CD68-positive area and the CD68 intensity per microglia were significantly elevated in the sCSF1R-injected hemisphere (Fig. 5g-i). In parallel, expression of the homeostatic microglial marker P2ry12 was significantly reduced, whereas expression of the DAM marker Clec7a was markedly elevated in the same region (Fig. 5j-l). Together, these results demonstrate that sCSF1R is sufficient to trigger microglial activation in the adult mouse brain, supporting its role as a potent modulator of microglial state in vivo.

sCSF1R ameliorates amyloid pathology in 5×FAD mice

Given that sCSF1R promotes microglial activation both in vitro and in vivo, we next examined its impact on amyloid pathology in the 5×FAD mouse model of AD. To this end, we stereotactically injected purified sCSF1R protein into the right hippocampus and heat-inactivated sCSF1R protein (Ctrl) into the contralateral hippocampus of seven-month-old 5×FAD mice for seven days. Consistent with results in wild-type mice, sCSF1R induced robust microglial activation in 5×FAD brains, as evidenced by a significant increase in CD68-positive area and CD68 signal intensity per microglia (Fig. S3a-c), accompanied by a notable reduction in microglial branching (Fig. S3d-e).

To evaluate the effect of sCSF1R on amyloid pathology, we performed immunofluorescence staining of Aβ plaques in the hippocampus. sCSF1R-injected hemispheres showed a marked reduction in plaque burden compared to contralateral controls (Fig. 6a-b). Confocal imaging revealed increased microglial density accompanied by decreased amyloid deposition in the sCSF1R-injected region (Fig. 6c-f). Quantification of plaque-associated microglia demonstrated a significant increase in microglia surrounding amyloid deposits, with elevated CD68 signal indicating enhanced microglial engagement and phagocytic activity (Fig. 6g-j). Notably, amyloid plaques appeared more compact following sCSF1R treatment, consistent with reduced LAMP1 immunoreactivity, suggesting attenuation of plaque-associated neuritic dystrophy (Fig. 6k-n). Collectively, these findings indicate that sCSF1R mitigates amyloid pathology and associated neuronal damage by promoting microglial activation and enhancing plaque clearance.

Discussion

In this study, we identify the soluble ectodomain of CSF1R as a previously unrecognized regulator of microglial function and amyloid pathology in AD. We show that sCSF1R levels in the CSF are consistently elevated in AD patients across three independent cohorts. Notably, sCSF1R levels in CSF show a positive correlation with core AD biomarkers—including tTau, pTau, and Aβ42—as well as a modest association with MoCA scores. While these correlations do not imply causation, they may reflect a potential protective or compensatory response during disease progression that warrants further investigation. In mouse models, sCSF1R levels increase in response to amyloid deposition and neuroinflammatory signals. Functionally, sCSF1R promotes microglial migration, survival, inflammatory signaling, and phagocytic clearance of Aβ. Elevating sCSF1R levels in 5×FAD mouse model of amyloid pathology enhances microglial recruitment and clustering around plaques, reduces Aβ burden, and alleviates neuritic dystrophy (Fig. 7). Together, these findings uncover an unexpected and beneficial role for sCSF1R in modulating microglial responses during AD progression, and point to sCSF1R as a potential biomarker and therapeutic candidate.

Our results expand the current understanding of CSF1R biology. Traditionally, CSF1R has been characterized as a receptor tyrosine kinase essential for microglial development and homeostasis, acting through membrane-bound signaling in response to CSF1 and IL-34 [1, 51]. Loss-of-function mutations in CSF1R cause ALSP, highlighting its indispensable role in myeloid cell maintenance [6–8]. In the context of AD, CSF1R signaling has emerged as a critical modulator of microglial activation and disease progression [9–13]. However, while most studies have focused on membrane-bound CSF1R, our findings reveal that its cleaved soluble form, sCSF1R, also exhibits biological activity and modulates microglial phenotype in vivo.

Importantly, proteolytic shedding of CSF1R is mediated by ADAM17 and γ-secretase [27–30], a process previously viewed as a mechanism for receptor down-regulation. Our study challenges this paradigm by demonstrating that sCSF1R retains the ability to influence microglial behavior. This parallels recent discoveries regarding sTREM2, another ADAM17 substrate, which promotes microglial survival and migration, enhances phagocytic activity, and is elevated in the CSF of AD patients [36, 42, 52, 53]. Like sTREM2, sCSF1R may function as an extracellular immunomodulator, reshaping the microglial landscape in response to pathological cues.

The convergence between sCSF1R and sTREM2 is particularly striking. Both are derived from myeloid-specific membrane receptors, are cleaved by the same sheddase (ADAM17), and are elevated in disease-associated microglial (DAM) states. Our data suggest that sTREM2 promotes CSF1R shedding, raising the possibility of a coordinated regulatory loop. Such a feedforward mechanism could amplify microglial priming and reactivity, enhancing their capacity to respond to Aβ pathology [54]. While TREM2 expression is consistently upregulated in DAM and critical for the transition to the activated microglial phenotype, CSF1R expression is often downregulated in DAM, possibly reflecting a shift from homeostatic signaling to alternative modes of regulation [55, 56]. This dynamic divergence highlights distinct but potentially complementary roles for TREM2 and CSF1R in shaping microglial responses during AD progression.

Our findings also have therapeutic implications. While previous studies have shown that CSF1R deletion or pharmacological inhibition can be beneficial in AD models, these effects are largely attributed to microglial depletion rather than changes in sCSF1R. CSF1R inhibitors, such as PLX3397 and PLX5622, have been extensively employed to ablate microglia, albeit with variable outcomes: some studies report reduced neuroinflammation and improved cognition [14–19], whereas others demonstrate impaired plaque compaction and exacerbated neurodegeneration [21]. These variable effects likely depend on disease stage, brain region, and microglial heterogeneity. Rather than eliminating microglia entirely, selectively targeting microglial function may offer a more nuanced strategy. In contrast, our findings identify sCSF1R as a distinct modulator that directly shapes microglial responses. Administration of sCSF1R enhances protective microglial activities—such as migration, survival, plaque engagement and clearance—without disrupting the broader microglial network, thereby preserving essential homeostatic and neuroprotective functions while suppressing neurotoxic phenotypes. Together, these observations suggest that CSF1R inhibition and sCSF1R activity represent mechanistically independent yet potentially complementary processes in AD.

Moreover, sCSF1R may serve as a valuable biomarker for disease-associated microglial activation. Its consistent elevation in the CSF of AD patients points to a regulated biological process, suggesting that sCSF1R may exert functional roles beyond being a byproduct of receptor cleavage. As with sTREM2, which is being evaluated as a biomarker for early-stage AD and microglial activation [52, 57, 58], sCSF1R could offer insight into the dynamic transitions of microglial states and may serve as an indicator of therapeutic response.

Despite these promising findings, several important limitations remain to be addressed. The specific receptor or co-receptor(s) through which sCSF1R exerts its bioactivity are currently unknown. It remains unclear whether sCSF1R acts via residual membrane-bound CSF1R, engages alternative membrane receptors, or functions through entirely distinct mechanisms. Furthermore, while our short-term studies in amyloid models demonstrate beneficial effects of sCSF1R administration, the impact of sustained elevation—particularly in the context of late-stage disease or in models of tauopathy—remains to be determined. Finally, it is not yet known whether sCSF1R influences other key microglial functions, such as lipid metabolism or synaptic pruning, which are increasingly recognized as central to AD pathogenesis. Addressing these questions will be critical to fully understanding the therapeutic potential and biological role of sCSF1R in neurodegenerative diseases.

In conclusion, we identify sCSF1R as a bioactive effector of microglial function that promotes amyloid plaque clearance and protects neuritic integrity. These findings revise the canonical understanding of CSF1R signaling by revealing an active role for its soluble ectodomain. sCSF1R represents a new class of endogenous modulators with both biomarker and therapeutic potential in AD and possibly other neurodegenerative disorders involving microglial dysfunction.
