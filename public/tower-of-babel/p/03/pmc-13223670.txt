Improving healthcare professionals’ usage of artificial intelligence-powered risk recommenders through enhancement of trust and acceptance: a rapid realist review protocol
Authors: George Frederick Glass, Chin-Siang Ang, Marianne E Weiss, Xiuyi Fan, Jennifer Anne Cleland, Jo-Anne Manski-Nankervis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionHealthcare professionals working in busy hospital environments are expected to make multiple back-to-back critical decisions related to patient assessment and treatment. Fatigue from a combination of complex decision-making over multiple patients can lead to less efficient care and an increased risk of error and harm. Artificial intelligence (AI) risk recommendation systems, hereafter referred to as AI risk recommenders, have the potential to reduce the impact of decision fatigue by prompting healthcare professionals with appropriate recommendations for patient care and management. A key barrier to the effective usage of such systems is the establishment of trust and subsequent acceptance among healthcare professionals. However, little is currently known about how trust and acceptance can be engendered. The aim of this review is to develop a theory explaining what influences healthcare professionals’ usage of AI risk recommenders and how trust and acceptance, facilitate their usage of such systems.Methods and analysisWe will conduct a rapid realist review to develop a programme theory exploring how trust and acceptance of AI risk recommenders are established among healthcare professionals and how these mechanisms influence system usage. We will use the following databases—MEDLINE (Ovid), EMBASE (Elsevier), the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL (EBSCOhost)), PubMed, The Cochrane Library, The Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) Xplore, The Association for Computing Machinery Digital Library, Scopus (Elsevier), Web of Science (Clarivate) and ProQuest Dissertation and Theses. The review will focus on identifying the resources and processes that stimulate trust and acceptance, leading to the actual use of the system in clinical practice. The review will be guided by the four steps of realist review described by Rycroft-Malone. Article searching and retrieval was conducted on 15 November 2025; full-text screening is ongoing and the review is expected to be completed by May 2026.Ethics and disseminationThis study does not require formal ethics approval, as it does not involve primary research. Findings will be shared in peer-reviewed publications, conference presentations and engagement with relevant policy-makers involved in the development and integration of AI risk recommenders within hospital settings. Through these efforts, we aim to support the effective utilisation of such systems, leading to improved decision-making and patient care outcomes.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251155251Keywords: Artificial Intelligence, Clinical Decision-Making, Health informatics

Introduction

Improving decision-making through artificial intelligence

Decision-making is a critical responsibility of healthcare professionals who must make calibrated judgements on diverse elements over the course of patient care, such as identifying presenting problems, determining optimal treatment strategies and planning follow-up.1,3 Healthcare professionals make such decisions constantly during their workday and are expected to ensure accuracy even when information or resources are limited.2 4 However, sequential decision-making without adequate opportunity for rest has been found to be a direct antecedent to decision-fatigue, potentially leading to less efficient care and unnecessary treatment variation—such as endoscopists having a reduced rate of detection of adenomas with increasing number of hours spent performing endoscopies and triage nurses recommending less resource-efficient interventions with increasing time since their last break.2 3 5 6 While decision fatigue is prevalent across healthcare settings, it has a profound impact in the acute hospital environment, where healthcare professionals manage sicker patients and clinicians have to make an average of 15.7 decisions per patient encounter.7 8 Such decision-fatigue in hospital staff has been linked to a higher risk of patient harm.1 2 5 In addition, it has been linked to variation in treatment that can lead to unnecessary cost to healthcare systems.2 3 5 6 For example, an examination of healthcare waste in the USA from 2012 to 2019 found that such variability in care was estimated to cost US$8 billion per year.9

Automated decision-support tools and prompts embedded within patients’ electronic health records have been identified as a potential solution to mitigate the impact of decision-fatigue by reducing healthcare professionals’ cognitive load. These tools assist clinicians by providing recommendations and actionable advice to support decision-making.2 Artificial intelligence (AI) risk recommendation systems (hereafter referred to as AI risk recommenders), commonly embedded within Clinical Decision Support Systems (AI-CDSS), are designed to highlight patient risks and generate recommendations for healthcare professionals. Such AI-enhanced systems offer personalised insights and treatment recommendations based on the data available in patients’ electronic health record.10,12

The provision of such information can reduce the risk of error in healthcare professionals’ assessment and judgement, as they can build on AI-generated prompts to guide their reasoning and potentially revise initial decisions they may not have otherwise reconsidered.13,15 The impact of such AI systems on healthcare professionals’ decision-making was demonstrated in a recent scoping review, which found that clinicians supported by AI performed better than unassisted clinicians in decision-making in 61 of 86 (71%) randomised controlled trials.16

Trust and acceptance of artificial intelligence by healthcare professionals

Despite growing evidence of their technical performance, trust and acceptance of AI in routine clinical decision-making remain major barriers to widespread uptake.15 This has been attributed to various reasons, such as low transparency of algorithms, unclear instructions on how to proceed following the AI risk recommenders’ alert and low trust among healthcare professionals in the recommendations provided.17 Trust has been variably defined across disciplines, but is commonly understood as a willingness to rely on an agent despite uncertainty regarding outcomes.18 Acceptance, when applied to technology such as AI, refers to an individual’s intention to use that technology in their setting.19 20

Although analytically distinct, trust and acceptance are closely related and both are necessary for clinicians to meaningfully engage with and use AI risk recommenders in clinical decision-making. Trust enables clinicians to rely on AI recommendations, particularly in situations characterised by uncertainty or ambiguity,18 21 22 while acceptance reflects their willingness to incorporate the system into their workflow and decision processes.20 23 From this perspective, trust and acceptance can be viewed as separate judgements by healthcare professionals that together lead to the usage of AI risk recommenders. Hence, it is essential to study both constructs and how they contribute to subsequent usage of such systems.

Evidence gaps on understanding mechanisms of trust and acceptance on adoption of AI

Five recent literature reviews have examined trust and acceptance of AI among healthcare professionals.24,28 However, important gaps remain about the understanding of how trusted, acceptable AI systems can be built and implemented for healthcare professionals and how specific system features translate into trust and acceptance. A key limitation of these reviews is their tendency to treat trust and acceptance as endpoints. As a result, there is no exploration of actual system usage or the mechanisms that lead to this desired outcome. This leads to a paucity of information on how specific system features, design strategies or implementation approaches can be established to foster trust and acceptance and why these approaches may be effective in some contexts but not in others.

For example, with regard to trust, one review sought to explore constraints and facilitators of trust in AI-CDSS by clinicians, focusing on qualitative studies published from 2020 to 2024.24 While this review identified themes such as the need for system transparency, human-centric design principles and rigorous validation to enhance trust, it did not examine how these elements led towards trust development.24 Another review explored explainability and integrability of medical AI systems as means of increasing acceptance, drawing on literature from 2014 to 2024.28 While this review highlighted the importance of communicating an AI system’s confidence or certainty of its prediction to improve acceptance, it did not address how such information should be effectively designed and communicated in an AI system’s output to subsequently achieve this outcome.28 As a result, existing reviews largely describe factors associated with trust and acceptance but provide limited insight into how, why and under what circumstances these factors influence clinicians’ use of AI systems.

There is a clear need for better understanding of how trust and acceptance can be effectively fostered in healthcare professionals exposed to AI risk recommenders, thereby supporting sustained and meaningful usage. Moreover, while the development and implementation of AI decision support systems are rapidly increasing, their potential benefit for clinical decision-making may not be fully realised without a robust understanding of how to engender trust and acceptability among end users.17 29

As the ultimate endpoint of interest is system usage, which we posit is facilitated through the mechanisms of trust and acceptance, we need an explanatory approach to better understand what would make an AI risk recommender work in the different contexts of usage in healthcare settings. This can be best achieved through a rapid realist review to synthesise the key insights and strategies that engender trust and acceptance of AI risk recommenders by healthcare professionals and hence their usage.30 31

Review objectives

The objective of our rapid realist review is to address the question ‘How and why do mechanisms such as trust and acceptance influence healthcare professionals’ use of AI risk recommenders, and in what context does this occur?’

Methods and analysis

Design

Realist reviews are synthesised insights from the literature to unpack the mechanisms that contribute to the successful outcomes of a programme or initiative, merging both quantitative and qualitative data to better analyse their processes and impacts.32 Such reviews offer a greater degree of flexibility to enhance understanding of the ingredients to make complex interventions succeed, such as the delivery of new healthcare programmes and nursing care protocols, providing greater explanation on both what is needed for success, the contexts in which they work and their circumstances.30 33 34 As a result, the realist method is well-positioned to fill the gap in evidence highlighted above, focusing on how healthcare professionals establish trust and acceptance of AI risk recommenders and hence adopt and use them.

With a wide number of AI risk recommenders developed to date, covering domains from the prediction of inpatient falls to the prediction of patient deterioration,35 36 it is critical that we establish the evidence and insights to guide how they can be best developed and introduced to lead to meaningful usage by healthcare professionals. The urgent need for such synthesised insights to guide healthcare professionals and policymakers points to the need for an accelerated approach to insight generation, such as the rapid realist approach, using a targeted outcome of interest and a narrowed search strategy.31 The use of ‘rapid’ is a reflection of our pragmatic adaptation of the realist method to meet such time-sensitive needs. This approach has been successfully deployed when synthesising evidence to guide the usage of novel technologies, such as the use of telemental health to guide service provision during the COVID-19 pandemic,37 and the development of new interventions such as the first pathway for frail older persons in Ireland.38 Our rapid realist review will allow us to retain the core ingredients of realist inquiry (understanding how, why and in what contexts interventions work) while streamlining processes such as the scope, search and synthesis.31

Proposed approach

Following the steps of realist review and synthesis as described by Rycroft-Malone, we will first define the scope of our rapid realist review, forming the basis of the questions for our review.30 Through initial review of relevant extant theories related to our topic, we will generate an initial programme theory on the antecedents and processes through which trust and acceptance of these systems are established among healthcare professionals, and how these mechanisms contribute to subsequent system use. This will then be refined in subsequent steps through our review of articles to provide explanatory insights that can inform future design, implementation, and evaluation of AI risk recommenders in healthcare settings. The steps of reporting our protocol are aligned with the Realist And Meta-narrative Evidence Syntheses: Evolving Standards I (RAMESES I) guidelines for realist reviews.39 Our review has been registered on PROSPERO (CRD420251155251). Article searching began on 15 Nov 2025, full-text screening is ongoing. The review is expected to be completed by May 2026.

Conceptual framing and programme theory development

The development and refinement of programme theory will be guided by the context–mechanism–outcome (CMO) heuristic that underpins realist inquiry. In line with the conceptualisation proposed by Dalkin et al,40 mechanisms will be understood as comprising two interrelated components: Mechanism (Resources) and Mechanism (Reasoning). Resources refer to the features, strategies, or actions introduced by the AI risk recommender or its implementation (eg, design features, explanations, training, deployment strategies), while reasoning refers to the cognitive, emotional, or motivational responses these resources elicit among healthcare professionals (eg, trust, acceptance).

In this review, outcomes will be conceptualised as healthcare professionals’ usage of AI risk recommenders—their interaction with the recommender and adoption of its output into their decision-making. This would allow us to include studies that involve healthcare professionals’ engagement with fully-deployed AI risk recommender systems,41,43 to think-aloud studies and experiments with healthcare professionals engaging with prototypes of AI risk recommenders.44,46 Context will encompass the individual, organisational and technical conditions under which these systems are introduced and used, and which shape whether and how mechanisms are triggered. Through this framing, the review will focus on micro-level processes to explain how specific system features and implementation strategies interact with clinicians’ reasoning in particular contexts to produce observed patterns of use. figure 1 demonstrates a visualisation of the C–M–O as described above.

The steps of our rapid realist review will be adapted from Rycroft-Malone et al’s guidance on conducting a realist review, which they have broken into four steps—Step 1: defining scope of review and identifying potential theories, Step 2: searching for and appraising the evidence, Step 3: extracting and synthesising findings and Step 4: developing a narrative synthesis.30

Step 1: define scope of review and identify potential theories

We defined ‘usage’ as healthcare professionals’ interaction with the AI risk recommender and adoption of its output into their decision-making. This led to our inclusion of aspects such as engagement with the AI risk recommender in the manner it was intended for, reliance on the AI risk recommender and its output and adoption of the AI risk recommender in their practice. Recognising that the applications and hence usage of AI risk recommenders vary across studies, we will be iteratively expanding and adapting this definition to cover the breadth of examples we find in the literature. We then sought to identify potential theories that explain trust, acceptance and usage, identifying the key elements—our potential Mechanism (Resources)—that lead to healthcare professionals experiencing greater trust and acceptance in the AI risk recommender and hence use it.

As a starting point, we will leverage the Theory of Trust and Acceptance of Artificial Intelligence Technology (TrAAIT) as our primary potential theory.47 Drawing on theories of Trust,18 21 48 49 the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) and Information Systems Success,23 50 this theory explains factors that predict trust and acceptance of AI systems by healthcare professionals in their clinical setting. As shown in figure 2, it suggests that healthcare professionals assess three qualities of an AI system before deciding to trust in it: (1) information credibility—whether the AI system provides information that is up to date, accurate and of sufficient quality, (2) system performance, whether the AI system is reliable and adaptable to their needs and (3) application value, whether they find the AI system easy to use and their satisfaction with it. These qualities are aligned with the quality of Ability from the Integrative Model of Organisation Trust, Effort Expectancy from UTAUT and Performance Expectancy from UTAUT, respectively.23 Once healthcare professionals assess these three qualities and decide to put their trust in the AI system, they will then choose to accept and use it.47

Theory of Trust and Acceptance of Artificial Intelligence Technology (TrAAIT) adapted from Stevens et al.47 AI, artificial intelligence.

The relationship between healthcare professionals’ trust and acceptance is also improved by the presence of organisational assurances (the degree of organisational support and availability of super-users to support healthcare professionals’ usage of the AI system), their exposure and length of time using the AI system (termed as user experience) and age, with older healthcare professionals placing a greater weight on the trustworthiness of the AI system as a determinant of whether they will choose to accept it.47 Through TrAAIT, trust and acceptance are not outcomes but sequential processes shaped by how healthcare professionals interpret, experience and reason about AI risk recommenders within their specific clinical contexts.40 51

Other potential theories we will draw on are the Integrative Model of Organisational Trust, due to its widespread usage to guide the development of other theories to study trust in technology and autonomous systems,21 22 and the UTAUT model, a harmonised model for acceptance of organisational technologies by employees.23 We will continue to search and integrate other relevant theories that we find in the literature. These theories will guide us in locating literature for insights on the Mechanism (Resources) that stimulate the responses of trust and acceptance, which in turn lead to system usage. For example, our background literature search found two Mechanism (Resources) so far—Explainable AI (XAI) and effective communication and explanation by the AI risk recommenders that would better resonate with healthcare professionals.52,55

Research to date has frequently focused on improving system transparency through approaches such as XAI and the communication of predictive uncertainty, with the intention of supporting clinicians in interpreting and appropriately using and relying on AI outputs.52,54 However, emerging evidence suggests that such approaches may not consistently produce the intended effects if not used in the right context or communicated effectively. For example, a recent experiment studied the effect of XAI and uncertainty communication on medical students’ interpretations of an AI risk recommender’s prediction for ovarian and colon cancer.56 Some of the model’s explanation of its predictions using non-clinically relevant features conflicted with the students’ clinical reasoning, confusing them. In addition, the students found it hard to understand the AI system’s explanation of certainty behind its predictions. This led to the students expressing less trust in the system as they could not understand its logic and rationalise its conflict with their own clinical knowledge. This highlights the possibility that design features intended to promote trust may instead trigger unintended reasoning processes that undermine confidence in AI-CDSS, depending on how information is presented and interpreted within specific user contexts.

In contrast, the delivery of appropriately contextualised communication and explanation of an AI risk recommender’s outputs could facilitate healthcare professionals’ interpretation of its insights.55 This could provide the information for better decision-making if the recommendations could be relied on, or if a meaningful override is warranted.

The combination of such insights as described above will inform the development of an initial programme theory, which will be iteratively refined through subsequent review of the literature.

Step 2: search for and appraise the evidence

Article selection

We will apply recommendations from the Cochrane Rapid Reviews Methods Group to ensure a rigorous article selection and appraisal process within a specified timeframe.57,59

Search strategy

Consistent with realist methodology, we will draw on a broad range of evidence, including qualitative, quantitative and mixed-methods studies. Searches and analytic decisions will be iterative and theory-driven, with additional searching undertaken where necessary to test, refine or extend emerging programme theories.

Our search will aim to identify studies that provide insight into the resources introduced by AI risk recommenders and how these influence healthcare professionals’ reasoning, trust, acceptance and subsequent usage of the system. The preliminary search strategy has been developed in conjunction with our university librarian and is detailed in online supplemental file 1. Our article search and retrieval of articles from sources was completed on 15 November 2025.

Sources

We will use the following databases for our search strategy—MEDLINE (Ovid), EMBASE (Elsevier), the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL (EBSCOhost)), PubMed, The Cochrane Library, The Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) Xplore, The Association for Computing Machinery (ACM) Digital Library, Scopus (Elsevier), Web of Science (Clarivate) and ProQuest Dissertation and Theses. The databases are selected to capture relevant literature covering healthcare, implementation science and human-AI interaction. In addition, we will do backward and forward citation searching on all included articles. We will also search conference proceedings from the key conferences on human-AI interaction—the International Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems, ACM Multimedia and the National Conference of the American Association for Artificial Intelligence.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We will include studies involving healthcare professionals using AI risk recommenders in clinical decision-making within the hospital setting, including doctors, nurses, allied health professionals and pharmacists. Eligible studies will include both quantitative and qualitative studies, literature reviews, theses and dissertations.

We will exclude letters, editorials, commentaries and opinion pieces unless they are built on an earlier research study done by the authors. Studies focusing exclusively on staff not involved in the use or interpretation of AI risk recommenders’ outputs, such as operational, administrative or healthcare support staff, as well as students, will be excluded. We will exclude studies exploring the use of AI risk recommenders in community settings, given our focus on intended use in the hospital environment.

In addition, because our focus is on purpose-built AI risk recommenders with the capacity for improvement and expansion to improve trust and acceptance and hence usage, we will exclude studies examining healthcare professionals’ interactions with ‘off the shelf’ large language models with limited opportunity for enhancement and improvement specifically for healthcare professionals, such as ChatGPT. We also excluded studies that sought healthcare professionals’ feedback and thoughts on ‘AI in general’ as we wanted to scope the use case of AI specifically on risk recommenders.

The review will be limited to English language studies published between 2015 and 2025, in view of the large number of articles published in this field over the past 10 years. For example, when running our search strategy using MEDLINE (Ovid), we retrieved over 2400 articles in English alone. This demonstrates the volume of articles to evaluate and examine within one language alone, leading to our decision to restrict to English. The decision to keep to English alone is a pragmatic one as well, in view of the volume of articles. We chose to commence from 2015 as that marked the launch of IBM Watson Health, one of the earliest initiatives to establish an AI risk recommender for healthcare professionals’ usage.

Article selection process

Article selection will be guided by realist relevance, our assessment if the article provides sufficient details on the Mechanism (Resources) and Mechanism (Reasoning) that contribute towards patterns of usage and engagement with the risk recommender system. Only articles that provide such details will be included, as they will produce the insights and information that contribute to our building and testing of the programme theory. This will be aligned with the RAMESES I quality standards’ definition of article relevance in realist reviews.39 60 All article titles and abstracts will be imported into Covidence for consistent tracking and documentation of articles imported through the search strategy and their selection and assessment. Duplicate articles will be identified and removed using Covidence. One reviewer (GFG) will be the primary screener of titles and abstracts. A second reviewer (C-SA) will check a random sample of 25%, with the minimum target of 80% agreement in the sample checked. This approach of having a random check by the second reviewer instead of a full examination of all articles is an accepted approach in rapid review methodology, allowing a balance of efficiency and rigour.57 Discrepancies will be resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (MEW) where necessary.

Following this, we will then proceed to a full-text assessment of the articles in Covidence to further filter only relevant studies for our review. Just like our previous step, the full-text assessment will be led by GFGJ, with a random sample of 25% checked by C-SA, again targeting the minimum of 80% agreement in the sample checked. Discrepancies will be resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (MEW) where necessary.

Step 3: extract and synthesise findings

Data extraction from articles

One reviewer (GFGJ) will be the primary extractor of data, using a standardised data extraction sheet. Instead of a list of standardised questions like a traditional systematic review, we will tabulate explanatory accounts and details on the study on an Excel sheet, detailing how, why and in what contexts the design, nature or deployment of the AI risk recommender contributed to healthcare professionals’ trust, acceptance and usage. Data extraction will be iterative and driven by our programme theory, with coding linked to the development and refinement of our initial programme theory.

From the articles chosen we will capture the descriptors of context, resources, reasoning, outcomes and rigour and relevance. This approach will meet the best practices when reporting a realist review.30 39 60 Instead of assessing articles’ risk of bias, we will examine their relevance (ie, if they add to our theory) and rigour (ie, if we are able to trust the study’s inferences based on their reported data).30 31 60 We will judge the article authors’ confidence in explanations by iterative theory testing and triangulation across data sources. Similar to the article selection process, a second reviewer (C-SA) will check a random sample of 25%, with the minimum target of 80% agreement in the sample checked. Discrepancies will be resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (MEW) where necessary.

Outcomes to be analysed

Our outcome is usage of the AI risk recommender by healthcare professionals. This would include engagement with the AI risk recommender in the manner it was intended for, reliance on the AI risk recommender and its output and adoption of the AI risk recommender in their practice.

Secondary outcomes are (1) trust in the AI risk recommender’s provided, (2) acceptance or rejection of the AI risk recommender, (3) the proportion of clinical decisions applied that aligned with AI risk recommender’s outputs, (4) the incidence of alerts responded and (5) the incidence of alerts dismissed.

Data synthesis

We will apply realist logic to the analysis of the articles. Extracted data will be coded and synthesised to develop, test and refine the initial programme theories of how, why, for whom and in what contexts AI risk recommenders are trusted, accepted and used by healthcare professionals.

Data synthesis will result in explication of chains of Context–Mechanism–Outcome (C–M–O) that will be used to construct the final programme theory.

Step 4: develop narrative

We will reflect on our initial programme theory and share our interpretation of the validity of the initial programme theory and refinements to be incorporated into a final programme theory with expert stakeholders in the realm of AI risk recommender deployment and usage, such as nurses and doctors overseeing the development and deployment of AI risk recommender. We will ask their thoughts on each of the C–M–O chains and their relevance, and which should be prioritised. We will seek their consensus on identifying which is the most important C–M–O to focus on, and which might be considered irrelevant. This is aligned with the recommended steps of realist review,30 31 61 as their direct experience in this field ensures the insights of practitioners are integrated within our C–M–O chains. These engagements will be achieved through platforms such as email or meetings. If our engagements identify gaps or flaws to our current C–M–O chains, we will change them and assess if additional iterative searching is required before commencing accordingly.

We will then create a narrative describing the development of the initial programme theory and refinements leading to the final programme theory

Discussion

Our protocol provides an outline for a rapid realist review, which seeks to explain how and why AI risk recommenders are used by healthcare professionals. Through our development and refinement of a programme theory through context–mechanism–outcome (C–M–O) configurations,30 40 62 our review will allow a deeper examination of the underlying mechanisms and contexts through which they are engaged.

Our approach is particularly relevant when exploring complex interventions enabled by technology, where outcomes are dependent on the interaction between system design, users and organisational settings, such as the successful integration of AI risk recommenders into healthcare professionals’ practice.24 35 37 43 44 47 Our review can hence contribute to a clearer understanding of how AI risk recommender systems can be implemented, informing the future design and deployment of AI risk recommenders for healthcare professionals.
