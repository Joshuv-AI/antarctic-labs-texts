Biochemical and genetic biomarkers associated with nicotine dependence in Mexican smokers
Authors: Gissela Borrego‐Soto, Yadira Xitlalli Perez‐Paramo, Francisco Hernández‐Cabrera, Fatima Miroslaba Alvarado‐Monroy, Gilberto Borrego, Alejandro Robles‐Zamora, Philip Lazarus, Augusto Rojas‐Martinez
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractCigarette smoking remains an important health concern and is still a leading cause of preventable mortality. Nicotine is the substance responsible for sustained tobacco use and dependence. Identification of biomarkers underlying nicotine dependence behavior is important to identify people at risk for this dependence. In the present study, we identified biochemical and genetic biomarkers of nicotine dependence detected by the Fagerström Test for Nicotine Dependence (FTDN) in Mexican smokers. The nicotine metabolites nicotine‐N′‐oxide, trans‐3′‐hydroxycotinine‐glucuronide (3HC‐O‐Gluc), and nicotine‐N‐Gluc (Gluc) were useful to differentiate nicotine‐dependent from non‐dependent subjects (p < .0001) with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.7818. Genetic variants in CYP2A6, FMO3, and UGT2B7 (rs2431413, rs28363545, and rs7439326, respectively) were associated with nicotine dependence (p = .03, p = .01, p = .01, respectively). Variations in the enzymatic activity of CYP2A6 were associated with altered nicotine‐N′‐oxide and 3HC‐O‐Gluc levels. Decreased urinary levels of 3HC‐O‐Gluc and increased nicotine‐N′‐oxide were associated with a decrease in the functional activity of CYP2A6. A strong positive correlation was observed between the ratio of urinary 3HC/cotinine, a measure of CYP2A6 activity, and the levels of 3HC‐O‐Gluc (p < .0001, r = .6835), while a strong negative correlation was observed with nicotine‐N′‐oxide (p < .0001, r = .6522) in nicotine‐dependent subjects. No correlations were observed in non‐nicotine‐dependent subjects. These data suggest that particular urinary nicotine metabolites and genetic variants involved in nicotine metabolism are useful to identify subjects with nicotine dependence in the Mexican population.

INTRODUCTION

Tobacco use remains an important health concern and is a leading cause of preventable mortality. Smoking cigarettes is associated with many health consequences, including lung and other cancers, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and cardiovascular diseases. 1 Genetic and environmental factors play an important role in smoking initiation and persistence. 2 , 3 Data from twin studies suggest that 33%–71% of the variation in liability to nicotine dependence may be attributed to heritable influences. 4 , 5

Tobacco smoke contains more than 7000 chemicals, and nicotine is the substance responsible for dependence. 6 Nicotine, which mimics the action of acetylcholine, binds and activates nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs). Activation of these receptors results in the release of various neurotransmitters, such as dopamine, which is essential for the reinforcing effects. 7 Prolonged nicotine exposure results in persistent activation of dopaminergic neurons that stimulate the rewarding effects of nicotine. 8 , 9 , 10

Nicotine is mainly metabolized in the liver by the enzymes CYP2A6, UDP‐glucuronosyltransferases (UGTs), and flavin‐containing monooxygenases. 11 The major metabolic pathway for nicotine is hydroxylation by CYP2A6 to produce the unstable nicotine ∆1(5′) iminium ion intermediate, which is then rapidly converted to cotinine by aldehyde oxidase 1 (AOX1). 12 Cotinine can then be oxidized to 3‐hydroxycotinine (3HC), and nicotine, cotinine, and 3HC are all glucuronidated by UGT enzymes. 13 , 14 Nicotine and cotinine are N′‐oxidized to produce nicotine‐N′‐oxide by flavin monooxygenase enzymes (FMO1, FMO2, and FMO3) 15 , 16 and cotinine oxide by CYP2C19 and CYP2A6, 17 respectively. While CYP2A6 has been suggested to play a role in the formation of 4‐hydroxy‐4‐(3‐pyridyl)‐butanoic acid (HPBA), 18 it is presently unclear whether CYP2A6 is the sole enzyme involved in this pathway (Figure 1). 19

Partial loss of functional variants in CYP2A6 is associated with reduced daily cigarette consumption compared to subjects with faster metabolism. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 Thus, genetic variants in nicotine metabolic enzymes might modulate cigarette consumption and nicotine dependence. A variety of studies have identified genes associated with smoking behavior and nicotine dependence, 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 but there are limited data on markers of nicotine metabolism products associated with the risk of dependence. The identification of both specific genetic variants and nicotine‐derived metabolic products underlying nicotine dependence is an important step to identify mechanisms of nicotine dependence and groups of people at risk of nicotine dependence and to implement prevention strategies for tobacco use, including individualized pharmacotherapies. In the present study, we identified biochemical and genetic biomarkers of risk for nicotine dependence, with low and high levels of nicotine dependence defined by FTDN.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study subjects and data collection

This study was approved by the ethics committee of the School of Medicine of the Tecnologico de Monterrey, Mexico (code EGTCT. V.1.1. Date January 18, 2018) and ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki were followed. A group of 96 daily smokers were recruited between 2018 and 2020 in the Monterrey metropolitan area; 92% (88) were subjects from the state of Nuevo Leon, Mexico; 28% (27) were women; and 72% (69) were men. The ancestry of this Mestizo subpopulation in northeastern Mexico has the Native American contribution of 56% (range: 27.4%–81.2%), the European contribution of 38% (range: 16.7%–70.5%), and the West African contribution of 6%. 31 Informed consent was provided by all subjects, and each answered questionnaires about smoking habits, sociodemographic characteristics, and the Fagerström test for Nicotine Dependence (FTND). Approximately 5 mL of peripheral blood anticoagulated with EDTA and 50 mL of urine were collected from each participant, and samples were transported to the laboratory at 4°C. Urine was stored at −80°C in 1 mL aliquots. Genomic DNA was extracted from lymphocytes using a QIAamp DNA Blood Kit (Qiagen) following manufacturer instructions and stored at −20°C until use. All samples were collected in the morning between 9 a.m. and 12 p.m. None of the subjects analyzed in this study used to smoke menthol cigars.

Quantification of urine nicotine metabolites

Nicotine metabolites were examined using an Acquity liquid chromatography—tandem mass spectrometer (LC‐MS/MS system) (Waters Corporation) as described by Chen et al. 13 and Perez‐Paramo et al. 16 Quantification was performed for urinary nicotine and the following urinary nicotine metabolites: nicotine, nicotine‐N′‐oxide, nicotine‐Gluc, cotinine, cotinine‐Gluc, cotinine‐N′‐Oxide, 3HC, 3HC‐O‐Gluc, and 4HPBA. Repeat testing was performed for 15% of all samples chosen randomly to corroborate measurements.

Next‐generation sequencing (NGS) genotyping

A custom panel was designed to sequence 123 SNPs in 43 genes related to nicotine addiction and metabolism (Table S1). Genetic variants associated with nicotine metabolism and dependence were selected based on those reported in the PubMed database. SNPs with allele frequencies equal to or greater than 5% in the population of Mexicans living in Los Angeles reported in the database of the 1000 genomes project (www.1000genomes.org) were chosen. Specific oligonucleotides were designed using Illumina's Design Studio program (Illumina Inc.). The design rendered 116 amplicons, max amplicon length of 375 bp, and 100% coverage. DNA quality and concentrations were evaluated using a Nanodrop 8000 spectrophotometer and (Thermo Scientific) Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies). Library preparation was made using the AmpliSeq Custom amplicon kit following manufacturer guidelines. In brief, 10 ng of genomic DNA was used as a template to amplify DNA targets. DNA library clean‐up was performed using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter). The quality and concentration of the libraries were evaluated in an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, adjusted to a concentration of 12 pM. All variants were analyzed using the DNA Amplicon software (Illumina) and visualized on the Variant Calling software (Illumina) for the human genome assembly hg19 (GRCh37).

Data analysis

Subjects were divided into two groups according to nicotine dependence levels as measured by FTDN: non‐nicotine dependent (NND, no‐, and low‐dependence subjects) and nicotine dependent (ND, low to moderate, moderate to high, and high‐dependence subjects). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test failed to show a normal distribution of all data; therefore, non‐parametric probes were used for comparisons. The Mann–Whitney U‐test for non‐parametric distributions was used to analyze characteristics of the participants like age, body mass index (BMI), and cigarettes per day (CPD), and the Fisher's exact test was used for gender differences between the NND and ND groups. Nicotine and its metabolites were normalized as total nicotine equivalents (TNE, equivalent to the total level of urinary nicotine plus its eight measured metabolites) in separate analyses. Multiple logistic regression (MLR) analysis was performed to determine the specific nicotine metabolite levels that characterize the ND group, and a receiver operating curve (ROC) was executed to analyze the classification model of the two groups. Spearman's (r) correlation coefficients were calculated between normalized nicotine and metabolites against the nicotine metabolite ratio (NMR), defined by the 3HC/cotinine ratio. Subjects were stratified by median into two groups (low and high) according to the NMR, with the normalized levels of nicotine and its metabolites compared within the low and high NMR groups using the Mann–Whitney U‐test. The Kruskal‐Wallis test and the Dunn's multiple comparisons test were used to analyze nicotine and its normalized metabolites within the genotypes of the metabolizing enzymes CYP2A6, FMO3, and UGT2B7 (rs2431413, rs28363545, and rs7439326, respectively). Items from FTND, like time to first cigarette and CPDs, were associated with the genotypes of genes codifying for nicotine metabolizing enzymes using the Fisher's exact test. All analyses were two‐sided and considered significant if p < .05 for all tests.

RESULTS

Subject characteristics

A group of 96 smokers were recruited at the School of Medicine of the Tecnologico de Monterrey. Characteristics of the subjects are shown in Table 1. All subjects reported smoking daily; the average age at which they started smoking was 15 years. The FTDN showed subjects with different levels of tobacco dependence (32% low, 24% low to moderate, 28% moderate to high, and 4% high). No dependence on tobacco was detected in 12% of the participants. The NND group contained 40 subjects (42%; 60% men), and the ND group contained 56 subjects (58%; 80% men). No differences were found in age, age of onset of tobacco use, and BMI between the NND and ND groups. However, differences were found between men and women (p = .0386) and in the CPD smoked the last 3 days prior to sample collection as part of the study (p = .0009).

Nicotine metabolites levels and CYP2A6 activity

Decreased CYP2A6 enzyme activity may direct nicotine metabolism to other pathways such as N‐oxidation and glucuronidation. 19 Therefore, we used the NMR (3HC/cotinine), an efficient biomarker of CYP2A6 activity, to analyze the impact of CYP2A6 enzymatic activity on the formation of nicotine‐N′‐oxide and the nicotine and 3HC glucuronides and whether it is correlated with nicotine dependence. A strong correlation was observed between urinary cotinine and 3HC with the urinary NMR (r = −.6791, p < .0001; r = .6257, p < .0001, respectively), validating the use of the NMR as a biomarker of CYP2A6 activity (Figure 3A, B). A positive correlation was observed between the NMR and the 3HC‐O‐Gluc levels (r = .6098, p < .0001; Figure 3E), while a negative correlation was observed for nicotine‐N′‐oxide (r = −.5278, p < .0001; Figure 3F). No correlations were observed between the NMR and nicotine and nicotine‐N‐Gluc (p = .1605; Figure 3C,D). Subjects were also stratified based on the median of high versus low NMR groups. Differences between the lowest versus highest NMR groups were observed for cotinine, 3HC, nicotine, 3HC‐O‐Gluc, and nicotine‐N′‐oxide (p < .0001; Figure 3G–K), but no differences were observed between the lowest versus highest NMR for nicotine‐N‐Gluc (Figure 3L). Interestingly, strong correlations were found between the NMR and 3HC‐O‐Gluc and nicotine‐N′‐oxide in the ND group (r = .6835, p < .0001; r = −.6522, p < .0001, respectively; Figure 4B',C'), while no correlations were found in the NND group (r = .4919, p = .0015; r = −.3291, p = .0407, respectively; Figure 4B,C). No correlations were observed between NMR and nicotine‐N‐Gluc in the ND and NND groups (Figure 4A,A'). After stratifying by low and high NMRs, significant differences were observed for nicotine‐N‐Gluc, 3HC‐O‐Gluc, and nicotine‐N′‐oxide in the ND group (p = .045, p = .0001, p = .0002, respectively). Differences were only found for 3HC‐O‐Gluc in the NND group (p = .0039; Figure 4D–F').

Nicotine dependence and nicotine metabolizing enzyme genotypes

Readings generated by NGS for all 96 subjects had quality scores higher or equal to Q30 and coverage >1000X. The Hardy–Weinberg Equilibrium of all the variants was calculated, and those that did not show equilibrium were discarded. An average of 123 SNPs, six insertions, and four deletion variants were detected per sample. Approximately 4% were 5′‐untranslated region (UTR) variants, 9% were in the 3′‐UTR, 20% were intergenic, 26% were in the coding region, and 41% were intron variants (Table S2). Of the coding variants, 48% were missense, 1% were nonsense, and 51% were synonymous. Statistical differences were found in 15 genetic variants between the NND and NND groups. Differences were found in genes related to neurotransmission: CHRNA5, CHRNA3, CHRNB4, DRD2, DRD3, GABBR2; and in genes related to nicotine metabolism: CYP2A6, FMO3, and UGT2B7. Figure 5 describes the genetic variants and the differences between the ND and NND groups. Although we found genetic variants in CHRNs, DRD3, and GABBR2 genes, we focused on variants related to the nicotine metabolic pathway.

To analyze the effect of the CYP2A6 rs2431413 variant, we analyzed nicotine metabolites measured as a ratio of TNE. Differences were shown in 3HC, 3HC‐O‐Gluc, and nicotine‐N′‐oxide levels between wild‐type and variant genotypes (p = .015, p = .0113, and p = .0070, respectively; Figure 6), as recently described by Perez‐Paramo et al. 19 No differences were observed between CYP2A6 genotypes and other metabolite levels. Likewise, we analyzed the effect of the FMO3 rs28363545 variant on the metabolite levels measured as a ratio of TNE. Differences were observed in 3HC‐O‐Gluc levels between wild‐type versus variant and between heterozygotes versus variant genotypes (p = .0229 and p = .0108, respectively), suggesting that this variant indirectly affects 3HC‐O‐Gluc levels (Figure 7). Likewise, Bloom et al. have reported evidence that polymorphisms in FMO3 indirectly influence nicotine clearance. 34 Further studies are needed to study the effects of this variant on nicotine metabolite levels.

Analysis of nicotine dependence phenotype versus nicotine metabolic enzymes genotypes

The first item of the FTND (time to first cigarette) has been demonstrated to be the most informative metric for nicotine dependence. 35 CPD is also an FTND item potentially related to nicotine dependence. 36 We found that 19% of CYP2A6 rs2431413 wild‐type allele homozygotes versus 43% of variant carriers (heterozygous and variant homozygous) reported smoking within the 6–30 min after awakening (p < .0001). Similarly, 15% of wild‐type homozygous subjects reported smoking within 31–60 min after awakening, while 22% of variant carriers reported the same urgency to smoke (p = .0015). For FMO3, 11% of subjects homozygous for the rs28363545 wild‐type allele reported smoking within less than 5 min after awakening, while 32% of variant carriers reported the same urgency to smoke (p < .0001). Likewise, 6% of subjects homozygous for the UGT2B7 rs7439326 wild‐type allele versus 22% of variant allele carriers reported smoking within less than 5 min after awakening (p = .001) (Figure 8; Table 3). No statistical differences were found between genetic variants and CPD, with only CYP2A6 rs2431413 major allele homozygotes exhibiting differences, potentially due to a small sample size (Table S4).

DISCUSSION

In the present study, we describe metabolic and genetic markers of nicotine dependence as determined by the FTDN. We found that the urinary nicotine metabolites 3HC‐O‐Gluc and nicotine‐N′‐oxide are useful markers to discriminate ND from NND smokers. In addition, we found that the CYP2A6 rs2431413, FMO3 rs28363545, and UGT2B7 rs7439326 variants are associated with nicotine dependence. The CYP2A6 rs2431413 variant was also associated with an increase and decrease, respectively, in urinary nicotine‐N′‐oxide and 3HC‐O‐Gluc levels. Although UGT2B7 rs7439326 variant was associated with nicotine dependence, no changes in urinary 3HC‐O Gluc levels were observed.

Several studies have identified biomarkers for nicotine metabolism and tobacco exposure. However, useful biomarkers to define nicotine dependence are still limited to the analysis of TNE or the most abundant nicotine metabolites such as cotinine, 3HC, and their glucuronides. Benowitz et al., report that the molar sum of urinary levels of nicotine, cotinine, and 3HC can be used as a biomarker of daily nicotine intake. 37

Muhammad‐Kah et al., found weak to moderate associations between urinary TNE in 24 h urines and nicotine dependence scores. 38 Bergen et al., reported correlations between urinary NMR and TNE with FTND score in the Multiethnic Cohort Study. 39 Overmeire et al. found correlations between urinary levels of nicotine, cotinine, and 3HC with FTND score, especially with time before the first cigarette after waking. 40 Jung et al. found that urinary cotinine levels are useful to predict high nicotine dependence with an AUC = 0.82. 41 Salivary cotinine levels were associated with nicotine dependence measured by the FTND, especially with daily tobacco consumption and time to first cigarette after waking. 42 In the present study, we show that the levels of all the nicotine metabolites examined, except for nicotine‐N‐Gluc and cotinine‐N′‐Gluc, were different between the NND and ND groups. A MLR analysis indicated that the best discriminators between NND and ND groups were 3HC‐O‐Gluc and nicotine‐N′‐oxide.

We found that nicotine‐N′‐oxide is a potential biomarker for nicotine dependence. Nicotine‐N′‐oxide formation accounts for 3%–5% of total urinary nicotine metabolites in humans. 33 , 43 Nicotine is oxidized to nicotine‐N′‐oxide by FMO1, FMO2, and FMO3, 44 which are expressed both hepatically and in extrahepatic tissues including brain. 45 In rats, nicotine N′‐oxide has been found to be the second most abundant nicotine metabolite in blood and brain. 46 Teitelbaum et al. demonstrated that nicotine is N‐oxidized in the brain by FMO3 and that nicotine‐N′‐oxide positively modulates human α4β2 nicotinic receptor activity. 47 In the present study, FMO3 genotypes did not modify urinary nicotine‐N′‐oxide levels, but we observed that the FMO3 rs28363545 homozygous wild‐type T/T genotype protected against nicotine dependence (p = .02, OR = 0.32), while FMO3 genotypes containing the rs28363545 variant deviate from this behavior. In addition, we found differences in 3HC‐O‐Gluc levels between FMO3 rs28363545 heterozygotes and homozygotes variant genotypes, suggesting an indirect effect of this polymorphism on 3HC metabolism. Likewise, Bloom et al. have reported evidence that polymorphisms in FMO3 indirect effect upon another nicotine metabolism pathway like nicotine to cotinine conversion. 34

Additional studies are necessary to evaluate the enzymatic activity of the rs28363545 variant of FMO3. As was reported by Perez‐Paramo et al, the enzyme activity of the FMO3 rs72549322, rs12072582, rs2266782, and rs1736557 variants showed lower catalytic efficiencies compared to wild‐type FMO3 alleles. 16 Other genetic variants in FMO3 have been described that are associated with decreased levels of urinary nicotine‐N′‐oxide 16 , 48 , 49 , 50 or have been related to levels of nicotine dependence, 47 , 51 , 52 but they fail to show associations in the present study. Analyses of genetic variants in FMO3 have shown similar allelic frequencies among Caucasians, African, Asians, Americans, and Hispanics populations. 53

In contrast, in subjects with deficient CYP2A6 activity, increased levels of nicotine‐N′‐oxide have been found. 16 , 19 This deficiency causes rerouting of nicotine metabolism to other pathways, including nicotine oxidation and nicotine glucuronidation, with a corresponding decrease in 3HC‐O‐Gluc formation. 19 In the present study, deficiencies in CYP2A6 activity as measured by the NMR resulted in increased levels of nicotine‐N′‐oxide and decreased levels of 3HC‐O‐Gluc, particularly in ND subjects. The rs2431413 SNP in CYP2A6 has been associated with decreased enzyme function. 54 By stratifying subjects by genotype, subjects homozygous for the G variant had significantly decreased levels of 3HC and 3HC‐O‐Gluc and increased levels of nicotine‐N′‐oxide.

It has been hypothesized that slow nicotine metabolizers would smoke fewer cigarettes and less often than fast nicotine metabolizers. 21 , 22 , 55 Although the association of CYP2A6 enzymatic activity with nicotine dependence is not clear, there is some evidence suggesting this association. Butler et al. reported an association between high CYP2A6 activity and increased nicotine reinforcement. 21 Kubota et al. reported that CYP2A6 genotypes correlated with the number of CPD and that subjects with high enzyme activity smoked the first cigarette of the day earlier than low‐activity group, suggesting an association with nicotine dependence. 22 However, Bagdas et al. found in a mouse model that inhibition of CYP2A5 (ortholog of human CYP2A6) induced enhanced nicotine preference and increased plasma levels of nicotine. 20 We observed that subjects homozygous for the CYP2A6 rs2431413 variant (G/G) had higher levels of nicotine‐N′‐oxide, lower levels of 3HC‐O‐Gluc, and were prone to nicotine dependence. No differences were found in nicotine and cotinine levels between rs2431413 CYP2A6 genotypes.

Up to 25% of nicotine metabolism can be explained by glucuronidation. 56 Variants in the genes involved in nicotine glucuronidation influence the clearance of nicotine and its metabolites, and therefore could impact nicotine dependence phenotype. 13 , 33 , 57 However, genetic variants in UGTs enzymes associated with nicotine dependence have not been reported. 58 We found associations of the variant rs7439326 of UGT2B7 with nicotine dependence. This enzyme glucuronidates 3HC. Other UGTs like UGT2B17 and UGT1A9 also participate in 3HC‐Gluc formation; however, we did not find variants associated with nicotine dependence in these genes. We did, however, find that high levels of 3HC‐O‐Gluc in nicotine dependence subjects may be influenced by NMR.

Time to first cigarette has been demonstrated to be the most informative metric for nicotine dependence in the FTDN. 35 We found a strong association between this item and variant carriers (heterozygous plus homozygous) of rs2431413 (CYP2A6), rs28363545 (FMO3), and rs7439326 (UGT2B7). We also found associations between CPD and the CYP2A6 rs2431413 variant, while no associations were found with the FMO3 and UGT2B7 variants. The fact that no associations were observed for CPD for the latter two variants is consistent with previous studies suggesting that there is no relationship between CPD in highly dependent subjects 59 , 60 and that time to first cigarette is the stronger determinant item of nicotine dependence in the FTDN. 61 , 62 , 63

Limitations of this study include the small sample size. An increase in the number of samples and the analysis of the variables found in this study in other populations would validate our findings. Another limitation is that we did not analyze the functional effect of genetic variants on enzyme activity. Further studies are needed to study the effects of these genetic variants on nicotine metabolite levels and nicotine dependence. An understanding of the mechanisms of nicotine dependence involving these genetic variants and the levels of nicotine metabolites will contribute to the treatment and prevention of nicotine dependence and therefore smoking‐related diseases.

In conclusion, we found differences in nicotine metabolism and nicotine metabolite levels between ND and NND groups, as well as genetic variants in nicotine metabolism enzymes playing a role in nicotine dependence behavior. Our data propose relevant biochemical (nicotine‐N′‐oxide and 3HC‐O‐Gluc) and genetic (rs2431413, rs28363545 and rs7439326) biomarkers of nicotine dependence in a Mexican population. Additional studies are needed to corroborate these findings.
