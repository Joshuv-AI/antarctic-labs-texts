MMP12 exacerbates pulmonary inflammation by enhancing IL-17A expression
Authors: Yuling Xu, Xun Wang, Ning Yang, Xinyu Yang, Lin Gu, Zhaohui Tong, Nan Song, Jieqiong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Community-acquired respiratory distress syndrome (CARDS) toxin is a key driver of Mycoplasma pneumoniae–mediated disease, yet its pathogenic mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Here, we established female C57BL/6J murine pneumonia models by intratracheal injection of CARDS toxin (700 pmol per mouse). In wild-type mice (n = 3 per group), single-cell and bulk RNA sequencing were performed to characterize functional alterations of lung macrophages. Pulmonary inflammation was evaluated by immunostaining and PCR analyses in wild-type (n = 3), Lyz2-Mmp12−/− (n = 4), and Mmp12-CreERT2×Rosa26-iDTR mice (n = 5). To interrogate downstream signaling, interleukin-17A (IL-17A) signaling was blocked using a neutralizing antibody. Clinical relevance was assessed using proteomic analysis and ELISA measurements of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) proteins from disease control (n = 26) and M. pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP, n = 58) patients. We demonstrate that CARDS toxin exposure markedly increases the abundance of Mmp12high macrophages in the murine lung, accompanied by overactivation of pro-inflammatory cytokine signaling. Macrophage-specific Mmp12 knockout attenuated CARDS toxin-induced pulmonary inflammation. Mechanistically, MMP12 promoted T helper 17 cells activation and subsequent IL-17A secretion via SPP1 and FN1, thereby exacerbating pulmonary inflammation. Notably, IL-17A blockade significantly mitigated these responses. Furthermore, MMP12 and IL-17A levels were elevated in the BALF of MPP patients. Collectively, these findings define a pro-inflammatory MMP12–IL-17A axis mediating CARDS toxin-driven pulmonary pathology, highlighting promising therapeutic targets for managing M. pneumoniae–associated respiratory disease.

INTRODUCTION

Mycoplasma pneumoniae is a major cause of community-acquired respiratory infections in children [1,2]. Although most M. pneumoniae infections are generally mild and self-limited, about 25% of patients require hospitalization because of severe pneumonia or extrapulmonary complications [3,4]. M. pneumoniae adheres to the host respiratory epithelial cell surface, leading to motile ciliary dysfunction, intracellular metabolism alterations, and nutrient depletion [5,6]. Early therapeutic intervention is therefore critical for preventing progression to severe pneumonia.

Macrolides, quinolones, and tetracyclines are commonly used to treat M. pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP) in adults [7]. However, the increasing prevalence of macrolide-resistant M. pneumoniae strains in recent years has reduced the efficacy of antibiotic therapy [8], thereby complicating antibiotic selection for pediatric patients with MPP. A deeper understanding of the pathogenesis of M. pneumoniae is crucial for identifying therapeutic targets and effectively combating the current epidemic.

Community-acquired respiratory distress syndrome (CARDS) toxin, an adenosine diphosphate–ribosylating and vacuolating cytotoxin, is a key virulence factor produced by M. pneumoniae [9,10]. CARDS toxin directly binds to the surface receptors on host respiratory epithelial cells and is internalized through clathrin-mediated endocytosis, leading to cell swelling, nuclear lysis, and cell vacuolization that ultimately results in cell apoptosis [[11], [12], [13]]. In addition, CARDS toxin alone can induce motile cilia stagnation and activate the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, thereby triggering the release of pro-inflammatory mediators and reproducing cytopathic features similar to those observed during M. pneumoniae infection [14,15]. Levels of CARDS toxin positively correlate with disease severity and the toxin can persist in patients for up to two years while eliciting strong immunogenic responses [16,17]. The novel CARDS toxin amino acid fingerprint sequence KELED (amino acids 268−272) is essential for CARDS toxin-mediated cytotoxicity [18]. Despite these advances, the molecular circuitry through which CARDS toxin drives inflammatory responses remains incompletely understood, highlighting the need to identify the immune cell populations and inflammatory mediators involved in this process.

Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) are a class of zinc proteases that exhibit elastase activity and degrade extracellular matrix components, including fibronectin, laminin, and both fibrillar and non-fibrillar collagens [[19], [20], [21]]. Because of their marked and disease-specific overexpression, MMPs have been widely recognized as biomarkers for disease monitoring, diagnosis, and therapeutic evaluation [22,23]. Matrix metalloproteinase 12 (MMP12) is a macrophage-derived metalloproteinase implicated in a broad range of pathological conditions, including multiple cancers, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, tissue fibrosis, and neurological disorders [24]. Notably, MMP12 can exhibit either pro-inflammatory or anti-inflammatory effects depending on the tissue context and disease state [25,26]. However, its role in M. pneumoniae infection-induced lung disease and the regulatory mechanism involved remain unknown.

The interleukin-17 (IL-17) family, consisting of six cytokine members (IL-17A−IL-17F), is critical in regulating inflammatory responses. IL-17A is primarily produced by T helper 17 (Th17) cells, γδ T cells, macrophages, and neutrophils, and plays a crucial protective function in mucosal immunity during bacterial and fungal infections. Excessive IL-17A is frequently associated with various inflammatory and autoimmune diseases [27]. However, its functional hierarchy and molecular crosstalk in toxin-induced pulmonary inflammation remain unelucidated.

Here, we identified that MMP12 is upregulated upon administration of M. pneumoniae CARDS toxin. Our findings establish the MMP12–IL-17A that contributes to toxin-induced lung injury and suggest that chemical modulators targeting the MMP12 or IL-17 pathways may serve as a potential therapeutic strategy for diseases associated with M. pneumoniae infection.

RESULTS

CARDS Toxin Triggers Pulmonary Inflammation in Murine Models

Mice were intratracheally given recombinant CARDS toxin to simulate M. pneumoniae infection. To map CARDS toxin distribution in lung tissue, we administered green fluorescent protein (GFP)-tagged CARDS toxin intratracheally, which showed uniform localization in the lungs (Figure S1). Heat-inactivated CARDS toxin (15 min at 100 °C) was used as a control [9]. To exclude the possible contribution of Escherichia coli endotoxin in recombinant protein preparations, we use a His-tagged KALAD sequence mutation CARDS toxin (CARDSmut) purified from E. coli as an additional control. As revealed by hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining, lung tissue from CARDS toxin-treated mice exhibited significantly exacerbated pulmonary pathological lesions, compared with those from control and CARDSmut toxin-treated mice (Figure 1A). A histopathological scoring system was used to assess the lung pathology [28] and scores in CARDS toxin-treated mice were higher than those in control and CARDSmut toxin-treated mice (Figure 1B). The expression of pro-inflammatory cytokine genes, including Il-1β, Il-6, Il-17a, Cxcl5, and Ccl22, was significantly upregulated in the lungs of CARDS toxin-treated mice (Figure 1C). Additionally, increased numbers of CD68+ macrophages and neutrophils (Ly6G+ cells) were observed in the lungs of the CARDS group (Figure 1D–1E). Among these, 90 genes were overlapping. Using the Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID), Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis showed that CARDS toxin-induced DEGs were enriched in immune- and inflammatory-related pathways (Figure S2). Mmp12, a protease-encoding gene involved in macrophage migration and inflammatory regulation, was significantly upregulated by CARDS toxin (Figure 2A–2C). The mRNA expression of Mmp12 was confirmed by real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) (Figure 2E). MMP12 protein expression strongly accumulated following CARDS toxin treatment (Figure 2F–2G). Further analysis revealed that Mmp12 was predominantly expressed in macrophages (Figures 3E and S3A). A subset of macrophages with high Mmp12 expression emerged in the CARDS toxin-treated group, which also highly expressed Fabp5, Spp1, Trem2, and Gpnmb (Figures 3F and S3B–S3D). We retrieved a set of inflammatory and cytokine genes and calculated the scores based on the expression levels of established inflammatory response and cytokine genes (Table S2) [38,39]. Mmp12high macrophages exhibited significantly higher inflammatory and cytokine scores than other macrophages (Figures 3G–3H and S3E–S3F). Collectively, these data indicate that CARDS toxin-enriched Mmp12high macrophages are the primary source of pro-inflammatory cytokines, further suggesting that Mmp12 may drive pulmonary inflammation by upregulating cytokine expression.

Furthermore, consistent with in vivo findings, CARDS toxin significantly enhanced the expression of Mmp12 and pro-inflammatory factors in both murine bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) and human THP-1 cells (Figure S4). To elucidate how CARDS toxin drives Mmp12 expression in macrophages, we conducted an in-depth analysis of our scRNA-seq data. This analysis revealed that CARDS toxin-upregulated genes in macrophages were significantly enriched in the functional categories of structural constituent of ribosome, ribosome subunit, and ribosome (Figure S5). These results suggest that CARDS toxin may exert its regulatory effect on Mmp12 expression in macrophages by remodeling ribosome structure and modulating its translational activity.

Macrophage-specific Mmp12 Knockout Attenuates Pulmonary Inflammation

To investigate the role of Mmp12 in macrophages, we crossed Mmp12flox/flox mice with Lyz2-Cre mice to generate macrophage-specific Mmp12-knockout mice (Lyz2-Mmp12−/− mice). Seven days post-CARDS toxin treatment, histological analysis of lung tissues revealed that pulmonary pathological damage was significantly alleviated in Lyz2-Mmp12−/− mice (Figure 4A). The histopathology score of Lyz2-Mmp12−/− mice was lower than that of their control counterparts (Mmp12flox/flox mice) (Figure 4B). Next, we examined the mRNA expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, which were significantly downregulated in the lungs of Lyz2-Mmp12−/− mice (Figure 4C). Notably, this phenotypic profile of Lyz2-Mmp12−/− mice was consistent with that of Mmp12high macrophages ablation mice, which was generated by the Mmp12-CreERT2×Rosa26-iDTR system and treated with tamoxifen and diphtheria toxin (DT) (Figure S6). Additionally, Lyz2-Mmp12−/− mice showed reduced CD68+ macrophages and neutrophil infiltration compared with Mmp12flox/flox mice (Figure 4D–4E). IL-17A is a Key Mediator of Downstream Signaling in the CARDS–MMP12 Axis

To decipher the effector molecule of the CARDS toxin–MMP12 axis in pulmonary inflammation, we analyzed the bulk transcriptome changes in the lungs with or without CARDS toxin treatment. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis showed that CARDS toxin-induced DEGs were enriched in IL-17 signaling, cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, and Th17 cell differentiation (Figure 5A). scRNA-seq analysis revealed that IL-17 family members, primarily Il-17a and Il-17f, were expressed in lung tissue and were upregulated in the CARDS toxin-treated group (Figure S7). Further analysis revealed that Il-17a was predominantly expressed in T cells (Figure 5B). T cells were further divided into seven clusters based on cell type-specific genes (Figures 5C and S8A). The proportions of Th17 cells, γδ T cells, regulatory T cells, and memory T cells increased in CARDS toxin-exposed lung tissues, while naïve T cells decreased (Figure S8B, Table S1). Furthermore, we next performed CellChat analysis of the scRNA-seq datasets to identify potential cell-to-cell communications between Mmp12high and Th17 cells, defined by secreted ligand genes expressed in Mmp12high cells and their cognate receptor genes expressed in Th17 cells. The results indicated that the communication of the CARDS toxin-treated group was slightly higher than that of the CARDSmut toxin-treated group (Figure 5E). SPP1 and FN1 were identified as the key signaling ligands with a significantly upregulated expression in Mmp12high cells (Figure 5F–5G). CD44, a confirmed substrate of MMP12 [40], serves as a critical mediator of crosstalk between Mmp12high cells and Th17 cells (Figure 5F). Notably, Mmp12 knockout and inhibition of its enzymatic activity resulted in decreased expression of Spp1 and Fn1, as well as a reduction in IL-17A expression (Figures 5H and S9, Table S3). More importantly, we isolated CD4+ T cells from C57BL/6J murine spleens, induced their differentiation into Th17 cells in vitro, and subjected them to treatment with recombinant MMP12, SPP1, or FN1 proteins, respectively. Our data demonstrated that these proteins potentiated Th17 cell activation and elevated IL-17A expression (Figure 5I–5J, Table S3). Taken together, these results suggested that the CARDS–MMP12 axis drives IL-17A secretion via SPP1/FN1 signaling during M. pneumoniae infection.

Blocking IL-17A Signaling Alleviated Pulmonary Inflammation

We administered IL-17A–neutralizing antibody intraperitoneally to specifically block the pulmonary IL-17A signaling pathway (Figure 6A). Anti-IL-17A antibody treatment significantly decreased total protein in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) samples (Figure 6B). As revealed by HE staining, lung tissue damage was significantly attenuated after IL-17A antibody treatment (Figure 6C–6D). Simultaneously, levels of inflammatory cytokines (Il-1β, Il-6, Cxcl5, and Ccl22) and the percentage of inflammatory immune cells (CD68+ macrophages and neutrophils) were significantly reduced in the lungs after IL-17A antibody neutralization (Figure 6E–6G). The IL-17 Signaling was Activated in BALF from Patients with MPP

Finally, we collected and profiled the proteome of BALF samples from patients with MPP and disease controls (DCs) (Figure 7A). In conclusion, we propose that M. pneumoniae CARDS toxin triggers respiratory epithelial apoptosis; cell debris is then recognized by macrophages, inducing MMP12 production. Subsequently, Mmp12high macrophages communicate with Th17 cells via Spp1 and Fn1, boosting IL-17A production and contributing to alveolar damage and chronic inflammation. Our findings establish a critical pro-inflammatory axis involving MMP12 and IL-17A that mediates CARDS toxin-induced pulmonary pathology, uncovering potential therapeutic targets for mitigating M. pneumoniae-associated respiratory disease.

DISCUSSION

M. pneumoniae is a leading cause of community-acquired pneumonia in humans, particularly children [1,2]. While most M. pneumoniae infections are mild and not life-threatening, a subset of cases progresses to severe or refractory pneumonia. Beyond inducing diverse respiratory tract symptoms [41], M. pneumoniae can also cause extrapulmonary diseases such as meningoencephalitis, myocarditis, and nephritis [4,42,43]. However, the molecular mechanisms through which M. pneumoniae infection drives pulmonary inflammation remain incompletely understood.

The CARDS toxin is a unique exotoxin produced by M. pneumoniae that induces cell swelling and vacuolization [9,10]. Previous studies have shown that M. pneumoniae CARDS toxin can trigger NLRP3 inflammasome activation and the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines in macrophages via MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway [15]. To further investigate the mechanisms, we developed a mouse model of CARDS toxin-induced inflammation. Consistent with previous studies, bronchial epithelial damage, alveolar septum thickening, and inflammatory cell infiltration were observed in CARDS-treated mice. Pro-inflammatory cytokine levels were also significantly upregulated in the lung tissues following CARDS exposure. Together, these findings confirm that the CARDS toxin, as the only toxin produced by M. pneumoniae, plays an essential role in pulmonary inflammation. However, inflammation induced by CARDS toxin differs from that observed in the M. pneumoniae infection model, as it only represents the effect of a single virulence factor. Thus, future studies incorporating both models will be required to further validate the physiological and pathological relevance of our findings.

MMP12, a macrophage-derived metalloproteinase involved in extracellular matrix degradation [24,44], has been implicated in numerous pathological conditions, including multiple cancers [[45], [46], [47]], chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [48,49], asthma [50], tissue fibrosis, and neurological disorders [[51], [52], [53]]. Notably, its functional role can vary depending on disease context, exhibiting either pro-inflammatory or anti-inflammatory activities. CD44, a well-established substrate of MMP12, functions as a critical mediator of communication between Mmp12high macrophages and Th17 cells. Furthermore, we demonstrated that SPP1 and FN1 act as mediators of crosstalk between Mmp12high cells and Th17 cells, suggesting that Mmp12high macrophages may promote IL-17A secretion via the SPP1/FN1 axis.

Future studies will be required to elucidate whether MMP12-mediated CD44 cleavage directly regulates IL-17A production in Th17 cells. Macrophages are the predominant immune cells present in the alveoli of humans and mice, maintaining homeostasis by removing endogenous proteins and exogenous pathogens [54,55]. We demonstrated that CARDS toxin drives Mmp12 expression in macrophages by altering ribosome architecture and activity. Additionally, Mmp12-positive macrophages highly expressed Spp1, Fabp5, Trem2, and Gpnmb, similar to the recently described pulmonary macrophage subpopulation [40,56]. Thus, Mmp12high macrophages share phenotypic similarities with tissue-resident alveolar macrophages while remaining functionally distinct. Consistent with its regulatory effect on MMP12, CARDS toxin-mediated remodeling of ribosome architecture and activity may act as a molecular trigger to drive Spp1 secretion in macrophages.

The IL-17 cytokine family comprises six members (IL-17A to IL-17F) that mediate their functions through IL-17 receptors [27,57,58]. IL-17A exerts a crucial pivotal role in respiratory infections and is correlated with the pathologic outcomes such as pneumonitis and asthma. Elevated levels of BALF IL-17A positively correlate with lung lesion severity in children with MPP [45]. M. pneumoniae infection elicits aberrant IL-17A production, with Th17 cells representing the primary cellular source of this pro-inflammatory cytokine [59]. We further demonstrate that MMP12 enhances IL-17A secretion by Th17 cells, potentially through the amplified signaling of pro-Th17 cytokines that critically promote Th17 cell activation and functional polarization.

In conclusion, we demonstrate that M. pneumoniae CARDS toxin induces upregulation of both MMP12 and IL-17A signaling pathways, thereby contributing to pulmonary inflammatory injury. Targeting the MMP12–IL-17A axis may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for M. pneumoniae-associated pulmonary diseases.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Animal Models

All the mouse lines used in this study were backcrossed and maintained in a C57BL/6J background. Adult female mice aged 8–10 weeks were used to establish the mouse model. Wild-type mice were obtained from Vital River Laboratory (Beijing, China). Lyz2-Cre, Mmp12flox/flox, Rosa26-iDTR, and Mmp12-CreERT2 mice were provided by Cyagen Biosciences (Jiangsu, China). Rosa26-iDTR mice were mated with Mmp12-CreERT2 mice to generate Mmp12-CreERT2×Rosa26-iDTR mice. Mmp12flox/flox mice were mated with Lyz2-Cre mice to generate Lyz2-Mmp12−/− mice. Wild-type C57BL/6 mice (total n = 65), Mmp12flox/flox (total n = 10), Lyz2-Mmp12–/– (total n = 10), and Mmp12-CreERT2×Rosa26-iDTR mice (total n = 20) were used in this study and randomly divided into the indicated groups. Group sizes were based on our previous experience and were considered sufficient to detect biologically meaningful differences while minimizing animal use. To model pulmonary inflammation, mice were intratracheally administered recombinant CARDS toxin (700 pmol per mouse), with heat-inactivated CARDS toxin (100 °C for 15 min, 700 pmol per mouse) and CARDSmut toxin (700 pmol per mouse) used as control (n = 3 per group for wild-type mice, n = 4 per group for Lyz2-Mmp12−/− mice, and n = 5 per group for Mmp12-CreERT2×Rosa26-iDTR mice). For IL-17 blocking, mice intraperitoneally received 15 μg of anti-IL-17A antibody (Cat#MAB421; R&D Systems, Minnesota, USA) on day 1 after CARDS treatment and subsequently received 7.5 μg of anti-IL-17A antibody on days 2, 4, and 6 post-CARDS treatment (n = 3 per group). Tamoxifen (Cat#T5648; Sigma–Aldrich, Missouri, USA) was dissolved in corn oil by shaking overnight at 37 °C and was intraperitoneally injected at 100 mg/kg every 48 h for five consecutive times. For DT treatment, Mmp12-CreERT2×Rosa26-iDTR mice were injected intraperitoneally with 200 ng DT for 14 days (administered for 5 days, followed by 2 days off; n = 5 per group). Mice were housed and maintained in specific pathogen-free facilities with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, with controlled temperature (22 °C) and humidity (40%–60%). Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. The left lung was harvested for evaluation of pathological lung injury, while the right lung was collected for other experimental analyses. All staining assessments were performed in a blinded manner. No mice were excluded from the analysis. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting In Vivo Experiments) guidelines. The study was reviewed and approved by the Beijing Chaoyang Hospital Animal Care and Use Committee (25–2037).

Participants Enrollment

This study enrolled pediatric patients hospitalized with MPP at Beijing Chaoyang Hospital (n = 26 for the DC group, n = 58 for the MPP group, Table S4). The diagnosis of MPP was established in accordance with standardized clinical, laboratory, and radiological criteria: age under 18 years; positive for both M. pneumoniae nucleic acids and specific antibodies; typical clinical features of pneumonia, including fever, cough, and abnormal lung sounds; and confirmed pulmonary infiltration evident on chest radiographs. Disease control cases were defined as pneumonia patients who tested negative for M. pneumoniae via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays. Patients were excluded if they were outpatients, had a history or evidence of chronic diseases, lacked complete clinical data, or had coinfections with other pathogens. Written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians, and assent was obtained from pediatric participants where appropriate. All procedures involving human samples were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and applicable local regulatory requirements and laws. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Chaoyang Hospital (2021-Ke-501).

Proteomic Analysis

For human BALF collection (n = 6 for the DC group, n = 6 for the MPP group), a bronchoscope was wedged into the target bronchus, and 100–200 mL warmed sterile normal saline was injected in aliquots, then aspirated into ice-chilled tubes. The BALF was centrifuged and the supernatant was processed for proteomic analyses. As reported previously [60], BALF samples were passed through 70 μm cell strainers and centrifuged at 4 °C for 15 min. For liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry analysis, Tris phosphine (Pierce, Illinois, USA) was added to the supernatant. Subsequently, iodoacetamide (Sigma–Aldrich, Missouri, USA) was added for protein alkylation and the mixture was incubated at 25 °C in the dark with shaking at 300 rpm for 1 h. Protein digestion was performed overnight at 37 °C using mass spectrometry-grade trypsin gold (Promega, Wisconsin, USA). Dried peptides were dissolved in 20 μL of loading buffer (1% formic acid and 1% acetonitrile) and 10 μL of the products were used for analysis. The peptides were quantified by peak area derived from MS1 intensity. The protein intensity was calculated by the intensity of unique and razor peptides. Fold change (FC) was calculated on the mean between the MPP and DC patient groups. The statistically significant was filtered with false discovery rate-adjusted P < 0.05 and |log2(FC)| > 1.

BALF Total Protein Determination

Mice were anesthetized intraperitoneally with sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg) and BALF was harvested through intratracheal catheter insertion, gently injecting and retracting 3 mL of ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution. The samples were then centrifuged at 400 g for 6 min at 4 °C. For quantification of total proteins, BALF supernatants were assessed using a BCA protein quantification kit (Cat#P0010; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instruction.

RNA Extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent (Cat#DP424; TIANGEN, Beijing, China). RNA was reverse-transcribed to complementary DNA (cDNA) using the PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Cat#RR036A; Takara, Shiga Prefecture, Japan). cDNA was used to quantify relative mRNA levels in a 384-well format with a LightCycle 480 SYBR green I Master (Roche, Basel-City, Switzerland), and mRNA expression was normalized to that of Gapdh. The primer sequences of targeted genes are listed in Table S5.

Immunohistochemistry and Histology

Lung issues were fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4 °C and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin sections were cut at 3 μm, and the slides were stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Briefly, paraffin slides were first dewaxed with xylol and rehydrated through a graded alcohol series. Then, slides were stained with hematoxylin for 2 min to stain the nucleus. Sections were dehydrated in 95% ethanol for 1 min and counterstained with eosin for 15 s. Finally, slides were mounted with composite resin, and images were captured using a Pannoramic 250 Flash system (3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary). For recognizing the disease severity, the histopathology score was evaluated as previously described [28].

For immunofluorescence staining, the paraffin slides were dewaxed with xylol and rehydrated through a graded alcohol series and tested using a fluorescence immunohistochemical kit (Cat#RS0035; ImmunoWay, Jiangsu, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instruction. Sections were blocked with 3% normal goat serum and 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 1 h. After overnight incubation of primary antibodies (anti-CD68, Cat#BA3638, BOSTER, Hubei, China; anti-Ly6G, Cat#ab25377, Abcam, Cambridgeshire, UK; anti-MMP12, Cat#22989-1-AP, Proteintech, Hubei, China; anti-IL-17A, Cat#sc-374218, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, California, USA) at 4 °C, sections were washed 3 times with PBS and stained with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. DNA was stained with Hoechst 33342 (Cat#H3570, Thermo Fisher, Massachusetts, USA). Immunofluorescence images were obtained using a 63×/1.40 oil objective on a Zeiss LSM 900 and analyzed with ZEN3.8 software (ZEISS, Baden-Wurttemberg, Germany). The percentage of positive cells and mean fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ 1.54d software. The raw data related to the percentage of positive cells are listed in Table S6.

Cell Culture

THP-1 cells were maintained in an RPMI 1640 medium with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. To induce macrophage differentiation, THP-1 cells were treated with 150 nM phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, Cat#P1585; Sigma–Aldrich, Missouri, USA). Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were isolated from the femurs and tibias of two 10-w-old C57BL/6J mice. BMDMs were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 20 ng/mL macrophage colony stimulating factor (M-CSF, Cat#416-ML-050; R&D Systems, Minnesota, USA). The medium containing M-CSF was refreshed on day 3. After 7 days of culture, differentiated BMDMs were collected for subsequent experiments. For toxin treatment, THP-1 cells and BMDMs were stimulated with 100 μg of heat-inactivated CARDS (control), CARDSmut, or CARDS toxin. After 24 h, cells were harvested for quantitative real-time PCR analysis.

ELISA Assay

The Human IL-17A ELISA Kit (Cat#E-EL-H5812), Human TNF-α ELISA Kit (Cat#E-EL-H0109), Human S100A9 ELISA Kit (Cat#E-EL-H6197), Human MMP12 ELISA Kit (Cat#E-EL-H0133), and Mouse IL-17A ELISA Kit (Cat#E-EL-M0047) were purchased from Elabscience (Hubei, China). For the analysis of protein concentrations in BALF from patients (n = 20 for the DC group, n = 52 for the MPP group), as well as in BALF and lung tissues from mice, the above ELISA kits were used. All assays were performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentrations of each protein were measured at an optical density of 450 nm using a microplate reader (Agilent Technologies, California, USA).

Cloning and Plasmids

The coding sequence of CARDS toxin was PCR cloned from M. pneumonia M129 (GenBank accession number: NC000912.1). The coding sequence of GFP was cloned from pEGFP-N1 vectors (Cat#P0133; Miaoling Plasmid, Hubei, China). The plasmids expressing recombinant CARDS and GFP-CARDS toxin were amplified by PCR and cloned into the pET-28a vector (Cat#P0023; Miaoling Plasmid, Hubei, China) using NdeI and XhoI restriction enzyme sites. The CARDSmut plasmids were generated by PCR-based site-directed mutagenesis. All constructs were verified by DNA sequencing (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China).

CARDS Toxin Purification

CARDS toxin-related proteins were prepared according to the previous protocol [9] with some modifications. Plasmids were transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3). Expression was induced by the addition of IPTG (0.5 mM) to an exponentially growing culture (optical density at 600 nm = 0.8) grown shaking at 37 °C for 3 h. After shaking for an additional 16−20 h, the protein was purified using nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid affinity chromatography and eluted with lysis buffer containing 400 mM imidazole. For further purification, we used the AKTA explorer chromatography system with HiPrep Sephacryl S-200 HR column (Cytiva, Massachusetts, USA) to eliminate imidazole. The elution fractions were concentrated and used for subsequent animal experiments.

Flow Cytometry

Th17 cells were differentiated from CD4+ T cells isolated from the spleens of C57BL/6J mice (n = 10 mice) as previously reported [61]. MMP12 (200 ng/mL, Cat#HY-P70342), SPP1 (200 ng/mL, Cat#HY-P78358), FN1 recombinant protein (10 μg/mL, Cat#HY-P3160), and GM6001 inhibitor (10 μm, Cat#HY-15768) were purchased from MCE (Beijing, China). All cells were treated with the indicated drug for 48 h, washed, resuspended in an FACS buffer (PBS plus 1% FBS and 5 mM EDTA, pH 7.4) and blocked with FcR-blocking antibody (Cat#130-092-575; Miltenyi Biotech, North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany) for 10 min. Cells were stained for 30 min at 4 °C with 7-aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD, Cat#420403; BioLegend, Beijing, China) and FITC anti-CD4 antibodies (Cat#100509; BioLegend, Beijing, China). Add fixed buffer and permeabilization buffer and incubate cells for 40 min in the dark at 4 °C. Cells were then stained for 60 min in the dark at 4 °C with phycoerythrin (PE) anti-IL-17A (Cat#506903; BioLegend, Beijing, China) and allophycocyanin (APC) anti-RORγT antibodies (Cat#17-6981-80; BioLegend, Beijing, China). Cell aggregates were excluded from the analysis based on forward scatter-height/forward scatter-area (FSC–H/FSC-A) characteristics. All cytometry data were collected by BD Fortessa (BD Biosciences, California, USA). Data were analyzed with FlowJo v10.6.2 (BD, Oregon, USA).

Bulk RNA-seq Experiments and Analysis

Lung tissues were collected 7 days after surgery. Total RNA was purified, reverse-transcribed to cDNA, and sequenced. RNA-seq reads for each sample were aligned to GRCm38 with STAR (v2.7.3a) [62], and DEGs were identified with the DESeq2 package (v1.42.1) [63]. PCA and clustering were performed using the “RunPCA” function of Seurat (v5.3.0) to visualize the distribution of the different groups. GSEA analyses were used to generate enrichment plots for RNA-seq data [64], and GO term enrichment analysis was performed using Enrichr (v3.4.0) [65]. Genes significantly changed after CARDS treatment were determined using false discovery rate-adjusted P < 0.05.

scRNA-seq and Data Analysis

Fresh lung tissue of CARDSmut and CARDS toxin-treated mice was gently dissected, washed with pre-cooled PBS buffer, and then digested at 37 °C for 30 min with shaking using the Lung Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotech, North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instruction. Cell suspensions were filtered through 70 μm cell strainers and centrifuged at 400 g for 5 min at 4 °C, after which red blood cells were removed with red blood cell-lysing buffer. For scRNA-seq, CD45+ cells were sorted from total cells using CD45 MicroBeads (Cat#130-052-301; Miltenyi Biotech, North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany). Cells were loaded according to the manufacturer's protocol for the Chromium Single Cell 3′ Library & Gel Bead Kit (10× Genomics, California, USA). Library quality was evaluated using an Agilent 4200 system (California, USA) prior to sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, California, USA). Raw gene expression matrices were generated using Cell Ranger (v.4.0.0), with the mouse GRCm38/mm10 genome serving as the reference.

For downstream analysis, scRNA-seq data were processed as previously described [66]. Briefly, gene expression matrices were imported into R (v4.3.2) and analyzed using the Seurat package (v4.3.0) [38]. Low-quality cells were filtered out if they had < 200 genes, > 6000 genes or mitochondrial transcript content exceeding 25%. After quality control, 41,176 high-quality cells were retained. Counts were normalized using a global-scaling approach (scale factor = 10,000) followed by natural-log transformation. Highly variable genes were identified using the “FindVariableFeatures” method with variance-stabilizing transformation. Sample-derived batch effects were removed with the Harmony algorithm under default parameters [67].

Following normalization and identification of highly variable genes, the top 30 principal components were selected for dimensionality reduction in principal component analysis based on “ElbowPlot”. “FindNeighbors” and “FindClusters” functions were then implemented for cell clustering. Two-dimensional embeddings were generated using UMAP for visualization. Cluster biomarkers were determined by the “FindAllMarkers” function. Cell type annotation was conducted using a dual-validation strategy: automated annotation was performed with SingleR (v2.12.0) [68], followed by manual verification based on canonical marker genes. A dot plot depicting cell type-specific marker genes was generated to comprehensively visualize the molecular signatures of each cell type. DEGs of each cell type between CARDS and control samples were identified using the “FindMarkers” function.

For cell–cell communication analysis, we utilized the CellChat pipeline [69], implemented in the R package (https://github.com/sqjin/CellChat, v2.2.0). Briefly, the cell–cell communication was separately inferred using the “computeCommunProb” function, followed by merging of the respective ligand–receptor networks via the “mergeCellChat” function. To identify the upregulated signaling ligand–receptor pairs between Mmp12high macrophages and Th17 cells, we used the “netVisual_bubble” function with the default parameters in the package.

Quantification and Statistical Analysis

All the data were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical significance was calculated using SPSS (v27.0). We used a two-tailed Student’s t-test to compare two groups of normally distributed data. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare multiple groups of normally distributed data, followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc comparisons, as noted in the figure legends. Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare inflammatory and cytokine scores, and Welch’s t-test was employed to compare gene expression levels from scRNA-seq data. The sample size was not predetermined by statistical methods. Experiments were not randomized and no samples were excluded. The investigators were blinded during the assessment of all staining assays. For all tests, differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05 (∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001). Detailed statistical results are listed in Table S7.
