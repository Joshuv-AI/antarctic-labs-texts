Evaluating the Impact of Colored Compomer on Oral Hygiene in 5–8-year-old Children and Assessment of Parental Acceptance: A Cohort Study
Authors: Ishani Ratnaparkhi, Jasmin Winnier, Shilpa Shetty Naik, Aastha Sharma, Kruti Kruti, Mayuri Gorule
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundManagement of children's behavior at dental offices remains an ongoing challenge. Encouraging a sense of control in children is an effective noninvasive behavior management technique recognized for reducing disruptive behavior in the dental clinic. Allowing child to choose the color of their restoration might give them a sense of autonomy, which may improve their attitude toward oral hygiene practices.AimAssessment of the motivational effect of colored compomer on children's oral hygiene and to evaluate parental acceptance of the same.Materials and MethodsA cohort study was conducted with 40 children aged 5–8 years. Parents' consent was obtained prior to the procedure. Based on their preferred compomer, group I selected colored compomer (Twinky Star), while group II selected conventional compomer. Oral hygiene was evaluated using the oral hygiene index-simplified (OHI-S) and plaque at baseline, at 3 weeks, and 3 months postrestoration. Parental acceptance of the colored compomer was assessed using a validated questionnaire.ResultsAt a 3-week interval, the OHI-S score shows no statistically significant difference between the study group (1.27 ± 0.38) and the control group (1.45 ± 0.44; p > 0.05). However, plaque scores were statistically significant in the study group (0.83 ± 0.33) when compared to the control (1.09 ± 0.29; p < 0.01). After 3 months, a statistically significant difference was seen in OHI-S (0.81 ± 0.26) and plaque scores (0.53 ± 0.17) than the control group (1.13 ± 0.44 and 0.83 ± 0.22, respectively; p < 0.05).ConclusionsColored compomer might act as a motivating tool, improving children's oral hygiene, and was well accepted by parents.How to cite this articleRatnaparkhi I, Winnier J, Naik SS, et al. Introduction

The quest for a material combining the fluoride-releasing capability of conventional glass ionomer with the durability of composite led to the development of polyacid-modified composite, or compomer. This material releases fluoride and undergoes an acid–base reaction in the presence of saliva. In children, especially with high caries risk, the use of colored restoration helps in retaining their fluoride-releasing capabilities and can be revived by topical fluoride application. Since their introduction to the market in 1993, compomers have been utilized for the restoration of primary teeth.1 Studies conducted by Arora et al. and Kramer and Frankenberger et al. have stated that hybrid composite and compomer exhibit comparable success rates in pediatric dentistry and demonstrate higher clinical success when compared to glass ionomer cements.2,3 When compared to conventional composites, compomers contain a small amount of glitter particles that produce a color effect ranging from natural tooth color with a sparkle to shades of pink, blue, and green.3

Addressing anxiety in young children using behavior control methods presents considerable difficulties for pediatric dentists. Dental anxiety and fear are significant public health concerns worldwide, leading to challenges in behavior management and reluctance to seek dental care, especially in children with active caries. Elevated levels of anxiety can adversely affect a child's willingness to cooperate and the overall effectiveness of dental care. Establishing a positive dentist–patient relationship is essential for alleviating children's fears and facilitating successful treatment. Pediatric dentists can utilize a variety of behavior management techniques customized to meet individual needs, helping to reduce anxiety and improve cooperation during dental procedures.1

Allowing the child a sense of control is an effective, noninvasive behavior management technique known to reduce disruptive behavior in clinical settings. One of the most commonly used nonverbal communication methods involves asking the child to raise a hand to stop the dental procedure, thereby sharing and enhancing their sense of control.4 Similarly, offering the child the opportunity to choose the type or color of the restoration to be placed in their teeth can also be a successful approach.1 Despite its credible mechanical properties and positive impact on behavior, colored compomer material often falls short in terms of esthetic appeal from a parental perspective.5 Parents tend to prefer conventional tooth-colored materials, owing to their esthetics.6

Thus, the aim of this study was to assess of motivational effect of colored compomer on children's oral hygiene and to evaluate parental acceptance of colored compomer restoration in primary molars.

Materials and Methods

The study was conducted at the Outpatient Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry from March 2024 to June 2024. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethical Committee (approval number: IREB/2024/PEDO/09) and was registered at Clinical Trials Registry—India (CTRI) under the number CTRI/2024/04/065350.

Sample size was determined by using the estimated mean and standard deviation (SD) values and keeping alpha (α) error at 5% and beta (β) error at 20%, power at 80%, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The total sample size was 40, with 20 participants in each group.

Children were assessed for eligibility; those who met the inclusion criteria were divided into two groups based on their preferred compomer. Children aged 5–8 years with no prior dental history and who had a class 1 cavity in the second deciduous molar extending into dentin (D1) were included in the present study. Children with high caries rate, systemic diseases, children whose parents did not give consent, and those with Frankel rating of 1 or 2 were excluded from the current study. Prior to the procedure, written consent was obtained from the parents.

After assessment for eligibility, 40 children meeting the inclusion criteria were divided into two groups of 20 each according to the child's preferred restorative material.

Group I (study group)—Twinkie Star (colored compomer).

Group II (control group)—Conventional compomer restoration.

Prior to the procedure, assessment of oral hygiene was done using the oral hygiene index-simplified (OHI-S) and plaque index (Silness and Loe, 1964), and simultaneously, a preprocedure questionnaire was given to the parent. A questionnaire adapted from a study done by Agrawal et al.7 This questionnaire was modified and sent to 10 pediatric dentists, each with a minimum of 5 years of experience, for validation. Based on their suggestions, necessary revisions were made, resulting in the development of a final eight-item questionnaire, which was statistically validated by obtaining a Cronbach's Alpha value of 0.8. To evaluate parental acceptance and satisfaction with the compomer used, parents were asked to rate their level of acceptance on a five-point Likert scale

The procedure was conducted by a single examiner. Caries removal was done using a round bur (BR41), followed by a straight fissure bur, extending the cavity depth to D1, indicating the lesion was limited to the outer one-third of the dentin. After the removal of caries, the cavity was etched (DeTrey Conditioner 36, Dentsply) for 30 seconds and rinsed with water, followed by application of bonding agent (3M ESPE Adper Single Bond) with an applicator tip and cured for 20 seconds. Compomer (Twinky star) was condensed into the cavity and cured for 20 seconds. Occlusion was assessed for high points after restoration, which was adjusted as necessary, followed by finishing and polishing of the restoration. Children were advised to follow a regular oral hygiene procedure. A similar procedure was carried out for conventional compomer.

Oral hygiene was assessed using the OHI-S and the plaque index at baseline, intervals of 3 weeks, and 3 months. A postprocedure questionnaire was administered to the parents at the 3-month follow-up.

Statistical Analysis

Data was subjected to statistical analysis using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS, version 26.0, IBM). Normality of numerical data was checked using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and it was found that the data did not follow a normal curve; hence, nonparametric tests have been used for comparisons. Intergroup comparison (two groups) was done using the Mann–Whitney U test.

For all the statistical tests, p < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant, keeping α error at 5% and β error at 20%, thus giving a power to the study of 80%.

Results

A total of 40 participants were enrolled in the study, divided equally into two groups of 20 participants each. The cohort consisted of 18 females and 22 males, with a mean age range of 6.05–6.25 years. At 3 weeks postintervention, the mean OHI-S score in the study and control group was 1.27 and 1.4, respectively, which was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Whereas at 3 months postintervention, the mean OHI-S score in the study group was 0.81 and the control group was 1.130, respectively, with p < 0.05, indicating enhanced oral hygiene practices who received colored compomer (Table 1).

Discussion

Dental materials such as multicolored restorations are seen to have a positive influence on children's behavior.2 The aim of the present study was to assess the motivations and the effect on children's oral hygiene by using colored compomers.

Children who participated in the study were (5–8 years) in the preoperational stage of development, which is characterized by maturation of attention and cognitive abilities, and also by facilitation of interpersonal and social communication.1 Additionally, children were more expressive and showed more responses to motivational approaches.8

Using a variety of colors during restoration can cater to individual preferences and increase patient satisfaction. The reason for compromised oral health in children is fear and anxiety, which leads to avoiding further dental visits and negative experiences. To diminish this, multicolor restorations are used as a strategy to enhance the overall dental experience. The Silness and Loe Plaque Index (1964) and OHI-S (1964) were used as assessment tools to evaluate oral hygiene.

Because of its practicality, adaptability for large populations, and convenience of administration, the OHI-S is widely used. It provides measurable and objective results by evaluating calculus and debris in order to assess oral cleanliness.

High sensitivity and reproducibility have been reported for the Silness and Loe plaque index.1 When comparing the two groups, the plaque index assessment showed a noteworthy trend towards significance as well as a significant reduction within each group. This improvement could be explained by children's enhanced knowledge of restorations they chose and the dentist's explanation that maintaining good oral hygiene will preserve the restoration's appearance.1

Children who participated in choosing the color of restoration were more likely to accept dental intervention as a whole, especially younger ones.9 Cianetti et al. stated that the use of multicolored restorations encouraged positive behavior in children, lessened their anxiety, and promoted good maintenance of restoration.10 A study conducted by Juliet and Gurunathan et al. concluded that using multicolored restorations helps in making dental appointments more engaging and can serve as a motivational tool and causing an increase in the child's cooperation and interest throughout the treatment.11

Studies conducted by Duggal et al. and Kavvadia et al. demonstrated that commercially available compomers exhibit high clinical success rates, comparable to those of amalgam, making them a viable alternative for restoring primary teeth in children.12,13 Consequently, the colored fillings may remain esthetically pleasing for a longer duration, as children develop a sustained interest in preserving their appearance.1

Beisbrock et al. found that children between the ages of 6 and 15 years showed significant improvement in their plaque scores following a 1-month evaluation.14

The findings of the present study are consistent with previous research by Arora et al. reported a significant reduction in plaque index within individual groups and a trend toward significance when comparing groups. This improvement was attributed to increased awareness among children regarding oral hygiene.2

In 2019, Melebari et al. demonstrated a near-significant difference observed between test groups with a significant reduction in plaque index within groups.1

A study conducted by Preethy and Somasundaram et al. concluded that while colored compomer materials demonstrate reasonable mechanical properties, they do not meet the expectations of parents. However, the results from the present study indicate that colored compomer acts as a motivational tool and was better accepted by parents than conventional compomer restorations. This concludes that the motivational benefits of colored compomer may surpass its esthetic limitations in encouraging oral health habits among children.

Limitations

The study's small sample size and confined geographical area are its drawbacks. Future clinical trials should consider larger sample sizes, and a wide geographical area should be considered for future trials. Furthermore, other elements that affect children's oral hygiene, habits, and parental involvement in dental care were not considered in this study.

Conclusion

Children's oral hygiene practices significantly improved after the use of colored compomer. OHI-S and plaque index scores decreased significantly in the study group following the use of colored compomer. These results suggest that when compared to conventional compomer, colored compomer can act as an effective motivational tool for improving oral hygiene in children and was well accepted by parents.
