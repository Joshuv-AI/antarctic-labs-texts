Granulomatous Hypophysitis Masquerading as a Pituitary Macroadenoma: A Diagnostic Challenge
Authors: Vivian M. Crowder, Jessica K. Williams, Steven P. Weitzman
Published: 2026

Case Presentation

A 62-year-old postmenopausal Hispanic woman with remote subtotal thyroidectomy presented to an outside emergency department after a low random cortisol level was noted during evaluation for weakness. Outside records demonstrated stable vital signs, adrenocorticotropic hormone was 6.4 (reference, 7.2-63.3 pg/mL) and cortisol was 11 (3-11 μg/dL). After administration of 250 μg cosyntropin, cortisol rose to 20 μg/dL at 30 minutes and 22 μg/dL at 60 minutes, which was interpreted as adequate and she was discharged.

One day after her discharge, she presented to our emergency department reporting 1 week of nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, orthostatic lightheadedness, and 1 month of polydipsia. She appeared lethargic with pallor, dry mucous membranes, active emesis, and right upper quadrant tenderness. Serum sodium level was 154 mEq/L (136-145 mEq/L) and serum osmolality was 337 mOsm/kg (289-308 mOsm/kg), with sodium level later rising to 169 mEq/L. Liver function test results were normal, and ultrasound showed gallstones. Urine output remained high at 500 mL every 2 to 3 hours despite intravenous fluids, and she was diagnosed with diabetes insipidus (DI). Treatment with desmopressin normalized urine output and serum osmolality. A low-dose cosyntropin stimulation test showed an inadequate cortisol response, and a pituitary panel revealed central hypothyroidism. Pituitary magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a 12 mm sellar mass with a thickened, enhancing infundibulum

What is the diagnosis?

Answer

Granulomatous hypophysitis. MRI demonstrated a sellar mass (Fig. 1) and a thickened infundibulum (Fig. 2). Given the combination of DI, stalk thickening, and atypical clinical presentation, biopsy was pursued to distinguish inflammatory disease from pituitary adenoma. Transsphenoidal biopsy revealed plasmacytic and histiocytic inflammation; stains were negative for bacterial, fungal, and acid-fast organisms. Granulomatous hypophysitis is an uncommon inflammatory disorder representing <1% of sellar lesions in adults and predominantly affects women.1 Etiology may be idiopathic or associated with conditions such as tuberculosis, sarcoidosis, granulomatosis with polyangiitis, syphilis, or Langerhans histiocytosis.2 Typical presenting symptoms are headache and visual changes,1 which were absent in this case. Instead, her findings reflected panhypopituitarism, reported in ∼50% of cases, and DI, seen in up to one third of cases. DI often correlates with infundibular thickening as seen on this patient’s MRI.1 Treatment options include corticosteroids or immunosuppression, whereas surgery is reserved for diagnostic uncertainty or significant mass effect.1,3 In this case, central adrenal insufficiency was initially missed because the high-dose cosyntropin stimulation test is less sensitive than the low-dose protocol for early central adrenal insufficiency.
