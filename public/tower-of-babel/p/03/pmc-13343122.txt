Encapsulation enhances stromal cell survival for safe neonatal therapy: a pilot study
Authors: Jasmine Lee, Kelly Henige, Fikir Mesfin, Sharon Joseph, Krishna Manohar, Chris Shelley, Jianyun Liu, Minglin Ma, Minseok Lee, Troy Markel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) have demonstrated therapeutic benefit in preclinical models of necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), yet clinical translation remains limited by concerns about immune clearance and tumorigenic risk. Encapsulation strategies offer a potential solution by limiting systemic exposure while preserving efficacy. We hypothesized that encapsulation of MSCs would improve cell survival and maintain cell identity in this proof-of-concept study.MethodsLuciferase-expressing MSCs were generated via lentiviral transduction and validated for luminescence. Mice received MSCs either intraperitoneally (n=4) or encapsulated in the surgically implanted thread-reinforced encapsulation device (THRED) (n=4). Bioluminescence imaging tracked viability over 8 days. Viability and identity of extracted cells were assessed by flow cytometry and stromal cell marker analysis.ResultsThe bioluminescent signal declined in both groups, but MSCs delivered in the THRED retained significantly greater viability on day 4 (p=0.0286) and day 8 (p=0.0286). Cells extracted from the device remained viable (p=0.0286) and maintained stromal cell marker expression matching non-implanted controls (n=4). Flow cytometry demonstrated the viability of explanted cells, as well as preserved identity based on cell surface markers.ConclusionEncapsulation within the THRED preserves MSC viability and identity in vivo. This approach provides a clinically practical method to deliver MSC therapy during initial NEC surgery or drain placement with retrieval at a later procedure. This serves as a proof-of-concept and offers a feasible solution to current barriers in neonatal MSC therapy.Keywords: Animal Experimentation, Cell Biology, Neonatology

Introduction

Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) is one of the most severe and unpredictable gastrointestinal emergencies in premature infants, marked by intestinal inflammation, ischemia, and necrosis.1 Despite advancements in neonatal care, NEC remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality, with few effective therapies beyond supportive care and surgical intervention.2,5 In recent years, mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC) therapy has emerged as a promising approach in preclinical NEC models, where MSCs have demonstrated potent anti-inflammatory, regenerative, and immunomodulatory effects.6,12

MSCs are hypothesized to exert their therapeutic effects primarily through paracrine signaling rather than direct tissue integration.13 In experimental NEC models, MSCs have been shown to suppress proinflammatory cytokines, enhance epithelial restitution, modulate macrophage polarization, and improve survival outcomes.9 11 12 These benefits are thought to arise from MSC-driven immune regulation, secretion of growth and repair factors, and mitochondrial transfer to damaged epithelial cells.14 However, studies consistently demonstrate that the persistence of viable MSCs in vivo is limited, with rapid clearance likely contributing to variability in therapeutic efficacy.15 This underscores the need for strategies that not only harness the beneficial properties of MSCs but also prolong their presence and activity within injured tissues. Unfortunately, significant translational barriers hinder clinical application of MSCs in neonates.16 Systemically or intraperitoneally delivered stromal cells face several challenges—including rapid immune clearance, uncertainties regarding tumorigenicity, and concerns about long-term biodistribution—which make unregulated cell-based therapies difficult to implement in vulnerable newborns.17 A safe, controllable, and retrievable delivery method for stromal cells is critically needed to unlock the clinical potential of MSC therapy in the neonatal population.

The thread-reinforced encapsulation device (THRED) offers a novel solution. Originally developed for islet transplantation,18 the THRED enables durable cell encapsulation while maintaining biocompatibility and retrievability.19 20 This approach allows for potential clinical use in surgical NEC, where the device could be implanted during initial laparotomy in which all necrotic bowel is excised and subsequently removed during a second-look laparotomy or planned ostomy takedown, delivering therapeutic benefit to marginally ischemic or questionably viable bowel without long-term risk. Notably, up to 50% of neonates with NEC may require surgery, and these infants often represent the most critically ill subset of this vulnerable population.21 A retrievable delivery method like the THRED has the potential to offer targeted MSC therapy to these high-risk patients, maximizing therapeutic benefit while minimizing safety concerns.

In this proof-of-concept study, we evaluated the in vivo viability, immune profile, and functional stability of MSCs encapsulated in the THRED using a mouse model. This foundational work aims to validate the THRED platform as a protective, translationally feasible delivery strategy that can support future therapeutic testing in large animal NEC models.

Methods

THRED preparation

The THRED encapsulation platform was fabricated based on the scalable, retrievable design originally reported as the thread-reinforced alginate fiber for islet encapsulation device by An et al.18 First, two 50 nylon sutures were twisted into a double helix and folded to form a robust four-strand core. This core was coated with a 7% (w/v) polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) solution in N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF). Importantly, 2.5% (w/v) calcium chloride (CaCl₂) was dissolved into the PMMA/DMF mixture to create a nanoporous, Ca²⁺-releasing polymeric layer that promotes uniform hydrogel adherence. Next, the coated thread was placed into a tubular mold containing 2% (w/v) sterile Pronova alginate solution (Novamatrix, Norway). After 4 min of Ca²+-initiated cross-linking, the device was transferred into a bath of 95 mM CaCl₂ and 5 mM barium chloride (BaCl₂) for an additional 5 min to complete gelation. This two-step calcium-mediated process produced a thin, strongly adhered alginate hydrogel shell.

Stromal cell culture

Human umbilical cord-derived MSCs were purchased from ATCC (Manassas, Virginia, USA) and cultured in mesenchymal stem cell basal medium (ATCC) plus mesenchymal stem cell growth kit for adipose and umbilical-derived MSCs—low serum (ATCC). Cells were cultured following the handling procedure provided by ATCC. For bioluminescent tracking, MSCs were transduced with pLenti PGK V5-LUC Neo (w623-2) (Addgene plasmid # 21471). Successful transduction was confirmed by spectrophotometric measurement of luminescence in tagged cells compared with non-transduced controls. MSCs were used between passages 5 and 8 (see online supplemental figure 1). Luciferase-expressing cells demonstrating consistent signals were expanded for use in downstream in vivo encapsulation experiments.

For mice receiving free intraperitoneal injections, dissociated MSCs were suspended in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at a concentration of 1 × 10⁶n cells per injection. The cell suspension was then loaded into 1 mL syringes fitted with 27-gauge, 1.5-inch needles for direct intraperitoneal delivery. For animals in the THRED group, the same number of MSCs (1 × 10⁶ million cells) was resuspended in 2% (w/v) sterile Pronova alginate and injected into 1-cm segments of polyethylene tubing (Intramedic, Fisher Scientific, BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, USA) into which a single THRED had been inserted. The filled tubing was incubated for 4 min to allow initial cross-linking, and then the device was transferred into a cross-linking solution of 95 mM CaCl₂·2H₂O and 5 mM BaCl₂·2H₂O for an additional 4 min to complete hydrogel formation. The THREDs were then rinsed in sterile saline and maintained in sterile PBS on ice until surgical implantation.

Animal model

All animal procedures were approved by the Indiana University Animal Research Protections Office (IACUC Protocol #19110) and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines. Adult C57BL/6J mice (8–10 weeks old) were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, Maine, USA) and housed under standard conditions. One day prior to the experiment, the abdominal region of each mouse was shaved in preparation for bioluminescent imaging.

On the day of the experiment, mice either received intraperitoneal (IP) injections of MSCs or underwent surgical implantation of the THRED (n=4/group). Mice were allowed to recover from surgery, if applicable, and were then transported to the imaging facility. Anesthesia was administered during imaging with the in vivo imaging system (IVIS) (Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Four animals were imaged at a time (n=2 MSC and n=2 THRED-MSC to avoid bias). Imaging was performed in two groups daily for a total of 8 days, excluding days 5 and 6 (no imaging available on weekends). Following the final imaging session, mice were euthanized.

Animal procedure

Mice undergoing surgical implantation of the THRED were anesthetized with inhaled isoflurane at 3% for induction and maintained at 1.5% throughout the procedure. Each mouse received a subcutaneous injection of 1 mL of 0.9% normal saline for volume support and 3.35 mg/kg of buprenorphine for analgesia. After induction, the preshaved abdomen was prepped and draped in a sterile fashion. A small left lower quadrant incision was made through the skin and then through the peritoneum. The THRED-MSCs or non-encapsulated cells were inserted into the peritoneal cavity, and both the peritoneal and skin layers were closed using a single figure-of-eight suture with 5–0 polypropylene. Postoperatively, animals were monitored on a warming pad until full recovery from anesthesia.

In vivo bioluminescent imaging

To assess MSC viability over time, all mice underwent daily in vivo bioluminescent imaging using the IVIS Lumina Series III system housed in the Indiana University School of Medicine In Vivo Imaging Core. Mice were anesthetized with 3% inhaled isoflurane for induction and maintained at 1.5% during imaging. D-luciferin potassium salt was administered via intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 150 mg/kg approximately 10 min before imaging to ensure optimal substrate distribution. Animals were positioned supine in the imaging chamber, and bioluminescent signals were captured using standardized acquisition settings. Total photon flux (photons/s) was quantified over regions of interest (ROIs) defined in the abdominal region using Living Image software. Imaging was repeated throughout an 8-day period, excluding a weekend (days 5 and 6). After each session, animals were allowed to recover fully from anesthesia and were returned to their colony.

Flow cytometry

Apoptosis assay (n=4/group). Encapsulated cells were extracted from the THRED using an extraction buffer (100 mM EDTA in Dulbecco’s PBS supplemented with 10 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid, pH 7.4). Encapsulated MSCs were first washed with PBS and then incubated in the extraction buffer on a shaker at 37°C for 15 min. Liberated cells and non-transplanted MSC controls were then stained with propidium iodide (PI) and PE-conjugated Annexin V (BD Biosciences) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Samples were acquired on a BD LSRFortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), and data were analyzed using FlowJo software (BD Biosciences, Ashland, Oregon, USA).

MSC immunophenotyping (n=4/group). THRED-encapsulated MSCs were liberated as above. Liberated cells and non-transplanted MSC controls were washed with PBS and stained with positive MSC markers (CD90 and CD44) and negative markers (CD45) (see online supplemental figure 2). Samples were acquired on a Cytek Aurora spectral flow cytometer (Cytek Biosciences, Fremont, California, USA) and analyzed using FlowJo software (BD Biosciences).

Statistics

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA). Data were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables were compared using one-way analysis of variance with post hoc testing for multiple comparisons with Dunnett’s test, while non-parametric data were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney or Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s correction, where appropriate. Results are expressed as mean±standard deviation (SD). A p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

To validate the in vivo viability of luciferase-tagged MSCs, we performed serial bioluminescence imaging over an 8-day period. Mice injected with unlabeled MSCs (n=4) exhibited no detectable luminescence at any time point, while those receiving luciferase-expressing MSCs (n=4) demonstrated a strong signal on day 0, which declined progressively each day (figure 1A). Raw IVIS images further confirm this pattern: the signal was absent in unlabeled controls (figure 1B), highest at day 0 in the luciferase group (figure 1C), moderately reduced by day 1 (figure 1D), and undetectable by day 8 (figure 1E). As seen in figure 1F–I, mice implanted with an empty THRED device (n=4) were imaged and showed no detectable bioluminescent signal. Mice receiving THRED-encapsulated luciferase-tagged MSCs and mice receiving intraperitoneal luciferase-tagged MSCs were also imaged on day 0. Quantification of total flux on day 0 showed a higher signal in the THRED-MSC group compared with the IP-MSC group, though this difference was not statistically significant.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that encapsulating MSCs within a THRED enhances in vivo viability and preserves MSC identity compared with free-cell delivery. Using longitudinal bioluminescent imaging, we observed that while luminescent signal declined in both groups over time, stromal cells delivered in the THRED maintained significantly greater viability by day 4 through the end of the study. Cells recovered from the device after 8 days remained phenotypically consistent with non-transplanted controls, retaining expression of CD90 and CD44 with minimal CD45 expression, and demonstrated comparable viability on Annexin V and PI staining. Collectively, these findings indicate that the THRED platform supports sustained MSC survival and function in vivo, thereby establishing a foundation for its future application in neonatal disease models, such as NEC.

Although these findings do not evaluate therapeutic efficacy, prior works have demonstrated the therapeutic potential of MSCs in experimental models of intestinal ischemia and reperfusion injury, inflammatory bowel disease, and NEC.622,26 However, previous studies have consistently shown that one of the primary limitations to MSC therapy is poor in vivo survival following transplantation.15 27 28 Systemic and local delivery approaches often result in rapid loss of viable cells within days, attributed to inflammation, hypoxia, and immune-mediated clearance.15 27 Several groups have attempted to address this by modifying delivery routes, using biomaterial scaffolds, or employing encapsulation strategies to improve engraftment and persistence.29 30 Our findings align with prior work demonstrating that physical protection and spatial confinement can enhance stromal cell retention and viability.26 29 31 However, many of these systems lack mechanical stability or retrievability, limiting their translational potential. Like the alginate encapsulation material utilized in the THRED, polyethylene glycol and polylactic-co-glycolic acid are mechanically strong and biocompatible; however, these substances are costly with poor biodegradation or inflammatory degradation byproducts. Alternatively, hyaluronic acid and collagen are similarly highly biocompatible but limited by poor mechanical strength and rapid degradation when not chemically enhanced. The THRED not only maintains biocompatibility, mechanical stability, and permeability, but functionality and translatability are not compromised by expense, chemical alteration, or regulatory concerns.19

As previously discussed, the THRED platform used in this study was originally developed by An et al. for pancreatic islet transplantation.18 In this work, we applied the same device for the delivery of stromal cells to evaluate in vivo viability, recovery, and immune response. This extends the use of this system beyond islet cells and provides early evidence that the device can support other cell types relevant to neonatal disease models. The THRED platform builds on these principles by combining a biocompatible hydrogel environment with a retrievable mesh structure that can be surgically implanted and removed, allowing for both localized delivery and subsequent cell recovery. The preservation of stromal cell phenotype and viability observed in our model suggests that this approach may overcome a key barrier to the clinical translation of MSC therapies for neonatal diseases.

The improved in vivo viability of encapsulated MSCs observed in this study highlights the potential of the THRED platform as a clinically translatable method for controlled, localized MSC delivery. This improvement likely reflects the physical protection provided by the hydrogel barrier, which permits nutrient and cytokine exchange through paracrine mechanisms while limiting exposure to host immune factors.18 30 As a result, the encapsulated MSCs are protected from the host immune system while simultaneously protecting the host from uncontrolled implantation of MSCs. Additionally, the permeable hydrogel allows MSCs to enact their therapeutic benefits through the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines and growth factors in a targeted fashion.32 Notably, Mesfin et al. demonstrated that THRED-MSCs maintained functional cytokine production when compared with their unencapsulated counterparts.26

Given these findings, the THRED device may have practical translational potential in neonatal surgery. Because it can be placed and retrieved surgically, it could be incorporated into a drain positioned at the time of initial NEC intervention—either during peritoneal drain placement or bowel resection—allowing localized delivery of MSCs directly to the site of inflammation. The persistence and viability demonstrated in this study support the feasibility of such short-term placement. The device could be retrieved either at the time of drain removal or during subsequent surgery to restore bowel continuity, ensuring that therapy remains both controlled and reversible. These findings demonstrate that the THRED can maintain MSC survival and function in vivo under healthy conditions, providing a foundational proof-of-concept for future studies in neonatal disease models. However, the THRED is too large to be employed in a neonatal mouse model and thus is better suited for evaluation in large-animal systems such as the preterm NEC piglet model,33 which would also more accurately reflect neonatal intestinal physiology, disease pathology, and surgical conditions.

This study has several limitations. First, although it demonstrates feasibility in healthy adult mice, the model does not reproduce the inflammatory, immune, or physiological features of neonatal intestinal disease, limiting its ability to predict success in diseased neonates. This limitation affects three facets: age of disease onset, presence of disease, and disease intervention. NEC primarily affects premature neonates, and healthy adult mice would not possess the immature intestinal and immune systems at the core of the NEC disease process; therefore, therapeutic efficacy is unable to be assessed in this study. Additionally, our proposed method of placing the THRED during drain placement or surgical resection was not reproduced, limiting our proposed clinical methodology to theoretical suggestions for future experimentation. Further large-animal studies will be required to determine the feasibility and safety of device use in a disease model. Because of size constraints, the THRED cannot be utilized in neonatal mouse models. Therefore, subsequent studies will examine its safety and efficacy in healthy and diseased neonatal piglet models to better approximate clinical translation. This would use the preterm porcine NEC model previously established by our lab34 and surgical drain placement or small bowel resection with simultaneous placement of the THRED, with comparison to similar experimental groups. Both short-term and long-term outcome assessments could be considered for a more accurate appraisal of clinical translatability. Notably, though MSCs have continued to show potential therapeutic benefits in NEC animal models, clinical translation in neonates with NEC has yet to be studied. Further extensive investigation is required beforehand, including analysis of MSC-secreted cytokines, growth factors, and extracellular vesicles; how the host immune pathways are affected, and identification of the mechanism by which MSCs function.

Second, the use of human MSCs in a murine host introduces xenogeneic immune interactions that may affect cell survival and complicate the interpretation of immune responses, though this approach provides valuable preliminary data on human cell behavior in vivo. However, encapsulation of these cells was suggested to be protective against xenogeneic immune interactions and has demonstrated improved cell survivability of pancreatic islet cells co-encapsulated with MSCs.19 32 35 Third, the short study duration captures only early viability patterns and does not assess long-term persistence or functional recovery of encapsulated cells. Future studies are necessary to validate therapeutic efficacy and assess optimal timing of device removal, the need for additional device insertion, and long-term cell survival, functionality, and device safety. Fourth, the modest sample size is a limitation of this pilot study. However, the longitudinal IVIS design allowed repeated within-animal assessment of MSC viability over time, thereby reducing animal use while enabling comparison of signal decay between delivery strategies. Future studies with larger cohorts will be necessary to confirm these findings and evaluate therapeutic efficacy in neonatal NEC models.

Next, another limitation of this study is that MSC viability declined over time even within the THRED, likely reflecting the inherent metabolic and inflammatory stress associated with in vivo implantation; however, the significantly slower rate of decline compared with free-cell delivery underscores the protective effect and relative superiority of encapsulation. Prior studies have noted challenges with long-term biocompatibility of a foreign device such as the THRED, with resulting peri-capsular fibrosis and inflammation,19 yet Long et al. noted improvement in fibrotic capsular formation during co-encapsulation of islet cells with MSCs, and An et al. noted minimal fibrotic encapsulation with device placement in the subhepatic space, suggesting either inherent anti-inflammatory or immunosuppressive potential between the human MSCs and murine hosts.18 32 Future studies should focus on histological assessment of the fibrotic tissue capsule, solutions for long-term compatibility, and consider the use of syngeneic or allogeneic models.

Finally, these experiments focused on cell survival and phenotype rather than therapeutic efficacy; future disease-model studies will be required to determine whether sustained viability translates into clinical benefit, including analysis of the following: histologic and macroscopic injury scores, clinical sickness severity scores, survival rates, intestinal recovery, inflammatory cytokine profiles (e.g., tumor necrosis factor-alpha, interleukins (IL)-6, IL-1β, and IL-10), and immune cell populations. Despite these limitations, this work provides foundational data demonstrating that the THRED can sustain MSC viability and phenotype in vivo, supporting its advancement toward disease-specific testing in translational neonatal models.

Conclusion

Encapsulation within the THRED preserves MSC viability and identity in vivo. This platform offers a clinically practical approach for localized MSC delivery with the added ability of being retrievable, addressing key safety and feasibility barriers to neonatal MSC therapy. Future studies in large-animal disease models will be essential to evaluate therapeutic efficacy and establish the THRED as a viable strategy for targeted, controlled MSC delivery in diseases like NEC.
