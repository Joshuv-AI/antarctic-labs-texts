Preferences for Infection Prevention in Hospital-Acquired Infections
Authors: Shalini Elangovan, Sameera Senanayake, Indumathi Venkatachalam, Moi Lin Ling, Nicholas Graves, Sanjeewa Kularatna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionWhat factors most affect health care professionals’ decisions when selecting infection prevention and control practices for hospital-acquired infections across different contexts?FindingsIn this survey study including 256 health care professionals, cost-effectiveness was consistently the most important factor associated with adoption, while implementation duration was least important. Two latent classes identified were evidence seekers from low- and middle-income countries who valued strong clinical evidence, and efficiency seekers from high-income countries who prioritized cost-effectiveness.MeaningThese results suggest there are global contextual differences in preferences for characteristics of infection prevention and control practices that may explain willingness to adopt infection prevention and control practices across settings.

Introduction

Health care–associated infections (HAI) are acquired while receiving medical treatment in various health care settings, including acute facilities, long-term care facilities and outpatient settings.1 They are often preventable and result in increased mortality risk, morbidity, and costs to health services.2,3 Infection prevention and control is widely implemented across health care settings to reduce the spread of HAIs and improve patient outcomes. As resources are scarce for infection prevention, it is important to prioritize cost-effective programs. But other factors play into a decision to adopt a novel program. The first Global Report on Infection Prevention and Control by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 2023 recommended infection prevention and control (IPC) measures to reduce transmission of HAIs by up to 70%.4 While the report lists cost-effective practices as “good,” other defining characteristics of good IPC practices are not well-described.5 We suggest that characteristics that make an IPC program likely to attract an investment decision by practitioners are not completely understood. To address these knowledge gaps, and to estimate practitioner preferences when selecting IPC programs, a discrete choice experiment (DCE) was performed.

This DCE explores practitioners’ preferences for key attributes of IPC programs, and assesses the relative importance of these attributes when selecting practices to prevent HAIs. A global respondent sample was targeted for 2 reasons. The limited number of potential participants in the country where the study was conducted would not have provided sufficient statistical power. Although a local sample would have enhanced internal validity, it would have restricted external generalizability, reducing relevance of the findings across health systems.

Methods

DCE is a stated-preference survey method used to quantify individual decision-making processes and preference strengths.6 The DCE has 4 phases, including attribute development, pretesting to assess functionality of the DCE, piloting to generate priors, and statistical analysis (Box). This study was designed and reported in accordance with the DIRECT checklist and conducted in accordance with the American Association for Public Opinion Research (AAPOR) Best Practices for Survey Research.7 This study was approved by the Duke-NUS Departmental Ethics Review Committee and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Attribute and Attribute Level Selection

A systematic literature search was conducted to identify characteristics of IPC practices targeting HAIs. PubMed and Embase were searched between 2014 and 2024 to ensure relevance to contemporary IPC practices, resulting in the inclusion of 2 studies (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1).8,9

Expert opinion from 3 IPC professionals was sought to exclude noncritical characteristics (eAppendix 1 and eTable 1 in Supplement 1). Potential characteristics were reviewed for relevance, actionability, and suitability for inclusion. Characteristics considered noncritical to IPC decision-making, redundant, or not amenable to meaningful trade-offs were excluded to reduce cognitive burden and ensure feasibility of the DCE. The first expert was the head of infection prevention and epidemiology in the largest national acute tertiary hospital, the second was the chair of an infection prevention committee at the national center for infectious and communicable diseases and the third was a senior consultant specializing in infectious diseases and HAIs. Remaining characteristics were merged into 4 attributes with 3 levels each (Table 1; eMethods 1 in Supplement 1).

Choice Task and Design Considerations

Each choice task presented 2 unlabelled alternatives describing IPC practices, along with an opt-out option indicating a preference for neither practice, allowing for a more realistic simulation of clinical scenarios (Table 2).10 At the beginning of the DCE survey, participants were informed that each IPC practice in a choice task was assumed to reduce the risk of HAIs by 10%, reflecting a clinically plausible and meaningful reduction.11,12 A fractional factorial design was used to generate 18 choice tasks split into 2 blocks of 9, sufficient for statistical precision while avoiding excessive cognitive load and participant fatigue (eAppendix 2 in Supplement 1).13,14

Respondent Recruitment

Inclusion criteria were that respondents were health care professionals older than age 21 years who had participated in IPC decision-making. There were no restrictions based on country of practice or origin, or health care setting. Potential participants were identified through publicly available information in publications from 10 IPC journals (eTable 2 in Supplement 1). Email invitations were sent to eligible authors, and screening questions excluded individuals who did not meet the inclusion criteria. In underrepresented regions, email addresses were retrieved from national medical journals.

Statistical Analysis

A pilot DCE was developed in Ngene version 2.0 (ChoiceMetrics) to generate informative priors for the final design. Pilot data were analyzed using a multinomial logit (MNL) model, and resulting parameter estimates were used to inform the final DCE. A dummy-coded, bayesian d-efficient design with Gaussian random draws was used to generate choice sets for the final DCE. We used dummy coding as reference levels are coded as zero, making it easy to interpret.15

Main effects were estimated for all models. MNL parameter estimates served as priors for mixed MNL (MMNL) and 2-class latent class models with demographic covariates. Models were estimated via maximum likelihood using 100 modified Latin hypercube sampling draws, with parameters fixed within each class in the latent class model. Latent class analysis (LCA) was used to identify preference heterogeneity by estimating models with increasing numbers of classes and selecting the 2-class solution based on model fit and interpretability. Respondents were probabilistically assigned to each class based on their estimated class membership probabilities. Class labels were applied post hoc for interpretive purposes and were based on the dominant attributes influencing choice within each class. Statistical significance was assessed using robust Wald tests, and confidence intervals were constructed using standard errors to reflect estimation uncertainty with significance indicated at P < .05.16

The sample size for the final DCE was calculated using S-estimates generated from the pilot DCE. The S-estimate represents the minimum sample required to estimate all model parameters with 95% certainty, while the Sb mean estimate indicates the minimum sample required to estimate each parameter.17 The largest Sb mean estimate was 1386 respondents per block, which was unfeasible. The next largest Sb mean estimate of 81 per block was used, resulting in a minimum sample size requirement of 162. The final sample of 256 with 128 respondents per block exceeded this estimate. To assess potential nonresponse bias, the first 20 respondents and the last 20 respondents were compared in a wave analysis.

The relative importance of each attribute was estimated for each class using the differences between the highest- and lowest-part worth utilities. The larger the utility coefficient, the greater an attribute was estimated to affect decision-making. The reference level was assigned a coefficient of zero. A positive coefficient indicated preference for that level over the reference, while a negative coefficient indicated preference for the reference level. Alternative specific constants (ASCs) represent baseline preference for selecting an IPC practice relative to the opt-out, independent of attribute levels.18

To explore regional differences in attribute importance, we conducted a subgroup analysis by fitting separate MMNL models for each of the 6 WHO regions: Europe, Africa, Americas, Eastern Mediterranean, South East Asia, and Western Pacific. Uncertainty was managed using 10 000 Monte Carlo simulations to generate distributions and 95% CIs surrounding the mean relative importance values. Scenario analyses was conducted to estimate the probability of uptake for an IPC practice, both overall and stratified by respondent class. Realistic scenarios representing 3 implemented IPC programs were developed in consultation with an IPC expert, namely portable high-efficiency particulate air filters, UV-C disinfection machines, and automated surveillance programs. A fourth, baseline scenario composed entirely of reference levels for each attribute was also included. The purpose of the scenario analysis was to illustrate how estimated preferences translate into uptake probabilities for clinical IPC practices, rather than to provide a comprehensive factorial exploration of all attribute combinations.

Results

Respondent Demographics

A total of 16 982 invites were sent and 256 complete responses were received giving a response rate of 1.5% overall (161 male [62.9%]); the median age cohort was ages 40 to 49 years (84 respondents [32.8%]), and a plurality were from Europe (103 [40.2%]) followed by the West Pacific region (50 [19.5%]) (Table 3). Response rate was highest for the African region (27 of 989 [2.7%]), followed by Western Pacific (50 of 2792 [1.8%]), Eastern Mediterranean (23 of 1681 [1.4%]), Europe (103 of 7155 [1.4%]), South East Asian (16 of 1201 [1.3%]), and Americas (37 of 3164 [1.2%]) regions (eTable 3 in Supplement 1). No respondents were excluded. The wave analysis found no significant differences between early and late responders across characteristics (eTable 4 in Supplement 1). The majority of respondents were involved in IPC decision-making at least 5 times in the past year (225 [87.9%]). Most respondents were very familiar with clinical evidence (146 [57.0%]) but only somewhat familiar with economic evidence (139 [54.3%]).

Mixed Multinomial Logit Model

With the opt-out specified as the reference, ASCs were negative (eTable 5 in Supplement 1), indicating that respondents were less likely to select either IPC practice compared with opting out. Systematic reviews and randomized controlled trials had a positive coefficient of 1.70 (95% CI, 1.35-2.04), hence respondents were likely to choose IPC practices supported by strong clinical evidence, compared with the baseline of narrative reviews and expert opinion (coefficient of 0). Respondents also preferred IPC practices that were quicker to implement (0.47; 95% CI, 0.24-0.70), involved small investments with large health returns (2.15; 95% CI, 1.77-2.54), and requiring fewer additional minutes per patient (0.98; 95% CI, 0.72-1.24).

Latent Class Model

The latent class model identified 2 distinct groups (Table 3). Class 1 placed greater importance on the quality of clinical evidence and class 2 on economic costs and benefits. Accordingly, class 1 with 126 respondents (49.2%) was labeled evidence seekers while class 2, including 130 respondents (50.8%), was labeled efficiency seekers. Evidence seekers tended to be younger than efficiency seekers but had similar sex distributions, with male respondents representing the majority for both classes (evidence seekers, 84 [64.6%]; efficiency seekers, 77 [61.1%]). The ASC was positive for evidence seekers and negative for efficiency seekers, indicating that evidence seekers were inclined to select an IPC practice over opting out while efficiency seekers were significantly less likely to do so. Country income status was defined according to the World Bank country income classification.19 All 96 respondents from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) were evidence seekers, while the majority of respondents (126 of 160 [78.7%]) from high-income countries (HICs) were efficiency seekers.

Respondents in both classes showed the strongest preference for systematic reviews and randomized clinical trials (evidence seekers: 1.19; 95% CI, 0.80 to 1.57; efficiency seekers: 1.30; 95% CI, 0.82 to 1.77) (Table 4). The valuation of attribute levels for time required for implementation was similar across classes, with 3 months being the most preferred (evidence seekers: 0.28; 95% CI, 0.06 to 0.50; efficiency seekers: 0.41; 95% CI, 0.12 to 0.70). Economic costs and benefits were also valued similarly, with a small investment in IPC yielding large health returns being the most preferred (evidence seekers: 0.59; 95% CI, 0.02 to 1.16; efficiency seekers: 2.87; 95% CI, 0.34 to 2.19). Similarly for the time required per patient, 5 minutes was the most preferred (evidence seekers: 0.33; 95% CI, 0.09 to 0.56; efficiency seekers: 1.62; 95% CI, 0.73 to 2.52).

Relative Attribute Importance

For evidence seekers, the most important attribute was the quality of clinical effectiveness evidence (48.9%; 95% CI, 39.3%-59.6%), followed by economic costs and benefits (24.2%; 95% CI, 12.1%-35.0%), the time required to complete the IPC action (14.4%; 95% CI, 7.4%-22.0%), and time required for initial implementation (12.5%; 95% CI, 6.2%-19.2%) (eTable 6 in Supplement 1). Efficiency seekers placed the greatest importance on economic costs and benefits (45.8%; 95% CI, 40.2%-51.7%), followed by time required to complete the IPC action (25.9%; 95% CI, 19.9%-31.6%), quality of clinical effectiveness evidence (20.6%; 95% CI, 15.7%-25.6%), and, similar to evidence seekers, ranked time required for initial implementation as least important (7.6%; 95% CI, 4.1%-11.4%).

Subgroup Analysis

The analysis revealed distinct preference structures across regions (eTable 6 in Supplement 1). Economic costs and benefits were the most strongly valued attribute across all regions, although similarly valued with the quality of clinical evidence in the Americas region. The Africa and Americas regions valued the additional time per patient highly compared with other regions (26.9% and 23.8% respectively), which was the least important in Eastern Mediterranean (11.8%). Implementation time was generally less influential, ranging from 7.4% in the Americas to 16.2% in Africa.

Scenario Analysis

The base scenario had the lowest predicted uptake overall (38.2%) and within both classes (evidence seekers, 71.5%; efficiency seekers, 3.8%). Evidence seekers consistently demonstrated high likelihoods of adopting IPC practices, with uptake rates exceeding 90% regardless of attribute combinations (eTable 7 in Supplement 1). In contrast, efficiency seekers were more selective, showing reduced uptake when key attributes were less than ideal (eFigure 2 in Supplement 1). For instance, uptake fell to 43.9% for UV-C disinfection machines, which were associated with uncertain outcomes, moderate costs, and an additional 30 minutes of patient time.

Discussion

This study elicited preferences for selecting IPC practices for HAIs using DCE methodology and provides several important insights for global infection prevention policy and practice. Our findings demonstrate that respondents consistently preferred attributes associated with benefit-maximizing levels. We identified global heterogeneity in preferences, both between respondents from HICs and LMICs and respondents from different WHO regions. Furthermore, reported HAI prevalence varies across WHO regions, with estimates of 11.2% in the Eastern Mediterranean, 9.0% in South East Asia, 6.5% in Europe, and 3.2% in the Americas.20 The WHO global survey on infection prevention and control highlighted disparities in preparedness and the completeness of infection prevention programs, using the IPC Assessment Framework (IPCAF) which scores IPC implementation components across countries. The WHO survey found differences in IPC implementation level of different WHO regions with Eastern Mediterranean having the highest IPCAF score, followed by Europe, Western Pacific, Americas, South East Asia, and Africa regions.21 These differences in HAI prevalence and IPC implementation highlight that context matters, as what is feasible or prioritized in one region may not be in another. However, our findings show that cost-effectiveness was consistently the most important and implementation duration the least important attribute across regions except for the EMR, where the time burden of carrying out the IPC action ranked lowest.

Evidence generation in LMICs is slowed by severe resource constraints, a shortage of specialized health care infrastructure and personnel, and large gaps in basic epidemiological data despite bearing the vast majority of the global disease burden.22 This limited evidence base may have heightened the value LMIC respondents placed on high-quality clinical evidence. Differences in IPCAF scores between HICs and LMICs have also been reported with HICs having more advanced national IPC programmes.20 LMICs are also disproportionately affected by HAI, a situation compounded by limited resources to address disease burdens.23 The WHO report on the burden of endemic health care–associated infection worldwide reported a HAI prevalence of 10.1% in LMICs, compared with 7.6% in HICs in 2011.24 In line with this, respondents from LMICs face urgent demands from high HAI rates and less developed IPC infrastructures, which may explain the high likelihood of IPC adoption observed in our scenario analysis. Conversely, respondents from HICs may be more selective in implementing IPC measures because mature systems together with fewer HAI allow IPC practices with the greatest return on investment to be prioritized.

Our findings present potential implications for shaping practice. The consistently high uptake probabilities among evidence seekers could pose challenges if low-value IPC practices are available for implementation. In such cases, resources might be diverted to interventions that yield limited benefits. Incorporating cost-effectiveness evidence into decision-making may help optimize resource allocation to high-value practices.25 Efficiency seekers valued both cost-effectiveness and the time required for health care professionals to carry out IPC actions. This suggests that among HIC respondents, efficiency of IPC practices is a key driver of adoption. These findings can inform the design of IPC practices in high-income settings by emphasizing efficiency to support implementation. This is consistent with models such as IPC-CASCADE, which highlight the value of tailoring interventions to clinical and IPC workflows to optimize resource use.26

Limitations

There are limitations to this study. First, although the respondents had a global profile, with all 6 WHO regions being represented, the sample size per country is not sufficient to capture meaningful country-level differences, limiting the generalizability of our results. Furthermore, as LCA partitions the sample into subgroups, estimation precision within each class is reduced, and class-specific results should be interpreted as exploratory descriptions of preference heterogeneity rather than confirmatory findings. Response rates in health-related DCEs are often highly variable and have shown a declining trend over time.27 Despite designing our survey with a limited number of attributes and choice tasks to minimize respondent burden,28 the overall response rate was only 1.5%, highlighting the challenges of engaging our target population and the limitations of a broad email-based recruitment approach. Participants were identified through authorship in IPC journals and may therefore represent health care professionals with greater engagement in IPC research, introducing the potential for nonresponse bias. Although a wave analysis did not identify differences between early and late responders, unmeasured differences cannot be excluded which may limit external validity. Nevertheless, our sample size exceeded the S-estimates generated by Ngene, a common approach for estimating sample size in DCEs, and was sufficient for statistical significance.17,29,30 Second, unobserved attributes may influence IPC decision-making, potentially contributing to higher opt-out rates, particularly among efficiency seekers. This is an inherent DCE limitation, as not all relevant attributes can be included.18 Furthermore, IPC effectiveness was fixed at a 10% reduction to minimize uncertainty for respondents, which may have affected the quality of clinical evidence attribute. Lastly, economic costs and benefits was described qualitatively rather than with monetary values to facilitate participation from respondents across diverse international contexts, with different costs and cost-effectiveness thresholds. However, qualitative levels may limit respondents’ ability to map preferences to economic trade-offs. Accordingly, findings related to this attribute reflect perceived economic value rather than willingness-to-pay or cost-effectiveness thresholds.

Conclusions

In this survey study of health care professionals, cost-effectiveness consistently drove decision-making when selecting IPC practices against HAI, while implementation duration was generally less important. Distinct latent classes highlight contextual differences between regions. Future research should prioritize analyses of LMIC respondents to capture broader contexts influencing IPC decision-making and to validate preference patterns observed here.
