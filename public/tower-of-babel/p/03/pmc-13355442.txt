CKD Prevalence in Rural Population of Sri Lanka
Authors: Boris Bikbov
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

The analysis performed by Abeysekera et al.1 evaluates chronic kidney disease (CKD) prevalence in the Kandy District of Sri Lanka. The study has a strong methodology, was implemented using advanced population sampling, reports both unadjusted and age-standardized estimates, presents multiple metrics of other noncommunicable disease prevalence and their relationship with CKD, and provides important evidence on the population awareness of CKD risk factors. All this information is extremely useful for planning of public health interventions.

However, the statement that CKD prevalence in the rural sector was similar to that in the urban sector requires careful evaluation. Although the total unadjusted CKD prevalence was indeed similar (15.4% vs. 12.5%), rural participants had almost twice higher prevalence of albuminuria compared with their urban counterparts (11.2% vs. 6.1%), suggesting greater exposure to factors leading to kidney damage and albuminuria. At the same time, the rural compared with urban population has a lower prevalence of diabetes (26.1% vs. 36.9%), obesity (19.8% vs. 25.7%), and ischemic heart disease (4.4% vs. 10.1%); and comparable prevalence of hypertension (61.1% vs. 63.4%) and median age (57 years in both groups).

This discrepancy between the distribution of classic CKD risk factors and the higher albuminuria prevalence suggests that the rural population of the Kandy District may be exposed to factors associated with CKD of unknown etiology—that has albuminuria as the major marker and was previously demonstrated in the agricultural provinces of Sri Lanka’s northern districts Anuradhapura and Polonnaruwa, and southern Badulla district.2 CKD of unknown etiology prevalence was highest among communities that practice slash-and-burn agricultural cultivation and that rely on water wells located close to irrigation systems and/or situated below the water table level, and lower in communities without irrigation seepage into wells.2,3 The personal protection of farmers and treatment of drinking water can substantially reduce the CKD of unknown etiology burden.4 The nontrivial 5% absolute difference in prevalence of albuminuria observed in the study by Abeysekera et al.5 suggests that there is a room for improving CKD of unknown etiology prevention in the rural populations. Once again, these findings demonstrate the urgent need for joint efforts among health, environmental, and agricultural services to protect farming communities.
