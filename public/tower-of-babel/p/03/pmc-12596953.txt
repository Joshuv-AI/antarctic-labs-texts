Spatial heterogeneity determines the gastrointestinal microbiome signatures in Pekin ducks
Authors: Haonan Zhao, Zhigang Hu, Jinlong Ru, Zezhong Wang, Jie Wei, Wei Zhou, Shuisheng Hou, Yunsheng Zhang, Xia Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe gastrointestinal tract (GIT) hosts a complex and abundant microbial ecosystem that is essential for maintaining animal health and optimizing production performance. However, most studies have been confined to individual intestinal segments, underscoring the need for a more holistic understanding of microbial communities across various GIT segments in animals, including Pekin ducks which is important to global poultry meat production. Here, we conducted 16S rRNA sequencing to characterize the microbial landscape along four GIT segments in 313 Pekin ducks (raised with commercial diet) and correlated them with blood biochemical indicators at day 42. Analyses of microbial composition, diversity, functional potential, and co-occurrence networks revealed significant spatial heterogeneity in microbial colonization patterns along the GIT. The small intestine exhibited higher α-diversity than the cecum, and the most complex co-occurrence network presented in ileum. Besides, segment-specific functional specialization of intestinal microbiota reflected their adaptation to local environments. This compartmentalization of GIT microbial communities into functional units underscores regionally encoded functional heterogeneity. These findings enhance our understanding of GIT microbiota in Pekin ducks, informing strategies for feed optimization and breeding improvements.Keywords: Spatial heterogeneity, Gastrointestinal, Pekin ducks, Microbiome, Biochemical indicators

Introduction

The global demand and production of duck meat continue to rise annually, with Pekin ducks serving as one of the primary breeds for poultry meat production, particularly in Asia and Northern and Central Europe (Kokoszynski et al., 2019). However, the breeding program of Pekin duck still needs to be optimized to meet the growing market demand.

Emerging evidence establishes the duck gastrointestinal tract (GIT) microbiome as a critical interface between environmental inputs and host physiology, such as nutrient digestion, immune system development, and feed efficiency improvement (Belkaid and Hand, 2014; Chen et al., 2021; Tian et al., 2020c). Studies highlight that factors such as diet, antibiotic use, and environmental temperature can significantly shape the gut microbiota composition, underscoring its role in promoting health and preventing diseases (Qin et al., 2020; Song et al., 2022; Tian et al., 2020c). As a multi-organ system, the duck GIT harbors pronounced regional diversity in microbial communities, with distinct environmental conditions across segments driving colonization patterns and functional specialization (Ma et al., 2024a; Zhang et al., 2025a). Despite this complexity, previous studies have focused disproportionately on single GIT regions (e.g., cecal or fecal microbiota), overlooking inter-regional interactions, structural influences, and functional potential across the entire GIT (Dai et al., 2018; Lyu et al., 2021; Qin et al., 2020). Therefore, a systematic investigation into the spatial heterogeneity of microbial communities in multiple intestinal segments of Pekin duck is urgently needed.

Blood composition reflects essential health traits, as well as the metabolic and nutritional status of animals (Jiang et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2024). Specifically, electrolytes in the bloodstream play a crucial role in animals for acid-base balance, cellular homeostasis, synthesis of tissue protein, electrical potential of cell membranes, enzymatic reactions, etc. (Borges et al., 2007; Xu et al., 2024). The gut microbiota, acting as a bridge between extra-intestinal sites and systemic metabolism, modulates processes such as energy homeostasis, glucose metabolism, and lipid metabolism, directly influencing blood biochemical indicators (Heianza et al., 2019; Hu et al., 2022; Karlsson et al., 2013; Yao et al., 2024). For instance, Pediococcus acidilactici alleviates obesity-related dyslipidemia in rats by altering blood lipid profiles (e.g., cholesterol, high density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglyceride) and suppressing inflammation (Yavorov-Dayliev et al., 2023), while Christensenella minuta mitigates acetaminophen-induced liver injury in mice by reducing alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and inflammatory markers (IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, etc.) (Yao et al., 2024). Microbial metabolites like short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)—including acetate (lipogenesis), propionate (gluconeogenesis), and butyrate (cell differentiation)—further mediate host-microbiota interactions (Amabebe et al., 2020; Carvalho and Saad, 2013; Chakraborti, 2015; Davis, 2016). Thus, elucidating the interplay between regional GIT microbiota and blood biomarkers could clarify how microbial diversity impacts Pekin duck physiology and homeostasis.

In this study, we analyzed the microbial diversity of four intestinal segments in 313 Pekin ducks using high-throughput 16S rRNA sequencing and correlated these findings with blood biochemical indicators. Functional potentials across segments were analyzed, microbial networks were constructed, and relationships between microbiota composition and host physiology were systematically evaluated. This work provides a comprehensive understanding of GIT microbial distribution, diversity, function, and interactions in Pekin ducks, offering insights for optimizing microbial feed additives and advancing breeding strategies.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The experiments were approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of the Institute of Animal Sciences (IAS), Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (CAAS), Beijing, China (IAS20160401).

Experimental animals

A total of 313 male Pekin ducks from a random mating population were raised for this study on the Pekin duck breeding farm of the Institute of Animal Sciences, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Beijing, China. At 1 day of age, 313 Pekin ducks were housed individually in single cages equipped with nipple drinkers and tubular feeders until 42 days of age. The dimension of cage was 50 cm (width) × 50 cm (length) × 30 cm (height). During the initial three days after hatching, ducks were exposed to continuous 24 L:0 D (24 h of light and 0 h of dark), transferring to 20 L:4 D (20 h of light and 4 h of dark) from 4 to 42 days of age. All the ducks were fed with full water and commercial diet. The composition and chemistry of the commercial diet were detailed in our previous research (Zhang et al., 2017).

Growth and feed efficiency traits collection

The initial body weight (iBW) of each duck was measured at 22 d of age. Total feed intake (FI) and average daily feed intake (ADFI) was recorded and calculated from 22 d to 42 d of age, with the final body weight (fBW) being measured at 42 d of age. According to the measured data, the average daily gain (ADG) was calculated, whereafter the feed conversion ratio (FCR) were obtained referring to our previous method (Zhang et al., 2017). Biochemical indicators evaluation

Before slaughtering all the 313 ducks at 6 weeks of age, we collected blood samples from wing veins of each duck by BD vacutainer tubes with heparin after 6 h of fasting. Ducks were euthanized using gas inhalation via carbon dioxide. Using the centrifugation at 3500 × g for 5 min at 4°C, the plasma was obtained and then stored at −20°C for subsequent analysis. A total of 20 biochemical indicators in plasma were measured. Due to the lack of commercial ELISA kits developed for duck, the kits validated in human blood were used. The ALT (catlog# CH0101201), AST (catlog# CH0101202), alkaline phosphatase (ALP) (catlog# CH0101203), total protein (TP) (catlog# CH0101008), albumin (ALB) (catlog# CH0101002), glucose (GLU) (catlog# CH0101102), lactatedehydrogenase (LDH) (catlog# CH0101208), lactic acid (LAC), uric acid (UA) (catlog# CH0101054), cholesterol (CHOL) (catlog# CH0101152), triglyceride (TG) (catlog# CH0101151), high density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) (catlog# CH0101161), low density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) (catlog# CH0101162), Ca (catlog# CH0101251), P (catlog# CH0101257), non-esterified fatty acids (NEFA) (catlog# RB30951), total bile acid (TBA) (catlog# CH0101005), and pyruvate (PYR) (catlog# RC40901) concentrations were measured using colorimetric enzymatic assay kits (Maccura Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Chengdu, China). All assays were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions on a Hitachi 7080 automatic biochemical analyzer (Tokyo, Japan). Then the AST/ALT and the ratio of ALB to globulin (A/G) were calculated.

16S rRNA sequencing and analysis

Upon euthanasia, the gastrointestinal tract was immediately excised. Four intestinal segments (duodenum-D, jejunum-J, ileum-I, cecum-C) were isolated, and the luminal contents from the middle part of each segment were gently expressed into sterile DNA-free collection tubes (catlog# AM12400), samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently stored at −80°C until DNA extraction (Lyte et al., 2025). Genomic DNA was extracted from 300 mg of each sample using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). DNA was eluted in 50 µL of Kit Solution C5, DNA concentration and purity (A260/A280 ratio) were measured using a NanoDrop™ One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A negative extraction control, consisting of molecular-grade water (RT121), was processed simultaneously with each batch of extractions (one control per 12 samples) to monitor for contamination from reagents and the extraction process itself. The DNA concentration of the negative control, as measured by Qubit™ dsDNA HS Assay Kit, was below the detection limit. The V3-V4 region of bacterial 16S rRNA genes was amplified using 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) primers. Next, the sequences after amplification were sequenced on a HiSeq 2500 System (Illumina) via PE250 strategy, with the melting temperature being set as 56°C and 30 PCR cycles being performed.

According to an amplicon sequencing analysis workflow Ampliseq (https://nf-co.re/ampliseq/2.11.0/, version 2.11.0) using DADA2 and QIIME2, reads quality controlling, primer trimming, ASV inferring, taxonomic classification (based on SILVA reference database (v138)), and abundance analysis were performed (options filter_ssu = "bac, arc", exclude_taxa = "mitochondria, chloroplast", and min_len=150). Alpha-diversity indices (Ace, Chao1, Observed species, Pielou, Simpson, and Shannon) were calculated using the vegan R package (version 2.6-10, https://github.com/vegandevs/vegan). Differences in alpha-diversity between the two groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and statistical significance was annotated with p-value symbols. Beta-diversity was evaluated by principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) based on Bray–Curtis distance matrices using vegan R package (version 2.6-10, https://github.com/vegandevs/vegan). The PICRUSt2 (version 2.5.3, https://github.com/picrust/picrust2) software and ggpicrust2 R package (version 1.7.1, https://github.com/cafferychen777/ggpicrust2) were used to predict the differentially microbial function potential between duodenum and jejunum, jejunum and ileum, ileum and cecum. The inappropriate predicted pathways (such as eukaryotic process, human disease, and plant pathways) were then removed.

Microbial co-occurrence network construction

All relative abundances of bacteria were then centered log-ratio (CLR) transformed. Based on the Spearman correlation, the microbial co-occurrence networks among microorganisms in different GIT regions were constructed by the Hmisc (version 5.2-0, https://hbiostat.org/r/hmisc/) and igraph (version 2.1.1, https://r.igraph.org/) R packages. The Gephi software (version 0.10.1, https://gephi.org/) was used to visualize the co-occurrence networks. All the correlation relationships with |r| > 0.3 and p < 0.05 were considered significant. To further detect these networks, we evaluated various network topology parameters (the number of vertices, number of edges, clustering coefficient, average betweenness, and average separation) using the vegan (version 2.6-10, https://github.com/vegandevs/vegan) and igraph (version 2.1.1, https://r.igraph.org/) R packages. The edges only presented in one GIT region network was defined as specialist edges, while the edges appeared in more than one GIT region network were regarded as generalist edges. To assess the complexity of these microbial co-occurrence networks, the numbers of positive and negative correlation for every network were counted.

Distinctive taxonomic characteristics of microbiomes across four gastrointestinal tract regions

The V3-V4 region of bacterial 16S rRNA genes was amplified and sequenced from four intestinal segments of 313 Pekin ducks, yielding 200,067,618 raw reads. After reads splicing and quality filtering, an average of clean 79,364 tags per sample were retained, with a mean length of 247 bp. Using the SILVA 138 reference database, 329,580 high-confidence ASVs were identified from the processed data, ensuring robust taxonomic resolution with minimal false positives. These ASVs were classified into 83 phyla, 193 classes, 450 orders, 683 families, 2,412 genera, and 3,108 species. At the genus level, Ligilactobacillus was most abundant in the duodenum and jejunum, while Fusobacterium and Bacteroides dominated the ileum and cecum, respectively (Fig. 1A). The Venn diagram showed 1,230 genera shared across all four intestinal segments (Fig. 1B), while segment-specific unique genera were identified as follows: duodenum (131), jejunum (150), ileum (56), and cecum (35). Comparative analysis of alpha-diversity indices demonstrated that the duodenum and jejunum harbored microbial communities with significantly higher richness and greater homogeneity compared to the ileum and cecum (Fig. 1C). Beta-diversity analyses (PCoA and NMDS, Fig. 1D-E) further demonstrated distinct compositional differences among segments, though no significant separation was observed between the duodenum and jejunum. These results highlight pronounced spatial heterogeneity in gut microbiota distribution and diversity across intestinal segments, with the proximal regions (duodenum/jejunum) harboring richer, more uniform communities than the distal ileum and cecum.

Niche-specific microbial functional potentials across different gastrointestinal tract regions

Differential microbial functional profiles across four intestinal segments were predicted. Compared to the jejunum, the duodenum microbiota exhibited significant enrichment of two pathways critical to host physiology, including “Bile secretion” (related to lipid digestion), and “alpha-linolenic acid metabolism” (related to omega-3 fatty acid synthesis), underscoring its role in nutrient processing (Fig. 2A). The jejunum microbiota showed elevated activity in multiple pathways compared to the ileum community. Among these, eight pathways were predominantly related to biosynthetic and metabolic processes, such as “Steroid hormone biosynthesis” (linked to endocrine regulation), “Glycosphingolipid biosynthesis−lacto and neolacto series” (linked to cell membrane integrity), “Steroid biosynthesis” (linked to cholesterol and hormone production), “Ether lipid metabolism” (linked to membrane lipid remodeling and microbial stress resistance), “Phosphonate and phosphinate metabolism” (linked to microbial phosphorus utilization and C–P bond compound degradation), “Various types of N-glycan biosynthesis” (linked to protein glycosylation and cell surface modification), “Other types of O-glycan biosynthesis” (linked to bacterial cell wall polysaccharide and capsule formation), and “Biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids” (linked to membrane fluidity regulation and bacterial stress adaptation) (Fig. 2B). In contrast, the cecum microbiota displayed a unique functional signature marked by eight pathways, including “Glycosaminoglycan degradation” (linked to carbohydrate metabolism), “Aldosterone−regulated sodium reabsorption” (linked to electrolyte balance), “Ether lipid metabolism” (linked to membrane lipid remodeling and microbial stress resistance), “Steroid biosynthesis” (linked to cholesterol and hormone production), “Protein digestion and absorption” (linked to amino acid release and microbial utilization of dietary proteins), “Carbohydrate digestion and absorption” (linked to degradation of complex carbohydrates and energy acquisition), “Glycine, serine and threonine metabolism” (linked to amino acid interconversion and one-carbon metabolic pathways), and “Glycerolipid metabolism” (linked to lipid turnover, membrane synthesis, and energy storage) (Fig. 2C). Together, these results highlight the segment-specific specialization of intestinal microbiota in functional potential, reflecting their adaptation to the distinct physicochemical environments and nutrient availability along the gut. Such differentiation underscores the coordinated microbial contributions to host digestion, metabolism, and homeostasis throughout the intestinal tract.

Microbial co-occurrence networks strength varies across gastrointestinal tract regions

To examine the correlations among microorganisms in each GIT region, Spearman correlation coefficients were computed based on the bacteria relative abundances (CLR transformed) at the genus level for each sample. Microbial co-occurrence networks were constructed by filtering correlation relationships with an absolute correlation coefficient > 0.3 and a significance threshold of p < 0.05 (Fig. 3A). The topological properties analysis revealed distinct characteristics among the four networks (Fig. 3B). The jejunum microbial co-occurrence network exhibited the most vertices (n = 200), whereas the ileum network demonstrated the greatest number of edges (n = 2,568) and the highest clustering coefficient (0.29) compared to the other regions. The duodenum network displayed the highest average betweenness centrality (136.70) and the highest average separation (2.54). These findings suggest that the ileum microbial community possesses a more complex network structure, characterized by a higher number of edges and clustering coefficients. Notably, the duodenum exhibited a relatively high proportion of generalist edges (52.60 %; present in at least 2 GIT regions), whereas the ileum contained the highest proportion of specialist edges (81.54 %; present in only 1 GIT region). This indicates that the ileum harbors a greater number of unique microorganisms, leading to the establishment of novel microbial correlation relationships (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, the proportions of positive and negative correlations within each network were analyzed. Interestingly, the majority of microorganisms across all regions except for the ileum exhibited more positive correlations than negative ones with other microorganisms (Fig. 3D). Only a small subset of microorganisms displayed more negative correlations than positive ones (Fig. 3D).

Differential correlation between microbiota and biochemical indicators across gastrointestinal tract regions

As fundamental markers of an animal’s health, metabolism, and nutritional status, biochemical indicators are influenced by gut microbiota (Hu et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2024). A total of 20 biochemical indicators were collected from the blood of Pekin ducks, with details shown in Additional File 1 Table 1. Spearman’s correlation analysis revealed numerous interrelations among these indicators (Fig. 4), such as a strong positive correlation between CHOL and HDL-C (r = 0.88) and a negative correlation between ALT and AST/ALT (r = −0.68). These findings suggest that biochemical indicators are not independent but rather interact with each other.

To investigate the influence of GIT microbial community composition and diversity on homeostasis and physiological functions, genera with detection rates >30 % and relative abundances >0.05 % were selected for the analysis. Fig. 5A-D illustrated the correlations between 20 biochemical indicators and the α-diversity indices of microbiota in each GIT region. Among these correlations, 4 indicators (A/G, ALB, NEFA, and PYR) in duodenum, 3 indicators (LAC, P, and TP) in jejunum, 3 indicators (Ca, HDL-C, and P) in ileum, and 11 indicators (ALT, AST, CHOL, GLU, HDL-C, LAC, LDL-C, NEFA, P, TBA, and TP) in cecum were positively correlated with 3 diversity indices (Simpson, Shannon, and Pielou), but negatively correlated with 2 indices (Chao1 and Ace). These results suggest that higher expression of these indicators reflects more evenly distributed microbial communities but reduced diversity, likely due to the dominance of common species and the scarcity of rare species in corresponding GIT regions. Conversely, 4 indicators (ALT, AST, GLU, and LAC) in duodenum, 11 indicators (A/G, ALB, ALP, AST, AST/ALT, LDH, LDL-C, NEFA, PYR, TG, and UA) in jejunum, and 2 indicators (A/G and ALP) in cecum were negatively correlated with 3 indices (Simpson, Shannon and Pielou), but positively related to 2 indices (Chao1 and Ace). This indicates that specific environmental or biochemical conditions in certain regions may promote the growth or presence of new species, thereby increasing species richness while reducing community evenness.

Discussion

The gastrointestinal tract represents a highly complex and regionally diverse system, harboring a diverse and dynamic community of gut microorganisms (Martinez-Guryn et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2020). Each segment of the GIT fulfills distinct physiological functions, which are critical to sustaining overall health and productivity in poultry (Martinez-Guryn et al., 2019; Tropini et al., 2017; Xiao et al., 2017). Previous studies on the GIT of ducks have predominantly focused on the interactions between microbiota and the host within a single GIT region, while the effects of regional differences on the microbial community structure, and the functional potentials of the duck microbiota throughout the GIT have been neglected (Dai et al., 2018; Lyu et al., 2021; Qin et al., 2020). Based on 16S rRNA sequencing, we systematically investigated the spatial heterogeneity of microbial communities in four GIT regions in Pekin ducks to gain a more comprehensive understanding of their community composition and functional dynamics.

Along the GIT regions in Pekin ducks, we found a different trend in microbial community composition, that the small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, and ileum) had significant higher α-diversity than the cecum, which is consistent with the former research (Ma et al., 2024b). The predominance of lactic acid-producing bacteria, such as Ligilactobacillus spp., and Lactobacillus spp., in the small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, and ileum) is consistent with their well-established roles in carbohydrate fermentation and promoting the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which play a pivotal role in keeping the gut health (Bjerrum et al., 2006; Hati et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2025). Aligned with the previous report (Ma et al., 2024b), Bacteroides was the dominant genus in the cecum, while showing markedly lower abundance in the other 3 sections. The prevalence of Bacteroides, a well-characterized mucin-degrading taxon, suggests active mucin degradation and efficient utilization of complex carbohydrates in the cecal environment (El Kaoutari et al., 2013; Lin et al., 2023). Bacteroides species contain a much larger number of genes encoding carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) that can break down host mucins and dietary polysaccharides (Tailford et al., 2015).

Differentially microbial functional profiles among GIT regions displayed unique functional signature in each region. Notably, the significantly enriched pathway in duodenum includes “Bile secretion”. The duodenum is known to be the site of lipid digestion and absorption facilitated by bile (Hundt et al., 2025). Bile acids (BAs) are important determinants of the abundance, diversity and metabolic activity of the microbiota. The amphipathic nature of BAs facilitates membrane insertion and disruption in bacteria (Sannasiddappa et al., 2017; Tian et al., 2020b). BAs also can compromise bacterial viability through FXR-mediated activation of antimicrobial pathways or inducing DNA damage, protein misfolding, and oxidative stress (Begley et al., 2005; Inagaki et al., 2006; Liu et al., 2017). Despite their toxic effects, BAs also support microbiota diversity. It has been suggested that BA secretion may provide metabolic energy to sustain diverse microbiota (Li et al., 2017). Beyond compositional changes, BAs alter microbial functions. For instance, deoxycholic acid (DCA), taurocholic acid (TCA), and tauroursodeoxycholic acid at sublethal concentrations disrupt bacterial nucleotide and carbohydrate metabolism in murine models (Tian et al., 2020a). The jejunum, as the primary site for absorption of nutrient-derived carbohydrates, amino acids, small peptides, and vitamins (Sundin et al., 2017), harbors microorganisms whose dominant functions are significantly focused on synthesis and metabolism in this work, highlighting its crucial role in host nutrient digestion and absorption (Myer et al., 2016). Among them, “steroid hormone synthesis” and “steroid synthesis” are closely related to the gut microbiota. Sex hormones (one kind of steroid hormones) and gut microbiota exhibit bidirectional interactions (Santos-Marcos et al., 2023). Murine studies showing microbial colonization alters estradiol/testosterone levels, with post-pubertal microbiota displaying sex-specific differences reversible by castration (Kamimura et al., 2019; Markle et al., 2013; Yurkovetskiy et al., 2013). Clinical observations of microbiota shift during hormonal transitions (puberty, menopause, PCOS) (Insenser et al., 2018; Liang et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2020), while the microbiota may influence sex hormone levels conversely, for instance, affecting non-ovarian estrogen levels in men and postmenopausal women through the enterohepatic circulation (Dominianni et al., 2015; Flores et al., 2012). In cecum, observed priority pathways, such as “glycosaminoglycan degradation” and “aldosterone-regulated sodium reabsorption”, confirmed the cecum microbial function in fermentation and maintenance of electrolyte balance (Dabareiner and White, 1997; Stumpff et al., 2019; Tu et al., 2025). Glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), commonly referred to as mucopolysaccharides, persistently exist in the intestinal tract either in free forms or as integral components of proteoglycans. Evidence suggests that GAGs serve dual roles as both nutrient sources and microbial colonization sites for gut microbiota (Rawat et al., 2022). Analogous to the secretory mucus layer, proteoglycans undergo continuous turnover, that result in the release of degradation products into the mucus, thereby providing additional nutrient substrates for luminal bacteria (Moran et al., 2011). Critically, GAGs represent key targets for enzymatic degradation by commensal microbiota, highlighting their functional importance in sustaining gut microbial ecology (Yamaguchi and Yamamoto, 2023). Another focused process “aldosterone-regulated sodium reabsorption” plays a pivotal role in maintaining electrolyte balance and fluid homeostasis (Tsilosani et al., 2022). Previous researches have indicated an interaction between gut microbiota and aldosterone regulation (Gonzalez-Correa et al., 2023; Mizoguchi et al., 2023; Pasanen et al., 2019). The gut microbiota has a localized renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), which is important for the absorption of sodium and water from the colon and the control of blood pressure (Pasanen et al., 2019). Additionally, mineralocorticoid receptor blockade can reduce the gut sympathetic tone, thereby improving the gut microbiota dysbiosis in spontaneously hypertensive rats (Gonzalez-Correa et al., 2023).

The microbial co-occurrence networks in our study also demonstrate pronounced spatial heterogeneity along the four GIT regions, as their clustering patterns vary significantly depending on the factors (pH, oxygen, digesta flow rate, nutrient availability, host immunity and etc.) in different intestinal regions, that is also been proved in other animals (Wen et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2025b). The topological structures of the microbial co-occurrence networks varied across the four GIT regions. Specifically, the cecal microbial co-occurrence network showed the lowest complexity, with the least vertices (n = 102), least edges (n = 722), and lowest average betweenness (60.10) compared to the others. This is probably due to the differentiation of bacterial niche breadth in the cecum that may mitigate direct competition among diverse taxa, consequently reducing their interactions (Zhang et al., 2025b). Unlike the other regions, where microorganisms are exposed to abundant digestive fluids and mechanical activities, the cecum provides a relatively stable environment characterized by slower flow rates and neutral to mildly acidic pH. This stability likely contributes to a more conserved bacterial community structure, ultimately leading to fewer microbial interactions (Ma et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2021). Moreover, across the small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, and ileum) regions, a significant proportion of microorganisms in each network had a higher frequency of positive correlations than negative correlations with other microorganisms, suggesting that microorganisms within the GIT regions usually establish mutualistic interactions for mutual growth and metabolic activities, which contribute to the stability and equilibrium of the microbial ecosystem (Fassarella et al., 2021). Further work is essential to uncover the specific mechanistic of these microbiota-biochemical indicator interactions, thereby facilitating the development of feeding regimens and health management protocols for Pekin ducks breeding.

Conclusion

In this study, we conducted 16S rRNA sequencing to characterize the microbial communities across four GIT regions in 313 Pekin ducks. Analyses of microbial community composition, diversity, function, and co-occurrence networks demonstrated pronounced spatial heterogeneity in microbial colonization patterns along four regions. The small intestine had significant higher α-diversity than cecum, and duodenum had the largest number of specific microorganisms. The ileum exhibited the most complex microbial co-occurrence network. Besides, segment-specific functional specialization of intestinal microbiota reflected their adaptation to local environments. This compartmentalization of GIT microbial communities into functional units underscores regionally encoded functional heterogeneity. These findings provide valuable insights into the spatial heterogeneity of microbial communities in various GIT regions of ducks and their correlation with biochemical indicators.
