Integrated phenotype, endogenous hormones and transcriptome analysis revealed the mechanism of response of Phoebe bournei seedlings to shade signals
Authors: Honghao Huo, Xiaoli Wei, Fuyin Jiang, Shijing Feng, Jing An
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstory tree seedlings are subjected to prolonged shading stress imposed by the canopy foliage, which significantly impedes their growth. A hallmark of shaded environments is a reduced red to far-red light ratio (R: FR). This study elucidates the physiological and molecular responses of the endangered tree species Phoebe bournei to shading signals. Seedlings were exposed to white light (control) and simulated shading environments with R: FR ratios of 1.5, 0.8, and 0.2. The findings reveal that an increase in the proportion of far-red light significantly enhances seedling height, root-collar diameter, internode length, petiole length, leaf surface area, and leaf biomass. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in each treatment group predominantly enrich pathways associated with hormone signaling, stress responses, and photosynthesis. Validation experiments demonstrate that shading promotes the activity of Rubisco and RCA enzymes, total chlorophyll (Chl) accumulation, and elevated levels of hormones including indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), gibberellic acid (GA3), salicylic acid (SA)/methyl salicylate (MeSA), cytokinins (CK), abscisic acid (ABA), and jasmonic acid (JA). Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) identifies seven hub genes linked to photosynthesis and plant hormone regulation: MYB, KSC, SUAR, CESA POD, CESA, and SAUR. Collectively, shading signals induce P. bournei seedlings to elongate their stems and petioles, enhance photosynthetic enzyme activity, and accumulate specific hormones, with pertinent genes actively participating in light signal transduction. This research sheds light on the shading response mechanism of P. bournei, providing a robust theoretical framework for the breeding of shade-tolerant trees and the conservation of endangered species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12289-z.Keywords: Phoebe bournei, R:FR, Transcriptome, Weighted gene co-expression network analysis, Phytohormones, Morphological traits

Introduction

As a crucial abiotic factor, light not only acts as an original energy supplier for photosynthesis of plants, but also provides environmental cues to shape the development and growth of plants. The biological evolution of plants confers optimal abilities to access and utilize light resources, as well as to sense and thrive under low light conditions, ultimately ensuring their continuation and succession in nature [1]. Light availability exhibits great variation due to seasonal fluctuations, geographical distribution, species, and surrounding vegetation, which affect light intensity, quality, and photoperiod. For plants growing at the forest edge or beneath the canopy, the photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) they receive may vary due to the absorption and reflection of wavelengths by overlying vegetation. For these plants, red light (R, 600–700 nm) and blue light (B, 400–500 nm) are greatly absorbed by the upper layer of the forest, thereby leading to a decreased ratio of R: FR, termed as “shading signal” [2]. It is shown that the R: FR ratio ranges from 1 to 1.5 in natural light and drops to below 1.0 in the presence of neighbors or under the canopy [3, 4].

FR radiation is an important environmental signal that regulates the whole life cycle of plants. When the ratio of red light to far-red light (R: FR) in the environment changes, plants can rapidly perceive this change through the red light receptor—phytochrome (PHYs)—and respond in a timely manner, ultimately exhibiting the shade avoidance syndrome (SAS). This response allows plants to re-evaluate the balance between growth and resource allocation, thereby optimizing morphogenesis, enhancing their ability to capture light energy, and improving their adaptability in competition [5]. However, unlike the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana and other crops, understory seedlings must endure long-term shade, complicating their response to shaded environments. For understory seedlings, growing tall may not be the optimal coping strategy since they are unable to outgrow above the canopy [6]. Although evidence has shown that seedlings display superiority over mature trees in acclimating to shade sufficient light supply in the seedling stage is still critical for the development of woody plants. In recent years, the SAS mechanism has been extensively studied in the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana. However, the response mechanisms of understory tree seedlings to shading stress under shaded conditions remain largely unknown [7]. In the SAS mode, plants sense changes in R: FR and rapidly trigger photoreceptors encoded by phytochrome genes, particularly phyB, which converts from the active Pfr to the inactive Pr form under shading conditions. This releases the suppression of phytochrome-interacting factors (PIFs) and initiates downstream signal transduction pathways [8]. These pathways control a wide spectrum of physiological and biochemical responses, in turn leading to morphological and physiological plasticity such as stem elongation, leaf hyponasty and early flowering, enabling plants to antagonize the shading stress [9]. However, it remains largely unknown whether woody plant seedlings share similar SAS strategies as crops to cope with shaded environments. Besides the phenotypic changes, various endogenous phytohormones such as auxin, gibberellin (GA), ethylene (ET) and cytokinin (CK) have been reported to be involved in SAS machinery [10].However, research on the involvement and regulatory mechanisms of endogenous hormones in understory tree seedlings under low R: FR conditions is limited, and these aspects remain unclear. Additionally, different species exhibit varying responses to shading signals, thus warranting further investigation into the shading coping strategies of a broader range of species.

Phoebe bournei (P. bournei) is an evergreen broad-leaved tree belonging to the genus Phoebe of the Lauraceae family, which is a precious resource for timber supply, so it is an excellent tree species for afforestation and landscaping in southern China at an altitude of 200–1000 m [11]. Its timber is favorable for industrial and furniture material with high economic value [12]. As an end-of-succession species, P. bournei grows naturally in moist and extremely shaded forests, and possesses excellent ability to acclimate and cope with long-term shade. However, the mechanism of the response of P. bournei to FR enrichment is currently unrevealed. Recent compelling works explored the adaptive response of P. bournei to the threaten stress of cold, heat, drought, and salt [13–15]. A previous study reported that simulating shade signals by reducing light intensity to 50.5% and 21.8% of full light significantly increased plant height and crown diameter, as well as elevated light-saturated leaf photosynthetic rate, CO2 diffusion, and photorespiratory CO2 release rate in P. bournei [16]. Our group discovered that exposure to 35% and 10% of full light notably altered the transcriptome of P. bournei, affecting genes related to photosynthesis, nitrogen metabolism, plant hormone signal transduction, among others [17]. However, these studies used shading net or black polyethylene film to create shade environment, which failed to reflect the change of light quality in the natural environment, such as F: FR.

To better understand and simulate the characteristics of natural shading signals, this study conducted a large-scale field investigation into the heterogeneity of the light environment under P. bournei forests in their natural state. Actual R: FR values under various shading conditions were obtained, providing shading parameters for woody plants and a reliable background for experimental shading setups. By analyzing the morphological, physiological, biochemical, and transcriptomic characteristics of P. bournei seedlings under different light environments, along with their interrelationships, the study explored the physiological and molecular mechanisms of P. bournei seedlings’ response to shading. This research offers insights into the adaptive mechanisms of woody plants to shading signals and provides a theoretical foundation for the breeding management of P. bournei and the conservation of endangered forest resources.

Materials and methods

Seedlings

Phoebe bournei seeds were collected in November 2020 from the same mature tree growing in Ludeng Village, Taijiang County, Guizhou Province, China (altitude: 1500 m; latitude: 26.63°N; longitude: 108.34°E). The plant material was authenticated by An Jing from Guizhou Normal University and Professor Wei Xiaoli from Guizhou University. Voucher specimens have been deposited in the Laboratory of the College of Forestry at Guizhou University under voucher number XF20140728-087.

Prior to sowing, the seeds were subjected to cold stratification for 30 days. Subsequently, they were sown in a mixed growth medium and germinated under controlled conditions in a warm incubator, including a temperature of 25 ± 2 °C, relative humidity of approximately 30–50%, and light exposure provided by LED lamps (Elite series LED-30).In June 2021, 600 seedlings with uniform growth (mean height 8.5 cm, base diameter 2.73 mm) were selected and transplanted into 300 pots (diameter: 15.5 cm, height: 14.0 cm) containing a mixed medium of yellow soil and perlite (4:1), with two seedlings per pot. The nutrient contents of the substrate were as followings: total nitrogen (8.40 g/kg), alkaline nitrogen (0.28 g/kg), total phosphorus (2.41 g/kg), available phosphorus (0.24 g/kg), total potassium (4.73 g/kg), and available potassium (1.83 g/kg). Seedlings were allowed to acclimate for one month before light treatment.

Experimental design

In this experiment, we designed the different R: FR ratios at a fixed photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) according to natural light conditions in nature forest of P. bournei, including R: FR = 1.5 (L1),R: FR = 0.8 (L2) and R: FR = 0.2 (L3) and the white LED light (R: FR = 9, CK) as a control. Total PAR (60 µmol·m− 2·s− 1) was controlled by light quality incubator (FH-740, Hipoint, Taiwan, China) and light environments at different R: FR ratios were created by inputting a certain percentage of FR light (730 nm) to the basal white LED light (R: FR = 9). A total of 200 seedlings were randomly selected and divided into four groups (n = 50 per treatment), which were separately place in four plant light quality incubators with the same PAR and different R: FR ratio between July 28 and September 28, 2020. The other environmental parameters for the four treatments were the same, as followings: light/dark cycle of 14 h/10 h, day temperature at 28 ± 0.5℃, night temperature at 20 ± 0.5℃ and relative humidity of 75 ± 1%, CO2 concentration at 400 µmol/mol, and watering every 3 days.

Experimental method

Morphological traits measurement

The height, root collar diameter, and internode length of the seedlings in each group (n = 50) were measured using digital caliper and steel measure tape before treatment and every 15 days during treatment. At the end of the light treatment, 5 seedlings were randomly selected from each treatment group and 3 mature leaves from the top layer that sprouted and matured during the light treatment in each seedling were harvested. The surface areas of leaves were measured using EPSON V700 Photo system (Win SEEDLE) and petioles length were measured using rulers in minimum unit of 0.1 cm (n = 15).For analyzing the vertical structure of leaves in response to four treatments, 5 mm× 5 mm leaves were cut along the main vein at 1/3 of the base of the blades and immediately fixed with FAA(Formaldehyde-acetic acid-ethanol Fixative). Paraffin embedded samples were cut into sections at 9 μm and stained with Safranine O-Fast Green. Random view of fields were captured under 10× and 20× microscope. Thickness of overall leaf, palisade tissue, sponge tissue, upper and lower epidermis were measured by Digimizer4.2.6 software. 15 fields were selected for each treatment and their average values were calculated.

Determination of physiological and biochemical parameters

Appropriate amount of fresh mature leaves were collected from five independent seedlings in each group and timely enclosed in sealed bags, then were stored at −80℃for further analysis of chlorophyll (Chl) content, enzymatic activities and phytohormone. Each experiment was repeated for three times.

Determination of Chl content

The frozen clean leaves (0.2 g per sample) were homogenized and Chl was extracted using in 25 mL of 80% acetone as solvent for 24 h. Then 3 mL of the leach liquor were analyzed in UV-2100 spectrophotometer (UNICO, Princeton, NJ, USA) and the absorbance at 649 nm and 665 nm were recorded. Each sample was subjected to four biological replicates. The concentration of Chla and Chlb were calculated according to the formula: Chla (mg·mL− 1) = 13.65 A665−6.88 A649; Chlb (mg·mL− 1) = 24.96 A649−7.32 A665. Consequently, pigment contents in 1 g of leaves were calculated by (C×V)/(W×1000), where C (mg⋅mL− 1) represents the pigment concentration; V (mL) is the volume of the extract; and W (g) is the weight of fresh leaves. A represents spectrophotometric absorbance.

Determination of RCA (Rubisco Activase) and Rubisco (Ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase)

The frozen leaves in each group were homogenized in cold PBS (Phosphate Buffered Saline) and centrifuged for 20 min. The supernatant samples were used to detect the activities of rubisco and RCA using commercially available ELISA kits obtained from Shanghai Keshun Biological Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). These tests were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The absorbance of reaction products at wavelength of 340 nm (Rubisco) and 450 nm (RCA) were measured by microplate reader (Rayto, RT-6100), respectively. Their enzymatic activities were calculated according to the standard curves using corresponding detected absorbance values.

Net photosynthesis rate (Pn)-PAR curve

The Pn-PAR response curve was measured using Li-6400XT portable photosynthesometer (Li-COR, USA) with red and blue light, and the light intensity gradient were set at 2000, 1800, 1500, 1200, 1000, 800, 400, 200, 150, 100, 50, 20 and 0 µmol·m− 2·s− 1 under CO2 concentration of 360 µmol·mol− 1. The measurement was conducted from 9:00 to 11:30 at room temperature of 25℃. One leaf per plant was measured, and three replicates were measured per treatment. The relationship between Pn and PAR was fitted with the non-right-angled hyperbola model, and parameters of light saturation point (LSP), light compensation point (LCP) and dark respiration rate (Rd) were calculated accordingly.

Evaluation of phytohormones

The leaf samples collected earlier were immediately frozen in nitrogen and then homogenized into powder, which were transferred into a 2-mL tube of appropriate amount for further enrichment of hormones. Afterwards, 0.5 mL of reagent containing isopropyl alcohol: water: HCl at 2:1:1 was added to the tube for 30 min, followed by the addition of 1 mL dichloromethane for 30 min of stirring. The hormones-resolving organic phase layer were withdrawn after super centrifugation, followed by further purification using 200 µL of a 50% methanol-water solution. The resultant supernatant was purified using C18 column and obtained. Then, the hormone content was measured on Agilent SB-C18 (1.8 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm) combined with ESI-triple quadrupole-linear ion trap (QTRAP)-MS platform. Multi Quant Software was used for data analysis.

A total of 11 hormone species were identified, including Gibberellin A3 (GA3), Abscisic acid (ABA), Salicylic acid (SA), Methyl salicylate (MeSA), Jasmonic acid (JA), trans-Zeatin-riboside (tZR), 1-Aminocyclopropanecarboxylic acid (ACC), N6-isopentenyladenine (N6-IP), zeatin, 3-Indolepropionic acid (IPA) and Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA).

RNA extraction and sequencing

After 60 days of light treatments, four independent seedlings were randomly selected in each group under different R: FR. About 3–6 fresh mature leaves were collected from each seedling and rinsed in distilled water for three times. Then these samples were immediately frozen and stored in liquid nitrogen for RNA extraction and genetic sequencing analysis. Total RNA was extracted by a RNAprep Pure Plant kit (DP441, Tiangen, China) and the RNA quality was analyzed by a NanoPhotometer spectrophotometer (IMPLEN, CA, USA). The mRNA fraction was enriched through magnetic beads with oligo (dT) and the corresponding reverse transcription of cDNA libraries for sequencing were constructed by NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions [18]. The RNA-seq was performed at Novogene with Illumina Novaseq6000 system (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) [19].

Differentially expressed gene identification and enrichment analysis using GO and KEGG

The raw data output from Illumina Novaseq6000 sequencing machine were filtered by removing low-quality reads and adapters using Trim Galored v0.6.517. Low-quality reads were defined when the reads carrying bases with Qphred ≤ 20 exceeded 50% of the whole number of bases. The Q20 and Q30 values were calculated by FastQC to assess base quality. Then the clean reads were mapped to the reference genome after obtaining the reference sequence and annotation information from designated website and creating index using HISAT v2.1.0 tool. Gene expression was standardized using the FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads) method. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between two groups were analyzed using edgeR package in R by determining the fold change (FC) of gene abundance comparing control group and the significance of FC (P value) by the Student’s t-test. Then the P value was adjusted using the Benjamini & Hochberg method to correct the false discovery rate. |log2FC|≥1 and adjusted P value < 0.05 were identified as threshold for significant DEGs. In this study, three pairs of DEGs of seedlings at R: FR ratios of 1.5, 0.8 and 0.2 were compared with the seedlings treated with white light at R: FR ratio of 9. Venn diagram was drawn to visualize the overlapped or specific DEGs across the three DEGs sets using the VennDiagram and UpSetR package of R. Then the genes in seven clusters were subjected to functional enrichment analysis using clusterProfiler package in R software. Gene Ontogeny (GO) analysis was performed using agriGO v2 [20] with a minimum number of 5 mapping entries for each GO category. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis was calculated using the DAVID Bioinformatics Resources 6.8 [21] from the reference genome annotation [22] with a count threshold of 2 and P-value < 0.05. Enrichment figures were illustrated using the R package ggplot2.

Weighted gene co-expression network construction and hub genes enrichment

To screen important gene clusters contributing to the phenotype characters of P. bournei seedlings under different shading conditions, a weighted gene correlation network analysis (WGCNA) associating gene-trait parameters was constructed using 16 samples by WGCNA v1.68 R package [23]. After removing genes with low expression levels (FPKM < 1) and normalizing the expression matrix, the Pearson method was used to calculate the correlation between any two genes, thereby constructing a correlation matrix. Next, a weighted adjacency matrix was created using a soft threshold power (β) of 5, which was converted into a topological overlap matrix (TOM). Then the co-expressed genes were hierarchically clustered into different modules presented in various colors by TOM similarity using dynamic tree cut algorithm with a minimal gene module size of 100 and merge cut height of 0.25. The WGCNA software was used to visualize the co-expression network and node information of the target module, and the GO and KEGG databases were employed to perform enrichment analysis on the genes in the target module. The genes in the module of interest were subjected to enrichment analysis using GO and KEGG database as described.

Validation of DEGs by quantitative real-time PCR

The mRNA level of key genes involving shading stress under various R: FR radiation conditions was further validated using qRT-PCR. The extraction of RNA was described as above in the RNA-sequencing. An equal amount of purified RNA (1 µg) was reversely transcribed into cDNA using a PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix kit (Takara, Shanghai, China), and the resultant products were amplified using a ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix kit (Vazyme) on C1000 Touch™Thermal Cycler system (Bio-Rad). The primers used in the qRT-PCR were provided in supplementary Table 1 with UBC2 (Ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme E2 2) used as an internal control. Each experiment were repeated in four biological replicates. The expression of target genes was calculated according to the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analysis

The continuous variables with normal distribution were presented as means ± SD (standard deviation) and their difference among the four treatments were compared using one-way ANOVA followed by pairwise comparisons using Bonferroni test. A two-way ANOVA analysis was used to compare the difference of the repeated measurements of height and root collar diameter of seedlings among the four groups at five consecutive time points and show the independent and interactive effects of the two factors, “treatment with different R: FR ratio” and “time”. All data were processed using SPSS and R software. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Phenotypic traits of P. bournei seedlings in response to gradient R: FR radiation

The P. bournei seedlings were exposed to four different light conditions (Fig. 1A) and their morphological traits was monitored during 60 days. There was significant difference in seedling heights between four treatments (P < 0.001, Fig. 1B). The final average height of seedlings in the R: FR = 0.8 and R: FR = 0.2 groups was significantly higher than the control and R: FR = 1.5 groups (both P < 0.001). With the increase of FR input, the seedlings exhibited more stem elongation (Fig. 1C). All seedlings had a slower growth in height within the first 30 days of treatment and obviously accelerated from 30 days to 60 days. Seedlings in the R: FR = 0.8 and R: FR = 0.2 groups had a greater height increment than other conditions (Fig. 1D and E). Besides, a statistical difference in the growing tendency of root collar diameter was also shown across the four groups within 60 days of treatment (Ftreatment=68.532, P < 0.001). They showed significantly lower root collar diameters when exposed to R: FR = 0.8 and R: FR = 0.2 compared to R: FR = 9 and R: FR = 1.5 (P < 0.001), and seedlings in R: FR = 0.8 group and R: FR = 0.2 group showed similar trend of diameter increment (Fig. 1F and H). FR supplementation also notably drove the changes of internode of P. bournei seedlings (Fig. 2A and B). Similarly, the petiole length of seedlings under R: FR = 0.8 and R: FR = 0.2 increased by 66.6% and 100%, respectively, respectively, compared with control (R: FR = 9) (Fig. 2C). We then observed the change of overall biomass and its allocation to the organs of leaf, stem and root under different R: FR. As shown in Table 1, the seedlings achieved highest overall biomass at 1.5 of R: FR, and they were comparable in the other three groups. Additionally, the observational data revealed that, compared to the R: FR = 9 and R: FR = 1.5 groups, the P. bournei seedlings in the R: FR = 0.8 and R: FR = 0.2 groups exhibited a noticeable chlorotic phenotype visible to the naked eye. Generally, the leaf biomass increased while the root biomass decreased under lower R: FR radiation. The stem biomass did not exhibit significant changes across all treatments, resulting in a progressively higher ratio of above-ground to below-ground biomass as the R: FR ratio decreased. Furthermore, when the R: FR ratio decreased to a certain threshold, the seedlings collectively exhibited a chlorotic phenotype.

With regard to the traits of leaves, dark green color and thick leaves were observed under R: FR of 9 or 1.5, while they showed yellow-green and thinner appearance in other conditions. The cultivated 30-day seedlings had 3 ~ 4 leaves (one layer) at the beginning of the light treatment, while a new layer with another 3 ~ 4 mature leaves sprouted after 60 days of light treatment (a branching cycle), and control group had less leaf number due to shedding or shoot extraction delay in a few of them and there were comparable number of leaves in the other three lighting conditions (Table 2). Response of the anatomical structure of P. bournei seedling leaves to varied shading signals

The leaves of P. bournei seedlings exhibit a characteristic differentiation structure, encompassing the upper and lower epidermis, palisade tissue, and spongy tissue (Fig. 3). Under artificially simulated shaded conditions, the anatomical structure of these leaves undergoes notable alterations. Specifically, in a light environment with an R: FR ratio of 0.2, the thickness of the palisade tissue decreases by 21.07% and 9.24% compared to R: FR ratios of 9 and 1.5, respectively, while the spongy tissue thickness diminishes by 23.39% and 9.50%, respectively. In the R: FR = 0.8 treatment, the palisade tissue thickness decreases by 20.26% and 8.31% compared to R: FR ratios of 9 and 1.5, respectively, and the spongy tissue thickness decreases by 24.24% and 10.50%, respectively (Table 3), with all differences reaching a significant level (p < 0.05).

The phototropic responses of P. bournei seedlings’ leaflets under varied shade signals

Figure 4 illustrates the response curves of the net photosynthetic rate (Pn) of Phoebe bournei seedlings as a function of photosynthetically active radiation (PAR). Within the PAR range of 0–200 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹, the Pn of all treatment groups exhibits a linear increase with the escalation of PAR. Beyond 200 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹, the rate of Pn augmentation decelerates, eventually plateauing or exhibiting a slight decline upon reaching the light saturation point.

Light response curve of P.bournei seedlings under different shading signals

The influence of varying R: FR ratios on photosynthetic characteristic parameters is pronounced: as the shading intensity deepens (R: FR decreases), the light saturation point (LSP) undergoes a significant increase, whereas the light compensation point (LCP) and dark respiration rate (Rd) experience notable reductions (P < 0.05). Notably, there is no significant disparity in LSP between the R: FR ratios of 0.8 and 0.2; similarly, the LCP does not differ substantially between the R: FR ratios of 9 and 1.5 (p > 0.05) (Table 4).

Genetic basis of dynamic changes in P. bournei seedlings exposed to various ratio of R: FR

The high-throughput RNA-seq analysis of transcriptome in four light treatments (n = 4 for each treatment) generated a total of 223.46 GB clean data, with at least 12.69 Gb for each sample and a percentage of Q30 bases above 92.75%. Partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) clearly reveled four distinct clusters for light conditions at different R: FR with each cluster exhibiting good similarity as reflected by the explained variance of the two principal components (Dim1 and Dim2) (Fig. 5A). Three DEGs clusters were created by comparing with transcriptomes under three FR-enrichment conditions (R: FR ratio at 1.5, 0.8 and 0.2) with control transcriptome, respectively. A total of 1332 DEGs (662 downregulated and 670 upregulated) were identified based on the screening standard. An increasing number of DEGs were identified as the R: FR decreased, with 262, 564, and 989 DEGs detected at R: FR = 1.5, 0.8, and 0.2, respectively. (Fig. 5B). KEGG analysis enriched the DEGs at each R: FR treatment comparing with control, and the results showed that the principally changed gene cluster were APETALA2/ethylene response factor (AP2/ERF), glutathione S-transferases (GST) and calmodulin-like proteins (CML) when R: FR = 1.5, E2.1.1.128, GLRX, MYBP and cellulose synthase (CESA) when R: FR = 0.8, while small auxin up RNAs (SAUR) and homeodomain leucine zipper (HD-ZIP) were significantly affected when R: FR decreased to 0.2 (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Venn diagram showed the pairwise intersection sets of the three DEGs clusters in each treatment comparing control and the center set outlined 61 DEGs (6 upregulated and 55 downregulated) that were commonly shared among various lighting treatments (Fig. 5C). The DEGs in each compartment of Venn diagram were further analyzed using GO and KEGG analysis. FR-enrichment propelled photosynthetic pigment and phytohormones accumulation of P. bournei seedlings

Based on the above GO enrichment analysis of RNA-seq results in response to FR-enrichment, we then evaluated the physiological traits on photosynthesis and phytohormones. Rubisco and RCA are key enzymes determining the rate of carbon assimilation in photosynthesis. Rubisco and RCA presented relatively lower activities under R: FR = 9 and their activities gradually increased when gradient FR light was supplemented. Consequently, when R: FR reduced to 0.8 and even lower, the activity of Rubisco maintained at higher level and no further changes were monitored while the activity of RCA remained increasing (Fig. 6A). The contents of overall Chlorophyll gradually elevated with the decrease of R: FR, the fraction of Chla reduced and Chlb increased in shading conditions, compared with that of shading-free conditions, in turn yielding a lower ratio of Chla/Chlb in response to treatment, especially at 0.2 rate of R: FR (Fig. 6B).The Pn -PAR curve revealed that Pn increased with PAR when PAR was less than 200 µmol·m− 2·s− 1 under each treatment, which slowed down afterwards and remained flat or even decreased when PAR reached the LSP. At a fixed PAR, seedlings grown under low R: FR exhibited higher Pn (Supplementary Fig. 3). LSP significantly increased, and LCP or Rd significantly decreased when the R: FR decreased to 0.8 and 0.2 compared with the control (P < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 2).

Utilizing HPLC-MS/MS technology, a total of 11 types of plant hormones were identified to accumulate differentially under varying light conditions. GA3, ABA, MeSA, and ACC were accumulated in high abundance in leaves of P. bournei seedlings (Fig. 6C), and lower abundance (< 0.005 ng/mg) of CK (tZR, N6-IP and zeatin) were detected (Fig. 6C). Most of the detected hormones significantly increased in the presence of shading signals, which was particularly true for seedlings exposed to 0.2 rate of R: FR. There was no statistical difference in the measured hormones except SA between groups of R: FR = 9 and R: FR = 1.5, indicating that in shading-free low percentage of far red light failed to obviously affected the hormone synthesis. GA3, ABA, MeSA and JA profoundly elevated in 0.2 of R: FR group comparing with them in 0.8 of R: FR group. Therefore, in paralleled with the morphological plasticity, the FR supplement also modulated the physiological traits of P. bournei seedlings by hormones generation.

Co-expressed gene network analysis of the DEGs associating with hormones and Chl contents

A mutual correlation coefficient between two random genes from transcriptome libraries in four treatments were used to construct a gene co-expression network to link these gene modules to the physiological traits of the seedlings. An overall of 11 modules labeled with different colors were clustered and a heat map showing module-trait correlations was shown in Fig. 7A. By the module-trait correlation analysis, four modules (MEblue, MEpurple, MEbrown and MEred) were filtered based on the correlation coefficients (r) and P values as marked in each well of the panel. The MEblue module enriched 3984 genes and most of them were positively associated with all the measured physiological features, with the highest correlations to Chlb, Rubisco activity and the content of ABA, JA and Zeatin (r ≥ 0.8, P < 0.001). The MEpurple module deposited 105 genes and exhibited similar module-trait correlation tendency to that of the MEblue module in spite lower r values less than 0.7 were reflected. The MEbrown module contained 1440 genes and had significant adverse association with all the 11 kinds of hormones (r>−0.8 and P < 0.05) as well as Chlb, Rubisco and RCA, with the highest correlation with IAA (r=−0.95, P < 0.001), GA3 (r=−0.94, P < 0.001) and ABA (r=−0.94, P < 0.001). Moreover, the MEred module were negatively associated with 11 kinds of hormones (P < 0.05) with the r values ranging from − 0.58 to −0.74. To screen the candidate genes in each module that contributing to the shading environmental stress, a Venn diagram was drawn to find the overlapped genes between each module and the 1332 identified DEGs as previously screened. Consequently, 594 DEGs in MEblue module, 3 DEGs in MEpurple module, 334 DEGs in MEbrown and 119 DEGs in MEred module were filtered, respectively (Fig. 7B). Next, the DEGs from each module (blue, purple, brown and red) were used to construct correlation network, and the nodes with high correlation indicated important transcription factors (TF, V-shaped) and pathways (colored) involving shading stress (Fig. 7C and supplementary Fig. 4). KEGG enrichment analysis were further applied to illustrate these screened important DEGs within each module. As shown in Fig. 7D, in the overlapped 594 DEGs in blue module, 208 genes were annotated to KEGG database and 7 significant gene families were clustered to be associated with photosynthesis and phytohormones, including MYB, beta-Ketoacyl-CoA synthase (KSC), SAUR, CESA, laccase (E1.10.3.2), peroxidase (E1.11.1.7) and ferric-chelate reductase (NADH) (E1.16.1.7). In the brown module vs. DEG, 92 genes were annotated and 7 key gene clusters were determined, including MADS-box, SWEET, E2.1.1.128 (norcoclaurine 6-O-methyltransferase, 6OMT), PIP, GLRX, Cytochrome P450 enzymes (CYP82C4, and CYP71A) in plant development. Besides, 4 critical gene families comprising of EREBP, CML, CALM and HSP20 were identified in the overlapped red module. No key DEGs were screened in the purple module.

The DEGs screened by WGCNA analysis were then functionally clustered using GO analysis. In the blue module, these DEGs were significantly enriched in processes of phenylpropanoid metabolism, cell wall synthesis and assemble, cellular carbohydrate metabolism and response to hormones. In brown module, DEGs were predominately annotated to process of monooxygenase activity regulation. In the Ca2+ signaling cascades, 13 CALM/CML genes were significantly affected by R: FR ratio, as reflected by the fact that approximately half of the related (7/13) genes were upregulated in shading stress, especially for R: FR = 0.2 group, which eventually played roles in stomatal closure and cell wall reinforcement.The DEGs were predominately attributed to six hormones signaling cascades, including ABA, JA, SA, IAA, zeatin and GA. Discussion

Shade signal is an important abiotic stress caused by light fluctuations and surrounding plants, especially for those plants grown in forest ecological system. In this perspective, we investigated the photomorphogenesis, physiology and molecular signatures of P. bournei seedlings when exposing to four light conditions with different R: FR ratio.

Shading within canopies or neighbors represents a major threat to the growth of seedlings in natural environments. For SAR in plants, stem elongation, reduced branching, hyponastic leaf orientation, early flowering and accelerated senescence are well-established features [10]. Of all the response traits to shade, increased stem elongation epitomizes all the morphological changes under shade stress, which facilitates to raise leaves to the upper strata or gap of the canopy to greatly capture and utilize the sunlight [24]. It was shown that shade signal elicited change in the hypocotyl length of SAS model plant Arabidopsis thaliana within 45 min, and more rapid change in gene expression in 15 minutes [25]. In our study, stem-like structures (internode, petiole, etc.) elongation was slow within 30 days of shade exposure, while they obviously accelerated afterwards (Fig. 1C-E). P. bournei seedlings shared similar trait of stem elongation as the SAS plants under lower R: FR. The seedlings also higher leaf biomass allocation (Table 1), reduced Chl a/b ratio(Fig. 6B), increased Rubisco/RCA activities (Fig. 6A) under lower R: FR, and these phenotypes are expected to be homogeneous to some shade-tolerant species [26–28]. Ntawuhiganayo et al. reported that shade-tolerant species had higher fractional biomass in leaves and branches than the shade-intolerant species, and these differences were greater under low radiation [29]. In our study, content of Chla decreased while Chlb increased with a resultant decrease of Chla/b ratio to below 1.5 in higher FR conditions (Fig. 6B), which was in line with the previously reported trait of plants acclimating the shading condition by reducing Chl a/b ratio below a cut-off value of 3 [30, 31].Interestingly, the P. bournei seedlings showed both SAS and shade-tolerant features under low R: FR and PAR, and both these features ensured more light capture and energy fixation from the environment (Fig. 6A). Nowadays, emerging evidence demonstrate that FR photons (701–750 nm) in sunlight could be used efficiently for photosynthesis, which is especially important for leaves in vegetation shade [32]. FR photons accounted for 10–14% of leaf gross photosynthesis in a tree and an understory species in deep shade [33]. The use of FR photons contributes to the acclimation of plants to low light environment. The understory woody plants especially at seedlings stage may utilize specific strategies distinct from the herb and crop plants in coping with low R: FR, and more stress defense traits should be explored or validated in this species or other homogeneous members in further studies.

In this study, we focused the change of leaf transcriptome due to higher biomass allocation in this organ under low R: FR. With the decrease of R: FR ratio, more DEGs contrasting to control emerged (Fig. 5B). By a further clustering analysis using KEGG and GO, we found that genes related to response of hormones and cell wall synthesis were prominently affected by FR-enrichment. Multiple hormones were elevated in shading conditions, especially for auxin, GA3, JA, ABA and CK (Zeatin) (Fig. 6C-D).Auxin occupies a central role in regulating rapid cell expansion, division, and differentiation and promote photosynthetic carbon assimilation by enhancing photophosphorylation [34]. In the WGCNA analysis of trait-gene correlation, multiple DEGs were filtered in the auxin signaling pathway, such as AUX1/IAA, ARF and SAUR. The downstream auxin response factor ARF and effector genes SAUR were deposited at higher level under R: FR ratio of 0.8 and 0.2. The auxin-mediated cell expansion under shade, neighbor proximity, and high temperature relays on the reinforced expression of SAUR family [35]. Of the overall 24 members in SAUR family, 10 members were annotated and eight of them accumulated in response to shade stress (Fig. 7D), indicating their significant roles in executing the leaf-enlarging function [36, 37]. Besides the classical signaling cascades, auxin also potentially regulated Ca2+ signaling38. Related studies revealed that auxin analog-induced Ca2+ signaling and Ca2+-binding protein can rapidly transduce auxin response during root growth [38, 39]. Furthermore, ABA, JA, GA and CK were also filtered as key effectors attending the shading response as identified by WGNGA (Fig. 7A). The increase of CK is able to promote the growth and photosynthesis of leaves and the promotion of photosynthesis by GA was reflected in the increase of Rubisco content and carboxylation activity. In consistent with the previous reports that low R: FR ratio evoked increased auxin, GA contents in leaves during shade avoidance, our study also found the elevation of these hormones (Figur 6C-D) [40, 41]. ABA plays multidimensional roles in abiotic and biotic stresses by interacting with other hormones such as JA, ethylene, and SA.Previous studies have demonstrated that ABA treatment suppresses shade avoidance responses, prioritizing stress coping mechanisms over light competition [42]. Interestingly, this study found that under shaded conditions, the expression levels of ABA receptor PYR/PYL and PP2C genes in P. bournei seedlings were downregulated (Fig. 8), whereas ABA accumulation levels were upregulated compared to those under white light (Fig. 6C). We posit that this may represent a complex regulatory strategy evolved in P. bournei under low-light stress. On one hand, although the shaded environment is not a typical drought or salinity stress, the energy limitation and impaired carbon assimilation it induces may trigger conserved plant stress response mechanisms, leading to increased ABA synthesis to inhibit growth, conserve energy, and enhance stress resistance. On the other hand, the downregulation of PP2C genes may not solely depend on ABA concentration but could be influenced by light signals (such as those mediated by phytochromes), circadian rhythms, or cross-regulation by other hormones (e.g., GA、IAA).JA and SA-mediated signaling pathways are predominately associated with resistance to external damages and pathogen infection, and these two hormones are decreased in shade avoidance, in turn leading to compromised stress defense [41]. Whereas, JA and SA and their associated downstream MYC2 gene were increased under low R: FR in our study (Fig. 8), which might suggest a well growth-defense trade-off in this woody plant in the seeding stage. MYC2 is a central gene connecting JA signaling pathways with auxin, ABA, GA and SA37. Meanwhile, MYC2 also mediates the crosstalk between JA signaling and light perception, phytochrome signaling, and the circadian clock [43].

Our study also outlined the critical role of cell wall synthesis in FR-enrichment condition (Fig. 10). Cell walls are primarily assembled by three types of polysaccharides-cellulose, hemicellulose and pectin or lignin under the guide of numerous enzymes dedicating to throughout cellular growth. The synthesis of cell walls is responsible for large scale coordinated cell division, anisotropic expansion, and vertical growth of a plant, which serves as an important resource for energy and provide structural support for plants. In our study, a critical family of CESA enzyme for converting UDP-glucose to cellulose are frequently upregulated (88.9%) in low R: FR light (Fig. 10). Moreover, a wide spectrum of genes targeting the final synthesis of lignin from phenylalanine were promoted, especially for phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), 4CL and peroxidase (Fig. 10). The formation of lignin reinforces cell structural integrity, mechanical tension, and resistance to pathogens to stabilize the secondary cell wall. It was concluded that light stimulated the generate of lignin while shade suppressed its synthesis in most angiosperms [44]. However, a promoting impact of shade on lignin content was shown in Scots pine, which was considered an adaptive mechanism to this stress environment [44]. Furthermore, higher expression of xyloglucan endo-transglycosylases (XET; EC 2.4.1.207) in low R: FR endows higher flexibility during cell wall remodeling and expansion in leaves of P. bourein [45]. These cell wall related genes concentrating on cellulose and lignin were co-expressed with several hormones. Previous studies revealed the co-expression between cellulose biosynthesis and auxin signaling in Arabidopsis thaliana [46]. Plants tissues undergoing cell expansion expressed higher auxin responsive genes of SAUR [35]. Recent studies elucidate that ABA participates in cell wall acidification in tomato, and modulate secondary cell wall formation and lignin deposition in Arabidopsis [47, 48]. More and more evidence on the association between other hormones such as JA, SA and CK and cell wall synthesis emerge, whereas, their interaction network in shading stress should be explored in further studies.

Based on the current progress of research and the findings of this study, we propose a response mechanism model for the seedlings of Phoebe bournei to shade stress characterized by a reduced red light/far-red light ratio (R: FR)(Fig. 11). In this model, Phoebe bournei perceives alterations in the light environment through the photoreceptor PHYB, subsequently modulating the expression of transcription factors such as AP2/ERF, MYB, HD-ZIP, and stress-responsive genes like HSF/HSP20 to counteract the shade-induced stress. These genes, either directly or indirectly, regulate the auxin-centric plant hormone network, while simultaneously promoting the activation of calcium ion (Ca²⁺) signaling pathways and inducing the expression of genes associated with cell wall relaxation. The aforementioned processes ultimately drive the synthesis of cellulose/lignin and the remodeling of xyloglucan, resulting in morphological traits such as pronounced elongation of the hypocotyl and internodes, expansion of leaf area, and optimized allocation of leaf biomass in the seedlings.Furthermore, the upregulated expression of photosynthesis-related genes (Rubisco, RCA) in conjunction with an increase in chlorophyll content collectively enhances the light utilization efficiency of the seedlings. Nevertheless, the detailed validation of this model necessitates further research for substantiation.

Conclusions

This study explored the response mechanism of Phoebe bournei seedlings to shading stress characterized by a reduced red light/far-red light ratio (R: FR). The results showed that low R: FR conditions promoted morphological adaptive changes in Phoebe bournei seedlings, specifically manifested as increased plant height, internode elongation, petiole extension, and leaf area expansion.Physiological analysis further revealed that the shaded environment enhanced the activity of key photosynthetic enzymes such as Rubisco and RCA, increased total chlorophyll content, and regulated the levels of various phytohormones. In addition, through weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), this study identified 7 candidate genes (MYB, KSC, SUAR, CESA, POD, CESA, SAUR) involved in the shade avoidance response of seedlings. These changes at the physiological and molecular levels may be important factors driving the aforementioned morphological and photosynthetic adaptive adjustments.This study not only deepens the understanding of the shade adaptation mechanism of endangered and characteristic tree species but also provides references for the breeding of shade-tolerant varieties and the formulation of conservation strategies. However, the study still has certain limitations: first, it has not clarified the direct causal relationship between genes and phenotypes through functional verification experiments; second, the simulated experiment cannot fully replicate the complexity of natural habitats, which may limit the practical reference value of the results.Future studies will further conduct functional verification of the identified core genes and collect actual samples from forest stands as much as possible to carry out experiments.
