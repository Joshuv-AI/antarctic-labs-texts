Multiunit and oscillatory activity in macaque V1 is modulated by blinking in a context-dependent way
Authors: Surpiya Murali, Beshoy Agayby, Michael C Schmid, Barbara F Händel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEye blinks modulate neural activity in visual areas even if the visual input is unchanged. Is the influence of blinking defined by the motor output of the blink? We analyzed blink-related neural activity with laminar resolution in V1 of two macaque monkeys in two conditions, viewing a video and at rest. During free viewing a video, blinks induced a modulation of the local field potential (LFP) in the theta, beta, and gamma band with a granular/infragranular focus. The multiunit activity (MUA) decreased around blink execution. Surprisingly, when comparing the results to blinks executed during the rest condition, we found that MUA increased around blinks. The blink-related LFP power changes, while increasing after a blink in both conditions, were significantly different in amplitude and latency. Our findings show that the blink induced modulation of V1 activity is not determined by the motor execution but depends on the condition in which the movement is executed. This suggests that interactions between movement and neural processes in sensory areas are context-dependent. These interactions may play an important role in predictive coding within the framework of active sensing.Keywords: active sensing, brain oscillation, early visual area, free viewing, MUA, predictive coding spontaneous blinks

Introduction

Blinking is an integral part of ongoing natural motor behavior. The most common type is spontaneous blinking, which consists of non-consciously executed blinks with an average occurrence between 10 and 15 times a minute (Zametkin et al. 1979; Doughty 2001; Burr 2005; Kaminer et al. 2011). The neural correlates of blinks have been previously investigated. Studies on voluntary blinks with functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) in humans described increased Blood Oxygen Level Dependent activity in oculomotor structures, the cerebellum as well as the occipital cortex (Gawne and Martin 2000, Gawne and Martin 2002; Bristow et al. 2005a; Bristow et al. 2005b). Importantly, by circumventing the visual effect of a blink by stimulating the retina via a light source in the mouth, Bristow and colleagues could show that the blink-induced increase in the early visual pathway (V1/2/3, Lateral Geniculate Nucleus - LGN) persisted despite the fact that in their specific setup a blink had no visual consequences as the retinal stimulation through the roof of the mouth was not affected by the closure of the eye. Animal studies confirmed the effect of blinks on early visual areas by investigating reflex blinks. Microelectrode recordings in the monkey visual cortex showed that the firing rate was reduced in V1/2/3/4 following a blink onset (Gawne and Martin 2000, Gawne and Martin 2002). Similarly, a reflex blink-induced reduction in firing rate was described for the cat area 17 (Buisseret and Maffei 1983). In humans, such an effect of blinking (voluntary and spontaneous) has been confirmed in electrocorticography (ECoG) recordings (Golan et al. 2016). Further, a set of electroencephalography (EEG) and magnetoencephalography (MEG) studies have established frequency-specific power changes due to spontaneous blinks (Bonfiglio et al. 2009; Bonfiglio et al. 2011; Bonfiglio et al. 2013; Liu et al. 2017; Liu et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2020). One pronounced effect was an increase in delta power (≤4 Hz) shortly after a blink in the precuneus as well as a slightly later reduction in alpha power (8–12 Hz) over the parietal and frontal cortex. Overall, previous studies show that blinks have a representation in the visual cortex prevalent in lower frequencies as well as spiking activity and this neural modulation can be independent from the visual change that is usually introduced by a blink due to the closure of the eyelid.

While it is shown that a blink can influence neural activity in V1 even if the blink does not introduce a change in the visual input, the role of the motor planning, sensory prediction, and motor execution is not so well investigated. Our aim was to understand if the influence of blinking on V1 is related to the motor aspect of blinking or if the influence differs dependent on the experimental condition the blink is executed in. Indeed, there is evidence that spontaneous blinks are influenced by cognitive factors such as task difficulty (Oh et al. 2012) or stimulus relevance (Murali and Händel 2021). An important observation we made in a previous study was the role of attention in blink modulation. While unattended visual input led to a blink probability increase shortly after stimulus onset, this increase was enlarged if the visual input was attended. Interestingly, such modulation was also present for non-visual input. While unattended auditory input did not lead to a visible modulation of blink probability, once the auditory input was attended, the modulation was clearly visible (Brych et al. 2021c). Other studies have also found that attention and/or cognitive load has an effect on the strength of the decrease or duration of blink suppression (Fogarty and Stern 1989; Siegle et al. 2008; Oh et al. 2012). Studies on the neural activity of blinks have mostly focused on comparing the visual influence of a blink with an externally introduced visual darkening (Gawne and Martin 2000, Gawne and Martin 2002; Bristow et al. 2005a; Bristow et al. 2005b).

Only recently, a comparison between neural correlates of spontaneous blinks under different sensory stimulation and under different cognitive loads was conducted in humans. Using EEG and MEG, delta power increase following a blink was shown to be influenced by the prevalent sensory input (higher during auditory stimulation [Liu et al. 2020]) and the cognitive load (smaller during mental arithmetic compared to rest [Liu et al. 2019]). The authors of those studies suggested a neurocognitive mechanism for blink processing. As MEG and EEG signals pick up post synaptic potentials summed over a large area and can struggle to identify the underlying neural source of activity, we explicitly analyzed V1 activity with a laminar resolution.

Our aim was to validate that the low frequency representation of blinks can indeed be located in V1 and test if layer specificity can give neural evidence if blink-related changes are based on a top-down process. We further aimed to understand the relationship between the frequency effect and blink-related spiking activity. Additionally, while the context dependency has only been described for frequency specific changes we wanted to see if the modulation due to context persists in multiunit activity (MUA).

To this end, we analyzed the neural activity in V1 laminar recordings in two rhesus monkeys with respect to blinks which were either executed while attentively watching a video or while resting in front of a blank screen. We found that the condition in which the blink was executed indeed influenced the blink-related neural response in V1 in granular/infragranular low and higher frequency power and granular/infragranular MUA. More specifically, while a blink during rest led to an increase in MUA and a pronounced increase in power in various frequency bands in the local field potential (LFP) signal, a blink during visual stimulation led to a decrease in MUA and a reduced and delayed effect on LFP power.

Materials and methods

Subjects

Two female rhesus monkeys (Macaca mulatta) were used to obtain electrophysiological and eye movement data (Monkey 1, 4 years old, 6 kg; Monkey 2, 5 years old, 9 kg). A third monkey had been recorded, however, showing low frequency noise that exceeded the signal found in the other two monkeys by more than a factor of 100, it was excluded from further analysis. The detailed methods for the headpost and chamber implants, surgical and anesthesia procedures, postoperative care, and implant methods were described in a previous manuscript (Ortiz-Rios et al. 2021). All procedures were approved by the UK Home Office, and they comply with the Animal Scientific Procedures Act (1986) on the care and use of animals in research and the European Directive on the protection of animals used in research (2010/63/EU).

Electrophysiological recordings

The activity of all the cortical layers within V1 was recorded with acutely implanted laminar probes. A laminar Plexon S-probe (Plexon Inc., Texas, USA) was used with 24 contacts (100 μm electrode spacing, 15 μm electrode diameter) and a total length of 100 mm and a probe diameter of 300 μm. Guide tubes were used to penetrate the granulation tissue on top of the dura and to stabilize the probe. At the beginning of each session, a moving grating patch was placed in the receptive field of the recorded neurons. Layer 4c was then determined based on the channel showing the earliest multiunit activity envelope (MUA) in response to the grating. Current source density (CSD) was used alongside the MUA, but MUA latency was the main indicator. Please see Supplementary Fig. S11 for more details. The probe was placed so that it covered the 2 mm of the V1 cortical sheet thickness placing layer 4C in the lower third of that thickness (Maier et al. 2011; Vanni et al. 2020).

Raw electrophysiological signals were recorded using a Blackrock recording system (BlackRock Microsystems, Inc.) and sampled at 30kS/s. Data analysis was done in MATLAB using the Fieldtrip toolbox (Oostenveld et al. 2011) and custom scripts. A detailed description of the setup and the localization of V1, as well as layer 4C within V1 can be found in a previous publication (Ortiz-Rios et al. 2023). Multiunit activity envelop (referred to as MUA) was also extracted as described in the previous publication (Ortiz-Rios et al. 2023). A high-pass filter (4th order Butterworth) with a cutoff of 150 Hz was applied to the raw signal (sampled at 30 k S/s). The signal was then rectified and downsampled to 500 Hz.

Naturalistic viewing task

Experiments were carried out in an electromagnetically shielded booth with reduced audiovisual input. Animals were positioned at a distance of 84 cm in front of a ViewPIXX computer screen (RR: 120 Hz, VPIXX technologies), with a field of view +/− 16° horizontally and +/− 9.5° vertically. Experiments were programmed and executed using MWorks with custom scripts that used the MWorks Experiment Language. The head of the animals was stabilized using a headpost implant for the duration of the recordings. Recordings took place in a recording booth that was completely dark with the only source of light coming from the experimental display.

The animals passively viewed 20 video sequences in the experiment with no constraints to their eye movements. Each video sequence lasted 30 s (movie condition) and was followed by a blank gray screen for 30 s (blank screen condition). Figure 1 depicts an example. The video sequences consisted of four videos depicting a variety of activities and four edited versions of each video (such as tile scrambling and phase scrambling). The videos played in full screen and therefore were occupying the visual field covered by the monitor. The video sequences were played sporadically across recording sessions with the animals so that they were not used to them and actively viewed them each time. Previous assessments with fMRI demonstrated that viewing these video sequences resulted in strong activity throughout the visual cortex and fronto-parietal networks (Ortiz-Rios et al. 2021).

Exemplary visual input during the recording. After a 30 s video presentation followed a 30 s blank screen. Videos were presented randomly and the monkeys were allowed to freely move their eyes throughout the experiment. There was no task involved.

Eye movement analyses

Eye position was recorded monocularly with Eyelink 1000 plus with a sampling rate of 1000 Hz; the raw signal was calibrated and converted to visual degrees by Mworks based on the distance between the animal’s head and the monitor and the dimensions of the monitor. The eye-tracker was calibrated before each session using a 12-point calibration routine. During visual calibration, animals were performing saccades to visual stimuli that appeared at different locations (12 points) on the screen. The animals were trained to fixate on these stimuli for 500 ms with adequate precision (<2° visual angle). Upon successful fixation, the animals received a reward in the form of fruit juice or water. The synchronization between electrophysiological recording and eyetracking was ensured via event markers sent to the electrophysiological data acquisition system.

Blinks were detected based on missing eye vector data. First, potential eye blinks were detected if data was missing for a minimum of 50 ms and a maximum of 1000 ms. Then, blinks that were less than 100 ms apart were combined. The onset of the blink was defined by the first time point where the eye data was missing. The blink detection criteria were similar to previous human studies (Brych et al. 2021c). Blink rate during blank condition was on average 6.8 (SD 2.6) and during movie condition 7.8 (SD 2.2). There was no significant difference between the blink rate during the movie and the blank condition (paired t-test: t(19) = 1.31, P = 0.205).

Two sessions have been used from monkey 1 and five sessions have been used from monkey 2. Each session consisted of an average of 12 trials (SD 5.3). A trial refers to the presentation of a movie for 30 s (movie condition) and a subsequent 30 s period of blank screen (blank screen condition). Please note that the number of trials (and accordingly data) was the same for the two conditions within each monkey.

Analysis of MUA around blinks

The envelope multiunit activity (MUA) signals were obtained by applying a high pass filter at 150 Hz to the raw signal, rectifying and then downsampling the signal to 500 Hz (Super and Roelfsema 2005). To analyze the time-resolved modulation of MUA around a blink, we first combined the data from the different sessions for each monkey. As a second step, we low-pass filtered the MUA data at 10 Hz and cut out periods of 0.4 s around blink onset (0.2 s before and 0.2 s after) and averaged (ie taking the mean) over the different channels for each blink separately. Lastly, we conducted a t-test for blank screen vs movie conditions on non-overlapping 0.002 s time windows. A permutation-based approach (Maris and Oostenveld 2007) was applied to correct for multiple testing. Please note that for this comparison, no baseline correction was applied.

For plotting MUA for the three layers separately, channels were averaged (ie taking the mean) as to their position with respect to the granular input layer 4C. The earliest sink in response to visual stimuli (run in another paradigm) was used to identify the granular input layer 4C and accordingly, MUA signals were identified as belonging to the supragranular (L2/3), granular (L4), and infragranular (L5 and L6) layers. Supragranular layer consisted of the average over the most superficial channels up to and including the 5th channel above 4C. Granular layer included channels between the 4th channel above 4C up to and including the channel at 4C. The infragranular average included all deep channels below (not including) the 4C channel. Please note that as the probe had different locations with respect to 4C, the number of channels that were included in the average of the supra- and infragranular layer could differ between sessions.

LFP and CSD

The LFP signals were obtained by downsampling the raw signal to 500 Hz and then applying a notch filter at 50 Hz to remove any line noise. This was done for each channel separately.

The CSDplotter toolbox (Pettersen et al. 2006) was used to calculate the CSD profiles (Schroeder et al. 1998) around blinks for the movie and the blank screen using the cubic spline iCSD method. This method applies an interpolation between channels and does not require the elimination of the top and bottom channels (Pettersen et al. 2006).

Frequency power changes around blinks

The spectral power was assessed by means of Short Time Fourier Transform (STFT) on the LFP signal. The frequency analysis was done in shifting time windows of 0.5 s. First a Hanning window was applied, then the frequency demodulation followed by means of STFT. The time window was shifted in steps of 2 ms in an overall time period of −0.5 s to 0.5 s around blink onset. Frequencies targeted were 2 to 100 Hz in steps of 2 Hz, according to the 0.5 s window length. The time in time-frequency plots refer to the center of the 0.5 s window. In order to find frequency bands relevant for further analysis, we visualized the averaged changes in the different frequencies with respect to a blink (Fig. 2). To this end, power values were baseline corrected (relative baseline change, baseline from −0.5 to −0.3 s), data from the different sessions were combined and all blinks irrespective of whether they occurred during a movie or a blank were averaged (ie taking the mean) over both monkeys. Based on this visualization (not shown, we only plot the time frequency average for the monkeys separately in Fig. 2C), we chose three frequency bands which we refer to as theta (4–8 Hz), beta (16–30 Hz) and gamma (35–80 Hz). We used these bands for a consistent analysis in the monkeys. We then extracted the time resolved power over these different frequency ranges with the approach described above, however, with and without baseline correction. In case of a baseline, the mean over the time between −1.5 and −0.5 s before blink onset was subtracted from the data around the blink for each blink event. This was done for each movie condition, session, channel and monkey, separately. Power values were then averaged over channels. A t-test on the difference between power values around blinks during blank screen and movie condition in each time window was conducted for each monkey and frequency band separately, taking blinks from all sessions into account. A permutation-based correction (Maris and Oostenveld 2007) was applied for which the time-resolved t-tests were repeated 1000 times on permuted data. The value with the maximum absolute t-value was used to find a distribution of t-values from the permuted data. The original t-values were considered significant if they fell above or below the maximum or minimum 5% of the permuted t-values.

In addition, we also tested the difference in the latency of peak power between the blank screen and movie condition. To this end, we determined the time-point of the highest power within one frequency band within a time period of +/− 0.5 s around blink onset. This was done separately for the theta, beta and gamma band ie power was averaged between 4-8 Hz (theta), 16-30 Hz (beta), and 35-80 Hz (gamma) (frequency resolution: 2 Hz). The time resolution was, as described above, determined by the shifting time window. Accordingly, there was a power value each 2 ms. The latency was compared by means of a two-sided t-test for each monkey and each frequency-band, separately. Please note that we had ample reason to not baseline correct the MUA when comparing blink induced changes between conditions. We know that the sensory input into V1 is different between movie and blank condition during the time before the blink (designated baseline). The sensory input during the blink, however, is the same between conditions (ie no visual input reaches the retina as the eyes are closed, comparable during movie and blank condition). Accordingly, the comparison of raw data between conditions seems to be well controlled while considering a pre-blink baseline is likely to introduce a bias. Concerning the analysis of frequency specific LFP power, one can discuss the use of a baseline as one might argue that frequency specific power in V1 is possibly not as strictly modulated by visual input as MUA. Previous work on non-invasive human electrophysiology (EEG and MEG) has applied a baseline to analyze blink induced effects (Bonfiglio et al. 2013; Liu et al. 2017; Liu et al. 2020). MEG/EEG data consists of voltage changes (post synaptic potentials) summed over large areas of the brain. Even after applying source reconstruction, the signal picked up is based on the summation of tens of thousands of (pyramidal) neurons (Murakami and Okada 2006). The baseline applied in human electrophysiology might not be adequate if analyzing invasive data recorded exclusively from V1. Accordingly, we have applied both approaches, ie with and without baseline, to the power analysis of the LFP data.

Current source density

The earliest current sink in response to visual stimuli (assessed in another paradigm which was run for each monkey and session, see (Ortiz-Rios et al. 2023)) was used to identify the granular input layer 4C (dashed horizontal line in Fig. 2B and C). Frequency power changes around blinks

The time-frequency representation around blink onset shows a clear power increase due to blinking. Importantly, the figure shows an average over the movie and the blank condition for both monkeys separately. This visualization helped to identify those frequency bands with the most pronounced modulation in power due to blinks. We found an increase in relative power change in both monkeys around blink onset with a focus in a low frequency band (up to 8 Hz), in a frequency range between 15-30 Hz (beta) and also at higher frequencies (>35 Hz, gamma). When extracting the three frequency bands that showed the strongest blink-related change (ie theta, beta and gamma band as marked in Fig. 2C) and plotting the time frequency representation of the power, now separately during the movie condition and during the blank screen condition, we found a condition specific modulation (Fig. 3 for baseline corrected data, Supplementary Fig. S9 for raw data).

Figures 4 and 5 show that for the theta band, there is an increase around blink onset for both the movie and the blank screen condition for all sessions in both monkeys with a focus of this increase below layer 4C in six out of seven sessions. The main difference between the blank and the movie condition is the timing of this increase. Accordingly, when looking at the difference plot (blank–movie condition) the data shows an increased theta power for blank compared to movie condition (positive peak) around blink onset and an increased power in the movie condition (negative peak) after blink onset. All sessions of both monkeys showed such increase–decrease pattern at and/or below layer 4C, only one session shows a stronger pattern above layer 4 (Figs. S1–S5).

The beta band shows, similar to what was observed in Fig. 3, a variable pattern between monkeys but now in a layer specific fashion; while monkey 1 shows the focus of the beta power modulation to be comparable to the theta power modulation, monkey 2 rather shows a beta power modulation that is comparable to the gamma power modulation.

The gamma band in Figs. 4 and 5 show an ongoing activity for both the movie and the blank screen condition around and below layer 5C. This ongoing activity, which seems largely independent of the blink event, is higher during the movie compared to the blank screen condition and very consistent over all sessions. The pattern is found in six out of seven sessions (Figs. S1–S5). Blink-related gamma power changes on the other hand are more variable across conditions and sessions. However, when looking at the difference between conditions, there is a positive/negative pattern around blink onset for most sessions, similar to the theta band findings. It is interesting to notice that, in contrast to the theta findings, the difference in gamma activity between blank and movie condition is also found above 4C. Indeed, four out of seven cases show a temporally precise pattern of increased gamma power before blink onset and decreased power after blink onset with an additional laminar focus in the supragranular layer.

Discussion

Blinks introduce a change of neural activity in the early visual area V1. We compared this blink-related response between blinks executed during experimental conditions which differed, amongst other aspects, in attentional requirements and visual input and revealed a pronounced difference in various activity measures. The MUA showed a qualitative difference between conditions, ie an increase around blinks during the blank screen, but an equally strong decrease in MUA around blinks during the movie condition. This modulation was prevalent in the granular and infragranular layers. When analyzing frequency-specific effects, we found that the amplitude as well as the latency of the blink-related power modulation in the theta (4–8 Hz), beta (16–30 Hz) and gamma (35–80 Hz) band also showed a significant difference between conditions. Theta power, for example, increased more strongly around a blink during the blank screen condition, while this increase was less pronounced and later in time for blinks during the movie condition. The laminar distribution of blink-related power differences between conditions suggests a focus in the granular and infragranular layers for theta and gamma power and an additional focus in the supragranular layer in the gamma band. A sink source pattern related to the blink along with a latency difference between conditions was found in the CSD, mainly in the granular and infragranular layers. Overall, the data shows that blink-related neural responses in the early visual cortex are not simply introduced by the motor execution of a blink but are dependent on the condition in which the blink is executed. This suggests that the sensory input and the resulting attentional state influence how a blink is represented in cortical areas that process visual inputs. We discuss how this blink representation might be related to the role of blinks in attentional disengagement.

MUA decreases around blinks while watching a movie, but MUA increases during rest

For the movie condition of the experiment at hands, the monkeys watched movie sequences including high contrast motion. This resulted in a rich, continuously varying visual input which effectively drove V1 neural activity. When blinks occurred during such movie watching, MUA decreased with a negative peak at ~100 ms after blink onset. This finding matches well with the results from previous electrophysiology studies in macaques, cats and humans looking at blinks during various visual stimulation (Gawne and Martin 2000; Bristow et al. 2005b; Golan et al. 2016; Golan et al. 2018). For instance, Gawne and Martin (Gawne and Martin 2000) showed a decrease in firing rates in monkey V1 shortly after a blink (detected as maximum deflection in the electrooculogram). Similarly, Golan (Golan et al. 2016) reported an activity minimum at blink offset across visual areas (V1-V4) in human ECoG recordings.

We find that the blink-related change in MUA was significantly different when the blink was executed during the blank screen condition. There are two pronounced differences between the blank screen and the movie condition; one is that visual input was greatly reduced, unchanging and predictable as only a gray screen and the surrounding area was visible. Such setting is classically used as baseline or rest condition and assumed to mark a low attention state and pose little cognitive load. Also during the movie conditions there was no task, however, previous work has demonstrated that viewing these exact video sequences results in strong activity throughout visual cortex and fronto-parietal networks, suggesting that the input was attentively viewed by the animals even without a task (Ortiz-Rios et al. 2021). A difference in attentional state between movie and blank screen condition therefore likely constitutes another possible difference between conditions.

What leads to the differential modulation of blinks on the MUA dependent on the viewing condition? In our data, the MUA already showed a difference between conditions well before blink onset. This might be due to the fact that MUA is overall increased during the movie condition, reflecting the higher amount of processing of visual input. If we take the amount of raw MUA as indicator of the level of sensory processing, we could interpret the pattern such that the processing drops to the blank screen level due to a blink during the movie condition. However, during the blank screen condition, there is no blink introduced increase in visual input. In both conditions, a blink leads to a complete shutdown of visual input, and only the preceding and following visual input is different. The observed differential modulation in MUA, ie an in- vs. decrease dependent on the condition, is therefore hard to reconcile with the equally present decrease in visual input in both conditions. The MUA, averaged over the supragranular, the granular and the infragranular layer shows that the effect of blinks is not only found around layer 4 but also in the infragranular layer. This would again suggest that the change in MUA is not solely introduced by sensory changes in the input from LGN. Another possible explanation is that the blink induced MUA modulation marks a process that is not dependent on the visual input. While a change in visual input usually leads to an increase in V1 MUA, a decrease has been reported following the onset of an attentional cue (Cox et al. 2019). In this study, the appearance of the visual cue indicating the direction of the relevant target position introduced a pronounced but short lasting (~100 ms) MUA decrease in primary visual cortex with a latency of ~100 ms. This could suggest that blinks during active viewing are comparable to a cue to shift spatial attention. During rest, such a forced attentional shift would not be necessary. A possible attentional function of blinks will be discussed further below.

Our CSD plots are somewhat variable but do show a reoccurring pattern with respect to the blink. More specifically, during the blank condition there is a sink related to the blink event with a focus in the granular and infragranular layer, whereas during the movie condition a source is found during that time period in most cases. An interpretation is rather complicated as in some sessions the CSD pattern in response to a blink seems to reflect an increase in activity during the blank condition and an almost simultaneous decrease in the movie condition, comparable to what is seen in the MUA activity. In other sessions however, there seems rather a temporal shift in blink-related activity, comparable to what is seen in the theta band. Overall, the CSD pattern clearly suggests that there is a differential modulation due to blinks in V1 which is layer specific.

Blink-related power changes in the theta, beta, and gamma range

For the theta band, we found a clear increase in power related to the blink. Previous research, analyzing power changes due to blinking, has described such an increase specifically in the delta (0.5 to 3/4 Hz) band (Bonfiglio et al. 2009; Bonfiglio et al. 2011; Bonfiglio et al. 2013; Liu et al. 2017; Liu et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2020). The power increase could be localized to the precuneus and was found during different conditions, such as free viewing or passive fixation with and without a visual stimulus. Since in our study the theta frequency band (4–8 Hz) overlapped with the delta band in Bonfiglio’s studies (0–4 Hz), the results seem at least partly congruent. That the latency of the increase is different between the studies might be caused by the different paradigms and species used.

To further understand what the power modulation in the low frequencies could indicate, we looked at how the power changes were represented in the different layers and how the latency changed with condition. We found that the theta power was most pronounced in the infragranular layer well below layer 4C for both the movie and the blank screen condition. Only in one case (monkey 1, session 1) the modulation peaked in the granular layers. As to the difference between viewing conditions, the theta power increase was significantly earlier and more pronounced for blinks during the blank screen condition. The maximum difference in power was again mostly confined to the infragranular layer (five sessions out of seven) and two sessions showing an additional local increase in the difference in the granular layers (monkey 1, session 1; monkey 2 session 5).

Visual input has shown to induce theta power in rat V1, generated through external input or by recurrent activity within cortical layers (Zold and Shuler 2015). Frequency specific analysis of activity following sensory processing suggests that theta oscillations reflect feedforward sensory processing from V1 to V4 (Bosman et al. 2012; Grothe et al. 2012; Van Kerkoerle et al. 2014; Bastos et al. 2015; Spyropoulos et al. 2018), possibly even more than gamma (Kienitz et al. 2021). However, LGN input is mostly found in layer 4, therefore our layer distribution with a similarly strong focus on infragranular effects does not suggest input from the thalamus. The found distribution of theta power over the layers could suggest that the blink-related change in theta marks an output signal that is either sent downstream to the LGN or the Pulvinar. It could also mark top-down input from higher visual areas (Bastos et al. 2015). Previous work has described lower theta V1 power if attention was directed towards the visual stimulus compared to away from it (Spyropoulos et al. 2018). Indeed, this would fit to our observation, as attention was likely lower during the blank screen condition, which showed the stronger and earlier theta power modulation. However in our case, the theta modulation was linked to the blink event. Following the idea of attention induced effects, the difference in theta power modulation between conditions could indicate that blinks differ between the observers’ state as to the attentional modulation associated with the blink.

Taken together, our data shows evidence that theta power increase is related to a blink event and that the amplitude and latency of this increase is influenced by the state of the observer. The laminar distribution of this modulation in theta power is rather indicative of processes that is independent of bottom-up sensory information.

Gamma activity, as a response to visual input, is often found to be strongest in superficial layers (Buffalo et al. 2011; Xing et al. 2012) suggesting that gamma might serve feedforward signaling to higher cortical areas (Fries 2015). This was supported by granger causality measures in human MEG (Michalareas et al. 2016). Interestingly, we found ongoing gamma power approximately in the granular layer. The granular layer is the main target of inputs coming from LGN (Felleman and Van Essen 1991). Our observed sustained gamma power in both conditions (independent of the blink event) could therefore mark input from LGN representing visual input. Figures 4, 5, and S1–S5 show that the ongoing gamma power is higher for the movie condition which would be in line with such an interpretation as the visual input was more intense compared to the blank screen condition.

Changes in gamma power with respect to blinks has, to our knowledge, not been described so far. Gamma power around blink onset is such modulated that it shows a power increase before and around blink onset for the blank screen condition exceeding the power around blink onset during the movie condition. Sometime after the blink onset, gamma power during the blank screen condition drops below the power found during the movie condition (Fig. 3). When plotting the power difference between blank and movie condition (blank–movie) over the different electrodes (Figs. 4, 5, S1–S5), this should be converted to a red-blue pattern with red before and during blink onset, and blue afterwards. The distribution of blink-related change of gamma power was quite variable over channels; however, such a pattern can be found in six out of seven cases in the infragranular layer and in four out of seven with an additional supragranular focus. Despite its variability, it might be interesting to note that the pattern is not confined to the location where the ongoing gamma activity was found. This would suggest that the gamma difference is not caused by the sensory input sent from LGN. It might, as suggested before (Michalareas et al. 2016), mark signals that are sent upstream from V1 along the visual pathway. For the movie condition, there would be a smaller and delayed blink-related signal that is forwarded from V1, whereas during the blank screen condition there is an earlier and stronger blink-related signal.

When assessing the beta band, we, unfortunately, found that the variance over sessions and monkeys was very high. While for monkey 1, theta and beta seemed to be relatively similar, for monkey 2 it would be beta and gamma that were more alike. Given that also the layer-specific pattern was very variable, we would like to refrain from intending an interpretation.

Overall, the condition specific-latency change as well as the laminar pattern in the observed gamma and theta power modulation due to blinks suggests that the change in power is not based on a change in sensory input.

Function of blinks

Spontaneous blinks are to a certain extent driven by the need to maintain the tear film of the eye. About 3–4 blinks per minute are required for this purpose (Norn 1969). However, the average rate of blink occurrence is between 10 to 15 times a minute (Zametkin et al. 1979; Doughty 2001; Burr 2005; Kaminer et al. 2011), which means that ~70% to 80% of spontaneous blinking is not driven by such physiological needs. Higher cognitive functions in- and outside of vision can up- and down-regulate blinking which suggests a sensory domain general relevance of blinking in cognition (Gregory 1952; Bauer et al. 1985; Oh et al. 2012; Brych and Händel 2020; Murali and Händel 2021). More specifically, behavioral studies have associated the blink with attentional disengagement (Smilek et al. 2010; Nakano et al. 2013; Salvi et al. 2015; Ang and Maus 2020). Work on bistable percept indicates that a period without blinking reflects a phase in which a perceptual reinterpretation is facilitated (Brych et al. 2021c; Murali and Händel 2024) across sensory modalities, likely grounded in a domain-general attentional mechanism. However, a function of spontaneous blinks has not yet been established. Looking at our neural data, a blink resulted in a decrease in MUA and a reduced power in the theta and gamma range when there was attended visual input. Such modulation has also been found in connection with externally induced changes in attention towards sensory input. A decrease in MUA in V1 has been reported following the onset of an attentional cue (Cox et al. 2019) and low-frequency power has been shown to be reduced if a blink was executed during a task vs. rest (Liu et al. 2019).

We would like to reconcile the behavioral and neural evidence and put forward the idea that the decrease in MUA and the reduction and latency change in theta power marks an attentional disengagement from (visual) sensory input. Such disengagement is particularly important while attentively scanning visual input by helping to disengage and initiate an attentional shift. Such a mechanism is not necessary during rest; hence the neural responses are different. This leads to a well-defined and testable prediction of blink modulation in experimental and natural settings.

The context-dependent difference in blink-related activity might be realized via changes in dopamine. It has been shown that the concentration of dopamine can lead to frequency band specific changes in monkey V1 (Zaldivar et al. 2018). The authors showed an increase in gamma power with increased dopamine level specific for a condition where a movie was presented whereas they found a decrease in gamma power during a rest condition. The link to dopamine is specifically intriguing as, albeit details are still discussed, blinks have also been related to the dopamine level (Karson 1983; Bologna et al. 2012).

We want to point out that attention is only one possibility amongst others to explain why blink induced neural changes are different between the movie and the blank condition. An attentional difference is assumed as previous work has demonstrated that viewing these exact video sequences results in strong activity throughout visual cortex and fronto-parietal networks, suggesting that the input was attentively viewed by the animals even without a task (Ortiz-Rios et al. 2021). Concerning the blank condition, there is no proof of reduced attentional load. Indeed, also studies comparing task to rest do not control attention during rest and rely on the assumption that the attentional level is low during the designated rest period. In order to claim that the observed effects of blinks were affected by attention, a monitored parametric modulation of attention should have been included, allowing to correlate the attentional demand with the neurophysiological changes. Considering research on eye movements, however, any sort of test might be problematic. Tasks add confounds as to motor output and will strongly affect blink timing and blink probability (Brych et al. 2021a, b). Rate and timing of blinks have further been shown to be influenced by memory content (Karson et al. 1981; Ortega et al. 2022), another relevant factor for most tasks. A way to measure the attentional demand without the inclusion of a task might be necessary. Pupil dilation has long been discussed as a marker of attentional control (Kahneman and Beatty 1966). Interestingly, pupil size during rest also exhibits power fluctuations in human occipito-parietal areas with an increase in beta power (~16 Hz) starting before pupil dilation onset (Radetz and Siegel 2022), which is comparable to our findings for spontaneous blinks. This similarity might be mediated by the involvement of the locus coeruleus (LC). The activation of brainstem ascending arousal network (AAN) nuclei including the LC is associated with spontaneous blinks during rest (Demiral et al. 2023). Similarly, there is a relationship between activity in the LC and pupil dilation (Megemont et al. 2022). Interestingly this study also shows that the variations in the relationship between pupil diameter and LC activity is strongly correlated with the prevailing brain state as indicated by hit rate and decision bias. Similarly, the relationship between attentional control and pupil dilation has been shown to rely on brain state related factors such as time on the task (Van Den Brink et al. 2016).

Nevertheless, the most prominent difference between movie condition and blank condition is the visual input, however only before and after the blink as during the blink the eye is closed. However, watching a movie might introduce processing steps that affect blink-induced neural activity in a specific way.

Blink related neural responses have interesting potential utilities in enhancing brain function assessment technologies, applicable to both clinical and non-clinical domains. For example, blink-related brainwaves have been shown to detect brain function changes resulting from subconcussive head impacts in sports (Sattari et al. 2023), as well as evaluate cognitive performance in complex real-world environments (Page et al. 2024; Ziccardi et al. 2024). Forwarding the understanding of blink-related neural responses can improve monitoring cognitive health and performance in high-stakes contexts.

Possible role in active sensing

The active sensing framework proposes that organisms actively control their sensory input through movements to optimize perception (for reviews, see [Schroeder et al. 2010; Yang et al. 2018]). Unlike passive sensing, where stimuli are externally imposed without the organism's influence, active sensing involves self-initiated actions like eye movements, head movements, and other voluntary adjustments that influence the sensory input. A systematic modulation of eye movements and gaze has for example been shown in humans during walking (Sprague and Ballard 2003; Turano et al. 2003) and other real-life situations (Hayhoe 2000; Hayhoe and Ballard 2005). Additionally, active sensing mechanisms have been shown to alter sensory processing. For example, cortical somatosensory neurons alter their responsiveness to mechanosensory stimuli as touch transitions from passive reception to active engagement, such as reaching in primates or whisking in rodents (Nelson et al. 1991; Hentschke et al. 2006). Saccades lead to an increase in sensitivity in the target region of the saccade (Armstrong and Moore 2007; Noudoost et al. 2010). Similarly, in humans there is evidence that active touch is processed differently than passive touch (Ackerley et al. 2012). Predictive coding theories suggest that the brain creates predictions about sensory input based on past experiences and current goals and that it is these predictions that guide sensory sampling and processing (Millidge et al. 2021). Accordingly, an interplay between directed motor output and the way sensory input is processed is assumed.

If blinks play a relevant role in sensory processing, they can be part of an active sensing strategy. Recent work by Yang and colleagues (Yang et al. 2024) has shown that blinks indeed have a function in sensory processing by enhancing contrast sensitivity to low spatial frequency input. Also the change in early visual activity related to blinking we report might indicate a modulation in visual input processing. However importantly, as the change in early visual activity was dependent on the context in which the blink was executed this would indicate that not only the movement execution itself is relevant in active sensing but that the situational demand is shaping the influence the movement has on sensory processing. This would fit very well with the concept of predictive coding.

Conclusion

Overall, our findings describe for the first time a difference in blink-related V1 spiking activity due to the viewing condition. This demonstrates that the influence of blinking on V1 activity is not related to the motor execution but modulated by the cognitive and sensory input state of the subject. This context-dependence of neural modulation highlights the complexity of the relationship between motor output, internal factors and early sensory processing.
