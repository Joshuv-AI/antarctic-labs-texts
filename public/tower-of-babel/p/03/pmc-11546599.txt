Evaluation of Serum Proteome Sample Preparation Methods to Support Clinical Proteomics Applications
Authors: Carly
A I Twigg, Jesenia M Perez, Joohyun Ryu, Benjamin K Hanson, Valerie J Barrera Estrada, Stefani N Thomas
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSerum contains several proteins that are associated with disease-related processes. Mass spectrometry (MS)-based proteomics approaches greatly facilitate serum protein biomarker development. However, the serum proteome complexity presents a technical challenge for the accurate, sensitive, and reproducible quantification of proteins by MS. Thus, efficient sample preparation methods are of critical importance for serum proteome analyses. In this study, we evaluated the technical performance of two serum proteome sample preparation methods using sera from patients with high-grade serous ovarian cancer and patients with benign nongynecological conditions with a goal of providing insight into their compatibility with clinical proteomics workflows. One method entailed the use of immobilized trypsin (SMART Digest Trypsin) with RapiGest SF, an acid-labile surfactant designed to enhance the in-solution enzymatic digestion of proteins. The other method incorporated a commercially available sample preparation kit, iST-BCT, which contains standardized reagents. Significantly higher protein sequence coverage, albeit with lower digestion efficiency, was obtained with the immobilized trypsin + RapiGest SF workflow, whereas the iST-BCT workflow was quicker and had marginally better reproducibility. Protein relative abundance analysis revealed that the serum proteomes clustered primarily by the sample processing workflow and secondarily by disease state. We conducted a time course study to determine whether differences in the relative abundance of diagnostic high-grade serous ovarian cancer serum protein biomarker candidates were biased according to the duration of enzymatic digestion. Our results highlight the importance of optimizing enzymatic digestion kinetics according to the peptide targets of interest while considering the sensitivity of the downstream analytical method utilized in clinical proteomics workflows designed to measure biomarkers.Keywords: serum, proteomics, biomarker, mass spectrometry, RapiGest SF, iST-BCT, SMART Digest Trypsin

Introduction

Blood is an optimal biological fluid for minimally invasive health monitoring and diagnosis because it carries oxygen and nutrients throughout the body and it is in close contact with all tissues and organs. Accordingly, blood-based biomarkers have essential roles in precision (personalized) medicine based on their ability to differentiate between normal and diseased individuals, classify patients into those who respond to drugs or interventions and those who are nonresponders, stratify diseases according to their subtypes, and predict disease outcomes.1,2 Serum is the fluid component of blood that is devoid of fibrinogen and coagulation factors. The advantages of using serum for diagnostic tests include the lack of the requirement for an anticoagulant for its production from blood, the enhanced stability of analytes in the primary blood collection tube, and a wide availability of validated analytical methods due to its role as the standard sample type for clinical chemistry tests.3−5 Given that serum is relatively easy to extract from blood and it contains several proteins that are associated with disease-related processes, an increasing number of serum proteomics studies are being conducted for the early detection of disease, measurement of therapeutic toxicity and disease response, and the discovery of novel therapeutic drug targets.6−12

Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) is the method of choice for comprehensive proteomics studies due to its ability to provide unbiased profiles of the human proteome.13 The serum proteome complexity and large dynamic range of protein concentrations present technical challenges for the accurate, sensitive, and reproducible quantification of proteins by MS. The complexity of serum as a specimen matrix for targeted MS assays presents additional, unique challenges.

In targeted MS assays with an intended clinical use based on the measurement of peptides representing protein biomarkers, the sample preparation process should be highly reproducible. Denaturation, reduction, and enzymatic digestion are critical components of this process, given the need for the reproducible liberation of target peptide(s) from the protein(s) of interest and their subsequent detection by MS. Additionally, matrix effects, defined as all properties of a sample, other than the entity intended to be measured, can introduce significant bias in targeted MS assays.14 This is of particular concern for assays with serum as the matrix. Accordingly, reproducible and efficient sample preparation methods are important for the quantitative measurement of proteins in serum using MS.15−17 This becomes particularly important for studies with large cohorts.18

The multiple steps required to prepare serum for MS-based proteomic analysis can be time-consuming. Conventional protocols often require >20 h for lysis, reduction, alkylation, digestion, desalting/peptide purification, and drying. Consequently, these protocols introduce the risk of contamination, sample loss, and artifactual amino acid modifications that result in decreased peptide and protein identifications. Serum proteome sample preparation methods that are rapid, reliable, reproducible, and minimize artifactual modifications are advantageous. However, the speed of these sample preparation methods needs to be balanced by the sensitivity of the downstream analytical detection method.

In this study, we evaluated the technical performance of two serum proteome sample preparation methods with a goal of providing insight into their compatibility with clinical proteomics workflows. One method entailed the use of SMART Digest immobilized trypsin + RapiGest SF, an acid-labile surfactant designed to enhance the in-solution enzymatic digestion of proteins by solubilizing them and rendering them more susceptible to enzymatic proteolysis without inhibiting enzyme activity. The other method incorporated a commercially available sample preparation kit, iST-BCT, which contains standardized reagents. The iST-BCT kit was developed to enable the robust processing of biological fluids for mass spectrometry-based analysis within 2 h. We evaluated both methods using human serum from patients with high-grade serous ovarian cancer and patients with benign nongynecologic conditions as disparate types of clinically relevant biospecimens with relevance for biomarker development studies. Specifically, we evaluated the analytical performance and technical reproducibility of each workflow based on the protein sequence coverage, digestion efficiency, biochemical properties of the identified peptides, and protein relative abundance. We also conducted a time course study using one of the sample preparation workflows to determine the relationship between enzymatic digestion kinetics and the relative abundance of diagnostic biomarker candidates.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Reagents

Pierce BCA protein assay kit, SMART Digest Trypsin, dithiothreitol (DTT), LC–MS-grade water, LC–MS-grade acetonitrile (ACN), formic acid (FA), and trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Iodoacetamide (IAM) was purchased from Millipore Sigma. RapiGest SF was purchased from Waters. The iST-BCT Sample Preparation kit was purchased from PreOmics.

Sample Preparation

Six human serum samples obtained from three patients with benign nongynecological health conditions and three patients with high-grade serous ovarian cancer were used. The serum was obtained from a biorepository at the University of Minnesota. Deidentified serum specimens in the biorepository were processed from blood originally obtained by the University of Minnesota Tissue Procurement Facility with approval by the University of Minnesota Institutional Review Board under Protocol 0407M62504.19 The IRB determined that the current study met the criteria for exemption from IRB review.

Protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay. For proteomic analysis, 100 μg of protein was processed in triplicate with either the iST-BCT kit or with a modified SMART Digest kit/in-house RapiGest SF digestion protocol, referred to hereafter as the SMT workflow.

The iST-BCT Sample Preparation Kit (PreOmics) was used in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 100 μg of serum was lysed with 50 μL of LYSE-BCT buffer at 95 °C for 10 min. Samples were transferred to an iST CARTRIDGE and digested with 50 μL of DIGEST buffer at 37 °C for 1 h. Following digestion, samples were acidified with 100 μL of STOP buffer. Samples were bound to the CARTRIDGE by centrifugation at 3800g for 1 min and purified with 200 μL of WASH 1 buffer, then with 200 μL of WASH 2 buffer. The CARTRIDGE was then placed into a COLLECTION tube, and samples were eluted twice with 100 μL of ELUTE buffer, followed by drying using vacuum centrifugation.

For the SMART Digest + RapiGest SF workflow (SMT workflow), 100 μg protein was diluted to 2 μg/μL with 0.1% RapiGest SF in 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8. Next, 5 μL of SMART Digest Trypsin magnetic resin, 150 μL SMART Buffer, and 5 mM TCEP were added, diluting samples to 0.5 μg/μL. Samples immediately underwent reduction and digestion incubation at 70 °C, 1000 rpm for 90 min. Samples were subsequently separated from the magnetic beads to stop the digestion, followed by alkylation with 15 mM IAM for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Samples then underwent centrifugation at 13,000g for 10 min to pellet insoluble material. The peptides were then desalted using C18 solid-phase extraction.

SMT Workflow Digestion Time Course Study

Serum protein (200 μg) was digested using the SMT workflow, and 10 μg aliquots were removed at each time point (0, 5, 10, 30, 45, 90, 120 min) for electrophoretic separation on a 4–12% Bis-Tris gel followed by Coomassie blue staining. Relative quantification (densitometry analysis) was conducted using ImageJ software. The mean gray value was measured using an identical selection frame across all lanes, and the density of the bands was calculated by inverting the measured value (255-X). The values were normalized to the predigest lane to represent the percentage of undigested protein present throughout the enzymatic digestion time course. Time points of 0, 5, 30, and 90 min were selected for subsequent analysis by targeted MS using parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) as described in the section below.

LC–MS/MS analysis

LC–MS/MS analysis was performed using a Vanquish high-performance liquid chromatography system (Thermo Scientific) coupled to a Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). Samples were reconstituted in 5% ACN/0.1% FA and 4 μg peptides were injected into an Accucore RP-MS C18 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 2.6 μm, 80 Å). Peptides were separated using a gradient comprised of mobile phase A (0.1% FA in water) and mobile phase B (90% ACN/0.1% FA in water). The gradient was as follows: 5% B for 0.5 min, 5–55% B over 60 min, 55–95% B over 1 min, and 95% B for 5 min, at a constant flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. Samples were introduced into the mass spectrometer via an Ion Max HESI-II source with a spray voltage of 3.80 kV. Data was acquired with a full MS/dd-MS2 Top10 method using the following parameters: full MS1 (70,000 resolution, 1e6 AGC target, maximum injection time 30 ms, 200–1800 m/z scan range) and dd-MS2 (17,500 resolution, 5e4 AGC target, maximum injection time 50 ms, isolation window 2.0 m/z, HCD collision energy 30). Ions with z = 2 to 6 were selected with a dynamic exclusion of 1 s.

A scheduled parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) assay was developed using a Q Exactive Plus Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer coupled with a Vanquish HPLC system interfaced with an Ion Max HESI-II source (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Mobile phases A and B contained 0.1% FA in water and 90% ACN/0.1% FA, respectively. The peptides were separated on an Accucore RP-MS C18 column (50 × 2.1 mm, 2.6 μm) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a linear gradient of 5–25% mobile phase B for 6 min at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. For measurement of the target peptides, the mass spectrometer was operated in the PRM mode. Mass spectra were acquired in the profile mode with a setting of 35,000 resolution at 200 m/z, 2e5 AGC target, 100 ms maximum injection time (IT), 1.6 m/z isolation window, and normalized collision energy of 27. Ionization source parameters were as follows: spray voltage, 3.8 kV; sheath gas, 26; auxiliary gas, 6; capillary temperature, 380 °C. A scheduled method was used with 0.3 min retention time windows monitoring the following 2+ precursor ions: 501.777 m/z (TGLQEVEVK peptide from CO3; UniProt ID P01024), 590.340 m/z (DLLLPQPDLR peptide from LRG1; UniProt ID P02750), and 626.814 m/z (VQPYLDDFQK peptide from APOA1; UniProt ID P02647). The integrated peak areas of the extracted ion chromatograms of 3 transition ions from each peptide were summed to represent the relative abundance of the monitored peptide.

The raw data dependent acquisition mass spectrometry data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE20 partner repository with the data set identifier PXD051002. The raw PRM data have been deposited to the PeptideAtlas SRM Experimental Library (PASSEL)21 with the data set identifier PASS05885.

Data Analysis

Raw mass spectrometry data was processed using Proteome Discoverer 2.5 (Thermo Scientific) and searched using SEQUEST HT. The following search parameters were used: enzyme specificity was set to trypsin with 2 maximum missed cleavage sites and peptide precursor and fragment mass tolerance were set to 10 ppm and 0.02 Da, respectively. Oxidation of methionine (Met), addition of acetyl at the N-termini of each peptide, and cysteine (Cys) carbamidomethylation were set as dynamic modifications for the analysis of chemical and artifactual post-translational modifications. For all other analyses, Cys carbamidomethylation was set as a static modification. A UniProt SwissProt Homo sapiens database (downloaded on 2021-05-04) was used for protein identification. Percolator was used to determine the confidence of peptide and protein identifications using the q-value set to <0.05 and a strict false discovery rate (FDR) of 0.01. Label-free quantification of protein abundances were calculated using Proteome Discoverer 2.5. Protein grouping was conducted using the principle of maximum parsimony. A total of 258 proteins were analyzed in this study. The abundances of these proteins were log2-transformed and median-normalized based on the median from each sample preparation method prior to statistical analysis. Welch’s t test was used to determine statistical significance in proteins that were quantified in ≥2 technical replicates (n = 191 proteins). Two-way ANOVA with Tukey multiple comparisons test was used to determine the statistical significance of proteins that were quantified across all biological samples (n = 62 proteins). Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.4.1.

Results and Discussion

Workflow Overview

The overall workflow of this study is presented in Figure 1. Aliquots each containing 100 μg protein were prepared from three healthy patient-derived human serum samples (n = 3 biological replicates: NC1–NC3) and three high-grade serous ovarian cancer serum samples (n = 3 biological replicates: OC1–OC3). Each 100 μg aliquot was subjected to proteome sample preparation using either the iST-BCT kit (serum protein concentration during enzymatic digestion: 2 mg/mL) or the SMT method (SMART Digest immobilized trypsin + RapiGest SF surfactant; serum protein concentration during enzymatic digestion: 0.5 mg/mL). Sample preparation full process replicates (technical replicates) were prepared using the NC1 and OC1 samples: NC1 iST1–iST3, NC1 SMT2–SMT3; OC1 iST1–iST3, and OC1 SMT1–SMT3). Due to limited reagent availability, n = 2 instead of 3 technical replicates were prepared for the NC1 SMT sample (NC1 SMT2, NC1 SMT3).

Experimental overview of serum proteome complexity analysis. A) Two sets of three 100 μg aliquots of protein from three noncancerous (NC) and three ovarian cancer (OC) patient-derived human serum samples were prepared and one aliquot per sample group was prepared with technical replicates. B) One set of aliquots was processed with the PreOmics iST-BCT sample preparation kit (iST workflow) and one set was processed using the SMART Digest Trypsin + RapiGest SF workflow (SMT workflow). The samples were analyzed by LC–MS/MS using data-dependent acquisition.

With the iST-BCT kit, the reduction and alkylation steps were conducted concurrently for 10 min at 95 °C, whereas these steps were conducted separately for 90 min at 70 °C and 30 min at room temperature, respectively, for the SMT workflow. The enzymatic digestion occurred for 60 min at 37 °C using a combination of Lys-C and Trypsin enzymes with the iST-BCT kit compared to a duration of 90 min at 70 °C using only Trypsin for the SMT workflow. The desalting/peptide purification and drying steps were similar between the two methods. Each sample was subjected to LC–MS/MS analysis using a data-dependent acquisition method.

With a future goal of adapting one of the two serum proteome sample preparation methods to a streamlined clinical proteomics workflow, a single-shot 60 min LC–MS/MS analysis was conducted. Deep serum proteome profiling would necessitate protein depletion and/or fractionation prior to LC–MS/MS analysis.6

Biological and Technical Reproducibility

Using our single-shot LC–MS/MS method to analyze the proteome of nondepleted and nonfractionated serum specimens, a total of 258 proteins were quantified with ≥1 unique peptide (Table 1 of the Supporting Information). Significantly higher numbers of peptides and proteins were quantified using the SMT workflow compared to the iST workflow, and these differences were significant at a level of p < 0.01 (unpaired 2-tailed t test). Among the technical replicates for NC and OC, an average (mean) of 1963 ± 135 vs 1139 ± 60 peptides and 150 ± 5 vs 135 ± 7 proteins were quantified using the SMT and iST workflows, respectively (Table 2 of the Supporting Information).

The reproducibility of each workflow, as assessed by the percentage of quantified proteins, was very similar between the two workflows. For the iST workflow, the average (mean) percent of proteins quantified across all biological replicates was 81%, and the average percent of proteins quantified across all technical replicates was 87%. The reproducibility of the SMT workflow was nearly identical with an average of 80% of proteins quantified across all biological replicates and 86% of proteins quantified across all technical replicates (Figure 2). As another indication of the similar technical reproducibility of both workflows, the median CV of the quantified proteins from the iST workflow was 8.9% vs 10% for the SMT workflow (Figure S1). On the basis of protein relative abundance, the technical replicates of each workflow exhibited strong correlations (r = 0.99–1.0) (Figure S2). The range of correlations of protein relative abundance across all samples processed using the iST workflow was marginally higher (r = 0.86–1.0) compared to the samples processed using the SMT workflow (r = 0.8–1.0) (Figure S2).

The proteins quantified across all technical replicates (boxed numbers) were used for the biological replicate comparisons. After evaluating the biological and technical replicate performance of each workflow based on the number of quantified proteins, we next assessed differences in the two workflows based on protein sequence coverage as a proxy for digestion efficiency. Among the proteins quantified in ≥2 of the 3 NC and OC technical replicates, the sequence coverage distribution was skewed to the right for the samples processed using the SMT workflow vs the iST workflow (Figure 3a, b). The difference in sequence coverage among the common set of proteins quantified across all of the samples (n = 62 proteins) was assessed using 2-way ANOVA followed by Tukey multiple comparison testing. Each sample group-based comparison (NC: iST vs SMT and OC: iST vs SMT) was statistically significant (p < 0.0001) (Figure 3c). The higher sequence coverage of the SMT workflow-processed serum samples, irrespective of the sample group, is shown in Figure 3d. The median protein sequence coverage of the iST serum proteome samples was 20.9% vs 35.3% for the SMT serum proteome samples. The significant difference in protein sequence coverage was not recapitulated at the level of protein relative abundance (Figure S3).

Enzymatic Digestion Efficiency and Amino Acid Modifications

To more comprehensively evaluate the differences in sequence coverage of the SMT vs iST workflow-processed serum samples, we evaluated the lengths, missed cleavage rates, and amino acid compositions of the identified peptides, given that these parameters are inter-related. The SMT workflow yielded significantly longer peptides compared to the iST workflow (average length: 16 vs 13 amino acids, respectively) (Figure 4a). However, the iST workflow resulted in a significantly overall higher digestion efficiency as determined by the number of missed cleavages (Figure 4b). Whereas 87% of the peptides from the iST workflow were identified without any missed cleavages, only 46% of the peptides from the SMT workflow were identified without any missed cleavages. The trend of a greater digestion efficiency from the iST vs the SMT workflow was consistent when evaluating the percentage of peptides with 1 (12% vs 36%) and 2 (0.19% vs 17%) missed cleavages.

Peptide sequence length and missed cleavages. (a) Identified peptides were significantly longer in the SMT vs the iST workflow (n = 3745 peptides). (b) iST workflow resulted in higher digestion efficiency as assessed by the percentage of peptides with 0, 1, or 2 missed cleavages (n = 3745 peptides). **** = p < 0.0001 (unpaired 2-tailed t test).

To determine the biochemical differences among the identified peptides, we evaluated their amino acid compositions and their associated hydrophobicity indices (Figure 5). The hydrophobicity index is a measure of the relative hydrophobicity, or the degree that an amino acid is soluble in water.22 In a protein, hydrophobic amino acids are generally not solvent-exposed, whereas hydrophilic amino acids are likely to be in contact with the aqueous environment. Significant differences in the occurrence of all amino acids except Threonine (T), Gly (G), and Ser (S) were observed. Notably, these amino acids have neutral hydrophobicities of 13, 0, and −7, respectively, at pH 2. The amino acids in the peptides identified from the iST workflow trended toward being comprised of amino acids with more hydrophobic (40–74) and neutral (−20–39) hydrophobicity indices, whereas the amino acids in the peptides identified from the SMT workflow were generally more hydrophilic (<20).

Distribution of amino acids among identified peptides from each workflow. (a) Amino acids within all identified peptides. (b) Relative distribution of amino acids. (c) Normalized hydrophobicity index of amino acids relative to glycine (neutral; 0 value). * = p < 0.05, ** = p < 0.01, and **** = p < 0.0001. n.s. = not significant.

Cysteine (C) is a hydrophobic (hydrophobicity index = 52), reactive amino acid that is subject to the formation of disulfide bonds between the thiol groups within its side chain.23 Reduction of these disulfide bonds, followed by the carboxymethylation-mediated alkylation of free cysteines, are essential steps in most bottom-up proteomic sample preparation workflows.24 To assess the efficiency of these reactions using the iST and SMT workflows, we evaluated the percentage of cysteines identified in their carbamidomethylated forms compared to their unmodified forms. The SMT workflow resulted in a significantly higher percentage of carbamidomethylated cysteines (97.6%) compared to the iST workflow (21%) (Figure S4), indicating a nearly complete efficiency of the reduction and alkylation reactions in the conditions used for the SMT workflow, which entailed reduction at 70 °C for 90 min conducted concurrently with trypsin digestion, followed by alkylation at room temperature for 30 min postdigestion.

Methionine is a very hydrophobic amino acid (hydrophobicity index = 74) that is susceptible to oxidation when it is exposed to an aqueous environment, such as that which exists during proteome sample preparation methods involving enzymatic digestion.25,26In vitro methionine oxidation is a reversible process that is influenced by solvent accessibility and protein structure.27,28 With the use of the oxidation of methionine as a representative example of artifactual post-translational modifications (PTMs), the SMT workflow was more efficient compared to the iST workflow. Across 3745 peptides, an average of 30% of methionines were identified in their oxidized form using the iST workflow versus 5.9% using the SMT workflow (Figure S4). These results suggest that the buffer conditions for the SMT workflow (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8) do not promote artifactual methionine oxidation. A caveat to note in the interpretation of the data is that the ionization efficiencies of peptides with oxidized versus nonoxidized forms of methionine differ; hence, it is possible that these two forms of the methionine-containing peptides did not have similar rates of identification.

Relative Serum Protein Abundance

After establishing the robust biological and technical reproducibility of each workflow and demonstrating that statistically significantly higher protein sequence coverage was achieved with the SMT workflow versus the iST workflow, we conducted relative protein abundance-based hierarchical clustering analysis to evaluate additional differences between the workflows. The concentrations of endogenous serum proteins span approximately 10 orders of magnitude (albumin: 40 g/L to interferon lambda 3: 1.7 ng/L).29 Given that the methods we used in this study did not include the use of depletion or fractionation, the proteins we quantified were biased toward those with high endogenous concentrations (mg/L level) as determined by the Human Protein Atlas (proteinatlas.org). The pattern of z-scores in the heat map in Figure 6a indicates overall similar trends in relative protein abundance across all of the samples. However, noticeable differences exist based on the clustering of the samples. The NC1 and OC1 technical replicate samples processed with each workflow exhibit the greatest extent of similarity, whereas the OC2, OC3, NC2, and NC3 biological replicates are more closely clustered based on the sample preparation workflows compared to the disease states of the samples.

Serum proteomes cluster primarily by sample processing method and secondarily by disease state. (a) Heat map of relative protein abundances. (b) PCA plot of serum proteomes.

The sample clustering is more prominent in the PCA plot shown in Figure 6b. Two distinct groups of samples emerge from this analysis: samples processed using the iST workflow (green-filled shapes) and the samples processed using the SMT workflow (blue-filled shapes). Similar to the pattern that emerged with the heat map, samples processed using the same method are more closely clustered than the biological replicates, irrespective of the disease states. For example, the proteomic profile of OC2 iST is more similar to the profile of NC2 iST than it is to the profile of OC2 SMT. These results highlight the significant differences in the analytical properties of the serum proteomes that are derived from the iST vs the SMT workflow.

Application to Clinical Proteomics Workflows for Serum Protein Biomarker Analysis

The trend in diagnostic biomarkers approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in recent years suggests an opportunity for the development of multibiomarker panels, as opposed to single biomarkers.30 Given their ability to be highly multiplexed, targeted MS assays measuring multiple peptides from different proteins can greatly facilitate these biomarker development efforts.31−35 In this context, sample preparation workflows that are reproducible, rapid, and yield high protein sequence coverage are advantageous. Whereas reproducibility and speed could be prioritized for targeted MS assays measuring only a limited number of peptides, high protein sequence coverage would be a considerable asset for highly multiplexed targeted MS assays given the impractical aspect of conducting a digestion time-course kinetics experiment to optimize the recovery of every peptide in the assay panel.

Given these considerations and based on the experimental conditions of our study, the iST serum preparation workflow would be well-suited for the development and validation of targeted MS assays comprised of only a limited number of peptide targets given its speed (70 min for denaturation, reduction, alkylation, and enzymatic digestion) and good reproducibility (81% of proteins quantified across all 3 biological replicates, 87% of proteins quantified across all 3 technical replicates, and 8.9% median CV of protein relative abundances across all 3 technical replicates). The SMT workflow would be compatible with the development and validation of highly multiplexed targeted MS assay panels largely based on the relatively high protein sequence coverage (median: 35.3%) but also based on the workflow’s good reproducibility (80% of proteins quantified across all 3 biological replicates, 86% of proteins quantified across both technical replicates, and 10% median CV of protein relative abundances across both technical replicates) and moderately rapid speed (120 min for denaturation, reduction, alkylation, and enzymatic digestion).

To evaluate the influence of enzyme digestion kinetics on the ability to detect measurable differences in diagnostic serum protein biomarker candidates between the OC and NC specimens, we conducted a time course study using the SMT workflow (Figure 7a). In our standard SMT workflow, the trypsin digestion was conducted for 90 min; however, for this experiment, we selected a range of shorter and longer time points: 5, 10, 30, 45, 90, and 120 min (Figure S5a). Normalizing the intensity of the stained gel bands in each lane to the intensity of the undigested protein from the 0 min time point, and assuming that undigested proteins have MW > 3 kDa according to the average mass of tryptic peptides,36 80.7%, 73.1%, 69.8%, 61.9%, 60.4%, and 55.1% of protein remained undigested after 5, 10, 30, 45, 90, and 120 min, respectively (Figure S5b).

Next, we sought to determine whether differences in the relative abundance of diagnostic high-grade serous ovarian cancer serum protein biomarker candidates were biased according to the duration of the enzymatic digestion. We selected three diagnostic biomarker candidates that were identified from other published studies, and we measured their relative OC vs NC abundances in a PRM targeted MS method: Apolipoprotein A1 (APOA1, UniProt ID: P02647),6,37 Complement 3 (CO3, UniProt ID: P01024),6,38 and leucine-rich alpha-2 glycoprotein 1 (LRG1, UniProt ID: P02750).6,39 The results from these published studies indicated that C3 and LRG1 were more abundant in the sera of high-grade serous ovarian cancer patients compared to healthy controls, whereas APOA1 was more abundant in the sera of healthy controls. In our study, among these three proteins, LRG1 was the only biomarker candidate whose expected direction of relative abundance was recapitulated irrespective of the digestion time point for the SMT workflow (Figure 7). LRG1 fold-changes of 2.895, 2.469, and 2.235 were observed in the OC versus NC samples at the 5, 30, and 90 min time points, respectively. No significant OC vs NC differences in the relative abundance of C3 or APOA1 were observed at any of the digestion time points. As expected, significant differences (p < 0.01) were observed in the relative abundance of each protein between each of the time points, which reflects the digestion kinetics. Greater quantities of the target peptides were liberated from the intact proteins with increased time. Although the target peptides were detectable by PRM following 5, 30, and 90 min of digestion, the digestion of the respective proteins did not yet reach saturation, which influenced the ability to recapitulate their expected relative abundances as diagnostic biomarkers for high-grade serous ovarian cancer.

Conclusion

Reproducible and efficient sample preparation methods are of critical importance for MS-based serum proteomics analyses. The lack of interlaboratory harmonization of serum preparation protocols among clinical laboratories is an acknowledged source of preanalytical variation in clinical proteomic analysis. Consequently, variability in blood sample handling including temperature, clotting time, centrifugation speed, and centrifugation acceleration levels could have differential impacts on serum compared to plasma proteomic analysis.40

We designed our study to focus on the technical reproducibility of two serum proteome sample preparation methods using sera representing two disparate disease states: high-grade serous ovarian cancer and benign nongynecological conditions. We evaluated the performance of these methods using immobilized trypsin (SMART Digest Trypsin) with the surfactant RapiGest SF and the commercially available iST-BCT sample preparation kit with a goal of providing insight into their compatibility with clinical proteomics workflows. Our study provides an initial comparative assessment of the technical performance of these two methods for the proteomic analysis of serum. Significantly higher protein sequence coverage was obtained with the SMART Digest immobilized trypsin + RapiGest SF workflow, although with lower digestion efficiency compared to the iST-BCT workflow. The serum proteomes clustered primarily by the sample processing workflow and secondarily by disease state.

Both serum proteome sample preparation workflows can be completed with ∼2 h. Options to enhance the speed of the SMART Digest + RapiGest SF workflow include optimizing the digestion kinetics based on the specific surrogate peptides of interest. Concentrations of RapiGest SF greater than the 0.1% (w/w) we used in our workflow can enhance the digestion of proteolytic-resistant or hydrophobic proteins.41,42 Other methods to increase the kinetics of proteolytic cleavage include the use of ultrasonication, microwave, solvents, and immobilized enzyme reactors.43 Lower-cost alternatives that do not sacrifice sample preparation throughput have recently been described.44

As we demonstrated in our time course study (Figure S5 and Figure 7), optimizing the enzymatic digestion kinetics according to the peptide targets of interest while considering the sensitivity of the downstream analytical method is a critical step in workflows designed to measure biomarkers. Although our target peptides of interest were detected by PRM, the differences in their relative abundance between the OC and NC samples were peptide-dependent. At the 5, 30, and 90 min time points, the performance of LRG1 as a diagnostic biomarker was as expected; however, this was not the case for the other two biomarkers of interest, APOA1 and CO3. The lack of significant differences in the relative abundances of these proteins between the OC versus NC samples at any of the time points was likely influenced by the enzymatic digestion kinetics.

Of note, our study was designed to incorporate the use of a single-shot LC–MS/MS analysis, which precluded deep serum profiling. The identification of >1000 serum proteins is possible via deep serum profiling approaches incorporating the depletion of abundant proteins, extensive offline peptide-level fractionation (∼24 fractions), and the use of more advanced mass spectrometers with faster scan speeds and higher sensitivity.6,45,46 Although antibody-based depletion of the most abundant serum proteins improves protein depth, this is often achieved at the expense of the precision of protein quantification. A more comprehensive assessment of the difference in the performance of the iST-BCT versus SMART Digest + RapiGest SF methods would likely be achieved by adopting a deep serum profiling approach; however, these large-scale and resource-intensive approaches are not compatible with routine clinical applications.
