Risk Factors Influencing the Specificity of Skin Tests for Bovine Tuberculosis in a Low-Prevalence Region
Authors: Alberto Gomez-Buendia, Roxana Triguero-Ocaña, Marta Muñoz-Mendoza, Marta Alvarez-Fidalgo, Jorge Mourelo, Jose Luis Saez, Beatriz Romero, Julio Alvarez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Skin tests are the main official diagnostic method for granting, maintaining, and regaining the officially tuberculosis free (OTF) herd status in the European Union. Although highly specific, repeated testing may lead to the culling of animals not infected with bovine tuberculosis (bTB) and the suspension of OTF status, a problem especially relevant in low-prevalence regions where most herds are OTF. Identifying risk factors that affect the specificity of the single (SIT) and comparative (CIT) intradermal tuberculin tests is therefore essential to optimize control measures.Materials and Methods:We analyzed 119,465 herd tests (comprising 3,588,477 skin tests) from 23,893 OTF herds tested over 5 years (2014–2018) to estimate the animal-level reactor probability and herd test specificity while accounting for relevant covariates.Results:Median reactor probabilities were low (SIT: 0.050%; CIT: 0.034%). Dairy herds had higher SIT-reactor odds compared to beef herds, whereas CIT showed the opposite pattern. Reactor risk increased over time and was higher in summer compared to spring for SIT, while a reduced risk was observed in cooler seasons for both tests. Herd test specificity remained high (median values >97% for SIT and CIT) but declined with increasing herd size, particularly for SIT. Over 5 years, the median cumulative probability of detecting ≥1 reactor in a herd was 10.9%, with higher values in dairy and large herds.Conclusion:Although skin tests maintain near-perfect animal-level specificity under field conditions, herd-level performance was influenced by several covariates, particularly herd type and size. In dairy herds, using CIT when cross-reactions are suspected and bTB-infection has been ruled out, could mitigate OTF suspensions in low-prevalence settings.

Introduction

Bovine tuberculosis (bTB) remains a major concern for cattle health and international trade, necessitating the implementation of control and eradication programs worldwide.[12] The intradermal tuberculin test, commonly referred to as the skin test, has historically served as the cornerstone of these programs due to its operational simplicity and high combined sensitivity and specificity. Nevertheless, despite its widespread use, limitations in diagnostic performance may hinder eradication efforts.[3]

Within the European Union, the tuberculin skin test (single or comparative) and the interferon-gamma (IFN-g) assay are the official diagnostic tools for granting, maintaining, and regaining officially tuberculosis free (OTF) herd status, as well as for intra-community animal trade, in accordance with Delegated Regulations (EU) 2020/689 and 2020/688 under Regulation (EU) 2016/429. In Spain, herds undergo periodic testing to maintain OTF status, with testing frequency determined by regional prevalence. The single and particularly the comparative intradermal skin test (SIT and CIT hereafter) are considered highly specific, with reported values reaching 99.98% under field conditions.[4] However, in very low-prevalence populations, rare cross-reactions, despite the high specificity, may result in the unnecessary culling of animals infected with microorganisms other than members of Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTC).

Moreover, classifying a nonconfirmed animal as a reactor leads to automatic suspension of the herd’s OTF status, triggering movement restrictions and trade limitations, with associated economic losses and potential erosion of stakeholders’ confidence in eradication programs.[56] In such low-prevalence settings, diagnostic specificity becomes particularly critical, as even marginal reductions can translate into a substantial proportion of cross-reactions at the herd level.

Cross-reactivity with nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) and other bacteria has been identified as a primary source of false-positive reactions.[78910] However, other herd- or region-level factors that may influence test specificity remain insufficiently explored, particularly in Spain. Although previous studies have investigated factors associated with diagnostic failures to the skin test in infected settings,[1112] limited evidence exists regarding determinants affecting specificity under low-prevalence conditions.

Although Spain is classified as a high-prevalence country within the European Union,[13] certain regions, such as Galicia, in the northwest, have achieved OTF status under Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2021/1911. In this region, the potential impact of specificity-related issues could be particularly relevant. A clearer understanding of factors influencing skin test specificity is therefore essential to refine diagnostic strategies, minimize unnecessary culling, and ensure the sustainability of eradication programs.

This study aims to identify risk factors influencing skin test specificity for bTB in a low-prevalence region using a Bayesian hierarchical logistic regression model applied to 119,465 herd tests conducted over 5 years. By adopting this approach, we seek to provide robust and updated estimates of specificity and identify factors that contribute to an increased risk of test-positivity in noninfected herds, thereby supporting evidence-based optimization of bTB control policies in low-prevalence settings.

Materials and Methods

Study population

The study was conducted in Galicia, a region in the northwest of Spain divided into four provinces (A Coruña, Lugo, Ourense, and Pontevedra). Since 2015, herd-level TB prevalence in the region has been <0.1%, and in 2019, Pontevedra became the first peninsular province to be declared OTF, and the remaining provinces achieved OTF status in 2021.[14]

Results from all bTB tests performed in Galicia between 2014 and 2018 were initially considered. Data were subsequently filtered to include herds that: held OTF status in January 2014, were tested annually during the 5-year study period, had no detection of MTC through postmortem investigations (culture and/or direct PCR) conducted on slaughtered reactors or on animals with lesions identified during routine abattoir inspections.

From these herds, data were collected on location (province), production type (beef or dairy; fattening herds were not included), and size (categorized into terciles: <10, 10–31, and >31 animals). Information was also recorded on the type of skin test performed (SIT or CIT), the date of each herd test during the study period, and test results. Animals were classified as SIT reactors if there was an increase in the skin fold thickness of ≥4 mm at the bovine PPD inoculation site and/or clinical signs were observed (diffuse or extensive edema, exudation, necrosis, pain, or lymphatic inflammation). For CIT, animals were considered reactors if the increase at the bovine PPD site was ≥4 mm and exceeded the avian PPD reaction, according to the standard interpretation criteria.[15]

Statistical analysis

Differences in herd size by production type and by skin test were assessed using a Mann–Whitney (M–W) test. Associations between herd size and province were evaluated using the Kruskal–Wallis (K–W) test followed by a Wilcoxon post hoc comparison with Holm–Bonferroni correction. Nonparametric tests were used due to the markedly skewed distribution of herd sizes across groups, which violated the normality assumption (Kolmogorov–Smirnov test; P < 0.001); homogeneity of variances was also not met (Levene’s test; P < 0.001). For each herd test, the expected number of reactors was simulated using a binomial distribution based on estimated pij and observed nij. The probability of detecting at least one reactor in a herd test was calculated by considering the proportion of simulations in which one or more reactors were detected for each herd test. Herd test specificity was defined as 1 − pHRij. To evaluate whether the herd test specificity varied by year and season, separate beta regression models were fitted for CIT and SIT using the “betareg” package.[16]

Convergence was assessed visually using the “mcmcplots” package,[19] and formally using the Gelman-Rubin statistic.[20]

Results

Descriptive statistics

During the study period, 3,932,433 skin tests were performed as part of 145,639 herd tests conducted in 32,582 herds across Galicia. Of these, 23,893 herds (73.3%) met the inclusion criteria (no detection of MTC during the 5-year study period despite the possible detection of reactors), accounting for 3,588,477 skin tests (91.3% of all tests) and 119,465 herd tests.

Among the included skin tests, 3,147,065 were SIT (87.7%) and 441,412 were CIT (12.3%). SIT was performed in at least one herd test in 23,372 herds (97.8% of included herds), totaling 111,970 herd tests (93.7% of included herd tests). CIT was performed in 3389 herds (14.2% of included herds), comprising 7495 herd tests (6.3% of included herd tests).

Herds had a mean of 30.1 animals tested per herd test (median = 15, interquartile range [IQR] = 6–37). Dairy herds were significantly larger than beef herds (M-W test, P < 0.001), with mean sizes of 65.8 (median = 48; IQR = 28–81) and 16.2 animals (median = 10, IQR = 4–21), respectively. Herd size also differed significantly between provinces (K-W test, P < 0.001): Lugo had the largest herds (mean = 32.7 animals, median = 20, IQR = 8–41), followed by A Coruña (mean = 29.4, median = 13, IQR = 5–33), Ourense (mean = 25.6, median = 15, IQR = 6–32), and Pontevedra (mean = 20.3, median = 9, IQR = 3–25). All pairwise comparisons were statistically significant (Wilcoxon tests, P < 0.001). Furthermore, herds where CIT was performed were significantly (M-W test, P < 0.001) larger (mean = 68.2 animals, median = 41, IQR = 21–71) than herds where SIT was applied (mean = 58.0 animals, median = 15, IQR = 6–34).

Over the 5 years, 11,758 animals (0.33%) were skin test positive but MTC-culture negative, with annual proportions ranging from 0.17% to 0.53%. Of these, 11,364 (96.6%) were detected through SIT (0.36% of all animals tested with this test), and 394 (3.4%) through CIT (0.09%). The use of the type of skin test varied by year, with a progressive increase in the use of CIT [Table 1].

Herd test specificity

Herd test specificity varied considerably by year and season for both tests [Figure 1]. For SIT, specificity was lowest in summer and highest in winter (P < 0.001 across seasons). For CIT, a similar seasonal pattern was observed, although no significant difference was detected in fall (P = 0.254).

Specificity decreased with herd size (median 99.1% for <10 animals vs. 94.4% for >31 animals). Discussion

The inherent limitations in the specificity of the tuberculin skin test can result in the temporary suspension of the OTF status in a small proportion of non-bTB-infected herds. The undesired effect has a cumulative nature when herds are tested on a yearly basis, since there is an increasing likelihood that at least one animal within a herd will test positive over time, leading to repeated OTF suspensions.[21] Understanding the factors that influence this phenomenon is important for the establishment of better protocols to eradicate bTB and maintain the free status of non-bTB-infected herds, maximizing test specificity. This issue is particularly relevant in very low-prevalence areas where, even though the aim remains to eradicate the disease and investigate all existing suspicions of bTB, maintaining a reasonable level of non-bTB-infected culled animals is also a priority.[22]

Overall, the probability of a positive test result at the animal level in this study was very low. However, even a very high individual specificity may lead to more limited herd-level specificity for both SIT and CIT. In Galicia, herds are generally small, with an average of 66 and 30 animals in dairy and beef herds, respectively, which would limit this effect. The situation may be different in other regions of Spain, such as Castilla and León, which has the largest cattle census in the country and an average of 119 animals per herd, or in other neighboring countries, such as the UK, with an average of 175 animals per herd according to national data.

As expected, the specificity of the CIT was higher than the SIT, but median specificity estimates were above 99.9% in both tests. Interestingly, the mean CIT-specificity estimated here (99.87%) agreed with the estimate calculated from Goodchild et al.[4] with the same interpretation (bovine PPD ≥4 mm and >avian PPD). While the median reactor probability was similar for CIT and SIT, the mean reactor probability was three times higher for SIT (0.40%) than for CIT (0.13%), which could be expected given that the CIT takes into consideration the response to the avian PPD and thus could help to discard cross-reactions due to NTM in certain situations.

There was a higher probability of finding reactors to the SIT in animals from dairy herds compared to beef herds [Table 2]. A similar pattern was also observed with the IFN-g assay in OTF herds, where the odds of finding reactors were between 3.7 and 6.3 times higher in dairy versus beef cattle, depending on the commercial kit used.[23] This likely reflects the higher prevalence of M. avium subsp. paratuberculosis (MAP), an endemic pathogen in the study region[24] known to induce nonspecific reactions to the SIT[25] in dairy versus beef cattle.[26] In addition, the generally better nutritional, sanitary, and management conditions of dairy cattle may favor a more consistent immune reactivity to antigenic stimulation,[27] further contributing to the increased likelihood of SIT responses in these herds. In contrast, when the CIT was used, the probability of finding a reactor was lower in dairy herds than in beef herds [Table 2], further supporting the hypothesis that MAP (or other NTM) may disproportionately affect dairy herds, and demonstrating the usefulness of the CIT in these herds in low-prevalence settings.

During the study period, the odds of detecting reactors using the SIT increased [Table 2] even though herd and individual-level prevalence decreased according to data from the national eradication program in Galicia.[28] During the 2014–2018 period, CIT usage rose from 7.5% of animals and 3.8% of herds in 2014–23.0% and 12.8% in 2018 [Table 1], respectively. In Galicia, which achieved OTF status in 2021 after sustaining prevalence below 0.1% since 2015, the increased risk of SIT-reactors likely reflects the progressive refinement of surveillance protocols linked to improved training[29] and increased awareness of the risk posed by residual infection as provinces approached the OTF recognition. This effect was more pronounced in Pontevedra, the first province in Spain to be declared OTF in 2019, where CIT accounted for 74.3% of the animals tested in 2018, and there was an increased risk of observing a reactor in both SIT and CIT that was much higher for SIT [Table 2]. These patterns illustrate how intensified application of the regulated SIT protocol and risk-based implementation of CIT during critical phases of eradication can influence reactor detection independently of the underlying prevalence.

There was also a variation in the risk depending on the season in which the herd was tested, with an increased probability in spring and summer that decreased in fall and winter [Figure 1]. The most plausible explanation for this is an increase in exposure to NTM during warmer seasons, in agreement with known patterns of NTM survival, growth, and human-related NTM infections.[3031]

Herd size was excluded from the final models due to lack of association and improvement in model fit, suggesting that the number of animals in a herd did not influence the probability of an individual animal reacting to the skin test if it was not infected. It is known that larger herds are at a higher risk of infection and recurrence of bTB.[3233] This is largely attributed to management-related factors such as increased animal movements and farm visits. In contrast, herd size did not appear to alter the intrinsic probability that a noninfected animal would react to the skin test in OTF herds. Nevertheless, as herd size increases, more animals are tested, thereby increasing the probability that at least one false-positive reactor will be identified. For example, assuming an animal-level specificity of 99.5%, the probability of identifying at least one reactor would increase from 22.2% in a herd of 50 animals to 52.9% in a herd of 150 animals.

This effect of increasing herd size may explain the slightly (and unexpectedly) higher median herd test specificity observed for SIT (97.7%) compared with CIT (97.2%), as CIT-tested herds tended to be larger (56.4% of CIT-tested herds had >31 animals versus 31.0% of SIT-tested herds) [Table 1]. When stratified by herd size, mean herd test specificity was higher for CIT than for SIT in larger herds, although median estimates were more similar [Table 3]. This suggests that SIT performance may be disproportionately affected in a subset of large herds. The effect was particularly evident in dairy herds, which were generally larger (72.4% with >31 animals) and in which SIT herd test specificity was lower than in beef herds [Table 3]. The size-related decline in specificity was more pronounced for SIT in dairy herds, whereas CIT herd test specificity remained more consistent across production types within comparable size categories [Supplementary Table 3.1; Supplementary Figure 3.9]. These results reinforce the utility of CIT in large dairy herds to ensure reliable herd-level classification and reduce the risk of unnecessary OTF suspensions. Given that bTB eradication programs rely on repeated testing, it is essential to consider the cumulative long-term effects of test limitations under OTF conditions. Over the 5-year study period, the accumulated reactor risk indicated a mean probability of losing OTF status at least once of 10.9%, corresponding to approximately one in ten herds [Table 4]. This probability was higher in dairy and larger herds. These findings support consideration of alternative herd-level classification models, such as risk-based testing approaches proposed by van Dijk,[21] rather than strictly input-based strategies that mandate repeated whole-herd testing regardless of herd characteristics.

The wide 95% PPIs for herd test specificity (e.g. SIT: 46.0%–99.6%; CIT: 80.5%–99.5%) reflect distributions with pronounced left tails, likely driven by a small subset of herds, principally large dairy herds [Supplementary Table 3.4] with markedly reduced specificity. In contrast, the relatively narrow interquartile ranges (SIT IQR: 94.6%–98.8%; CIT IQR: 95.4%–98.3%) indicate that most herds maintained high specificity for both tests.

The primary limitation of this study was the assumption that reactor animals not confirmed by bacteriological culture were truly noninfected with members of the MTC. The sensitivity of the bacteriological culture is imperfect, with reported values ranging from 30% to 100% depending on disease stage and sampling factors.[343536373839] Consequently, some reactors may have been false-negative by culture. Modeling studies in low-prevalence herds have suggested that under certain scenarios, bTB-infected herds may remain undetected for extended periods.[4041] Nevertheless, we consider the potential impact on our findings to be limited, as the majority of herds (4048/4753; 85.2%) had reactors identified in only a single herd test and no recorded reactors in preceding years.

Conclusions

Our findings demonstrate that while the specificity of SIT and CIT in OTF herds in Galicia was very high (99.95% and 99.97%, respectively), their performance was influenced by season, production type, and year of study. The higher odds of reaction to the SIT observed in dairy herds, which could be attributed to their increased probability of exposure to certain cross-reacting microorganisms (e.g. MAP or other NTMs), combined with a generally higher health status that could favor stronger immune responses to antigenic stimulation, highlight the potential usefulness of the CIT to minimize the number of cross-reactions once the presence of MTC in the herd has been ruled out through a detailed risk assessment. Conversely, in beef herds, where exposure to cross-reacting agents may be lower but the risk of having an undetected MTC infection may be higher due to other risk factors (e.g. lower biosecurity, extensive management, increased likelihood of false negative responses), the SIT may be particularly well-suited due to its greater sensitivity. Overall, this study supports the implementation of risk-based testing strategies to sustain progress towards bTB eradication in low-prevalence settings while maintaining confidence among stakeholders.
