Efficacy of Select Gene Targets for the Management of Western Flower Thrips Through RNA Interference
Authors: C. M. Senthil Kumar, Ritesh G. Jain, Stephen J. Fletcher, Neena Mitter, Karl E. Robinson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWestern flower thrips (WFT) Frankliniella occidentalis cause extensive damage to crops either by direct feeding injury or by transmitting diseases. Current management strategies against this pest rely heavily on synthetic insecticides, leading to issues such as resistance development and environmental concerns. Several safer alternatives including RNA interference (RNAi), also referred to as post‐transcriptional gene silencing (PTGS), are being extensively researched to develop species‐specific molecular pesticides for the management of this pest. However, the success of RNAi depends on the selection of lethal targets and the delivery of double‐stranded RNA (dsRNA) to the target pest. In this study, we demonstrate the uptake of dsRNA by WFT through artificial diet feeding and its distribution within the insect. Several candidate gene targets were evaluated, leading to the identification of promising RNAi targets for thrips management. In addition, dsRNA production was successfully standardized using a recombinant bacterial expression system for large‐scale applications. We also observed degradation of naked dsRNA following feeding, likely due to nuclease activity associated with thrips feeding processes, highlighting a key limitation for RNAi efficacy. Overall, our findings support the potential of RNAi as a targeted pest management strategy for WFT. The results emphasize the importance of strategic gene selection, improved delivery systems, and scalable dsRNA production for the development of effective RNAi‐based molecular biopesticides.

Introduction

Western flower thrips (WFT), Frankliniella occidentalis (Pergande) (Thysanoptera: Thripidae), is a highly invasive and economically important pest due to feeding and ovipositional damage, virus transmission, and the ability to rapidly reproduce, spread, and develop insecticide resistance. WFT is also the primary vector for tospovirus transmission worldwide (Kirk and Terry 2003; Maris et al. 2003; Mouden et al. 2017). Current management measures are heavily reliant on chemical pesticides; however, over‐reliance on insecticides has resulted in the development of increasingly resistant populations across the globe, making WFT and their transmitted viruses a persistent and increasingly resilient menace in horticultural crops (Badillo‐Vargas et al. 2015; Gao et al. 2012). Several alternative strategies to manage this pest have been suggested, including cultural, biological, and rotation of chemicals with different modes of action, but the threat posed by this pest still persists.

Risks posed by existing insecticides and the general concern for transgenic crops among the public have triggered a demand for alternative pest control solutions which are residue‐free, non‐transgenic, and safe to the environment, pollinators, and natural enemies (Taning et al. 2016). Recently, RNA‐based technologies, which utilize exogenously supplied double‐stranded RNA (dsRNA) to silence a homologous gene target in an organism, also known as RNA interference (RNAi), are emerging as a promising tool for developing low‐risk and environmentally safe alternatives to existing plant protection measures (Cagliari et al. 2019; S. Liu et al. 2020).

RNAi‐mediated plant protection can be broadly classified into two categories: (1) host‐induced gene silencing (HIGS) and (2) spray‐induced gene silencing (SIGS) methods. With HIGS, the dsRNA is expressed in the host plant as an endogenous transgene, which is ingested by the insect upon feeding and invokes silencing of the target gene. In SIGS, the synthesized dsRNA is sprayed onto the target pest/plants, resulting in knockdown of the target gene upon entry into the host insect (S. Liu et al. 2020; J. Zhang et al. 2017; Rakesh et al. 2024). SIGS can be achieved by ingestion or, in certain insects, by direct contact‐mediated uptake following topical application of dsRNA, depending on its formulation and the ability of dsRNA to traverse biological barriers and enter target insect cells (F. Khan et al. 2025; S. Yan et al. 2020; Y. Liu et al. 2025).

Several studies have investigated the application of RNAi in F. occidentalis using both feeding and injection‐based delivery approaches, with varying levels of success. Oral delivery through artificial diets (AD) has been shown to induce gene knockdown and affect insect development in some cases, including RNAi targeting insulin‐like peptide genes that resulted in developmental defects and reduced survival (Kim and Kim 2023). However, the efficiency of RNAi via feeding remains inconsistent and is strongly influenced by the composition of the feeding medium, which affects dsRNA stability and uptake. For example, different feeding solutions can result in substantial variation in gene silencing efficiency, with some formulations showing limited or no knockdown, while others improve RNAi outcomes (T. Zhang et al. 2022). In addition, ingested dsRNA may be degraded in the insect gut or fail to reach target tissues efficiently, thereby limiting RNAi efficacy. These limitations have led to the development of alternative delivery approaches such as micro‐ or nano‐injection, which allow direct delivery into the hemocoel and generally result in more consistent gene silencing responses (Raffin et al. 2022).

Topical application of dsRNA is an attractive option for crop protection, but it requires large‐scale synthesis of dsRNA (Jain et al. 2022; Mitter et al. 2017). In vitro synthesized dsRNA is not presently cost‐effective for field applications, but this issue can be overcome by production of dsRNA in mutant bacteria and using the purified dsRNA for large‐scale applications (Ma et al. 2020; Taning et al. 2016; Vatanparast and Kim 2017). The major disadvantage of using naked dsRNA in the environment is that dsRNA is highly labile and can be easily degraded by various factors including UV radiation as well as nuclease activity associated with insect feeding processes, posing a serious threat to its practical utility (Andongma et al. 2020; Fletcher et al. 2020). In F. occidentalis, salivary secretions and digestive processes play important roles in feeding and host interaction, and may contribute to the degradation of exogenous nucleic acids, thereby reducing RNAi efficiency (Li et al. 2025).

Another important factor is that the amount of foliar‐applied dsRNA/siRNAs transported to various cells gets diluted without a protective or uptake enhancement mechanism in place, which is a major obstacle for widespread use of this technology (Niu et al. 2018; Tenllado and Díaz‐Ruíz 2001). The issue has been elegantly overcome by the use of a nanoparticle‐based technology, where the dsRNA was mixed with nanosheets/polymer and used for spray applications (Mitter et al. 2017; Y. Yan et al. 2026). This technology has considerably improved the stability of the dsRNA in plants and has been found to offer effective protection against plant viruses and insects (Jain et al. 2022; Mitter et al. 2017).

In addition to efficient dsRNA delivery, the success of RNAi‐based pest management critically depends on the selection of suitable target genes capable of inducing strong biological effects following gene silencing. Previous studies across diverse insect orders have demonstrated that genes involved in essential physiological and cellular processes such as ion transport, intracellular signaling, chromatin regulation, development, and water homeostasis represent promising RNAi targets. However, the efficacy of target genes can vary considerably between insect species, highlighting the importance of systematic evaluation of candidate genes for species‐specific RNAi‐based pest management strategies (Cedden and Bucher 2025; Buer et al. 2024).

Despite growing interest in RNAi‐based management of WFT, critical translational gaps remain. Previous studies have either optimized delivery conditions without evaluating a broad gene target panel (Andongma et al. 2020), or assessed target lethality through plant‐mediated delivery systems that introduce confounding host‐plant variables (Han et al. 2019). In this study, we aimed to develop an RNAi‐based species‐specific biopesticide strategy for the management of WFT by identifying efficient gene targets and evaluating dsRNA delivery through AD feeding. In addition, we performed quantitative validation of gene knockdown, assessed dsRNA degradation during feeding, and standardized scalable production of dsRNA for future field applications. Using Cy3‐labeled dsRNA and confocal fluorescence imaging, we demonstrate the uptake of dsRNA by WFT through AD feeding and provide visual evidence of its internal distribution within the insect, thereby providing important insights for the development of RNAi‐based molecular biopesticides for the management of F. occidentalis.

Materials and Methods

Insect Culture and Identification

F. occidentalis (WFT) adults were collected from zucchini plants (Cucurbita pepo var. Regal Black) maintained in an evaporatively cooled glasshouse maintaining a day: night temperature of 25°C and 18°C, respectively, located at The University of Queensland, St Lucia, Brisbane, Australia. Collected WFT were identified based on morphological characters (OEPP/EPPO 2002). The morphological identity was further confirmed by sequencing the mitochondrial cytochrome oxidase subunit I (mtCOI) following the protocol followed by Macharia et al. (2015) using universal barcoding primer pairs, LCO1490 and HCO2198 (Folmer et al. 1994) at the Australian Genome Research Facility (Brisbane, Australia). WTF colonies were maintained on zucchini plants in insect‐proof cages in an environmental growth chamber (Conviron, Australia) at 26 ± 1°C with a day: night regimen of 16:8 h, respectively. Second‐generation insects were used for further studies.

Standardization of Thrips AD Feeding Assays

WFT AD feeding assays were standardized using three concentrations of sucrose at 10%, 25%, and 50% supplemented with either red color food grade dye (1% v/v) or with acridine orange (0.1% v/v). Insects (10 per replicate and three biological replications each) were released into small 35 mm cell culture plates (Nunc, Australia) and secured with a stretched layer of Parafilm. The respective diet (300 µL) was pipetted onto the surface of the Parafilm and secured with another layer of Parafilm to create a diet sachet. The insects were allowed to feed for 24 h, after which the insects fed with red dye were examined under a stereo microscope (Zeiss, Australia) to confirm feeding. Whereas, the insects fed with acridine orange were examined under a fluorescent microscope (Nikon, Japan) to confirm feeding by thrips.

Detection of dsRNA Uptake by Confocal Microscopy

To confirm the uptake of dsRNA by WFT in AD feeding, dsRNA was initially synthesized from a PCR amplified linear transgene encoding a Green fluorescent protein (GFP) expression cassette flanked by T7 polymerase recognition sequences using HiScribe T7 High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit (New England BioLabs Inc.) following the manufacturer's instructions. Prior to use, GFP‐dsRNA was purified using RNeasy Plus Mini kit (Qiagen, Australia) and labeled with Cy3 fluorescent dye (GE Life Sciences, AU) as per manufacturer's instructions. For bioassays, 200 ng of Cy3 labeled GFP‐dsRNA (Cy3‐GFP‐dsRNA) was mixed in 150 µL of 10% sucrose solution and fed to thrips as described above. Control thrips were fed with 10% sucrose solution only. After 24 h, the insects were examined under a confocal microscope (Leica, Germany) to confirm the uptake of dsRNA.

WFT Transcriptome Assembly and Annotation

Publicly available SRA data sets were assembled to capture expressed transcripts across developmental stages for RNAi target identification. Although reference genome assemblies of F. occidentalis are available, a de novo transcriptome assembly was generated to prioritize actively expressed genes relevant for functional RNAi screening. Unannotated F. occidentalis transcriptome files available in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRR) were used for assembly (SRR1462430, SRR7085636, SRR7140917, SRR1713042, SRR1826954, SRR1826955, SRR1826956, SRR1153466, SRR492945, and SRR1153458). Transcripts were assembled from processed read files using Trinity, and functional annotation of the transcript set was carried out according to the Trinity/Trinotate pipeline. Peptides were predicted by translating the longest open reading frames (ORFs) using TransDecoder. Homology searches were conducted with BlastP (peptide) and BlastX (transcript) against the SwissProt database. All resulting files were integrated into a Trinotate SQL database to generate a comprehensive annotation report.

Design of RNAi Constructs

To design RNAi constructs, seven gene targets were selected from the WFT transcriptome based on their reported efficacy in other insect groups, such as coleopteran, dipteran, lepidopteran, and thysanopteran insects, based on the available literature. The targets selected were vacuolar ATP synthase subunit B (V‐ATPase‐B), cactin (cact1—targeting the 3′ region and cact2—targeting the central region of the cactin gene), FACT complex (FACT), Ras opposite (ROP), fusilli (fus), and aquaporin (AQP) (Table 1). A local BLAST search for these genes was performed against the WFT transcriptome to find matching sequences using Geneious software. Following identification, the assessment of potential off‐target effects on non‐target organisms was undertaken essentially as per Jain et al. (2022). Briefly, transcriptome data sets of humans (Homo sapiens), honey bees (Apis mellifera, Apis florea, Apis dorsata, and Apis cerana), and the monarch butterfly (Danaus plexippus) were retrieved from the NCBI database. Jellyfish v2.3.0 was then used to identify regions with the fewest intersecting k‐mers within the non‐target organism data sets. Gene‐specific primers were subsequently designed using Primer 3 (https://primer3.ut.ee/) with T7 promoter sequences added at the 5’ end (Table 2) to amplify respective gene target expression cassettes from WFT cDNA for in vitro transcription of dsRNAs (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1).

RNA Extraction and cDNA Synthesis

Total RNA was isolated from ~20 adult thrips, which were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and homogenized using a hand‐held micro‐pestle in TRISure reagent (Meridian Biosciences, AU) following the manufacturer's instructions. Precipitation of RNA was by the standard isopropanol‐ethanol method, and the total yield was quantified using a NanoDrop 1000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). cDNA synthesis was carried out using a SensiFAST cDNA synthesis kit (Bioline, UK) with 1 µg of WFT extracted total RNA following the manufacturer's protocol. PCR cycling conditions were: primer annealing at 25°C for 10 min followed by reverse transcription at 42°C for 15 min, and enzyme inactivation at 85°C for 5 min followed by a hold at 4°C. The synthesized cDNA was stored at −20°C until required.

Synthesis of dsRNA

Amplification of linear templates by PCR was performed in 25 µL reactions containing 5× MyTaq Reaction Buffer, 1 U of MyTaq DNA Polymerase, 10 μM each of respective forward and reverse primer pairs (Table 2), and ~75 ng of cDNA. PCR conditions for all the gene targets were: initial denaturation at 95°C for 1 min, followed by 35 cycles at 95°C for 15 s, 58°C for 15 s, 72°C for 30 s, and a final extension step at 72°C for 10 min. Amplified products were resolved on 1% TAE agarose gel and visualized with ethidium bromide. Purified PCR products (1 µg) were used as linear in vitro transcription templates in 20 μL reactions incubated at 37° C for 3 h using HiScribe T7 High‐yield RNA synthesis kit (NEB) following manufacturer's instructions. The presence of opposing T7 promoters enabled simultaneous synthesis of sense and antisense RNA strands, which annealed during the reaction to form dsRNA. The synthesized dsRNA was treated with DNAse I digestion using TURBO DNase (Invitrogen), purified with TRIsure reagent, and precipitated following the standard ethanol‐sodium acetate method. Purified dsRNA was resuspended in nuclease‐free water, quantified using a NanoDrop 1000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and, after checking integrity by 1% TAE agarose electrophoresis (90 V, 1 h), was stored at −20°C for bioassay studies.

RNAi Feeding Assays

Thrips were fed with dsRNA targeting the selected genes at concentrations of 100 or 250 ng µL−1 in a total volume of 300 µL 10% sucrose solution. The experimental setup for the AD feeding assay was as described above in Section 2.2. The treatments included dsRNA of target genes including V‐ATPase‐B, ROP, FACT, cact1, cact2, fus, and AQP. Each treatment consisted of three independent biological replicates, with 10 insects per replicate maintained in separate feeding arenas. Control insects were fed either 10% sucrose solution supplemented with GFP‐dsRNA at the corresponding concentrations or a sucrose solution alone. Mortality was recorded daily for up to 7 days, after which the experiment was terminated.

Gene Expression Analysis by Quantitative Reverse Transcription PCR (RT‐qPCR)

Insects remaining alive in the comparatively less lethal treatments (AQP, cact2, FACT, and fus) from the bioassays treated with dsRNA at 250 ng µL−1 were collected after 7 days for gene expression analysis. Due to the comparatively high mortality observed in V‐ATPase‐B and cact1 treatments, sufficient surviving insects were not available for RNA extraction. Therefore, RT‐qPCR analysis was focused on the less lethal treatments to verify whether gene silencing had occurred despite the relatively lower mortality observed in these treatments. Collected insects were snap frozen in liquid N2, and total RNA was extracted using TRIsure reagent following manufacturer's instructions. Total RNA was also extracted from insects fed with GFP‐dsRNA and sucrose, which served as non‐target and negative controls, respectively. RT‐qPCR was performed with target‐specific qPCR primers (Table 3). The qPCR primers were designed so that their target does not match the target region of the respective dsRNA effectors. The level of gene expression in treatment groups was assessed using the SensiFAST SYBR No‐ROX One‐Step kit (Bioline, AU) as per manufacturer's instructions on a Rotor‐Gene Q platform (QIAGEN). Briefly, a 20 µL reaction for each target gene contained 2× SensiFAST SYBR No‐ROX One‐Step Mix, 10 μM each of forward and reverse primers (Table 3), 0.2 μL of reverse transcriptase, 0.4 μL of RiboSafe RNase inhibitor, and 5 ng of template RNA in PCR‐grade water. RT‐qPCR was carried out with the following 3‐step cycling conditions: reverse transcription for 10 min at 45°C, polymerase activation for 2 min at 95°C, then 40 cycles of denaturation for 5 s at 95°C, primer annealing for 10 s at 60°C, and extension for 5 s at 72°C. There were three biological replicates and two technical replicates for each target region. Transcript normalization was done with Actin gene (Badillo‐Vargas et al. 2015), and relative expression of each target gene was calculated using the ΔΔC t method (Pfaffl 2001) and compared with non‐target control treatment.

Degradation of dsRNA Used in Feeding Experiments

dsRNA of different target genes from RNAi feeding assays 7 days post treatment (dpt) was collected and stored at −20°C to assess the possible degradation of dsRNA due to thrips feeding activity. Leftover dsRNA‐supplemented AD from assays that had run for 7 days targeting genes FACT, Fus, Cact1, and AQP were run on 1% agarose gel along with unfed dsRNA of respective genes maintained under identical conditions and used as controls. The degradation of dsRNA was visualized after staining the gel with ethidium bromide.

Expression of dsRNA in Bacteria

For bacterial expression of dsRNA, the V‐ATPse‐B gene fragment was cloned into the Pst I/Nhe I site of the L4440 vector MCS which has flanking T7 promoters following the methodology of Tian et al. (2009) with modifications. Briefly, forward and reverse primers (Thrips PstI‐F ‐ AAAA CTGCAG GGACCTTTGGTGATTTTGGA and Thrips NheI R‐ CTA GCTAGC AACACAGATTTGCCTGGGAC) substituted with overhangs and restriction enzyme (RE) sites, pstI and NheI, respectively (underlined portions) (Badillo‐Vargas et al. 2015) were used to amplify the V‐ATPase‐B gene fragment from total cDNA synthesized above. Second round gel‐purified PCR amplicons and the L4440 expression vector were digested with Pst I and Nhe I high‐fidelity enzymes (New England BioLabs Inc.) and ligated using standard protocols. Ligation reactions were transformed into DH10B cells following the standard heat shock protocol and a clone selected by growth on Luria‐Bertani (LB)‐Ampicillin (100 μg mL−1) agar plates and by digestion of extracted L4440‐V‐ATPase‐B with Pst I and Nhe I. The respective confirmed clone was transformed into Escherichia coli HT115 bacteria lacking RNase III, which expresses T7 polymerase upon induction with isopropyl β‐d‐thiogalactoside (IPTG), and constitution of the expression plasmid reconfirmed by Pst I and Nhe I digestion.

For dsRNA production, E. coli HT115 (L4440—V‐ATPase‐B) was grown to late log phase in Terrific Broth media supplemented with 100 μg mL−1 ampicillin and 12.5 μg mL−1 tetracycline in a ventilated flask at 37°C in a shaking incubator at 250 rpm. A 1/100 volume was used to inoculate the expression culture, which was not more than 10% of the vessel volume (200 mL in a 2 L flask). The expression culture was incubated at 37°C in a shaking incubator at 250 rpm until the 0.4 OD600. At this point, IPTG was added at a concentration of 0.4 mM to induce T7 polymerase and incubated at the above conditions for another 4 h. The bacteria were pelleted, and the expressed V‐ATPase‐B‐dsRNA was extracted following the TRIsure protocol as described above. The extracted dsRNA was confirmed by resolution on a 1% TAE agarose gel along with in vitro synthesized dsRNA and empty L4440 vector as controls.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism Version 8.0 for Windows, GraphPad Software, La Jolla, California, USA. Mean and standard error were calculated for each data set. Percentage mortality data were subjected to arcsine transformation prior to analysis, and statistical differences among treatments were determined by one‐way ANOVA followed by Fisher's LSD test. Relative gene expression data were analyzed using an unpaired two‐tailed t‐test with Welch's correction.

Results

Insect Identification

F. occidentalis was identified based on diagnostic taxonomic characters, including eight‐segmented antennae, fully developed wings, ocellar setae positioned outside the ocellar triangle, and the morphology of the terminal abdominal segment (Supporting Information S1: Figure S2). The mtCOI region sequence of the insect, deposited in GenBank under accession number PX248635, exhibited 100% identity with multiple F. occidentalis sequences in the NCBI database, confirming the species identity.

Standardization of Artificial Feeding and dsRNA Uptake by Thrips

In our preliminary studies to standardize the AD for feeding WFT, it was observed that among the sucrose concentrations, the insects fed on 10% sucrose AD within 24 h, compared to other, higher concentrations of sucrose. The feeding of sucrose was confirmed by the presence of red dye or acridine orange in the gut of the insect as observed by stereo or fluorescent microscopy (Figure 1). This concentration of sucrose (10%) was used in all our future feeding assays. Further, in order to investigate the uptake and distribution of dsRNA by thrips via AD, a sucrose diet was supplemented with Cy3‐GFP‐dsRNA, fed to thrips, and subsequently observed by confocal microscopy. The distribution of Cy3 labeled dsRNA inside the body of the insect, especially in the gut region of the insect, could clearly be observed by the emitted fluorescent signals, in contrast to control insects, which were negative for fluorescence (Figure 2). The results of the experiment demonstrated the effective ingestion of dsRNA by WFT through AD.

RNAi Feeding Assays

Our initial studies indicated that there was no significant mortality among the treatments except in the case of V‐ATPase‐B‐dsRNA treatment (> 40% mortality) (data not shown) when treated at 100 ng µL−1. However, when the dose of the targets was increased to 250 ng µL−1, a significant increase in mortality was observed among the treatments when compared with the controls (GFP‐dsRNA and 10% sucrose) (Figure 3). Also, there were significant differences in mortality among the different dsRNA molecules tested (F = 19.06; p < 0.001; df = 8.18). Thrips mortality (mean ± SE) caused by different gene targets varied from 26.5 ± 2.1% to 53.3 ± 3.3%. More than 50% mortality was observed in the thrips treated with V‐ATPase‐B‐dsRNA and Cact1‐dsRNA. This was followed by ROP‐, fus‐, AQP‐, and cact2‐dsRNA treatments, whereas the control mortality in case of GFP‐dsRNA and sucrose treatments was 12.9 ± 1.4% and 6.9 ± 3.7%, respectively (Figure 3).

Gene Knock Down in Insects

Feeding WFT with dsRNA of different target genes led to a reduction in mRNA levels of the corresponding genes. Significant difference in target mRNA expression compared to GFP‐dsRNA control was observed in insects treated with dsRNA of AQP (t = 8.976; df = 2.929; p = 0.0032), Cact2 (t = 20.51; df = 2.0; p = 0.0024), and FACT (t = 6.417; df = 2.513; p = 0.0128). Although insects treated with Fusilli dsRNA showed approximately 2.0‐fold reduction in transcript level (~50.5% knockdown), the reduction was not statistically significant (t = 1.108; df = 2.597; p = 0.3598). Reduction in transcript levels in insects up to 4.8 (AQP), 4.3 (cact2), and 3.5‐fold (FACT) compared to GFP‐dsRNA control was observed (Figure 4).

Degradation of dsRNA Used in Feeding Experiments

Usually, dsRNA is stable under room temperature conditions in the absence of nucleases. However, to ascertain if there is any possibility of degradation of dsRNA due to thrips feeding activity, like in other insects, residual sucrose diet containing dsRNA of different targets (7 dpt) from the dsRNA feeding experiment was resolved on a gel along with assay control dsRNA (Figure 5). It was observed that dsRNA of target genes (FACT, fus, Cact1, and AQP) fed to WFT was found to be completely degraded post‐feeding by thrips. Prior to feeding by thrips, the concentration of dsRNA was 250 ng µL−1 and post‐feeding, when resolved on gel, they were observed only as degraded smears. Whereas, the corresponding unfed diet containing dsRNA stored under identical conditions was found to be intact and did not show any sign of degradation (Figure 5), suggesting that thrips feeding activity contributed to dsRNA degradation.

One μL each of the remaining and control dsRNA diets of different targets (7 dpt) from the experiment was loaded on the gel for visualizing dsRNA degradation. Sucrose served as a negative control.

Expression of dsRNA in Bacteria

In vitro transcription of dsRNA is relatively expensive for large‐scale field experiments. To overcome this issue, we synthesized dsRNA in a bacterial system. For this, initially, the V‐ATPase‐B gene fragment was cloned to L4440 vector. The cloned product was then transformed to E. coli HT115 bacteria, dsRNA expression induced with IPTG, dsRNA subsequently extracted and compared with in vitro transcribed dsRNA (Figure 6). A dsRNA yield of about 655 ± 24.5 μg was obtained from 20 mL of recombinant bacterial broth culture in our experiments. Our results demonstrate the feasibility of large‐scale production of dsRNA of potential gene targets of thrips using bacterial expression systems.

Discussion

dsRNA‐mediated gene silencing, also known as RNAi, has been a breakthrough technology for functional genomic studies and represents a potential tool for the management of insect pests. Since the discovery of RNAi, numerous studies show the introduction of exogenously synthesized dsRNA or siRNA into an organism is able to trigger highly efficient gene silencing through interference of endogenous mRNA homologous to the presented siRNA (Hoang et al. 2022). RNAi‐based control strategies for cell‐content feeding insects especially thrips, has the potential to be developed into next‐generation thrips‐specific molecular biopesticides. However, it is essential to identify suitable target genes (Bilgi et al. 2017), to develop sprayable formulations for longer stability on plants (Christiaens et al. 2018), and to define the mechanisms of translocation from plants to insects for sustained protection against target pests and potentially vectored pathogens (Jain et al. 2022; Mitter et al. 2017).

Accurate analysis of gene silencing in a target insect can be achieved only if the uptake of dsRNA is confirmed in the insect, or an underestimation of target gene silencing will occur (Bilgi et al. 2017). For initial screening of effective RNAi targets, the use of sucrose‐based AD feeding is preferred since it is high throughput, logistically easy to set up, and feeding can be observed using non‐toxic diet additions such as dyes (Andongma et al. 2020). In the current study, we evaluated the ability of thrips to feed in the AD system and to take up dsRNA by supplementing a basic sucrose diet with an inexpensive food dye indicator, or Cy3 labeled dsRNA. Among the various concentrations of sucrose diet, we screened, the insects preferably fed on 10% sucrose diet compared to the other concentrations, evident by the presence of food dye in the insect gut. Our study was consistent with other researchers who have successfully used sucrose‐based diets with various markers to screen dietary delivery of various dsRNA to insects, including WFT (Andongma et al. 2020; Bilgi et al. 2017; Ghosh et al. 2017; Jain et al. 2022; Singh et al. 2019; Whitten et al. 2016)

To demonstrate the cellular uptake and biodistribution of dsRNA in thrips, dsRNA was labeled with the fluorescent dye Cy3 and its localization in insect tissues following feeding was examined using confocal microscopy. Fluorescent labeling of dsRNA to confirm uptake has been successfully demonstrated in other insects, including the Asian long‐horned beetle and whitefly (Dhandapani et al. 2020; Jain et al. 2022). In the present study, Cy3 fluorescence was detected within the digestive tract of F. occidentalis following AD feeding, confirming ingestion and internal distribution of dsRNA. These observations are consistent with previous reports of dsRNA uptake via oral delivery in insects, and provide direct visual confirmation of dsRNA uptake under AD feeding conditions in F. occidentalis.

The success of RNAi‐induced knockdown critically depends on selecting vital gene targets (Dhandapani et al. 2020; A. M. Khan et al. 2018). The efficiency of these targets is further influenced by factors such as the essentiality of the target gene, the efficiency of transcript knockdown (Knorr et al. 2018), the target's transcriptional efficiency, and the dose of the RNA molecule (Scott et al. 2013) as well as the mode of delivery. We chose several gene targets for our thrips oral feeding assay based on their reported efficiency in WFT and other insects including, Tribolium castaneum and B. tabaci, when delivered either by micro‐injection (Badillo‐Vargas et al. 2015; Bingsohn et al. 2017; Knorr et al. 2018; Ulrich et al. 2015) or by expression in transgenic plants (Raza et al. 2016).

Among the selected gene targets, vacuolar ATP synthase subunit B (V‐ATPase‐B) has been successfully demonstrated as an effective RNAi target in several insects including WFT. V‐ATPase is essential for nutrient uptake and ion balance in insects and knockdown of this gene causes significant mortality in insects (Badillo‐Vargas et al. 2015). We recorded more than 50% mortality in WFT fed with V‐ATPase‐B dsRNA at 7 dpt, which was consistent with the earlier reports of Badillo‐Vargas et al. (2015) who recorded 55% reduction in survivorship of V‐ATPase‐B dsRNA‐injected thrips at 14 dpt. A similar level of mortality has been observed in Thrips palmi fed with dsV‐ATPase‐B (Rakesh et al. 2024) proving the effectiveness of this target across various species of thrips.

Cactin (cact) and fusilli (fus) are part of highly conserved Toll signaling pathway. Cactin plays a major role in embryonic and larval development in insects. Earlier studies have indicated that knockdown of cactin gene resulted in ~50% adult mortality in T. castaneum (Bingsohn et al. 2017). Interestingly, the two dsRNA constructs targeting distinct regions of the cactin gene produced markedly different outcomes. The Cact1 dsRNA, designed against the 3′ region of the transcript, induced > 50% mortality, whereas Cact2, targeting the middle region, caused only ~27% mortality despite eliciting comparably high transcript knockdown (~77%). A dissociation between the degree of gene knockdown and lethal outcome has been reported previously in T. castaneum following cactin silencing (Bingsohn et al. 2017), and region‐dependent differences in dsRNA efficacy have similarly been observed in mosquitoes, where constructs targeting the 3′ region of an apoptosis gene were more lethal than those targeting the 5′ or middle regions (Pridgeon et al. 2008). The basis for this effect in WFT remains unclear, but may reflect sequence‐dependent variation in RNAi response or variation in the biological consequences of silencing distinct regions of the target transcript. These findings further underscore the importance of screening multiple dsRNA constructs derived from different regions of a candidate gene and caution against relying on a single construct when assessing RNAi target potential, even where strong transcript knockdown is confirmed (Joga et al. 2016).

Fusilli has been identified as an essential gene in maternal dorsoventral asymmetry and embryonic viability in Drosophilla melanogaster (Wakabayashi‐Ito et al. 2001) and has been reported to be lethal to early developmental stages of T. castaneum (Bingsohn et al. 2017). Contrastingly, the knockdown efficiency of WFT fus gene homologue causing mortality was less effective in comparison with the other Toll signaling pathway gene, cactin.

The Ras opposite (Rop) gene is responsible for essential biological processes such as vesicle trafficking and membrane fusion (Knorr et al. 2018). Earlier RNAi studies conducted with Rop dsRNA had resulted in > 45% and 80% mortality in Meligethes aeneus and T. castaneum, respectively at 6 dpt (Knorr et al. 2018; Ulrich et al. 2015). In our study, we also observed ~40% mortality in the test insects fed with Rop dsRNA.

AQP are osmoregulatory genes which maintain osmotic homeostasis in insects and have been found to be promising targets in phloem‐feeding insects (Jain et al. 2022; Singh et al. 2019; Tzin et al. 2015). However, in our study, the knockdown of AQP gene did not result in high mortality. The FACT (facilitates chromatin transactions) complex interacts with the nucleosomes and are essential for optimal transcription efficiency (Belotserkovskaya and Reinberg 2004). In our studies, though the knockdown of AQP and FACT complex genes were up to 70% and 60%, respectively in the treated insects, but did not translate into high mortality (> 40%) as observed in previous studies (Han et al. 2019; Knorr et al. 2018).

In our study, the mortality observed in the GFP‐dsRNA control insects (12.9 ± 1.4%) was higher than that of the sucrose‐only control (6.9 ± 3.7%). This disparity between controls may partly reflect physiological stress associated with confinement in the AD arena and restricted access to plant tissue, independent of any dsRNA‐mediated effect. Similar background mortality has been reported in confined AD feeding systems for other small insects (Andongma et al. 2020), and is an acknowledged limitation of this bioassay format. Accordingly, the GFP‐dsRNA treatment was included as the primary baseline for interpreting gene‐specific mortality, allowing non‐specific effects of dsRNA ingestion and feeding conditions to be partitioned from target‐mediated silencing effects. The significantly elevated mortality observed in V‐ATPase‐B and Cact1 dsRNA treatments above this GFP‐dsRNA baseline confirms that lethality in these treatments reflects genuine RNAi‐mediated gene silencing rather than an artefact of the diet system. Future studies involving larger sample sizes may further improve the interpretation of comparatively modest mortality responses observed among candidate gene targets.

Among the several factors that influence the efficiency of RNAi in insects, upregulation of the target gene and efficient degradation of dsRNA by nucleases or other biological factors associated with insect feeding are of paramount importance, which may lead to incomplete or partial silencing of the target gene (S. Liu et al. 2020). We observed degradation of the dsRNA, 7 days post‐feeding in AD. Similar observation on the rapid degradation of naked dsRNA by feeding activity has been reported earlier in WFT (Andongma et al. 2020), Acyrthosiphon pisum and other hemipterans (Allen and Walker 2012; Cao et al. 2018). Though the feeding behavior of thrips is different from hemipterans, the salivary proteins of WFT were found to be similar with a potential role in extra‐oral digestion (Stafford‐Banks et al. 2014). Therefore, it is likely that degradation of naked dsRNA occurs during the feeding process, reducing the effective availability of dsRNA for uptake by the insect.

Nevertheless, high mortality by certain gene targets despite potential degradation of dsRNA during feeding could be due to the essential nature of the target gene, where even partial silencing significantly reduces the fitness of the host (S. Liu et al. 2020). Our studies highlight that dsRNA stability is adversely affected during exposure to thrips feeding activity, which may negatively influence RNAi efficacy over time. The results further emphasize the need for protection of dsRNA and development of appropriate formulations to prevent dsRNA degradation and improve its stability during the feeding process. Since the present study was conducted using an AD feeding system under controlled laboratory conditions, future studies on host plants will be necessary to determine whether plant‐associated factors such as nucleases, secondary metabolites, and natural feeding behavior influence dsRNA uptake and RNAi efficacy under more realistic conditions.

Recently, nanoparticle‐based delivery systems have emerged as a promising approach to enhance the stability and efficacy of dsRNA in RNAi applications. A range of nanoparticle and polymer‐assisted formulations have been shown to protect dsRNA from degradation by nucleases present in insect gut fluids and other biological environments, thereby improving RNAi efficiency and insect mortality (Bhadrapriya et al. 2023). In addition to improving cellular uptake, these delivery systems can enhance dsRNA persistence by shielding it from enzymatic degradation during feeding and transport within the insect. For example, polymer‐clay hybrid delivery systems such as the BenPol platform have demonstrated improved dsRNA stability in larval midgut fluids and serum‐containing environments, resulting in enhanced RNAi responses following oral delivery in insects (Y. Yan et al. 2026). Similarly, layered double hydroxide (LDH)‐based systems and other nanoparticle‐assisted formulations have been reported to improve dsRNA stability, protection, and delivery efficiency in both plants and insects (Jain et al. 2022). These studies suggest that nanoparticle‐mediated delivery approaches may help address key barriers associated with naked dsRNA, including degradation by salivary, gut, and environmental nucleases, and warrant further investigation for improving RNAi efficacy in thrips.

Although improving dsRNA stability and delivery remains essential for enhancing RNAi performance, a deeper mechanistic understanding of dsRNA processing within the insect is equally important for optimizing RNAi efficacy. The present study evaluated RNAi efficacy primarily through phenotypic bioassays and RT‐qPCR‐based validation of target gene knockdown, but emerging molecular approaches may provide deeper insights into RNAi responses in insects. Recent studies have highlighted the utility of advanced techniques such as RISC‐bound small RNA sequencing, which enables direct assessment of dsRNA processing, guide strand selection, and identification of functionally active siRNAs associated with the RNA‐induced silencing complex. Integration of such approaches in future studies may improve understanding of RNAi efficiency and facilitate the design of more effective RNAi‐based pest management strategies (Cedden 2026).

For large‐scale field applications, the cost‐effective production of dsRNA remains a critical requirement. In this context, bacterial expression systems based on plasmid‐engineered E. coli have been optimized and demonstrated as viable platforms for large‐scale dsRNA production (Ma et al. 2020; Vatanparast and Kim 2017). In the present study, we standardized dsRNA production using a recombinant bacterial system, which provides a scalable and economically feasible approach for generating dsRNA for future field applications. Although bacterially produced dsRNA has been successfully used for RNAi applications in insects (Ahn et al. 2019; Taning et al. 2016), its efficacy in inducing RNAi responses in F. occidentalis was not evaluated in our study and warrants further investigation. Together, these findings support future development of practical RNAi‐based molecular biopesticides for the management of F. occidentalis.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the potential of RNAi as a targeted and effective strategy for the management of WFT (F. occidentalis). Using an AD system, we confirmed ingestion and cellular uptake of dsRNA in F. occidentalis and validated through Cy3‐labeled imaging. Among the gene targets tested, V‐ATPase‐B and cactin induced significant mortality, highlighting their potential as candidates for RNAi‐based pest control. However, variability in knockdown efficiency and mortality across other targets highlights the need for careful gene selection and region‐specific dsRNA design.

Importantly, we observed degradation of naked dsRNA over time during feeding, indicating reduced dsRNA stability that may limit RNAi efficacy. This finding emphasizes the need for protective formulations to enhance dsRNA stability and delivery. Nanoparticle‐based delivery systems may provide future opportunities to improve dsRNA stability and reduce degradation during delivery and feeding, which warrants further investigation. Additionally, the successful standardization of bacterial dsRNA production provides a scalable and cost‐effective platform for potential field applications.

Overall, the successful application of RNAi in thrips will depend on a multifaceted strategy integrating effective target selection, improved delivery systems, and scalable dsRNA production. These findings contribute to the development of RNAi‐based molecular biopesticides and provide a foundation for future translational research in sustainable pest management.
