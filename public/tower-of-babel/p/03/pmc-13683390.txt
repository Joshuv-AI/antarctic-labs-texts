Dynamic contrast-enhanced ultrasound predicts microsphere biodistribution in real-time during transarterial radioembolization
Authors: J. L. van der Hoek, T. J. Snoeijink, A. Visser, K. D. E. de Bree, M. E. Krommendijk, J. G. M. Greve, H. R. Liefers, W. M. Brink, M. Versluis, J. Arens, S. Manohar, E. Groot Jebbink
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTransarterial radioembolization (TARE) treatment efficacy critically depends on the microsphere biodistribution in the liver. Current treatment planning protocols rely on a pre-treatment scout procedure to evaluate this biodistribution for a selected catheter configuration. However, replication of the catheter configuration during treatment is challenging and often results in suboptimal tumor targeting. Here we present and experimentally validate a concept to improve tumor targeting using dynamic contrast-enhanced ultrasound (DCE-US) imaging to predict the microsphere biodistribution in real-time, using ultrasound contrast microbubbles as microsphere precursors.MethodsDCE-US-guided TARE was performed in three ex vivo perfused porcine livers using a customized normothermic machine-perfusion platform. In each liver, one lobe was selected for targeting, and a 9L4 ultrasound transducer was fixed to image this location. A microcatheter was inserted in the hepatic artery under fluoroscopic guidance. Several catheter positions were analyzed in real-time using ultrasound contrast microbubbles and time-intensity curves (TICs). The catheter position was optimized based on peak TIC intensity, which was retained for subsequent injection of non-irradiated 165Ho-microspheres. The resulting microsphere biodistribution was evaluated by R2* mapping in MRI.ResultsHere we show that DCE-US can be used to identify feeding arteries, enabling optimization of the catheter placement to maximize peak intensity within the target lobe. MRI confirms the relative microsphere biodistribution as predicted by DCE-US in two quantifiable cases (Case 2: 99.2% [MRI] vs. 98% [DCE-US]; Case 3: 70.2% [MRI] vs. 80.9% [DCE-US]).ConclusionsDCE-US enables accurate prediction of microsphere biodistribution, providing real-time feedback for immediate intra-operative optimization of the catheter position during TARE.

Introduction

Transarterial radioembolisation (TARE) is an established locoregional treatment for unresectable and chemorefractory liver cancer1. The procedure involves the transcatheter injection of microspheres, which contain the radioactive isotope yttrium-90 (90Y) or holmium-166 (166Ho), into the hepatic artery or its branches2–4. Due to their size (typical average diameter of 25–32 µm5), these microspheres lodge in the hepatic arterioles, interrupting the local blood flow while simultaneously delivering targeted radiation over time.

TARE treatment efficacy is directly related to the realized microsphere biodistribution. After microsphere injection, a high tumor-absorbed dose with a low non-tumor-absorbed dose (high T/N ratio) is associated with a higher median overall survival. A sub-analysis of the SARAH trial demonstrated that patients receiving a median tumor-absorbed dose of ≥ 100 Gy had a significantly longer median overall survival (14.1 months) compared to those receiving <100 Gy (6.1 months)6, while a mean non-tumor-absorbed dose of ≤ 40 Gy is recommended for safety7. Although microspheres exhibit a natural tendency to accumulate in tumors due to their enhanced arterial vascularity8, the microsphere biodistribution is significantly influenced by the catheter configuration used, including injection flow velocity9–11, injection flow profile12,13 and catheter tip location14–16. Consequently, the choice of catheter configuration for TARE has a direct impact on treatment outcome.

Current treatment planning strategies for TARE involve injecting scout particles using the candidate catheter configuration, typically one to two weeks before the treatment procedure. In this scout procedure, technetium-99m macroaggregated albumin (99mTc-MAA) beads (95% between 10 – 100 µm diameter17) are commonly used as scout particles, which can be visualized by single-photon emission computed tomography/computed tomography (SPECT/CT) to assess their biodistribution18,19. Alternatively, it was shown that a small amount of 166Ho-microspheres can also be used as a scout dose for 166Ho-TARE20. The use of identical particles in both the scout and therapeutic procedures, combined with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) dosimetry enabled by the paramagnetic properties of 166Ho-microspheres21, was shown to improve the prediction of intrahepatic microsphere biodistribution22.

The predictive value of the scout procedure is compromised, however, by the long time interval between the scout and therapeutic TARE procedures (typically 1–2 weeks). This increases the likelihood of changes in hemodynamics at the time of treatment (e.g., alterations in blood pressure or hepatic arterial flow distribution due to tumor progression) and errors in replicating the chosen catheter configuration, resulting in suboptimal microsphere deposition23,24. Moreover, the scout procedure is limited to evaluating a single injection strategy per injection location, as accumulated microsphere lodging complicates iterative optimization of the catheter configuration.

We propose an augmented TARE protocol that integrates real-time ultrasound feedback by using ultrasound-contrast microbubbles as microsphere precursors for improved control over the microsphere biodistribution. Our recently conducted in-vitro study demonstrated that ultrasound-contrast microbubbles and non-radioactive 165Ho-microspheres follow similar trajectories downstream of the first major arterial bifurcations when injected using the same catheter configuration25. Given that these microbubbles are sufficiently small to pass through the capillary bed (mean diameter of 2.5 µm, with 90% of all microbubbles smaller than 8 µm26), unlike embolizing microspheres, sequential microbubble injections can be used to iteratively optimize the catheter configuration for improved tumor targeting during TARE. The resulting microbubble biodistribution can then be quantified using dynamic contrast-enhanced ultrasound (DCE-US), thereby establishing what we term the DCE-US-guided TARE approach. However, it remains to be shown whether microbubbles can accurately predict the microsphere biodistribution in a realistic perfused liver model.

This study presents a proof-of-concept for DCE-US guided TARE in healthy ex-vivo machine-perfused porcine livers. We show that real-time DCE-US feedback can be used to optimize catheter positioning for targeting specific regions. Microsphere injections after optimization produce depositions that correlate with the DCE-US predictions, demonstrating that microbubbles can accurately predict the microsphere biodistribution. These results suggest that DCE-US could be a promising tool to improve treatment planning for TARE, although the findings are preliminary and clinical feasibility remains to be established.

Methods

Approach

The envisioned iterative optimization of the catheter configuration in DCE-US-guided TARE is illustrated in Fig. 1. Three healthy porcine livers were used to demonstrate the augmented TARE procedure. An arbitrary location, such as part of a liver lobe, was designated as the target region to which the microsphere biodistribution should be confined. An ultrasound transducer was fixed to image this region (Fig. 1A) to evaluate catheter configurations in terms of their ability to deliver microbubbles to the target region (Fig. 1B). Real-time quantification of the microbubble presence in tissue was performed using DCE-US and time intensity curve (TIC) analysis (Fig. 1C, D). Organ and blood procurement

Porcine livers were retrieved from a local slaughterhouse (Vion Groenlo BV, Groenlo, The Netherlands). Slaughtering was done following the guidelines of the Netherlands Food and Consumer Product Safety Authority (NVWA). The liver was prepared according to a custom protocol inspired by the procurement protocol for human livers of XVIVO (XVIVO B.V., Groningen, The Netherlands), which is described by Snoeijink et al.27. Within 30 minutes from cardiac arrest28, the portal vein (PV) was cannulated and the inferior vena cava was dissected in order to flush the liver through the PV cannula with a cold (1–5 °C) saline solution (NaCl 0.9% infusion solution, B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany). The flush included an initial 1 L of heparinized (Heparin-Natrium LEO, LEO Pharma GmbH, Ballerup, Denmark) saline solution (25.000 IU/L) to prevent blood clot formation in the liver vasculature, followed by 2 L of non-heparinized saline solution to flush out remaining blood. After cannulation of the PV and initiation of the flush, the liver was isolated and put on ice for transport. For the perfusate, 5 to 10 L of non-autologous blood was collected as well, with additives including 6 mL/L of saline solution, 1.8 mL/L of 50% glucose solution (G-50, B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany), and 15.000 IU/L heparin.

After transport, the aorta was cannulated on ice, and all arteries from the aorta up to the bifurcation of the hepatic artery (HA) were ligated, such that the blood supply through the aorta and HA was only directed to branches that supplied the liver27. Excess tissue was dissected, and the liver was weighed (Kitchen scale 15 kg, Hendi, De Klomp, The Netherlands). Cold ischemia time was limited to 4 hours, including transport.

The liver condition was investigated after liver preparation by using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). A 1.5 T MRI (MAGNETOM Aera, Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) with an 18-channel body array coil and a 32-channel spine coil was used to acquire anatomical images using a three-dimensional T1-weighted DIXON sequence. Sequence parameters were: TR/TE1/TE2 = 6.75/2.39/4.77 ms, flip angle = 10°, field-of-view = 400 × 364 mm, voxel size = 1.1 × 1.1 × 1.1 mm, number of slices = 128.

Machine perfusion: the LiverTwin

An experimental machine perfusion platform developed in-house, coined the LiverTwin, was used for ex-vivo normothermic perfusion of porcine livers. The main components of the system are shown in Supplementary Materials 1(Fig. S1). For the HA side, flow was facilitated by an iLA active console (I) (Xenios AG, Heilbronn, Germany) with a ¼ inch DP3 mixed-flow rotary pump head (II), which can provide a pulsatile flow with control over flow rate, amplitude, frequency, and systolic fraction. For the PV side, flow was facilitated by a Maquet Rotaflow pump console (III) (Maquet Cardiopulmonary GmbH, Rastatt, Germany) with a ⅜ inch centrifugal pump head (IV), which can regulate non-pusatile flow rates. Two hollow-fiber oxygenators with integrated heat exchangers were used: on the HA side a PLS-i (V) (Maquet, Rastatt, Germany) and on the PV side a MiniLung petite (VI) (MEDOS Medizintechnik AG, Stolberg, Germany). The blood temperature was regulated by the integrated heat exchangers and a Hico Variotherm 550 heater/cooler system (VII) (Hico Medical Systems, Köln, Germany).

The dual blood supply to the liver was facilitated by creating the flow circuits as shown in Fig. 2. The liver was placed in a custom-built reservoir (1) with a height-adjustable organ net that facilitates multimodal medical imaging. The liver remained blood-submerged throughout the experiment to support MR imaging and prevent dehydration of the superficial tissue. The outlet connections of the reservoir were located at the base to ensure direct recirculation of sedimenting red blood cells. From the reservoir, blood is pumped through two separate blood flow circuits for the HA and PV side using rotary blood pumps (2a, 2b), which was then directed to the oxygenators (3a, 3b). Tempered water was supplied to both oxygenators by the heater/cooler system to maintain a blood temperature of 37 °C (4). Compressed air (21% O2) was supplied to the oxygenator on the HA side (5), whereas the oxygenator on the PV side only functioned as a heat-exchanger to mimic the lower oxygen saturation in the PV29. Ultrasound clamp-on flow sensors (Sonoflow CO.55/140 v2.0, Sonotec GmbH, Halle, Germany) were installed downstream of the oxygenators on both the HA and PV side (6a, 6b). This placement enabled correction for flow damping caused by the HA side oxygenator, ensuring that the correct flow profile reached the arterial liver vasculature. The blood pressure was monitored by including sphygmomanometers (Durashock DS54, Welch Allyn, Skaneateles Falls, US) on both sides (7a, 7b), attaching the sensors to pressure tubing (Edwards Lifesciences B.V., Breda, The Netherlands) that was connected close to the HA and PV cannulas.Fig. 2Schematic overview of the setup used for ex-vivo liver perfusion.Flow circuits for ex-vivo normothermic machine perfusion of porcine livers, including the hepatic arterial (HA) and portal venous (PV) blood flow circuits for the dual blood supply to the liver, a gas supply to the HA oxygenator, and the heat exchange flow circuits connected to both oxygenators.

Fluoroscopy: catheter navigation and analysis points

The clinical radioembolization setting was replicated by conducting the experiments in a hybrid operating room, as shown in Fig. 3. The microbubbles and microspheres were injected using a microcatheter (Progreat 2.7 Fr. 130 cm, Terumo Interventional Systems, Zaventem, Belgium) that is commonly used in TARE22. The microcatheter was inserted through a hemostatic valve installed directly upstream of the arterial cannula and advanced within the arterial vasculature to investigate multiple injection positions. Navigation of the microcatheter was performed under fluoroscopic guidance (ARTIS Pheno, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) was performed using an iodine-based contrast agent (Iomeron® 300 mg/ml, Bracco Imaging, Milan, Italy), administering 60 mL at 3 mL/s. Dynamic contrast-enhanced ultrasound: prediction of 165Ho-microsphere biodistribution

Before each experiment, a target region was selected based on an initial DSA to represent a treatment area within the healthy liver tissue. A 9L4 ultrasound transducer connected to the Acuson S2000 ultrasound system (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) was fixed to image at this location, enabling assessment of a chosen catheter position in its ability to target the treatment area. During the fixation of the transducer, its positioning was carefully selected for the presence of major vessels, which may introduce signal non-linearity and should be avoided in DCE-US comparison31. In experiments 2 and 3, a second 9L4 transducer was fixed to image at a location close to the target site, serving as a reference site that represents healthy liver tissue where microsphere deposition should be avoided (Fig. 4). This reference transducer was used solely for experimental quantification and comparison and is not envisioned for use in the clinical setting.Fig. 4Overview of the target and reference ultrasound probe fixation.Photo of an ex vivo perfused porcine liver used for development of the methods, showing two fixed 9L4 ultrasound transducers positioned at the target and reference sites for optimized targeting while monitoring off-target deposition in an adjacent lobe.

Contrast-enhanced ultrasound imaging was performed using the clinical Acuson S2000 ultrasound system. A non-linear pulsing scheme, Contrast Pulse Sequencing, was used, with a power of 0.25%, transmit frequency of 4 MHz, imaging depth of 3.5–4.5 cm, frame rate of 13 Hz, dynamic range of 80 dB, and contrast gain of 10 dB. The rationale for these settings was presented in previous work32. The imaging depth was set to include the full cross-section of the lobe, varying per liver. This resulted in a mechanical index (MI) between 0.07 and 0.09 during acquisition, ensuring that MI < 0.1, which is associated with minimal microbubble destruction33. The focus depth was set as deep as possible to achieve the most homogeneous acoustic field across the imaged region. The acquisition time was set to 60 seconds to capture the wash-in and wash-out of microbubble injections.

The clinically approved ultrasound contrast agent Sonovue (Bracco, Geneva, Switzerland) was used for DCE-US acquisition34. The microbubble injection solution was prepared by resuspending a Sonovue vial with 5 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution (Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA), swirled until suspension was reached visually. The solution was drawn into a 1 mL syringe, which was gently swirled before each injection to keep the microbubbles suspended. The injection solution was then loaded into the microcatheter by manually injecting 0.2 mL of the injection solution into tubing coupled to the microcatheter. Subsequently, a controlled microbubble injection was realized by initiating a 5 mL PBS flush using an injection pump (PHD ULTRA, Harvard Apparatus, Holliston, USA) at a steady flow rate of 10 mL/min.

Real-time feedback on microbubble arrival dynamics from a chosen catheter position was achieved by DCE-US. With this technique, microbubble injections were quantified directly after acquisition by a time-intensity curve (TIC) algorithm developed in-house. The direct quantification was facilitated by feeding the live ultrasound images into this algorithm via a frame grabber (HDMI video capture, Earkings, Bemmel, The Netherlands). Before the first acquisition, a region of interest (ROI) was defined on the B-mode image to capture the contrast-enhanced imaging. After initiating the microbubble injection, live ultrasound frames were converted to grayscale and stored for analysis. Immediately after acquiring the last frames, the brightness values of each frame were summed to quantify the intensity. The baseline offset given by the intensity of the first frame was subtracted from each frame to obtain the final TIC. Optimization of the catheter position was performed iteratively based on peak intensity, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

The TICs were plotted directly on a screen positioned next to the ultrasound system. At each catheter position, three injections were analyzed to determine repeatability. Before each injection, residual microbubbles from previous injections were eliminated using a high-pressure ultrasound burst to destroy the microbubbles. For each measurement, the recorded TIC was added to the measurement set to allow direct analysis of the new catheter position with respect to the previous positions. For experiments 2 and 3, TICs of the reference position (control) were also determined in real-time, plotted in the same figure as the target TICs for comparison. For all experiments, the TIC with the highest peak intensity was highlighted on the screen, with its corresponding catheter position indicated to directly identify the optimal catheter placement.

Holmium microsphere injection & analysis

Non-radioactive 165Ho-microspheres were injected at the optimized catheter position as identified via DCE-US analysis. The injected solution consisted of 300 mg 165Ho in a poly-L-lactic acid (PLLA) shell (QuiremSpheresTM, Quirem Medical B.V., Deventer, The Netherlands), suspended with 4.5 mL PBS and 0.5 mL of pluronic buffer solution (0.1% pluronic, Quirem Medical B.V., Deventer, The Netherlands)35. The 165Ho-microspheres were injected using the injection pump with the same injection settings as those used for the microbubbles to avoid differences in biodistribution9,12, using microsphere injection fractions of 0.5 mL followed by a 5 mL PBS flush.

Following administration of the 165Ho-microspheres, perfusion of the liver was stopped, and the liver was transported back to the MRI to assess liver condition after perfusion and to determine the 165Ho-microsphere biodistribution. Holmium-sensitive T2*-weighted images were acquired using a multi-echo gradient recalled echo (mGRE) sequence, which were then stored for post-processing to assess the microsphere biodistribution. Sequence parameters were: TR/TE1/ΔTE = 1040/1.7/2.4 ms, number of echoes = 12, flip angle = 90°, field-of-view = 400 × 400 mm, voxel size = 1.56 × 1.56 mm, slice thickness = 4.0 mm, number of slices = 34. R2* maps were generated by fitting the multi-echo data within the liver to a mono-exponential signal model, in which increased values indicate the presence of 165Ho-microspheres36. Baseline liver signal was removed through thresholding at the maximum value measured in lobes without visible microsphere deposition, followed by median filtering with a kernel size of 5 × 5 pixels to improve visualization of the spatial biodistribution of the microspheres. The resulting R2* maps were finally normalized to the maximum R2* value, yielding a qualitative 165Ho-microsphere biodistribution map within the liver.

The inclusion of the reference ultrasound transducer in the second and third cases enabled comparison between the DCE-US prediction and the MRI-derived 165Ho-microsphere biodistribution. For quantification, regions of interest were defined on the R2* maps at the transducer positions, with the physical transducer footprint extended by a margin of 1.5 times the transducer size to account for uncertainty in transducer placement. The transducer position was determined either from fluoroscopy or, if the transducer was outside the fluoroscopy imaging region, from a photograph of the placement on the liver, with the gallbladder used as an anatomical landmark.

Statistics and Reproducibility

Three ex vivo livers were analyzed on a case-by-case basis. For each case, all generated TICs were normalized to the maximum peak intensity measured at the target position within that case. For each injection location, mean peak intensities were calculated from three measurements, and the corresponding standard deviations were reported (±σ). In addition, two ratios were computed using the mean peak intensities: the relative signal improvement with respect to the first injection location, calculated separately for the target and reference positions (TP/T₁ and RP/R₁), and the target-to-reference signal ratio (TP/RP).

Discussion

In this proof-of-concept study, the potential of dynamic contrast-enhanced ultrasound (DCE-US) guided transarterial radioembolization (TARE) was investigated. We demonstrated that real-time feedback from DCE-US could be used to achieve targeted microsphere biodistributions in the liver. In the first case, a substantial 165Ho-microsphere deposition was observed both within the target region as well as in an adjacent lobe, which was not monitored during catheter positioning due to fixation of the transducer during measurements. Notably, as the liver was not fully submerged in blood during MRI acquisition, increased R2* values were also observed along the border of the organ due to susceptibility artefacts that do not reflect the presence of 165Ho-microspheres. In the second case, the microsphere deposition was specific to the target region with minimal deposition in the reference region (99.2% vs. 0.8%), closely matching the DCE-US prediction (98% vs 2%). In the third case, a more evenly distributed microsphere deposition was found, which was higher in the target area compared to the reference area (70.2% vs 29.8%), consistent with the DCE-US prediction (80.9% vs 19.1%).

The study was designed to evaluate the performance of DCE-US guided TARE. First, the method demonstrated moderate repeatability, as reflected by distinguishable TICs and a mean standard deviation below 10% across all cases. Some variability in the TICs can be caused by differences in the injected microbubble bolus. As the injected volume and concentration were only controlled visually, improved control over these parameters may improve repeatability. Second, DCE-US demonstrated its potential to achieve a highly specific microsphere biodistribution, largely confined to the target region. In the second and third cases, the microsphere biodistribution was predominantly limited to the areas monitored by the two fixed ultrasound transducers, which were used solely for quantitative assessment of the DCE-US prediction, and only one transducer is envisioned for clinical use. In the first case, where only a single fixed transducer was used, microsphere depositions were also observed outside the target region, suggesting that the catheter position could have been further optimized to achieve a more targeted distribution. Finally, DCE-US demonstrated its advantage as a real-time analysis method by capturing the impact of small deviations in catheter position. Minor displacements near the feeding artery led to significant differences in the peak intensity of microbubble injections. These results not only confirm the high sensitivity of radioembolization to catheter placement but also highlight the key challenge of the current procedure in terms of accurate replication of the catheter configuration between the scout and therapeutic procedures.

The accuracy of the DCE-US prediction method is influenced by several factors. First, the difference in size and density of the microbubbles and microspheres results in different dynamics in the flow5,26. Despite these differences, we demonstrated a moderately strong correlation between microbubbles and microspheres trajectories in a previous in-vitro study (p < 0.05, r = 0.88)25. The observed discrepancies were most likely caused by these inherent physical differences. Although 165Ho-microspheres were used in both the previous and current studies, the DCE-US treatment planning method is also expected to be applicable to 90Y-based microspheres, given their comparable physical properties (e.g., size and density)37. Second, accurate quantification of microbubble concentrations is a challenge in DCE-US. Current methods for quantitative DCE-US imaging rely on multiple assumptions and simplifications38, such as the microbubble size distribution, inter-bubble distances, and the elasticity of the blood vessel wall39,40. Our in-house developed algorithm prioritized computational speed to enable rapid assessment, rather than accurate quantification of microbubble concentrations. Additional quantification results from a preliminary experiment (provided in Supplementary Materials 7(Figure S25 & S26, Table S10)) were compared to those obtained using the commercial software VueBox (Bracco, Switzerland, Geneva), an off-line post-processing application for DCE-US-based tissue perfusion quantification41. As VueBox outputs the TICs in dB, a linear regression between both result sets was performed. This fit showed good agreement, with a maximum mean difference of 0.59 dB, indicating that a high computational speed of our DCE-US algorithm is achieved with only minimal loss of accuracy. Finally, DCE-US did not provide a three-dimensional overview of the expected microsphere biodistribution, as the region of analysis is restricted to the two-dimensional imaging plane. In this study, the assessment of different catheter positions relied on comparing their resulting peak intensities, which required transducer fixation and provided limited spatial coverage. To address this limitation, the transducer could be moved during an additional microbubble injection to scan a wider area and help identify the biodistribution boundaries of the candidate catheter configuration.

The MR-based ¹⁶⁵Ho-microsphere biodistribution was evaluated using a custom-made MATLAB script that generated relative dose maps based on the linear R2* relaxivity of 165Ho-microspheres42. While this approach is conceptually similar to the clinically used Q-Suite software (Quirem Medical B.V., Deventer, The Netherlands), Q-Suite produced unreliable results in this study due to the extended interval between pre- and post-treatment MRI scans. As a result, the relative dose maps could not be directly compared with Q-Suite outcomes, and the accuracy of the generated maps in this study could not be assessed. While the determined relative microsphere distributions visually corresponded to the T2*-weighted multi-gradient MRI scans (see Supplementary Materials 3), it should be considered an approximate representation rather than an exact measurement.

While ex-vivo perfused livers provide a suitable platform to demonstrate the proof-of-concept of DCE-US guided TARE, the platform also presents certain challenges. One key limitation encountered in this study was the difficulty in maintaining a stable liver condition throughout the experiments. This was reflected by suboptimal blood gas analysis results, particularly a consistently low pH (6.85–7.15) outside of the physiological range (7.35–7.45)43, and insufficient oxygen saturation (69–86% in case 2; 41–51% in case 3). Although bicarbonate was administered at the start to correct for acidosis, the dosage was likely insufficient to fully restore physiological pH. Despite these metabolic imbalances, the anatomical MR scans did not reveal significant deterioration of liver parenchyma, aside from minor spots in the periphery of certain lobes. Furthermore, the homogeneous biodistribution of microbubbles in the region of interest and peripheral deposition of microspheres suggest that the deterioration in liver condition did not significantly impact the macro- and microvasculature.

Previous studies have explored the use of contrast-enhanced ultrasound to assess the biodistribution in transarterial chemoembolization44–46. In these studies, intra-arterial injections of contrast microbubbles were used to identify tumor feeding arteries, providing feedback for the catheter position prior to injection of chemoembolization particles. However, these applications focused on qualitative assessment rather than microbubble signal intensity analysis, as vessel identification relied solely on visualizing the presence of contrast enhancement downstream of arterial bifurcations. Quantitative analysis provides greater control in treatment planning by enabling direct comparison of different catheter configurations. Particularly in cases where contrast enhancement is observed at multiple locations and visual comparison is non-trivial (e.g., Case 3, Fig. 9), quantification supports a more objective and substantiated choice for the optimal catheter configuration. Furthermore, quantitative evaluation facilitates assessment of tumor perfusion, contributing to more comprehensive tumor localization and potentially enabling detection of lesions not evident on other imaging modalities31,47. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to employ ultrasound-based analysis to quantitatively optimize the biodistribution in locoregional therapy.

Other methods have also been studied to improve prediction of the microsphere biodistribution in TARE. For contrast-enhanced cone-beam CT, a perfusion approach was demonstrated to offer similar predictive accuracy to the conventional 99mTc-MAA-based scout analysis, although both approaches tended to overestimate tumor dose at higher activity levels48. A recent retrospective study examined the use of intra-procedural cone-beam CT for predicting the tumor-absorbed dose based on contrast enhancement patterns49. While a strong correlation was found for tumor regions, the agreement was moderate for non-tumor tissue. Due to the small patient cohort, results could not be generalized. For MRI, the EMERITUS-1 trial investigated the feasibility of intra-procedural MRI-based dosimetry50. In two presented cases, intra-procedural MRI dose mapping suggested alternative treatment strategies. However, repositioning of the microcatheter could not be performed within the MRI, meaning that direct optimization of the catheter configuration was not possible in this setting.

The DCE-US approach shows potential to improve TARE treatment planning, building on the established safety and efficacy of contrast microbubbles, combined with the advantages of ultrasound imaging in this setting, i.e., real-time and portable. Ultrasound is non-ionizing and has a small equipment footprint, making it highly suitable for use in hybrid operating rooms or interventional suites. In such settings, an ultrasound system is often readily available, making the incorporation of DCE-US with a single transducer for enhanced treatment planning feasible. The real-time feedback offered by DCE-US can moreover aid in the diagnosis and grading of tumors51, which can be used to further enhance treatment planning and monitoring.

Microbubbles are already well-established in diagnostic imaging and offer a safe alternative to 99mTc-MAA. Microbubbles typically consist of an inert gas core and a stabilizing shell, which are both excreted by expiration and metabolism, respectively52. They are associated with a very low prevalence of minor adverse effects (approximately 1 in 3000 examinations53). Clinically approved microbubbles such as SonoVue/Lumason (Bracco, Geneva, Switzerland) and Optison (GE Healthcare, Princeton, NJ) have a short intravascular half-life of approximately one minute, depending on the formulation54,55. As a result, the DCE-US prediction iterations can be performed on short intervals and microbubble accumulation is unlikely, also because of the low injection volume needed (0.1–0.2 ml per injection). Although these agents are approved for intravenous use and thus intra-arterial use is currently outside their indication for use, several studies have explored intra-arterial administration of various clinically approved microbubble formulations56–58.

In current clinical practice, catheter configuration during radioembolization is typically assessed using contrast-enhanced cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT). The introduction of DCE-US offers several advantages over this approach. First, current results indicate that ultrasound contrast microbubbles can predict the microsphere biodistribution, a relationship that has yet to be established for iodinated contrast agents used in CBCT. However, contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) has already been shown in transarterial chemoembolization to alter treatment planning by identifying superselective feeding arteries that were subsequently targeted instead of those selected based on angiography59,60. Second, repeated CBCT acquisitions for assessment and optimization of the catheter configuration increase patient radiation exposure, whereas optimization using DCE-US can be performed with minimal safety concerns. Third, iodinated contrast agents are unsuitable for patients with renal failure and may cause contrast-induced nephropathy61,62, whereas ultrasound contrast microbubbles represent a much safer alternative with low rates of adverse effects51. Finally, CBCT provides only static images, which limits its utility in predicting depositions in non-tumor tissue and in tumors with different vascularity49, whereas CEUS enhancement patterns have been reported to outperform CBCT-derived patterns in delineating tumor boundaries to enhance treatment planning63.

Adoption of the DCE-US prediction in TARE is facilitated by a series of applications of contrast microbubbles that have emerged in TARE over the last few years64. One of these applications is the monitoring of radiation response after TARE by contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS). For high doses of radiation, tumor blood vessels are destroyed in addition to damaging the DNA65, such that changes in the microvasculature can be monitored after TARE. Using CEUS, changes in the microvasculature were detected within 2 weeks after TARE that correlated with the response as monitored by contrast-enhanced MRI66. Another technology that has emerged is ultrasound-triggered microbubble destruction (UTMD) for improving radiosensitization of tumors67. A randomized pilot clinical trial by Eisenbrey et al. showed promising results for the application of UTMD within TARE, with a greater tumor response for a group of 15 participants that underwent sonoporation in combination with TARE (93%) compared to a control group of 10 participants that solely underwent TARE (50%)68. These results further encourage the adoption and integration of the investigated DCE-US guided TARE approach, which could precede UTMD.

While real-time feedback from DCE-US predictions has the potential to improve targeting accuracy compared to the current scout procedure, the latter cannot be omitted entirely. In addition to determining the radiation dose, the scout procedure is essential for detecting extrahepatic depositions and assessing the lung shunt fraction to determine eligibility for TARE. For identifying extrahepatic depositions, contrast-enhanced ultrasound might be employed by scanning organs surrounding the liver where extrahepatic depositions are common, such as the gallbladder and duodenum, for microbubble presence directly after an injection69. However, detecting microbubbles in deeper lying organs such as the duodenum may be challenging. For assessment of the lung shunt fraction, contrast-enhanced ultrasound cannot be used in the same way, as microbubbles pass the capillary bed, such that microbubbles are expected in the lungs following injection. Although DCE-US cannot currently replace the scout procedure, future development of alternative methods for estimating the lung shunt fraction may make this feasible.

A limitation of this study is that the DCE-US guided TARE protocol was demonstrated in the context of super-selective TARE, targeting a single lobe or segment of the liver. While this approach is clinically relevant for treating localized tumors, it does not reflect scenarios where a microsphere biodistribution across multiple lobes is required, for example for multifocal tumors. As a result, the applicability of the current protocol remains to be demonstrated in that context.

Another limitation of the study is that microbubble-based prediction was evaluated only for catheter position changes, using standard injection parameters. Alternative injection strategies, such as pulsatile injections, increased injection velocities, or the use of different catheters, may influence microbubble and microsphere biodistribution patterns70, potentially improving or compromising the accuracy of the prediction method. On the other hand, the real-time feedback provided by DCE-US could prove to be ideal to assess these parameters, which may instead further improve optimization of the catheter configuration.

Finally, the use of a healthy ex-vivo perfused porcine liver to demonstrate DCE-US guided TARE limits the direct clinical translation of the prediction method. First, the absence of tumors results in different hemodynamic conditions, as microspheres lack a preferential deposition site after arterial injection. Second, the ex-vivo liver allowed direct fixation of the ultrasound transducer, enabling stable and consistent ultrasound imaging. In-vivo, however, factors such as the presence of surrounding organs or structures, respiratory motion may pose additional challenges. Nevertheless, these difficulties can largely be mitigated through current ultrasound scanning protocols, in which imaging from an alternative angle or under breath-hold helps prevent acoustic shadowing (e.g. by the rib cage), as well as through appropriate patient preparation, such as fasting to reduce the effects of bowel gas71. It should also be noted that the deeper location of the liver and the presence of tumors in vivo can introduce variations in ultrasound attenuation, for which several strategies exist to enhance the robustness of the demonstrated DCE-US approach, such as defining tumor-specific ROIs or using alternative TIC-derived parameters, including time to peak or area under the curve72. Third, microbubble recirculation was not observed in the ex vivo perfused liver. This is likely due to microbubble destruction in the oxygenators and buoyancy toward the free interface of the reservoir, preventing re-entry into the perfusion circuit. In vivo, however, microbubbles recirculate and can produce delayed secondary peaks after approximately 60 seconds60, potentially affecting TIC-derived parameters that rely on the wash-out phase and requiring correction of TICs when comparing curves for rapid catheter position optimization in clinical practice. Lastly, although anatomical and vascular structures are similar between porcine livers and human livers, differences in their overall shape may affect the generalizability of the findings in this study.74

Conclusions

Real-time feedback from DCE-US guided TARE enabled effective optimization of catheter positioning to realize targeted microsphere depositions in ex-vivo porcine livers. Microsphere depositions were consistently observed in the target lobe across all livers, and microbubble biodistributions corresponded with the 165Ho-microsphere biodistribution between target and reference regions. These results demonstrate that DCE-US guided TARE can enhance microsphere targeting and hold promise for improved treatment outcomes, warranting further clinical investigation.
