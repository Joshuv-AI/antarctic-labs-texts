Unsaturated alginate oligosaccharides alleviate insulin resistance and fatty liver in non-obese NAFLD mice by regulating bile acid metabolism through gut microbiota
Authors: Qianqian Cha, Wenrui Zhao, Liyuan Ran, Yu Wang, Xiaofei Wang, Fei Xu, Zichao Yu, Yingjie Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Despite the rising global incidence of non-obese non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), this condition has received limited attention. Unsaturated alginate oligosaccharides (UAOS) have shown promising potential in the management of metabolic diseases. In this study, we used liver-specific growth hormone receptor (GHR) knockout mice as a model for non-obese NAFLD and treated them with UAOS. Our findings demonstrated that UAOS effectively ameliorated insulin resistance and hepatic steatosis in GHR-deficient mice. Additionally, UAOS improved intestinal barrier integrity, altered gut microbiota composition, and increased the relative abundance of beneficial bacteria, including Dubosiella, Akkermansia, Lachnoclostridium, Faecalibaculum, Romboutsia, and Turicibacter, while reducing the prevalence of harmful bacteria. Fecal metabolomics analysis revealed significant reductions in taurohyodeoxycholic acid and isodeoxycholic acid. Furthermore, UAOS may modulate the synthesis and secretion of secondary bile acids through the gut microbiota, potentially inhibiting hepatic bile acid synthesis and contributing to the maintenance of bile acid homeostasis via the FGF15-FGFR4-CYP7A1 signaling pathway. These mechanisms ultimately contributed to improved hepatic lipid metabolism. Together, these results position UAOS as a promising prebiotic candidate for the treatment of non-obese NAFLD.

Introduction

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is the most prevalent liver disease worldwide. Historically, research on NAFLD has predominantly focused on obese individuals. However, NAFLD is also common in those with a normal body mass index (BMI) [1], a condition known as non-obese NAFLD. A 2020 meta-analysis revealed that approximately 40 % of the global NAFLD population falls into the non-obese category, with nearly 20 % of these individuals classified as lean [2]. The significant proportion of non-obese NAFLD cases underscores the need for increased attention to this condition. Both non-obese and obese NAFLD are strongly associated with metabolic syndrome and exhibit severe metabolic dysregulation. Notably, non-obese NAFLD patients show a higher prevalence of cardiovascular disease [3], highlighting the urgency of developing effective therapeutic strategies for this population. Liver-specific growth hormone receptor (GHR) knockout mice display key features of insulin resistance and fatty liver without significant changes in body weight [4]. Previous studies from our group have shown that liver GHR deficiency in these mice disrupts bile acid metabolism, leading to alterations in gut microbiota composition [5]. These phenotypic similarities to non-obese NAFLD patients make GHR knockout mice a suitable model for studying non-obese NAFLD [6].

Alginate, a natural polysaccharide found abundantly in the cell walls of brown algae such as kelp, sargassum, and giant kelp, serves as a valuable precursor for alginate oligosaccharides (AOS). AOS are linear, low-molecular-weight polymers composed of 2–25 monosaccharides, obtained through enzymatic cleavage of alginate. These oligosaccharides possess favorable biochemical properties, including low molecular weight, high water solubility, low viscosity, and efficient intestinal absorption, making them highly promising for biomedical applications [[7], [8], [9]]. Enzymatically produced AOS are characterized by high safety profiles and minimal by-products. Importantly, the non-reducing ends of these enzymatic hydrolysis products contain unsaturated double bonds, which enhance their biological activity, resulting in unsaturated alginate oligosaccharides (UAOS) [10]. Although UAOS have been widely investigated for their prebiotic properties and metabolic benefits, most studies have focused on obesity-associated metabolic disorders [11]. In this study, we explored the therapeutic potential of UAOS in a liver-specific GHR knockout mouse model, which recapitulates key features of non-obese NAFLD. This approach provides a novel framework to elucidate the metabolic actions of UAOS beyond conventional obesity-related contexts.

The gut microbiota, often referred to as the “microbial organ” of the human body, plays a critical role in food digestion, nutrient absorption, and its interactions with other host organs, forming various “axes” [[12], [13], [14]]. Among these, the gut-liver axis is particularly significant, as the intestine and liver are directly connected via the portal circulation [15]. Intestinal metabolites that reach the liver can profoundly influence liver physiology. Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota has been extensively implicated in the pathogenesis of NAFLD [16,17], driving considerable interest in microbiota-targeted therapeutic approaches such as probiotics, prebiotics, synbiotics, and fecal microbiota transplantation. Studies have consistently demonstrated the efficacy of probiotics in animal models of NAFLD, yielding promising therapeutic outcomes [18].

Bile acid metabolism, closely interwoven with the gut-liver axis, involves a complex physiological cycle encompassing hepatic synthesis and secretion, intestinal transformation and reabsorption, and recycling via enterohepatic circulation [19]. Bile acids, produced by the host and modifiable by the gut microbiota, serve as critical mediators in the host-microbiome interaction and are pivotal regulators of glucose and lipid homeostasis [20,21]. Consequently, bile acids have emerged as attractive therapeutic targets for NAFLD. Current bile acid-focused drugs primarily act through the farnesoid X receptor (FXR), with obeticholic acid being a prominent FXR agonist [22]. This agent indirectly suppresses the expression of cytochrome P450 family 7 subfamily A member 1 (CYP7A1) and is under investigation for the treatment of primary biliary cirrhosis and NAFLD [22,23].

Given the gut microbiota's critical involvement in bile acid metabolism, we aim to regulate the progression of non-obese NAFLD and explore new drugs and therapeutic targets through the modulation of gut microbiota and bile acid metabolism. This study investigates the therapeutic effects of UAOS on non-obese NAFLD using liver-specific GHR knockout mice. We explore changes in the gut microbiota composition in these mice following UAOS treatment and elucidate the mechanisms by which UAOS improves non-obese NAFLD by modulating host bile acid metabolism through the gut microbiota. The findings will enhance our understanding of the interactions between gut microbiota and the host, providing experimental evidence and a scientific basis for the development of prebiotic and probiotic strategies for non-obese NAFLD.

Materials and methods

Preparation of UAOS

UAOS were prepared using purified recombinant AlyC7 as previously described [24]. In brief, 1.2 mg/ml of AlyC7 was incubated with 2 mg/ml of alginate substrate in 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 9.0) containing 0.5 M NaCl at 30 °C overnight. The reaction was terminated by boiling the mixture for 10 min. Residual undegraded alginate was removed by centrifugation at 6000 rpm using a 3 kDa cut-off ultrafiltration tube. The resulting filtrate was mixed with pre-cooled 95 % (v/v) ethanol to precipitate the alginate oligosaccharides, followed by washing the precipitate three times with ethanol to remove residual salts. Finally, the precipitate was freeze-dried and stored for further use.

Animals and experimental design

Liver-specific GHR knockout mice (referred to as NO-NAFLD mice) were generated by crossing GHR-Flox mice with Alb-Cre mice, which express Cre recombinase specifically in the liver. Littermate GHR-Flox mice were used as healthy controls (HC mice). All mice were male to eliminate gender differences. Only male mice were used to reduce hormonal variability, but this remains a limitation, as sex-specific effects should be addressed in future work. The mice were divided into four groups: HC, NO-NAFLD, NO-NAFLD_UAOS, and NO-NAFLD_ALG. Mice in the NO-NAFLD_UAOS and NO-NAFLD_ALG groups were gavaged with UAOS solution and sodium alginate solution, respectively, at a dose of 150 mg/kg body weight per day, while mice in the HC and NO-NAFLD groups were gavaged with an equal volume of saline. After continuous gavage for 10 days, an insulin tolerance test (ITT) was performed. Following an additional 5 days of gavage, the mice were euthanized, and serum, liver, jejunum, ileum, and cecal contents were collected for further analysis. The 15-day intervention was selected based on the significant improvement in insulin tolerance observed at day 10, indicating that UAOS exerted measurable metabolic benefits within this period. Although relatively short, this duration was sufficient to capture early therapeutic responses; longer studies are warranted to assess their durability.

All animal procedures were performed in accordance with the Guidelines for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of Shandong Provincial Hospital and were approved by the Experimental Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of Shandong Provincial Hospital with experimental facility license number No. 2022-080.

Biochemical analysis

Serum concentrations of HDL-C, LDL-C, ALT, AST, ALP, T-Bil-V, D-Bil-V, I-Bil-V, and TBA were measured using an automatic biochemical analyzer (Mindray, China). The ITT was performed after 10 consecutive days of intragastric administration. Insulin was intraperitoneally injected at a dose of 0.75 U/kg body weight after a 5-h fasting period. Blood glucose levels were measured at 0, 15-, 30-, 45-, and 60-min post-injection using a blood glucose meter, with blood collected from the tail vein. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated.

Histopathological analysis

Fresh liver and jejunum tissues were collected, washed with saline, and fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for 24 h. Fixed liver tissues were placed in a 15 % sucrose solution at 4 °C for dehydration, followed by transfer to a 30 % sucrose solution at 4 °C for further dehydration and sinking. OCT embedding agent was applied around the dehydrated tissues, which were then rapidly frozen on a freezing table. Once the OCT embedding medium turned white and hardened, the tissues were sectioned. The tissue sections were cut to a thickness of 8–10 μm and stained with Oil Red O. The fixed liver and jejunum tissues were dehydrated sequentially with graded alcohol (75 %–100 %), cleared with xylene, and embedded in paraffin. The sections were cut to 4 μm thickness after paraffin embedding, followed by H&E staining after dewaxing and rehydration. Images were captured using a digital pathology slide scanner (KFBIO, China). The length of the small intestinal villi and the depth of crypts were measured using ImageJ software.

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from frozen liver and ileum tissues using FreeZol Reagent (Vazyme, China), according to the manufacturer's instructions. One microgram of RNA was used for gDNA removal and first-strand cDNA synthesis (Vazyme, China). Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed to analyze the expression of liver lipid metabolism-related genes (Acc, Fasn, Atgl, Hsl, Mgl, Fabp1, Fatp2, and Fatp5), liver bile acid metabolism-related genes (Fxr, Ntcp, Oatp1, Bsep, Mrp2, Cyp7a1, Cyp7b1, Cyp8b1, Cyp27a1, and Fgfr4), and ileal bile acid metabolism-related genes (Fxr and Fgf15). Primer sequences used in this study are listed in the Supplementary Data (Table S1). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as an internal control. qPCR results were analyzed using the 2^-(ΔΔCt) method, and data were processed and visualized using GraphPad Prism 9 software.

Cecal content microbiome sequencing and data analysis

Cecal contents were collected from the mice, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C. Total genomic DNA was extracted from the microbial community using the E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The extracted DNA was used as a template to PCR amplify the V3-V4 variable region of the 16S rRNA gene using the upstream primer 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and the downstream primer 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) carrying the barcode sequence. The purified PCR product was used to construct a library with the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq Kit and sequenced on the Illumina Nextseq2000 platform. UPARSE v7.1 software (http://drive5.com/uparse/) was used to cluster quality-controlled sequences into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) and remove chimeras at 97 % similarity [25]. RDP classifier (http://rdp.cme.msu.edu/, version 2.11) was used to align the Silva 16S rRNA gene database (v138) for OTU species taxonomy annotation [26]. Few taxa remained significant after multiple testing correction, so unadjusted p-values were used to capture biologically relevant changes. All data analyses were performed on the MajorBio cloud platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com), as follows: Alpha diversity was calculated using mothur software (http://www.mothur.org/wiki/Calculators) [27]; PCoA analysis (principal coordinate analysis) based on the Bray-Curtis distance algorithm was used to test the similarity of microbial community structure between samples; LEfSe (Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size) analysis (http://huttenhower.sph.harvard.edu/LEfSe) (LDA >2, P < 0.05) was used to determine the bacterial groups with significant differences in abundance from the phylum to the genus level between different groups [28]. Species were selected for correlation network analysis based on spearman correlation |r| > 0.6 and p < 0.05 [29].

Fecal metabolomics sequencing and analysis

Fecal samples were collected prior to euthanasia, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for later analysis. A 400 μL solution (Methanol:Water = 7:3, V/V) containing an internal standard was added to 20 mg of sample, vortexed for 3 min, and sonicated in an ice bath for 10 min. The sample was vortexed again for 1 min and placed at −20 °C for 30 min. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min (4 °C), the supernatant was collected and centrifuged again at 12,000 rpm for 3 min. A 200 μL aliquot of the supernatant was transferred for LC-MS analysis [30]. Three LC-MS methods were employed: one aliquot was analyzed with positive ionization on a T3 column with a gradient elution of 0.1 % formic acid in water (solvent A) and 0.1 % formic acid in acetonitrile (solvent B); another aliquot was analyzed with negative ionization using the same gradient; and a third aliquot was analyzed using negative ionization on a HILIC column. Few metabolites remained significant after multiple testing correction, so differential metabolites were identified based on VIP (VIP >1) and P-value (P < 0.05, Student's t-test). VIP values were derived from OPLS-DA results, which also included score plots and permutation plots generated using the R package MetaboAnalystR [31]. Data were log-transformed (log2) and mean-centered before OPLS-DA. A permutation test (200 permutations) was performed to prevent overfitting. Identified metabolites were annotated using the KEGG Compound database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/compound/), and metabolites were mapped to the KEGG Pathway database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html) [32].

Data analysis

All experimental data are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses and graph generation were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.1). Differences between groups were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). A p-value of ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant (∗p ≤ 0.05, ∗∗p ≤ 0.01, ∗∗∗p ≤ 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p ≤ 0.0001, compared with the NO-NAFLD group).

Results

UAOS improves insulin resistance and liver health in non-obese NAFLD mice

UAOS improves insulin resistance, and alleviates liver inflammation in non-obese NAFLD mice

Previous studies have shown that unsaturated alginate oligosaccharides (UAOS) with a degree of polymerization (DP) of 2–4 exhibit enhanced biological activity. To produce UAOS with a DP of 2–4, we heterologously expressed and purified the alginate lyase AlyC7 from the marine bacterium Vibrio sp. C42 [24]. The AlyC7 gene was cloned into the expression vector pET-22b(+) and transformed into Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) cells for heterologous expression. Protein expression was induced with IPTG, and the recombinant AlyC7 was purified using Ni2+-affinity chromatography, yielding a high-purity enzyme. Under optimal temperature and pH conditions, purified AlyC7 enzyme was used to enzymatically hydrolyze alginate, and the resulting hydrolysis products were separated and analyzed using high-performance liquid chromatography high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) to isolate UAOS with a DP of 2–4 [24] (Fig. 1A).Fig. 1UAOS improves insulin resistance, alleviates liver inflammation, and alters bile acid metabolism in non-obese NAFLD mice. UAOS improves insulin resistance, alleviates liver inflammation, and alters bile acid metabolism in non-obese NAFLD mice. (A) UAOS enzymatic preparation process; (B) Insulin tolerance test (ITT); (C) Relative expression of inflammatory factor genes in the liver.

To assess the effects of UAOS and alginate on insulin resistance, we performed ITT on four groups of mice (HC, NO-NAFLD, NO-NAFLD_UAOS, and NO-NAFLD_ALG) 10 days after treatment. Although alginate exhibited a tendency to improve insulin resistance, UAOS significantly restored insulin sensitivity in non-obese NAFLD mice to near-normal levels (Fig. 1B). After 15 days of UAOS administration, liver weight and the liver index were reduced in the treatment groups (Fig. S1). To investigate the effects on hepatic inflammation, we measured the relative expression of inflammatory factor genes in the liver. Both UAOS and alginate administration significantly reduced the expression of Il-10, with UAOS also significantly decreasing Il-1β and Tnf-α levels (Fig. 1C). Both UAOS and alginate administration improved liver function markers such as AST, ALT, and ALP (Fig. S2). Although some indicators did not reach statistical significance, these results suggest that both UAOS and alginate help to improve liver function and reduce inflammation in non-obese NAFLD mice. Notably, UAOS showed a more pronounced effect in improving insulin resistance compared to alginate.

UAOS improves fatty liver in non-obese NAFLD mice

Liver appearance in the HC group was dark red and shiny, while that of the NO-NAFLD group was enlarged and light red (Fig. 2A). In contrast, the liver appearance in the UAOS and alginate treatment groups was similar to the HC group, indicating a return to normal liver morphology. Histological analyses further supported these observations. H&E and Oil Red O staining revealed normal hepatocyte structure in the HC group, while the NO-NAFLD group displayed significant lipid accumulation in hepatocytes. In the treatment groups, especially the UAOS group, lipid vacuoles and droplets were markedly reduced (Fig. 2A). We further examined the expression of genes involved in hepatic fat synthesis, degradation, and transport. The expression of Acc (acetyl-CoA carboxylase) and Fasn (fatty acid synthase), key enzymes in fat synthesis, was significantly elevated in the fatty liver mice. Treatment with both UAOS and alginate significantly reversed these changes, suppressing fat synthesis (Fig. 2B). Additionally, the expression of genes involved in lipid degradation (e.g., Atgl, Hsl, Mgl) and transport (e.g., Fabp1, Fatp2, Fatp5) decreased. UAOS improves the intestinal mucosal barrier in mice

Since alginate can only improve fatty liver in non-obese NAFLD mice but did not affect insulin resistance, we selected the UAOS-treated group for subsequent analysis. Histological examination of the ileum revealed that UAOS administration significantly promoted the growth of intestinal villi. This was evident not only in increased villus length but also in a denser overall morphology. The average villus length in the UAOS group was significantly greater than in the NO-NAFLD group (Fig. 3A and B), indicating an expanded surface area for intestinal nutrient absorption. Additionally, crypt depth in the ileum was significantly reduced after UAOS administration (Fig. 3A–C). Crypts are invaginations in the intestinal mucosa that house stem cells and support epithelial cell regeneration. The reduction in crypt depth suggests accelerated cell turnover, allowing faster migration of new cells to the villus surface [33].Fig. 3Effects of UAOS on intestinal mucosal barrier. UAOS modulates the gut microbiota composition in non-obese NAFLD mice

We evaluated the effects of UAOS on the cecal microbiota in mice using 16S rRNA sequencing. Significant changes in gut microbiota composition were observed at both the phylum and genus levels following UAOS administration. At the phylum level, Firmicutes and Bacteroidota were the dominant taxa, comprising over 70 % of the microbiota. UAOS administration increased the abundance of Firmicutes and decreased Bacteroidota. Additionally, the relative abundances of Patescibacteria, Actinobacteriota, and Verrucomicrobiota showed an increasing trend (Fig. 4A). At the genus level, UAOS administration enriched beneficial genera such as Lactobacillus, Candidatus_Saccharimonas, Dubosiella, norank_f_norank_o_Clostridia_UCG-014, and Akkermansia, while reducing the abundance of potentially harmful genera, including norank_f_Muribaculaceae, Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, and Erysipelatoclostridium (Fig. 4B). Principal component analysis (PCA) showed that the microbiota composition of the NO-NAFLD_UAOS group was more similar to the NO-NAFLD group than the HC group, though distinct differences were still apparent (Fig. 4C). In terms of α-diversity, indices such as Ace and Shannon showed no statistically significant differences among the groups (Fig. 4D). However, trends in the Ace and Shannon indices, as well as core curve analysis, suggested higher species diversity in the gut microbiota of the NO-NAFLD group (Fig. 4D and E). UAOS inhibits bile acid synthesis by regulating the FGF15-FGFR4-CYP7A1 signaling pathway

Broad-target metabolomics analysis of mouse fecal samples revealed significant alterations in metabolites after UAOS administration. Compared to the NO-NAFLD group, 65 metabolites were significantly upregulated, while 68 were downregulated (Fig. 6A). Pathway enrichment analysis of these differential metabolites identified significant involvement of ABC transporters, highlighting the critical role of the gut microbiota in lipid metabolism (Fig. 6B). Additionally, pathways related to “Regulation of lipolysis in adipocytes” and “Fat digestion and absorption” suggest that UAOS may promote lipolysis and mitigate hepatic fat accumulation by influencing these metabolic routes (Fig. 6B). The enrichment of “Bile secretion” and “Primary bile acid biosynthesis” pathways further suggests that UAOS may modulate bile acid metabolism (Fig. 6B). Among the differential metabolites, three secondary bile acids were significantly downregulated: taurohyodeoxycholic acid, isodeoxycholic acid, and 3-epideoxycholic acid. Serum total bile acid (TBA) content was significantly increased in the NO-NAFLD group, but this change was reversed after UAOS administration (Fig. 6D), prompting an investigation of bile acid metabolism-related gene expression in liver and ileum tissues. In the liver, the expression of Cyp7a1 was significantly decreased in the NO-NAFLD_UAOS group, while Fgfr4 expression was significantly increased (Fig. 6E). In the ileum, the expression of Fxr and Fgf15 was significantly upregulated (Fig. 6F). These findings suggest that UAOS may enhance the release of FGF15 by activating intestinal FXR. FGF15, transported to the liver via portal circulation, binds to FGFR4 on hepatocytes, thereby inhibiting CYP7A1 activity. This regulatory mechanism reduces bile acid synthesis in the liver and may improve hepatic glucose and lipid metabolism.

In summary, UAOS administration significantly influences metabolite profiles and bile acid-related pathways, supporting its role in modulating lipid metabolism and improving metabolic health in non-obese NAFLD.

Specific microbial taxa may be associated with reduced bile acid synthesis

UAOS may inhibit bile acid synthesis by regulating the FGF15-FGFR4-CYP7A1 signaling pathway, thereby modulating hepatic glucose and lipid metabolism. To investigate the relationship between bile acid metabolism and gut microbiota, we conducted a Spearman correlation analysis comparing the levels of two significantly reduced bile acids in the metabolome, related gene expressions, and OTUs from 16S rRNA sequencing. As shown in Fig. 7, based on correlation patterns, the top 50 most abundant genera were classified into three clusters: Cluster 1: Microbes in this cluster exhibited positive correlations with the expression of Fxr, Fgf15, and Fgfr4, and negative correlations with the levels of taurohyodeoxycholic acid, isodeoxycholic acid, and other bile acid metabolism-related genes. Key genera in this cluster included Akkermansia, Lachnoclostridium, Faecalibaculum, Dubosiella, and Romboutsia, which were enriched in the UAOS-treated group (Fig. 5, Fig. 7). Cluster 2: Microbes in this cluster displayed negative correlations with the expression of Fxr, Fgf15, and Fgfr4, and positive correlations with bile acid levels and other related gene expressions. This cluster primarily consisted of Bacteroides, Parabacteroides, Mucispirillum, Mycoplasma, and Odoribacter, which were more abundant in the NO-NAFLD group (Fig. 5, Fig. 7). Discussion

Both alginate and UAOS demonstrated beneficial effects in alleviating the fatty liver phenotype in non-obese NAFLD. Alginate, a natural polysaccharide, shows promise in improving lipid metabolism through mechanisms such as regulating the gut microbiota, reducing inflammatory responses, and enhancing the liver's lipid-processing capacity [34]. In line with these properties, alginate effectively alleviated hepatic lipid accumulation, while UAOS conferred additional benefits by further mitigating hepatic steatosis and improving insulin sensitivity. The added benefits of UAOS may stem from its smaller molecular size, which enables it to more effectively influence cellular signaling pathways—such as enhancing insulin signal transduction or indirectly improving insulin sensitivity by modulating specific gut microbes, potentially through mechanisms distinct from alginate's effects on hepatic lipid accumulation. By demonstrating that UAOS can ameliorate hepatic steatosis and improve insulin sensitivity in this unique non-obese NAFLD setting, our findings advance the existing alginate literature and underscore the potential of marine bioresources in developing novel therapeutic agents that target multiple metabolic dysfunctions occurring in the absence of obesity. Although UAOS demonstrated beneficial effects in this study, its translational application may be limited by interspecies differences in bile acid composition, individual variability in gut microbiota, and uncertainties regarding effective dosing, which warrant further investigation.

As a prebiotic, UAOS cannot be directly absorbed by the human body but exerts its effects via the gut microbiota. ABC transporters play an important role in nutrient absorption, metabolite transport, and the maintenance of gut microbial community structure [35]. Fecal metabolomic analysis revealed that the ABC transporter-related pathway was significantly enriched following UAOS administration (Fig. 6B), suggesting that gut microbiota may play a crucial role in the treatment of fatty liver. UAOS altered the microbial community in non-obese NAFLD mice, reducing the relative abundance of harmful bacteria like Bacteroides, Mucispirillum, and Mycoplasma, while promoting beneficial bacteria such as Dubosiella, Akkermansia, Lachnoclostridium, Faecalibaculum, Romboutsia, and Turicibacter. Among these, Akkermansia muciniphila has attracted considerable attention as a “next-generation probiotic” due to its ability to increase intestinal mucus layer thickness, protect the intestinal barrier, reduce inflammation, and improve fatty liver through the regulation of L-aspartate metabolism [36,37]. Additionally, genera such as Lachnoclostridium and Faecalibaculum are frequently linked to the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) [38,39], particularly butyrate, which promotes intestinal health, reduces inflammation, and supports liver health, thus combating fatty liver development. The enrichment or balance of these beneficial bacteria holds promise for improving fatty liver through complex intestinal microbial interactions and presents an exciting direction for future therapeutic strategies.

Non-obese individuals with NAFLD often exhibit bile acid metabolism disorders and elevated bile acid levels [5,40]. Liver-specific GHR knockout mice, in addition to developing fatty liver, also exhibit elevated bile acid levels. The relationship between gut microbiota and the enterohepatic circulation of bile acids is complex but plays a crucial role in host metabolic health. The gut microbiota converts primary bile acids into secondary bile acids through various enzymatic reactions. These secondary bile acids not only aid in fat emulsification and absorption but also act as signaling molecules that regulate host metabolism and immune responses through G protein-coupled receptors (e.g., TGR5) [41,42] and nuclear receptors (e.g., FXR) [43]. In this study, UAOS altered the gut microbiota composition, thereby affecting the secretion of secondary bile acids and the enterohepatic circulation, leading to reduced levels of taurohyodeoxycholic acid and isodeoxycholic acid. Notably, Akkermansia was significantly enriched following UAOS administration, and A.muciniphila plays a significant role in regulating bile acid metabolism, influencing the host's metabolic health. Akkermansia and other beneficial microbes may help regulate bile acid metabolism, potentially influencing insulin sensitivity and hepatic lipid deposition. This intricate gut microbiota-bile acid metabolism interaction is critical for understanding the pathogenesis of metabolic diseases and could provide a foundation for developing novel therapeutic strategies.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that UAOS has significant therapeutic potential in the non-obese NAFLD mouse model. It effectively alleviates insulin resistance, reduces hepatic lipid accumulation, and optimizes intestinal structure and function. UAOS promotes the growth of beneficial bacteria such as Akkermansia, Lachnoclostridium, Faecalibaculum, Dubosiella, and Romboutsia, while inhibiting harmful bacteria, thereby improving the intestinal microenvironment. Additionally, UAOS may regulate the synthesis and secretion of secondary bile acids through the gut microbiota, potentially inhibiting liver bile acid synthesis by modulating the FGF15-FGFR4-CYP7A1 signaling pathway. This regulation helps maintain bile acid homeostasis and controls hepatic lipid metabolism.

In conclusion, UAOS emerges as a promising prebiotic candidate for treating non-obese NAFLD. Future studies should explore the suitability of these specific microbial groups as probiotics and investigate their detailed mechanisms in UAOS-mediated bile acid metabolism regulation and NAFLD improvement, thus providing a stronger scientific foundation for clinical applications.
