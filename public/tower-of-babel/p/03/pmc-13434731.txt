Thioflavin-T derivatives with controlled AIE and ACQ properties as potential one-photon and two-photon amyloid markers
Authors: Agata Hajda, Patryk Rybczyński, Weronika Andrzejczak, Borys Ośmiałowski, Joanna Olesiak-Bańska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aggregates of organic dyes are not only interesting from a fundamental science perspective but also have significant implications in application as Aggregation Induced Emission (AIE) and Aggregation Caused Quenching (ACQ) dyes. Here, we present BF2-functionalized benzothiazoles, sharing the same core, but various electron-donating, amino groups substituted at the amino nitrogen by alkyl or aromatic groups and 1,4-phenylene acting as a conjugated bridge between donor and acceptor. Difference between alkyl/aromatic donors results in the opposite ACQ/AIE mechanisms, i.e., in the water environment dyes with alkyl-donors exhibit ACQ, whereas those with aryl-donors demonstrate AIE. Here, we evaluate how AIE or ACQ affects the performance of dyes as amyloid probes, in relation to the form of the dyes (monomers or aggregates) which are bound to amyloids. For this propose we measured one-photon properties like absorption, fluorescence, fluorescence quantum yield, fluorescence lifetime, and binding constants. Additionally, we evaluated two-photon properties and presented that 2PA can be used to distinguish between the form of a dye (monomer vs aggregate) upon binding to amyloids. Since our dyes differ only in one functional group (aryl/alkyl amine), evaluation of their structure-properties relationship as fluorescent probe is highly beneficial for better understanding of ACQ/AIE luminogens interaction with amyloids.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54354-x.

Introduction

Amyloids are insoluble aggregates associated with various diseases e.g. Alzheimer disease, Huntington disease and Type 2 diabetes1,2. Those are composed of characteristic β-sheet structures which constitute β-sheets assembled into protofilaments, which further associate into fibrils2. Due to their biological and medical importance, detection of amyloids is an extensively studied issue. One of the most widely used detection methods are fluorescence-based techniques, which relies on changes in emission intensity or position of the maximum wavelength of a fluorescence band upon binding to amyloids. The standard probe for amyloid detection is Thioflavin-T (ThT), which consists of benzotiazole core, N,N-dimethylaniline moiety as a donor and 1,4-phenylene spacer joining donor with the acceptor by two single C–C bonds3,4. Limiting the rotation around the single bond leads to an increase of fluorescence quantum yield (FQY) in higher viscosity environments and upon binding to amyloids. ThT is used in routine confirmation of amyloid presence in solutions and in kinetics studies on amyloids growth5,6. However, its properties have limitations in various aspects e.g. lack of specificity, emission below 500 nm. New fluorescent markers are developed, which outperform ThT in terms of higher enhancement of emission upon binding7, selectivity8,9, redshifted emission10–12, two-photon properties13–15 and better biocompability (like blood–brain barrier permeability)16,17. In terms of in vivo measurements, valuable tool for visualization of amyloids is two-photon fluorescence microscopy (2PFM), which relies on the simultaneous absorption of two photons (2PA), followed by fluorescence. In comparison to one-photon fluorescence microscopy, 2PA-based techniques are used in amyloid detection in the brain due to deeper penetration of light in the sample18–20. However, ThT and other amyloid-binding fluorophores are characterized by low value of two-photon absorption cross-section (σ2) , which is a key parameter determining fluorophore performance in 2PFM. Due to different selection rules21 fluorophores which perform well under one-photon (1P) excitation, may not perform in the same manner under two-photon (2P) excitation. However, to obtain effective 2P fluorescent probes the charge-transfer properties may be enhanced by introduction of conjugated spacers, like aromatic rings, which are hydrophobic22,23. Such structural modification limits water-solubility and introduces aggregation in aqueous solutions24.

Recently, we proposed charge-neutral derivatives of ThT, such as ThT functionalized with a BF2 moiety, which exhibit σ2 values ranging from 2 to 800 GM in organic solvents, depending on the molecular architecture25,26. Nevertheless, these types of molecules tend to aggregate in aqueous solutions26,27, forming two distinct classes of aggregates based on their optical properties: those exhibiting aggregation-induced emission (AIE) and those exhibiting aggregation-caused quenching (ACQ). Both phenomena can be useful in bioimaging application28–30. AIE luminogens (AIE-gens) possess enhancement of FQY upon aggregation and remarkable properties in vivo31,32, thanks to red-shifted emission, high FQY in aqueous solutions, photostability33 and multiphoton properties in aggregated state31,34. The growing popularity of AIE-gens as amyloid probes is noticeable35–38. On the other hand, large groups of fluorescent dyes act as ACQ luminogens, which upon aggregation present quenched luminescence. They also presents important application in bioimaging, since the luminescence is quenched in water, but upon disaggregation in an apolar environment can emit light in monomeric state28. This feature can be used in polarity sensing, which is also interesting aspect in amyloid studies, due to different polarity in various amyloid binding pockets39,40.

Surprisingly, the question of what form of a dye binds to amyloids in cases where dye aggregates are already present in aqueous solution usually is not addressed in the amyloid-binding studies. Fluorescence enhancement or shift in emission wavelength is described—without insight into the molecular form involved in amyloid binding. In pure solvents, the behavior of dyes is relatively straightforward: in hydrophobic media, they exist as monomers, whereas in aqueous solutions they tend to form aggregates24. These processes are typically reversible and strongly influenced by solvent polarity for a given dye structure. However, amyloids are complex structures with multiple binding sites differing in polarity, hydrogen bonding networks, and the functional groups of their constituent amino acids41,42. Due to these facts, precise description of the form of a dye that takes part in amyloid binding is crucial for the design of markers with switchable fluorescent properties.

Here, we propose 2nd generation of BF2-functionalized benzothiazoles, which have the same core, but various electron-donating, amino groups attached to 1,4-phenylene with modulated AIE, ACQ properties as fluorescent probes for amyloids. The 1st generation of dyes with the same core presented binding with amyloids25, thus similar performance is expected in case of the current series. Probes combine electron-accepting moiety (CF3-substituted benzothiazole-BF2 core) and four donors (–NMe2, pyrrolidine and their aromatic counterparts) see Fig. 1. We selected two pairs of dyes. Their photophysical properties, AIE and ACQ phenomena, and one-photon and two-photon properties have been studied before, both experimentally and with DFT calculations26. A crucial aspect of this work is evaluating how AIE or ACQ affects the performance of the dyes as amyloid probes, in relation to the form of the dyes monomers or aggregates, which is bound to amyloids. Since our dyes differ only in a single functional group (aryl/alkyl amine), evaluating their properties is highly beneficial for a better understanding of how ACQ/AIE luminogens interact with amyloids, as the structural modification is minimal yet leads to opposite effects.Fig. 1Illustration of general structure of dyes 1–4. Experimental

Materials and measurements

Bovine insulin (BI) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (I5500). Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), anhydrous, ≥ 99.9% was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Milli-Q water is obtained from purifying system from Hydrolab. Fluorescein is suitable for fluorescence, free acid (46955) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Used HCl 36% analytical grade was from Avantor and NaOH pellets (anhydrous), ≥ 98% from Honeywell.

Synthesis and characterization of probes

The synthesis and structural characterization of dyes in organic solvents 1–4 were already published.26.

Incubation and characterization of amyloids

Incubation of the bovine insulin amyloids

Bovine insulin was dissolved in HCl solution (pH ∼ 1.5). The final concentration of peptide was 2 mg/mL. The samples were incubated in an Eppendorf Mixer C for 48 h at 45 °C, with agitation set to 700 rpm. The amyloid concentration stated in this manuscript in all experiments is reported as the monomer equivalent concentration.

Characterization of amyloids on transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

2 µL solution was placed on plasma cleaned standard amorphous carbon on copper 200 mesh grid (Agar Scientific) for 30 s, blotted, and stained with additional 2 µL of uranyl acetate for 30 s. The samples were studied using Talos F200i transmission electron microscope (Thermofisher Scientific) at 200 kV accelerating voltage.

Measurements with amyloids

All dyes were initially dissolved in pure DMSO (stock had 500µM concentration), to reach 5% DMSO volume in aqueous solution with amyloids. This is a common practice in more hydrophobic fluorescent probes used for bioimaging. The samples were prepared as follows: the proper volume of dye stock solution in DMSO was mixed with water and pure DMSO was added to reach 5% volume of that solvent in the final solution, then amyloid solution (prepared as in “Incubation of the bovine insulin amyloids”) addition was followed by dilution using the same solution that was used for amyloid incubation—water with HCl (pH ~ 1.5). We tested if dilution with water (pH ~ 7) alter binding to amyloids, we did not observe significant differences in terms of enhancement of fluorescence in the same region of wavelengths was presented. Additionally, for dyes without amyloids, changes in pH shifted absorption maxima only by few nm.

Absorption and fluorescence spectroscopy

One-photon absorption spectra were measured with a Jasco V-670 spectrophotometer in quartz cuvettes from Hellma. Stock solution of dyes was prepared by dissolution in DMSO (500uM) and all solutions were prepared prior to use. In final samples with amyloids volume fraction of DMSO was 5%. Fluorescence and excitation spectra were carried out using an FS5 Spectrofluorometer (Edinburgh Instruments) equipped with a Xenon lamp. One-photon excited fluorescence spectra was measured on FS5 spectrofluorometer from Edinburgh Instruments. Preparation of solutions was the same for all dyes. For measurements with different solvents quartz cuvettes from Hellma with 1cm light path were used. Excitation wavelengths for dye 1 and 2 was 390m, while for dye 3 and 4 was 400nm.

Fluorescence quantum yield

The FQY was measured using the SC-30 Integrating Sphere Module for a FS5 spectrofluorometer from Edinburgh Instruments. Amyloid concertation for FQY was chosen based on the highest fluorescence enhancement for dye concentration. All dyes had final 1 μM concertation, while amyloids 100μM (100 × ratio between concentrations of dye and amyloids) in solution of 5% DMSO. The same content of amyloids in each sample ensures the same scattering. Reference sample for integrating sphere was the same concertation of amyloids in 5%DMSO to ensure the same scattering as sample. Absorption of these concentrations were proper for using integrating sphere. Error of FQY determination by Integrating Sphere Module is 2%, which is provided value by the producer.

Fluorescence lifetime

Time-correlated single photon counting (TCSPC) was employed to obtain fluorescence lifetime decay using an FS5 Edinburg Instruments spectrofluorometer, equipped with a 450 nm ps pulsed diode laser source: LED EPLED-450. Fitting of data was done in program dedicated to FS5 Edinburg Instruments spectrofluorometer - Fluoracle.

Saturation binding studies/dissociation constant

The solution of insulin amyloids (increasing concentration) was added to dyes and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C (constant concentration c = 20 nM at a level that avoided self-aggregation (ACQ and AIE) of dyes)26. The solutions were measured in quartz cuvette on FS5 Spectrofluorometer (Edinburgh Instruments). All points were tested in triplicate. The averaged values were plotted as a function of concentration to obtain Kd. Mean value with standard deviation was calculated and Michaelis–Menten curve was fitted in OrginPro 9.0. Fluorescence was collected through a microscope objective (Nikon Plan Fluor, 40×, NA 0.75), and 2PEF signals were collected in the epifluorescence mode on microscope body. Samples and references were illuminated with output power set between 20-30mW depending on measured dye (reference always was measured with the same power as sample). Results and discussion

Dyes were synthesized according to the protocol described previously26. We investigated one-photon properties of dyes upon binding to amyloids like one-photon absorption (1PA) in UV–Vis range, one-photon excited fluorescence (1PEF), one-photon excitation (1PE), FQY, fluorescence enhancement, fluorescence intensity enhancement and saturation binding studies. We selected model amyloid system: bovine insulin amyloids, since it provides relatively uniform amyloids, and because it provides relatively uniform fibrils that form a stable colloidal suspension rather than large aggregates that sediment out of solution14,44. This enables optical measurements in solution over extended periods of time. Obtained amyloids were characterized by transmission electron microscope (TEM), which confirmed the formation of mature fibrils (Fig. S1). The amyloid concentration stated in this manuscript in all experiments is reported as the monomer equivalent concentration. Dyes 1 and 2 were almost non-fluorescent in aqueous solution with 5% DMSO volume fraction, due to ACQ, while dye 3, 4 presented intense fluorescence due to AIE effect. These observations are in line with previous studies regarding aggregation of dyes in aqueous solutions26 (Table S1). Fluorescence intensity of dyes was monitored in solutions with increasing amyloid concentrations. In Fig. 2 we can see clear tendency that emission increased upon increasing amyloid concentration, what indicates specific interaction with mature fibrils of amyloids. Due to low emission intensities of probes 1 and 2 in aqueous solutions, their fluorescence intensity enhanced enormously upon binding to amyloids (around 350 and 550 times, for dye 1 and 2, respectively). On the contrary, for probe 3 and 4 fluorescence enhancement upon binding was relatively low, between 1.6 and 5.5-fold, respectively. The fold of enhancement was studied in both the nanomolar (nM) and micromolar (μM) concentration regimes. Due to increasing solvent polarity dyes 1 and 2 showed a red-shift in λem (Fig. S2a, b) whereas dyes 3 and 4 did not follow such a dependence (see Fig. S2c, d). As expected for push–pull molecules, we observed also lower values of FQY in polar DMSO (values between 0.2 and 9.5%) as compared to non-polar solvents, like THF or CHCl3, where values up to 100% were obtained—see Table S1. This observation may be further helpful for deeper understanding of microenvironment of dyes inside the binding pockets of amyloids.

We calculated dissociation constant values (Kd) from saturation binding curves presented in Fig. S3 (Table 1). We can observe that overall dyes with aromatic amines, being AIE dyes, show noticeable difference in binding than ACQ dyes, which is visible based on Kd values. AIE dyes have similar Kd with value 297 ± 50 nM and 243 ± 86 nM, for dye 3 and dye 4, respectively. For ACQ dyes the values are different, dye 1 has two-times higher Kd value than dye 2 (Kd = 694.7 ± 66nM and Kd = 318 ± 24 nM, respectively). Firstly, it may be surprising, since the difference between dye 1 and 2 is only in –NMe2 group and pyrrolidine group. To assess whether the observed fluorescence enhancement is specific for mature fibrils rather than any insulin monomers, emission spectra were recorded after incubating the dyes with a solution of bovine insulin monomers. As shown in Fig. S4, emission intensity for dyes with monomers is significantly lower than with amyloids. Results indicate clear fluorescence enhancement upon interaction of dyes with amyloid fibrils, and not with a protein itself.

A comprehensive characterization of one-photon optical properties (1PA, 1PE, 1PEF, FQY) was conducted for all molecules bound to amyloids, using solutions with saturated fluorescence intensity, corresponding to amyloid concentrations ten times higher than the dye concentrations (100:1, ratio). Summary of positions of 1PA maxima (λ1PA) and λem is presented in Table 1. All spectra of 1PA, 1PE, 1PEF for the dyes are presented in Fig. S5. Dyes 1, 2, 3 present similar maxima of 1PA upon binding to amyloids (around 440nm), while dye 4 has 1PA significantly blue-shifted (1PA maximum at 420nm). Dyes 1, 2 present almost similar λem around 485nm, while for dyes 3 and 4 the emission is red-shifted: for 3 to 556nm, for 4 to 537.5nm. Difference in λem upon binding for pair of dye 1 and 3 is clearly visible on Fig. S7 presented in SI. FQY upon binding to amyloids has higher values for ACQ molecules, with value 82% and 96% for dye 1 and 2, respectively. AIE molecules present lower values of FQY upon binding with amyloids, with large difference between the dyes: 70% and 37% for 3 and 4, respectively.

An intriguing question is about the form of the molecules that bind to amyloid fibrils in the presence of pre-existing dye aggregates in solution: do the dye aggregates themselves interact with the fibrils or does binding to amyloids is performed only by dye monomers.

First let’s focus on ACQ dyes. Comparison of optical properties of dyes aggregates (in the same solution as used for amyloid studies, namely 5% DMSO solution) and monomers (measured in THF) is presented in Fig. 3 a, b. Both forms- aggregated and monomeric dyes—were compared to give broader overview on differences in optical properties (1PA and 1PEF). Here we chose THF as a solvent, where monomers of dyes were confirmed26. Comparing absorption spectra of dye monomers and aggregates, we see clear differences in the shape of spectra and spectral ranges. Absorption of dyes aggregates presents sharp band at 356 nm and a tail above 400 nm. The same dyes in THF present one main band at 427 nm, so significantly red-shifted in comparison to the most pronounced band in absorption spectra of aggregates. After addition of amyloids, absorption looked similar as in THF, with one main band around 446 nm and 448 nm, for dye 1 and dye 2, respectively—see Fig. S7 a. Similarly, λem for dye 1 (484 nm) and dye 2 (485 nm) in amyloids (Fig. S5a, b) remain similar as λem in THF (485 nm and 489 nm for dye 1 and dye 2, respectively). The values of FQY are also highly similar between THF and solution of amyloids for dye 1 and dye 2 (dye 1 and dye 2 have FQY in THF equal 83% and 100%, while in amyloids 82% and 96%)—see Tables 1 and S1. On the contrary, in the aggregated form dye 1 and dye 2 have very low FQY, 1% and 2%, respectively26. Considering information from absorption, emission position and FQY for alkyl-derivatives we can conclude that dye 1 and dye 2 bind to amyloids as monomers, in a microenvironment similar to THF. To confirm our hypothesis, we additionally measured fluorescence lifetime (τ). From our previous studies, we know that dyes in aggregated form and monomer state present various fluorescence lifetimes26. We measured values of τave for dyes in the same solution with and without amyloids addition and compared to τave values of dye monomers and aggregates measured previously (see Table 2). Detailed data about lifetime components is presented in Tabel S2. For dyes 1 and 2 bound to amyloids, τave values correspond to those measured for monomers of dyes in THF. Before amyloid binding aggregated form of the dyes, present longer τave, similar as in the case of aggregates of dyes measured in THF/water mixtures (see Table 2). Now, let us discuss AIE probes. 1PA for dye monomers and in aggregate form (in the same solution in which amyloids are dissolved, for details see experimental section) do not present profound changes (Fig. 3c, d). There is one main absorption band for monomers and aggregates between 350–500 nm, similar as in case of dyes bound to amyloids (Fig. S6c, d). At a closer look, in the 1PA of aggregates and in amyloids, the absorption band is broader and with features at longer wavelengths, which is marked with an arrow in the Fig. S6c, d. In the literature, there are evidences that such broadening might be a fingerprint for non-monomeric state of dyes.47 Fluorescence of dye 3 and 4 in aggregated state is blue-shifted compared to monomer state with higher intensity (Fig. 3), blue-shift is also present upon binding to amyloids (Table 1). These dyes exhibit a limited linear dependence of λem on solvent polarity (Fig. S2c, d). While an effect of polarity is observed, particularly for dye 3, the emission maximum does not follow a red-shift with increasing solvent polarity, suggesting the involvement of additional environmental factors influencing the emission position. In the case of dye 4, a deviation from the expected trend is observed, with the emission maximum being more red-shifted in acetonitrile (ACN) than in DMSO. Described shifts in emission do not give us clear answer regarding surrounding microenvironment of molecules. FQY of dye 3 is higher in aggregated forms—aggregates in 5% DMSO had FQY of 36%, the same value as measured previously for aggregates in THF/water mixture26, while monomers in THF had FQY equal to 11%. Next, we measured fluorescence lifetime data and observed that AIE dyes present almost identical values of τave in amyloids (dye 3: τave = 3.68 ns and for dye 4: τave = 7.00 ns) as.

in aggregated form before binding—see Table 2. Fluorescence lifetime for these two cases is similar as in the case of aggregates of dyes measured in THF/water mixtures (for dye 3: τave = 3.82 ns and for dye 4: τave = 7.72 ns)26. These values of τave are significantly longer than τave for monomer state in THF26 (for dye 3: 0.96 ns and for dye 4: 2.61 ns). These observations suggest that dye aggregates, rather than monomers, are the dominant form bound to the amyloid structures in case of dye 3 and dye 4. The observed enhancement of fluorescence may result from restricted molecular motions or specific interactions, such as π–π stacking with aromatic side chains in insulin and aggregates of dyes 3 and 4.

Molecules studied here have high two-photon absorption cross-sections (up to 600 GM) in CHCl3, which was proved by us in the previous article26. To evaluate their potential as two-photon probes for amyloid detection, we measured σ2 of dyes bound to amyloids. It is not popular approach for assessing 2PA, however can give more detailed information about two-photon properties and potential performance of fluorescent probe in bioimaging14,25. Additionally, we aimed to assess whether 2PA measurements support our hypothesis that alkyl-amine derivatives bind to amyloids as monomers, whereas aryl-amine derivatives bind as aggregates, with two representative dyes 1, and dye 3.

Two-photon excited fluorescence (2PEF) technique using femtosecond pulsed laser excitation (see Experimental Section) was employed to determine σ243. To confirm observation of two-photon process, we measured the dependence of 2PEF intensity on the incident laser power, P (see Fig. S8). All samples present quadratic dependence, which indicates 2P process. We performed 2PEF measurements in a wide range of excitation wavelengths and obtained σ2 and two-photon brightness (σ2,B) spectra of dyes bound to mature insulin fibrils. Figure 4a, b compares the σ2 values of dyes to their 1PE spectra. We observe that for dye 1 in 2PA spectra there are two maxima, at 860 nm and 900 nm, indicating that the most prominent 1PA band at 450 nm is no longer the most effective in 2P regime. More complex story is revealed for aryl-substituted amine. For dye 3, the maximum of 2PA is also slightly blue-shifted as compared to the maximum of 1PE. However, the most intriguing part is a small value of σ2. From previous experimental data and DFT calculations26 we expected dye 3 to have higher σ2 than dye 1 (see Fig. 4c, d, where σ2 of dye 1 and dye 3 in amyloids is compared to σ2 of the same dyes in CHCl3). The differences between dye aggregates and monomers of dye 3 are minor in the one-photon regime, while their two-photon response is different. Based on theoretical principles, this is possible: since 1PA and 2PA follow different selection rules21, the same electronic transition may be either enhanced or suppressed in 2PA compared to 1PA. In the literature there are examples of 2PA modulation regarding dyes aggregates49, compared to their monomer state. In J-aggregates (head-to-tail arrangement of dye molecules) 2PA can be enhanced50,51, while in H-aggregates (molecules are stacked face-to-face) 2PA is usually limited49. However, not all aggregates fall into these two groups, which makes prediction of optical properties uncertain.

Conclusions

We demonstrated that subtle modifications to the functional groups of molecules can substantially affect both, their optical properties and binding modes in amyloid structures. Alkyl-substituted amines with ACQ properties were found to bind to amyloid fibrils predominantly in a monomeric form, whereas aryl-substituted amines (with AIE properties) exhibited predominant binding as aggregates. These observations were supported by one-photon measurements, including 1PA, 1PEF, and τ analyses. To address the ambiguities observed for dye 3, 2PA measurements were additionally performed, providing further insight into the binding characteristics of monomeric and aggregated dye species, in the amyloid environment. The measured σ2 values in amyloids were comparable to those observed for dye aggregates, confirming that the aggregated form of dye 3 is bound to amyloids. Thus, 2PA can help to distinguish the form of molecules (monomers or aggregates), which were hard to differentiate using 1PA, 1PEF. Moreover, evaluation of the two-photon performance of fluorescent probes upon binding to their target analytes provides a clearer understanding of which molecules exhibit superior properties upon binding since 2PA might differ significantly. It is important in the view of bioimaging. Additionally, we show that the two-photon brightness of dye 1 and dye 3 bound to amyloids reaches 243 GM and 73 GM, respectively, which are values suitable for applications in two-photon bioimaging48. Our findings regarding the different form of dyes binding to amyloids may contribute to a better understanding of small molecule–amyloid interactions in aqueous environments, particularly considering that organic dyes often exhibit phenomena such as AIE and ACQ. These insights could also be valuable for molecular dynamics simulations, as incorrect assumptions about whether monomers or aggregates are bound to amyloids may lead to inaccurate predictions of binding sites, binding energies, and the overall stability of dye–amyloid complexes.
