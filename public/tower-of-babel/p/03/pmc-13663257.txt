Moonlighting intention and turnover intention among Chinese nurses: latent classes and associated factors
Authors: Zhaoping Liu, Yingxia Liu, Jie Zheng, Lu Chai, Fei Liu, Liuyi Zhang, Yang Liu, Min Jin, Bei Hu, Li Wang, Siyao Ni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe rise of the gig economy has increased moonlighting among healthcare professionals. In China, nurse moonlighting and multi-site nursing practice remain emerging and context-dependent phenomena. Existing studies largely describe behaviors, with limited insight into subgroups and mechanisms underlying moonlighting intention among nurses.ObjectiveThis study aimed to identify latent classes based on Chinese nurses’ moonlighting intention and turnover intention, and to examine factors associated with membership in these classes.DesignA multicenter cross-sectional survey with item-level latent class analysis was conducted.Setting(s)Nursing staff in medical institutions in seven geographic regions of China (northeast, north, central, east, south, northwest, southwest).ParticipantsRegistered nurses (n = 1,879) from 21 hospitals in 7 cities across 7 provinces recruited via convenience sampling.MethodsData were collected June–October 2024. Measures included demographics (gender, age, years employed, professional title, marital status, education, income, etc.), the Occupational Identity Scale, the Perceived Organizational Support Scale, and a Moonlighting Intention Scale. Latent class analysis identified joint classes of moonlighting and turnover intention. Univariate analyses and multinomial logistic regression were used to identify factors associated with class membership.ResultsA four-class solution was selected as the preferred model for the joint patterns of moonlighting intention and turnover intention (Entropy = 0.89; LMR/BLRT p < 0.001). The four classes were labelled as the Low MI–Low TI class (21.13%), High MI–Low TI class (23.36%), High MI–Moderate/High TI class (39.49%), and Uncertain MI–Low/Uncertain TI class (16.02%). Using the Low MI–Low TI class as the reference, nurses whose spouses did not have stable employment, those aged 25.1–30 years, those working in primary hospitals, and those with higher occupational identity had higher odds of belonging to the High MI–Low TI class, whereas higher perceived organizational support was associated with lower odds of membership in this class. For the High MI–Moderate/High TI class, spouse’s unstable employment, unmarried/divorced/widowed status, male gender, age 25.1–30 years, and working in a primary hospital were associated with higher odds of membership, while no children, more than 15 years of practice, formal establishment, higher income, higher occupational identity, and higher perceived organizational support were associated with lower odds of membership. For the Uncertain MI–Low/Uncertain TI class, age 25.1–30 years was associated with higher odds of membership, whereas monthly income of 7,001–12,500 RMB was associated with lower odds.ConclusionThe relationship between moonlighting intention and turnover intention among Chinese nurses is heterogeneous rather than uniformly positive. Moonlighting intention should not be interpreted simply as a sign of withdrawal or impending turnover. Profile-specific retention strategies are needed, particularly for nurses with both high moonlighting intention and elevated turnover intention. Strengthening occupational identity, perceived organizational support, employment security, income protection, and career development pathways may help reduce turnover-related risk while addressing nurses’ diverse work and career needs.

Introduction

Nursing workforce shortages and nurse retention have become major challenges for health systems worldwide. The State of the World’s Nursing 2025 report indicated that the global nursing workforce increased from 27.9 million in 2018 to 29.8 million in 2023, but a shortage of 5.8 million nurses remained in 2023, with substantial inequities across regions and countries [1]. In China, the rapid ageing of the population, increasing chronic disease burden, and growing demand for high-quality care have further intensified pressure on the nursing workforce. Recent multicenter and multi-provincial studies have shown that turnover intention remains prevalent among Chinese nurses and is associated with factors such as job satisfaction, quality of working life, income, workload, workplace violence, and sense of belonging [2, 3]. Because high turnover intention may exacerbate workforce instability and compromise care quality, identifying modifiable individual and organizational factors related to nurses’ retention has become an important issue in nursing management.

Alongside these workforce challenges, changes in the digital economy and labor market have expanded opportunities for multiple jobholding, commonly referred to as moonlighting or side-hustling [4, 5]. Moonlighting generally refers to holding an additional paid job while maintaining a primary occupation [6, 7]. In the healthcare sector, related terms such as multiple jobholding and dual practice have been used to describe health workers’ engagement in additional employment across institutions or sectors [8–10]. For nurses, moonlighting may be driven by “push” factors, including financial pressure, unstable employment, low income satisfaction, and limited organizational resources, as well as “pull” factors, including professional development, autonomy, skill diversification, and career exploration [8, 11, 12]. In China, nurse moonlighting and multi-site nursing practice remain at an early stage of policy and practical exploration [13, 14]. In recent years, the National Health Commission of China has promoted new nursing service models, including “Internet Plus Nursing Services”, which allow qualified nurses to provide extended nursing services after institutional assessment and under regulated quality and safety management. However, these models remain policy-guided and institutionally regulated, and should not be equated with unrestricted or mainstream nurse moonlighting. Although emerging models such as Internet Plus Nursing Services and multi-site practice have created new possibilities for flexible nursing work, traditional hospital management systems, high workload, limited time and energy, and market-related risks may restrict wider adoption [11, 15]. Therefore, moonlighting intention among Chinese nurses should be understood as an emerging and context-dependent work intention rather than as an established mainstream practice.

The relationship between moonlighting and turnover intention is theoretically complex. On the one hand, multiple jobholding may provide supplementary income, professional learning opportunities, and skill diversification, thereby helping employees meet personal and career needs while remaining in their primary jobs [15–17]. Research on dual practice among health professionals suggests that secondary work may sometimes increase work-life satisfaction and contribute to retention in the public sector [10]. On the other hand, moonlighting may also increase workload, role conflict, fatigue, and burnout, all of which are relevant to nurses’ intention to leave and patient safety [18]. Evidence from South African nurses indicated that moonlighting was associated with a higher intention to leave their primary jobs [19]. Similarly, labor economics research suggests that multiple jobholding may function either as a strategy for human-capital accumulation or as a stepping stone to occupational mobility [17]. These findings indicate that moonlighting should not be interpreted as a uniformly beneficial or harmful phenomenon; rather, its meaning may depend on the extent to which it coexists with turnover intention and on the personal and organizational context in which it occurs.

From a theoretical perspective, perceived organizational support and occupational identity may help explain why nurses with similar moonlighting intention differ in turnover intention. Organizational support theory proposes that employees form general beliefs about the extent to which their organization values their contributions and cares about their well-being [20]. Based on social exchange theory, nurses who perceive greater organizational support may feel stronger reciprocity, attachment, and commitment to their employing institution, thereby reducing withdrawal cognition. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis found a moderate negative association between perceived organizational support and turnover intention among nurses [21]. Occupational identity reflects the extent to which nurses recognize the value, meaning, and social contribution of their profession [22]. Higher occupational identity may strengthen nurses’ professional belonging and reduce the likelihood that external work opportunities are interpreted as substitutes for the primary nursing role. Studies among Chinese nurses have also shown that professional or occupational identity is negatively associated with turnover intention [23]. Therefore, occupational identity and perceived organizational support may be particularly important in distinguishing nurses whose moonlighting intention reflects development-oriented exploration from those whose moonlighting intention coexists with elevated turnover intention.

Although previous studies have provided valuable insights, several questions remain insufficiently explored. First, many studies on moonlighting or multiple jobholding among nurses have focused on actual behavior or prevalence, whereas moonlighting intention has received less attention [12, 24]. Intention is important because it may represent an earlier cognitive stage before actual moonlighting behavior occurs and may be more sensitive to organizational and policy interventions. Second, previous research has often examined moonlighting intention and turnover intention as separate linear outcomes, which may overlook heterogeneity within the nursing population. Nurses with high moonlighting intention may not form a homogeneous group: some may intend to moonlight while remaining committed to their primary job, whereas others may view moonlighting as a pathway toward eventual departure. Third, evidence from the Chinese nursing context remains limited, especially regarding how occupational identity, perceived organizational support, family responsibilities, employment conditions, and institutional context jointly relate to different patterns of moonlighting and turnover intention.

Latent class analysis (LCA) provides a person-centered method for identifying unobserved subgroups based on patterns of responses across multiple indicators [25]. Compared with variable-centered approaches, LCA can reveal qualitatively distinct profiles and provide a basis for more targeted nursing workforce management. Applying LCA to moonlighting intention and turnover intention simultaneously may help clarify whether moonlighting intention among nurses is more likely to represent a retention-compatible strategy, a turnover-related risk signal, or an uncertain transitional state.

Therefore, this study aimed to identify latent classes of Chinese nurses according to their joint response patterns for moonlighting intention and turnover intention, and to examine factors associated with membership in these classes. The use of latent class analysis allowed us to describe heterogeneity within the nursing population rather than treating moonlighting intention and turnover intention as uniform linear outcomes. This approach may help clarify whether moonlighting intention is accompanied by consistently high turnover intention or whether different moonlighting–turnover intention profiles exist. The findings are expected to provide evidence for more differentiated nursing workforce management and retention strategies in the Chinese healthcare context.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional design with latent class analysis was used.

Participants

Between June 3 and October 3, 2024, we conducted multicenter convenience sampling of registered nurses in seven geographic regions of China (northeast, north, central, east, south, northwest, and southwest). One city from each region was selected based on feasibility and access rather than through probabilistic sampling. Hospitals in each city were selected based on existing cooperation and their willingness to participate. We sought to include one tertiary, one secondary, and one community hospital in each city on a convenience basis when possible. Researchers disseminated the online questionnaire for registered nurses using internal communications and QR codes until the target sample size was reached. In secondary and tertiary hospitals, a minimum of four departments (internal medicine, surgery, obstetrics and gynecology, and pediatrics) were invited; in community hospitals, all registered nurses were invited due to reduced staffing levels. Institutional approvals were secured prior to data collection. A total of 21 hospitals from 7 cities in 7 provinces participated.

The inclusion criteria are: (1) registration as a nurse; (2) formal employment by the hospital; (3) provision of informed consent to participate in this survey. The exclusion criteria encompass nurses who were on leave or participating in training courses during the data collection period.

Data collection

The survey was administered via the online platform “WenJuanxing” (https://www.wjx.cn/). After obtaining authorization from the responsible persons at the participating medical institutions, the questionnaires were disseminated, accompanied by relevant information regarding the study. The survey aimed to identify moonlighting–turnover intention profiles and factors associated with these profiles among Chinese nurses. To prevent data omission and redundancy, participants were required to answer all questions and were permitted to complete the questionnaire only once.

Measures

Moonlighting intention scale

This study used the Moonlighting Intention Scale developed by Seema, which was originally designed for IT professionals and later translated into Chinese for use across different occupational groups [4, 26]. The scale includes 7 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale, f from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree, with higher scores indicating stronger moonlighting intention. As no nurse-specific moonlighting intention scale was available in China, this scale was used to assess general moonlighting intention among nurses. To evaluate its applicability to the nursing context, six experts in nursing management, clinical nursing, and scale evaluation assessed the relevance and applicability of each item. The I-CVI values ranged from 0.83 to 1.00, and the S-CVI/Ave was 0.93, indicating good content validity. Several item wordings were refined based on expert feedback. The Cronbach’s α coefficient in this study was 0.933.

Turnover intention scale

This study used the Mobley Employee Turnover Intention Scale, as modified by Griffeth and Sellaro. The scale contains four items rated on a five-point scale to assess employees’ intention to leave. Higher scores indicate stronger turnover intention of employees to leave, whilst a lower score denotes a diminished intention to depart [27]. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale in this study was 0.749.

Occupational identity scale

This study used the Occupational Identity Scale developed by Tyler and McCallum to assess occupational identity among Chinese nurses. The scale comprises 10 items, with a 5-point Likert scale from “completely inconsistent” (1 point) to “completely consistent” (5 point). Higher scores indicate stronger occupational identity [28]. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale in this study was 0.941.

Perceived organizational support scale

This study used the simplified Chinese version of the Perceived Organizational Support Scale, originally developed by Eisenberger [20] and translated by Farh [29]. This scale includes 8 items. Minor wording changes were made to the item content in accordance with the research objectives and target population, emphasizing the respondents’ perceptions of organizational support in the medical work context. A 5-point Likert scale was employed, ranging from “strongly disagree” (1) to “strongly agree” (5). A superior score signifies a higher perceived level of organizational support within the medical profession. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale in this study was 0.911.

Data analysis

Responses on the seven moonlighting and four turnover items (5-point Likert) were recoded to three ordered categories—1–2 = disagree (1), 3 = uncertain (2), 4–5 = agree (3)—to improve interpretability and model stability. This pre-specified trichotomization (a) reduced sparsity from skewed extreme options while preserving ordinality; (b) aligned levels with practice-relevant decision states (disagree/uncertain/agree); and (c) harmonized indicators to limit response-style noise.

Using these categorical indicators, latent class analysis (LCA) was conducted in Mplus 8.3. Class enumeration used AIC, BIC, and aBIC (lower = better) plus Entropy (closer to 1 = better separation), with LMR-LRT and BLRT (p < 0.05) comparing K versus K–1 classes. Final selection also considered parsimony and avoidance of very small classes. Joint modelling of moonlighting and turnover items yields classes that reflect their co-occurrence patterns, providing more actionable guidance than modelling each construct separately.

Post-classification analyses were performed in SPSS 25.0: categorical variables as n (%), continuous variables as mean ± SD; group differences tested with ANOVA and χ²; and correlates of class membership estimated by multinomial logistic regression (α = 0.05).

Results

From June to October 2024, we distributed 1,926 questionnaires to relevant medical institutions and received 1,879 valid responses, yielding an effective response rate of 97.56%. Among the respondents, 87.2% were female; the mean age was 35.364 ± 12.568 years, and the mean years in practice was 12.948 ± 8.634 years. Concerning educational qualifications, 74.2% of the participants had a bachelor’s degree. Married nurses accounted for 74.8%, of which 82.4% had spouses with stable employment. Regarding professional titles, 52.9% held intermediate professional titles. Furthermore, 36.6% were employed in the internal medicine department, and approximately 58.6% worked in tertiary hospitals. Approximately 56.0% of nursing personnel had a monthly income ranging from 2,501 to 7,000 RMB. Refer to Table 1 for specifics.

Common methodological bias test

Harman’s single-factor test was used to conduct factor analysis on all items of the research variables. The results showed that there were three common factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, which explain 68.83% of the total variance. The first factor accounted for 37.47% (< 40%), suggesting no severe common method bias.

Latent classes of moonlighting intention and turnover intention

Exploratory LCAs were estimated using item-level, three-category indicators from both scales. Models with one to five classes were fitted. As the number of classes increased, AIC, BIC, and aBIC decreased, and both LMR-LRT and BLRT remained significant (p < 0.05). Although the five-class solution showed lower AIC, BIC, and aBIC values, the additional class did not provide a sufficiently distinct and substantively interpretable pattern beyond the four-class solution. Considering model parsimony, interpretability, entropy, and the practical usefulness of the classification, the four-class solution was selected as the preferred model (Table 2). Average posterior probabilities by class ranged from 0.884 to 0.981, indicating good class separation. The sample sizes of the four classes were 397, 439, 742, and 301, respectively. Overall, the four-class solution showed acceptable classification quality and was retained for subsequent analyses. Figure 1 shows the conditional response probabilities across latent classes. To further illustrate the item-level characteristics of the four latent classes, the mean scores of moonlighting intention and turnover intention items across classes are shown in Fig. 2.

Characterization and naming of latent classes

In the four-class solution selected as the preferred model, Class 1 (Low MI–Low TI; 21.13%) showed predominantly disagree responses on both moonlighting (MI1–MI7) and turnover (TI1–TI4) items. Class 2 (High MI–Low TI; 23.36%) exhibited agree probabilities > 0.60 on most moonlighting items, while turnover items were mainly disagree. Class 3 (High MI–Moderate/High TI; 39.49%) was characterized by high probabilities of agreement on moonlighting items and uncertain/agree on turnover. Class 4 (Uncertain MI–Low/Uncertain TI; 16.02%) showed mostly uncertain responses on moonlighting items and disagree/uncertain on turnover.

Univariate analysis of latent classes of nurses’ moonlighting and turnover intention

The mean total scores for moonlighting intention and turnover intention in the sample were 23.298 ± 7.193 and 11.202 ± 3.318, respectively. Significant differences (p < 0.05) were observed in perceived organizational support and Occupational Identity scores across the four latent classes. Significant differences were noted in baseline characteristics such as gender, age, years in practice, professional title, administrative position status, institution type, labor relation, average monthly night shifts, personal monthly income, marital status, spousal employment stability, and number of children (P < 0.05). Multinomial logistic regression of factors associated with latent class membership

Multinomial logistic regression was conducted to examine factors associated with latent class membership, using the Low MI–Low TI class as the reference category. Compared with the Low MI–Low TI class, nurses whose spouses did not have stable employment, those aged 25.1–30 years, those working in primary hospitals, and those with higher occupational identity had higher odds of belonging to the High MI-Low TI class. By contrast, nurses with a monthly income of ≤ 2,500 RMB and those with higher perceived organizational support had lower odds of belonging to this class. For the High MI-Moderate/High TI class, unmarried/divorced/widowed nurses, nurses whose spouses did not have stable employment, male nurses, nurses aged 25.1–30 years, and nurses working in primary hospitals had higher odds of class membership. In contrast, nurses with no children, those with more than 15 years of practice, those with formal establishment, those with higher monthly income, and those with higher occupational identity and perceived organizational support had lower odds of belonging to this class. For the Uncertain MI-Low/Uncertain TI class, nurses aged 25.1–30 years had higher odds of membership, whereas those with a monthly income of 7,001–12,500 RMB had lower odds of membership. Occupational identity and perceived organizational support were not significantly associated with membership in this class. Detailed estimates are presented in Table 4.

Discussion

This study used latent class analysis to examine the joint patterns of moonlighting intention and turnover intention among Chinese nurses. Four distinct latent classes were identified: the Low MI–Low TI class (21.13%), the High MI–Low TI class (23.36%), the High MI–Moderate/High TI class (39.49%), and the Uncertain MI–Low/Uncertain TI class (16.02%). These findings suggest that the relationship between moonlighting intention and turnover intention is heterogeneous rather than uniformly positive. Although previous studies have often linked moonlighting with increased turnover risk among nurses, the present findings indicate that this association may differ across subgroups [19, 30]. This suggests that moonlighting intention among nurses may not necessarily reflect an intention to leave the profession or their current institution. Rather, it may be related to financial needs, career exploration, skill development, or income supplementation, rather than merely reflecting dissatisfaction with their current nursing position.

The identification of the High MI–Low TI class is particularly important. This class suggests that moonlighting intention may coexist with relatively low turnover intention. Therefore, moonlighting should not be interpreted only as a withdrawal behavior. For some nurses, it may represent a complementary employment strategy rather than a direct precursor to resignation [10, 16]. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that multiple jobholding may serve different functions, including income supplementation, skill development, and career exploration [31]. Compared with the Low MI–Low TI class, nurses working in primary hospitals were more likely to belong to both the High MI–Low TI class and the High MI–Moderate/High TI class. This finding suggests that nurses working in primary hospitals may have access to fewer organizational resources, or lower income expectations, which may be associated with stronger moonlighting intention and turnover-related cognition. Conversely, in better-supported organizational environments, moonlighting intention may be less likely to coexist with elevated turnover intention.

The multinomial logistic regression results further clarified the different associations of occupational identity and perceived organizational support across latent classes. Higher occupational identity was associated with greater odds of belonging to the High MI–Low TI class, but lower odds of belonging to the High MI–Moderate/High TI class. Higher perceived organizational support was associated with lower odds of belonging to both the High MI–Low TI class and the High MI–Moderate/High TI class, compared with the Low MI–Low TI class. Because the per-point ORs for occupational identity and perceived organizational support were close to 1, these associations should be interpreted as modest. When expressed per one standard deviation increase, occupational identity corresponded to higher odds of membership in the High MI–Low TI class (OR ≈ 1.29) and lower odds of membership in the High MI–Moderate/High TI class (OR ≈ 0.81). Similarly, a one-SD increase in perceived organizational support corresponded to lower odds of membership in both high-moonlighting profiles (OR ≈ 0.72 for both classes). These results suggest profile-specific associations rather than primary causal effects. Therefore, occupational identity should not be interpreted simply as a factor that reduces moonlighting intention. Instead, nurses with stronger occupational identity may still consider moonlighting, but they may be less likely to combine moonlighting intention with elevated turnover intention [4, 32].

Labor and personnel relations also appeared to be relevant to the High MI–Moderate/High TI class. Compared with contract employment, formal establishment was associated with lower odds of membership in the High MI–Moderate/High TI class. This may reflect the relevance of employment security, benefits, and clearer career expectations to a lower likelihood of coexisting high moonlighting intention and elevated turnover intention. In a Serbian public health study, secondary employment was associated with a higher intention to leave compared to their non-moonlighting counterparts [33]. Previous studies have reported an association between moonlighting and higher turnover intention among nurses [19]. Overall, these results suggest that moonlighting intention may be more likely to coexist with turnover intention when organizational resources, employment security, or career development opportunities are limited. Conversely, stronger organizational protection and support may help distinguish nurses who consider moonlighting without elevated turnover intention from those with higher turnover-related risk. Nursing managers may consider strengthening organizational support, income security, employment protection, and transparent career development pathways to reduce the likelihood that moonlighting intention coexists with elevated turnover intention.

Family-related factors were also associated with latent class membership. Compared with the Low MI–Low TI class, male nurses were more likely to belong to the High MI–Moderate/High TI class. Nurses with no children were less likely to belong to the High MI–Moderate/High TI class than those with two or more children. This may indicate that greater family responsibility is associated with a higher likelihood of combining moonlighting intention with turnover intention. In addition, nurses whose spouses lacked stable employment were more likely to belong to both the High MI–Low TI class and the High MI–Moderate/High TI class. This suggests that household financial pressure may be an important background factor for moonlighting intention. This explanation is consistent with previous evidence that multiple jobholding may be related to household financial constraints and career exploration [31].

Income showed a class-specific association with moonlighting–turnover intention classes. Nurses with a monthly income of ≤ 2,500 RMB were less likely to belong to the High MI–Low TI class than those with a monthly income of 2,501–7,000 RMB. This may reflect limited time, energy, access to secondary employment opportunities, or greater dependence on the current position among very low-income nurses. Evidence from different industries similarly suggests that the association between moonlighting and turnover may depend on the nature of secondary employment and the skills involved [34]. In contrast, nurses with higher monthly income were less likely to belong to the High MI–Moderate/High TI class. These findings suggest that income may shape moonlighting–turnover intention profiles differently across subgroups.

For the Uncertain MI–Low/Uncertain TI class, nurses aged 25.1–30 years were more likely to belong to this class, whereas nurses with a monthly income of 7,001–12,500 RMB were less likely to belong to this class. Occupational identity and perceived organizational support were not significantly associated with this class. This suggests that the uncertain profile may be more closely related to early career exploration and income conditions than to professional or organizational attachment. Future studies should further explore whether this group represents a transitional state between stable retention and higher-risk profiles.

These findings have several implications for nursing management. First, nurses with high moonlighting intention should not be treated as a homogeneous risk group. For nurses in the High MI–Moderate/High TI class, interventions may focus on strengthening organizational support and supporting occupational identity, enhancing income security, and providing clearer promotion pathways. Primary hospitals and contract-employed nurses may require additional attention because limited resources and weaker employment security may increase the likelihood that high moonlighting intention coexists with turnover intention. For nurses in the High MI–Low TI class, management strategies may focus on providing legitimate developmental opportunities, flexible scheduling, and skill-enhancing internal work opportunities. Family-related support, such as flexible scheduling and family-friendly policies, including childcare support, may help reduce the combined pressure of family responsibility, moonlighting intention, and turnover intention.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design only allows associations to be identified and cannot establish causal relationships. It remains unclear whether moonlighting intention contributes to turnover intention, whether turnover intention promotes moonlighting intention, or whether both are shaped by common individual, family, and organizational factors. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify these dynamic relationships. Second, although the sample covered seven major geographical regions in China, nurses from tertiary hospitals accounted for a relatively large proportion of the sample. This may limit the representativeness of nurses from primary healthcare institutions. Third, some potentially relevant factors were not included, such as hospital-specific policies regarding moonlighting, job satisfaction, local economic conditions, and the actual type, frequency, and intensity of secondary employment. These factors may further explain differences in moonlighting–turnover intention profiles and should be considered in future research. Fourth, the use of most-probable class assignment may underestimate standard errors despite good classification indices. Finally, trichotomizing Likert responses may reduce information granularity, although this approach improved model interpretability and reduced sparsity in the latent class analysis. Future studies using longitudinal designs and more detailed measures of actual moonlighting behavior are warranted.

Conclusions

Latent class analysis identified four moonlighting–turnover intention profiles among Chinese nurses, indicating substantial heterogeneity in the relationship between moonlighting intention and turnover intention. Moonlighting intention should not be viewed uniformly as a sign of withdrawal or impending turnover. Family economic conditions, age, institutional type, employment security, income, occupational identity, and perceived organizational support were associated with different profiles. Profile-specific retention strategies may focus on strengthening organizational support, occupational identity, income security, employment security, and career development pathways, particularly for nurses with both high moonlighting intention and elevated turnover intention.
