Cost analysis of integrating depression treatment into chronic care in Malawi: evidence from a cluster randomised controlled trial

Introduction

Depression is a highly prevalent mental health condition that affects nearly 300 million people1 2 and is ranked as the leading cause of disability worldwide.3 In low- and middle-income countries (LAMICs), the mutually reinforcing relationship between poverty and mental illness further perpetuates high prevalence,4 with approximately 80% of the global burden of years lived with disability from anxiety and depression accounted for by LAMICs.5 Yet fewer than a quarter of individuals in these settings have access to depression care.6 7

A major reason for the treatment gap is that depression care is perceived as expensive, particularly in low-resource settings with competing priorities and limited budgets.8 9 An approach to overcome this is to integrate depression care into existing healthcare services.10 In some LAMICs, integration involves training primary care providers to identify, treat and refer patients with depression.10,12 It also entails strengthening supply chains to ensure psychotropic medications are available in primary healthcare facilities, and that depression care leverages existing administrative capacity and data systems.

Another cost-saving measure is to provide group-based behavioural therapies, which are less expensive per patient treated than one-on-one services. Interventions with group-based formats include Problem-Solving Therapy,13 Problem Management Plus (PM+)14 and Interpersonal Psychotherapy (IPT).15 Prior research indicates these interventions are feasible for implementation. For example, a study in earthquake-affected communities in Nepal documented a greater than 95% treatment retention rate for participants enrolled in group PM+, indicating its feasibility.16 While another in Pakistan qualitatively showed feasibility among women randomised to receive group PM+ in a conflict setting.17 Additionally, these interventions have been shown to be effective at achieving symptom remission among specific populations with depression.18,20 For instance, a study in Uganda showed that participants in group IPT observed significantly lower mean depressive symptom scores compared with a control group at a 6-month follow-up.20 However, the effectiveness of group-based PM+ has only been assessed in a small number of populations, leading the WHO to promote an expanded set of evaluations around the globe.

A third approach to generating cost savings in mental health service delivery is task-shifting, which involves redistributing service delivery to those with fewer formal qualifications, such as lay counsellors. In Pakistan, PM+ delivery by lay health workers was highly effective in treating anxiety and depression.21 Similarly, in Uganda, group support psychotherapy (GSP) delivered by lay health workers for depression among people living with HIV was deemed adequate for treating depression.22 A third study, in India, found that psychoeducation, IPT and antidepressant therapy (ADT) provided by lay health workers were cost-effective approaches for alleviating depression and anxiety symptoms.23

In Malawi, depression is among the primary contributors to disability24; however, it remains undetected and untreated among a large majority of the population.25 26 This is driven, in part, by resource constraints characteristic of LAMICs. Malawi also has one of the highest HIV prevalence rates in the world,27 and the government has established a robust longitudinal HIV care system.28 In 2015, the Ministry of Health (MoH) collaborated with Partners In Health (PIH), a non-profit healthcare delivery organisation in Neno District, Malawi, to expand the HIV care delivery platform to screen, diagnose and treat a portfolio of non-communicable diseases, including hypertension, type II diabetes, asthma, epilepsy and psychotic disorders such as schizophrenia. This new model is called IC3 (Integrated Chronic Care Clinics).29 This makes the Malawian HIV care delivery system ideal for integrating depression care. In 2021, as part of a cluster randomised controlled trial (RCT), the MoH and PIH furthered their partnership and expanded IC3 to integrate depression screening, diagnosis and treatment.30

In this study, we conducted a cost analysis of integrating depression care into IC3, using a cost accounting approach: time-driven activity-based costing (TDABC). Specifically, we examined the marginal cost of introducing group PM+ (primarily delivered by lay counsellors) and ADT (which was being prescribed by trained unspecialised clinicians) into IC3 in Neno District, Malawi. We hypothesised that the per-patient cost of service delivery would range from US$150 to US$200 per patient for a five-session course of PM+ or an eight-session course of ADT. This study contributes to the literature on the financial feasibility of depression care—by evaluating a stepped care model of group PM+ and ADT. This evaluation was also implemented in a high needs setting with immediate policy implications: namely, a rural, underserved community in Malawi among adults with chronic health conditions, with implementation jointly executed with the Ministry of Health to evaluate scalability.

Methods

Study design

This study represents a cost analysis of integrating depression care within IC3 as part of a cluster RCT)—Integrated Chronic Care Clinics- Depression Module (IC3D)—which began in 2021 at all IC3 clinics in Neno District. Clinics were randomly assigned to one of five clusters, constituting two to three clinics that received intervention roll-out at 3-month intervals. Participants were then recruited into the study using an open cohort design at the individual level within each cluster. To prevent contamination, we tracked individuals using client ID information from cloud-based electronic health records linked across facilities. If a client attended a different facility, we would be able to identify them as ineligible until the relevant start date. Further information on the RCT can be found elsewhere.30 The RCT design of the trial does not factor into the methods of the current study, as the current study is an assessment of costs corresponding to treatment arms of the trial.

This study quantifies the additional resource requirements for providing depression care services within the existing IC3 health service delivery platform. Observed expenditures within the first year of delivering group PM+ and ADT to a cohort of 487 patients were used to conduct the analysis.

Study setting

Neno is in the mountainous Southern region of Malawi. It has a population of 140 000 and is one of the poorest and most rural districts in the country.31 Most residents live below the poverty threshold, and access to basic social services such as clean water, sanitation, secondary education and electricity is below 50%.31 32

Neno District operates 14 health facilities, ranging from 12 dispensaries and health centres, serving up to 500 patients per month, to two secondary-level hospitals caring for over 500 patients monthly. Patients with chronic health conditions in Neno District are enrolled and treated at IC3 within these facilities at no out-of-pocket cost to the patient. IC3 offers comprehensive treatment for various chronic care conditions, including HIV/AIDS, hypertension, diabetes, asthma, epilepsy and serious mental illness (bipolar disorder, schizophrenia). In 2020, before integrating depression care, the IC3 model served approximately 13 000 patients annually.

Sample

Active patients at IC3, aged 18 or older, who were diagnosed with major depressive disorder were eligible for inclusion in the study. Patients were screened for depressive symptoms by data clerks using a locally validated version of the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-2, PHQ-9).33 34 Patients who scored greater than zero on the PHQ-2 and above nine on the PHQ-9 further received a diagnostic assessment based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-IV) criteria, administered by lay counsellors and counsellors with bachelor’s degree level training. Patients with a positive diagnosis proceeded to enrolment. All patients provided informed consent prior to enrolling. Those without a diagnosis of depression received a psychoeducation session and continued to receive screening regularly, consistent with the open cohort design.

Care model

The IC3D model provides depression care across all 14 health facilities in Neno District using an evidence-based stepped care model. Patients diagnosed with depression are provided with psychoeducation on depression and informed of depression treatment options. Patients diagnosed with moderate depression (PHQ-9 score of 10–14) are encouraged to receive group PM+, while group-based PM+ and ADT are jointly promoted for those diagnosed with moderate-to-severe and severe depression (PHQ-9 score greater than or equal to 15). Patients with moderate depression may receive ADT if PM+ is declined. The treatment regimen is re-evaluated for patients who remain depressed after 12 weeks of receiving treatment.

Malawian lay counsellors and psychosocial counsellors administer five sessions of group-based PM+ for 1.5–2.5 hours, weekly. PM+ is a low-intensity psychological intervention that applies cognitive and behavioural strategies to support clients facing adverse circumstances to manage and address their problems.14 ADT is provided by trained non-specialised clinical officers, with a daily dosage of 20 mg of fluoxetine as the first-line treatment and 25 mg of amitriptyline as the second-line. ADT is re-evaluated at each monthly visit for efficacy and side effects. Counsellors and clinical officers were trained to provide these interventions and receive routine one-on-one supervision.

Measures

Patient measures at the baseline include depression symptom severity, other clinical measures and demographic characteristics. Depression symptom severity was measured using the PHQ-9 questionnaire. Other clinical measures include diagnoses and biomarkers related to HIV/AIDS, hypertension, type II diabetes, asthma and epilepsy. Demographic information includes age, sex, educational background and employment status.

We obtained unit costs from four sources: invoices, receipts, catalogued expenses and payroll information stored within Partners In Health’s financial management software (Serenic Navigator).35 Staff time was valued at total salary, including vacation and benefits allowance. Capital costs for equipment and infrastructure were annualised over the item’s useful life, assuming linear depreciation.

Unit costs were gathered in 2021–2022 and were assigned to one of five cost categories: human resources, infrastructure, equipment, consumables and indirect costs. See online supplemental table 1 for a detailed overview of individual line items corresponding to these cost categories that were involved in care delivery. All expenditures were adjusted for inflation to 30 April 2025. For expenditures denominated in US dollars (USD) (eg, staff salaries), we adjusted using the US consumer price index (CPI)—medical category.36 For expenditures denominated in Malawian Kwacha (eg, fuel), we first converted costs to USD using the actual exchange rate prevailing in the year they occurred, thereby capturing local purchasing power at that time. Subsequently, these converted USD amounts were inflated to 2025 using the US CPI to maintain consistency and comparability across all costs.

Analysis

We conducted TDABC, a cost accounting framework that identifies activities encompassed within IC3D services and then records the economic costs of carrying out these activities. Specifically, costing corresponds to resource consumption associated with the standard care path that a client follows in IC3D.37 Economic costs are derived from two parameters: the unit costs of each resource consumed throughout the care delivery process, and the quantity of time clients typically interact with that resource.38 To estimate set-up costs like training, we employed an ingredient-based micro-costing approach whereby unit prices and quantities were indexed and aggregated.

Activities comprising IC3D services included screening for depressive disorder, diagnosis and enrolment in depression care services, administration of PM+, clinical supervision of PM+, administration of ADT and clinical supervision of ADT. Following TDABC best practices, we compiled time estimates through expert elicitation: providers and staff who routinely perform the activities provided self-reported time estimates. Costs per activity were calculated as the product of time estimates and capacity cost rates (ie, per minute costs) for each of the resources involved in each activity. To account for uncertainty in time estimates, we performed a one-way, deterministic sensitivity analysis—in which activity-based time estimates for allocated human resources, equipment and infrastructure were modulated ±20% from point estimates. Costs of PM+ and ADT per patient treated were then calculated using the aggregated costs across all relevant activities. Overall costs were computed as the product of per patient costs and the total number of clients who engage with each activity.

We elected to quantify economic rather than financial costs, meaning we incorporated the costs of all resources, including those donated or volunteered. We also assumed a societal perspective, including opportunity costs incurred by clients. Opportunity costs were calculated in terms of the average travel time to/from intervention sessions and time within the sessions themselves. We assumed that the value of this time was equivalent to earnings that could have been achieved from median GDP per capita in 2023 based on a 40-hour work week over 48 weeks per year.

We estimated the overall costs of implementing depression care (PM+ and ADT) in the first year of the clinical trial and separately per patient costs for PM+, ADT and combined PM+ and ADT. For PM+, we estimated the cost of a full course of treatment: five sessions, once per week. Likewise, for ADT, we estimated a course of treatment as eight monthly sessions and corresponding medication over this interval. For combined PM+ and ADT, we estimated a course of treatment inclusive of the five-session PM+ and eight-session ADT treatments. We examined the effects of missing data using a pattern mixture model approach, which imputed missing values using a weighted mean approach.

Our analysis’s base case for comparison was the absence of depression care within the IC3 care delivery platform. In rare circumstances when a patient is at imminent risk of suicide, they may be transported to a district hospital for acute services; however, we conservatively estimated the baseline cost of service delivery for depression care in Neno District was US$0.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design of the clinical service delivery model. They were not involved in conducting, reporting or disseminating this research. Patients were consulted, informed and consented to participate in the trial, the clinical components of which this manuscript examines. Additionally, a Data Safety Monitoring Board, which included Malawian clinical and technical experts, informed the overall implementation and protection of human subjects.

Results

Sample characteristics

From 1 September 2021, through 30 April 2022, 487 adults in Neno District were identified with major depressive disorder and enrolled in the clinical trial. Of these, 398 received treatment: 31 individuals received ADT only, 263 individuals received PM+ only, 104 individuals received PM+ and ADT, and 89 individuals received neither (typically a result of recovering from depression before eligibility for treatment).

Table 1 provides an overview of the clinical and demographic characteristics of the sample according to each treatment modality. Overall, enrollees were predominantly female and middle-aged, with approximately 17% having completed years of education equivalent to a primary degree and almost two-thirds (64%) employed at enrolment across the three modalities. Based on the nature of the cohort, which consisted of patients with chronic conditions, we observed high levels of comorbidities. As expected from the stepped model of depression care, recipients of ADT had higher average PHQ-9 scores at enrolment compared with recipients of PM+. Cost estimates

Online supplemental table 3 shows the total cost to the health system of integrating depression care services into the IC3 platform in Neno District was $62 806 (USD, 2025) for the first year of the IC3D trial: 1 September 2021 to 31 August 2022. This estimate reflects district-level coverage of screening, diagnosis, enrolment and treatment of depression care, upfront and recurrent training of personnel, and the opportunity costs borne by clients over the period. Accounting for uncertainty in time estimates of human resources, infrastructure and equipment allocated to IC3D tasks, we consider lower and upper-bound estimates to be $53 853 and $71 759, respectively.

Figure 3 presents the per patient costs of PM+, ADT and combined PM+ and ADT, segmented by cost category: human resources, infrastructure, equipment, consumables and indirect costs. PM+ totalled $90.21 per patient treated—including provider training, client screening, diagnosis and enrolment, five PM+ sessions, PM+ clinical supervision and indirect costs. The primary cost drivers were human resources (44.7%, $40.32 per patient), including counsellors and clinical supervisors, and consumables (29.8%, $26.86 per patient), chiefly petrol used to transport providers to/from sessions. From a clinical perspective, the PM+ sessions themselves only accounted for 35.6% ($32.07 per patient) of total costs. PM+ training accounted for a more significant percentage (40.9%, $36.88 per patient), while supervision accounted for a smaller proportion (19.5%, $17.55 per patient).

ADT totalled $137.53 per patient treated—inclusive of provider training, client screening, diagnosis and enrolment, eight ADT sessions, ADT clinical supervision and indirect costs. Again, the primary cost drivers were human resources (54.0%, $74.31 per patient), including clinical officers and clinician supervisors, and consumables (31.6%, $43.50 per patient), chiefly fluoxetine tablets. From a clinical perspective, the ADT sessions themselves accounted for 53.5% ($73.55 per patient) of total costs, including medication dispensing. ADT training accounted for 26.2% ($36.04 per patient) of the total cost, while supervision accounted for 17.6% ($24.23 per patient).

Combined PM+ and ADT totalled $208.96. The total cost per patient treated of the combined PM+ and ADT treatment modality included intake, screening, diagnosis, enrolment, as well as the combined costs of five PM+ and eight ADT sessions, factoring in related training and supervision for each.

Although this manuscript focuses on cost estimates for integrating depression screening, diagnosis and treatment into the IC3 platform in Neno, we also measured how the scale-up of depression care has translated into outputs. From a training perspective, this included the initial training of six lay counsellors, four psychosocial counsellors, four data clerks, one data officer, six clinical officers and one clinical psychologist. These individuals also participated in quarterly refresher training.

For screening and diagnosis, the PHQ-2 was administered 15 002 times across the 14 health facilities in the district. Of these, 21% screened positive. Next, 2465 individuals who screened positive on the PHQ-2 were further screened on the PHQ-9, 32% of whom screened positive. Subsequently, 724 individuals were assessed with the diagnostic tool, of whom 70% were diagnosed with major depressive disorder (n=506). Finally, 487 of 506 consented to participate.

As noted above, those receiving PM+ were invited to participate in five PM+ sessions, of which the median attendance was four sessions. Among those invited to receive ADT, sessions were offered every month, though (in practice) participants came to facilities less frequently. We therefore modelled the cost of eight ADT sessions over 8 months. Over the course of five PM+ sessions or eight ADT sessions, clinical supervisors were present two times per patient to review the quality of clinical care.

Discussion

This study found that district-wide screening at IC3, diagnosis and treatment for major depressive disorder—covering a catchment of approximately 140 000 individuals—cost $62 806 in the first year of implementation. This reflects the societal cost from the combined cost from the health system perspective and patient opportunity cost. From a societal perspective, the per patient cost was $90.21 for five sessions of group-based PM+, $137.53 for eight sessions of ADT and $208.96 for combined PM+ and ADT, with five and eight sessions, respectively.

Compared to other depression care interventions in past clinical trials in similar low-resource settings, these cost estimates appeared to be consistent, although modestly lower when accounting for training, supervision and support services.39 40 For example, Aldridge and colleagues reported implementation costs for psychoeducation, ADT and follow-up care delivered by primary care workers in Nepal as $367 per patient treated (USD, 2025) and service delivery costs to be an additional $21 per individual.39 In another example, focused on applying mhGAP guidelines and primary care-based counselling for severe depression in India, Patel and colleagues observed a societal cost of $325 per patient treated (USD, 2025),41 higher than the joint costs of PM+ and ADT in this study.

There are a few potential reasons for observing the comparatively lower costs of depression care in the Malawian setting, when viewed from an investment perspective. First, depression care was integrated into an existing healthcare delivery platform and, therefore, could draw from an existing set of resources—at least to a degree. Second, the PM+ modality was group-based, allowing cost-sharing to the extent that participants were participating in treatment at the same time. Third, salaries are lower in Malawi than in other LAMIC settings where previous trials have been conducted, leading to lower observed costs.42 Costs would be further reduced in a future version of the intervention that relied exclusively on lay counsellors.

While these costs were slightly lower than some estimates observed elsewhere, they were generally comparable. This is accounted for, in part, by the consistent price of fluoxetine across settings,43 which was a considerable cost driver of ADT. Motor vehicle fuel also contributed substantially to the costs of PM+ and ADT in support of transportation, and the price in Malawi (a landlocked country without significant fuel reserves) is higher than in other settings.44 45 Finally, while a degree of task shifting allowed lay counsellors to deliver PM+, aspects of clinical care—including clinical officer time for ADT sessions and psychologist time for clinical supervision—were necessary to ensure protocol fidelity.

We also note that at least a couple of costing studies report substantially lower costs of depression care, relative to those observed in our study or most prior studies. In these instances, the marginal costs of depression care are generally circumscribed to a narrow service component. For example, in a modelling study focused on treating depression among people living with HIV in sub-Saharan Africa, Zhong and colleagues reported an incremental cost of $0.26 per patient (USD, 2025) for depression care, which was narrowly circumscribed to consumable costs for fluoxetine.46 In another example, focused on GSP in Uganda, authors reported a $6225 marginal cost (USD, 2025) for offering GSP to 578 individuals, approximately $11 per patient.22 This did not incorporate costs of transportation or opportunity costs to patients, and costs for supervision are unreported.

A review of cost structures offers additional insights into the implementation of depression care in lower-resource settings. First, we observed that PM+ sessions and ADT sessions accounted for less than 50% of PM+ and ADT overall. Training costs were particularly high: a function of per diems, lodging, transportation and food over multiple days with a sizeable cohort of individuals. This is similar to observations made elsewhere in the literature about implementing depression care in low-resource settings, including in sub-Saharan Africa.39 47 In a more developed digital infrastructure setting, remote computer-based learning as part of routine work responsibilities could reduce these costs.48

Second, the cost of screening, diagnosis and related intake procedures was non-trivial: about 10% of total costs. In prior studies, these elements have been omitted, with investigators focusing solely on the cost of delivering therapy.46 However, these elements were critical to integrating depression care services throughout the district. Past evidence has evinced the importance of psychoeducation, protection of privacy during screening and standardised diagnostic procedures for ensuring that prospective clients offer accurate responses about their symptoms and that individuals with the greatest needs are correctly identified for treatment.23

Viewed more broadly, the total expenditure ($62 806) for district-level expansion of depression care underscores the potential financial feasibility of scale-up throughout Malawi. Based on Neno District’s population (160 000), scale-up throughout the country would translate to roughly $8.2 million, or approximately 3.3% of Malawi’s annual health budget in real terms.49 Evidence indicates that alleviation of depression also translates to a return on investment greater than 1:1, as adults with alleviated symptoms can contribute productively to the labour force and draw less on social welfare and others’ resources.50

The findings of this study have important policy implications. In the Malawian context, this study is aligned with Malawi’s policy consideration and healthcare goals, which acknowledge the high burden of mental health conditions like major depressive disorder and seek to strengthen mental healthcare.51 Additionally, this assessment can be used to inform work in other similarly situated low-resource settings, which are also often associated with a high burden of mental health conditions.5 However, it is important to note that cost estimation is only one aspect of policy analysis and needs to be contextualised alongside other considerations such as intervention effectiveness, system capacity and barriers to care.

Limitations

This study contains several limitations. First, we measured the marginal costs of introducing depression screening into the IC3 platform. We did not perform a cost analysis of the entire IC3 platform, though this has been published previously.52 Second, our cost accounting approach (TDABC) relies on expert-elicited time estimates for spent with clients when performing various activities. We have modelled uncertainty in these estimates. Third, we do not have estimates of clinical symptom remission among the 487 participants, which precluded our ability to conduct a formal cost-effectiveness analysis. Fourth, our study sample is disproportionately female across all three treatment modalities, limiting our ability to conduct this analysis on a more demographically balanced sample. Based on consultation with providers, our understanding is that this may be driven by more heightened stigma in discussing mental health issues among males compared with females. Past studies focusing on mental health in Malawi have similarly had a large majority of female representation.26 53 54 Fifth, enrollees within the trial were limited to those with comorbid chronic conditions and met eligibility criteria and therefore are unlikely to be representative of all individuals in Neno District, which limits the generalisability of our findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the financial feasibility, as well as documenting the clinical resources and corresponding investments, of integrating depression care services with group-based PM+ and ADT into the HIV care delivery system in a rural, low-resource setting in sub-Saharan Africa. With depression representing a leading cause of disability in Malawi and elsewhere, these contribute to a growing evidence base indicating that integrated depression care services can be strategically delivered in communities such as Neno District.
