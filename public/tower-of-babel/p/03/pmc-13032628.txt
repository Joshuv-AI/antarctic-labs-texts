Colitis-associated gut–vagus–brain signaling integrates tumorigenesis and neuroinflammation: comparative regulation by vagotomy and atropine
Authors: Duo Yun, Cong Yang, Xue Wang, Le Zhou, Mengzhen Jia, Jiarui Liang, Xinyu Hu, Liyuan Niu, Fengfeng Mo, Jiafeng Wang, Zhigang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColitis-associated cancer (CAC) is frequently accompanied by neuropsychiatric comorbidities, yet the underlying gut-brain signaling pathways remain elusive. Emerging evidence highlights the gut-brain axis, particularly the vagus nerve, as a pivotal mediator linking gastrointestinal pathology with neuropsychiatric dysfunction. This study aimed to dissect the dual roles of vagal signaling in a murine CAC model and to evaluate the therapeutic potential of the muscarinic antagonist atropine on CAC.MethodsThe azoxymethane/dextran sulfate sodium (AOM/DSS) was adopted to induce CAC mice model. We employed unilateral vagotomy and pharmacological intervention with atropine, a muscarinic antagonist, in CAC mice. We assessed colon tumorigenesis, intestinal inflammation, anxiety/depression-like behaviors, and neuroinflammation of CAC mice under vagotomy and atropine treatment. Vagotomy and retrograde viral tracing were used to verify the essential role of vagus nerve signaling in activating neurons in brain regions linked to inflammation-induced depression and anxiety, thereby clarifying the gut-neuro-brain circuit mechanism.ResultsThe findings revealed that CAC progression was associated with depression- and anxiety-like behaviors. Vagotomy significantly alleviated these behaviors, reduced c-fos expression in the NTS and lateral habenula/ midbrain-hindbrain boundary (LHb/MHb), and retrograde tracing confirmed a functional gut–NTS–LHb/MHb circuit activated by intestinal inflammation. Moreover, vagotomy improved CAC-induced behavioral deficits by reducing GFAP expression, alleviating neuroinflammation, though it failed to inhibit tumor growth or intestinal inflammation. In contrast, atropine treatment not only improved behavioral deficits but also reduced tumor number, downregulated cholinergic signaling and tumor markers, suppressed intestinal pro-inflammatory cytokines, and markedly ameliorated depression- and anxiety-like behaviors.ConclusionThese findings demonstrate the vagus nerve as a key gut-brain pathway linking CAC to depression and anxiety. Vagotomy disrupts inflammation-driven signaling, whereas atropine preserves anti-inflammatory vagal activity, positioning it as a promising therapeutic strategy for CAC-related anxiety and depression.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-03752-z.Keywords: Colitis-associated colorectal cancer (CAC), Vagus nerve, Intestinal inflammation, Atropine

Introduction

Colorectal Cancer (CRC) is the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally. Its clinical manifestations commonly include altered bowel habits, hematochezia, diarrhea, and unintended weight loss. While the exact etiology of CRC remains incompletely understood, it is widely recognized to be influenced by genetic factors, environmental exposures, and lifestyle choices [1]. Chronic inflammation is a recognized pathogenic factor in multiple gastrointestinal disorders, including ulcerative colitis (UC), Crohn’s disease, and CRC [2–3]. Notably, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is closely linked to the pathogenesis and progression of CRC, with IBD patients exhibiting a 2-3-fold increased risk of CRC compared to the general population [4]. CRC arising from IBD is termed colitis-associated colorectal cancer (CAC) [5]. Moreover, growing clinical evidence shows bidirectional links between gastrointestinal pathologies (e.g., CRC, IBD, intestinal infections, irritable bowel syndrome) and neuropsychiatric disorders [6]. This suggests that shared features of gastrointestinal diseases, particularly chronic pain and inflammation, may contribute to abnormal brain function via the gut–brain axis. The gut–brain axis has therefore emerged as an important therapeutic target for managing gastrointestinal and neuropsychiatric comorbidities, including IBD and depression.

As a key component of the gut–brain axis, the vagus nerve can detect gut-derived mediators including lipopolysaccharides (LPS), TNF-α, and IL-1β through direct and indirect mechanism [7–10]. These signals are transmitted to the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS), the primary afferent hub of the vagus nerve, which subsequently projects to multiple CNS regions, such as the hippocampus, amygdala, and thalamus, thereby modulating neural function [11–14]. Our recent work demonstrates that vagus nerve transection-vagotomy significantly ameliorates Salmonella-induced intestinal infection and anxiety-like behaviors [15]. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms through which the vagus nerve regulate brain neural circuits to influence depression and anxiety-like behaviors remain unclear.

Meanwhile, accumulating evidence indicates that the vagus nerve plays a critical driver of gastrointestinal tumorigenesis and progression. The vagus nerve can act directly on tumor cells through contact or by releasing neurotransmitters, thereby influencing tumor’s growth and proliferation [16]. Among these pathways, muscarinic receptor M3 (M3R) is the primary acetylcholine (ACh) receptor involved in the progression of gastrointestinal tumors [16, 17]. For instance, in gastric cancer models surgical denervation (vagotomy) or pharmacological inhibition of M3R significantly suppresses tumor incidence and progression [18]. Mechanistic studies further reveal that cholinergic M receptor activation has been shown to promote intestinal epithelial hyperproliferation and drive tumorigenesis in murine models. In contrast, Atropine, a muscarinic antagonist, exerts anti-tumor effects by blocking M3R, thereby suppressing cholinergic markers, downregulating immunosuppressive molecules, and enhancing anti-tumor immune responses [19]. Taken together, these findings raise a critical question: whether modulation of cholinergic signaling downstream of vagal activity, potentially involving M3R, can simultaneously alleviate CAC-induced depression- and anxiety-like behaviors while suppressing colorectal tumorigenesis.

To address this, we employed the AOM/DSS-induced CAC mouse model, combined with vagotomy and atropine intervention, to explore the role of the vagus nerve in mediating CRC development and its associated depressive and anxiety-like behaviors. To investigate the exact mechanisms by which the vagus nerve modulates brain neural circuits to influence depression- and anxiety-like behaviors, we assessed neuronal activation and neuroinflammatory responses in depression- and anxiety-related brain regions of CAC mice following vagotomy and atropine administration. Furthermore, retrograde tracing was further employed to delineate the specific neural pathway transmitting gut-derived inflammatory signals to the brain. Concurrently, we examined the impact of vagal cholinergic signaling on colorectal tumorigenesis by quantifying tumor biomarkers, intestinal inflammation, proliferative indices, and cholinergic activity. In summary, our study proposes that targeting vagal signaling, particularly vagal cholinergic signaling, may represent a novel and effective strategy to mitigate inflammation-driven colorectal carcinogenesis and its comorbid neuropsychiatric manifestations.

Materials and methods

Animals

All animal experiments were conducted using adult (8–10 weeks old) male BALB/c mice purchased from Dossy experimental animals CO., LTD. (Chengdu, China). The mice were given adaptive feeding for 1 week before the experiments. All experimental mice were conventionally housed in controlled conditions (a 12 h light/dark cycle, the humidity of 50 ± 15%, and the temperature at 22 ± 2 °C), with laboratory standard food (AIN-93 M) and water provided. The animal test protocol follows the guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals (eighth edition, ISBN-10: 0-309-15, 396-4), and was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Northwest A&F University (IACUC 2025 − 0917).

Experimental design and treatments

Experiment 1 (Vagotomy treatment)

The BALB/c mice were randomly allocated to the following groups (n = 10). (1) CON group: Mice underwent sham surgery as the control. (2) CAC group: mice underwent sham surgery followed by AOM/DSS as the model. (3) Vagotomy group (CAC + VAG): Mice underwent unilateral cervical vagotomy followed by AOM/DSS. The AOM/DSS-induced CAC model was injected intraperitoneally 12.5 mg/kg AOM (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). One week after AOM injection, the mice received 2% (w/v) DSS (MP Biochemicals, CA, USA) in their drinking water for 7 days, after which they were returned to normal water for 14 days. This step was repeated two more times. The experimental procedures were adapted from established protocols in previous studies [20]. Throughout the modeling period, changes in mice’ body weight were recorded daily. Disease activity index (DAI) was determined by body weight loss, blood infestation and stool consistency to assess the degree of colon damage. On the eleventh week, colonoscopy was performed on mice to assess the success of the modeling. Based on endoscopic findings, tumors were scored as follows: 1 (very small but detectable tumors), 2 (tumors covering 1/8 of the colon circumference), 3 (tumors covering 1/4 of the colon circumference), 4 (tumors covering 1/2 of the colon circumference), and 5 (tumors covering more than half of the colon circumference). Behavioral experiments were carried out the next week.

Experiment 2 (Retrograde tracing)

BALB/c mice were randomly allocated to the following groups (n = 10): (1) CON (NTS viral injection); (2) CON (LHb/MHb viral injection); (3) DSS (NTS viral injection); (4) DSS (LHb/MHb viral injection). All DSS-treated groups received 2% DSS in drinking water for 7 consecutive days to induce intestinal inflammation, while the CON groups received normal drinking water. On third day in DSS treatment, retrograde tracing viruses PRV-CAG-EGFP (BrainVTA, P03001) were stereotaxic ally injected into either the nucleus tractus solitaire (NTS) or LHb/MHb according to group assignment. Sections of brain and colorectal tissues from each group were fixed in paraformaldehyde for analysis.

Experiment 3 (Atropine intervention)

The BALB/c mice were randomly allocated to the three groups (n = 10). (1) CON group; (2) CAC group; (3) CAC + ATR group. The CAC model was constructed as described in Experiment 1. Dissolve atropine in PBS, the dosage of atropine was determined based on previous studies [21]. Mice were injected with atropine (Aladdin, Shanghai, China) (2 mg/kg) intraperitoneally once every two days, while the CON group receiving injections of PBS. Colonoscopy and behavioral tests were conducted as described in Experiment 1.

Unilateral cervical vagotomy

To avoid potential high mortality associated with bilateral cervical vagotomy, a unilateral cervical vagotomy was performed. The methods were consistent with the previous research [15]. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and a 1.5-cm ventral cervical incision was made. The right vagus nerve was isolated from the carotid artery. In mice undergoing vagotomy, at least 1 cm segment of the nerve was resected, while sham-operated mice underwent nerve isolation without transection. Subsequently, antibiotics were administered intraperitoneally to prevent infection. All animals were allowed a 2-week recovery period prior to subsequent experiments.

Stereotaxic intracranial injection

Using a stereotactic injection apparatus, retrograde tracing viruses PRV-CAG-EGFP (BrainVTA, P03001) was injected into the nucleus of the solitary tract (NTS) and LHb/MHb of 9-month-old BALB/c mice. Injection coordinates were set at M/L = 0.5 mm, A/P=-7.2 mm, D/V=-4.5 mm (NTS), and M/L = 0.25 mm, A/P=-2.06 mm, D/V=-2.5 mm (LHb/MHb). Each mouse received a 200 nL dose at a controlled rate of 100 nL/min. After the injection, a ten-minute wait allowed for virus diffusion. The injection site was sealed with sterile bone wax, and the scalp incision was sutured. After surgery, each mouse received antibiotics.

Behavior tests

All behavioral assessments (e.g., Novelty-suppressed feeding test, Tail suspension test, Marble burying test, Elevated plus maze test, and Open field test) were scored by investigators who were fully blinded to the group identity of the mice throughout the experiment to eliminate observer bias. The raw behavioral data for each individual mouse across all trials have been archived and are available for verification.

Open field test (OFT)

The open field test (OFT) was conducted to assess general locomotor activity in this study. Each mouse was gently placed in the center of a square black acrylic arena (40 cm × 40 cm × 40 cm). The mice were allowed to explore the arena freely for 5 min. Between trials, the arena was thoroughly cleaned with 75% ethanol to eliminate olfactory cues. Total distance (cm) was recorded to quantify locomotor activity.

Novelty-suppressed feeding test (NSFT)

Novelty-suppressed feeding test (NSFT) is mainly used to assess anxiety-like behavior and depression-related symptoms in rodents, such as mice. Mice were fasted for 24 h with ad libitum access to water. During testing, each mouse was placed in the center of an empty open-field arena for a 6-minute test, which contains a novel high-fat diet pellet (distinct from their regular chow). The latency to approach the novel food and take a bite was recorded.

Tail suspension test (TST)

Tail suspension test (TST), as a method to evaluate the depression behaviors of mice, was carried out according to the previous study [22]. Prior to testing, mice were gently restrained and secured for 6 min by the tail (2 cm from the end of the tail) using medical-grade adhesive tape. During the last 4 min of the test, the immobility time (in seconds) of each mouse was recorded. Immobility time was strictly defined as the absence of moving limbs and body.

Marble burying test (MBT)

The marble-burying test is primarily used to assess anxiety-like behaviors in rodents. The testing cage was covered with a 5-cm deep wood chip, upon which 20 glass marbles (15 mm in diameter) were evenly distributed. Each mouse was placed individually in the testing cage, and the number of marbles buried during a 30-minute session was recorded. A marble was considered buried when at least two-thirds of its surface area was covered by wood chips.

Elevated plus maze test (EPM)

The elevated plus maze test (EPM), which consists of two open and two closed arms (each measuring 5 × 50 cm) arms enclosed by a 30 cm high wall, is frequently used to assess anxiety-like behavior in animals. Rats were placed in the maze’s center at the time of official initiation and permitted to wander about freely for five minutes before their motions were timed and counted depending on how often they entered the open arm.

EPM is a behavioral assay widely used to evaluate anxiety-like responses in rodents. The apparatus consists of two open arms and two closed arms arranged in a perpendicular cross configuration, with a central square platform elevated 50 cm above the floor. In the test, a mouse was placed in the maze’s center facing an open arm and allowed to freely explore the maze for 5 min. The experimental apparatus should be swabbed with 75% alcohol to eliminate olfactory cues before each mouse was tested. Behavioral parameters including time spent in open arms, closed arms, and central area are automatically recorded using SuperMaze video tracking software. Anxiety levels are quantified by calculating the percentage of time accessing the open arms (OT% = [open arm time/ (open + closed arm time)] ×100).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The mice were anesthetized and decapitated quickly, and the serum and colon tissue were collected. The tissues were thawed slowly at low temperature. Serum samples were processed for the detection of corticosterone; colon samples were processed for the detection of LPS and inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-1β. All procedures were conducted according to the instructions provided with the ELISA kit (Xinle Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). A standard curve was generated to calculate the content, which was expressed as ng/µg protein.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Following euthanasia, colonic tissues (n = 3 per group) were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, rinsed with PBS, and sequentially dehydrated through an ethanol gradient (75%, 80%, 95%, and 100%). The tissues were then embedded in paraffin, and 4-µm-thick sections were prepared. After deparaffinization and rehydration through a descending ethanol series (100% → 95% → 80%), the sections were stained with H&E and imaged using an i optical microscope.

Immunohistochemistry and Immunofluorescence staining

After being anesthetized, the mice (n = 3 per group) were immediately perfused with cold normal saline, and sections of brain and colorectal tissues were fixed in paraformaldehyde for analysis. Immunohistochemistry and Immunofluorescence staining was performed according to the previously described methods [23]. The tissue was were subjected to dewaxing and dehydration using xylene and a gradient of ethanol. Subsequently, the slices were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting c-fos (1: 500, Abcam, ab18723), GFAP (1:1000, Abcam, ab7260), IBA-1 (1:500, Abcam, ab178847), M3R (1:500, Abmart, p20309), ChAT (1:500, Abways, P28329), α7nAChR (1:500, Abcam, ab216485), Ki67 (1:500, Proteintech, 28074-1-AP), COX-2 (1: 200, HUABIO, ET1610-23) and iNOS (1:200, Abways, P35228). The sections were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 30 min. After thorough rinsing, the sections were developed with DAB chromogen (Zhongshan Golden Bridge Biotechnology, China) for 10 min, followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin. Finally, the tissue sections were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series and cleared with xylene. For immunofluorescence staining, the sections were incubated with anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Servicebio, GB25303, GB21303) at room temperature for 2 h, followed by six washes with PBS. Subsequently, the sections were mounted with antifade mounting medium containing DAPI. Images were acquired using an inverted fluorescent microscope (DM5000 B, Germany), and positive cells regions were quantified using ImageJ software.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Tissue samples were homogenized in BIOZOL reagent at a 1:9 (w/v) ratio using 2.0 mL grinding tubes for total RNA extraction. RNA concentration was quantified using NanoDropOne (Thermo Fisher Scientific). According to the protocol of the ueIris II qRT-PCR System First-Strand cDNA Synthesis kit (from US Everbright Inc., Suzhou, China), reversing transcribe total RNA into cDNA. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using 2× SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix ((from US Everbright Inc., China) to measure target gene mRNA levels. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1 (Primer sequences used for semi-quantitative RT-PCR analysis). The Ct values were normalized to GAPDH as the endogenous control, and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analysis

All data were expressed as the mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were conducted using T-test and One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test (GraphPad Prism 8.0 Software, USA) for comparisons among multiple groups, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using the Prism software (GraphPad, version 9, San Diego, USA).

Results

Effect of vagotomy on tumorigenesis, intestinal inflammation, and depression/anxiety-like behaviors in CAC mice

To explore the modulatory effects of cervical vagotomy on CAC-induced tumorigenesis and depression/anxiety-like behaviors, BABL/C mice were subjected to establish an AOM/ DSS-induced CAC model, and unilateral vagotomy were performed prior to carcinogen exposure (Fig. 1A). Moreover, based on our previous findings, vagotomy does not significantly affect the normal intestinal or brain physiological status of BALB/C mice. Therefore, to minimize unnecessary surgical intervention and reduce animal use, a vagotomy-only control group was not included in this study. During the construction of the model, DAI of mice was recorded, and the number and score of tumors in the colorectum of mice were observed by endoscopy. Behavioral tests were conducted after model establishment to evaluate whether vagotomy could ameliorate depression- and anxiety-like behaviors associated with CAC.

Spleen/bodyweight serves as an indicator of systemic immune activation status. In this study, vagotomy was capable of restoring spleen weight and body weight in CAC mice to levels comparable to those observed in CON mice (Supplement Fig. 1A) (F (2, 23) = 15.9, p = 0.0407). In addition, the DAI of mice is calculated by recording changes in body weight, fecal water content, and hematochezia index during the experiment. Results showed that the DAI of CAC group was higher than CON group, while vagotomy failed to reduce the DAI. Endoscopy was used to observe the tumor of the colorectum of mice, there was no significant difference in number and scores of tumors between the CAC group and CAC + VAG group (Fig. 1B-E). H&E staining images of colon and tumor were shown in Supplement Fig. 1B-D. We observed that vagotomy showed no significance improvement effect on AOM/DSS-induced colorectal pathological damage (Supplement Fig. 1C). Furthermore, vagotomy failed to significantly ameliorate CAC-induced intestinal permeability and inflammation. Specifically, the LPS level, and pro-inflammation cytokines (IL-1β level, TNF-α, IL-17, and IL-6) of CAC mice showed no significant improvement following vagotomy (Fig. 1F-G, Supplement Fig. 1E-H). Also, vagotomy treatment failed to decrease the CAC-induced the elevation of anti-apoptosis gene, Bcl-2 (Supplement Fig. 1I).

Animal behaviors were carried out to assess the anxiety and depression of CAC mice. Assessment of depression- and anxiety-like behaviors were employed the NSFT, TST and marble-burying test. In addition, locomotor activity was evaluated using the OFT to control for potential confounding effects of altered mobility on behavioral assessments. No significant differences were observed in the total distance traveled in the OFT among groups, suggesting that the observed behavioral changes were not attributable to locomotor impairments (Fig. 1H). Notably, CAC mice exhibit anxiety-like behaviors (Marbles buried) and depression-like behaviors (Latency in NSFT, Immobility of time in TST), and accompanied by the increase of serum corticosterone levels, which is ameliorated by vagotomy (Fig. 1I-L). One-way ANOVA showed that these behavioral and endocrine alterations were significantly ameliorated by vagotomy (MBT: F(2, 23) = 4.86, p = 0.033; NSFT: F(2, 24) = 7.49, p = 0.014; TST: F(2, 23) = 7.57, p = 0.049; corticosterone: F(2, 15) = 8.35, p = 0.018). While vagotomy failed to improve tumorigenesis in CAC, our findings suggested that a mediated function of the vagus nerve in depression and anxiety associated with CAC.

Overall, vagotomy ameliorate CAC-induced anxiety and depression behaviors, while failed to affect intestinal inflammation and the occurrence and development of tumors. These findings demonstrate that unilateral vagotomy suppresses gut-brain communication, thereby mediating CAC-induced tumorigenesis, inflammation, and mood dysfunction.

Effect of vagotomy on neuronal activation in depression- and anxiety-related brain regions in CAC mice

To confirm that CAC-induced depression and anxiety are mediated by the vagus nerve, we examined neuronal activation in the NTS, along with serum cortisol levels as a biomarker of neuroendocrine hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis activity that plays a pivotal role in depression and anxiety pathogenesis. Results showed that unilateral vagotomy inhibited the neuronal activation in NTS compared to sham-operated controls, and the mRNA levels of c-fos in medulla oblongata was decreased in vagotomy-treated CAC mice (F(2, 16) = 10.24, p = 0.004) (Fig. 2A and B).

Furthermore, in order to explore the mediating mechanism of vagus nerve in depression and anxiety induced by CAC mice, c-Fos expression in depression- and anxiety-related brain regions, such as the LHb/MHb and amygdala were detected. Vagotomy significantly decreased c-Fos+ cells within LHb/MHb and amygdala respectively, compared to CAC group (LHb/MHb: F(2, 6) = 7.46, p = 0.036; amygdala: F(2, 6) = 13.66, p = 0.010) (Fig. 2C-E). The results demonstrated that vagotomy inhibited the activation of vagus afferent nerves in CAC mice and inhibit the neuronal activation of LHB-MHB and amygdala (LHb/MHb: F(2, 6) = 7.46, p = 0.036; amygdala: F(2, 6) = 13.66, p = 0.009). Using immunofluorescence staining in LHb/MHb, we observed increase in c-Fos+/GFAP+ co-localized cells in CAC mice versus wild-type controls, which was reversed by vagotomy of CAC mice (Fig. 2F).

Studies indicated that neuronal activation in LHb/MHb and amygdala may contribute to depressive and anxiety-like behaviors in CAC mice, and vagotomy alleviated these effects.

Effect of vagotomy on CAC-induced brain neuroinflammation responses

Anxiety- and depression-like behaviors are associated to neuroinflammation. Neuroinflammation was influence by astrocyte activation and inflammatory cytokines upregulation in the CNS [24, 25]. Astrocytes can also be induced by a variety of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines [26]. In addition, studies have revealed that the activation of astrocytes in the LHb/MHb can promote the onset of depressive-like behaviors [27]. To investigate the mechanism underlying vagotomy-mediated improvement of depression-anxiety comorbidity in CAC mice, we assessed glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) expression in hippocampal CA1, CA3, dentate gyrus (DG) subregions, and LHb/MHb, and IBA-1 expression in cortex.

Vagotomy significantly reduced GFAP-immunoreactive area in hippocampal and LHb/MHb compared to CAC mice (CA1: F(2, 6) = 8.10, p = 0.024; CA3: F(2, 6) = 13.90, p = 0.007; DG: F(2, 6) = 6.94, p = 0.044; LHb/MHb: F(2, 6) = 14.88, p = 0.008) (Fig. 3A-E). Moreover, vagotomy reduced IBA-1 expression in cortex (F(2, 6) = 6.83, p = 0.039), and downregulating pro-inflammatory mediators in the cerebral cortex in COX-2 and IL-1β mRNA respectively (Fig. 3F-H), and upregulating the mRNA level of anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 (Fig. 3I) (COX-2: F(2, 18) = 5.57, p = 0.014; IL-1β: F(2, 11) = 15.38, p < 0.001; IL-10: F(2, 13) = 29.39, p < 0.001). Additionally, synaptogenesis-related markers showed elevation in postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD-95) and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) expression (PSD-95: F(2, 15) = 7.24, p = 0.042; BDNF: F(2, 15) = 6.41, p = 0.016) (Fig. 3J-K).

Overall, our results confirmed that vagotomy could increase astrocyte density in the hippocampus and LHb/MHb, and inhibit neuroinflammation, thereby exerting beneficial antidepressant effects.

Effect of vagotomy on cholinergic signaling in CAC mice

To investigate the mechanisms underlying the dissociation between the amelioration of depression/anxiety-like behaviors and the lack of improvement in intestinal inflammation and tumorigenesis following vagotomy, we assessed the expression of key cholinergic signaling molecules in the colon via immunofluorescence staining. Immunofluorescence co-staining was performed to assess the co-localization of cholinergic neurons and M3R, thereby analyzing M3R activation. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that the expression of choline acetyltransferase (ChAT) and the M3R was significantly increased in CAC mice, alongside the co-localization of ChAT and M3R was also increased (ChAT: F(2, 6) = 103.4, p < 0.001; M3R: F(2, 6) = 7.40, p = 0.021) (Fig. 4A-C). A significant decrease in the expression of the α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (α7nAChR) (Fig. 4D), which plays an important role in cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway. Vagotomy treatment markedly reduced the immunofluorescence expression of ChAT, and decreased the level Ach in colon (ChAT: F(2, 6) = 103.4, p < 0.001; Ach: F(2, 15) = 4.88, p = 0.020) (Fig. 4C&E). The expression of M3R showed a decreasing trend, but it did not reach statistical significance, and the co-localization of ChAT and M3R showed no significant decrease (Fig. 4A&B). Notably, vagotomy failed to reverse the low expression of α7nAChR observed in the CRC model, with its level showing no significant difference compared to the non-vagotomized CAC group (Fig. 4D).

Retrograde tracing defines a specific vagus-NTS-LHb/MHb pathway in intestinal inflammation

To elucidate the neural circuitry linking intestinal inflammation to depressive/anxiety-like behaviors in AOM/DSS induced CAC mice, we employed pseudorabies virus (PRV)-based retrograde tracing in a DSS-induced acute colitis model (Fig. 5A). Injection of PRV into the LHb-MHb of DSS-treated mice resulted in robust EGFP⁺ labeling in the NTS (t = 4.177, df = 4, p = 0.014), compared with CON mice, indicating a direct projection from NTS to LHb/MHb (Fig. 5B-C). Moreover, PRV injection in the NTS led to abundant PRV⁺ neurons in the colon, demonstrating polysynaptic autonomic connections from colon to NTS (t = 2.836, df = 4, p = 0.047) (Fig. 5D-E). These connectivity patterns conclusively revealed a functional vagus nerve-NTS-LHb/MHb axis activated by intestinal inflammation.

Atropine alleviated the CAC-induced cholinergic signaling and tumor markers

To investigate the mechanisms underlying atropine-mediated attenuation of tumorigenesis, we assessed cholinergic signaling, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and tumor hallmarks. Given the significant role of muscarinic receptors, particularly M3R, in CRC progression, we analyzed M3R expression in the mouse colon and tumors using immunofluorescence and RT-qPCR. The study found that M3R expression in the colon of CAC mice was significantly higher than in CON mice. Atropine intervention significantly reduced both the protein and mRNA expression of M3R in the colon and tumors of CAC mice (Colon protein: F(2, 6) = 8.56, p = 0.025; Colon mRNA: F(2, 15) = 16.28, p < 0.001; Tumor protein: t = 4.155 df = 4, p = 0.014) (Fig. 7A-D and G). Furthermore, we quantitatively analyzed the expression and distribution of cholinergic neurons. The results showed that atropine intervention significantly reduced the ChAT-positive area in the colon and tumors of CAC mice (Colon protein: F(2, 6) = 33.1, p < 0.001; Tumor protein: t = 2.842 df = 4, p = 0.047) (Fig. 7E-F). The co-localization of ChAT and M3R was also reduced (Fig. 7A-B), and Ach levels in the colon of CAC mice were decreased (F(2, 12) = 6.75, p = 0.015) (Fig. 7H). These results collectively demonstrate that atropine intervention not only reduces the number of activated cholinergic neurons in the intestine of CAC mice but also suppresses both the expression and activation of M3R.

Atropine alleviated the neuronal activation in depression- and anxiety-related brain regions and neuroinflammation in CAC mice

To investigate whether the amelioration of CAC-induced depression- and anxiety-like behaviors by atropine involves the suppression of vagal signaling, we examined neuronal activation in LHb/ MHb and amygdala, which receive projections from the NTS and are critically involved in depression and anxiety. Representative images of c-fos immunohistochemistry staining of half of the brain were shown in Supplement Fig. 2G. Similar to the effects of vagotomy, atropine significantly reduced c-fos positive area in the LHb/MHb and amygdala (LHb/MHb: F(2, 3) = 32.56, p = 0.013; amygdala: F(2, 6) = 14.61, p = 0.008) (Fig. 9A-C), indicating suppression of neuronal activation in these depression- and anxiety-related brain regions. Furthermore, atropine decreased the proportion of GFAP-positive area in the hippocampus and LHb/MHb of CAC mice (hippocampus: F(2, 6) = 9.04, p = 0.039; LHb/MHb: F(2, 6) = 21.98, p = 0.005) (Fig. 9D-F), suggesting reduced astrocyte activation. Also, atropine intervention also significantly reduced the mRNA expression of TNF-α and increased the expression of IL-10 in the cortex (TNF-α: F(2, 15) = 15.61, p = 0.001; IL-10: F(2, 15) = 4.69, p = 0.055) (Supplement Fig. 2E-F), indicating that atropine can attenuate neuroinflammation caused by CAC. Collectively, these findings support the interpretation that atropine alleviates CAC-induced depression and anxiety by inhibiting vagal nerve activation decreasing the inflammatory cytokine, and restoring the astrocyte density.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that both vagotomy and pharmacological inhibition of vagal signaling with atropine effectively alleviate depression- and anxiety-like behaviors in CAC mice, accomplished by suppressing neuronal activation in mood-related brain regions and reducing neuroinflammation. Retrograde tracing experiment further confirmed that intestinal inflammation activates NTS neurons via vagal transmission, subsequently driving neuronal activation in the LHb/MHb circuitry. Notably, compared with vagotomy, atropine intervention provided additional benefits by improving intestinal inflammation and suppressing tumorigenesis through inhibition of cholinergic signaling.

A central finding of our work is the functional separation of CAC-driven depression and anxiety from peripheral tumorigenesis. Extensive evidence confirms that gastrointestinal disorders, such as CRC and UC, are frequently coexisted with neuropsychiatric conditions such as depression and anxiety. A population-based longitudinal study indicated an elevated risk of depression (19.0% vs. 12.8%) and anxiety (20.9% vs. 11.8%) in CRC patients [30]. Published studies have shown that, compared with healthy controls, patients with UC exhibit higher prevalence rates of anxiety and depression, as well as increased severity of anxiety- and depression-related symptom scales [5]. Our prior research also demonstrated that DSS-induced colitis mice consistently exhibit anxiety and depression-like behaviors alongside increased neuroinflammation [31]. The prevalent association between gastrointestinal disorders and neuropsychiatric symptoms underscores the gut–brain axis as a central pathway linking intestinal pathology to depression and anxiety-like behaviors. The vagus nerve, a pivotal component of gut-brain communication, has garnered significant attention for its role in regulating gastrointestinal homeostasis and integrating gut functions with emotional and cognitive processes. In our previous study, vagotomy alleviated Salmonella-induced gut infections induced anxiety and depression by enhancing GABA reduction to ameliorate intestinal inflammation. Importantly, vagotomy alone did not induce significant changes in body weight, intestinal inflammatory, microglial activation, or neuronal activation in the control mice [15]. These findings highlighting the vagus nerve’s essential function in transmitting gut-derived pathogenic signals to the brain. In this study, we similarly observed that vagotomy ameliorates depression- and anxiety-like behaviors in AOM/DSS-induced CAC mice (Fig. 1). Diverging from our findings, numerous studies have demonstrated beneficial effects of vagus nerve stimulation in ameliorating depression and anxiety [32]. We propose this divergence may be attributed to neuron-type specificity (e.g., cholinergic vs. non-cholinergic fibers) and the different brain-region in neuromodulation approaches [33].

Furthermore, extensive research indicates that intestinal immune cells communicate directly with afferent sensory fibers and/or the enteric nervous system through cytokines and immune mediators (e.g., IL-1β, TNF-α). These signals are ultimately relayed to the brain via vagal afferent neurons, contributing to neuropsychiatric disorders including depression and cognitive impairment [34–36]. Previous studies have demonstrated that gut inflammation–induced depression- and anxiety-like behaviors, as well as neuroinflammation, can be alleviated by vagotomy, demonstrating that gut microenvironment in inflammatory conditions drive cognitive deficits and neuroinflammation specifically through the gut-vagus-brain axis [37]. Consistently, our data show elevated intestinal pro-inflammatory cytokine in AOM/DSS-induced CAC mice. While vagotomy improved anxiety and depressive-like behaviors in CAC mice, it failed to attenuate intestinal inflammation or tumor incidence (Fig. 1 and Supplement Fig. 1). This discrepancy may arise from surgical complications of vagotomy, as the procedure disrupts vagal efferent fibers essential for the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway. The vagus nerve innervates multiple organs, and its efferent fibers are predominantly cholinergic. Acetylcholine suppresses inflammation in the gut by binding to α7nAChR and inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway; however, α7nAChR expression is suppressed in colorectal cancer models [38]. On the other hand, acetylcholine binding to mAChRs drives tumorigenesis and progression [39]. Population-based studies have found that increased expression of M3R and ChAT is associated with a higher risk of CRC and poor survival prognosis. Our results are consistent with this conjecture, while vagotomy effectively reduced the levels of Ach and ChAT, the failure to restore the critical anti-inflammatory receptor α7nAChR, coupled with the non-significant downregulation of M3R (Fig. 4). Based on these studies, we speculate that the release of intestinal inflammatory factors in AOM/DSS-induced CAC mice may be a key factor contributing to the development of depression and anxiety, with the vagus nerve playing a mediating role in this process.

Currently, the precise mechanisms by which gut-derived signals modulate specific brain regions via neural pathways remain incompletely elucidated. Primarily, the vagus nerve transmits peripheral inflammatory signals to the NTS, further propagating to brain regions including the amygdala, hippocampus, and hypothalamus [12]. Studies have demonstrated that stress-induced vagal activity could influence anxiety-relevant prefrontal and amygdala neuronal oscillations [14]. Prior studies also confirm that aberrant amygdala neuronal activity can be normalized by vagotomy [15]. Hyperactivation of the amygdala and LHb/MHb is implicated in anxiety and depression under chronic stress [40–44]. In our study, neuronal hyperactivity in mood-related regions (LHb/MHb, amygdala) was observed in CAC mice, which was significantly attenuated by vagotomy (Fig. 2). Furthermore, we performed retrograde tracing to further confirm that intestinal inflammation induces depression- and anxiety-like behaviors via the vagus nerve–NTS–LHb/MHb neural circuit (Fig. 5). On the other hand, central nervous system (CNS) neuroinflammation plays a pivotal role in driving anxiety and depression-like behaviors. Sustained overproduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, TNF-α) could disrupt neuronal functions such as synaptic plasticity and neurogenesis [12]. Vagus nerve mediated activation of brain regions (LHb/MHb, amygdala, hippocampus) further amplifies cerebral cytokine release, creating a vicious cycle of neuroinflammation [13]. This is exemplified by studies showing that chronic stress-induced gut microbiota alterations trigger neuroinflammation and hippocampal neurogenesis deficits via vagus nerve, ultimately leading to depressive behaviors [45]. Moreover, LHb neurons and astrocytes have been implicated in depression. Research demonstrated an increase of c-fos in the basal part of the lateral subdivision of lateral habenula (LHbLB), and an increase in GFAP-IR in the inferior portion of the medial complex (MHbl) in anxiety model [46]. Experimental studies demonstrate that under stress conditions, norepinephrine released from the LHb drives astrocyte activation, subsequently inducing depressive-like behaviors [27]. Consistent with prior reports, our results demonstrated CAC mice exhibited increased neuronal and astrocyte activation in mood-related brain regions, along with elevated neuroinflammation and reduced neurotrophic support (Fig. 3). Vagotomy largely reversed these changes, suggesting that vagal afferent signaling transmits gut-derived inflammation to the brain, contributing to depression- and anxiety-like behaviors.

Although vagotomy ameliorates CAC-induced depression and anxiety, inhibiting neuroinflammation, we observed that it fails to address the root causes of these depression and anxiety—persistent intestinal inflammation and tumor progression. Currently, the role of the vagus nerve in gastrointestinal tumorigenesis remains controversial. Numerous clinical studies have observed that vagotomised ulcer patients exhibit an increased risk of developing CRC, gastric cancer, etc [47]. For instant, in gastrointestinal carcinogenesis model, vagotomy significantly accelerated tumor development in hamsters [48]. This observation stands in stark contrast to recent research in the ApcMin/+ genetic mouse model, where vagotomy or extrinsic intestinal denervation were reported to reduce tumor size and proliferation [49]. The opposing outcomes may stem from fundamental differences between cancer models—our inflammation-driven CAC model versus a purely genetic CRC model. In the AOM/DSS model, the local inflammation may be the dominant driver of tumorigenesis. The vagus nerve efferent nerve plays a key role in regulating local inflammation in the body’s periphery; therefore, vagotomy may impair the resolution of inflammation and consequently exert adverse effects on inflammation-driven cancer development. Our finding thus contributes a crucial piece of evidence to this ongoing debate, suggesting that the therapeutic value of targeting the vagus nerve in CRC.

This prompted us to explore an alternative intervention capable of suppressing vagal overactivation while simultaneously mitigating intestinal pathology. The vagus nerve comprises heterogeneous neuronal populations, including GABAergic, cholinergic, serotonergic neurons, etc [50, 51]. Specifically, studies have demonstrated that neurotransmitters such as ACh, released by neurons, cancer cells, immune cells, and potentially gut microbiota, can stimulate tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis [52]. Therefore, we selected atropine—a muscarinic acetylcholine receptor (mAChR) antagonist—as a pharmacological alternative to vagotomy in CAC mice. According to previous studies, atropine has been widely used as an inhibitor of vagal activity, generally functioning by blocking the cholinergic pathways of the vagus nerve [53, 54]. Studies shown that brain cholinergic signaling plays a major role in regulating cognition [55]. It has been reported that cholinergic dysfunction in the brains of patients with cirrhosis and hepatic encephalopathy is correlated with the activity of the subdiaphragmatic vagus nerve [56]. Indeed, atropine treatment alleviated depression- and anxiety-like behaviors (Fig. 6). Similar to the ameliorative effects observed with vagotomy, atropine significantly suppressed neuronal activation in depression- and anxiety-related brain regions and attenuating neuroinflammation (Fig. 9 and Supplement Fig. 2). Based on these findings, we propose that inhibiting cholinergic neurotransmission in the vagus nerve may mitigate neuroinflammation induced by vagal hyperactivation, thereby improving depressive- and anxiety-like behaviors in CAC mice.

Meanwhile, atropine not only ameliorated CAC-induced depression- and anxiety-like behaviors but also delayed the onset and progression of colorectal tumorigenesis. Based on previous evidence suggesting a role for acetylcholine in promoting colorectal tumorigenesis, we assessed its level and found that atropine suppressed cholinergic signaling in CAC mice, reducing ACh levels and M3R/ChAT expression. (Fig. 7). However, accumulating evidence suggests that acetylcholine can also be produced by non-neuronal sources within the intestinal tumor microenvironment, including tumor cells, epithelial cells, and immune cells [28, 57]. For instance, studies have shown that acetylcholine can be produced in gastric cancer cell lines such as MKN45 and BGC823. This autocrine ACh promotes gastric cancer cell proliferation through the M3R and EGFR signaling pathways [52]. Therefore, a key limitation of our study is that we cannot definitively assign the observed acetylcholine to neuronal versus non-neuronal sources. This limitation should be acknowledged. The muscarinic receptor antagonist-atropine and the selective M3R antagonist 4-DAMP inhibit the proliferation and migration of human CRC cells, induce apoptosis, and reduce the expression of PD-L1, PD-L2, and M3R in CRC cells [28]. Pharmacological inhibition or genetic knockout of M3R suppresses gastric tumor development, while treatment with the non-selective muscarinic receptor agonist bethanechol increases adenoma formation and upregulates the oncogene Myc in AOM/DSS-induced mice [39]. These findings collectively highlight the therapeutic potential of atropine as a mAChR antagonist in delaying tumor progression. Consistent with these reports, atropine downregulated tumor-promoting markers (Myc, PD-L1 and PD-L2) (Fig. 7), and alleviated intestinal inflammation and tissue damage, including reductions in pro-inflammatory cytokines and iNOS/COX-2 expression (Fig. 8 and Supplement Fig. 2). While atropine delayed inflammation-driven intestinal tumorigenesis and reduced both M3R expression and intestinal inflammation, the lack of experiments using selective M3R antagonists (such as 4-DAMP) or genetic knockout of M3R limits our ability to establish a direct causal role for M3R in mediating the anti-tumor effects of atropine, which represents an additional limitation of the present study.

Our study still has certain limitations. Although we observed improvements in depression and anxiety-related brain regions and depression/anxiety symptoms after vagotomy in our research, the mechanism of action between brain region activation and depression/anxiety remains to be further explored. Secondly, the specific receptor on the vagus nerve that is activated by intestinal inflammation to modulate the activation of the NTS and subsequently influence the pathogenesis of depression and anxiety remains elucidated. Future investigations may utilize chemical genetic and neural tracing methods to investigate the detailed neural circuits involved in vagus nerve activation-induced depression and anxiety under inflammatory conditions, and determine the types of neurons that play a key role. Knocking out or inhibiting specific receptors on the vagus nerve can reversely verify which substances activate the vagus nerve under intestinal inflammatory conditions. In addition, we acknowledge that ChAT–M3R co-localization does not allow definitive identification of the cellular sources or establish causality at the single-cell level. Thus, these data indicate potential cholinergic involvement in M3R signaling rather than cell-type–specific activation, representing a limitation of this study that warrants future cell-specific or genetic investigations.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings demonstrate that vagotomy alleviates depression- and anxiety-like behaviors induced by CAC via blocking peripheral inflammatory signals from reaching the vagal CNS. This intervention suppresses neuronal activation in the amygdala and LHb/MHb brain regions associated with depression and anxiety, thereby inhibiting neuroinflammatory responses, ultimately rescuing CAC-related depression and anxiety. Importantly, atropine-mediated inhibition of cholinergic vagal signaling simultaneously ameliorates both colorectal tumor progression and affective disorders. This study underscores the significant potential of the vagus nerve in modulating gut-brain axis and delaying the co-progression of gastrointestinal diseases and neuropsychiatric symptoms. Significantly, we propose a novel and effective strategy targeting the gut-brain axis for preventing gastrointestinal tumors and associated behavioral abnormalities.
