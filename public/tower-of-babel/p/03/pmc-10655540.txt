Associations Between Fatigue and Endocrine and Non-endocrine Health Problems in Turner Syndrome: Cohort Study and Review
Authors: Anna G W Rosenberg, Veerle D A Dingemans, Anja G Bos-Roubos, Sanne Luijks, Arianne B Dessens, Ramon Dykgraaf, Jolien W Roos-Hesselink, Elisabeth F C Van Rossum, Aart J Van Der Lely, Laura C G De Graaff
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextTurner syndrome (TS) is a rare chromosomal disorder characterized by gonadal dysfunction, short stature, and heart defects, among other features. Women with TS often suffer from severe fatigue, for which they are typically referred to endocrinologists. The diagnostic work-up is generally time-consuming and invasive, and it rarely solves the problem. To prevent the personal and financial burden of unnecessary diagnostic procedures, it is crucial to understand fatigue in TS.ObjectiveTo explore the association between fatigue and endocrine and non-endocrine comorbidities in a—for rare disorders—large group of women with TS.Methods170 genetically confirmed women with TS who attended the TS Reference Center underwent a systematic health screening, including a structured interview, complete physical examination, biochemical measurements, perceived stress and fatigue questionnaires, and additional tests when indicated.ResultsMedian (interquartile range) age was 32.6 (23.9-41.4) years. Severe fatigue was experienced by 1 in 3 women with TS. Liver enzyme disturbances and body mass index were significantly associated with higher fatigue scores. Perceived stress was highly correlated with fatigue.ConclusionThere was no association between fatigue and most endocrine and non-endocrine disorders, which implies that fatigue is only partly explained by somatic disorders. The high correlation between perceived stress and fatigue suggests that TS-related neuropsychological processes may play an important role in the etiology of fatigue in women with TS. We provide a practical algorithm for the endocrine, non-endocrine, and psychological approach to fatigue in women with TS.

Turner syndrome (TS) is a rare genetic disorder caused by complete or partial loss of one of the X chromosomes (1, 2). Yearly, 1 in 2000 to 1 in 2500 live baby girls are born with TS (3), making it one of the most prevalent chromosomal abnormalities in females. The most common karyotypes are 45,X (40%-50%), mosaic 45,X/46,XX (15%-25%) or isochromosome (20%) (4, 5). Less common causes of TS include a mosaic karyotype with triple X cell line, mosaic karyotype with a Y-chromosome fragment, marker or ring X chromosomes, and unbalanced translocations. Women with TS can suffer from a wide variety of medical and developmental problems. The most prevalent characteristics of TS are premature ovarian insufficiency, cardiovascular problems, and short stature, but organ-specific problems (kidney, intestine) and learning difficulties are also common (2). The clinical phenotype is highly variable and differs among the different karyotypes. In general, a less severe phenotype is seen in women with a mosaic karyotype (5).

A study by Van den Hoven et al (6), in our center, showed that 32% of women with TS experience severe fatigue, which was higher than in the general Dutch population (7). Besides, they found that fatigue was associated with lower quality of life. The causes of increased fatigue in women with TS, however, are yet unknown, making symptom management difficult and often unsuccessful.

Fatigue, in general, can have several endocrine and non-endocrine causes, including thyroid disease, electrolyte abnormalities, cardiovascular disorders, renal disease, liver disease, digestive tract problems (eg, celiac disease), electrolyte abnormalities, anemia, and psychological stress, among others (8). Additionally, diabetes mellitus (DM) and obesity have been suggested to be associated with fatigue (9-11). Further, fatigue could be a side effect of medication (8).

As women with TS often have comorbidities such as obesity, type 2 diabetes, and autoimmune diseases (eg, Hashimoto hypothyroidism and celiac disease) (2), they are generally referred to internists/endocrinologists to find the cause of their fatigue. However, in our experience, fatigue is also common in women without these fatigue-causing comorbidities. This suggests that fatigue in women with TS might not have a somatic cause and that the current approach potentially leads to unnecessary diagnostic procedures and/or ineffective therapies. To prevent the burden of unnecessary somatic diagnostic procedures and (over)treatment, it is important to know whether there are somatic causes for the “unexplained” fatigue in women with TS.

In this study, we explore the association between fatigue and various endocrine and non-endocrine disorders in women with TS. Based on previous literature and our findings, we provide a practical algorithm for the approach to fatigue in these women. New insights presented in this study may improve quality of life and health care for women with TS.

Methods

This study was approved by the local Medical Ethics Committee of the Erasmus University Medical Center, Rotterdam, the Netherlands. Written informed consent was waived by the Medical Ethics Committee. All patients with genetically confirmed TS who visited the multidisciplinary TS care unit between December 2015 and October 2018 at the Erasmus University Medical Center, Rotterdam, the Netherlands, were included in the study. Some of the patients were also included in studies by Van den Hoven et al (6) and Savas et al (12). These studies concerned health-related quality of life and cortisol exposure in relation to comorbidities in TS.

Data Collection

Data were retrieved from the electronic health records of the patients. We retrospectively collected data on health problems, medication use, biochemical measurements, vital parameters, and standardized questionnaires.

Health problems

We collected data on the following endocrine and non-endocrine health problems: thyroid disease, DM, obesity, vitamin D deficiency, cardiac disorders, hypertension, renal disease, liver disease, celiac disease, electrolyte disorders, and anemia.

Thyroid disease was classified as overt hypothyroidism (low free thyroxine [fT4] with high thyroid-stimulating hormone [TSH]) subclinical hypothyroidism (normal fT4 with increased TSH), overt hyperthyroidism (high fT4 with decreased TSH) or subclinical hyperthyroidism (normal fT4 with decreased TSH). Women who used thyroid medication were also classified as having thyroid disease (category depending on the diagnosis). DM was considered present when the patient had a diagnosis of DM, used medication for DM, or when nonfasting glucose levels were repeatedly above the upper limit of normal. Obesity was defined as a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m2, overweight as BMI between 25.0 and 29.9 kg/m2 and underweight as BMI < 18.5 kg/m2. Vitamin D deficiency was diagnosed when 25-OH vitamin D levels were repeatedly below the reference range. Women who used vitamin D supplementation were separately analyzed. Cardiac disorders were considered present when the patient had at least one cardiac abnormality. Hypertension was not considered a cardiac disorder but was separately analyzed. High blood pressure was defined as a systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg and/or a diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg. Patients were considered as having renal disease when they had a clinical diagnosis of renal disease and/or when the estimated glomerular filtration rate was repeatedly below the reference range. For liver disease, 5 categories were distinguished based on biochemical measurements: (i) liver enzymes suggestive of steatosis (high gamma-glutamyl transferase [GGT] and high alkaline phosphatase [ALP]); (ii) liver enzymes suggestive of obstruction (high total bilirubin, often accompanied with high GGT and ALP); (iii) isolated ALP elevation; (iv) isolated GGT elevation; and (v) other liver enzyme disturbances. For electrolyte disorders, we screened for calcium, sodium, and potassium levels outside the reference range. Anemia was considered present when hemoglobin (Hb) levels were below the reference range. Additionally, mean corpuscular volume (MCV) levels were used to distinguish microcytic anemia (MCV <80 fL), normocytic anemia (MCV 80-100 fL), and macrocytic anemia (MCV >100 fL). Patients were classified as having celiac disease when a diagnosis was present in the electronic health record.

Standardized questionnaires

Patients were asked to fill out 2 standardized questionnaires before their visit to the outpatient clinic as part of regular patient care: the Checklist Individual Strength–20 (CIS-20) and the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10). Both questionnaires were in Dutch. The CIS-20 is used to assess subjective fatigue (7, 13). It consists of 20 statements that are rated on a 7-point Likert scale. There are 4 subscales: subjective feeling of fatigue, concentration, motivation, and activity. Total scores vary between 20 and 140 points. A total score ≥ 76 indicates problematic fatigue (14). For the subscale “subjective feeling of fatigue,” a score ≥ 35 indicates severe fatigue (15). The CIS-20 has been validated for multiple medical conditions (16). The PSS-10 measures perceived stress on a 5-point Likert scale (17). The total score ranges between 0 and 40 points. A higher score indicates more perceived stress.

Biochemical measurements

During or before the visits, venous blood samples were taken for general medical screening. This included evaluation of thyroid function (fT4, TSH), glucose metabolism (nonfasting glucose), liver function (alanine aminotransferase [ALAT], aspartate aminotransferase [ASAT], ALP, GGT, lactate dehydrogenase [LDH], total bilirubin), kidney function (estimated glomerular filtration rate, albumin), hematopoietic system (hemoglobin, MCV), electrolytes (calcium corrected for albumin [Ca] (18), sodium, potassium), low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, and 25-OH-vitamin D status. The reference values are given in the supplementary data, Supplementary Table S1 (19). Only results of blood samples that were taken 1 year before or after filing out the CIS-20 were collected.

Literature Search

Literature was searched in the databases Embase, Medline All, Web of Science Core Collection, PsycInfo, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials. The search was completed in November 2021. Only articles about fatigue in women with TS were included. Exclusion criteria were articles with fewer than 5 women, non-English articles, and articles without full text. Articles were first screened by abstract and title and second by full text. All articles were screened by 2 independent researchers (A.R., V.D.). A third researcher (L.d.G.) was consulted in case of disagreement. The full search strategy and flowchart of screened articles are included in the supplementary data, Supplementary Table S2 and Supplementary Fig. S1 (19).

Data Analysis

Data were analyzed with R version 3.6.0. Women who had not filled out the CIS-20 were excluded from all analyses. Continuous data are presented as mean ± SD when data were normally distributed and as median and interquartile range (IQR) when data were non-normally distributed. Categorical data are presented as count (N) and percentage (%). Wilcoxon rank sum tests (non-normally distributed continuous data), t tests (normally distributed continuous data) or chi-square tests (categorical data) were used to detect differences in characteristics between women with and without perceived fatigue (measured with the CIS-20). For the analysis of correlations, Spearman rho or Kendall tau (in case of ties) was calculated. Multivariate linear regression analyses were performed to assess the association between the total CIS-20 score (resembling fatigue; dependent variable) and several endocrine and non-endocrine disorders (independent variables). Selection of independent variables was based on clinical relevance and evaluated by a team of internist-endocrinologists, gynecologists, cardiologists, and psychologists. Since at least 10 observations are needed per independent variable to reliably estimate the coefficient (20), we calculated that we could include a maximum of 17 (dummy) variables. For the multivariate regression analysis, the different karyotypes were categorized into 3 categories as (i) 45,X; (ii) mosaic; and (iii) other.

Results

In total, 170 women were included in the study. Characteristics of all women are described in Table 1. Median age was 32.6 years (IQR, 23.9-41.4 years) and median BMI was 25.4 kg/m2 (IQR, 22.6-28.5 kg/m2). Most women had a complete 45,X karyotype (N = 93; 55%), 24 had mosaicism (14%). Other karyotypes were (mosaic) isochromosome (N = 25; 15%), polyploidy (N = 8; 5%; for example 45,X/47,XXX or 45,X/46,XX/47,XXX), deletion (N = 7; 4%; for example 46,X, del(X)(p11.2) or 46,X, del(X)(q21.1)), ring X chromosome (N = 5; 3%) and Y-chromosome fragment (N = 8; 5%). A total of 140 (82%) women were on hormone replacement therapy (HRT), mostly due to premature ovarian insufficiency, and 128 (75%) women had received growth hormone (GH) treatment during childhood.

The association between the CIS-20 score and endocrine and non-endocrine disorders is shown in Table 3. BMI was significantly associated with the CIS-20 score in the multivariate analysis. Also, the scatterplot showed a low, but significant, positive association between BMI and CIS-20 scores (tau = 0.11; P = .03; Fig. 1). Liver enzyme disturbances were more prevalent among women with problematic fatigue compared to women without problematic fatigue. However, the multivariate analysis showed no significant association between liver enzyme disturbances and the CIS-20 score. Liver enzymes suggestive of obstruction (high total bilirubin), however, were significantly associated with the CIS-20 score. BMI was significantly higher among women with liver enzyme disturbances (median 26.8 [IQR, 23.5-30.7] kg/m2) compared to women without liver enzyme disturbances (23.9 [21.6-26.6] kg/m2; P < .001). Women with problematic fatigue had less often received childhood GH treatment than women without problematic fatigue (64% vs 81%; P = .02; Table 1). However, when correcting for karyotype and age, the association between childhood GH treatment and total CIS-20 score was not significant (Table 3). There was a high positive correlation between the PSS-10 score and the CIS-20 score (ρ = 0.71; P < .001; Fig. 2), which was confirmed by the multivariate regression analysis.

Discussion

One-third of women with TS experience severe fatigue, which has been associated with a lower quality of life (6) and can possibly lead to high—and partly preventable—health care consumption. To reduce the burden of unnecessary diagnostic procedures and treatments, it is important to understand the causes of this disabling fatigue. Therefore, we studied endocrine and non-endocrine causes of fatigue in 170 women with TS.

Most endocrine and non-endocrine disorders were not associated with the fatigue score. Therefore, we conclude that the high prevalence of fatigue is only partly explained by somatic disorders. Given the high correlation between perceived stress and fatigue, we hypothesize that TS-related neuropsychological processes may play an important role in the etiology of fatigue in women with TS.

When considering liver enzyme disturbances, we found a significant association between fatigue and high total bilirubin. However, only 6 women had high total bilirubin levels, which reduces the clinical relevance of this finding. The prevalence of liver enzyme disturbances in general differed significantly between people with (41/54; 76%) and without severe fatigue (61/112; 54%; P = .04). However, after adjusting for BMI, childhood GH treatment, and HRT, this association disappeared. Further analyses revealed that BMI was significantly higher among women with liver enzyme disturbances (median 26.8 [IQR, 23.5-30.7] kg/m2 vs 23.9 [21.6-26.6] kg/m2; P < .001) and that BMI itself was positively associated with fatigue (Table 1). Therefore, we think that the “abnormal liver enzyme levels” alone are not associated with fatigue, but that this association might be confounded by BMI. Previous research also showed a positive association between elevated liver enzymes and BMI (22, 23). Since BMI is generally increased in women with TS (24), this could play a role in the high prevalence of liver enzyme disturbances in this population. However, other studies among women with TS did not find an association between liver enzyme levels and BMI (25, 26). Other factors that could possibly play a role include, for example, HRT and lifestyle. We found a significant, but modest, association between BMI and fatigue: one unit increase in BMI increased the CIS-20 score by 0.6 points. The correlation between BMI and fatigue was also low (tau = 0.11). The causality of this relation is unclear. On one hand, higher BMI might lead to higher fatigue scores. On the other hand, increased fatigue might lead to lower physical activity, which in turn leads to higher BMI. More research is needed to elucidate the relation between BMI, liver enzyme disturbances, and fatigue in women with TS.

Problematic fatigue was more common among women who had not received GH treatment during childhood. However, when correcting for age and karyotype in the multivariate regression analysis, there was no significant association between childhood GH treatment and fatigue.

Fatigue can also be a side effect of medication, like benzodiazepines, antidepressants, antihistamines, selective beta blockers, and proton pump inhibitors (27). Therefore, thorough review of medication should be part of the medical interview when assessing a patient with fatigue (Fig. 3).

The prevalence of overt hypothyroidism (22%) was similar to the previously reported prevalence of hypothyroidism in people with TS (2, 28). Additionally, we found that 15% of the women had subclinical hypothyroidism, which may progress to overt hypothyroidism later in life, albeit rarely (29). In an additional analysis, we found that the fatigue score was similar for TS women with and without subclinical hypothyroidism (median 61.0 [IQR, 43-86] vs 63.0 [49-81]). Based on the existing literature and our own results, we recommend annual screening for thyroid disorders. Once hypothyroidism is actually found, the regular guidelines for treatment of subclinical hypothyroidism should be followed (as in women without TS). The prevalence of other TS-associated health problems in our cohort in comparison with previous literature is given in the supplementary data, Supplementary Table S3 (19).

Previous research showed that women with a mosaic karyotype generally have a less severe phenotype (5). Therefore, we performed an additional analysis to identify differences in fatigue between women with and without a mosaic karyotype. However, the fatigue score did not differ between the groups. Also in the multivariate regression analysis, karyotype was not associated with fatigue scores.

We found a high correlation between perceived stress and fatigue; women with severe fatigue had higher stress scores than women without severe fatigue. This suggests that stress may play an important role in the etiology of fatigue in women with TS. Interestingly, we previously found that long-term stress hormone levels (cortisol concentrations in scalp hair) were higher in women with TS than in the control population (12). It is likely that these increased cortisol levels are, at least partly, due to the higher stress levels that women with TS experience (6, 12). This high perceived stress, in turn, might be explained by a TS-specific neuropsychological profile (relatively strong verbal capacities and weaknesses in visual-spatial, executive, and social-emotional functions) that is present in many women with TS (30). As the neuropsychological profile may contribute to the stress levels and fatigue in women with TS, routine clinical care for women with TS should include psychological counseling. Patient-tailored clinical neuropsychological assessment, including screening for emotional and behavioral problems and deficiencies in executive functioning, is especially important when patients suffer from somatically unexplained fatigue. Neuropsychological assessment provides insight into individual strengths and weaknesses. Together with daily life adaptations and additional psychosocial support, this may help reduce fatigue.

This is the first study to focus on endocrine and non-endocrine health problems in relation to fatigue in women with TS. Strengths of our study are the relatively large sample size (considering that TS is a rare syndrome), the inclusion of only adults with genetically confirmed TS of different karyotypes, and the fact that we have assessed both somatic and nonsomatic health problems. Our biggest strength, however, is that all women underwent a systematic health screening by the same multidisciplinary team of healthcare professionals. Because of this, there were no differences in diagnostics and treatment of women with and without fatigue. Besides, fatigue was measured with a validated questionnaire, which improved the reliability of our outcome measurement. However, some limitations remain. Most importantly, we had little data on hearing impairment and electrolyte levels like sodium levels and potassium. Therefore, we could only analyze these variables in subgroups of women who had no missing data. These additional analyses suggest that there is no difference in the prevalence of hearing impairment, hypo-/hypernatremia, or hypo-/hyperkalemia between women with and without severe fatigue, nor were they associated with the total fatigue score. Furthermore, we had no data on vitamin B12, as this was only measured when anemia or complaints related to vitamin B12 deficiency were present. Additionally, there was a high correlation between the PSS-10 and CIS-20 questionnaire in our study. As far as we know, the cross-correlation between these questionnaires has not been investigated in the general population. Given the cross-sectional study design of this study, we could not statistically assess causality, but only identify associations. Furthermore, given the exploratory character of our study, no correction for multiple testing was performed.

Conclusion

Overall, 1 in 3 women with TS suffers from severe fatigue. Apart from elevated liver enzymes and BMI, none of the somatic health disorders was significantly associated with fatigue. This suggests that the increased prevalence of fatigue cannot be solely explained by these somatic disorders. Given the high correlation between perceived stress and fatigue and given the previous finding of high hair cortisol levels in women with TS, we hypothesize that stress and neuropsychological processes (like deficiencies in executive functioning) play an important role in the etiology of fatigue in women with TS. To prevent the personal and financial burden of overdiagnostics and overtreatment, we recommend against invasive and extensive diagnostic testing to find a somatic cause for fatigue in women with TS. Instead, we recommend to follow the practical algorithm that we provide for the approach to fatigue in women with TS, which should be followed in addition to the regular Turner follow-up described in international guidelines.
