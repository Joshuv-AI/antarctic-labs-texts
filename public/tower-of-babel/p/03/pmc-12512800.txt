Successful use of ovarian vein as a ureteral substitute for long-segment ureteral stricture post-pelvic radiotherapy: a case report
Authors: Wenqiang Cai, Yan Wang, Xinyan Yan, Pingping Zhou, Xiaqin Huo, Weilin Pu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Long-segment ureteral stricture secondary to radiotherapy for female pelvic malignancies represents a severe complication, significantly impairing patients’ renal function and quality of life. Despite the availability of multiple treatment modalities, this condition remains challenging to manage, with high recurrence rates and perioperative complication rates.Case presentationWe report a 57-year-old female patient who underwent surgical treatment for cervical malignancy. After postoperative pelvic radiotherapy, the patient developed long-term left ureteral stricture. Both transurethral ureteral stenting and ureterocystostomy failed to achieve satisfactory results. Finally, the left ureter was successfully reconstructed using the ovarian vein. The patient’s postoperative course was uneventful, and no ureteral fistula or restenosis occurred during the 3-year follow-up.ConclusionsAlthough current evidence suggests that ovarian vein substitution for the ureter in treating radiation-induced long-segment ureteral stricture yields favorable prognosis at 3 years postoperatively, its long-term efficacy still requires further in-depth research.Keywords: Long-segment ureteral stricture, Ovarian vein, Pelvic radiotherapy, Ureteral reconstruction

Background

Complex ureteral strictures are predominantly caused by iatrogenic factors. With the widespread application of radiotherapy in patients with pelvic tumors, the incidence of post-radiation ureteral stricture has shown an annual increasing trend [1]. In cervical cancer patients, the 3-year and 5-year cumulative risks of ureteral stricture after brachytherapy are 1.9% and 2.7%, respectively [2]. Fokdal conducted a study on 1,860 cervical cancer patients who received radiotherapy and reported a 2.1% incidence of ureteral stricture [3]. Chorbińska noted that the early manifestation of radiation-induced ureteral stricture involves mucosal damage, which progresses to obstructive atherosclerotic endarteritis in the later stages [4]. The chronic inflammatory response triggers collagen deposition and fibrosis of the ureteral wall, while microvascular endothelial cell damage leads to local ischemia. These processes ultimately result in ureteral stricture or ischemic necrosis, accompanied by hydronephrosis and renal function impairment [5], [6]​.

Ureteral reconstructive surgery carries risks such as reduced bladder capacity, intestinal fibrosis, poor anastomotic healing, and intestinal fistula, with a relatively low cure rate [7]. Autopsy studies have confirmed that the diameters of the ovarian vein and ureter are well-matched (3–4 mm). Histological analyses have demonstrated satisfactory anastomotic healing without rejection reactions, indicating that ovarian vein repair holds unique value for managing radiotherapy-related long-segment ureteral strictures that are refractory to traditional treatments [8]. As an autologous graft, the ovarian vein offers advantages including anatomical proximity and convenient harvesting, thus avoiding the traumatic complications associated with traditional surgical approaches. The indications are postmenopausal patients or those who have undergone oophorectomy. The contraindications are ovarian vein developmental abnormalities (such as excessively small diameter < 3 mm, segmental occlusion), severe atrophy of renal parenchyma, thrombosis, and tumor invasion.

In this report, we provide a detailed description of the preoperative and intraoperative details regarding the management of a 57-year-old female patient with radiotherapy-related long-segment ureteral stricture secondary to cervical cancer.

Case presentation

A 57-year-old female patient underwent open radical surgery for stage IIA1 cervical cancer four years ago, with bilateral oophorectomy performed during the operation. Six months postoperatively, she received pelvic radiotherapy. Three months after completing radiotherapy, she was diagnosed with left ureteral stricture, however, attempts to place a ureteral stent via the urethra were unsuccessful at that time. Subsequent laparoscopic ureterovesical reimplantation also failed. The patient presented with recurrent fever, chills, and left-sided lumbar pain, prompting the initiation of percutaneous left nephrostomy as a definitive treatment modality.​.

Operative details

The patient’s CTU and anterograde ureterography revealed an 80 mm length of ureteral stricture(Fig. 1a, b). Laboratory and functional assessments showed the following: SCR was 172 µmol/L; VAS score was 4 points; KPS score was 60 points; and eGFR was 29.98 ml/min. Urodynamic evaluation demonstrated a bladder capacity of 250 ml, maximum urinary flow rate of 25 ml/s, and detrusor pressure of 40 cmH₂O. The bladder methylene blue test yielded negative results.

a The CTU showed that the upper segment of the left ureter was dilated, while the pelvic segment was stricture b The Anterograde ureterography showed dilation of the abdominal segment of the left ureter

The comprehensive preoperative assessment revealed no surgical contraindications. Following detailed consultation regarding the diagnosis, proposed surgicalintervention, and potential risks, the patient and her family provided informed consent through signed surgical and clinical research consent forms.

Surgical technique

The patient was administered combined intravenous-inhalational anesthesia and underwent tracheal intubation. Vital signs were continuously monitored and remained stable throughout the procedure. The patient was positioned in a supine position, and a urinary catheter was inserted preoperatively. Following skin disinfection and draping, a 10-cm longitudinal incision was made between the umbilicus and pubic symphysis. Upon entering the abdominal cavity, severe adhesions were observed in both the pelvic and abdominal cavities, which were carefully dissected to expose the surgical field. The left ureter was identified, with a severe stricture noted in its distal segment, this stenotic portion was meticulously dissected and excised. The distance between the ureteral stump and the bladder was measured at 80 mm.​.

The distal end of the left ovarian vein was identified in the pelvic funnel region of the left ovary. Using fine scissors and small forceps, the vein was carefully dissected from its distal segment (Fig. 2a), preserving the venous adventitia along with a small amount of surrounding loose connective tissue. The dissected vein measured 80 mm in length (Fig. 2b). It was then transected and gently transferred to a pre-cooled preservation solution (heparin-containing cold electrolyte-balanced solution), with the temperature maintained at 4℃ throughout to minimize endothelial cell damage. A blunt needle was inserted into the venous lumen, which was flushed with cold heparinized saline to confirm patency and structural integrity.​.

a The left ovarian vein was carefully dissected and measured 80 mm in length b The adventitia of the vein and a small amount of surrounding loose connective tissue were preserved c The distal segment of the ovarian vein was anastomosed with the distal end of the ureter using interrupted suture

Subsequently, the ovarian vein was prepared for inverted transplantation, with its centrifugal end oriented toward the renal pelvis and centripetal end toward the bladder. Anastomosis between the distal segment of the ovarian vein and the ureteral stump was performed using 4 − 0 Polyglactin 910 sutures, with a total of 8 stitches placed (Fig. 2c). Under guidance of a zebra guide wire, a 5-Fr double-J ureteral stent was inserted into the lumen of the ovarian vein and ureter (Fig. 3a).​.

a A 5-French double-J ureteral stent was placed in the lumen of the ovarian vein and ureter under the guidance of a zebra guide wire b The bladder and ovary veins were anastomosed using the Politano-Leadbetter procedure c The ovarian vein and anastomosis were reinforced with surrounding fat and peritoneal tissues

The bladder was filled with methylene blue solution, and a 1-cm incision was made on its posterior wall. The Politano-Leadbetter technique was employed to anastomose the ovarian vein to the bladder, one stitch was placed between the ovarian vein opening and the overlying bladder muscle layer to form the tunnel apex, followed by suturing of the ovarian vein to the bladder mucosa with 6 stitches. Finally, the bladder muscle layer was closed with interrupted sutures, embedding the ovarian vein to form a submucosal tunnel.​.

To test for anastomotic integrity, 200 ml of alizarin blue-containing normal saline was injected via the urinary catheter, with no leakage observed at the anastomotic sites (Fig. 3b).The ovarian vein and anastomosis were reinforced with surrounding fat and peritoneal tissues, providing support to prevent anastomotic dehiscence or ischemic necrosis (Fig. 3c).

The patient initiated oral nutrition on the first postoperative day. Analgesic medications were administered for 2 days, and antibiotic therapy was continued for 3 days. Early and gradual ambulation was encouraged. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 10. The urinary catheter was replaced every two weeks and ultimately removed one month after surgery.​.

Three months postoperatively, renal color Doppler ultrasound showed no hydronephrosis. Laboratory and functional assessments revealed SCR level of 117 µmol/L, VAS score of 1 point, and KPS score of 90 points.​.

Ureteroscopy was performed in the first and second postoperative years, respectively, with findings classified as Clavien-Dindo grade 1. To prevent subsequent stricture, the ureteral stents were replaced during these procedures. The corresponding SCR levels were 115 µmol/L and 130 µmol/L. Renal color Doppler ultrasound showed no hydronephrosis. Retrograde cystography demonstrated patency of the left ureter without contrast extravasation (Fig. 4a, b).

a Bladder retrograde contrast examination one year after the operation b Bladder retrograde contrast examination 2 years after the operation

The follow-up plan entails a review every two years, with examination items including serum creatinine measurement, urinalysis, CTU or MRU, and ureteroscopic evaluation for ureteral stent removal or replacement.

Discussion

Pelvic radiotherapy frequently results in extensive ureteral stricture and reduced bladder capacity. The risk of ureteral stricture is highest within the first 5 years post-radiotherapy, with a persistent annual incidence of 0.15% thereafter. Risk factors associated with increased ureteral stricture include advanced FIGO stage, tumor size > 5 cm, and baseline hydronephrosis. Additionally, an EQD2 dose of ≥ 77 Gy is significantly correlated with ≥ grade 3 ureteral morbidity [2, 9].​

Carilli reported on 25 patients with failed endoscopic management who underwent ureteral end-to-end anastomosis or reimplantation; approximately 15% experienced recurrence within 6 months of follow-up. A univariate logistic regression model identified ureteral stricture length > 2 cm and concurrent chemotherapy as predictors of recurrence [10]. While long-term nephrostomy can alleviate urinary tract obstruction and preserve renal function, it elevates the risk of urinary tract infections and impairs quality of life.​.

Radiotherapy-induced pelvic tissue adhesions, fibrosis, and increased fragility of blood vessels and intestinal walls complicate reconstructive procedures such as ileal/appendiceal ureteral substitution and autologous kidney transplantation, often leading to poor anastomotic healing and high rates of intestinal fistula [11]. Li conducted a multi-institutional study on tongue mucosa grafts for long-segment ureteral stricture repair, reporting a 97.6% overall success rate with a median follow-up of 35 months, this procedure is generally suitable for strictures less than 5 cm [12]. Appendiceal patch ureteroplasty, primarily indicated for right-sided ureteral stricture, has shown 100% subjective and objective success rates over an average 18-month follow-up for stenotic segments averaging 4.3 cm in length 2025[13]. However, its application is limited by appendix availability and length (typically 6–8 cm), as radiation-induced adhesions complicate harvest, and there is a 10–15% risk of appendiceal necrosis in radiated pelvic tissues [14, 15]​.

Ileal ureterostomy, while achieving a 3-year patency rate of 75–85% in non-irradiated patients with benign stricture, has a success rate dropping to 60–65% in radiation-induced cases due to increased risks of enterovesical fistulas and anastomotic dehiscence. Radiation-induced fibrosis of the ileal mesentery impairs microvascular perfusion, elevating the risks of segmental ischemia and leakage. Meanwhile, 25–30% of patients develop hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis [16].​

Klippel replaced ureters in 17 dogs using fresh rhesus monkey or human umbilical veins, achieving a 60% overall success rate with one dog surviving 36 months. Histologically, the umbilical vein structure remained intact at 2 years, with endothelial cells replaced by uroepithelial cells [17]. Engel evaluated tissue-engineered venous grafts in a long-term orthotopic minipig model, demonstrating multilayered urothelialization of seeded grafts at 48 weeks [18]. Brito used the great saphenous vein to replace 2 cm and 5 cm ureteral segments in two experimental dogs, with unobstructed urinary tracts confirmed by radiography at 2 and 4 months postoperatively [19].​

Paldo reported a case of laparoscopic ovarian vein repair for ureteral stricture, with favorable outcomes over 12 months of follow-up. As an autologous graft, it is not a universal solution but a valuable adjunct for select patients with radiation-induced stricturesthe ovarian vein offers advantages including anatomical proximity, matched vascular diameter, and optimal tissue compatibility, thereby avoiding traumatic complications [20]. In the present case, the patient demonstrated good initial outcomes over 3 years. However, current clinical follow-up data are predominantly limited to 1–3 years, with a paucity of data beyond 5 years. Future directions may involve combining venous grafts or venous stroma with artificial stents for long-segment ureteral reconstruction, attention should also be paid to whether the graft will expand or stricture due to changes in hemodynamics, as well as the long-term stability of renal function [21]. This technique is highly specialized, requiring implementation in high-resource centers with collaboration among urologists, vascular surgeons, and radiologists.​.

Conclusions

Radiation-induced ureteral stricture remains a challenging clinical problem with limited effective treatment options. The long-term efficacy of ovarian vein substitution for long-segment ureteral stricture requires further in-depth investigation. Future prospective multi-center studies, incorporating detailed patient stratification and extended follow-up, should aim to establish a comprehensive standardized system to provide reliable guidance for clinical practice.
