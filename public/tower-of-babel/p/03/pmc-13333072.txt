Effect Heterogeneity of CPAP on MACE by Nocturnal Hypoxia Burden in Older Adults OSAS: A Propensity Score Matching Study
Authors: Libo Zhao, Tianyi Wang, Yinghui Gao, Yaodong Ding, Yang Zhang, Dong Rui, Zehao Zhao, Xin Xue, Xiliang Zhao, Lin Liu, Yong Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSAS) is a common disease closely linked to cardiovascular events, with its prevalence markedly rising among aging populations. The core pathological feature of hypoxia clearly has an impact on cardiovascular health. As standard therapy, continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) holds undetermined long-term cardiovascular benefits in older OSAS patients, especially across different hypoxia phenotypes.MethodsThis multicenter cohort study enrolled 350 older OSAS patients, who were 1:4 matched into the CPAP group (n=70) and non-CPAP group (n=280) via propensity score matching to balance baseline confounders including age, BMI, apnea hypopnea index and comorbidities. With 4–5 years of follow-up and MACE as the primary endpoint, Cox regression and subgroup analyses stratified by OSAS severity and nocturnal hypoxia burden were performed.ResultsBaseline indicators were well-balanced between two groups. Regular CPAP use independently reduced MACE risk in older OSAS patients (adjusted hazard ratio (HR)=0.377, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.150–0.947, P=0.038). Advanced age and carotid atherosclerosis acted as independent risk factors for MACE (adjusted HR for age = 1.063, adjusted HR for CA = 3.011, both P < 0.001). Notably, subgroup analysis revealed that hypoxia phenotype dictates CPAP’s protective efficacy: prominent MACE reduction was observed in patients with high nocturnal hypoxia burden (T90 ≥ 1.5% or 5.0%), individuals under 70 years and overweight populations (aHRT90≥1.5% = 0.198, aHRT90≥5.0% = 0.120, aHRage<70 = 0.110, aHRoverweight = 0.306, all P < 0.05), whereas CPAP yielded no significant cardiovascular benefits in patients with mild hypoxia burden (T90 < 1.5%).ConclusionOur findings provide novel clinical evidence that adherent CPAP intervention is significantly associated with reduced long-term cardiovascular risks among older patients with OSAS. Clinically, individualized management tailored to hypoxia phenotypic features, instead of universal CPAP administration, may help maximize potential cardiovascular benefits and optimize comprehensive treatment strategies for this older population.Keywords: obstructive sleep apnea syndrome, OSAS, continuous positive airway pressure, CPAP, major adverse cardiovascular events, MACE, older adults, hypoxia phenotype

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSAS) is a common disorder associated with increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, and has a widespread global impact. As people age and their physical functions decline, the threat posed by OSAS to health becomes more pronounced.1 This syndrome can lead to recurrent physiological disorders and functional impairments, such as intermittent hypoxia, sleep interruption, and fluctuations in heart rate, blood pressure and intrathoracic pressure. Especially for the older adults, these immediate physiological disorders may evolve into chronic diseases, including cardiovascular diseases such as hypertension and coronary artery disease, cognitive decline, emotional disorders, and even premature death.2,3

Major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) mainly include myocardial infarction, malignant arrhythmia, heart failure and cardiogenic death, etc. It is closely related to the risk of all-cause mortality, imposing a huge burden on healthcare tasks. With the further development of the aging population structure, this burden is constantly increasing. Previous studies have suggested that with the improvement of the public’s living standards, the prevalence of OSAS is on the rise and runs parallel to the general trend of obesity and the high incidence of metabolic syndrome,4,5 both of which may further affect the occurrence and progression of cardiovascular diseases. Multiple studies have demonstrated that OSAS is a risk factor for MACE.6 Xie and Gami et al7,8 have pointed out that hypoxemia in patients with OSAS may drive parasympathetic nerve activation and induce slow-rhythm arrhythmias and other adverse cardiovascular events. Further correlation analysis between sleep breathing indicators and real-time electrocardiogram signals revealed that intermittent hypoxia could independently lead to an increase in ventricular repolarization instability.9 These may explain part of the reason for the increased risk of arrhythmia and sudden cardiac death in patients with OSAS, especially when arrhythmia instability is more obvious in phenotypes with heavy hypoxia burden.

The standard treatment for OSAS is mainly continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP), which applies positive air pressure to keep the airway open during sleep.10 The Lancet journal states that CPAP has been proven to be the lifeline for countless patients, as it can significantly alleviate the characteristic symptoms of OSAS, such as excessive daytime sleepiness and decreased sleep quality.11 Numerous studies have also demonstrated that CPAP can improve most sleep breathing monitoring indicators, such as apnea hypopnea index (AHI), oxygen desaturation index (ODI), and oxygen saturation levels.12 In addition, a randomized controlled trial indicates that CPAP is more effective than other methods such as mandibular advancement devices in reducing the severity of OSAS.13 A meta-analysis encompassing 19 trials clearly demonstrated that CPAP intervention lasting for more than 3 months could lower the 24-hour SBP by 5.01 mmHg and the DBP by 3.30 mmHg.14 CPAP should be beneficial to the cardiovascular health of patients with OSAS.

Many studies have investigated the potential risk association between OSAS and MACE. As the research progressed, it gradually became widely accepted that OSAS is a heterogeneous disease. Currently, the OSAS phenotype is mainly determined through various clustering analysis methods. Common studies on disease phenotypes are based on craniofacial features, airway anatomy, and obesity.15 Recently, researchers have increasingly recognized the crucial role of the “hypoxia phenotype” in the heterogeneity of OSAS. A recent study from Spain has indicated that the degree of hypoxia, T90, can predict carotid atherosclerosis (CA),16 and a study of European Heart Journal in 2024 once again demonstrated a definite correlation between CA and the cumulative risk of MACE over a decade.17 Earlier on the European Heart Journal, it was also reported that the nocturnal hypoxia burden can effectively predict cardiovascular mortality in men.18 Moreover, the standard treatment for OSAS — CPAP — has been proven to significantly improve the quality of life of OSAS patients.19 However, there are relatively few studies observing the impact of CPAP on the occurrence of MACE in older patients in China and analyzing the role of the hypoxia phenotype in it. Just as the latest clinical consensus released by European society of Cardiology (ESC) points out, the main challenges in cardiovascular risk management for older patients are also the lack of clinical data and intervention experience.20 This research aims to explore the association between CPAP intervention and the risk of MACE in older OSAS patients, and the corresponding clinical benefits of patients with hypoxia phenotype under CPAP intervention, thereby providing valuable insights for personalized intervention strategies for preventing cardiovascular events and improving quality of life.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

This was a multi-center cohort study completed in Chinese mainland. Initially, A total of 1290 subjects aged ≥60 years with OSAS diagnosed by gold standard overnight polysomnography (PSG) were enrolled from Chinese People’s Liberation Army (PLA) General Hospital, 960th Hospital of Chinese PLA, Beijing Chaoyang Hospital, Peking University People’s Hospital, Peking University International Hospital and Affiliated Hospital of Gansu University of Chinese Medicine from January 2015 to October 2017. There were typical symptoms such as snoring during sleep accompanied by apnea and daytime sleepiness. Physical examination revealed stenosis or obstruction in any part of the upper airway. OSAS was diagnosed based on an AHI of ≥5 times per hour described in the related content of JAMA.21 These OSAS patients did not include simple snoring, central sleep apnea events more than half of the total number of events, normal peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) < 90% due to maxillofacial deformity or lung disease. This study was conducted in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and had been approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the PLAGH (S2019-352-01). The basic information and clinical measurement data of patients were strictly managed and kept confidential. All patients or guardians gave informed consent to the study objectives and follow-up procedures.

Since this study mainly focused on the impact of CPAP intervention on MACE, we further excluded the following factors: patients with severe cardiac, renal, liver and other organ dysfunction, advanced tumor and other cachexia; Patients who had been hospitalized for myocardial infarction, unstable angina, or heart failure at baseline; Participants with incomplete or missing core clinical variables; Loss to follow-up or less than 6 months of follow-up. According to the literature review, the probability of MACE in patients with high risk of cardiovascular disease at baseline is about 18%–30%, and the incidence of male is significantly higher than that of female.22 The KAMIR study reported that the incidence of MACE in patients with acute coronary syndrome within 1 year was 10.9%.23 In this study, the average follow-up observation time was expected to be 4–5 years, the risk of MACE was set as 30%, and the allowable error δ was 0.05, so the expected sample size should be 323 individuals. Therefore, there were more than 1000 older OSAS patients at baseline, and the initial sample size was feasible. Considering the actual number of cases with good CPAP compliance, it was reasonable to compare the groups at a ratio of 1:4 (350 patients in total) based on propensity score matching (PSM).

Data Collection and Clinical Measurements

PSG

Patients completed PSG monitoring in each medical center in a calm state in the non-acute stage of the diseases. The subjects were prohibited to drink tea, coffee and other functional beverages before monitoring, and avoid using sedative-hypnotic drugs for them. The majority of participants underwent overnight sleep breathing monitoring using a device manufactured by Compumedics (Melbourne, Australia). AHI, ODI, mean and longest apnea duration, Mean SpO2, Lowest SpO2 and T90 were recorded for each patient. The parameters obtained from PSG monitoring were automatically analyzed and manually calibrated by two sleep technologists, and then checked by a senior sleep medicine physician. Respiratory events were determined according to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine criteria.24 A sleep apnea event was defined as a decrease of ≥ 90% in the peak signal of airflow intensity for more than 10 seconds. The hypopnea event was defined as a decrease in airflow intensity ≥ 30% for more than 10 seconds accompanied by a decrease in SpO2 ≥ 3%. The severity of OSA was determined according to the internationally recognized AHI, mild OSAS (5 times/h ≤ AHI < 15 times/h), moderate OSAS (15 times/h ≤ AHI < 30 times/h), and severe OSAS (AHI ≥ 30 times/h).

Clinical Indicators

Collect the patient’s general information such as age, gender, body mass index (BMI). Review and record the past medical history and personal history of each patient, such as their alcohol and tobacco use and comorbidities. In this study, only current smoking and alcohol habits were recorded, and never and past exposure to smoking and drinking were defined as the other status of comparison. According to the principle of not affecting the normal process of diagnosis and treatment and not increasing the burden of medical trauma, the laboratory test indexes of patients in the fasting state within 2 weeks before and after sleep breathing monitoring were collected, such as hemoglobin (HB), erythrocyte count, fasting blood glucose (FBG), uric acid, triglyceride, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C). Two senior technicians of the laboratory performed these tests, reviewed and issued the results. The conditions of comorbidities could be verified and recorded through the outpatient and inpatient medical record system. (1) Hypertension: it was necessary to refrain from smoking and drinking tea for half an hour before the blood pressure measurement. An experienced nursing staff measured arterial blood pressure in the upper extremity while the bladder was empty in a calm state. If the participant’s systolic blood pressure (SBP) was ≥ 140 mmHg and/or the diastolic blood pressure (DBP) was ≥ 90 mmHg, and the measurement data reached this standard three times on different days; Or the 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure average was ≥ 130/80 mmHg; Or a definite history of hypertension or the participant was receiving antihypertensive drug treatment;25 (2) Hyperlipidemia: the patient had a known history of elevated blood lipids, or the specific manifestations included abnormal increases in total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), or LDL-C concentrations in the blood, namely TC ≥ 4.7 mmol/L, TG ≥ 2.3 mmol/L, LDL-C ≥ 4.1 mmol/L, or lipid-lowering drugs were being used for treatment;26 (3) Diabetes mellitus: FBG level ≥ 126mg/dL, glycated hemoglobin level ≥ 6.5%, or any form of hypoglycemic therapy had been used;27 (4) Carotid atherosclerosis: the presence of carotid plaque focal lesions or carotid intima-media thickness ≥ 1.0 mm were detected by an ultrasound specialist using the vascular ultrasound probe at an appropriate frequency.28 General information, laboratory test parameters, and comorbidities, among other indicators, were used as covariates for further statistical analysis of the influencing factors of MACE.

Process of Follow-Up

MACE was used as the endpoint of the study, including myocardial infarction, cardiovascular death, and hospitalization for unstable angina or heart failure.5 Patients’ outpatient and inpatient records and telephone communication were used to collect relevant information for follow-up. Without knowing the results of PSG monitoring, researchers contacted patients or their close relatives by telephone every six months. The observation ended at the first occurrence of MACE or all-cause death, and the follow-up process was completed in February 2022. Follow-up results of participants were further validated by investigating outpatient visits and inpatient medical records during the study period. The cause of death was determined based on the discharge introduction letter or death certificate provided by the patient’s family.

Status of Grouping

Most of the subjects in this study were patients with moderate-to-severe OSAS, and CPAP was recommended as the first-line treatment for patients with moderate-to-severe level and obvious daytime sleepiness.29 For patients who voluntarily accepted this program, professional and technical personnel initially performed ventilator pressure titration, and followed up to adjust the most appropriate pressure level during the application process to maintain the compliance and effectiveness of wearing CPAP.

In terms of the design of this study, the use of PSM can effectively reduce the confounding bias, so as to achieve the effect similar to that of randomized controlled studies. According to the actual application during follow-up, the subjects were divided into CPAP group and non-CPAP group with a ratio of 1:4. The CPAP group consisted of 70 people, all of whom had good CPAP compliance and had complete and objective baseline data parameters, with wearing CPAP for ≥4h per night and ≥70% of the total natural days, that is, no less than 5 days per week,30 including patients who started to use CPAP within half a year of the initial follow-up and wore it regularly for more than 24 months. There were 280 people in the non-CPAP group (control group), which referred to patients who had never used CPAP or had poor adherence, including patients who had used CPAP for less than six months, or who had given up the treatment due to dry mouth, nasal pain, flatulence, economic conditions and other reasons.

Statistical Analyses

PSM was performed using R software version 4.2.1. In order to better control for possible confounding factors, 12 relevant variables covering demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, sleep respiratory indicators and chronic comorbidities were incorporated into the matching model, including age, gender, BMI, AHI, T90, smoking, drinking status and common cardiovascular-related comorbidities, and baseline balance was assessed mainly by P values. We completed the matching of each patient in the CPAP group with the corresponding patient in the non-CPAP group using the 1:4 nearest-neighbor matching without replacement with a caliper of 0.2 standard deviations of propensity scores. Common support assumption was verified to ensure reasonable sample overlap. SPSS 25.0 software was used for statistical analyses. Before the analysis of quantitative data, the normality and homogeneity of variance tests were conducted first. Quantitative data conforming to normal distribution were represented by mean ± standard deviation, and comparison between groups was performed by t-test. Data that did not conform to the normal distribution were represented by the median (interquartile range) [M(Q1,Q3)], and non-parametric tests were used for comparisons between groups. Categorical data were expressed as the number of cases (percentage, %) and chi-square tests were used to explore the differences. Kaplan–Meier curves were used to illustrate the cumulative incidence of MACE in the two groups with or without CPAP, and then the statistically significant indicators in Cox proportional hazards univariate analysis were included. Forward likelihood ratio (LR) method was used for multivariate analysis to reveal the relationships between CPAP and other factors and the occurrence of MACE. Sensitivity analysis was further performed in subgroups based on nocturnal hypoxia burden and severity of OSAS. All statistics were two-sided tests, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The Situation of Propensity Score Matching

The older patients with OSAS were selected as the research subjects. Based on the follow-up observation records, the subjects were divided into the CPAP group (good compliance) and the non-CPAP group (no application or poor compliance). According to the actual situation, further statistical methods were used to perform standardized group matching based on age, gender, BMI, AHI, complications, alcohol use and other factors, and the two groups were divided into a 1:4 ratio. Finally, there were 70 cases in the CPAP group and 280 cases in the non-CPAP group, totaling 350 individuals. Supplementary Table 1 exhibited the comprehensive matching of common factors between the two groups, such as age, BMI, alcohol consumption and the distribution of common complications, which showed no significant statistical differences between the groups (P > 0.05 for each item).

Comparison of Clinical Information Characteristics Between Groups

The subjects were divided into two groups according to whether they used CPAP or not, and the differences of each index between the groups were compared after controlling for multivariate PSM. As shown in Supplementary Table 2, there were no significant statistical differences in multiple indicators between the groups. But the level of LDL-C in the CPAP group was slightly higher than that in the non-CPAP group (2.67 mmol/L vs. 2.41 mmol/L, P = 0.034). The platelet count in the non-CPAP group was higher than that in the other group (210.0×109/L vs. 196.6×109/L, P = 0.031). Importantly, we observed a significantly lower incidence of MACE in the CPAP group than in the non-CPAP group (7.1% vs. 18.6%, P = 0.021, Figure 1).

Schematic diagram of the proportion of MACE occurrence in the two groups with or without CPAP application (*: P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Non-CPAP group: those who had never used CPAP and had poor compliance).

The study participants were categorized into two groups based on the occurrence of MACE, and intergroup differences in various parameters were analyzed. As shown in Table 1, patients in the MACE group were significantly older than those in the non-MACE group (68 years vs. 65 years, P < 0.001). Additionally, Erythrocyte counts and hemoglobin levels were lower in the MACE group compared with the non-MACE group (4.35 × 1012/L vs. 4.53 × 1012/L; 134.0 g/L vs. 140.0 g/L; both P < 0.05). The MACE group also had a significantly higher prevalence of smoking, atrial fibrillation, and carotid atherosclerosis (all P < 0.05). In contrast, the proportion of CPAP use was lower in the MACE group (8.8% vs. 22.2%, P = 0.021).

The Relationship Between CPAP Intervention and the Incidence of MACE

The median follow-up duration in this study was 46 months. The cumulative incidence of MACE was plotted using the Kaplan–Meier method for the two groups (with and without CPAP). As shown in Figure 2, the red and blue curves diverged noticeably over time, indicating a lower incidence of MACE in the CPAP group compared to the non-CPAP group (Plog-rank = 0.022). Univariable Cox proportional hazards analysis revealed that CPAP intervention was associated with a significantly reduced risk of MACE (HR = 0.360, 95% CI: 0.144–0.902, P = 0.029). Subsequently, variables listed on the left side of Table 2—including those with P < 0.1 in the univariable screening as well as other relevant factors that may have clinical implications—were incorporated into a multivariable Cox model. After adjustment using the Forward: LR method, CPAP therapy remained significantly associated with a lower probability of MACE (adjusted HR = 0.377, 95% CI: 0.150–0.947, P = 0.038; Supplementary Figure 1). Additionally, the multivariable Cox analysis identified aging and carotid atherosclerosis as significant factors influencing MACE occurrence in older patients with OSAS (adjusted HRage = 1.063, adjusted HRCA = 3.011, both P < 0.001).

Subgroup Analysis

In the analysis of the entire study population (n=350), CPAP use demonstrated a statistically significant association with a reduced risk of MACE (P = 0.038). Regarding MACE risk, no significant interactions were observed between CPAP treatment and gender, age ≥ 70 years, overweight-obesity, or severe OSAS (P for interaction > 0.05 for all). However, significant interactions were identified between CPAP use and the subgroups defined by T90 ≥ 1.5% and hemoglobin ≥ 130 g/L (P for interaction = 0.049 and 0.024, respectively). Multivariable Cox analysis was performed using the forward: LR method, followed by comprehensive subgroup sensitivity analyses. Specifically, no statistically significant effect of CPAP on MACE was found within either gender subgroup. In the subgroup of patients aged < 70 years and the severe OSAS subgroup, CPAP application was significantly associated with a lower risk of MACE (adjusted HR = 0.110 and 0.114, respectively, both P < 0.05; Table 3). Similarly, in other subgroups—including overweight-obesity, hemoglobin ≥ 130 g/L, and those with a greater nocturnal hypoxic burden (T90 ≥ 1.5% and ≥ 5%31)—CPAP use was also associated with a reduced risk of MACE (P < 0.05 for all; Figure 3). In contrast, among patients with a relatively lower hypoxic burden (T90 < 1.5%), CPAP did not exhibit a statistically significant effect on MACE incidence. It is noteworthy that aging and CA remained significantly associated with an increased risk of MACE across most subgroups (adjusted HR > 1.000 for all).

Discussion

This study suggests that well-compliant CPAP can be associated with a reduced risk of MACE in older patients with OSAS. Some clinical studies have also shown similar results. An observational study with an average follow-up of 10 years suggested that among male patients with OSAS, the risk of cardiovascular events in the severe population was significantly higher than that in mild-to-moderate patients or healthy controls, and CPAP treatment could reduce this risk.32 Another prospective study involving 1,116 women with a median follow-up of 72 months found that severe OSAS was closely associated with cardiovascular death, and adequate CPAP treatment (administered for at least 4 hours daily) might also reduce the risk of cardiogenic death in women.33

However, in recent studies, there have been inconsistent conclusions. A relatively new meta-analysis mentioned that four studies found CPAP to be associated with a reduced risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality, and ten studies showed that CPAP improved the incidence of MACE. However, a systematic review of over 160,000 people did not show a consistent benefit of CPAP on MACE in patients with OSAS.34 This might be related to the inconsistences in the form of the included studies (some were meta-analyses and some were randomized controlled studies), diagnostic criteria of OSAS (with sleepiness scale assessment and PSG monitoring), and the duration of CPAP application (whether there is a dose-dependent effect on the intervention method).35 The SAVE study, published in the New England Journal of Medicine, suggested that compared with routine care alone, the group that received CPAP treatment in addition did not successfully prevent cardiovascular events and related CVD in patients with moderate-to-severe OSAS.36 These overall negative results may be due to the limitations of the trial design and the low compliance with CPAP. Experts such as Chen’s interpretation of SAVE pointed out that patients who most needed CPAP, such as those with severe hypoxia at night and severe daytime sleepiness, were excluded in the study. While these patients had a greater risk of cardiovascular events and might benefit more significantly from CPAP treatment. Furthermore, the average application time of CPAP in SAVE study was 3.3 hours per night, which did not meet the standard for good compliance; Moreover, in the baseline stage of this study, all participants included those who already had cardia-cerebrovascular diseases. Such patients Frequently already had autonomic nerve regulation disorders and insomnia, and the decline in sleep quality may counteract the beneficial effects produced by CPAP intervention.37 Elbadawi et al conducted a meta-analysis involving 5,684 patients and found that after an average follow-up of 42.6 months, well-compliant CPAP could reduce the risk of MACE by 36%.38 Another meta-analysis with the same focus showed that CPAP compliance time of ≥ 4 hours per night could achieve a remarkable 57% reduction in the risk of MACE, subsequently, sensitivity analysis indicated that increasing the duration of CPAP could significantly reduce the risks of MACE and stroke.39 Overall, the application of CPAP can reduce the frequency of apnea and the degree of nocturnal hypoxia, improving sleep quality and respiratory ventilation is beneficial to cardiovascular health. As pointed out in the China Dongfeng-Tongji cohort study, even for the older adults with a higher genetic risk of CVD, maintaining a good sleep pattern during the follow-up period can reduce their probability of CVD.40 Therefore, the protective effect of well-compliant CPAP intervention on MACE is supported by certain theoretical basis.

In this study, it was found that the risk of MACE significantly decreased in older patients with OSAS after treatment. One possible highly significant explanation for this is that the treatment of OSAS may reduce the inflammation and oxidative stress associated with the disease itself.41 Due to the damage of the antioxidant system caused by free radicals, an obvious increase in oxidative stress reactions has been found in cardiovascular diseases. A meta-analysis including 35 studies involving 1,985 patients with OSAS also demonstrates that CPAP intervention can partially inhibit systemic inflammation, and various inflammatory markers in the blood, such as C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), IL-8, and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), are significantly reduced.42 Similar effects have also been observed in an asthma animal model, and CPAP can reduce the level of inflammation and airway responsiveness.43 Meanwhile, multiple studies have shown that the level of systemic inflammatory response is clearly associated with the risk of MACE.44 Inflammatory cytokines play a role in promoting arterial inflammation and the subsequent formation of atherosclerotic plaques. Arterial inflammation can lead to asymmetric focal arterial thickening, as well as the formation and progression of atherosclerotic plaques,35 which is the main pathogenesis of CVD.45

CPAP has been shown to exert beneficial impacts on a variety of comorbidities. Clinical investigations have already established that CPAP intervention not only leads to a reduction in blood pressure levels but is also associated with a decreased likelihood of new-onset hypertension.46 Furthermore, OSAS is intricately associated with body metabolic disorders. Conversely, the treatment of OSAS could modulate metabolic parameters. Shpirer et al reported that following 3 to 5 months of CPAP therapy, with the restoration of oxygen saturation, the levels of glycated hemoglobin in patients with severe OSAS decreased significantly.47 The TREATOSA-MS randomized controlled trial enrolled 100 patients, who were evenly allocated into two groups. The reversible rate of metabolic syndrome in the CPAP group was higher than that in the placebo control group (18% vs 4%, P = 0.04). And further investigations indicated that CPAP indeed facilitated a moderate reduction in visceral fat and an improvement in endothelial function.48 It is widely recognized that metabolic syndrome serves as a risk factor for CVD and associated mortality.49 Presumably, the effects of CPAP on regulating blood pressure and enhancing metabolism would be manifested in the control and reduction of the risk of MACE.

CPAP makes an influence on factors including vascular function and endocrine. Basal endothelial nitric oxide (NO) primarily stems from the action of endothelial NO synthase (eNOS). In untreated patients with OSAS, the endothelial expression of eNOS and phosphorylated eNOS (the activated form of eNOS) is decreased by 59% and 94% respectively. Simultaneously, both cyclooxygenase-2 and inducible NOS are upregulated in the oxidative stress response triggered by superoxide, which could give rise to platelet activation, endothelial dysfunction, and vasoconstriction. While the application of CPAP could significantly downregulate the expression levels of these two markers (cyclooxygenase-2 and inducible NOS), suggesting that CPAP could restore vascular endothelial function through regulating NO levels.50 And vascular aging serves as a crucial risk factor for cardiovascular dysfunction and MACE. Aging induces the deterioration of vascular elasticity and an elevation in peripheral vascular resistance.51 CVD, such as hypertension, is highly prevalent among the older population. OSAS may also directly or indirectly initiate and exacerbate cellular aging via exosomes through oxidative stress-related mechanisms. For untreated OSAS patients, exosomes in the circulatory system significantly augment the senescence of naive endothelial cells, with a notable increase in the expression of the senescence-related gene p16. Long-term compliance with CPAP treatment could partially reverse this senescent state.52 Further multi-omics analysis of circulating exosomes revealed that OSAS-related characteristic exosomal lipid profiles possessed the capacity to induce spontaneous invagination of the cell membrane. It also demonstrated that exosomes of individuals who had adhered to CPAP for one year ameliorated vascular endothelial permeability and functional disorders.53 An early study found that CPAP withdrawal frequently led to a rapid recurrence of OSAS, and related to impaired endothelial function, increased urinary catecholamines, elevated blood pressure and heart rate.54

In addition, the research by Jayaprakash et al demonstrates that CPAP treatment can significantly decrease cardiac injury markers such as N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) and atrial natriuretic peptide, suggesting that this intervention may mitigate the risk of heart failure in patients with OSAS.55 The incidence of chronic kidney disease in OSAS patients is higher compared to that in the general healthy population. A retrospective cohort study conducted by Moriya et al implies that early CPAP treatment may reduce tubular injury and decelerate the progression to end-stage renal disease.56 And preserving optimal renal function is advantageous for reducing cardiac afterload and maintaining the body’s internal environmental homeostasis. Notably, up to one-fourth of patients with OSAS experience psychological issues such as depression,57 and emotional disorders are acknowledged as risk factors for the incidence and mortality of CVD.58 Evidence suggests that CPAP intervention is advantageous for individuals with emotional disorders. In a specific study of individuals aged over 70 years, compared to the situation without CPAP, the symptoms of depression and anxiety in patients were significantly alleviated after 3 months of CPAP treatment.59 Martínez-García et al also reported comparable findings. After CPAP intervention, the patients’ quality of life, anxiety and depression status, and some neurocognitive test results all showed improvements.60 Therefore, CPAP intervention may improve or protect cardiovascular function through multiple pathways.

In the subgroup analysis, we found that older patients who were relatively younger, overweight-obese, had severe OSAS, and had a heavy burden of nocturnal hypoxia showed more significant cardiovascular benefits after CPAP intervention. The influence of age is relatively easy to understand. On the one hand, as mentioned earlier, aging can cause reduced vascular elasticity and increased peripheral circulation resistance. On the other hand, OSAS has a long-term latent effect on overall health through oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, with a cumulative effect on the pathological process. The new biomarker study on the cardiovascular risk of OSAS by Solelhac et al61 supports this view. The pulse wave amplitude drops (PWAD) index can reflect sympathetic nerve activity and vascular reactivity, that is, the lower the index, the worse the vascular reactivity. In two large sample cohort studies, it was proved that a continuous increase of the PWAD index by 10 times per hour was significantly negatively correlated with the cardiovascular event risk in OSAS patients (HR1 = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.73–0.99; HR2 = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.86–0.96). Moreover, further analysis revealed that OSAS patients with a higher PWAD index were more likely to reduce the risk of cardiovascular events through CPAP treatment, namely, patients whose autonomic nerve or vascular functions had not undergone significant changes or damage were more likely to benefit from CPAP intervention.

Recent hot research evidences indicate that hypoxic burden is a new measure and predictor of mortality risk and cardiovascular outcomes in sleep medicine study cohorts.62,63 The well-known HIPARCO randomized clinical trial found that good CPAP compliance, a high T90, and severe OSAS of individuals were more conducive to the control of blood pressure by positive pressure ventilation in patients with OSAS and refractory hypertension. This indicates that these factors represent the clinical phenotype in which CPAP treatment can enhance cardiovascular protection.64 The study also suggested a correlation between CPAP usage time and the reduction of 24-hour average SBP, indicating the positive effect of CPAP in managing blood pressure levels and regulating the sympathetic nervous system, and proving the benefits of its good compliance for cardiovascular health. A clinical intervention study suggested that CPAP treatment could improve renal hemodynamics and reduce the activity of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) in patients with OSAS. Moreover, this effect was more pronounced in the severe hypoxia subgroup.65 A study on epigenetics using blood samples indicated that the coexistence of OSAS and obesity altered the characteristics of microRNAs in extracellular vesicles. After half a year of CPAP treatment, the levels of microRNA 21, microRNA 126 and microRNA 320 in severely obese OSAS patients were significantly reduced. And these microRNAs were involved in the progression of CVD, metabolic syndrome and cancer.66 Furthermore, domestic research on different OSAS phenotypes in China have shown similar results. A considerable number of OSAS patients have a low respiratory arousal threshold phenotype. Due to the low arousal threshold, they rarely experience significant or severe nocturnal hypoxia. It is also pointed out that a low respiratory arousal threshold may be an important factor in the development of OSAS in non-obese patients.67 Frequent nocturnal awakenings in patients naturally have a significant impact on the compliance of CPAP use, which is consistent with the phenomenon of fewer CPAP cases in the subgroups with mild hypoxia and non-overweight in our study. So no statistical results related to MACE benefits were found. Additionally, the BMI is a key factor influencing the severity of OSAS,68 and the severity of OSAS and the degree of hypoxia are positively correlated with the concentration of hemoglobin in the blood.69 Therefore, this study demonstrates that other subgroups benefiting from CPAP intervention may all point to the same clinical significance. Because more severe OSAS, a higher BMI, and a greater hemoglobin content are all consistent with an increased burden of nocturnal hypoxia. So it comprehensively demonstrates that the patient phenotyping35 may have good clinical significance for precise management and improving prognosis.

Overall, to maximize the clinical benefits of CPAP intervention, it is essential not only to maintain high adherence to therapy but also to consider key factors such as hypoxia levels, severity of OSAS, and vascular elasticity, thereby enabling targeted and precision-based treatment strategies. Meanwhile, this study has several limitations that warrant further investigation. The sample size is relatively limited, which would to some extent affect the statistical efficacy and the generalizability of the conclusion. With such a limited number of events, these analyses carry the risk of instability and false-positive results. And the follow-up duration may be insufficient to capture long-term outcomes, so causal inference cannot be overinterpreted. This exploration should merely support the association between the OSAS phenotype and the efficacy of CPAP. Therefore, future research should incorporate larger cohorts and extended longitudinal follow-up. Well-designed, long-term, randomized controlled trials are needed to further validate the efficacy of CPAP and to allow sufficient time for the full impact of the intervention on chronic disease progression to manifest. To enhance data reliability, sustained intervention adherence should be promoted and encouraged, rigorous follow-up protocols implemented to minimize personnel attrition, and objective sleep breathing parameters collected before and after intervention to record variations in severity and associated hypoxemia—going beyond mere documentation of CPAP compliance. Despite the application of PSM to balance sleep respiratory parameters and common comorbidities, adjustments for antihypertensive/hypoglycemic medications use and other complications such as peripheral artery disease were insufficient. Hence, residual confounding effects and indication bias cannot be completely ruled out. The grouping based on long-term CPAP adherence may carry potential immortal-time bias, and limited clinical events restrict the generalizability of subgroup results. Additionally, incorporating data on body weight fluctuations, adjustments in major medication regimens, and the onset timing of new comorbid conditions would provide valuable covariate information, as these factors may influence cardiovascular outcomes. Furthermore, future studies should explore the potential effects of CPAP therapy on the development and progression of arterial plaque burden, including in coronary and carotid arteries.

Conclusions

Sustained compliance with CPAP therapy is associated with a significant reduction in the risk of MACE among older patients with OSAS over a 4–5 years period. This protective effect might be particularly evident in subgroups with severe OSAS, pronounced nocturnal hypoxia, and those who are overweight-obese. Recognizing the pivotal role of CPAP within the comprehensive management strategy for older OSAS patients is essential for optimizing clinical outcomes. Furthermore, the integration of phenotypic characterization—particularly the hypoxia-dominant phenotype—into risk stratification may enhance the precision of predicting individualized clinical benefits from CPAP intervention. Through subsequent prospective cohort studies and scientifically optimized statistical designs, we further validate and consolidate this clinical conclusions.
