Impact of Carina Width on Re‐Ablation Rate of Atrial Fibrillation After Primary Cryoballoon‐Ablation
Authors: Miriam Müller, Marja Hedman, Marianne Heikkilä, Juhani Junttila
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundReconnection of the pulmonary veins (PVs) is the primary cause of atrial fibrillation (AF) episodes after pulmonary vein isolation (PVI). Cryoballoon ablation does not always include the isolation of the carina due to anatomic variants and carina width has been associated with increased re‐ablation rate in previous radiofrequency ablation studies. Aim of our study was to assess the impact of carina width on the re‐ablation rate after cryoballoon‐PVI.MethodsWe included 518 patients who underwent cryoballoon ablation for AF between 2015 and 2022. Each patient underwent contrast enhanced chest computed tomography (CT) scan prior to ablation. We measured the shortest distance between the superior and inferior PV (carina width) and the size of the left atrium in CT images. In patients referred to re‐ablation due to recurring symptomatic and ECG documented AF we defined the reconnected veins. The minimum follow up after prior PVI was 1 year.ResultsIn all patients the right carina was wider than the left (8.04 ± 4.49 mm vs. 5.25 ± 3.03 mm, p < 0.059). One fifth (n = 102) of the patients underwent re‐ablation for AF. Left carina width was significantly associated with re‐ablation rate (in re‐ablation group 6.4 ± 2.7 mm vs. in no re‐ablation group 4.9 ± 3.0 mm, p < 0.001) whereas right carina width was not (8.3 ± 3.0 mm vs. 7.9 ± 3.6 mm, p = 0.421, respectively). Left atrium (LA) was larger in the re‐ablation group, but the size of the LA did not correlate with the left carina width (r = 0.075; p = 0.111). However, there was a significant correlation between the size of the LA and the width of the right carina (r = 0.251, p < 0.001). Also, in the re‐ablation group a significant correlation between right carina width and reconnection of the right PV's (RPV) was detected, which was not seen in the left PV's (LPV) (p = 0.015; p = 0.360, respectively).ConclusionThe left carina width and LA size are associated with the success rate of primary cryoballoon‐PVI. However, there was a correlation between the right carina width and the reconnection of the RPV's leading to re‐ablation. It seems that recognition of anatomical variants could be useful in the decision of ablation technique, but further studies are needed.

Introduction

Catheter ablation is a well‐established treatment for atrial fibrillation (AF) and prevention of AF‐recurrences [1]. According to the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines of the diagnosis and management of atrial fibrillation, AF ablation should be considered in patients with symptomatic paroxysmal AF as first line treatment on the basis of patient's choice and recommended after failed antiarrhythmic drug therapy [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) has been established as a standard procedure and it can be performed with radiofrequency (RF) energy, pulse field ablation or cryoballoon ablation. In previous studies, there have not been major differences in procedural success rate between different methods. However, studies showed a minor complication rate and shorter procedure times in favor of cryoballoon ablation versus RF ablation and thus cryoballoon ablation is used as first line therapy in many centers [9, 10, 11]. However, there is still approximately 20% recurrence rate of AF after initial PVI [12]. The primary cause for recurrence of AF after initial PVI has been shown to be reconnection of the pulmonary veins (PV's). Prior studies have shown favorite sites of gaps in the ablation lines after RF‐ and cryoballoon ablation including the carina area as well as epicardial connections between both atria [13, 14, 15, 16]. Another common anatomical cause of arrythmia recurrence after PVI has been thought to be the existence of common ostium of left PV's (LCO) [17]. Though, there have also been studies that suggested no difference in clinical outcome after cryoballoon ablation in patients with and without LCO [18]. Aim of our study was to assess the impact of carina width and left atrial (LA) size on the re‐ablation rate after cryoballoon PVI and to evaluate the effect of incomplete isolation of the carina area and the impact of LCO in the same setting.

Methods

The study was approved by the research services of Northern Ostrobothnia Wellbeing County, and the informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of this registry study.

Study Design, Setting and Patient Characteristics

This study was a retrospective single center registry study. All patients who underwent initial cryoballoon‐PVI with the old and new generation cryoballoon (ArcticFront and ArcticFront Advance, Medtronic, Minnesota, USA) in Oulu University Hospital from January 2015 until the end of December 2023 were included in the study. The follow‐up of the patients continued until the end of January 2024. A total of 518 patients from our ablation registry were included. All patients undergoing cryoballoon ablation had symptomatic paroxysmal or persistent AF documented by a 12 channel ECG prior to ablation. According to current guidelines, paroxysmal AF was defined as self‐terminating AF within 7 days after detection or cardioverted AF into stable sinus rhythm within one to 3 months after detection. Persistent AF was defined as a duration of AF less than 6 months. Patients with chronic AF were excluded (Central Illustration 1, 1).

Computed Tomography Scan (CT) and Measurements

All patients received contrast‐enhanced chest or heart CT scan before PVI to assess the LA anatomy and anatomy of the PV's according to hospital protocols. The CT protocol and slice thickness ranged from 1 to 3 mm, depending on the center of imaging. All cardiac or coronary CT scans, 64% of all CT scans (n = 329), were ECG‐gated and conducted during mid‐diastole. Imaging centers and their scanner models as well as the imaging protocols are presented in Table 1. The width of the right and left carina was measured from CT images by one well experienced electrophysiologist (M.M.) and one highly skilled radiographer (M.H.). The carina width was measured as the minimum distance between the superior and inferior PV [19]. The minimum distance between the superior and inferior PV's was determined utilizing a distance measurement tool on a three‐dimensional reconstruction model of the LA in sagittal view (Figure 1, 1). For cases with three right‐side veins, only the distance between the superior and inferior PV was measured, excluding the middle vein. LA 3D reconstruction images were generated using the SynGoVia analysis system (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany, version VB80H).

To confirm the reproducibility of our measurements both the left and right carina and LA measurements were repeated in a subset of 95 subjects. For intraobserver variability M.M measured 48 subjects of her own initial measurements and for interobserver variability M.M. repeated measurements of 47 subjects where M.H initially made measurements.

Ablation Protocol

All AF ablations were performed by five experienced specialists in electrophysiology. Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) was not routinely performed before ablation in patients with sinus rhythm. Patients showing up with AF, TEE was performed prior to PVI in case of interrupted anticoagulation to exclude intracardiac thrombi. Patients with low CHA2DS2‐VASc score (<2) and paroxysmal AF were not necessarily on anticoagulation before ablation. The standard groin access was used, generally two groin punctures of the femoral vein. An eight French sheath was introduced for the coronary sinus (CS) catheter used as a landmark for the transseptal puncture and pacing possibility in case of bradycardia and phrenic nerve stimulation. Transseptal puncture was done under fluoroscopy control without TEE guidance with an Abbot 8.5 French SL 0 or SL 1 sheath (Abbot Medical, Plymouth, MN) and a BRK 0 (Abbot Medical, Plymouth, MN) extra sharp transseptal needle. For ablation the SL sheath was switched to the Medtronic FlexCath 12.5 French sheath with the over‐the‐wire technique (Medtronic Inc, Pointe‐Claire, Quebec). The Medtronic Achieve circular mapping catheter was used to deliver the cryoballoon safely to the veins. A 28 mm sized cryoballoon was used in all cases. From 2015 until the end of April 2019 the old generation cryoballoon was used, after that we switched to the new generation cryoballoon.

During ablation of the right PV's (RPV) the CS catheter was placed in the vena cava superior and continuous phrenic nerve stimulation was performed as safety feature. The esophageal temperature was not measured during ablation. Successful isolation of the PV's was defined by bidirectional block, pacing from inside the isolated PV. During the procedure, heparin was given to achieve an activated clotting time >300 s. Protamine was given at the end of the procedure to neutralize the effect of heparin. Transthoracic echocardiography was done after the ablation procedure to exclude complications like cardiac tamponade. Ongoing anticoagulation, in case of novel oral anticoagulants, was interrupted 1 day before the procedure. Warfarin was continued if INR below 3. Anticoagulation was restarted immediately after the ablation and continued at least 2 months after ablation for all patients or permanently based on CHA2DS2‐VASc score. Antiarrhythmic medication was evaluated individually. All patients were prescribed for a proton pump inhibitor for 3 weeks after ablation.

Follow Up and Scheduling for Re‐Ablation

All patients had a follow‐up phone call after 3 months from the initial PVI. The questionnaire included the evaluation of remaining symptoms of AF. The first 2 months after ablation were defined as blanking time and arrythmia events during this period were not considered as an indication for re‐ablation. After the blanking time, patients with symptoms of AF were scheduled for re‐ablation. Patients not scheduled for re‐ablation were defined as control group. The interval between the first PVI and the re‐ablation varied. It depended on the persistence of symptoms, the appearance of new symptoms, and the referral hospital's routines.

Patients scheduled for re‐ablation underwent RF—ablation using the point‐by‐point technique and the Carto‐3 3D Mapping System of Biosense Webster (Biosense Webster, Inc., Irvine, CA). Re‐ablation was done either with the Thermocool‐SmartTouch irrigated ablation catheter (Biosense Webster Inc., Irvine, CA, USA) and the ablation index CLOSE protocol or with magnetic navigation (Stereotaxis corp., Saint Louis, MO, USA) and the Navistar irrigated ablation catheter [19].

Study Endpoints

The primary end point was AF recurrence after primary PVI which resulted in re‐ablation. The follow‐up time was defined as the interval from the initial PVI to the end of January 2024. Safety endpoints were defined as death, stroke, cardiac tamponade, phrenic nerve palsy and bleeding of the puncture site with need for surgery.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 22.0, for Windows (IBM Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed variables and their 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), or as median for non‐normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were reported as percentage or numbers. Differences between groups were evaluated with the t‐test in normally distributed continuous variables, Mann–Whitney U‐test was used in variables with skewed distribution. Normality testing was performed and distribution skewness between −1 and 1 were considered normally distributed. A p‐value <0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed for continuous and categorial variables which had significant association related primary end point. We did not perform time to end point analyses due to the nature of the end point; variable time for re‐ablation might have occurred from recurring AF symptoms such as variable time to referral and waiting times for re‐ablation. Therefore, the time‐to‐event would not be dependent only on symptom recurrence and thus would not be a reliable variable for scientifically meaningful time‐to‐event analyses.

Results

Study population included 518 patients (27% women, mean age 58 ± 10 years and a mean BMI 27 ± 3.6) with primary cryo‐PVI for AF. Twenty percent (n = 102) underwent re‐ablation due to recurring symptoms. Regarding the CHA2DS2‐VASc score, 344 patients (66%) were scored 0–1 points, 145 patients (28%) scored 2–3 points, and 28 patients (5%) scored more than 3 points. One patient's score was missing. The mean EF was 62% ± 8.9% measured in 443 cases. A significant gender difference was observed in the re‐ablation group, with women (26.1%) undergoing re‐ablation more often than men (17.3%) (OR 1.686, 95% CI: 1.064–2.672, p = 0.026). Most ablations were performed with the new generation cryoballoon (63.5%). There was a significantly lower re‐ablation rate with the old generation cryoballoon (2.65%) compared to the new (29.5%) (OR 0.665, 95% CI: 0.026–0.163, p < 0.001). There was no difference between operators and re‐ablation rate (p = 0.182).

The overall incidence of complications was low. The total complication rate was 3.0% including 14 phrenic nerve palsies (2.7%), which all recovered within 1 year. In addition, one (0.2%) clinically significant tamponade, which needed pericardial draining and one (0.2%) puncture site bleeding needing vascular surgery, were detected. There were no periprocedural stroke, atriaesophageal fistulae or procedure related deaths in this study population. Carina Measurements

In all patients the right carina was wider than the left (8.04 ± 4.49 mm vs. 5.25 ± 3.03 mm, p < 0.059). The left carina width was associated with the incidence of re‐ablation (left carina: re‐ablation group 6.4 ± 2.7 mm vs. no re‐ablation group 4.9 ± 3.0 mm; OR 1.168 95% CI: 1.072–1.273, p < 0.001) Right carina width was not associated with re‐ablation (right carina: re‐ablation group 8.3 ± 3.0 mm vs. no re‐ablation group 7.9 ± 3.6 mm, p = 0.421). Left carina width remained significantly associated in multivariable analyses after adjusting with other significantly associated variables such as LA area, gender and cryoballoon generation (adjusted OR 1.265, 95% CI: 1.144–1.400, p < 0.001). In the interobserver and intra‐observer analyses the measurements were very reproducible. In inter‐observer measurements for left carina, right carina and LA measurement the correlations were very well aligned (r > 0.98, p < 0.001 for all) and results for intra‐observer reproducibility were similar (r > 0.99, p < 0.001 for all).

Reconnected Veins

Left superior PV (LSPV) was reconnected in 48 cases (9.3%) and left inferior PV (LIPV) in 35 cases (6.8%). Among the RPVs, right superior PV (RSPV) was reconnected in 58 cases (11.2%) and right inferior PV (RIPV) in 61 (11.8%) of all redo cases. In the re‐ablation group, a significant association between right carina width and re‐connection of the RPV's were detected. However, this association was not seen between the left carina and the LPV's (RPV 6.3 ± 2.4 mm vs. 8.6 ± 3.0 mm, p = 0.015; LPV: 6.8 ± 3.2 mm vs. 6.2 ± 2.4 mm, p = 0.360, respectively). Left Atrial Size

The LA area was significantly greater in the re‐ablation group when compared to control group (28.3 ± 6.0 cm2 vs. 25.9 ± 5.9 cm2; p < 0.001, respectively. And there was a significant correlation between the size of the LA and the width of the right carina (r = 0.251; p < 0.001). However, the size of LA did not correlate with the left carina width (r = 0.075; p = 0.111). The parameters of LA are presented in Table 4.

Left Pulmonary Vein Common Ostium

In our population, 40 patients (8%) presented with LCO. One fourth (n = 10) of the patients with LCO underwent re‐ablation indicating that the re‐ablation percentage was slightly higher in LCO patients when compared to other AF patients. The mean size of the LCO was 20 ± 4.4 mm. There was no significant difference in LCO size between the re‐ablation group and the control group. LCO was also not significantly associated with re‐ablation as a categorial variable in regression analyses.

Discussion

Even though AF ablation techniques have remarkably developed in recent years, the rate of re‐ablations remains still at approximately 20%. The need for new risk markers for unfavorable AF ablation results are needed. LA morphology might play a significant role in the ablation results, but little is known about the effect of carina width on the need for re‐ablation.

Main Findings

Even though AF ablation results are widely explored, there are several unknown factors related to unsuccessful ablation results. Our data shows a significant association between the right carina width and reconnection of the RPV's but there was no association with higher incidence of re‐ablations in this group. Hence, in these patients, there usually is a need for additional carina isolation in re‐ablation procedure. Also, there was a significant correlation between the right carina width and the LA area. LA area also associates significantly with re‐ablation rate. Even though the left carina width is significantly associated with the need for re‐ablation in our study, there was no significant difference in reconnection of the left side PV's. The point by point RF technique and the CLOSE protocol always include the carina area, even without an additional carina line. Still sometimes this technique requires an additional carina line in order to isolate the PV's [19, 20, 21].

Ding‐In Shin et al. showed in small retrospective study a correlation between the width of the carina and the need for additional carina line to achieve isolation of the PV's. During PVI procedure fast anatomical mapping allows the measurement of the carina width correctly from the upper vein to the lower vein at the narrowest point. It has been suggested that need for additional carina ablation is related to a larger carina area in order to achieve successful isolation of the PV's [20]. However, ablation with the cryoballoon does not always include the carina area. The results of the Fire and Ice Trial though showed no significant difference in re‐ablation rate between RF‐ and cryoballoon ablation [9].

Role of Carina Conduction After Cryoballoon‐PVI

It has been demonstrated that there is an impact of supraventricular extrasystoles arising from the carina area on the onset of AF [14]. PVI with cryoballoon usually does not cover the carina area, while point by point ablations do. However, the Fire and Ice trial demonstrated that there is no significant difference between cryoballoon and RF ablation on the re‐ablation rates in larger population [3]. Also, a small study with 114 patients from Mulder et al. showed, that reconnection of the PV's was associated with increased AF recurrence. On the other hand, carina reconnection did not predict AF recurrence in that study [22].

A study from Terasawa et al. showed a correlation of the left side carina width and reconnection of the left PV's (p = 0.024 for Left superior pulmonary vein; p = 0.020 for left inferior pulmonary vein) [23]. However, we could not prove a significant correlation between left carina width and reconnection of the left PV's in our study population perhaps due to sample size. In multivariable analyses left carina width remained significant associated variable related to re‐ablation even after adjusting with confounding factors. This might suggest a significant independent role of left carina in the higher incidence of re‐ablations. A recently published study from Strazdas et al. showed no effect of additional non‐ostial cryoballoon ablation to cover the carina area in 1‐year clinical outcome [24]. As cryoballoon features have improved a better outcome in primary cryoballoon ablation with the new generation cryoballoon could be suspected.

However, in our study population, we observed a higher number of new generation cryoballoon cases within the re‐ablation group. This finding may be attributed to nearly twice as many procedures performed with the new generation cryoballoon compared to the old generation cryoballoon, as well as improved symptom monitoring and increased referrals for re‐ablations from other centers in recent years. As prior studies showed, there is a correlation between the LA size and recurrence of AF [25]. Also, in our study the LA size correlated with the re‐ablation rate. We also found a correlation between the right carina width and the LA size, but we could not show a significant association of the right carina width to the re‐ablation numbers. Therefore, we suspect that LA enlargement does not directly lead to stretching of carina. The carina seems to be an independent parameter leading to AF recurrence after initial cryoballoon‐PVI when the carina stays unisolated. Further studies are needed to determine whether the carina area should be isolated in re‐ablations after cryoballoon ablation, when there are still signals left at the edge of the carina or if the placement of the balloon should be adjusted to ensure the smallest possible area of uncertain isolation in the carina in the primary ablation.

Limitations

This study was a single‐center retrospective analysis with a relatively small group of patients from our local ablation registry. Since it is based on real‐life data, only one clinical assessment was conducted 3 months after the initial ablation procedure. Clinical follow‐up was performed by phone to evaluate symptoms caused by AF, and there were no routine ambulatory recordings of arrhythmias, such as Holter or Loop Recorder monitoring, after cryoballoon ablation during follow‐up. However, all symptomatic patients underwent ECG or Holter‐recording. The decision of re‐ablation was based on documented AF. Because referral centers in our area follow different protocols, indications for re‐ablation were not standardized and varied according to the referring hospital's practices. Consequently, some patients who might have benefited from re‐ablation may not have been referred to our center, and time delays between symptom onset after initial cryoballoon ablation and subsequent re‐ablation could differ due to varying strategies among referral centers. Still, the criteria for re‐ablation were consistent across all subjects. This may explain the difference seen in the re‐ablation group between the two generations of cryoballoons. Over time, referrals for re‐ablation may have increased, which could explain the higher number of new generation cryoballoon patients in our re‐ablation group.

We chose not to perform time‐to‐event analyses due to the nature of the endpoint. Re‐do ablation time is subject to various non‐associated factors such as variable waiting times and holidays.

CT imaging varied in hardware, protocols, and slice thickness by imaging site.

Only a single carina width measurement was used instead of an average of several measurements, but we confirmed the intra observer variability to be low, as had been shown also in other studies. Thus, these factors likely did not significantly impact the results.

Conclusions

The left carina width and LA size were associated with the success rate of primary cryoballoon‐PVI. Even though, the right carina width did not associate with re‐ablation rate, there was a clear correlation between the right carina width and the reconnection of the RPV's leading to re‐ablation. Left carina width seems to be an independent feature associated with re‐ablation after cryo‐PVI. Thus, anatomical features of LA could have an impact of the results of AF ablation, and probably the choice of ablation technique.
