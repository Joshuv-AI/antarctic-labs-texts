Learning Health Systems, Equity, and Bounded Justice: A Critical Scoping Review
Authors: Vivetha Thambinathan, Katie Lynch, Maricruz Moya, Maria G Flores, Rama Mwenesi Musalia, Joshua Rubin, Jenna Lepczyk, Francesca A Williamson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThe science of learning health systems (LHSs) has expanded to include equity aims. However, efforts toward this end are nascent. The purpose of this study was to critically examine how equity has been discussed in LHS literature.MethodsWe conducted a critical scoping review informed by the bounded justice concept. Using Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, Pubmed, Embase, Scopus, and CINAHL Complete (Ebsco) databases were searched to identify relevant literature from inception to June 2024. We used qualitative descriptive analysis for detailed extraction and to identify patterns across equity conceptions in the literature.ResultsThe search strategy generated 420 articles. Forty articles met the selection criteria. Our analysis generated four interrelated themes: (1) equity as central, (2) equity as reducing risk, (3) equity as process, and (4) equity defined as population of interest. Most articles fell into the equity as central theme. Notably, 35 (87.5%) articles were published between 2020 and 2024. We also identified five emerging equity‐centered LHS approaches.ConclusionsLHS equity science is emerging, though it still privileges narrow, Westernized approaches. It is imperative that future work more deeply engages health justice traditions to inform LHS theory, research, and practice.Keywords: bounded justice, community engagement, equity science, health equity, learning health systems

Introduction

In recent years, health professionals, policymakers, patients, and other invested partners have advanced the concept of a learning health system (LHS) to transform patient care and population health [1, 2]. The National Academy of Medicine currently defines an LHS as an organized effort where [3],

science, informatics, incentives, and culture are aligned for continuous improvement, innovation, and equity—with best practices and discovery seamlessly embedded in the delivery process, individuals and families actively participate in all elements, and new knowledge generated as an integral by‐product of the delivery experience.

More recently, greater attention has been given to equity in LHSs [4, 5, 6]. In 2022, the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality introduced the Health and Healthcare Equity and Justice competency domain for learning health scientists [7]. LHS literature in the last decade has increasingly featured tools for embedding equity into LHS projects and training [4, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. Compared to early visions of LHSs that prioritized health system functioning and efficiency [13, 14], the field is turning toward striving for health equity locally, nationally, and globally.

Advancing equity through LHSs involves key opportunities. First, health equity visions and definitions vary significantly across disciplines, institutional contexts, scientific associations, and communities (see Table 1). Varied terminology can undermine efforts to determine equity priorities and goals [15]. We use Braveman et al.'s [15] definition of health equity,

Health equity means that everyone has a fair and just opportunity to be as healthy as possible. Achieving this requires removing obstacles to health–such as poverty and discrimination and their consequences, which include powerlessness and lack of access to good jobs with fair pay; quality education, housing, and healthcare; and safe environments. For the purposes of measurement, health equity means reducing and ultimately eliminating disparities in health and in the determinants of health that adversely affect excluded or marginalized groups. (p. 2)

Health equity is a rights‐based, ethical principle and moral imperative to mitigate threats to human dignity, full participation in society, and opportunities to achieve the best possible health. This definition is grounded in a commitment to social justice and an appreciation of root causes of health inequity (e.g., racism, ableism) that make where people work and live (e.g., environment) and their social locations (e.g., social economic status), cultures, language, and beliefs salient in the unequal distribution of resources and opportunities to be as healthy as possible [16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. Though health equity is built upon social justice values and deeply intertwined with the moral and ethical frameworks informed by health justice, in this paper, we use health equity as a normative term and distinguish it from health justice in its operational utility within LHSs. Here, we also bring forward Creary's concept of “bounded justice” to interrogate the impossibilities of “attend[ing] to fairness, entitlement, and equity when the basic social and physical infrastructures underlying them have been eroded by racism and other historically entrenched ‐isms” [21, p. 242]. We position bounded justice as a critical analytical tool that makes visible the limitations of both health equity and health justice when conceptualized and operationalized in the context of LHS programs, policies, and interventions. Further, we demonstrate that clarity is needed about how LHSs can contribute to disrupting the root causes of health inequities.

A second opportunity concerns the people involved in LHS projects. Importantly, the LHS concept hinges upon engaging multiple invested parties [2, 22]. However, across health research areas, people and communities most impacted by health inequities and those with lived and professional expertise in equity have been historically and intentionally excluded from meaningful participation and leadership in health and healthcare change [23, 24, 25]. Bounded justice reveals that even with inclusive engagement, programs and policies often fail to recognize the fundamental exclusion (e.g., from society, history, and knowledge) that communities of interest experience. Like other areas of health research, LHS practitioners will need to grapple with moving beyond tokenistic and extractivist forms of engagement [26].

More centrally, we draw upon Creary's concept of bounded justice to consider a third opportunity [21]. Creary argued that well‐intended health equity initiatives often fail to account for the real‐world constraints on their intended benefits. Critically, the bounded justice concept unsettles Westernized epistemic and cultural norms of high‐resource health organizations, re‐orienting to and valuing expansive forms of knowledge production and organizational change at multiple scales. Creary called for a critically reflexive approach to understanding how sociohistorical, structural, ecological, interpersonal, and embodied dynamics work together to create existing health patterns. One implication of bounded justice is the need to reckon with the limits of improving health systems without addressing underlying sociohistorical conditions.

As the idea of the LHS matures, so too must the field's approach to equity [27, 28]. Indeed, the aforementioned opportunities warrant rigorous engagement with established health equity traditions and movements to realize the transformative potential of LHSs [23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28]. To pursue this goal, we first need to understand the current state of LHS equity work. Using bounded justice as a sensitizing construct [29], the purpose of this critical scoping review was to examine how LHS literature explicitly and implicitly constructs notions of equity and related concepts. Our research question was: How is equity conceptualized in LHS literature?

Methods

We assembled a team of faculty, staff, and students with varied expertise and interest in embedding equity into LHS projects. Each team member comes from historically and intentionally excluded communities, encompassing diverse backgrounds in race, ethnicity, gender, disability, language, religion, nationality, and class; one co‐author is an associate editor for Learning Health Systems. We held regular meetings to discuss equity in the field of LHS, determine research questions, and co‐create our approach to this review (see Figure 1). In addition to PRISMA guidance and the standard methodology for scoping reviews [30, 31], we selected a critical scoping review method, which involves applying a critical theory or concept to interpret, synthesize, and forward recommendations [32, 33]. This critical scoping review functions as a launch pad for imagining innovative ways of thinking about equity conceptualizations in LHS, taking stock of the field, and inviting reflections about how LHS have contributed to equity work. Throughout the process, we engaged in reflexive dialogue to consider how our varied positionalities influenced our interpretations of the literature, visions of equity [34], as well as our engagement with the bounded justice concept.

Search Strategy

The search strategy was developed in partnership with a librarian who specializes in health and health equity research (JL). We delimited the search period to 2007–2024 to align with the initial conception of learning health(care) systems [13, 14]. The detailed search strategy based on PRISMA guidelines is provided in Appendix A.

Study Selection

Title and abstract screening was conducted by three researchers (VT, KL, MM). Grounded in Braveman's health equity definition, the research team co‐developed screening criteria. The criteria were refined and finalized through a 20‐article pilot screening performed by VT, KL, MM (see Table 2). Using the NAM definition of a LHS, the research team assessed articles' use of a LHS through identifiable primary LHS components of interest, including engagement (patient or community, but distinct from learning communities), sociotechnical infrastructure, learning communities, learning cycle, performance to data (P2D), data to knowledge (D2K), implementation (K2P), and general LHS (Table 2). The term “general LHS” represented articles utilizing the terms “learning health” or “learning healthcare” to describe themselves, as well as those that are a known LHS organization/network to the research team. The three researchers then screened all articles independently and in case of disagreements, discussed the abstracts until a collective decision was reached. The researchers used the Covidence platform and their screening notes to document which of the equity criteria were addressed and this was used to support dialogue regarding disagreements. When uncertainty remained, the article was included for full‐text screening to be examined thoroughly. Articles without abstracts were also initially included and then assessed during full‐text screening done by two researchers (VT, FW). Articles were excluded if (a) they were abstract‐only, (b) LHS concepts were only mentioned in passing, or (c) did not adequately address 3 out of 5 equity criteria.

Data Extraction and Analysis

A data charting form was created to extract insights relevant to our research question. This form was piloted and reviewed by four researchers (VT, KL, MM, FW) to ensure attention to key data points. The extracted data included bibliographic information, health area/specialty, article type, focus of article, primary LHS component(s), equity concepts from criteria, and additional notes.

Following extraction, a qualitative analysis was conducted by collating the data according to various descriptive categories [35]. Using bounded justice as a sensitizing construct, two researchers (VT, FW) conducted a collaborative thematic analysis, which consisted of independently writing memos to capture thoughts that arise from engaging in immersive reading of full‐text papers and for developing thematic categories for organizing the literature [36]. Research meetings occurred throughout the data extraction process, with new themes and categories of interest emerging through an iterative process of thematic analysis and dialogue. In this process, key ideas related to the themes were brainstormed to help make sense of how the themes related to and differed from each other, while drawing connections to identify insightful patterns among them [37]. Once themes were finalized and defined through reflexive discussion with the larger research team (Table 4), data were then reorganized and assigned according to each theme, and points related to these themes clarified with reference to the full‐text articles.

Equity as Central

We identified 18 articles conceptualizing equity as central. This theme illustrated papers acknowledged and engaged the structural, systemic, and root causes of health inequities within and beyond LHSs, recognizing how societal infrastructures are “eroded by racism and other historically entrenched isms” [21, p. 242]. Examples included naming structural racism [6, 9, 42, 49, 54, 60]; systemic disparities in access and resource allocation, funding, and power [4, 8, 41, 63, 67]; the COVID‐19 pandemic's impact on historically oppressed communities [53, 63, 70]; colonialism and its perpetuation through health(care) systems [56, 66]. Two articles mentioned historical injustices [48, 64], including racist medical studies (e.g., Study of Untreated Syphilis in Tuskegee). Despite the varying characterizations, the articles discussed inequitable systems, hierarchies, social structures, power relations and institutional practices that sustain them. In terms of engagement, the articles within this theme undertook a holistic application of equity, in contrast to a specific “point‐in‐time” intervention. These articles shared important features for advancing equity in LHSs, including accountability, systems thinking, sustainability, inclusion of multiple perspectives, and collaboration with patients/community partners for learning. However, these elements were prioritized in different ways. In our examination of this theme, we identified five emerging equity‐centered LHS approaches (Figure 4).

Overview of emerging equity‐centered approaches to LHS projects.

The first equity‐centered approach focuses on producing contextualized data and learning to enhance equity‐oriented decision‐making [6, 56, 62, 70]. As Lee‐Foon and Reid argued about LHSs, “data are often viewed in a vacuum… often analyzed in an objective and positivist fashion, disconnected from the socio‐structural contexts from which they emerge” [62, p. 3]. Elements of equitable institutional data frameworks included various practices, such as collecting disaggregated data, qualitative research with patients, and engaging providers and community partners in analyses [6]. For example, Milligan et al. [56] underscored the importance of LHSs learning from and respectfully applying knowledges held within Indigenous communities. The second strategy was strengthening equity capacity‐building to foster an organizational culture of equity [8, 54, 70]. This strategy emphasized the training of equity‐oriented LHS scientists and leaders, including learning from scholars of color and community‐engagement practitioners.

Some articles employed the third equity‐centered strategy of fostering community trust, which supports continuous learning systems by anchoring the needs and perspectives of patients, caregivers, and communities [48, 64]. For instance, Natafgi et al. [48] found that the Learning Health Care Community model in Baltimore could be a viable way to improve community health care experiences, particularly when supported by integrated data systems, collaborative learning, and tailored community‐based approaches. Cressman further argued that a learning community with governance ensures social accountability, where those with lived experiences “would not just be in the room but will have their perspectives listened to and actioned upon” [67, p. 82]. The fourth approach involves using holistic equity frameworks for LHSs [4, 9, 60, 66]. These were typically practice‐based guidelines and case studies. Examples include an accountability checklist for implementing and evaluating equitable LHSs with Indigenous peoples [66] and the PETAL framework for integrating health equity into LHSs [4]. The final equity‐centered strategy was a health issue‐focused approach. Work in this area sought to address inequities in specific health issues, such as asthma [41], precision medicine [42], cancer care [49], and cardiovascular health [53]. This approach also emphasized addressing socio‐economic, environmental, and societal factors implicated in the inequities in specific health issues. These articles positioned the LHS as a way to address specific health inequities across sectors.

Regardless of which approach was used, it is critical to note that most articles in this theme were conceptual or commentary papers (14/18). Even within equity‐centered approaches, few articles discussed power‐sharing, decision‐making, or governance models, and even fewer have modeled this in practice. This could potentially be a sign of the field's immaturity, but also the field's deeper structural inequities tied to funding, political buy‐in, gatekeeping of health equity researchers of color, and so forth [23, 24, 25]. Notably, the articles we reviewed lacked explicit attention to disability and language diversity. Two papers focused on Indigenous communities call attention to rethinking what counts as data within LHSs and how literature often “endorsed the extraction of knowledge from the community,” instead of practicing co‐creation [66, p. 8]. This extractive approach has been acknowledged in calls for LHSs to prioritize capacity building in participatory research to advance health equity for, with, and by communities [54].

Equity as Reducing Risk

This theme reflected nine articles that conceptualized equity as the reduction of risk for specific populations. Many articles within this theme narrowed in on social determinants of health (SDOH) [38, 58, 69], labeling social needs [51, 59] or social risk factors [43] to reduce health disparities. Though three articles did not use SDOH language, they recognized risk or vulnerability through known disparities to categorize populations of interest [45, 61, 73]. These articles focused on justice‐involved youth, African American, Hispanic/Latino, Asian American, and LGBTQ+ populations.

Many articles located their conceptualizations of equity as reducing risk within health informatic tools, where LHSs have available data on patients' SDOH [43] to screen for “risk factors” and connect patients to resources [38, 45, 51, 69]. For instance, Theis et al. [51] conducted a study to inform the implementation of a module to collect and integrate patient‐reported social needs data into the EHR. However, given the limited availability of community resources, the authors questioned whether the intervention could provide the necessary social support to patients beyond offering more personalized care. Some articles showcased engagement to reduce or mitigate population‐specific risks. For example, using a learning community strategy, Myers et al. [73] formed patient and stakeholder advisory committees to develop intervention strategies and increase colorectal cancer screening in African American, Hispanic/Latino, and Asian American populations. Overall, articles in this theme primarily focused on reducing populations‐specific exposures to health risks and did not explicitly address underlying mechanisms that create inequities. Here, bounded justice reveals the epistemic and real‐world constraints of programs and interventions that do not have or acknowledge historical reference points.

Equity as Process

Eight articles framed equity in terms of processes. Most articles within this theme modeled equity‐oriented planning and engagement, with potential to be scaled up to strengthen whole‐system functioning in LHSs. One representation integrated “4R” care sequence templates (right information and right treatment to the right patient at the right time) to scale patient navigation for medically underserved women, thus systematically improving processes of cancer care delivery [44]. In another article by Piotrowski et al. [65], participatory action research was used as an inclusive, localized approach to learning, planning, and implementing an equitable mass drug administration program to prevent neglected tropical diseases in Nigeria. This intervention was expanded across two Nigerian states through the implementation of multilevel health system working groups, involving co‐researchers, capacity‐building workshops, and participatory tools. The authors characterized “challenging existing top‐down approaches to planning and facilitating local voices to be central in decision making” [65, p. i71] as a pragmatic strategy to enhance and understand pathways to improved healthcare access and delivery. Similarly, Ramasamy et al. [57] proposed equipping community coalitions to become local learning health communities and establish an analogous decision‐making system.

An important insight conveyed within this theme was the need for LHSs to “develop institutional and human capacities at different levels within the health system, and create space for reflective practice and learning within routine processes” [71, p. 87]. Centered on equity as a capacity‐building process, Vargas et al. [40] highlighted lessons learned from the development of research infrastructure as a process for inclusion of safety‐net populations in research, while a LHS, Type 1 Diabetes Exchange Quality Improvement Collaborative, demonstrated how their established platform as a process increased capacity for data networks and sharing best practices to promote equitable outcomes [52]. Additional studies in this theme explored culturally relevant care models [55] and inequities in patient partner recruitment processes for healthcare improvement [72]. Articles within this theme prioritized understanding the needs and experiences of priority equity‐deserving populations through participatory processes and engagement in systems. Nevertheless, as the bounded justice concept illuminates, there is a missing connection to the root causes of these inequitable structures and systems themselves. Without addressing the source of the systems' cumulative effects of disadvantage and resource distribution, ultimately, power does not become sustainably shifted toward communities of interest.

Equity Defined as Population of Interest

As the least common theme, “equity defined as population of interest” comprised five articles. These articles primarily focused on identifying variations across racial/defined groups, and at times, understanding perspectives of priority health equity populations. Framing the health problem of interest was typically centered on understanding the needs and experiences of specific equity‐deserving populations and demonstrating differences that emerge, rather than examining ways to break down barriers and improve overall healthcare access and quality. Illustrating this, in the paper by Kahn et al. [68], the study goals were to compare estimates of the gender diverse youth populations from a pediatric multi institutional health system database and from self‐reported data in a national youth survey and identify demographic differences in these estimates by sex assigned at birth and race and ethnicity. As such, this theme can be viewed as a starting point to establish an equity “problem.” The authors concluded with next steps: “more work is therefore needed to understand how to reduce barriers and improve access to health care for all youth of color, including those who are gender diverse” [68, p. 6].

Correspondingly, two empirical studies—one targeting high‐risk populations and the other, diverse populations in safety‐net health systems—identified evidence gaps that may worsen healthcare disparities and challenge systems‐level efforts [39, 50]. Both studies found that many hospitals focused efforts toward a one‐size‐fits‐all solution to streamline care, which may in fact exacerbate health disparities for historically and presently oppressed populations. Burkitt et al. [46] illustrated this issue in their quality improvement study, which aimed to reduce racial disparities in blood pressure control among Black veterans with severe hypertension. Ethical concerns were raised by facility‐level leaders and providers about targeting Black veterans rather than all veterans, yet the authors justified their intervention's rationale: “the rate of severe hypertension among White veterans remained the same… a targeted emphasis on a population with greater need did not decrease the standard of care received by the majority population” [46, p. 5]. In an article's discussion of patients', caregivers', and healthcare providers' perceptions of home recovery to inform care in the LHS, Dowzicky et al. [47, p. 723] contrasted their findings to another study with language: “AA race being associated with lower quality of life and patient‐reported outcomes scores in various postsurgical populations”. Notably, the authors did not use the word “racism” to contextualize this finding. This example illustrates how, within this conception of equity, researchers may reinforce problematic, historical causal associations to explain differences in disease prevalence and outcomes, such as treating race or identity merely as a variable of interest—rather than as a proxy for structural racism or oppression.

Discussion

The purpose of this critical scoping review was to characterize notions of equity espoused in LHS literature. Informed by the notion of bounded justice, we found both holistic and fragmented conceptions of equity in reports of health equity‐related LHS projects. A holistic approach tended to view equity through a historical context and as a comprehensive, systemic issue, with inclusive engagement. On the other hand, a fragmented approach focused on tackling symptoms rather than causes and focused on one “piece” of the equity problem without substantive systems change. Notably, 35 (87.5%) were published since 2020, the year multiple sectors, including health and health care, sought to address racial and health injustices in response to movements for Black life, racial justice, and COVID‐19 advocacy [25]. This post‐2020 “equity turn” in LHS literature represents a rapid response to pervasive historical equity conditions in society and structures, of which there may have been limited prior engagement with in‐depth health equity literature and/or absence of existing collaborations with health equity experts and communities of interests for guidance. This time period also signifies a tension many health systems face between accelerating the “achievement” of health equity and the necessity of slow, intentional trust‐ and capacity‐building to do this work well with the communities they serve [74]. This is especially salient in health systems where equity has since become a “new” priority. As one paper highlighted, “This addition [of equity] attempts to acknowledge and begin addressing decades of systemic biases that have negatively impacted the treatment of marginalized groups. In many systems, equity has also become a catch‐all term” [63, p. 16]. Indeed, limited engagement with equity traditions normalizes superficial, ahistorical, and underdeveloped visions that undermine efforts to mobilize change.

Nevertheless, Creary's concept of bounded justice prompts us to understand and disrupt the sociohistorical conditions that have intentionally undermined striving toward equity. To maximize their potential, LHSs must contribute to cross‐sectoral efforts to disrupt deep‐rooted structural racism. Without this commitment, LHSs risk reproducing infrastructures that enable the inequitable cyclical loop to maintain itself, resulting in the perpetuation of exclusive opportunities for those with access and keeping the door closed on the communities impacted by the inequities [74, 75, 76, 77]. Such a loop aligns with the broader Health Disparities Research Industrial Complex [24], which rewards the over‐investigation of and documentation of disparities and health equity tourism [25] while under‐investing in the translation of high‐impact, culturally congruent evidence into policy, practice, and systems change. Further, the exploratory nature of some LHS projects without clear attempts to “close the loop” mimic colonialist agendas, where the focus is on discovering what is wrong (i.e., deficit‐based) with populations, rather than what works or learning from care models that create the conditions for thriving (i.e., strength‐based) [77, 78, 79, 80, 81]. Even if perhaps well‐intentioned, the framing of equity as solely reducing risk (such as screening for risk and linking to services) often sidesteps the structural drivers of inequity and remains bounded by institutional logics and infrastructures that limit their transformative potential to advance equity. A broader epistemic problem lies within failing to name structural racism and discrimination as causal factors, misleading readers into attributing poor outcomes to race itself, thereby reifying biological differences. Thus, we argue LHS projects on disparities must align with its commitment to iterative, continuous, and accountable cycles of learning to sustain efforts to not only identify disparities, but also to research and co‐create life‐affirming care systems.

This study has implications for deepening learning from well‐established equity traditions. Bounded justice invites greater recognition of the constraints on LHS health equity interventions in light of the realities of sociohistorical conditions. This is not to say that the systems cannot become more equitable. Rather, resonant with the goal of LHSs, Creary advocates for intervening in multiple scales–informed by diverse knowledge and wisdom—to cocreate better systems [21, 82]. Informed by bounded justice, emerging equity‐centered LHS approaches (Figure 4), and equity literature [23, 24, 25, 78, 82], we offer actionable directions, including potential LHS design opportunities, for centering equity in health problems of interest, structure, people, programs and technologies, power‐sharing, and policy in LHS projects (Table 5). We write these mechanisms below as potential equity‐centered pathways for action and intervention in LHSs, rather than a prescriptive list, to advance health equity and justice. We also hope that in engaging with these equity‐centered LHS pathways, it provides a launch pad inviting questions toward imagining innovative solutions.

Although this study offers new insights into the state of equity in LHS theory and practice, it does have limitations. We used Braveman's definition of health equity to set the screening criteria. However, our criteria may have excluded relevant articles. We also recognized that the policing of equity language impacts what and who is researched, and how it is characterized in the literature [83]. Our research may have also been strengthened by involving patients and community members outside of academia, researchers in other national contexts, and practitioners who work in resource‐limited health settings. Additionally, assessing fidelity to the LHS concept was beyond the scope of this review. However, as the models for LHS expand [84], future research should evaluate diversity in how the LHS concept and practice are operationalized in the growing body of equity‐focused literature.

Conclusion

As we navigate a challenging political landscape that increasingly questions health equity, it is crucial to maintain the momentum of the equity science that the field of LHS has only just begun to cultivate. Though equity‐centered approaches are emerging, current imaginings of LHSs privilege narrow, Westernized conceptions of data, knowledge, practice, and what constitutes improvement (for who and toward what end?). The concept of bounded justice enables us to recognize how ‐isms reify these epistemic and cultural norms within LHS infrastructures and projects. Importantly, our findings reveal there is not one “right” way to embed equity into the science of LHS. Rather, more work is needed to explore the possibilities and constraints of leveraging the ideal of an LHS to design better systems toward health equity.
