METTL3 Methylation Induces Decay of Endogenous Retroelement Transcripts to Promote Tumor Immune Evasion
Authors: Xiaowei She, Jingqin Lan, Haokun Zhang, Gen Lu, Jialu Xu, Jiakun Zhang, Wenli Zhan, Shengjie Feng, Zejun Rao, Chengxin Yu, Baolin Han, Rui Zhang, Da Song, Yaqi Chen, Guihua Wang, Junbo Hu, Xuelai Luo, Haijie Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Suppression of endogenous retroelements and type I interferon signaling mediated by E2F4/SETD1A-induced METTL3 K513 methylation can be targeted to restore antitumor immunity and enhance responsiveness to immune checkpoint blockade in colorectal cancer.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer is among the most prevalent malignancies worldwide and remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality, particularly in its advanced stages (1). Currently, radical surgery combined with 5-fluorouracil (5-FU)–based adjuvant chemotherapy is a cornerstone strategy for the treatment of patients with colorectal cancer. However, after curative treatment, approximately 30% of stage I to III patients and up to 65% of stage IV patients remain at risk of recurrence, often with poor survival outcomes (2). This highlights the urgent need to understand the intrinsic mechanisms driving colorectal cancer progression and recurrence.

Mounting evidence has implicated that tumor progression and relapse are not isolated events but rather result from a dynamic interaction between tumor cells and their surrounding immune microenvironment (3). Notably, the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) has emerged as a central driver of therapeutic resistance and relapse (4). Within this context, epigenetic regulation has gained recognition as a crucial mechanism linking oncogenic signaling to immune evasion. Epigenetic alterations—including DNA methylation, histone modification, and RNA methylation—can profoundly reshape the TME by modulating the expression of cytokines, chemokines, immune checkpoints, and antigen presentation machinery (5–7). For example, histone methyltransferases such as EZH2 and SETDB1 repress IFN-stimulated genes and antigen presentation pathways, thereby limiting cytotoxic T-cell infiltration and promoting immune escape (8–10). Similarly, DNA methyltransferase (DNMT)–mediated silencing of endogenous retroelements (ERE) restricts innate immune activation and type I IFN signaling (11, 12). These findings underscore that epigenetic remodeling not only drives intrinsic tumor plasticity but also dictates the immune composition of the TME. Despite these insights, how non-histone lysine methylation contributes to tumor–immune cross-talk, particularly in colorectal cancer, remains poorly defined.

Epigenetic dysregulation within tumor cells plays a crucial role in various pathologic processes, often driven by aberrant posttranslational modifications (PTM; ref. 13). Among these, non-histone lysine methylation is a prevalent PTM that regulates the function of key tumor-related proteins and critical signaling pathways, thereby orchestrating various oncogenic processes (14). For instance, lysine methylation of proteins such as p53, AKT, and YAP influences cancer initiation (15–17), whereas the methylation of CD147 and MCT1 regulates lactate transport in TMEs, affecting tumor glucose metabolism (18, 19). Recent studies have also linked aberrant lysine methyltransferase activity—such as that of SETDB1, G9a/GLP, and SMYD3—to the suppression of IFN signaling and antigen presentation, thereby contributing to tumor immune escape (20–22). However, the contribution of lysine methylation to colorectal cancer progression and recurrence—especially in shaping the immune microenvironment—remains largely unknown.

In this study, we identify the dimethylation of methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3) at lysine 513 (K513) in recurrent colorectal cancer tissues using mass spectrometry. Our findings demonstrate that this modification is involved in colorectal cancer progression and recurrence. Furthermore, we explore the underlying mechanisms by which METTL3 K513 dimethylation influences colorectal cancer progression and recurrence and propose potential intervention strategies to improve colorectal cancer prognosis.

Materials and Methods

Sex is used as a biological variable. Our study examined male and female patients and animals, and similar findings are reported for both sexes.

Syngeneic mice tumor models

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Tongji Hospital (TJH-202303006). BALB/c mice (RRID:MGI:2161072) were obtained from GemPharmatech. The specified CT26 cells (2 × 105) were subcutaneously injected into 6-week-old BALB/c mice. Tumor sizes were measured at specified intervals, and volume was calculated using the following formula: 0.5 × length × width2. After the mice were sacrificed, the subcutaneous tumors were weighed, and the tissues were further investigated.

For the in vivo treatment model, anti–PD-1 (Bio X Cell, BE0146, RRID:AB_10949053) or control IgG antibodies (Bio X Cell, CP105, RRID:AB_2927524) were administered at a dose of 100 μg per mouse at specified times after CT26 cell inoculation. The E2F4 inhibitor was administered via daily i.p. injections at 20 mg/kg body weight for 14 consecutive days. For survival analysis, the endpoint was defined as the time when a progressively growing tumor reached 2,000 mm3. In most cases, animals with tumor volumes exceeding 2,000 mm3 were euthanized and removed from the study.

Cell lines and cell culture

The human embryonic kidney cell line HEK293T (RRID:CVCL_0063), human colorectal cancer cell lines (LoVo, RRID:CVCL_0399 and HT29, RRID:CVCL_A8EZ), and murine colon carcinoma cell line CT26 (RRID:CVCL_7254) were procured from the ATCC. Upon receipt, the cells were expanded, and early-passage frozen stocks were established. Cells within 10 passages after thawing were used for all experiments.

HEK293T, LoVo, HT29, and CT26 were respectively cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium, Leibovitz’s L-15 medium, McCoy’s 5a medium, and RPMI 1640 medium with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin, and 1% streptomycin. Cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling to confirm their identity. Cells were routinely tested for Mycoplasma every 3 months using a Mycoplasma qPCR Detection Kit (Beyotime, C0303S) and were confirmed to be Mycoplasma-free.

Patient specimens

This study analyzed paired primary and recurrent tumor specimens from nine colorectal cancer cases. Detailed patient characteristics are provided in Supplementary Table S1. The colorectal cancer tissue samples were obtained from Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Wuhan, China). Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) colorectal cancer tissue microarrays (TMA) were purchased from Superbiotek (cat. #COC1602), containing primary tumor tissues and matched adjacent normal mucosa from 69 patients with colorectal cancer with available clinicopathologic and follow-up data. Tumor stage was classified according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer eighth edition tumor–node–metastasis system, and information on recurrence/metastasis and overall survival (OS) was included. All immunohistochemistry (IHC) analyses for METTL3 K513me2, SETD1A, and E2F4 were performed on the primary tumor and adjacent normal tissues within this cohort. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital (Approval No. TJ-IRB20220723).

Plasmids

The Flag-SETDB1 plasmid has been described previously. The Flag-SETD1A and Flag-SETD1B plasmids were generously provided by Prof. Ping Wang (Tongji University, China). The HA-METTL3 and Flag-E2F4 plasmids were purchased from Miaoling Biotechnology. METTL3 (K513R) and SETD1A (R1495A) were generated using a site-directed mutagenesis kit (C214-01, Vazyme). The genes of SMYD1-5, SETD7, and SETD8 were inserted into the pcDNA3.1 (+) vector (RRID:Addgene_52535). The SETD1A and E2F4 promoter region, spanning nucleotides −2,000 to +100 relative to the transcription start site, was PCR-amplified and ligated into the pGL3-Basic luciferase reporter plasmid (RRID:Addgene_212936). The full-length SETD1A promoter construct served as a template to generate a series of promoter deletions.

IHC

IHC analysis was performed on paraffin-embedded subcutaneous tumors and TMAs following standard protocols. Tissue sections underwent deparaffinization, rehydration, and heat-mediated antigen retrieval before blocking with 5% BSA. After overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4°C and horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature, slides were digitized using a Pannoramic SCAN platform (3DHISTECH) at 400× magnification. Tumor regions were identified by pathologists, and protein expression was quantified via a semiautomated method assessing both staining intensity (mean optical density) and positive area percentage across three representative fields per core using Fiji (RRID:SCR_002285) software. A composite score (0–300) was calculated as follows: [1 × (% weak) + 2 × (% moderate) + 3 × (% strong)], with samples stratified into low/high METTL3 K513me2, SETD1A, and E2F4 expression groups based on median scores.

Immunoblot, immunoprecipitation, and immunofluorescence analysis

Protein extracts from colorectal cancer tissues and cultured cells were prepared using NP40 lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails, with protein concentrations determined by BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23225). For immunoblotting, equal protein quantities (20–50 μg) were denatured at 95°C for 10 minutes, resolved by 8% to 10% SDS-PAGE, and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes from Millipore. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) for 1 hour at room temperature prior to overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4°C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature and enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL)–based detection. For coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP), clarified lysates were incubated with antibody-conjugated Protein A/G beads or epitope-specific affinity beads (anti-Flag/HA) at 4°C overnight, washed extensively with lysis buffer, and analyzed by immunoblot. Immunofluorescence (IF) was performed on cells fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 minutes at room temperature, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100, and blocked with 2% BSA before sequential incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C and species-matched fluorescent secondary antibodies for 2 hours at room temperature, with nuclear counterstaining using DAPI. Confocal microscopy (Olympus FV3000) was employed for image acquisition and colocalization analysis.

Mass spectrometry analyses

Mass spectrometry analysis was conducted following previously described methods. In brief, the gel samples were sent to the protein genomics platform of Harbin Institute of Technology. Proteins eluted from the gel were precipitated using 0.1 mol/L ammonium acetate in 100% methanol. After a reduction–alkylation treatment (5 mmol/L DTT, 10 mmol/L iodoacetamide), the samples underwent overnight digestion with sequencing-grade trypsin. The resulting peptide mixtures were vacuum-dried using a SpeedVac concentrator and resuspended in water containing 0.1% formic acid (solvent A) for LC/MS-MS analysis. The peptides were separated using a Thermo Dionex UltiMate 3000 HPLC system, which was directly connected to an Orbitrap Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Data acquisition was performed in data-dependent acquisition mode with the Xcalibur 2.2 software (RRID:SCR_014593).

siRNA transfection

siRNAs were synthesized by AuGCT and transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, L3000008) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The siRNA sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Stable cell line construction

Stable knockdown cell lines were established through lentiviral transduction. Lentiviruses were produced by cotransfecting HEK293T cells with the plasmids pLV2-U6-shRNA-puro, PMD2.G (RRID:Addgene_12259), and psPAX2 (RRID:Addgene_12260). After viral infection, the cells were selected with puromycin for 48 hours. The short hairpin RNA sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Generation of knockout and knock-in cell lines

For knockout (KO) cell line generation, the single-guide RNAs (sgRNA) were cloned into the PX459 plasmid (62988, Addgene, RRID:Addgene_62988). After transfection, cells were incubated for 24 hours and selected with puromycin for 48 hours. Single cells were plated in a 96-well plate and cultured for 2 weeks. The KO cells were confirmed through immunoblotting and Sanger sequencing. sgRNA sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S4.

Knock-in cell lines were generated using the prime editing system. The system consisted of three plasmids: pU6-pegRNA-GG-acceptor (RRID:Addgene_132777), pCMV-PE2-P2A-GFP (RRID:Addgene_132776), and pU6-PE3 sgRNA. These plasmids were transfected into LoVo cells in a 9:3:1 ratio. Forty-eight hours after transfection, GFP-positive cells were sorted by flow cytometry, plated into 96-well plates, and cultured for 1 to 2 weeks. Single-cell clones were isolated and expanded into six-well plates. Genomic DNA was extracted from selected clones, and PCR and sequencing were used to confirm the targeted gene mutation. PegRNA and PE3 sgRNA sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S4.

m6A dot blot analysis

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent, quantified, and denatured before spotting (100 ng per sample) onto Hybond-N+ membranes. Following UV cross-linking (2,000 mJ/cm2), membranes were washed with phosphate-buffered saline with Tween 20 (PBST) (0.05% Tween 20) and blocked with phosphate-buffered saline with Tween 20 and bovine serum albumin (PBSTA) (3% BSA, 0.05% Tween 20) for 1 hour at room temperature. Hybond-N+ membranes were then incubated with anti-m6A antibody overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 2 hours at room temperature. The m6A signals were detected using ECL. For normalization, membranes were stained with 0.02% methylene blue to visualize spotted RNAs.

m6A RNA methylation quantification

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent and quantified. The m6A levels in RNA were then measured following the manufacturer’s instructions of the EpiQuik m6A RNA Methylation Quantification Kit (Colorimetric; P-9005, EpigenTek).

In vitro methylation assays

In vitro methylation assays were conducted using recombinant METTL3 protein purified from Escherichia coli and a commercially obtained SETD1A–SET complex (Active Motif, 81341). The reaction system (20 μL total volume) contained 50 mmol/L Tris-HCl (pH 8.6), 0.02% Triton X-100, 2 mmol/L MgCl2, 1 mmol/L Tris (2-carboxy-ethyl)-phosphin-HCl (TCEP), and 100 μmol/L S-adenosylmethionine (SAM). Purified METTL3 was incubated with the SETD1A–SET complex at 37°C for 2 hours, followed by termination with 5× SDS loading buffer. Reaction products were subsequently analyzed by SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting with antibodies.

optoDroplet assay

Light-induced condensation of METTL3 was examined using an optogenetic Cry2-based optoDroplet system. Human METTL3 wild type (WT) and METTL3-K513R were fused in-frame to mCherry–Cry2; mCherry–Cry2 alone (lacking an IDR-containing domain) served as the negative control. All constructs were sequence-verified.

LoVo and HT29 cells were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 and imaged 24 hours after transfection. Cells were exposed to blue-light activation (470 nm) using a pulsed LED source, and live-cell images were acquired using an Olympus confocal microscope.

Condensate quantification was performed following predefined criteria. Cells were scored as condensate-positive when discrete mCherry-positive puncta ≥0.5 μm in diameter were detectable and persisted for >10 seconds during imaging. Condensates that were transient (<10 seconds), diffuse, or below the detection threshold were classified as negative. The number and size of condensates per cell were quantified using ImageJ. At least 30 cells per condition from three independent experiments were analyzed.

Molecular simulation

Molecular simulations were initiated by extracting the K513 residue and SAM ligand from the crystallographic structure (PDB ID: 5TEY) using PyMOL (open-source version, RRID:SCR_000305). The K513 side chain was computationally dimethylated (K513-me2) through manual modification in PyMOL, followed by structural validation and capping of the noncanonical amino acid. Quantum mechanical optimization and vibrational frequency analysis of both K513-me2 and SAM were conducted at the r2SCAN-3c theory level using ORCA 5.0.4 (23, 24), incorporating Grimme’s dispersion corrections to obtain energetically stable conformations as simulation starting points. RESP2 charge derivation for SAM was performed via Multiwfn 3.8_dev (25), whereas Antechamber and parmchk2 within AmberTools23 (26) parametrized K513-me2 and SAM using the GAFF2 force field. Protein protonation states were predicted via the H++ server, with the AMBER14SB force field and TIP3P water model applied for system construction. Energy minimization employed sequential 2,500-step steepest descent and 1,000-step conjugate gradient algorithms. System equilibration comprised 5 nanoseconds of constant number of particles, volume, and temperature and 5 nanoseconds of constant number of particles, pressure, and temperature (NPT) simulations under Langevin thermostat control (300 K) with SHAKE-constrained hydrogen bonds (2 fs time step), utilizing a 10 Å cutoff and particle mesh Ewald electrostatics. Three independent 100-nanosecond production molecular dynamics (MD) runs were subsequently performed in the NPT ensemble.

Trajectory analyses encompassed root mean square deviation (RMSD), radius of gyration (Rg), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), hydrogen bonding, Dynamic Cross-Correlation Matrix (DCCM), and free energy landscape (FEL) calculations using cpptraj and MDAnalysis (27), visualized through matplotlib 3.4.12 (RRID:SCR_000305) in Python 3.9 (RRID:SCR_008394). Binding pocket dynamics were characterized via Fpocket 2.0 (28) and MoloVol (29), calculating cavity volumes (default parameters) and molecular volumes (1.2 Å probe radius, 0.1 Å grid spacing), with steric complementarity assessed as pocket-ligand volume difference. MM/PB(GB)SA binding free energies were computed using gmx_MMPBSA 1.6.2 (30) following acpype topology conversion, with the binding affinity defined byΔGbind = Gcomplex - (Gfree protein + Gfree ligand)

Convergence-verified final 25-nanosecond trajectories from triplicate simulations were employed for energy calculations to ensure statistical robustness.

Surface plasmon resonance assay

The His-METTL3 (354–580 aa)–METTL14 (107–395 aa) protein complex was purified by Sino Biological using the insect cell expression system. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) binding assay was conducted on a Biacore T200 instrument (GE HealthCare) using Series S NTA sensor chips at 20°C. His-tagged METTL3/14 complex was immobilized to flow cell 2 of the EDC/NHS preactivated chip in a running buffer containing 20 mmol/L HEPES (pH 7.5), 150 mmol/L NaCl, 1 mmol/L TCEP, and 0.05% Tween 20. Postimmobilization surface blocking was achieved through sequential 60-second pulses of ethanolamine and 10 mmol/L EDTA. SAM solutions (serially diluted in running buffer supplemented with 2% DMSO) were analyzed via single-cycle kinetics mode with a 90-second association at an 80 μL/minutes flow rate and 1,800-second dissociation monitoring. Data processing using Biacore Insight Evaluation software version 3.0.12 included reference subtraction (protein-free flow cell 1), double referencing (buffer-only injections), and solvent correction. Kinetic parameters were derived by global fitting of processed sensorgrams to a 1:1 Langmuir binding model.

Methylated RNA IP sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from METTL3 WT and K513R LoVo cells using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15596026) following the manufacturer’s recommended protocol. Following RNA extraction, DNase I treatment was performed to remove any contaminating DNA. The quality of the RNA was assessed by measuring the A260/A280 ratio using a NanoDrop. RNA integrity was evaluated by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis. The concentration of the qualified RNA samples was determined using a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer and Qubit RNA Broad Range Assay kit (Life Technologies, Q10210). rRNA depletion was conducted using the Ribo-off rRNA depletion kit (Vazyme, N406). The rRNA-depleted RNA was chemically fragmented into approximately 100-nucleotide-long fragments by treatment with 20 mmol/L ZnCl2 at 95°C for 5 to 10 minutes. Ten percent of the rRNA-depleted fragments were saved as “Input,” and the remainder was used for m6A IP with anti-m6A antibody (Synaptic Systems, 202203). The RNA–antibody complexes were then immunoprecipitated using Protein-G magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 88848) for 1 hour at 4°C with rotation, ensuring efficient capture of the m6A–RNA complexes. Subsequently, the Protein-G magnetic beads were extensively washed to remove nonspecifically bound RNA, and the immunoprecipitated RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent. For library preparation, both input and IP samples were used as input for RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) library preparation utilizing some components of the KCTM Digital mRNA Library Prep Kit (Seqhealth Tech. Co., Ltd.), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Unique molecular identifiers (UMI) were used to eliminate duplication bias and errors in the PCR and sequencing steps. The enriched libraries (200–500 bp) were quantified and sequenced on a DNBSEQ-T7 (MGI Tech Co., Ltd.) using the PE150 sequencing mode to generate paired-end reads.

Methylated RNA IP sequencing data analysis

Raw sequencing data were processed using Trimmomatic (RRID:SCR_011848) to remove low-quality reads and adaptor contamination, followed by UMI-based clustering to eliminate PCR and sequencing biases. Raw sequencing data were first filtered by fastp (version 0.23.4), low-quality reads were discarded, and the reads contaminated with adaptor sequences were trimmed. The deduplicated consensus sequences were mapped to the Homo sapiens reference genome GRCh38 with STAR software (version 2.5.3a). Peak calling was performed with exomePeak (version 3.8, RRID:SCR_001076), and m6A peaks were annotated using bedtools (version 2.25.0, RRID:SCR_006646). Peak distribution analysis was carried out with deepTools (version 2.4.1, RRID:SCR_016366), and differentially enriched m6A peaks were identified through a Fisher test implemented in Python. Sequence motifs in m6A peak regions were identified using Homer (version 4.10). Retrotransposons [long terminal repeat (LTR), long interspersed nuclear elements (LINE), and short interspersed nuclear elements (SINE)] were annotated using the RepeatMasker database (UCSC GRCh38/hg38, RRID:SCR_012954) with priority given to these elements. Annotation entries were filtered to retain retrotransposons ≥300 bp in length and with ≥50 genomic copies. m6A peaks identified via meRIP-seq were intersected with retrotransposon coordinates to define retrotransposon-specific m6A-modified regions.

RNA-seq

The RNA extraction and rRNA depletion procedures are analogous to those employed in methylated RNA IP sequencing (MeRIP-seq). For stranded RNA-seq library preparation, 2 μg of total RNA was utilized. The Ribo-off rRNA Depletion Kit (Human; MRZG12324, Illumina) and the KC Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (DR08402, Wuhan Seqhealth Co., Ltd.) were used, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The library fragments between 200 and 500 bp were selected, enriched, quantified, and subsequently sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7 sequencer using the PE150 model.

RNA-seq data analysis

The raw sequencing data were initially processed using Trimmomatic (version 0.36), in which low-quality reads were removed and reads containing adaptor contamination were trimmed. Cleaned reads were then aligned to the H. sapiens reference genome GRCh38 with STAR software (version 2.5.3a) using default parameters. The repeatmasker annotation file for GRCh38 was obtained using the UCSC Table Browser. Reads falling in repeat regions were counted by featureCounts (Subread-1.5.1, RRID:SCR_012919; Bioconductor, RRID:SCR_006442), and then reads per kilobase per million mapped reads (RPKM) was calculated. Genes differentially expressed between groups were identified using the edgeR package (version 3.12.1, RRID:SCR_012802). A P value cutoff of 0.05 and a fold change cutoff of 1.5 were used to judge the statistical significance of gene expression differences.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Quantitative real-time PCR assay was performed as previously described. The primers used for qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table S5. Data were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

RNA decay assay

After treating colorectal cancer cells with actinomycin D (5 μg/mL) for the indicated durations, total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent, followed by quantification and analysis via qPCR.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay for IFNβ and CXCL10

Tumor-conditioned media were centrifuged at 800 g for 2 minutes to remove cellular debris and stored at −20°C. IFNβ and CXCL10 levels in conditioned media were quantified using the Mouse IFNβ Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) Kit (RK00420, ABclonal) and the Mouse CXCL10 ELISA Kit (RK00056, ABclonal) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Cell surface MHC-I expression analysis

To assess cell surface MHC-I abundance, collected cells were washed with PBS and stained with FITC anti-mouse H-2Kb/H-2Db antibodies (BioLegend, clone 28-8-6, 114606, RRID:AB_313597) for 20 minutes at room temperature. FITC Mouse IgG2a, κ Isotype Ctrl Antibody (BioLegend, 400207, RRID:AB_2884007) was used as the isotype control. After staining, cells were washed, resuspended in 200 μL PBS, and immediately analyzed using a Beckman Coulter CytoFLEX flow cytometer. Data were analyzed with FlowJo software (RRID:SCR_008520).

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The WT and truncated promoter fragments of SETD1A or E2F4 were cloned upstream of the firefly luciferase reporter gene in a pGL3-Basic vector (RRID:Addgene_48743). The designated cells were cotransfected with the pGL3-promoter plasmid, a pRL-SV40 Renilla luciferase reporter plasmid, and an expression vector encoding the specified transcription factor (TF) or a control vector. Luciferase activities were measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega). The firefly luciferase activity was normalized to the Renilla luciferase activity and expressed as relative luciferase activity.

Chromatin IP assay

The chromatin IP (ChIP) assay was conducted using the ChIP-IT Express Kit (Active Motif) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, the indicated cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde, and the reaction was quenched with glycine. The fixed cells were harvested, lysed, and sonicated on ice. IP was carried out using an anti-E2F4 antibody or isotype control. Ten percent of the chromatin sample before IP was saved as the input control. ChIP-enriched DNA was analyzed by qPCR with primers targeting E2F4 binding sites in the SETD1A or E2F4 promoters (Supplementary Table S6). Enrichment was calculated as the fold change relative to IgG controls.

Preparation of single-cell suspensions from subcutaneous tumors

Subcutaneous tumor specimens were mechanically minced using sterile scissors and enzymatically digested in 3 mL of serum-free RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with collagenase IV (Sigma-Aldrich, V900893), hyaluronidase (Sigma-Aldrich, H3506), and DNase I (0.25 mg/mL; Roche, 10104159001). The digestion was performed for 1 hour at 37°C with constant agitation (150 rpm). Following complete tissue dissociation, enzymatic activity was quenched by adding 7 mL of serum-free RPMI 1640 medium. The cell suspension was sequentially filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 minutes. Erythrocyte lysis was achieved by incubating the pellet with 1 mL of ACK lysing buffer for 1 minute at room temperature, followed by immediate neutralization with 10 mL of PBS. The resulting single-cell suspension was maintained on ice for subsequent immunostaining procedures.

Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes analysis

Single-cell suspensions obtained from subcutaneous tumors were incubated with anti-mouse CD16/32 antibody (BioLegend, clone 93, 101320, RRID:AB_1574975) for 30 minutes to block Fc receptors. After washing and centrifugation, cell viability was assessed using the Zombie UV Fixable Viability Kit (BioLegend, 423106, RRID:AB_3719333) to exclude dead cells. Subsequently, the cells were stained for 30 minutes at room temperature with the following fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies: BV510 anti-mouse CD45 (BioLegend, clone 30-F11, 103137, RRID:AB_2561392), BV785 anti-mouse CD3 (BioLegend, clone 17A2, 100231, RRID:AB_11218805), PE anti-mouse CD8 (BioLegend, clone 53-6.7, 100707, RRID:AB_312746), APC anti-mouse CD49b (BioLegend, clone DX5, 108909, RRID:AB_313416), BV421 anti-mouse F4/80 (BioLegend, clone BM8, 123137, RRID:AB_2563102), BV605 anti-mouse CD11b (BioLegend, clone M1/70, 101257, RRID:AB_2565431), and FITC anti-mouse CD86 (BioLegend, clone A17199A, 159220, RRID:AB_3106043). Cells were then washed and permeabilized using 200 μL of Fixation/Perm Solution (BD Biosciences, 554714) for 30 minutes at room temperature, followed by washing with Perm/Wash Buffer. METTL3 lysine methylation is associated with colorectal cancer progression and recurrence

To identify non-histone proteins regulated by posttranslational lysine methylation and associated with colorectal cancer progression and recurrence, we performed mass spectrometry analysis of tissue lysates derived from three locally recurrent patients with colorectal cancer using a specific pan-lysine methylation antibody (Fig. 1A). This analysis yielded 47 lysine-methylated candidate proteins shared among all three tumors (Supplementary Table S7). Candidates were ranked according to peptide spectral counts and relative abundance, with the top five enriched proteins being EEF1A2, METTL3, GNL1, SUPT5H, and CHERP. Among these, METTL3, a core m6A methyltransferase with established roles in RNA modification and tumor biology, was prioritized for further investigation (Fig. 1B). Dimethylation at K513 of METTL3 was detected in all three tumor tissues (Supplementary Table S8; Supplementary Fig. S1A). Importantly, the K513 site is evolutionarily conserved across species (Fig. 1C). To confirm METTL3 K513 dimethylation, we generated a rabbit polyclonal anti-K513me2 antibody and validated it by dot blot (Supplementary Fig. S1B). IP of METTL3 from LoVo and HT29 colorectal cancer cells, followed by Western blotting with anti–pan-dimethylated lysine and anti-K513me2 antibodies, confirmed K513 dimethylation (Supplementary Fig. S1C).

We next determined the relevance of METTL3 K513 methylation in colorectal cancer progression and recurrence. First, IHC assays on nine paired recurrent and primary colorectal cancer tumors revealed significantly higher METTL3 K513 methylation and m6A levels in recurrent tumors (Fig. 1D and E). We further examined the relationship between METTL3 K513 methylation and clinicopathologic characteristics in colorectal cancer progression using a colorectal cancer TMA. The results revealed that METTL3 K513 methylation was elevated in colorectal cancer tissues versus adjacent normal tissues and in stage III/IV (advanced) versus stage I/II (early) tumors (Fig. 1F and G; Supplementary Fig. S1D). We found that METTL3 K513 methylation was positively related to recurrence and distant metastasis (Fig. 1H). Moreover, patients with high METTL3 methylation levels had shorter OS times compared with those with low METTL3 methylation levels (Fig. 1I). Notably, multivariate Cox regression analyses further revealed that METTL3 methylation was an independent and meaningful prognostic factor for predicting outcomes in patients with colorectal cancer (Fig. 1J). Taken together, these data suggest that METTL3 K513 methylation is associated with colorectal cancer progression and recurrence, highlighting its potential as a prognostic biomarker.

The SETD1A methyltransferase complex catalyzes the dimethylation of METTL3

To identify the methyltransferase responsible for METTL3 K513 dimethylation, we screened a panel of lysine methyltransferases (SMYD1-5, SETD7/8, SETDB1, SETD1A/B) in HEK293T cells. The results showed that only SETD1A overexpression, but not other lysine methyltransferases, significantly increased METTL3 K513 methylation (Fig. 2A). Meanwhile, we performed a co-IP assay from HEK293T cells overexpressing HA-METTL3 and conducted mass spectrometry analysis to identify the METTL3-interacting proteins. Mass spectrometry analysis identified specific peptide sequences of SETD1A, supporting the evidence for its interaction with METTL3 (Supplementary Fig. S2A; Supplementary Table S9). Flag-SETD1A and HA-METTL3 plasmids were cotransfected into HEK293T cells, and co-IP assays revealed an interaction between METTL3 and SETD1A (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, the interaction between endogenous METTL3 and SETD1A was confirmed using anti-METTL3 or anti-SETD1A antibodies in co-IP assays (Fig. 2C). Additionally, IF in LoVo and HT29 cells showed the colocalization of METTL3 and SETD1A (Fig. 2D). To determine the region of METTL3 that interacts with SETD1A, several truncated forms of METTL3 were utilized. The results revealed that the C-terminal domain (MT-A70 domain) was essential for SETD1A binding (Supplementary Fig. S2B and S2C), with K513 residing within this region.

Next, we detected the effect of SETD1A on METTL3 K513 methylation. Downregulation of SETD1A expression led to a significant decrease in the dimethylation of METTL3 at K513, as assessed by the anti-K513me2 antibody (Fig. 2E). Consistently, the same results were observed using the anti–pan-dimethylated-lysine antibody. The dimethylation levels of METTL3 increased with the increasing expression of SETD1A, whereas they were substantially restrained in SETD1A knockdown cells (Supplementary Fig. S2D and S2E). In addition, the methylation-deficient variant of METTL3, K513R, abolished the dimethylation upregulation of METTL3 induced by SETD1A overexpression in HEK293T cells (Supplementary Fig. S2F). To further confirm this observation, we generated METTL3 K513R knock-in colorectal cancer cell lines using the prime editing system (Supplementary Fig. S2G). The K513R mutation completely eliminated SETD1A-mediated METTL3 K513 methylation (Fig. 2F; Supplementary Fig. S2H). SETD1A exerts its methyltransferase activity through the assembly of a complex with several proteins, including ASH2L, RBBP5, and WDR5 (31). The R1495A mutation in SETD1A impairs its interaction with key proteins in the complex, resulting in decreased SETD1A methyltransferase activity (31). Our data indicated that overexpression of WT SETD1A, but not the SETD1A R1495A mutant, enhanced METTL3 K513 methylation (Fig. 2G; Supplementary Fig. S2I). Knockdown of WDR5, RbBP5, or ASH2L also reduced K513 methylation levels (Supplementary Fig. S2J). Furthermore, the in vitro methylation assay revealed that the SETD1A methyltransferase complex methylated METTL3 at K513 (Fig. 2H). These data collectively indicate that the SETD1A methyltransferase complex catalyzes the dimethylation of METTL3.

We conducted IHC staining using an anti-SETD1A antibody on previous clinical samples to assess its role in the progression and recurrence of colorectal cancer. The data showed that SETD1A expression was higher in recurrent tumors compared with primary tumors (Fig. 2I). Moreover, SETD1A was more highly expressed in colorectal cancer tissues than in adjacent tissues (Supplementary Fig. S2K). Notably, high SETD1A expression was significantly correlated with high METTL3 K513 methylation (Fig. 2J). In addition, KM survival analysis revealed that the OS time for patients with high SETD1A expression was obviously shorter than that for those with low SETD1A expression (Fig. 2K). We further explored the associations between SETD1A expression and prognosis in various cancer types. Based on the KM plotter and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data, high SETD1A expression was correlated with poor OS, recurrence-free survival, and progression-free survival (PFS) in colorectal cancer, gastric cancer, lung cancer, liver cancer, and melanoma (Supplementary Fig. S2L and S2M). These results suggest that SETD1A expression could influence the progression and recurrence of multiple cancers.

METTL3 K513 methylation enhances the interaction between METTL3 and SAM

Next, we examined the impact of SETD1A-mediated METTL3 methylation on its function. METTL3 K513R mutation led to a significant reduction in RNA m6A abundance compared with METTL3 WT, as assessed by m6A enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay and dot blot analysis (Fig. 3A). In addition, we used the same method to detect the relationship between SETD1A and RNA m6A modification. Our data revealed that knockdown of SETD1A impeded m6A levels, and the SETD1A R1495A mutant failed to enhance RNA m6A modification in LoVo and HT29 cells (Fig. 3B; Supplementary Fig. S3A–S3D). These data indicated that METTL3 methylation could regulate RNA m6A deposition.

We further investigated the underlying mechanism by which METTL3 methylation influences RNA m6A levels. Recent studies have highlighted the role of non-histone protein methylation in regulating protein stability, subcellular localization, and so on (32). However, we found that the K513 mutation did not affect the protein level or the cytoplasmic-nuclear distribution of METTL3 (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Fig. S3E). Given that K513 is located within the SAM-binding pocket of METTL3 (33), we further explored whether METTL3 methylation affects SAM binding. We generated the K513 dimethylation model based on the crystal structure of the METTL3–METTL14 complex (PDB ID: 5TEY) and performed molecular dynamics simulations. Following K513 methylation, both the backbone RMSD of the complex and the heavy atom RMSD of SAM decreased (Supplementary Fig. S3F and S3G). Analysis of the Rg and SASA revealed that K513 methylation enhances the complex’s compactness and reduces solvent exposure (Supplementary Fig. S3H and S3I). This modification also strengthens the hydrogen bond interactions between SAM and the protein while further lowering the RMSF of the B-chain residues (Supplementary Fig. S3J–S3N).

The final frame of both complexes was extracted at the simulation endpoint and visualized using PyMOL. The results showed more hydrogen bonds and salt bridges in the K513 dimethylated complex compared with the unmethylated complex (Fig. 3C). FEL analysis aligned with Rg and SASA data, demonstrating that the K513 dimethylated complex adopted a more compact conformation, as evidenced by a concentrated energy well compared with the two conformational spaces observed in the unmethylated complex (Fig. 3D). DCCM analysis revealed enhanced positive correlations in the METTL3 A-chain (residues 369–570) and weakened negative correlations in the METTL14 B-chain (residues 117–397; Fig. 3E). Further analysis of pocket volumes during simulations confirmed a reduced volume in the methylated complex (Fig. 3F), supporting improved structural stability.

We calculated the Gibbs binding free energy (ΔGbind) using molecular mechanics/poisson–boltzmann surface area (MM/PBSA) and molecular mechanics/generalized born surface area (MM/GBSA) methods. MM/PBSA results showed that ΔGbind for the methylated complex (−13.88 to −8.66 kcal/mol) was less favorable than that of the unmethylated complex (−25.52 to −31.12 kcal/mol). Similarly, MM/GBSA analysis indicated a less favorable ΔGbind for the methylated complex (−26.93 to −24.99 kcal/mol) compared with the unmethylated complex (−43.75 to −26.77 kcal/mol). Entropy contributions [estimated via the interaction entropy (IE) method] were consistently lower for the methylated complex (Supplementary Tables S10 and S11). These data suggest that K513 dimethylation modulates intermolecular interactions with SAM, potentially through altered electrostatic and van der Waals forces, contributing to reduced binding affinity.

To verify this result in vitro, we purified the METTL3(WT)–METTL14 and METTL3(K513R)–METTL14 complexes containing the methyltransferase domain (METTL3, residues 354–580; METTL14, residues 107–395; Supplementary Fig. S3O). SPR analysis revealed that the METTL3(WT)–METTL14 complex exhibited high SAM affinity (Kd = 23.9 μmol/L), whereas the METTL3(K513R)–METTL14 complex showed no concentration-dependent binding and no detectable SAM interaction (Fig. 3G and H). A previous study has suggested that SAM binding is required for METTL3 phase separation (34). We utilized an optogenetic tool, “optoDroplet,” to design constructs in which METTL3 WT or K513R were each fused to mCherry and the photolyase homology region of Arabidopsis thaliana Cry2, a light-activatable self-associating protein that induces phase separation upon blue light exposure (35). Notably, the K513R mutant failed to form condensates after blue light activation, indicating that METTL3 methylation at K513 is required for SAM binding (Fig. 3I). Taken together, these data demonstrate that METTL3 methylation plays a critical role in its binding to SAM (Fig. 3J).

EREs expression affected by the methylation of METTL3 at K513

To determine the biological importance of METTL3 dimethylation at K513, we conducted MeRIP-seq and RNA-seq on METTL3 WT and K513R LoVo cells (Fig. 4A; Supplementary Fig. S4A–S4C). MeRIP-seq analysis revealed enrichment of the canonical m6A consensus motif RRACH, with the top enriched sequence in our dataset (“GGACH”) representing a specific RRACH variant (Fig. 4B). We identified 24,743 and 15,574 m6A peaks in WT and K513R cells, respectively, indicating reduced m6A abundance in K513R mutant cells (Supplementary Fig. S4D). Furthermore, 6,979 m6A peaks showed significant reduction in K513R cells compared with WT (Fig. 4C; Supplementary Table S12). Consistent with previous studies (36, 37), our analysis of the genomic distribution of detectable m6A peaks revealed that in addition to being significantly enriched in the 3′ UTRs and exons of gene mRNAs, a substantial proportion of the peaks were located in retrotransposon regions, accounting for 12% of the total peaks (Fig. 4D). Strikingly, K513R cells exhibited a significant reduction in retrotransposon-associated m6A peaks (Fig. 4E; Supplementary Table S13). EREs include LTR-containing endogenous retroviruses (ERV) and non-LTR retrotransposons, such as LINEs and SINEs (38). Our data showed that both LTR and non-LTR retroelements displayed reduced m6A deposition in K513R cells (Fig. 4F; Supplementary Fig. S4E). Furthermore, RNA-seq analysis revealed a significant upregulation of retroelement expression in K513R cells (Fig. 4G; Supplementary Fig. S4F; Supplementary Tables S14 and S15). These data suggest that METTL3 K513 methylation affects the m6A modification of EREs.

We further validated the above results in colorectal cancer cells. Both the knockdown of SETD1A and the methylation-deficient mutant METTL3 K513 could promote the expression of ERV (Fig. 4H–J). We generated METTL3 KO CT26 cell lines using the CRISPR-Cas9 system and rescued them with mouse METTL3(WT) [Mus-METTL3(WT)] or the methylation-deficient mutant Mus-METTL3(K513R), which is homologous to human METTL3 K513 (Supplementary Fig. S4G). The same result was observed in these cells (Fig. 4K). More importantly, SETD1A knockdown increased EREs expression in METTL3(WT) cells but had no additive effect in METTL3(K513R) cells, which exhibited elevated basal EREs levels (Fig. 4L). The results indicate that SETD1A regulates EREs expression in a METTL3 K513 methylation-dependent manner. Prior studies suggest that m6A modification destabilizes retroelement transcripts by reducing their half-life, thereby promoting RNA decay (39). The RNA decay assay revealed that retroelement transcripts were significantly more stable in K513R cells (Fig. 4M). Together, SETD1A-mediated dimethylation of METTL3 at K513 is critical for suppressing EREs through m6A-dependent RNA decay.

METTL3 K513 dimethylation impedes the type I IFN response and antitumor immunity

The prevailing model suggests that EREs are the source of overproduced or accumulated nucleic acid intermediates, which can form immunogenic double-stranded RNAs (dsRNA) and DNAs (dsDNA), thereby activating pattern recognition receptors and triggering type I IFN responses (11, 40–43). Given that METTL3 K513 methylation could regulate the expression of EREs, we investigated its impact on type I IFN signaling. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that pathways associated with the type I IFN response were enriched in K513R mutant cells (Fig. 5A). Next, staining with a monoclonal J2 antibody that specifically recognized dsRNA observed elevated dsRNA levels in K513R cells (Fig. 5B). Moreover, the METTL3 K513R mutant led to the activation of proteins related to the type I IFN signaling pathway (Fig. 5C). Consistently, IFNβ and CXCL10 secretion increased significantly, along with upregulated IFN-stimulated genes (ISG; Fig. 5D and E; Supplementary Fig. S5A). As genes involved in antigen processing and presentation are direct ISGs (44), we next investigated the expression of MHC-I and genes in antigen processing and presentation pathways. Our data showed a significant increase in these genes in K513R cells (Fig. 5F; Supplementary Fig. S5B).

Given the critical role of the type I IFN response in antitumor immunity, we next examined changes in the tumor immune microenvironment in vivo. We found that the deficiency of METTL3 dimethylation inhibited tumor progression (Fig. 5G; Supplementary Fig. S5C). Flow cytometry analysis revealed a significant increase in tumor-infiltrating immune cells—particularly CD8+ T cells and NK cells—and a reduction in M2 macrophages in K513R mutant tumors compared with WT tumors (Fig. 5H; Supplementary Fig. S5D), indicating that METTL3 K513 dimethylation drives immune microenvironment remodeling. In contrast, subcutaneous tumors implanted in immunodeficient NOD/SCID/IL2Rγnull mice showed no significant growth difference between WT and K513R groups, suggesting that the tumor immune microenvironment plays a role in regulating the growth of K513R mutant tumors (Fig. 5I; Supplementary Fig. S5E). SETD1A promotes colorectal cancer immune evasion in a METTL3 K513 methylation-dependent manner

As SETD1A catalyzes METTL3 dimethylation, we next explored its role in colorectal cancer antitumor immunity. SETD1A knockdown increased cytoplasmic dsRNA accumulation (Fig. 6A; Supplementary Fig. S6A) and activated the nucleic acid sensing pathway (Fig. 6B). Similarly, SETD1A knockdown upregulated ISGs, surface MHC-I, as well as antigen processing and presentation-related genes (Fig. 6C–E; Supplementary Fig. S6B–S6D). Furthermore, in the CT26 subcutaneously implanted tumor model, we found that SETD1A knockdown impaired tumor growth compared with controls (Fig. 6F; Supplementary Fig. S6E and S6F). SETD1A-silenced CT26 tumors exhibited increased CD8+ T-cell and NK cell infiltration, alongside reduced M2 macrophages (Fig. 6G; Supplementary Fig. S6G). These data suggest that SETD1A is a regulator of type I IFN responses and antitumor immunity in colorectal cancer.

METTL3 dimethylation–induced colorectal cancer progression relies on type I IFN–mediated tumor immune microenvironment remodeling

ERE-derived dsRNA is known to activate IFN production via the cytosolic RNA sensor MDA5-MAVS, initiating immune responses. This dsRNA can also be reverse-transcribed into dsDNA, activating the cGAS-STING DNA-sensing pathway to elicit similar effects (Fig. 7A; refs. 38, 45). Importantly, GSEA revealed that RIG-I–like receptor signaling and cytosolic DNA-sensing pathways were elevated in K513R mutant cells (Supplementary Fig. S7A). To verify whether the function of METTL3 dimethylation depends on the above pathways, we generated CT26 K513R mutant cells with KO of Mda5, Cgas, or both (Supplementary Fig. S7B). Depletion of the RNA sensor MDA5 or DNA sensor cGAS in K513R cells partially attenuated ISG induction, whereas the simultaneous deletion of both further suppressed ISG upregulation (Fig. 7B). In vivo experiments (Fig. 7C) showed that MDA5 or cGAS KO partially restored tumor growth in K513R mutants, with dual KO exhibiting a stronger rescue (Fig. 7D; Supplementary Fig. S7C and S7D). The abundance of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells, NK cells, and M2 macrophages mirrored these trends (Fig. 7E; Supplementary Fig. S7E). These findings demonstrate that both cytosolic RNA- and DNA-sensing pathways are crucial for the IFN activation and immune response triggered by METTL3 K513 methylation deficiency.

To further investigate the role of type I IFN in this process, we administered an anti-IFNAR1 antibody to block the IFN receptor in vivo and performed animal experiments (Fig. 7F). The results revealed that the application of the anti-IFNAR1 antibody eliminated the K513R mutant–induced tumor suppression (Fig. 7G; Supplementary Fig. S7F and S7G). Furthermore, IFNAR1 blockade suppressed immune cell infiltration in K513R tumors (Fig. 7H; Supplementary Fig. S7H).

Given that the loss of METTL3 K513 dimethylation robustly activates type I IFN signaling and enhances intratumoral CD8+ T-cell and NK cell infiltration, we next asked whether this immune-stimulatory state could increase tumor sensitivity to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB). To assess this therapeutic relevance, we combined METTL3 K513R with anti–PD-1 treatment in the CT26 syngeneic tumor model (Fig. 7I). The combination of K513R and PD-1 blockade produced a significantly greater reduction in tumor growth than either monotherapy (Fig. 7J; Supplementary Fig. S7I and S7J) and markedly increased CD8+ T-cell infiltration (Fig. 7K; Supplementary Fig. S7K).

Collectively, these findings highlight type I IFN as the central mediator of tumor growth suppression caused by METTL3 K513 dimethylation deficiency and indicate that the loss of this modification functionally reprograms tumors into an immune-inflamed state that is markedly more responsive to PD-1 blockade.

Fluorouracil-induced E2F4 self-regulation activates SETD1A-mediated METTL3 methylation

Given the role of SETD1A-mediated METTL3 methylation in colorectal cancer, we further investigated mechanisms regulating SETD1A upregulation. Analysis of 594 colorectal cancer tissues via cBioPortal revealed low-frequency SETD1A genetic alterations (Supplementary Fig. S8A). Genetic variation is unlikely to account for the upregulation of SETD1A. Treating colorectal cancer cells with decitabine, a DNMT inhibitor, did not significantly alter SETD1A mRNA levels, suggesting other mechanisms may regulate SETD1A in colorectal cancer (Supplementary Fig. S8B). We screened TFs regulating SETD1A using JASPAR, ENCODE, ChIP-Atlas, CHEA3, and GTRD, identifying E2F4, a member of the E2F TF family (Fig. 8A). Analysis of TCGA data revealed a strong E2F4-SETD1A correlation across cancers (Supplementary Fig. S8C). The protein and mRNA levels of SETD1A were elevated in E2F4-overexpressing cells, whereas they were reduced in E2F4-silenced cells. Importantly, the levels of METTL3 K513 methylation showed a consistent trend with E2F4 (Fig. 8B; Supplementary Fig. S8D and S8E). Furthermore, we analyzed the potential binding sites of E2F4 on the SETD1A promoter and constructed segmented and mutated plasmids of the SETD1A promoter (Supplementary Fig. S8F and S8G). The dual-luciferase reporter assay indicated that E2F4 predominantly binds to site 1 (Fig. 8C). ChIP assays confirmed E2F4 binding to this site (Fig. 8D). HLM006474 is a potent E2F4 inhibitor that blocks DNA binding (46). Treatment with HLM006474 not only downregulated SETD1A expression and METTL3 dimethylation levels but also significantly reduced RNA m6A abundance (Fig. 8E).

Next, we found that E2F4 was increased in recurrent colorectal cancer tissues (Supplementary Fig. S8H). IHC analysis of TMA showed that E2F4 was highly expressed in tumor tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues (Supplementary Fig. S8I). Additionally, E2F4 expression positively correlated with SETD1A and METTL3 K513 dimethylation in colorectal cancer samples (Supplementary Fig. S8J and S8K). KM analysis showed that high E2F4 expression correlates with worse survival of patients with colorectal cancer (Supplementary Fig. S8L). Based on the KM plotter and TCGA data, similar to SETD1A, high E2F4 expression was associated with poor outcomes in multiple cancers (Supplementary Fig. S8M and S8N).

Mounting evidence has implicated that fluorouracil, a key therapeutic agent in colorectal cancer, induces widespread alterations in chromatin accessibility and the transcriptome (47). Furthermore, certain TFs exhibit self-activation during tumor progression, such as SP1 and FOXM1 (48, 49). Thus, we hypothesized that the activation of E2F4 results from its intrinsic capacity for self-activation, with fluorouracil further promoting this process. To validate this hypothesis, we treated LoVo cells with fluorouracil for different days. The results revealed that the mRNA levels of E2F4 gradually increased after fluorouracil treatment, followed by SETD1A mRNA expression (Fig. 8F). Interestingly, not only did the protein levels of E2F4 and SETD1A show a similar trend, but the methylation level of METTL3 at K513 also increased accordingly (Fig. 8G). ChIP-qPCR further confirmed enhanced E2F4 autobinding to its own promoter (Fig. 8H; Supplementary Fig. S8O), supporting a model in which 5-FU promotes E2F4 self-activation, thereby driving SETD1A-mediated METTL3 methylation.

To evaluate the therapeutic implications of targeting this axis, we tested HLM006474 in vivo (Fig. 8I). E2F4 inhibition alone moderately suppressed tumor growth and improved survival, whereas its combination with anti–PD-1 produced markedly greater tumor inhibition and significantly enhanced CD8+ T-cell infiltration (Fig. 8J–L; Supplementary Fig. S8P). These findings indicate that pharmacologic E2F4 blockade can phenocopy METTL3 K513R and enhance tumor responsiveness to PD-1 immunotherapy.

Discussion

METTL3 is the catalytic subunit of m6A modification, utilizing SAM as a methyl donor. Its PTMs significantly expand the functional diversity of m6A in cancer. ERK-mediated phosphorylation stabilizes METTL3, promoting tumorigenesis. Lactate induces METTL3 lactylation at residues K281 and K345, enhancing the immunosuppressive activity of tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells (50). Acetylation drives METTL3 nuclear localization, augmenting protein translation and suppressing breast cancer metastasis (51). SUMOylation of METTL3 represses its m6A methyltransferase activity, resulting in inhibited tumor growth (52). Although the initial proteomic screen was performed on a limited number of recurrent colorectal cancer samples without matched normal tissues, we minimized interpatient variability by focusing on proteins consistently detected across all cases. This exploratory approach identified high-confidence lysine-methylated candidates, among which METTL3 was prioritized for functional investigation. In our study, we discovered the presence of dimethylation at the K513 residue of METTL3 in recurrent colorectal cancer tissues and observed that its expression levels were elevated in colorectal cancer tissues. Importantly, METTL3 K513 methylation serves as an independent prognostic marker for patients with colorectal cancer, highlighting its clinical relevance.

SETD1A, a member of the mammalian SET1/MLL family of H3K4 methyltransferases, regulates gene expression through mono- to trimethylation of H3K4 (53). Beyond histone targets, SETD1A methylates non-histone proteins to drive oncogenic processes. For example, SETD1A dimethylates Hsp70 at K561, activating aurora kinase B to promote tumor proliferation (54), and monomethylates YAP at K342 to regulate its nuclear localization and oncogenic activity (17). Here, we demonstrate that SETD1A directly dimethylates METTL3 at K513, enhancing METTL3-SAM binding affinity and global m6A levels. This discovery expands the known PTM landscape of METTL3 and establishes methylation as a critical regulatory mechanism in colorectal cancer progression.

EREs, constituting approximately 50% of the human genome, are crucial for genome stability and evolution. Dysregulation of EREs is implicated in autoimmune disorders, neurodegeneration, and cancer. Previous studies have confirmed that DNA methylation and heterochromatin histone modifications play an important role in regulating EREs (55). Apart from DNA and histone modifications, several RNA modifications have been proposed to regulate ERE expression, particularly the m6A modification. The METTL3–METTL14 methyltransferase complex catalyzes m6A modification on human endogenous retrovirus (HERV) mRNA, thereby reducing its half-life and suppressing HERV expression (39). Fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO) alpha-ketoglutarate dependent dioxygenase mediates m6A demethylation of LINE1 RNA in mouse embryonic stem cells, thereby regulating LINE1 RNA levels and the local chromatin environment (56). Our research has uncovered that SETD1A-mediated METTL3 K513 dimethylation is a key regulator of EREs, as revealed by MeRIP-seq and RNA-seq analyses, providing novel insights into the regulatory mechanisms governing EREs.

The prevailing model suggests that ERE-derived nucleic acids activate cytosolic sensors, triggering type I IFN responses through “viral mimicry” (38). Numerous studies have demonstrated that modulating the expression of EREs can affect the IFN response and antitumor immunity. Loss of KDM5B in melanoma cells leads to the derepression of retroelement expression, triggering the type I IFN signaling pathway and stimulating antitumor responses (20). MED12 plays a key role in remodeling the immune TME in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma by epigenetically silencing retroelements, thereby influencing immune responses and tumor progression (57). Our study revealed that METTL3 K513 dimethylation impedes the type I IFN response and antitumor immunity. Moreover, the in vivo tumor growth defect observed in METTL3 K513R mutant cells was effectively rescued by deleting key components of the DNA- and RNA-sensing pathways or blocking the IFN receptor. These findings suggest that METTL3 dimethylation–induced colorectal cancer progression relies on type I IFN–mediated tumor immune microenvironment remodeling. METTL3 has been shown to modulate the IFN response by directly regulating IFNB mRNA (58). However, our sequencing results indicate that METTL3 methylation does not affect the m6A modification of IFNB in our model.

In the treatment of colorectal cancer, most patients receive 5-FU–based chemotherapy following radical surgery to eliminate residual cancer cells. Increasing evidence indicates that chemotherapy can induce an immunosuppressive TME, leading to tumor metastasis and recurrence (59). In our model, fluorouracil enhanced the self-regulation of E2F4, which subsequently triggered SETD1A-mediated dimethylation of METTL3 at K513, which may contribute to poor chemotherapy response (such as shorter OS or PFS) or tumor recurrence in certain patients. Notably, previous studies have demonstrated that DNA-damaging agents such as 5-FU can increase repeat RNA levels, which serve as a major source of dsRNA (60). Elevated dsRNA can trigger type I IFN responses, and the activation of the SETD1A/METTL3 K513 dimethylation pathway may function as a compensatory mechanism to reduce dsRNA accumulation and suppress type I IFN responses. This represents a form of tumor adaptation that contributes to immune evasion under chemotherapeutic stress. Moreover, we selected HLM006474 to target the E2F4/SETD1A/METTL3 axis, thereby enhancing sensitivity to ICB therapy in vivo. Our study discovered an immune evasion mechanism triggered by chemotherapy. Given that HLM006474 targets the E2F family and exerts broad downstream effects, future work will focus on developing METTL3 K513–specific inhibitors for clinical translation.

In summary, we identify the E2F4/SETD1A axis as a driver of METTL3 K513 dimethylation, enabling colorectal cancer immune evasion through ERE suppression and IFN pathway inhibition. This METTL3 methylation-dependent mechanism provides a new therapeutic strategy for inhibiting tumor progression and recurrence.
