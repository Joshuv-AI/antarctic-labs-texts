Isolated pathogens, antimicrobial susceptibility, and clinical outcomes in intensive care unit patients at a tertiary hospital in Ethiopia: a retrospective cross sectional study
Authors: Eden Tesfaye Beyene, Yonas Berhane Tesfay, Loza Tesfaye Beyene, Yared Getachew Tadesse, Biniyam Tedla Mamo, Megersa Yadeta Geleta, Hanna Gulema
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a global public health threat associated with poor patient outcomes; however, there are major gaps in routine surveillance and reporting. In low-income countries like Ethiopia, antibiotics tend to be misused, particularly in intensive care units (ICU).AimTo determine the bacterial profile, antimicrobial resistance patterns, and associated clinical outcomes among ICU patients at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital (TASH), Addis Ababa.MethodsAn institution-based, six-month (May 1, 2023—October 31, 2023) retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted in the ICU of TASH on 216 patients. Data regarding sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST), and patient outcomes were collected from the health management information system (HMIS). Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27 was used for data analysis. Bivariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis were performed to assess the association between AMR and intra-ICU mortality. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsEighty nine point four percent of infections were due to Gram-negative bacteria. Of the Gram-negative isolates, the most commonly isolated pathogen was Acinetobacter baumannii (31.5%), followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae (22.7%), and Escherichia coli (14.8%). Acinetobacter baumannii was predominantly carbapenem resistant. Eighty point one percent of bacterial isolates exhibited multidrug resistance. The lowest level of resistance was observed with amikacin across all microorganisms. Bloodstream infections (BSIs) were the most frequently reported infection type. Intra-ICU mortality was determined to have a significant association with BSI (AOR 2.89, 95% CI 1.11, 3.94), septic shock (AOR 5.34, 95% CI 2.32, 12.25), CRAB infection (AOR 2.22, 95% CI 1.11, 4.43), and surgical intervention (AOR 0.46, 95% CI 0.24, 0.88).ConclusionThe study demonstrates a high burden of multi-drug resistant gram-negative infections in the ICU. The findings also show the clinical impact of AMR on mortality, particularly in critically ill patients with septic shock and BSI. The study highlights the urgent need to strengthen antimicrobial stewardship programs, enhance infection prevention and control practices, and improve microbiological surveillance systems in ICU settings.

Background

Intensive care units (ICUs) frequently experience antibiotic misuse due to the treatment of highly vulnerable patients. Most patients in the ICU are prone to undergoing invasive procedures, with the majority having indwelling devices that are excellent media for bacterial infection. All the above-mentioned factors make ICUs an epicenter for the emergence and transmission of resistant pathogens. The COVID-19 pandemic played a 96ICU [1]. This was largely attributed to the increased admission of critically ill patients, widespread use of antibiotics, and changes in infection prevention practices [2, 3], even though reported rates of co-infections and secondary infections were low [4].

Studies in the ICU have shown that the most commonly isolated organisims are Gram-negative bacteria (GNB), many of which are multidrug-resistant [5–7]. Pseudomonas aeruginosa, carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterale (CRE), carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB), and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) are frequently isolated from cultures obtained in ICUs [5, 8]. Although carbapenems are considered last-resort in many of the local health facilities, a 2023 joint report by WHO and European center for disease control and prevention (ECDC) indicates increasing resistance to carbapenems [9]. Evidence from ICU patients in 56 different countries from Europe, Asia, Africa and North America as part of the study for monitoring antimicrobial resistance trends (SMART) surveillance program specified extended spectrum beta lactamase (ESBL) positivity in 36% of the bacterial isolates [10].

A study conducted in an ICU in Addis Ababa reported Acinetobacter species as the predominant isolate, followed by Klebsiella and Escherichia coli with high resistance to cephalosporin and a mortality rate of 32% [11]. At Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital, the largest referral center in Ethiopia, antimicrobial susceptibility testing conducted in 2016 showed that 96.9% of culture-positive samples demonstrated bacterial growth, with an overall multidrug resistance (MDR) rate of 73.4% [12].

In critically ill patients admitted to the ICU, antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has been associated with increased mortality and prolonged ICU stay [13–15]; however, some studies suggest that AMR is not an independent cause of mortality, and the causal relationship remains unclear [16, 17]. AMR also has major economic implications globally. The World Bank simulated that in the optimistic low AMR scenario, the expected world output losses would exceed 1 trillion dollars by 2030 and 2 trillion dollars by 2050 [16].

Initiatives have been taken globally to address AMR and its challenges. In 2015 WHO released a global action plan on AMR [18]. In 2016 the United Nations general assembly signed a declaration and formed the interagency coordination group on AMR to improve awareness, practice, antibiotic stewardship and support for researchers involved in the development of new antimicrobials [19]. Despite these initiatives the burden of AMR in ICU acquired infections remains high especially in low- and middle-income countries like Ethiopia [20]. Despite the high burden of AMR globally and regionally, no studies in Ethiopia have examined the association between drug-resistant pathogens and ICU mortality, highlighting the need for this investigation. Therefore, the aim of this study was to assess the spectrum of bacterial isolates, antimicrobial resistance patterns, and factors associated with intra-ICU mortality among patients admitted to the ICU of Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital.

Material and methods

Study design, area, and period

An institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted in the ICU of Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital (TASH) from May 1, 2023, to October 31, 2023. TASH is one of the biggest teaching governmental tertiary teaching hospitals in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, providing highly specialized patient care for more than 50 years. The hospitalserves approximately 350,000 patients annually. The ICU comprises medical, surgical, and pediatric units managed by respective specialty team. It has 18 beds, 8 ventilator machines, and 18 monitors; however, many of these resources are not fully functional, and patients often share equipment.

Sampling procedure

Of the 345 medical record numbers recorded in the ICU registration book during the study period, 302 had complete data and were entered into a computer database and assigned unique identification numbers. Simple random sampling was then performed using Microsoft Excel to generate random numbers and select the minimum required sample size of 216 from the 302 eligible records. This approach ensured that all eligible patients had an equal probability of being selected.

Study variables

Dependent variable

Intra ICU mortality.

Independent variables

Age, comorbidities, length of stay, surgical intervention, septic shock, site of infection, isolated pathogen, drug resistant pathogen.

Laboratory procedure and identification of pathogen

Blood specimens were inoculated into blood culture bottles and incubated aerobically at 35–37 °C. Bottles were inspected daily for evidence of microbial growth. When growth was detected, subcultures were performed on blood agar, chocolate agar, and MacConkey agar plates and incubated at 35–37 °C for 18–24 h under appropriate atmospheric conditions. Blood cultures with no visible growth after seven days of incubation were reported as negative.

Non-blood specimens, including urine, pleural fluid, peritoneal fluid, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), tracheal aspirates, wound swabs, and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL), were collected aseptically and processed according to standard microbiological procedures. Urine specimens were cultured on MacConkey agar, with bacteriuria defined as growth of a single bacterial species at a concentration of ≥ 105 CFU/mL in midstream urine specimens. Sterile body fluids and CSF were inoculated onto blood agar, chocolate agar, and MacConkey agar, while wound swabs, BAL, and tracheal aspirates were cultured on blood agar, chocolate agar, and MacConkey agar as appropriate. Plates were incubated at 35–37 °C for 18–48 h under aerobic conditions, with 5% CO₂ used for fastidious organisms when indicated.

Bacterial identification was performed using standard microbiological techniques, including colony morphology, Gram staining, and biochemical tests. Gram-negative bacteria were identified using indole, triple sugar iron (TSI), urease, motility, oxidase, and nitrate reduction tests. Gram-positive bacteria were identified using catalase, coagulase, and other relevant biochemical tests in accordance with Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines 2020.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was performed using the Kirby–Bauer disc diffusion method on Mueller–Hinton agar following CLSI 2020 guidelines. The antimicrobial agents tested included ampicillin (AMP: 10 μg), trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (SXT: 1.25/23.75 μg), gentamicin (GEN: 10 μg), amikacin (AMK: 30 μg), ciprofloxacin (CIP: 5 μg), ceftazidime (CAZ: 30 μg), ceftriaxone (CRO: 30 μg), cefepime (FEP: 30 μg), meropenem (MEM: 10 μg), and ertapenem (ETP: 10 μg). Vancomycin susceptibility for Gram-positive isolates was determined using minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)-based methods in accordance with CLSI recommendations.

Data collection, management and analysis

Data were collected from the Health Management Information System (HMIS) of Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital. A trained general practitioner collected the data using a structured questionnaire. Data collection was conducted from December 15, 2023, to March 1, 2024, using an electronic data collection tool (KoboToolbox). Data quality and completeness were ensured by the principal investigator through the development of standardized data entry forms to minimize entry errors. Data inconsistencies were identified through cross-checking with primary source documents, and Microsoft Excel was used for data cleaning prior to analysis. IBM SPSS version 27 was used for data analysis. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Factors associated with clinical outcomes were assessed using bivariate logistic regression, and variables with a p-value < 0.2 were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model. Variables with a p-value < 0.05 in the multivariable analysis were considered statistically significant. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with all independent variables showing VIF values < 5.

Operational definitions

Multidrug-resistance (MDR):

Non-susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories based on disk diffusion test [21].

Septic shock:

Sepsis with persistent hypotension requiring vasopressors to maintain a mean arterial pressure of 65 mmHg and above, and elevated lactate (> 2 mmol/L) despite adequate fluid resuscitation [22].

Carbapenem resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB):

Isolates of A. baumannii that were non-susceptible (resistant or intermediate) to at least one carbapenem (e.g., imipenem or meropenem), as determined by disk diffusion and interpreted according to CLSI guidelines [21].

Incomplete data:

If information on any one of the following is missing (age, comorbidities, site of infection, length of ICU stay, surgical intervention, clinical outcome, isolated pathogen and their susceptibility pattern) from the HMIS.

Gram-negative organisms accounted for 92% of isolates in the medical ICU (MICU), 84.5% in the surgical ICU (SICU), and 91% in the pediatric ICU (PICU). Acinetobacter baumannii infection was the most common organism in both MICU (34%) and SICU (32.3%), whereas Klebsiella pneumoniae infection predominated in the PICU (36.3%). The most common Gram-positive organism was Staphylococcus aureus infection (5.6%), while Streptococcus viridans was isolated from a single sample.

Antimicrobial drug susceptibility

Multidrug resistance (MDR) was observed in 80.1% of the isolates, with the majority occurring among Gram-negative bacteria. Klebsiella pneumoniae infection isolates demonstrated more than 90% resistance to third- and fourth-generation cephalosporins. Acinetobacter baumannii infection showed high resistance to meropenem (91.2%) and a resistance rate of 69.1% to amikacin (Table 3). Overall, amikacin exhibited the lowest resistance across all tested microorganisms. In the multivariable analysis, site of infection, septic shock, CRAB infection, and surgical intervention had statistically significant association with mortality. In contrast, comorbidities, length of hospital stay, type of isolated pathogen, carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE), and multidrug-resistant (MDR) infections were not significantly associated with mortality.

Discussion

The study showed that nearly 90% of bacterial isolates were Gram-negative organisms, with Acinetobacter baumannii infection being the most commonly isolated pathogen, followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae infection. Staphylococcus aureus infection was the most prevalent Gram-positive bacterial isolate. Bloodstream infections (BSIs) constituted the most common type of infection among patients admitted to the ICU. Overall, 80% of the bacterial isolates were multidrug-resistant (MDR). The highest resistance rates were observed against third- and fourth-generation cephalosporins, whereas the lowest resistance rates were observed with amikacin across all bacterial groups.

Gram negative bacteria (GNB) can cause infection in any organ system, and due to their inherent nature, they tend to be resistant to most antibiotics [23]. The prevalence of gram-negative bacteria in this study was comparable to other studies done in Malatya, Turkey, 85% [7], Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, 69% [24], but the prevalence was much less in Calcutta, India, 50% [6], and Gondar, Ethiopia, 43.6% [25]. Such disparities may be attributed to differences in patients’ clinical profiles, infection prevention and control practices, antimicrobial stewardship implementation, and patterns of antibiotic exposure across different healthcare settings. In contrast to the findings of this study, Klebsiella pneumoniae infection was reported as the most common Gram-negative isolate in other studies [6, 10, 26] while in a few studies A. baumannii was the most frequently detected organism [7, 8]. The predominant bacterial species may vary across time and settings, as they are strongly influenced by institutional antimicrobial stewardship practices. Only 10.6% of the isolates were Gram-positive bacteria (GPB). The higher prevalence of GNB compared to GPB observed in this study is consistent with reports from ICUs in low- and middle-income countries, where healthcare-associated infections caused by Gram-negative bacilli predominate due to invasive procedures, antimicrobial selection pressure, and challenges in infection prevention and control practices [7, 24, 27].

MDR bacteria were traditionally associated with healthcare-associated infections; however, in recent years, they have become increasingly prevalent in community settings as well [28]. In this study, 80.1% of the bacterial isolates were MDR, which is substantially higher than the prevalence reported in a study conducted in Italy (56%) [29], 55.7% in Southern India [8], and 52% in Egypt [30]. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in patients’ clinical conditions, as well as variations in the type, dosage, and duration of antibiotic use. A similar MDR rate (75.5%) has been reported from Hawassa, Ethiopia [26]. A study done 2 years back in TASH showed that MDR rates were 32.19%, 15.45%, and 10.73% in the neonatal ICU, causality ward, and surgical ward, respectively [31]. Despite the availability of antimicrobial stewardship guidelines, antimicrobial stewardship programs have not been fully implemented in Ethiopia, and adherence among healthcare professionals remains limited. Trends observed in tertiary hospitals across the country have shown high antibiotic utilization rates that frequently do not comply with AMR guidelines [32, 33].

In line with findings from other studies [7, 8, 34], the prevalence of carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii infection (CRAB) in this study was notably high (91.2%). This may be attributed to the frequent use of carbapenems among critically ill patients admitted to the ICU. Amikacin (15.6%−69.1%) was found to be the most effective antimicrobial against Acinetobacter and other microbes, likely due to its limited use in the hospital. Among the Enterobacterales, Klebsiella pneumoniae infection exhibited higher antimicrobial resistance rates than Escherichia coli infection, consistent with findings from other studies [8, 35]. Staphylococcus aureus infection demonstrated high resistance to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (75%), followed by ampicillin (58.3%). Additionally, the prevalence of vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE) in this study was 40%, which was comparable to findings from other studies and suggests an increasing trend [6, 7].

In this study, 53.2% of patients had poor clinical outcomes, which is higher compared with other reports, including 19% in Indonesia, 29.6% in the Amhara Regional State, Ethiopia.

[36], 45.8% in Hawassa, Ethiopia [37]. This difference may be explained by variations in ICU setup, staffing levels, case mix, and availability of trained personnel across different settings. Evidence from various studies has also shown that BSI is independently associated with increased mortality [38, 39]. Septic shock requires urgent intervention as it leads to multi-organ failure and has a mortality of 40–50% [22, 40]. Delayed ICU admission, old age, comorbidities are strong predictors of death in ICU patients with septic shock [22, 41]. In line with other studies [14, 42], this study has determined that CRAB infection was associated with increased mortality. This may be explained by the limited availability of antimicrobial agents used to treat CRAB, such as polymyxins, in Ethiopia. The presence of MDR pathogens was not significantly associated with mortality, and previous studies have also reported inconsistent findings regarding the relationship between MDR infections and patient outcomes [13, 15, 17, 34]. Differences in the availability of antibiotics used to treat MDR infections, variations in treatment facilities, and differences in study populations may explain these inconsistencies. Medical patients are often more critically ill with complex clinical presentations compared to surgical patients, who more frequently benefit from early source control and earlier discharge from the ICU [17].

The limitations of the study include that it was retrospective and data was collected from HMIS records, so access to important data, like prior antibiotic exposure of patients, could not be obtained. It was also a single-centered study conducted in the ICU and may not be representative of the wards, emergencies in the hospital and other hospitals. The study is also cross-sectional, so causality cannot be inferred. The sample size is relatively small, limiting the statistical power for rare pathogens or outcomes. No molecular methods for resistance confirmation were used (e.g., PCR), which could underestimate other resistant pathogens. ICU interventions could not be measured, which could affect AMR patterns and mortality. The study was also a 6 month study so it may not capture seasonal variations in pathogen prevalence or AMR patterns. Certain potential predictors of mortality, such as severity scores (e.g., APACHE II, SOFA), nutritional status, and timing of interventions, could not be included as retrospective chart review was done to collect data. ICU’s equipment constraints (non-functional monitors/ventilators) may affect patient outcomes and indirectly influence AMR prevalence. Finally, the exclusion of incomplete records and cases with polymicrobial growth may have introduced selection bias.

Conclusion and recommendation

Gram-negative bacteria make up the majority of pathogens isolated. Most ICU patients harbored multidrug resistance pathogens with a high level of resistance to commonly used antimicrobials. Bloodstream infections, septic shock, CRAB infection, and non- surgical admissions were determined to have a strong association with mortality. So the hospital should employ antibiotic stewardship program including the proper selection, prescription and use of antibiotics, regular trainings for health professionals. We also recommend looking into and strictly following infection prevention and control measures with routine surveillance and reporting of antimicrobial susceptibility.
