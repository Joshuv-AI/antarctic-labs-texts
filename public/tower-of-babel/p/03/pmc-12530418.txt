Clinical outcomes, cost-effectiveness and implementation of individualised physiotherapy using the Specific Treatment Of Problems of the Spine (STOPS) approach for chronic low back pain in Nigeria: a protocol for a prospective sequential clinical trial
Authors: Musa Sani Danazumi, Jon Ford, Bashir Kaka, Andrew Hahne
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDespite advances in the physiotherapy management of low back pain (LBP) worldwide, studies indicate that there is a large variation in the quality of physiotherapy management for LBP in Nigeria. This clinical trial aims to evaluate the implementation of individualised physiotherapy for chronic LBP in Nigeria using the Specific Treatment Of Problems of the Spine (STOPS) approach.Methods and analysisThis will be an implementation clinical trial using a non-randomised, prospective, sequential comparison design. One hundred and fifty-four participants with chronic LBP will be recruited at one hospital in Nigeria. In phase I, participants will receive 11 sessions of usual physiotherapy care. In phase II, consenting physiotherapists will undergo training in the implementation of the STOPS treatment approach, led by the original Australian developers. In phase III, participants will receive 11 sessions of individualised physiotherapy using the STOPS approach. Patient evaluation in phases I and III will be measured at baseline and at 5, 10, 26 and 52 weeks, and will evaluate the clinical outcomes (including primary outcomes of pain intensity measured with a 0–10 Numerical Rating Scale, and activity limitation measured via the Oswestry) and cost-effectiveness from the healthcare perspective (incremental healthcare costs relative to incremental quality adjusted life-years gained on the EuroQOL-5D-5L). Physiotherapist outcomes will include confidence in treating LBP and implementation behaviour measured at the start of phase I and at the end of phases I, II and III. Implementation feasibility and qualitative analysis of patient and physiotherapist experiences will also be explored.Ethics and disseminationThe trial has been approved by the Human Research Ethics Committees of La Trobe University, Australia (HEC22278) and Federal Medical Centre (FMC) Nguru, Yobe State, Nigeria (FMC/N/CL.SERV/355/VOL IV/131). The study results will be shared through peer-reviewed journals and conferences.Trial registration numberPACTR202305707317550.

Introduction

Low back pain (LBP) continues to be a major global public health problem and a challenge to international health systems.1 It is estimated that 245.9 million cases of LBP occur each year globally, and that between 80% and 90% of individuals will experience LBP at some point in their lifetime.2 In Nigeria, estimates of the annual prevalence of LBP range from 32.5% to 73.5%,3 which is comparable to annual prevalence estimates reported across Africa.4 Chronic LBP (CLBP), where pain persists for 3 months or more, accounts for most of the treatment and economic costs attributable to low back disorders.5

Identifying universal treatment strategies to mitigate LBP is challenging due to the multifactorial nature of the condition.6 7 To date, there is no single best treatment option for managing LBP,6 even though some treatments have demonstrated effectiveness.811 Conservative non-pharmacological care, such as physiotherapy, is now the preferred management approach.7 12 In Nigeria, physiotherapy treatment for LBP is readily available13 14 and has focused on back-care education, electrotherapy, manual therapy, exercise therapy and the McKenzie approach.15 16 Previous studies suggest that physiotherapy care for LBP in Nigeria is discordant with international guidelines,1416 and a recent survey has shown that promising new treatment approaches, such as individualised or stratified physiotherapy, are not recognised or used by physiotherapists in Nigeria.17 This may be due to a lack of expertise in these treatment approaches in Nigeria, meaning that training of physiotherapists in LBP management by senior clinicians and undergraduate and postgraduate educators is not incorporating the latest evidence-based treatment approaches.17 Additionally, ingrained preferences for passive treatment modalities by therapists and patients may have been a further barrier to date that has hindered the implementation of individualised physiotherapy in Nigeria.17

The Specific Treatment Of Problems of the Spine (STOPS) is a method of individualised physiotherapy that incorporates a biopsychosocial model of care.18 The basic premise of the STOPS approach is that LBP is multifactorial, and treatment needs to target each patient’s individual biological, psychological and social factors that cause or contribute to sustaining their low back condition.18 Based on this philosophy, the STOPS approach was developed1822 and has been shown to be effective102327 and cost-effective28 for early persistent LBP when implemented by trained physiotherapists in Australia.

While other individualised or stratified approaches such as cognitive functional therapy9 and the STarT Back stratified care model8 have also shown promise, we considered the STOPS approach well-suited to implementation in other countries (including low- and middle-income countries) given it draws on a range of treatment strategies that many physiotherapists around the world are already familiar with.17 29 30 Therefore, training of physiotherapists in the STOPS approach focusses on upskilling in biopsychosocial assessment and structured clinical reasoning, enabling a system to design an individualised treatment programme for each patient that matches their presenting problems.

The STOPS approach to individualised physiotherapy has not yet been evaluated outside of Australia. This clinical trial protocol outlines a planned evaluation of the STOPS approach to individualised physiotherapy for treating CLBP in one hospital setting in Nigeria. This will include a comprehensive evaluation of patient outcomes, cost-effectiveness, physiotherapist implementation behaviours and implementation feasibility. A single-centre design will enable an initial evaluation of implementation feasibility and outcomes to determine whether wider evaluation is warranted.

An overview of the study design.

This study design was suited to an initial implementation clinical trial, given only one lead physiotherapist from one hospital in Nigeria was available to travel to Australia for intensive training. This non-randomised design has been used previously in other implementation studies,31 32 although it does introduce the opportunity for systematic differences between patients presenting in each treatment phase. Other potential designs, such as a cluster-randomised or stepped wedge designs, were deemed unsuitable for a single centre without cross-contamination between patients or therapists.

Trial compliance

The clinical trial protocol has been registered with the Pan African Clinical Trial Registry (PACTR202305707317550) and will be reported in accordance with the Standards for Reporting Implementation Studies (StaRI) statement33 and the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDier) checklist.34

Patient and public involvement

There was no formal patient or public involvement in the design of the trial. However, a survey study of current physiotherapy treatment practices across all states of Nigeria was undertaken to understand the usual care for LBP in Nigeria prior to implementing the trial.17

Trial setting

The trial will be conducted in the outpatient physiotherapy department of Federal Medical Centre Nguru, Yobe State, Nigeria. This is a tertiary hospital with over 800 beds that is under the care of the Nigerian Federal Ministry of Health. The centre employs physiotherapists who treat patients with LBP in the outpatient department.

Physiotherapists’ eligibility criteria

Registered physiotherapists in Nigeria who are currently involved in LBP management and working at FMC Nguru, Yobe State, Nigeria, will provide the treatment in phases I and III. To be eligible, physiotherapists must be willing to participate in all three phases of the trial, including the completion of all training requirements.

Patient selection and recruitment

Patients presenting to the outpatient physiotherapy department at Federal Medical Centre, Nigeria, for treatment of CLBP will be invited to participate. Additional patients will also be sought via social media advertising and public notices. Participants recruited from different sources, both naturally presenting and those sought through advertising, will be analysed for any differences in presenting characteristics or outcomes. Participants will be advised verbally and via the ethically approved Participant Information Form that their physiotherapy treatment will be the same whether they participate in the trial or not, and they will not be disadvantaged if they choose not to enrol in the trial. Emphasis will be placed on voluntary participation, and sufficient time will be given to consider involvement. Eligibility criteria are summarised in box 1. Eligible patients will be invited to participate and will sign an informed consent form before being enrolled.

Blinding

All participants in phases I and III will be blinded to the study hypothesis. They will not be told that they are receiving usual care or a new treatment approach and will not be told that two treatment options are being compared in the trial. They will be informed that they will receive physiotherapy in accordance with the treatment approach being delivered by the physiotherapists at FMC at that time. This will minimise bias from patient expectation effects. Physiotherapists cannot be blinded in the study. The data analyst will be provided with grouped data without group labels, to blind them to the treatment group. The PhD student’s dual role as PhD student and lead physiotherapist supervising the other therapists at the study site may introduce opportunity for bias. However, this reflects the nature of practice at that centre, whereby the physiotherapists are largely influenced by the assessment and treatment instructions of the lead physiotherapist. To mitigate this risk as much as possible, the PhD student will not be involved in outcome assessment of patients or therapists, and multiple other therapists will be involved in the treatment of patients.

Phase I: usual physiotherapy care in Nigeria

In phase 1, physiotherapists at Federal Medical Centre Nigeria will provide treatment for participants with CLBP in accordance with their usual physiotherapy practice. The trial does not require the physiotherapists to follow any set treatment protocol; hence, the physiotherapists will be free to choose the treatment approach they wish to. Participants will attend 10×45 min physiotherapy sessions over a 10-week period, with an 11th session scheduled for 6 months after their commencement in the study to review progress.

Phase III: implementation and evaluation of individualised physiotherapy in Nigeria

In phase III, physiotherapists will apply the STOPS approach for individualised treatment of LBP as introduced in phase II. A new cohort of participants who present for treatment during phase III will be invited to participate in the study by their physiotherapist. Consenting participants will undergo a comprehensive assessment in their first session and then receive 10×45 min sessions of individualised physiotherapy treatment over a 10-week period, with an 11th session scheduled for 6 months post-study commencement to review progress. Potential contamination between phases will be minimised by ensuring that phase I patients re-presenting during phase III are not reinvited to participate.

Treatment will be based on the principles outlined in the STOPS trial treatment protocols.1822 The original STOPS protocols targeted people with early persistent pain and had a substantial emphasis on pathoanatomical factors within a biopsychosocial model.20 For the purposes of the current trial targeting a chronic pain population, treatment methods will be adjusted to target additional psychological and social barriers relevant to this population.

For each patient presenting in phase III, a comprehensive patient assessment will be completed by the PhD candidate physiotherapist to guide the development of an individualised treatment programme targeting plausible causal mechanisms. The assessment will aim to identify the following features on which treatment can be individualised:

Predominant pain type (nociplastic, neuropathic or nociceptive) using validated assessment criteria.3538

Implementation trial outcome measures

Physiotherapists’ evaluation

Physiotherapists will complete self-administered questionnaires that will be administered electronically and filled in by the physiotherapists at the end of phase I, the end of phase II and the end of phase III. Physiotherapists will return completed questionnaires to the researchers via email at each time point. The following outcomes will be evaluated:

Self-confidence: The self-confidence of physiotherapists towards clinical management of LBP will be assessed using the Practitioner Self-Confidence Scale (PCS).46 The PCS has four items that are scored on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 to 5 points (strongly agree ‘1 point’ to strongly disagree ‘5 points’ for items 1–3, and very well ‘1 point’ to very poorly ‘5 points’ for item 4). The PCS has a total score of 20, where 4 represents the greatest self-confidence and 20 represents the lowest self-confidence towards clinical management of participants with LBP.46 The PCS is reliable47 and has been used to evaluate trial implementation outcomes.4850

A score over 50 can be interpreted as a facilitator of the implementation process and a score below 50 can be interpreted as a barrier to the implementation process.52 The DIBQ-t is validated52 and has been used to evaluate trial implementation outcomes.48 53

Secondary outcomes

A range of secondary outcomes will be evaluated, including global effect, patient satisfaction, quality of life, work interference, sleep quality, pain self-efficacy, pain catastrophising and mental health symptoms (table 2 and online supplemental appendix 3).

Semistructured interviews of patients and therapists

A qualitative study of physiotherapists’ and patients’ experiences will be conducted after the completion of the usual care (phase I) and STOPS individualised physiotherapy treatment (phase III). Individual semistructured interviews will be conducted by a different researcher who has experience in qualitative interviews but was not involved in treating patients in the study. Interviews will be voice-recorded and transcribed verbatim, before undergoing thematic analysis. All discussions will be directed by a flexible topic guide (online supplemental appendix 4) developed from the pertinent literature.14 51 59 The topic guide comprises semistructured non-leading questions phrased in clear language. Data collection will continue until thematic saturation is reached, defined as the point at which no new themes emerge from subsequent interviews. To ensure diversity of perspectives, purposive sampling with maximum variation will be employed, considering factors such as age, gender, treatment phase and previous treatment experience.

Adverse events

Continuous clinical assessment of adverse effects (harmful or unpleasant) of treatment will be monitored by treating physiotherapists and recorded in their participant clinical notes, per their standard clinical practice. Physiotherapists will routinely enquire with participants each session about their response to treatment and any adverse effects of treatment or prescribed exercises. Treating physiotherapists will modify treatment based on any adverse reactions and will refer for additional care at the Federal Medical Centre if required.

Treatment adherence

The number of treatment sessions attended by each patient will be recorded by the physiotherapists. Patient compliance with the treatment will be reviewed by the physiotherapists at each treatment session via direct questioning and by reviewing the patient’s exercise charts,10 18 with the therapist rating the patients compliance on a scale of 1–10 each session. Patient-reported compliance will be assessed with a question on each follow-up questionnaire: ‘on a scale of 0–10, how adherent have you been with following the advice and exercises recommended by your physiotherapist on average over the last month (from “not at all” to “fully”)’.

Data management

Personal or identifiable participant information will be kept in medical records at the Federal Medical Centre, Nigeria. Physical data (eg, questionnaires) will be labelled only with trial identification codes to maintain confidentiality and will be securely kept in locked cabinets separately to any identifying information. All data will be entered into password-protected computers of the researchers, along with scanned copies of paper-based questionnaires for auditing and archiving purposes. Random audits will be conducted to ensure accurate data entry consistent with the scanned questionnaires. Deidentified electronic data spreadsheets will be accessible only by the research team through a secure organisational share drive. Trial monitoring (eg, recruitment and follow-up rates, review of compliance, review of any reported adverse events) will be undertaken at fortnightly meetings involving the research team members. As a low-risk study not involving medications or medical devices, and implementing common physiotherapy treatment modalities, a data monitoring committee will not be necessary. Any protocol changes will be reported to the ethics committees and reflected on the clinical trial registry. On trial completion, deidentified data will be available on receipt of reasonable request by the Human Research Ethics Committee of La Trobe University, Australia (ethics number: HEC22278) (email: humanethics@latrobe.edu.au).

Planned analysis

Data will be analysed using IBM SPSS-V.29.0 (SPSS) and Microsoft Excel (2010; Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, Washington, USA). No interim analyses are planned.

Sample size estimation

The sample size was estimated using G*Power V.3.1.1 software. The overall sample size to detect a between-phase standardised mean effect size (d) of at least 0.5 using a t-test of difference between two independent means, a two-tailed hypothesis, probability level (α) of 0.05, power (p) of 0.8 and allocation ratio (N2/N1) of 1, yielded 128 (64 per phases I and III). Accounting for a worst-case scenario of a 20% attrition rate requires the total sample size to be increased to 154 participants (77 per phases I and III).60 An effect size of this magnitude is consistent with the previous STOPS trial,10 making it a feasible target. The number of physiotherapists will not be able to be predetermined, as it is limited to the number of consenting and eligible physiotherapists working at the study centre at the time of the study. All eligible physiotherapists will be invited to participate, but it may not be feasible to train and include them all. To mitigate therapist attrition during this multi-year study, a minimum of 10 physiotherapists will be trained initially in the STOPS approach, with the option to train more if required after commencement of the trial.

Analysis of physiotherapists’ outcomes

A descriptive summary of the characteristics of the physiotherapists will be provided at baseline. Additionally, continuous data (self-confidence and implementation behaviour) will be analysed using linear mixed models to determine the differences between phases I, II and III.

Analysis of patients’ clinical outcomes (treatment efficacy)

A descriptive summary of the characteristics of the patients will be provided at baseline. Continuous outcomes for patients will be analysed using linear mixed models (treatment×time interaction, with time modelled as a repeated measure) adjusting for baseline score. Given that multiple physiotherapists will deliver the intervention in phases I and II, patient outcomes may be influenced by therapist-specific factors. To evaluate this potential clustering effect, we will include 'primary therapist' as a random effect in our linear mixed models if it improves model fit. Ordinal outcomes will be analysed using the Mann-Whitney U test, while the risk ratio and number needed to treat will be evaluated using χ2 analyses for dichotomous data. Analyses will focus on detecting between-group effects (with 95% CIs) at each time point (5, 10, 26 and 52 weeks). Participants will also be dichotomised according to whether they achieve the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) on outcome measures or not. The MCID for participants will be defined as 10/100 points for the Oswestry, 2/10 for the NRS pain scales, at least ‘much improved’ on the global rating of change scale, and ‘very satisfied’ on the treatment satisfaction scales. Given that these values may be too low in some contexts, the analyses will be repeated using a threshold of 50% reduction in Oswestry and NRS pain scores.10 18 All data will be analysed according to intention-to-treat analysis, whereby missing data for any reason (skipped question, participant dropout or loss to follow-up) will be handled via maximum likelihood estimation within linear mixed models.47

Cost-effectiveness

Healthcare costs including prescribed treatment and any co-interventions for each participant will be recorded from the date of the first enrolment to the 6-month follow-up. Healthcare resource utilisation will be obtained using standardised follow-up questionnaires completed by participants at baseline, 5, 10 and 26-week post-enrolment. A within-study cost-utility analysis will be conducted from the healthcare perspective and all costs will be valued based on Nigerian government charges and will be reported in Naira (and converted to other currencies as required for publication).

All analyses will be via intention to treat, with imputation used for missing data. First, partially missing information relating to healthcare resource utilisation will be replaced with relevant averages (eg, if a patient indicated that they consumed diclofenac but did not indicate the dosage, then the average dose will be imputed from all other patients in that treatment group at that time point). Fully missing data (such as patients who failed to return a questionnaire, missed an item of the EuroQol or failed to answer a complete question) will be handled via multiple imputations (five imputed datasets).61 62

Between-group differences (phase I vs phase III) in costs will be calculated via linear mixed models. For health outcomes, each patient’s time-weighted average EuroQol utility score across the 6-month study period will be calculated via the area under the curve method.63 This will yield health outcomes expressed in quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), which indicates the cost to gain one additional year of life spent in perfect health.64 The mean between-group difference in QALYs will then be derived from a linear mixed model adjusting for baseline scores, using the multiple imputed datasets. The mean between-group differences in healthcare and work costs will also be derived from linear mixed models based on the imputed datasets. These analyses will yield incremental costs and incremental health benefits. The Incremental Cost Effectiveness Ratio (ICER) will then be derived by dividing the incremental cost by the incremental QALYs, which indicates the cost to gain one additional QALY.61 64

To assess uncertainty around the ICER, non-parametric bootstrapping will be applied using a customised Microsoft Excel spreadsheet.65 This will involve generating 5000 randomly resampled data sets (1000 samples for each of the five imputed data sets). Each of the 5000 bootstrapped cost-utility pairs will then be graphed on the cost-effectiveness plane.31 64 66 A cost-effectiveness acceptability curve will then be generated to determine the probability that the STOPS treatment approach for LBP is cost-effective compared with usual care.

Analysis of qualitative data

The qualitative data will be analysed using interpretive description.67 The interview documents will be read several times by the investigators to understand the texts and the meaning of the content according to the local context. In the next step, the documents will be combined into meaningful and understandable units which will be labelled as codes. The coding process will be performed by two investigators separately, then compared and discussed, and a consensus will be reached within the research team. In the third step, the codes will be synthesised in detail and will be grouped into meaningful subcategories and labelled with appropriate titles. In the fourth step, subcategories will be merged into broader categories which will finally be pooled into major emerging themes.

Discussion

This prospective sequential comparison clinical trial will evaluate the implementation of the STOPS approach to individualising physiotherapy treatment for people with CLBP in Nigeria. With a recent practice survey showing that less than 5% of physiotherapists in Nigeria are familiar with or apply individualised or stratified treatment for CLBP, 17 this country provides a greenfield site for an implementation study. This comprehensive evaluation of patient and therapist outcomes and experiences, along with cost-effectiveness and implementation feasibility, will determine whether wider implementation studies involving the STOPS approach to individualised physiotherapy for LBP are warranted in Nigeria or other countries. The recruitment process started in August 2023, and the trial is expected to be completed in 2026.

As an initial implementation study in a single centre in Nigeria, the generalisability of our findings will be limited to the specific characteristics of the participant population, healthcare setting and available resources. These factors reduce the applicability of the findings to other contexts or health systems, particularly those with different organisational structures, patient demographics or resources. In addition, the availability of only one treatment centre for an initial implementation study precludes the ability to randomise patients to the two intervention arms without risk of contamination between groups, creating the potential for systematic differences between patients attending for treatment in each phase. It will also be important to consider the potential for variation in patient compliance and physiotherapist treatment fidelity, both of which are being measured in the study. Finally, we also recognise the limited blinding possibilities, particularly for treating physiotherapists, which may influence their implementation behaviours and engagement with the intervention in a clinical trial setting.

Resistance to change in clinical practice is a known barrier to implementation of new approaches. Although our recent survey shows that Nigerian physiotherapists are willing to learn new assessment and treatment methods for managing LBP, 17 there may be resistance to shifting from usual practice to a highly individualised evidence-based model of care. Any such concerns will be explored through qualitative interviews with physiotherapists involved in the trial and implementation feasibility metrics. Findings will inform the barriers and feasibility of expanding the implementation of this approach across more sites in Nigeria or in other low- and middle-income countries.
