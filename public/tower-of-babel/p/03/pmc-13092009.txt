Impact of gut microbiome, plasma metabolites, peripheral immune cells, and circulating inflammatory protein on chronic spontaneous urticaria: Bidirectional 2-sample Mendelian randomization study and mediation analysis
Authors: Lijun Deng, Yuxu Yao, Qian Wang, Zhenzhong Lu, Enze Li, Ying Hu, Jialu Chen, Guoli Wang, Tingting Yu, Jiang Ji, Qingqing Jiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGrowing evidence links gut microbiota to chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU), yet causality and underlying mediators remain unclear.ObjectiveWe investigated causal relationships and potential mediators—specifically plasma metabolites, immune cells, and inflammatory proteins—through which gut microbes influence CSU risk.MethodsWe applied 2-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) to genome-wide association study (GWAS) data on 430 gut microbial taxa, 1,400 plasma metabolites, 731 immune cell traits, and 91 inflammatory proteins. Analyses used inverse variance weighted, MR-Egger, weighted median, and weighted mode estimators, complemented by sensitivity, mediation, multivariable MR, and Bayesian colocalization tests for shared causal variants.ResultsEight gut taxa, 79 metabolites, 25 immune cell phenotypes, and 3 inflammatory proteins showed suggestive associated with CSU (all false discovery rate > .05). Galactonate mediated the effect of Paraprevotella on CSU, while N-acetyl leucine mediated the protective effect of Bacteroides; only the latter remained significant in multivariable MR. Immune cells and inflammatory proteins showed no significant mediation. Bayesian colocalization provided no evidence of shared causal variants between CSU and any of the 4 trait categories.ConclusionGut microbiota may suggestively influence CSU risk via specific metabolite pathways, particularly N-acetyl leucine, though colocalization did not support shared genetic causality and no association survived multiple testing correction at false discovery rate < .05. These findings offer hypothesis-generating insights and candidate targets for further functional validation.

Chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) is characterized by persistent itching and wheals lasting over 6 weeks. It significantly impairs quality of life, work productivity, and health care.1, 2, 3 Compared to healthy individuals, CSU patients show distinct gut microbiota profiles and lower diversity.4, 5, 6, 7 Nabizadeh et al4 reported reduced Akkermansia muciniphila, Clostridium leptum, and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii in CSU. Wang et al8 found higher Lactobacillus, Turicibacter, and Lachnobacterium and lower Phascolarctobacterium in CSU patients, potentially promoting disease via the G protein-coupled receptor. Gut microbiome changes in CSU may lower short-chain fatty acids and raise lipopolysaccharide levels, promoting mast cell–driven skin inflammation.6,9 Additionally, patients receiving combined probiotic and antihistamine therapy may experience a higher reduction rate in itch, the number of urticaria, total Urticaria Activity Score (as measured by UAS7), and significant improvements in quality of life (DLQI), Physicians Global Assessment Visual Analog Score (PhyGA-VAS), and Chronic Urticaria Quality of Life Questionnaire (CU-Q2oL).10,11 Despite these insights, observational studies cannot avoid issues of reverse causation and confounding, while MR analysis can effectively circumvent these problems.

Research suggests a strong association between CSU and metabolic syndrome, particularly in lipid and fatty acid metabolism.12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17 Kobayashi16 found significant increase in serum lipid and fatty acid levels between CSU patients and healthy controls, which disappeared after treatment. Moreover, antioxidants such as pantothenic acid, glutathione, and ascorbic acid can alleviate CSU symptoms.16 The research of Jian et al18 identified 42 distinct metabolites in CSU patients, including reduced N6-acetyl-l-lysine, malonic acid, and vitamin C, and elevated l-aspartic acid. In vivo and in vitro experiments confirmed that the addition of pyruvate or malonic acid significantly reduced IgE-induced mast cell activation.18 Additionally, metabolites in plasma such as short-chain fatty acids, tryptophan, amino acids, vitamins, and bile acids are involved in the modulation of innate and adaptive immunity by the gut microbiota.19, 20, 21 However, these designs cannot disentangle reverse causation (disease or medication may change metabolism) or confounding by diet, drugs, or lifestyle. We therefore used 2-sample Mendelian randomization to establish causal direction from microbe → metabolite → CSU, and 2-step mediation MR to quantify the proportion of the total microbial effect that is transmitted through specific plasma metabolites—an advance that correlation-based metabolome studies have never achieved. This moves the field from “what is altered” to “what drives CSU pathogenesis, and how much.”

As a chronic inflammatory disease, CSU is characterized by increased numbers of mast cells, eosinophils, and basophils in skin lesions, together with upregulated IL-4, IL-13, TNF-α, and other cytokines.22, 23, 24, 25, 26 These local changes are functionally mirrored by measurable quantities in peripheral blood: Basophil absolute counts indicate the circulating granulocyte pool available for skin recruitment and mediator release,27 whereas surface CD203c/CD63 mean fluorescence intensity rises logarithmically on IgE cross-linking and correlates with histamine release.28 Eosinophil side-scatter reflects granule density, whereas CCR3 (CD193) mean fluorescence intensity increases after stimulation and parallels cationic protein release.29,30 Because these peripheral immune phenotypes are heritable and occur among individuals, we used MR to test whether quantifiable immune traits—reflecting, for example, circulating basophil reserve, B-cell autoantibody potential, and dendritic cell interferon bias—exert causal influences on CSU risk, moving from descriptive association to mechanistic hypotheses.

Mendelian randomization (MR), an epidemiologic approach that uses genetic variants as instrumental variables, evaluates causality between exposures and outcomes.31, 32, 33 These genetic variants, fixed at conception, minimize environmental confounding and reverse causality.32 Two-sample MR analysis relies on 2 separate GWAS datasets from the same ethnic group without sample overlap.31 Mediation analysis assesses the effect of exposure on outcome through mediators.34 Thus, using extensive public GWAS summary data, we performed 2-sample and 2-step mediation MR analyses to assess the causal links between the gut microbiome, peripheral immune cells, circulating inflammatory proteins, and CSU; and to investigate the potential mediating roles of plasma metabolites, peripheral immune cells, and circulating inflammatory proteins from the gut microbiota to CSU.

Methods

Data source

Gut microbiome data were sourced from the GWAS Catalog (www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas). The study covered 430 taxonomic groups of microbes across various hierarchical levels, from phylum to genus (including 5 phyla, 10 classes, 17 orders, 21 families, and 38 genera). The research included studies ranging from IDs GCST90011301 to GCST90011730, encompassing a total of 8,956 participants across 5 cohorts.35 The identification of gut microbes was conducted by 16S rRNA gene sequencing, and the exploration of their genetic associations was achieved through single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) microarray technology.

Plasma metabolomics data, also extracted from the GWAS Catalog, documented 1,091 metabolites and 309 metabolic ratios from 8,299 Europeans36 under the study IDs GCST90199621 to GCST90201020. These data comprehensively profile 850 known metabolites, which encompass lipids, amino acids, xenobiotics, nucleotides, vitamins, carbohydrates, peptides, and energy compounds, alongside a subset of metabolites that are either partially identified or remain to be characterized.

Peripheral immune cell data, involving 3,757 Europeans,37 was analyzed by flow cytometry under study IDs ranging from GCST0001391 to GCST0002121. The findings encompassed 118 absolute cell counts, 389 median fluorescence intensities, 192 relative cell counts, and 32 morphologic parameters. Circulating inflammatory protein data were derived from Olink platform analyses, focusing on 91 proteins from 1,484 participants of European descent.38 This information is also archived in the GWAS Catalog, with study IDs spanning GCST90274758 to GCST90274848.

Data on CSU were obtained from the R12 version of the FinnGen biobank (ID L12_URTICA_IDIOPAT),39 which was updated on November 19, 2024; the dataset comprises 744 patients and 482,901 controls of European ancestry. The FinnGen study is a large-scale genomics initiative that has analyzed over 500,000 Finnish biobank samples and correlated genetic variation with health data to understand disease mechanisms and predispositions.

Study design

The study, as shown in the flowchart in Fig 1, began with a 2-sample MR analysis to investigate causal links among the gut microbiome, plasma metabolites, peripheral immune cells, circulating inflammatory proteins, and CSU. We then applied a 2-step MR analysis to identify the mediating roles of significant metabolites, immune cells, and inflammatory proteins in the gut–microbiome–CSU relationship, followed by multivariable MR (MVMR) and Bayesian colocalization to examine shared causal variants. SNPs were used as instrumental variables (IVs), which were based on MR’s assumptions:33 (1) close IV-exposure relationship, (2) IV-confounder independence, and (3) IV-outcome indirect influence solely via exposure.Fig 1Study design and flowchart. MR’s 3 core presuppositions are: (1) chosen SNPs have robust link to exposure of interest; (2) chosen SNPs are not associated with outcomes beyond their relationship with exposure; and (3) chosen SNPs are not connected to any possible confounding variables.

IV selection

In our study, we used strict criteria for selecting IVs, including only those with genome-wide significance (P < 1 × 10−5). To address linkage disequilibrium, we set an r2 threshold of <.001 within a 10,000 kb window and applied clumping to the variants. Additionally, all palindromic and ambiguously annotated SNPs were excluded to avoid potential issues in MR analysis.

We evaluated the strength of the IVs by the F statistic, which reflects the variance in exposure explained by the SNPs. The formula is F = [(N − K − 1)/K]/[R2/(1 − R2)], where K is the number of genetic variants and N is the sample size. By excluding IVs with an F statistic of <10, we ensured the robustness of our results (see Tables E1 and E2 in the Online Repository available at www.jaci-global.org).

Statistical analysis

Two-sample MR analysis

The MR approach was utilized to evaluate the causal relationships between the gut microbiome, plasma metabolites, peripheral immune cells, circulating inflammatory proteins, and CSU. We used the inverse variance weighted (IVW), MR-Egger, weighted median, and weighted mode methods to infer causality. The IVW method was prioritized for its typically highest statistical power, with the other methods serving as complementary analyses.

In sensitivity analyses, the MR-Egger intercept evaluates genetic pleiotropy; a significant result (P < .05) implies potential horizontal pleiotropy. The Cochran Q test identifies heterogeneity in causal estimates; a significant P < .05 suggests heterogeneity. When heterogeneity is found, MR-PRESSO software adjusts estimates by excluding outliers. Leave-one-out analyses also assess sensitivity.

To address the increased risk of type I error due to multiple comparisons, we used the Benjamini-Hochberg method to adjust the false discovery rate (FDR) for our primary IVW results. We considered an association statistically significant only if the FDR-adjusted q value (hereafter p_fdr) was <.05. Associations with PIVW < .05 but p_fdr ≥ .05 were interpreted as suggestive and hypothesis generating only. Notably, no association in our univariable MR analyses met the p_fdr < .05 threshold. The corresponding p_fdr values are reported in Tables E3 to E6 in the Online Repository available at www.jaci-global.org; footnotes in the Online Repository materials direct readers to these corrected estimates.

All computations were conducted by R v4.3.2 software, with the TwoSampleMR and MR-PRESSO packages facilitating the statistical assessments.

Reverse MR analysis

To assess the causal impact of CSU on gut microbiota (PIVW < .05), a reverse MR analysis was performed by using SNPs associated with CSU as IVs, with CSU as the exposure and gut microbial taxa as the outcome (see Table E7 in the Online Repository available at www.jaci-global.org). The reverse MR follows the same procedure as the standard MR.

Mediation analysis

To explore mediating causal relationships, we conducted a 2-step MR analysis using CSU-associated plasma metabolites, immune cells, and inflammatory factors as mediators (see Table E9 in the Online Repository available at www.jaci-global.org). We assessed their mediating effects between the gut microbiome and CSU by the product of coefficients method. We calculated β1 (gut microbiome’s effect on the mediator) and β2 (mediator’s effect on CSU), and derived the mediating effect as β1 × β2. The total effect was obtained from the MR analysis of the gut microbiome on CSU, with the direct effect calculated by subtracting the mediating effect from the total effect. The mediation proportion was calculated as [(mediating effect/total effect) × 100%], and we estimated the 95% confidence intervals using the delta method. We consider metabolites, immune cells, or inflammatory proteins that exist in a triangular relationship and have a significant mediating effect to potentially play a mediating role in the influence of the gut microbiome on CSU. When heterogeneity was present, we used MR-PRESSO MR estimates for mediating effects.

MVMR and colocalization analysis

To examine whether the identified mediation effects are robust to correlated exposures, we performed MVMR by simultaneously including both the microbial trait (Paraprevotella or Bacteroides) and the corresponding metabolite (galactonate or N-acetyl leucine) as exposures, with CSU as the outcome. MVMR estimates were obtained by the IVW-MVMR estimator, and heterogeneity was assessed by Cochran Q test.

To examine whether any of the 4 key traits (Paraprevotella prevalence, Bacteroides abundance, galactonate levels, and N-acetyl leucine levels) share the same causal variant with CSU, we performed 4 independent Bayesian colocalization analyses (coloc.abf) using a prior of 1 × 10−4; H4 > 0.7 was considered evidence of a shared causal variant.

Results

Notably, in the univariable MR analyses, none of the associations between gut microbiota, plasma metabolites, immune cell traits, or inflammatory proteins and CSU reached statistical significance after correction for multiple testing using a FDR threshold of <.05. All reported associations with PIVW < .05 had p_fdr > .05, indicating suggestive rather than definitive evidence. These negative findings underscore the complexity of CSU etiology and highlight the need for larger-scale studies.

Discussion

We integrated large-scale GWAS data to explore causal links between gut microbiota, plasma metabolites, immune cells, and inflammatory markers with CSU. Mendelian randomization identified 8 microbial taxa, 79 plasma metabolites, 25 immune cell types, and 3 inflammatory markers associated with disease risk. Mediation analysis further implicated galactonate (16% of total effect) and N-acetyl leucine (11%) as transducers of Paraprevotella and Bacteroides influences, respectively. However, these mediation proportions are relatively low, and the wide confidence intervals indicate substantial uncertainty in these estimates. In addition, MVMR indicated that only the N-acetyl leucine pathway remains robust when correlated exposures are modeled jointly, while colocalization provided no support for shared causal variants, highlighting the need for larger, multiancestry samples and functional validation before pursuing these metabolites as intervention targets. Therefore, interpretations of our findings should be cautious and not definitive.

The loss of the galactonate pathway in MVMR reflects high genetic collinearity between Paraprevotella and its metabolite; once their shared variance is partitioned, the independent microbial effect shrinks toward zero. This illustrates a limitation of simultaneous estimation when exposure and mediator are tightly coupled. This does not necessarily negate the 2-step finding; rather, it illustrates a limitation of MVMR when exposure and mediator are tightly genetically coupled. Two-step MR remains the classical approach precisely because it allows the intermediate to be tested without forcing simultaneous adjustment for its upstream correlate, thereby preserving statistical power for mediation detection.

Likewise, the absence of colocalization (none of the 4 traits reached the H4 > 0.7 threshold) does not contradict the MR evidence, but it does temper claims of a single druggable site and underscores the need for larger, multiancestry GWAS and functional follow-up before prioritizing these metabolites as intervention targets.

It is important to note that the mediation analysis relies on several strong assumptions, including the absence of unmeasured confounding between the exposure, mediator, and outcome, and the absence of mediator–outcome confounders affected by the exposure. Functional experiments have not been performed, and the wide confidence intervals of the mediated proportions imply that additional, unquantified pathways are likely to contribute. Future work should therefore confirm these assumptions with in vitro mast cell or T-cell assays, knockout models, or randomized dietary interventions.

We speculate that a high abundance of Bacteroides and Paraprevotella might confer protection against CSU, whereas an elevated F-Lachnospiraceae abundance could, in theory, increase risk. Previous observational data are inconsistent: Decreased Bacteroides has been reported in CSU patients,40 while the findings on F-Lachnospiraceae have been inconsistent across various studies.41,42 Such discrepancies likely reflect confounding, reverse causation, or population differences inherent to cross-sectional designs. Although our MR estimates are consistent with a causal role for these taxa, functional proof—such as gnotobiotic CSU models or controlled supplementation trials—is currently lacking. Likewise, the finding that Lachnospiraceae abundance predicts antihistamine response43 remains associational; prospective studies and mechanistic experiments are required before these bacteria can be considered validated therapeutic or diagnostic targets.

We speculated that higher Bacteroides abundance might confer protection via short-chain fatty acids (butyrate, propionate) and capsular polysaccharide A, which have been shown to suppress mast cell degranulation and foster regulatory T-cell differentiation in animal models.9,41 These effects could theoretically shift the TH1/TH2 balance toward an anti-inflammatory profile, thereby alleviating CSU symptoms.9,44 Our mediation MR analysis further confirmed that higher Bacteroides abundance correlates with lower CSU risk, mediated by N-acetyl leucine. However, whether these molecular events occur in human CSU tissue has not been directly demonstrated, and functional studies (eg, mast cell assays using Bacteroides-conditioned medium) are required to confirm this hypothetical pathway.

Our mediation analysis suggests that galactonate might partially convey the protective effect of Paraprevotella. Although galactonate can perturb the pentose–phosphate pathway and alter regulatory T-cell function in vitro,45,46 no study has yet linked Paraprevotella-derived galactonate to regulatory T-cell numbers or to CSU severity in vivo. Experimental validation (eg, galactonate supplementation in a CSU mouse model) is therefore necessary before any therapeutic claims can be made.

Additionally, we found that N-acetyl leucine mediates the protective effect of Bacteroides on CSU. Leucine, as an activator of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway,47,48 is closely related to the differentiation of TH1, TH2, and TH17 cells. N-acetyl leucine, a leucine derivative, has been proposed to activate the mTOR pathway and consequently modulate TH1/TH2 balance.47,48 Nevertheless, direct evidence that N-acetyl leucine modulates mTOR signaling or mast cell activity in CSU patients is currently lacking. Future in vitro mast cell assays and in vivo models are required to confirm such a pathway.

Our MR estimates genetically endorse basophils as a central axis in CSU: Higher circulating basophil counts were associated with lower disease risk, aligning with clinical observations that basopenia predicts poor omalizumab response.26,49 We previously showed that galectin-9 expression on basophils correlates with disease activity and omalizumab efficacy;50 taken together, our findings suggest that basophil abundance and galectin-9 intensity could serve as companion biomarkers to guide anti-IgE therapy. During active CSU, basophils migrate to skin lesions and release histamine, leukotrienes, and TH2 cytokines;51,52 omalizumab reverses this trafficking and increases peripheral basophil counts.53,54 Thus, genetically driven basophil preservation might identify patients with disease that will show optimal response to omalizumab, supporting the precision use of this therapeutic target.

We found that elevated levels of CCL19 are associated with a reduced risk of developing CSU. CCL19, a T lymphocyte chemoattractant, is involved in the migration and activation of immune cells. In allergic airway diseases, it helps to reduce the number of myeloid dendritic cells, suppresses the generation of TH2 cells,55 and decreases IgE production by affecting the TH1/TH2 balance.56 In atopic dermatitis lesions, CCL19 promotes the differentiation and migration of TH2 cells, with its expression increasing with age.57,58 Although the specific role of CCL19 in CSU is not well understood, CCL19 released by activated mast cells can interact with receptors on T and B cells, modulating the activation and migration of dendritic cells, T cells, and B cells.59 On this basis, we hypothesized that CCL19 may reduce the risk of CSU by either decreasing the migration of proinflammatory immune cells to inflammatory sites or by enhancing the migration of regulatory T cells.

Previous studies have indicated that TGF-β is associated with the genetic susceptibility to CSU, and its expression is elevated in CSU patients, positively correlating with disease activity.60 TGF-β can promote the development of IL-9–producing mast cells, potentially playing a significant role in food-induced allergic reactions.61 However, some researchers suggest that IL-10 and TGF-β can reduce IgE-mediated mast cell degranulation.62 TGF-β1, encoded by the TGF-β gene, can suppress the expression of the high-affinity receptor FcεRI on mast cells, reduce the release of mediators, and promote apoptosis.62 Regrettably, our analysis did not find a significant association between TGF-β levels and the risk of developing CSU, which may be related to study design or differences in CSU subtypes. Database limitations meant we were unable to perform subgroup analyses for type I and IIb CSU. In future analyses, we will further explore the relationship between them.

Interestingly, we found that increased levels of TGF-α are associated with a higher risk of developing CSU—a finding not previously reported in the literature. TGF-α is implicated in various pathophysiologic processes, including immunity, inflammation, and oncogenesis. In asthma, the production and release of TGF-α can be enhanced by IL-4, IL-13, and allergens, promoting airway remodeling.63 Eosinophils may contribute to tissue repair by releasing TGF-α and -β.64 Given the role of IL-4, IL-13, and eosinophils in CSU, as well as the proven efficacy of dupilumab, an inhibitor targeting these factors, in CSU patients,25,65,66 TGF-α may play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of CSU, offering a novel therapeutic target for future treatment.

We acknowledge that direct evidence for these pathways in CSU is still limited and that functional studies are needed to confirm their mechanistic contribution. We therefore plan to validate these hypotheses experimentally by preparing in vitro mast cell degranulation assays and a humanized CSU mouse model to test recombinant CCL19 supplementation and TGF-α neutralization on wheal formation and mediator release.

Study limitations

This is the first large-scale MR analysis to investigate the causal relationships between the gut microbiome, peripheral immune cells, circulating inflammatory proteins, and CSU. Our study has a key strength: The MR approach effectively circumvents common confounding factors and issues of reverse causality. However, there are limitations to our research. First, CSU has two main subtypes, type I and IIb, and the absence of GWAS data for these subtypes prevented us from conducting subtype-specific analyses. Second, our analysis focuses on individuals of European descent, and further research is needed to determine whether the findings are applicable to other populations. The SNP-microbe effect sizes and allele frequencies used as instruments were estimated in European GWAS; genetic heterogeneity across ancestries may alter these associations, necessitating replication in East Asian, African, and mixed cohorts. Additionally, the lack of detailed demographic information in public databases, such as sex and age, limits our ability to perform subgroup analyses. Furthermore, genetic polymorphisms may influence the associations of the analyzed SNPs, potentially affecting the accuracy of causal inferences. Importantly, while MR analysis helps minimize confounding and reverse causation, the strength of causal inference remains limited without validating findings through independent datasets or experimental evidence (eg, animal models, interventions). We have not yet provided functional follow-up studies or validation for our findings, which weakens the claims about galactonate and N-acetyl leucine as potential therapeutic targets. Future work will include in vitro and in vivo experiments to further explore their roles and validate these potential therapeutic targets.

Conclusions

In this large-scale MR study, 8 gut microbial taxa, 79 plasma metabolites, 25 immune cell traits, and 3 inflammatory proteins showed suggestive causal associations with CSU (all with nominal PIVW < .05 but p_fdr > .05). Mediation analysis identified galactonate and N-acetyl leucine as potential intermediaries of the microbiome-CSU relationship; notably, only the N-acetyl leucine pathway remained evident in MVMR. Immune cells and inflammatory proteins showed no evidence of mediating effects. These results provide hypothesis-generating insights into CSU pathogenesis and potential biomarker candidates, but they must be interpreted with caution and require functional validation and replication in independent cohorts before any therapeutic or diagnostic application.
