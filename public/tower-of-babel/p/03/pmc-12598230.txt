Biceps Augmentation of Anterosuperior Rotator Cuff Tears
Authors: Colton D Norton, Larry D Field
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnterosuperior rotator cuff tears involve the supraspinatus and subscapularis tendons, but repair should be approached as a single entity using the comma tissue as a guide for both tear recognition and reduction. These tears can be challenging to accurately identify and repair, particularly if they are chronic and/or retracted. The authors present their technique for identification and repair of retracted anterosuperior rotator cuff tears augmented using the transposed biceps tendon.

Anterosuperior rotator cuff tears (ASRCTs) are defined as combined tears of both the subscapularis and the supraspinatus. These complex lesions lead to significant pain and impaired shoulder function. The incidence of these combined tears varies in the literature, but some studies have identified anterosuperior rotator cuff pathology in nearly 50% of all rotator cuff injuries.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Successful arthroscopic repair of ASRCTs achieves decreased pain and improved shoulder function.7 As with all arthroscopic shoulder pathology, identification of the ASRCT begins with thorough diagnostic glenohumeral joint arthroscopy. Lo and Burkhart8 were the first to describe the comma tissue as the anatomic connection between the subscapularis and supraspinatus. Identification of this comma tissue along with mobilization and reduction are keys steps to achieving a successful repair.

Arthroscopic repair of anterosuperior tears can be performed by first using an intra-articular approach to accomplish subscapularis repair subsequently followed by arthroscopic repair of the supraspinatus within the subacromial space. However, this approach has disadvantages, including increased swelling that can limit visualization as well as difficulty obtaining anatomic reduction.9 It is of the authors’ preference to complete the repair of the subscapularis, comma tissue, and supraspinatus for large ASRCTs from an exclusively subacromial viewing perspective after glenohumeral joint arthroscopic identification and mobilization of the comma tissue. The authors present a reproducible sequence and technique for repair of retracted ASRCTs that also incorporates a transposed biceps tendon designed to augment and offload the repair construct.

Surgical Technique

It is the senior author’s preference to perform all rotator cuff repairs with the patient in the beach-chair position. The operative extremity is prepped and draped in standard fashion, and bony anatomic landmarks are referenced when establishing arthroscopic portals. A standard posterior portal is created, and a 30° arthroscope is introduced into the glenohumeral joint. An anterosuperior portal is created using an outside-in technique, and diagnostic arthroscopy is performed. As demonstrated in Video 1, the anterior anatomy often is distorted in retracted subscapularis tears, and the first and very important step is to accurately identify the anatomy and pathology (Fig 1). When medialized subscapularis and comma tissue are recognized using the initial posterior viewing portal, the anterior portal cannula and trocar are redirected more medially as necessary to position the anterior cannula medial to the identified comma tissue so that it penetrates the medially translated rotator interval capsule (Fig 2). Positioning the anterior cannula medial to the comma tissue allows the comma tissue to then be translated laterally by leveraging rotation of the anterior cannula. Lateralizing the comma tissue leads to subscapularis and the rotator interval capsule lateralization as well, bringing all of these structures into better view (Fig 3). The rotator interval tissue is then excised using an arthroscopic shaver (DYONICS; Smith & Nephew, Memphis, TN) to aid in mobilizing the comma tissue and subscapularis tendon and also to improve identification of the subscapularis tendon when subsequently viewed from the subacromial space. Care must be taken to preserve the comma tissue as it serves an extremely important role in supporting reduction and repair of ASRCTs.

The subacromial space is entered next, and a thorough bursectomy is performed while viewing from the posterior portal. After bursectomy has been completed, the lateral portal is used for viewing for the remainder of the procedure. At this point, acromioplasty and distal clavicle excision are performed as indicated. Accessory posterolateral and anterolateral portals are then created and the greater and lesser tuberosities abraded. The biceps is then completely freed from the bicipital groove via release of the transverse humeral ligament using an arthroscopic shaver placed into the accessory anterolateral portal. After biceps tendon mobilization is completed, the biceps is grasped and transposed onto the abraded greater tuberosity without performing biceps tenotomy either proximally or distally, where it will later be secured using suture anchor sutures. An arthroscopic grasper is then introduced via the accessory anterolateral portal to grasp the comma tissue and assess the reducibility of the subscapularis and supraspinatus as a unit (Fig 4). If additional mobilization of the subscapularis tendon is necessary then it is accomplished at this point while viewing from the subacromial space.

View from lateral portal within subacromial space, right shoulder of a patient in the beach-chair position. Grasper is used to manipulate comma tissue (CT) and lateralize both the supraspinatus (SSP) and subscapularis (SCC).

The authors begin the repair of the rotator cuff tendon and comma tissue by placing a triple-loaded polyether ether ketone 5.5-mm anchor (HEALICOIL PEEK; Smith & Nephew) into the abraded lesser tuberosity and, using a retrograde suture passing device (IDEAL Suture Grasper; Mitek, Fond du Lac, WI), suture anchor sutures are passed through the subscapularis tendon and comma tissue. A second 5.5-mm anchor (HEALICOIL PEEK; Smith & Nephew) in this case example is then placed at the anterior margin of the greater tuberosity.10 These suture anchor sutures are then passed both around and through the biceps and through the supraspinatus tendon so as to secure these structures and also to converge them to each other. Using the biceps tendon in this way allows the biceps to serve both as autograft augmentation and as a physiologically tensioned convergence post (Fig 5). Finally, for the case example shown, a third 5.5-mm anchor (HEALICOIL PEEK) is placed into the greater tuberosity near the interval between the supraspinatus and infraspinatus tendons. Again, using retrograde suture passage via anterior and posterior portals, the sutures from this third anchor are passed through the supraspinatus tendon to create a single row rip-stop suture construct, accomplished by placing relatively laterally positioned horizontal mattress sutures through the supraspinatus along with 2 separate simple sutures placed more medially than the mattress sutures. In addition, the authors always converge any torn tendon with intact rotator cuff tissue, and this was accomplished in this case by passing one suture limb through the intact infraspinatus to converge it with the detached supraspinatus to further offload the repair construct (Fig 6). The authors also strongly recommend and perform subscapularis and comma tissue knot tying first as this knot tying sequence aids in lateralizing the detached supraspinatus tendon, thereby reducing its retraction and tension (Table 1). The final step in the repair is tying the supraspinatus sutures and convergence sutures to the infraspinatus, thus completing the repair (Fig 7).

Discussion

ASRCTs are complex lesions that can lead to significant shoulder dysfunction. Traditional arthroscopic surgical procedures have used an intra-articular subscapularis repair technique followed by subacromial space surgery to repair the supraspinatus tear. However, multiple authors have published techniques that involve repair of both tendons while visualizing exclusively within the subacromial space.9,11,12 Regardless of repair strategy, the initial, key step is identification of the subscapularis tear, and this is often made more reliable and much easier by recognizing the comma tissue. As originally described by Lo and Burkhart, the comma tissue guides the surgeon to the torn upper border of the subscapularis in cases of retracted tears.8 It also serves as the anatomic link between the subscapularis and supraspinatus and can be manipulated to reduce both tendons simultaneously, as demonstrated in this video case example.

There are several advantages that our technique provides. The importance of recognizing the comma tissue and its significance cannot be overstated. The authors’ technique of redirecting the anterior trocar and cannula so that it remains medial to the comma tissue allows the surgeon to effectively lateralize the comma tissue, its attached subscapularis, and the rotator interval capsule tissue. Lateralization of these anterior glenohumeral joint structures greatly improves a surgeon’s ability to then mobilize these structures, thus restoring the familiar appearance of the “anterior triangle” formed by the subscapularis, biceps tendon, and rotator interval. Failure to perform this maneuver may leave the surgeon anatomically disoriented and compromise ASRCT repair.

Another advantage of this “subacromial-view” approach to ASRCT repair is the ability to maintain an excellent view of and access to the comma tissue. This comma tissue is not as well visualized from an intra-articular viewing perspective, even using a 70° arthroscope, in the authors’ opinion. Also, incorporation of the comma tissue into the repair is considered vital by the authors since repairing the comma tissue has been shown to provide significant biomechanical advantages.11,13 Also, tying the subscapularis sutures before the supraspinatus sutures aids a great deal in lateralization and reduction of the supraspinatus tissue, thus creating a lower tension repair. The main disadvantage to this technique is the learning curve for surgeons who are more accustomed to using an intra-articular subscapularis repair technique and less accustomed to viewing the subscapularis and comma tissue from a subacromial viewing perspective. There may also be a learning curve for retrograde suture passage as per this technique for surgeons more accustomed to antegrade suture passing devices commonly used for rotator cuff repair (Table 2).

Incorporation of the biceps tendon into the repair of massive posterosuperior rotator cuff tears using the “Bio-SCR” technique has recently been shown to improve clinical outcomes with a low complication rate.14 As described by McClatchy et al.,14 there are numerous advantages to the Bio-SCR technique in the setting of massive posterosuperior tears that apply to ASRCTs as well. Compared with commonly used grafts for superior capsule reconstruction such as human dermal allograft and fascia lata autograft, the biceps tendon has the benefit of no additional cost, no donor-site morbidity, and potential biomechanical advantages. Because we do not perform tenotomy of the biceps anywhere along its course in this technique, the biceps theoretically can act as both a static and dynamic humeral head depressor. Because the transposed position of the biceps is more central and superior on the humeral head, the new course of the biceps is longer which theoretically provides a static depressive force on the humeral head. Because of the promising clinical results recently demonstrated using the Bio-SCR technique in massive posterosuperior tears we feel that this technique can be similarly employed in repair of ASRCTs.

Arthroscopic repair of ASRCTs is challenging because of the anatomic deformities that are often seen arthroscopically in retracted ASRCT tears. This technique demonstrates a reproducible way to identify the distorted anterior shoulder anatomy always associated with retracted subscapularis tears along with a reproducible method to utilize the comma tissue to guide the repair of the subscapularis and supraspinatus as a single unit while viewing from the subacromial space.
