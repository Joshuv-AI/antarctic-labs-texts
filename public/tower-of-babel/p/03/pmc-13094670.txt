Laboratory Protocols for Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus Transmission Biology and Management Research
Authors: Diogo M Galdeano, William Ingram, Anna Erickson, Curtis R Carlson, Bryce W Falk, Yen‐Wen Kuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus (CLas), the causal agent of Huanglongbing, is transmitted by the Asian citrus psyllid (Diaphorina citri) in a persistent, propagative manner. Reproducible transmission studies require not only controlled inoculation assays but also standardized propagation of citrus host plants, maintenance of D. citri colonies, and reliable pathogen detection. This study presents a comprehensive set of coordinated protocols that collectively support controlled CLas transmission experimentation. The workflow includes propagation of Citrus macrophylla for colony and assay use; generation and maintenance of CLas‐infected citrus and D. citri colonies; synchronization of D. citri populations to enable CLas acquisition; and a controlled single‐D. citri transmission assay with defined inoculation access periods, and a Dellaporta‐based nucleic acid extraction method compatible with citrus and psyllid tissues. Quantitative PCR detection is integrated throughout to verify infection status, determine CLas titer, and monitor CLas movement and systemic colonization in citrus over time. By combining standardized plant production, vector infection systems, transmission assays, and molecular diagnostics, these coordinated methods provide a unified framework for testing hypotheses about vector competence, pathogen–vector interactions, microbial co‐infections, host susceptibility, and environmental effects on transmission. Together, the procedures enable consistent and comparable assessment of CLas acquisition, dissemination, and transmission outcomes under controlled laboratory conditions. © 2026 The Author(s). INTRODUCTION

Huanglongbing (HLB), or citrus greening disease, is one of the most destructive diseases threatening global citrus production (da Graca et al., 2016). The causative agent, Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus (CLas), is a phloem‐limited, unculturable bacterium transmitted in a persistent propagative manner by the Asian citrus psyllid (ACP, Diaphorina citri) (Chen et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2024). Given the lack of effective therapies and the severe economic impact of HLB, improving our understanding of CLas transmission biology and identifying ways to disrupt its spread remain urgent research priorities.

CLas transmission by D. citri involves a complex circulative pathway through the insect vector. After ingestion, the bacterium must traverse several physiological barriers, first the midgut epithelium, then survive and replicate in the hemolymph, and finally invade the salivary glands to be successfully transmitted to a new host during feeding (Ammar et al., 2011; Ghanim et al., 2016; Lin et al., 2022). Each of these barriers can restrict pathogen movement and contribute to variability in transmission efficiency. Critically, acquisition during the nymphal stages appears to be necessary for CLas to complete this journey. Adults acquiring CLas are largely unable to transmit it, whereas nymph‐acquired adults achieve much higher transmission efficiencies (∼73% to 83%) (Ammar et al., 2020; Pelz‐Stelinski et al., 2010).

Several other factors compound this variability. The latent period required for CLas to reach the salivary glands can range from 7 to 16 days, but its duration remains incompletely resolved and may vary across experimental conditions (Ammar et al., 2020; Li et al., 2025; Pacheco et al., 2020). D. citri genetic background also plays a role; certain isofemale lines differ dramatically in transmission efficiency, possibly due to variation in immune response genes like hemocyanin (Ammar et al., 2018). Moreover, CLas titers in infected D. citri can decline over time, particularly when insects are maintained on healthy plants (Nian et al., 2023; Pelz‐Stelinski et al., 2010). These biological and technical variables make it challenging to interpret results across studies without a standardized and well‐controlled protocol.

Previous CLas transmission assays have varied widely in acquisition and inoculation procedures, age ranges of D. citri used, and post‐inoculation monitoring periods. These differences have limited reproducibility and comparability among studies. The protocol presented here builds upon prior work by standardizing these parameters and incorporating defined biological timing to ensure consistent infection outcomes. This method can be applied to studies of vector competency, host susceptibility, and pathogen–microbe interactions.

This protocol provides a reproducible method for evaluating CLas transmission that mitigates known sources of variability. D. citri populations are age‐synchronized and exposed to CLas‐infected citrus plants during their nymphal development, ensuring that pathogen acquisition occurs during the life stage most conducive to successful transmission. Unlike previous assays relying on mixed‐age D. citri populations or variable acquisition periods, this method reduces experimental noise by synchronizing insect populations and standardizing plant material. A limitation is that the environmental conditions could affect the D. citri emergent timeline and the extended duration required for systemic infection detection, which may constrain high‐throughput applications.

By integrating biological considerations such as vector developmental stage, CLas acquisition timing, and pathogen movement within both D. citri and citrus hosts, these methods provide a robust platform for fundamental and applied research on CLas transmission. The coordinated protocols enable reproducible measurement of transmission efficiency and support studies examining factors such as vector genetics, microbial interactions, plant susceptibility, and potential disease management strategies. This article includes detailed protocols for developing and maintaining CLas‐infected citrus and D. citri colonies, synchronizing D. citri populations, facilitating CLas acquisition, performing controlled single‐insect transmission assays, extracting nucleic acids using the Dellaporta method, and propagating C. macrophylla for colony and assay use. Together, these methods provide a comprehensive and reproducible workflow for conducting controlled CLas transmission research in laboratory settings.

Cautions

CAUTION: CLas is a regulated, phloem‐limited bacterium associated with citrus HLB. All work involving CLas‐infected plant material or D. citri vectors from HLB‐endemic areas must be conducted in an approved Biosafety Level 3 (BSL‐3) facility or equivalent containment structure, in compliance with state and federal regulations governing select agent‐exempt plant pathogens. Personnel must follow institutional biosafety protocols and local regulatory requirements for containment, waste disposal, and decontamination.

CAUTION: Environmental conditions are critical for consistent D. citri development and synchronization. Inadequate light intensity, short photoperiods, or suboptimal temperature and humidity can result in poor emergence rates and asynchronous adult populations. Rearing should be performed under tightly controlled conditions to ensure uniform cohorts for transmission assays.

CAUTION: Host plant condition strongly influences D. citri oviposition and nymphal development. Plants used for colony maintenance and acquisition assays must be well‐nourished and irrigated. Moreover, plants must have abundant young flush shoots to support egg laying and nymphal feeding. Insufficient flush availability can drastically reduce oviposition, impair psyllid synchronization, and compromise subsequent transmission assays.

CAUTION: Prevent cross‐contamination between infected and uninfected D. citri colonies by maintaining separate rearing rooms or enclosed cages with dedicated tools and personnel. Even minimal contamination (via escaped adults or contaminated plant material) can compromise colony integrity and invalidate experimental results.

NOTE: All protocols involving animals must be reviewed and approved by the appropriate Animal Care and Use Committee and must follow regulations for the care and use of laboratory animals. Appropriate informed consent is necessary for obtaining and use of human study material.

A CONTROLLED LABORATORY METHOD FOR ASSESSING CANDIDATUS LIBERIBACTER ASIATICUS TRANSMISSION BY DIAPHORINA CITRI

This basic protocol provides an integrated, standardized workflow for evaluating the transmission of CLas by ACP, D. citri. The procedure begins by generating age‐synchronized adult D. citri on healthy Citrus macrophylla plants, followed by a short mating period. Synchronized adults are then transferred onto CLas‐infected citrus plants exclusively for oviposition. Because eggs are deposited directly onto infected plants, all subsequent nymphal development occurs on CLas‐positive C. macrophylla tissue, enabling efficient pathogen acquisition by the developing offspring. Upon emergence, same‐day adults are collected within a controlled emergence window and maintained on healthy C. macrophylla for 14 days to allow CLas replication and dissemination within the vector, D. citri. These CLas‐infected D. citri adults are then used in a single‐insect transmission assay in which individual psyllids are confined to healthy C. macrophylla plantlets (with four leaves) for a 48‐hr inoculation access period (IAP). After IAP, psyllids are collected for qPCR‐based confirmation of CLas infection status and titer, and recipient plants are monitored over time to assess initial infection, local spread, and systemic colonization. This protocol provides a reproducible system for producing CLas‐infected D. citri, quantifying their infection status, and measuring their ability to transmit CLas to citrus hosts.

Expected Outcome

This protocol produces age‐synchronized D. citri adults that develop from egg to adult on CLas‐infected citrus and subsequently undergo a 14‐day incubation period, resulting in D. citri with verified CLas infection. qPCR analysis of 25–30 psyllids confirms infection status and provides accurate CLas titer measurements for the individuals used in transmission assays.

Following controlled IAPs, citrus plantlets exposed to single CLas‐infected psyllids exhibit predictable infection progression. Early localized infection is detected in the feeding leaf at 21 days, directional CLas movement is observed in successive lower leaves at 60 days, and systemic infection is confirmed at 180 days. DNA extracted from sampled citrus tissues consistently supports qPCR‐based detection and quantification of CLas across all sampling points.

Overall, the combined procedures provide a reproducible system for evaluating CLas acquisition by D. citri, transmission efficiency to citrus, and subsequent intra‐plant CLas movement. This framework supports rigorous studies of vector competence, pathogen dynamics, and host–pathogen interactions.

GENERATION OF CANDIDATUS LIBERIBACTER ASIATICUS‐INFECTED CITRUS MACROPHYLLA AND DIAPHORINA CITRI

Maintaining healthy and reliable source colonies of CLas‐infected citrus plants and age‐synchronized D. citri populations is a foundational prerequisite for reproducible transmission assays. CLas is phloem‐limited and unculturable in vitro, and its successful acquisition, replication, and subsequent inoculation via D. citri depend upon robust insect colonies, appropriate host plant flush growth, and well‐controlled environmental conditions. Moreover, experimental variability in vector competence often stems from differences in source plant age/flush stage, insect developmental stage, and rearing conditions. For this reason, the present protocol describes, step by step, how to propagate citrus plants to the appropriate size and flush readiness, how to maintain and synchronize D. citri populations, and how to establish and maintain CLas‐infected donor plants under containment. The method ensures that acquisition access periods (AAPs) can be reliably delivered and that inoculation experiments are supported by a well‐characterized donor system. The data obtainable from this maintenance protocol include insect emergence and synchrony metrics, source plant flush status, CLas infection titers in both plants and insects, and the stability of the donor system across sequential experimental rounds. By clearly defining these upstream steps, the protocol enhances downstream reproducibility of transmission assays, supports comparative studies of vector genetics, host genotype or microbial co‐infections, and underpins mechanistic research into CLas–D. citri interactions.

DELLAPORTA TOTAL NUCLEIC ACID EXTRACTION

This protocol describes the Dellaporta method (Dellaporta et al., 1983) for extracting total nucleic acids from D. citri or plant tissue. The procedure relies on detergent‐mediated cell lysis, potassium acetate precipitation of proteins and polysaccharides, and isopropanol precipitation of nucleic acids. The method is rapid, requires minimal specialized equipment, and yields nucleic acids suitable for downstream applications such as PCR‐based assays.

CITRUS MACROPHYLLA PROPAGATION

Reliable and uniform citrus plant material is essential for maintaining D. citri colonies and supporting downstream experiments involving CLas acquisition, transmission, and plant infection dynamics. This protocol describes a straightforward and reproducible method for propagating citrus cuttings under controlled greenhouse conditions. The procedure includes selecting appropriate donor branches, preparing cuttings, applying rooting hormone, and establishing the cuttings in small pots under high humidity to induce rooting. When performed correctly, most cuttings develop strong root systems within one month and can be transplanted into larger pots or cones for use in psyllid colony maintenance or experimental assays.

Commentary

Successful and reproducible measurement of CLas transmission depends on strict control of several biological and technical variables. The most important parameters are outlined below and grouped according to the major stages of the workflow.

Psyllid Synchronization and Developmental Acquisition

• Emergence window control: Only same‐day‐emerged D. citri should be used. Mixing adults across multiple emergence days introduces large variability in infection titers and transmission efficiency.

• Adult incubation period: The 14‐day post‐emergence incubation on healthy plants allows CLas titers to reach higher levels. Shorter intervals could reduce transmission efficiency (Galdeano et al., 2025).

Source Plant Quality and Flush Availability

• Abundant flush: Poor flush directly suppresses oviposition and nymphal survival, leading to low emergence and poorly synchronized D. citri.

• CLas source plant titer: Low‐titer donor plants yield inconsistent psyllid infection rates. Plants should be screened by qPCR prior to acquisition.

Transmission Assay Design

• Psyllid‐to‐plant ratio: Transmission assays are conducted with one psyllid per plantlet to enable precise attribution of infection outcomes; higher psyllid densities may increase overall transmission rates but can obscure differences attributable to factors influencing transmission competence. The conditions could be modified based on the needs.

• IAP: An IAP of 48 hr provides consistent transmission under these conditions, although longer exposure periods may be required for larger plants or lower‐titer psyllids.

• Plant developmental stage: Plantlets must contain four fully expanded leaves. Larger plants dilute early infection signals and require longer monitoring.

Sample Processing and qPCR Detection

• Midrib sampling: CLas titers are highest in midrib tissues and must be used for reliable detection.

• Contamination control: Dedicated tools for infected and uninfected samples are essential to prevent false positives.

Understanding Results

This protocol produces stable and interpretable transmission rates when executed correctly. CLas‐positive psyllids emerging from this workflow typically exhibit high and stable bacterial titers following the 14‐day incubation period. (Galdeano et al., 2025) In the transmission assays, the citrus plantlets:

• Day 21: CLas may be undetectable in the feeding leaf even with successful infection due to the early stage of infection

• Day 60: CLas are detected in a few leaves, which could indicate their distribution.

• Day 180: Systemic colonization is confirmed in randomly sampled mature leaves.

Early detection at Day 21 indicates successful local inoculation, whereas detection at later timepoints reflects subsequent bacterial movement and establishment within the plant. The absence or variability of CLas detection at intermediate or late stages is commonly observed in transmission assays and should be interpreted with caution, as multiple biological factors can influence systemic progression.

This system could also support detection of partial transmission phenotypes, including reduced CLas movement in plants or unstable colonization patterns associated with vector genetics, microbial co‐infections, or host resistance traits.
