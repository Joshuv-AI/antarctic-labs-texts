Elastography for distinguishing lymphoma from benign and metastatic lymphadenopathy: a systematic review and Bayesian meta-analysis
Authors: Fatemeh Mahdavi Sabet, Shayan Forghani, Amirhossein Shahsavand, Mohammadamin Kharaghani, Reza Samiee, Fahimeh Zeinalkhani, Elham Neshan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDifferentiating lymphoma from benign and metastatic lymphadenopathies using conventional ultrasound remains clinically challenging. Despite the utility of ultrasound elastography in assessing tissue stiffness, the intermediate stiffness profile of lymphoma frequently leads to misclassification. To address this diagnostic gap, we conducted a comprehensive systematic review and Bayesian diagnostic meta-analysis to evaluate the performance of various elastography modalities.MethodsFollowing PRISMA 2020 guidelines (PROSPERO: CRD420251184980), we systematically searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library. Original studies evaluating elastography against valid reference standards were included, and methodological quality was assessed using the QUADAS-2 tool. Diagnostic performance was synthesized via a robust two-tiered Bayesian bivariate hierarchical random-effects model to calculate pooled sensitivity, specificity, diagnostic odds ratios (DOR), and likelihood ratios (PLR/NLR) for both overall and modality-specific analyses.ResultsTwenty studies encompassing 2,342 patients and 2,626 superficial lymph nodes (981 benign, 895 lymphoma, 750 metastatic) were included. In differentiating lymphoma from benign lymphadenopathy, Shear Wave Velocity (SWV) exhibited the highest sensitivity (0.913) and lowest negative likelihood ratio (NLR, 0.117). Conversely, aggregated Strain Elastography (SE) scoring achieved the highest specificity (0.909) and positive likelihood ratio (PLR, 7.59). When distinguishing lymphoma from metastatic nodes, Virtual Touch Tissue Imaging (VTI) techniques demonstrated robust diagnostic yield for identifying lymphoma; specifically, the VTI ratio achieved the highest diagnostic odds ratio (DOR, 39.4), with a sensitivity of 0.92 and an NLR of 0.11.ConclusionUltrasound elastography provides significant modality-specific diagnostic value for stratifying lymphadenopathy. SWV effectively excludes lymphoma from benign nodes, whereas SE scoring optimizes its confirmation. Furthermore, VTI techniques robustly distinguish lymphoma from metastasis. However, given substantial inter-study heterogeneity, elastography should be integrated as a complementary multiparametric adjunct to guide biopsy triage, rather than replace definitive histopathological evaluation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40644-026-01089-7.Keywords: Elastography, Lymphoma, Lymphadenopathy, Strain ratio, Shear wave elastography, Bayesian meta-analysis

Introduction

Lymphadenopathy is a common presentation in clinical settings and can be caused by a wide range of infectious, inflammatory, and malignant factors [1]. While only a minority of unexplained cases harbor malignancy, prompt identification is critical [2]. Consequently, diagnostic algorithms must balance early cancer detection against the risks and costs of unnecessary invasive procedures [3].

Ultrasound is widely used as the first-line imaging modality for superficial lymphadenopathy because it is rapid, inexpensive, and sensitive for detecting morphologic nodal abnormalities. While grey-scale features (such as size, shape, and hilum preservation) and Doppler vascular patterns are useful, considerable overlap remains in the sonographic appearance of benign, metastatic, and lymphomatous nodes [4, 5]. As a result, image-guided core needle or excisional biopsy is still frequently required when ultrasound findings are indeterminate, with implications for patient burden, procedure-related risk, and healthcare resource use [6].

Ultrasound elastography has emerged as a valuable adjunct, estimating tissue stiffness via Strain Elastography (SE) or quantitative Shear-Wave Elastography (SWE) [7–9]. Extensive literature, confirms that elastography can reliably differentiate stiff metastatic carcinoma from softer benign nodes [10–12], and has been incorporated into multi-parametric ultrasound protocols for superficial lymph nodes and several other organs [13, 14].

However, lymphoma presents a distinct diagnostic challenge. Studies consistently report that lymphoma exhibits intermediate stiffness values, stiffer than reactive nodes but softer than metastatic nodes [15, 16]. This “stiffness overlap” creates a significant risk that lymphoma may be misclassified as benign or metastatic lymphadenopathy, highlighting a critical gap in current diagnostic capabilities [8, 17, 18]. From a clinical standpoint, accurate differentiation is vital since staging, systemic therapy, and prognosis differ markedly between lymphoma and metastatic disease [19, 20]. Furthermore, the lack of standardized protocols and threshold values has prevented a consensus on which specific elastography technique is superior for this specific differentiation [8].

To address these uncertainties, this study conducts a comprehensive systematic review and Bayesian diagnostic meta-analysis evaluating the performance of different elastography modalities in distinguishing lymphoma from both benign and metastatic lymphadenopathy. By comparing available techniques and quantitative parameters across studies, we aim to determine whether elastography provides reliable discriminatory value in this context and to identify the approaches that demonstrate the most consistent diagnostic performance.

Methods

Overview and search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 checklist and was registered with PROSPERO (Registration ID: CRD420251184980). A comprehensive search of four electronic databases (PubMed (n = 246), Embase (n = 942), Web of Science (WOS, n = 181), and the Cochrane Library (n = 14)) was performed on July 2025. The search query combined terms related to elastography (such as elastography, shear wave elastography, strain elastography, and acoustic radiation force impulse imaging) with terms for lymphoma. Retrieved records were managed using EndNote, which was used to identify and eliminate duplicates. Additionally, the reference lists of all included studies were manually screened to capture any further relevant articles.

Study selection

After removing duplicates, the remaining studies underwent a two-step screening process. First, two independent researchers reviewed titles and abstracts to exclude irrelevant studies. Next, the full-text articles of potentially eligible studies were thoroughly reviewed using predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, consultation with a third researcher.

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) Original research articles; (2) Studies evaluating the diagnostic accuracy of an elastography technique for lymphoma or comparing continuous elastography values between lymphoma and a control group; (3) For diagnostic accuracy studies, comparison of the index test (elastography) to a valid reference standard; (4) For diagnostic accuracy studies, availability of sufficient data to reconstruct 2 × 2 contingency tables (True Positives, False Positives, False Negatives, and True Negatives); and (5) For continuous outcome studies, reporting of summary statistics for both lymphoma and control groups.

The exclusion criteria were: (1) Case reports, reviews, commentaries, systematic reviews, and conference abstracts; (2) Preclinical studies; and (3) Studies involving duplicate cohorts or those with incomplete/unavailable data for the required outcomes.

Data extraction

A standardized Excel spreadsheet was used for data extraction, which was performed independently by two researchers. Discrepancies were resolved through group discussion.

Extracted data included study characteristics and population characteristics. Key test details were also recorded, including the specific elastography Method (e.g., “SE Score,” “SWV,” or “VTI Score”), the Reference Standard, and the nature of the Comparison Group. For the diagnostic accuracy meta-analysis, we extracted 2 × 2 contingency table data: True Positives (TP), False Negatives (FN), True Negatives (TN), and False Positives (FP). For the continuous outcome meta-analysis, we extracted the mean, standard deviation (SD), and sample size (N) for elasticity values in both the lymphoma and control groups.

Quality assessment

Two researchers independently assessed the methodological quality of the included diagnostic studies using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies 2 (QUADAS-2) tool [21]. This tool evaluates the risk of bias across four key domains: (1) patient selection, (2) index test, (3) reference standard, and (4) flow and timing. Any discrepancies in scoring were resolved through discussion to reach a consensus.

Statistical analysis

Our analysis was structured in two main parts: (1) a Bayesian diagnostic test accuracy (DTA) meta-analysis based on 2 × 2 table data, and (2) a frequentist meta-analysis of continuous data using Standardized Mean Differences (SMDs). All statistical analyses were conducted on a per-lesion basis.

Diagnostic accuracy meta-analysis (Bayesian bivariate model)

The DTA meta-analysis was performed separately for two distinct clinical questions: (1) Differentiating lymphoma from benign conditions. (2) Differentiating lymphoma from metastatic cancers.

For each clinical question, the diagnostic performance was evaluated using a two-tiered modeling approach. The Initial Model (Model 1) was designed to ensure statistical stability and higher resolution by aggregating similar techniques (combining all Strain Elastography [SE] scoring systems into a single category) and exclusively including modality groups backed by at least 2 studies. Subsequently, a Detailed Model (Model 2) was conducted to provide a more granular assessment. This secondary model separated the aggregated groups into their specific scoring systems and incorporated additional, highly specific modalities even if they were represented by only 1 study (such as Strain Ratio [SR] and VTI Ratio). This approach allowed us to balance robust baseline estimates with comprehensive detail for specific techniques.

We implemented a Bayesian bivariate hierarchical random-effects model using the rjags package. This framework jointly models the logit-transformed sensitivity and specificity, accounting for their inherent correlation.

The Bayesian bivariate models were run using four parallel MCMC chains. Each chain consisted of an adaptation phase of 10,000 iterations and a burn-in period of 40,000 iterations. After burn-in, 100,000 posterior samples were generated and thinned by 10, yielding 40,000 samples for analysis. (3) Visually inspecting trace plots. All Bayesian summary results are reported as posterior medians with 95% Credible Intervals (CrIs).

Continuous outcome meta-analysis (frequentist SMD model)

For studies reporting continuous elasticity values, a separate meta-analysis was performed to pool the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD). This analysis was conducted using the meta package.

We employed a random-effects model to pool SMDs and their 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs). Heterogeneity was quantified using the statistic and the between-study variance ().

Several separate meta-analyses and subgroup analyses were performed based on the specific clinical comparison and the elastography method.

Publication bias and sensitivity analyses

For the Bayesian models, we assessed small-study effects using two methods: (1) Model-Based Covariate: We included a coefficient in the JAGS model to formally test for an association between study precision and log(DOR). Evidence of bias was inferred if the 95% CrI for this parameter excluded zero. (2) Deek’s Funnel Plot: A linear regression of log(DOR) against study precision (1/), where P < 0.10 suggests asymmetry. A leave-one-study-out (LOO) sensitivity analysis was conducted to ensure no single study disproportionately influenced the pooled results.

Results

Literature search and study selection

The initial search yielded 1,384 records. After removing 315 duplicates, 1,069 underwent title and abstract screening. Excluding 968 irrelevant studies left 101 for full-text assessment, resulting in the final inclusion of 20 studies for systematic review and meta-analysis (PRISMA flow diagram in Figure S1).

Characteristics of included studies

Table 1 summarizes the clinical characteristics of the 20 included studies (12 prospective, 4 retrospective, and 4 cross-sectional). In total, the studies evaluated 2,342 patients and 2,626 superficial lymph nodes (981 benign, 895 lymphoma, and 750 metastatic). Ten studies assessed mixed superficial locations (axillary, inguinal, and cervical), while the remaining ten focused exclusively on superficial cervical nodes.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated using the QUADAS-2 tool. Detailed domain-level assessments for each study are presented in Figure S2. Risk of bias varied across studies. In patient selection (D1), risk was mixed. The index test (D2) was often rated high risk. For the reference standard (D3), most studies were unclear due to lack of reporting on whether interpretation was blinded to index test results. Flow and timing (D4) generally showed low risk of bias.

Bayesian meta-analysis of diagnostic performance

Differentiating lymphoma from benign lesions

A Bayesian meta-analysis was conducted to evaluate the diagnostic performance of different elastography modalities in distinguishing lymphoma from benign lymphadenopathies. Detailed cutoff values for each modality are presented in Table S1. The analysis was conducted in two tiers: an initial aggregated model evaluating the four primary modalities (combining 4-, 5-, and 6-point Strain Elastography (SE) scores), followed by a detailed secondary model stratifying all seven specific modalities and scoring systems.

Aggregated modality analysis (Model 1)

Shear Wave Velocity (SWV) emerged as the most effective modality for identifying lymphoma, demonstrating the highest pooled sensitivity of 0.913 (95% CrI: 0.664–0.983), best overall Diagnostic Odds Ratio (DOR) of 33.1 (95% CrI: 5.95–199), and strongest Negative Likelihood Ratio (NLR) of 0.117 (95% CrI: 0.023–0.441). Conversely, the aggregated SE Score was most reliable for confirming lymphoma, exhibiting the highest pooled specificity (0.909) and Positive Likelihood Ratio (PLR) of 7.59 (Table 3).

Detailed modality analysis (Model 2)

A secondary model evaluated seven distinct modalities, separating the SE scoring systems and including the Strain Ratio (SR) (Table S2). In this detailed analysis, SWV maintained the highest pooled sensitivity at 0.923 (95% CrI: 0.727–0.984), closely followed by the 5-Point SE Score (Alam/Tsukuba), which achieved a sensitivity of 0.902 (95% CrI: 0.515–0.988).

Regarding specificity, the 6-Point SE Score (Lenghel) yielded the highest pooled specificity at 0.966 (95% CrI: 0.892–0.992), resulting in a superior PLR of 10.5 (95% CrI: 1.75–52.7). However, its sensitivity was notably low (0.376). When considering overall diagnostic efficacy (DOR), SWV performed best (35.2, 95% CrI: 6.80–197), followed by the 5-Point SE Score (29.2, 95% CrI: 2.95–297) and SR (28.5, 95% CrI: 2.15–340). Both the 5-Point SE Score and SWV exhibited excellent capability in ruling out lymphoma, with NLRs of 0.132 and 0.105, respectively.

Heterogeneity and small-study effects

Substantial between-study heterogeneity was observed, particularly in sensitivity (median τ = 1.74 for Model 1; τ = 1.6 for Model 2) compared to specificity (τ = 0.62 for Model 1; τ = 0.64 for Model 2). Importantly, the evaluation of small-study effects revealed no significant bias. In both models, the 95%CrI for β_precision crossed zero (Model 1: -1.96 to 1.98; Model 2: -1.96 to 1.95), indicating that smaller, less precise studies did not systematically report artificially inflated diagnostic odds ratios.

Detailed modality analysis (Model 2)

The inclusion of specific sub-modalities, notably the VTI Ratio, provided deeper diagnostic insights. The VTI Ratio exhibited exceptional performance, achieving the highest overall diagnostic accuracy with a pooled DOR of 39.4 (95% CrI: 8.30–218). It also recorded the highest sensitivity at 0.92 (95% CrI: 0.82–0.97) and the most effective NLR at 0.11 (95% CrI: 0.04–0.25), making it highly effective at ruling out metastatic disease (Table S3).

Other highly sensitive modalities included SWV (0.89, 95%CrI: 0.79–0.95) and SR (0.88, 95%CrI: 0.68–0.97). In contrast, the SE scoring systems prioritized specificity. The 6-Point SE Score (Lenghel) demonstrated the highest pooled specificity at 0.91 (95% CrI: 0.74–0.98), followed closely by the 5-Point SE Score (Alam/Tsukuba) at 0.87 (95% CrI: 0.66–0.97) and the VTI Score at 0.87 (95% CrI: 0.69–0.96). Alongside the VTI Ratio, SR also showed strong overall diagnostic utility with a DOR of 31.2 (95% CrI: 5.38–215).

Heterogeneity and small-study effects

Heterogeneity in this comparison was moderately high, though slightly more pronounced in specificity (median τ = 1.28 for Model 1; τ = 1.30 for Model 2) than in sensitivity (median τ = 0.92 for Model 1; τ = 0.85 for Model 2). Analysis of the β_precision parameter indicated no significant evidence of small-study effects or publication bia. The 95%CrI for β_precision included zero in both the primary (-1.959 to 1.938) and detailed secondary models (-1.924 to 1.986).

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias analysis

To further evaluate the validity of the findings alongside the Bayesian models, frequentist publication bias assessments and sensitivity analyses were performed.

For the comparison distinguishing lymphoma from benign lymphadenopathies, Deeks’ funnel plot asymmetry test revealed no significant evidence of publication bias (P = 0.71). In contrast, publication bias was detected in the comparison differentiating lymphoma from metastatic lymph nodes (Deeks’ test, P = 0.002). The corresponding Deeks’ funnel plots for these evaluations are provided in Figures S3-4.

Additionally, a leave-one-out (LOO) sensitivity analysis was systematically conducted for each diagnostic comparison. This analysis confirmed the robustness of the models, ensuring that the overall pooled diagnostic metrics were not disproportionately influenced or skewed by any single study (Tables S4, S5).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this systematic review and meta-analysis is the first to comprehensively evaluate the diagnostic performance of elastography specifically for differentiating lymphoma from benign and metastatic lymphadenopathy. Previous meta-analyses have largely focused on the broader binary distinction between benign and malignant lymph nodes [12, 37]. While our analysis reveals complex and partially overlapping elastographic characteristics among these diverse entities, it concurrently identifies clear diagnostic trends across both qualitative and quantitative elastographic techniques.

A consistent diagnostic pattern emerged across the evaluated modalities. Quantitative techniques, particularly Shear Wave Velocity (SWV), demonstrated superior sensitivity for detecting lymphoma, whereas qualitative approaches, such as Virtual Touch Tissue Imaging (VTI) and Strain Elastography (SE), yielded higher pooled specificities. This trade-off underscores the complementary roles of these modalities in clinical practice.

Our analysis reveals that quantitative modalities exhibit modest specificity when differentiating lymphoma from benign lymphadenopathy. For example, Vinayagamani et al. observed that applying an optimal SWV threshold of 3 m/s resulted in all false-negative malignant lesions being lymphomas [28]. Conversely, SE demonstrated the highest specificity in this context; however, its overall diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) and area under the curve (AUC) were compromised by markedly limited sensitivity. The variable viscoelastic properties of lymphomatous tissue frequently manifest as softer elastographic appearances, predominantly yielding an elasticity score of 2, which is conventionally classified as benign [18].

For differentiating lymphoma from metastatic lymph nodes, SWV achieved the highest sensitivity (89.7%) and a robust AUC (0.805), albeit with only moderate specificity (). This diagnostic limitation reflects an inherent overlap in biomechanical properties, as both malignancies elevate nodal cellularity and disrupt tissue architecture [36]. Moreover, the accuracy of SWV is frequently constrained by a technical ceiling effect inherent to contemporary elastography platforms. By artificially capping maximum stiffness measurements, these systems truncate values for intensely indurated metastatic nodes, as seen in small cell carcinoma, thyroid carcinoma, and adenocarcinoma [28, 32, 34]. Consequently, these exceptionally stiff metastases mimic the softer elastographic profile of lymphoma, thereby diminishing the modality’s discriminatory capacity. Conversely, VTI scoring exhibited the highest specificity (87.2%) and DOR (24.8) for identifying metastatic nodes. This renders VTI highly efficacious for ruling out lymphoma when the distinctly stiff, heterogeneous patterns characteristic of metastases are present, as lymphoma typically demonstrates a more uniform elastographic stiffness [14, 24]. Nonetheless, the clinical utility of VTI remains encumbered by the “capricious” and highly variable presentation of lymphoma; because tissue stiffness evolves throughout disease progression, lymphomatous nodes may span the entire VTI grading spectrum [26].

Qualitative assessment of elastographic color patterns offers adjunctive diagnostic value. Although a homogeneous blue pattern suggests a benign etiology, it cannot reliably differentiate benignity from lymphoma. In contrast, “rim” patterns demonstrate high specificity for metastasis [11]. Applying the 5-point SE scoring system [38], metastatic nodes predominantly present with substantial red areas (> 45.0%, score 3), peripheral red zones with central green areas (score 4), or red regions with or without a green rim (score 5) [15]. However, the applicability of certain established classifications remains contentious. Both the Furukawa 4-point [39], and Alam 5-point [38], scales were principally developed to identify metastases, thereby excluding lymphoma from their initial validation cohorts. This foundational omission restricts their clinical utility when lymphoma represents a primary differential diagnosis. To address this, Lenghel et al. formulated a 6-point scale explicitly validated across benign, lymphomatous, and metastatic populations [23]. While our results indicate that the 6-point system achieves higher specificity compared to the 4- and 5-point scales in isolating lymphoma, the 5-point system ultimately yielded the optimal DOR across both differential scenarios.

Across all quantitative modalities, a consistent stiffness gradient emerged: metastatic nodes were the most rigid, benign nodes the softest, and lymphomas exhibited an intermediate profile. This intermediate stiffness reflects distinct histopathological architectures. Whereas metastatic nodes provoke significant desmoplastic responses, cortical damage, and early accumulation of keratin and fibrin, lymphomas exhibit minimal stroma. Their firmness derives primarily from dense clonal lymphoid proliferation rather than a fibrotic matrix, yielding an elastographic profile that closely approximates benign lesions [11, 14, 15, 26, 31, 33, 36].

Although overall pooled data indicated lymphomas are stiffer than benign nodes, SWV subgroup analyses revealed a greater standardized mean difference (SMD) between lymphoma and reactive nodes (SMD = 1.20) compared to granulomatous nodes (SMD = 0.55), though this difference lacked statistical significance (P = 0.44). This diminished contrast is likely due to the propensity for calcification in granulomatous diseases, which artifactually elevates stiffness and increases the risk of false-positive findings [16, 18, 34, 40]. Consequently, these findings underscore the necessity of interpreting elastographic measurements in conjunction with conventional B-mode ultrasound features and the broader clinical context. Furthermore, it is important to acknowledge that the stiffness of metastatic lymph nodes varies significantly depending on the histological subtype of the primary tumor. For instance, metastases originating from thyroid carcinoma frequently demonstrate increased stiffness due to microcalcifications, whereas adenocarcinoma metastases generally present with softer elastographic profiles [26, 33]. Due to the limited availability of primary data, subgroup comparisons stratifying by the origin of the metastasis were not feasible in this analysis.

Current literature advocates for a sequential workflow rather than using elastography in isolation. For example, Vinayagamani et al. suggest first utilizing conventional ultrasound to differentiate benign nodes from lymphoma, noting that reticular patterns favor a lymphomatous etiology [28]. Elastography is then used as a subsequent step to distinguish lymphoma from metastasis. Overall, integrating elastography with conventional B-mode ultrasound significantly enhances diagnostic performance. While Chami et al. noted that the addition of SWE improved sensitivity at the expense of specificity, broader evidence demonstrates simultaneous gains across both metrics [11, 15, 33]. Notably, combining SWE with conventional ultrasound improves radiologist accuracy when differentiating metastatic nodes from lymphoma, providing the most pronounced diagnostic benefit to less experienced readers [15]. Furthermore, multimodal strategies extend beyond B-mode imaging; integrating Doppler imaging with strain ratio evaluations yields a higher cumulative diagnostic accuracy than employing either modality independently [14, 41, 42].

Anatomical location directly impacts elastography measurements. Cervical nodes often appear artifactually stiffer than axillary nodes due to their proximity to the trachea and major vessels [16]. Additionally, achieving uniform probe compression is difficult given the neck’s natural curvature, anatomical depressions, and superficial nodal positioning [43]. These regional structural challenges degrade imaging quality and reduce the sensitivity of shear wave elastography [33]. Beyond these technical limitations, anatomical location may also correlate with intrinsic tissue stiffness. Chami et al. demonstrated that the elastographic stiffness of lymphomas differs significantly depending on whether the node is located in the head and neck, axilla, or inguinal region. Interestingly, this regional variability was not observed in benign or metastatic lesions [11]. Unfortunately, insufficient data in the primary literature precluded a subgroup analysis based on anatomical location in the present study.

The included studies generally reported high diagnostic reliability for elastography, with agreement metrics ranging from 0.79 to 0.999. Despite these high values, no clear reproducibility pattern emerged across the different elastographic modalities. Theoretically, quantitative elastography reduces operator dependence and inter-observer variability compared to qualitative methods [27], as qualitative strain elastography remains inherently limited by the requirement for consistent manual tissue compression [24]. These high rates of inter-observer agreement must be interpreted with caution. Some studies calculated this agreement based on retrospective reviews of pre-acquired elastographic cineloops or recorded movies [14, 22]. This methodology primarily measures the inter-reader agreement of existing images rather than the true inter-operator reproducibility of live image acquisition.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, there is inherent methodological heterogeneity across the included studies, encompassing variations in elastography systems, probe frequencies, region of interest (ROI) selection, and operator technique. Patient populations and baseline lymph node characteristics also varied widely. The presence of publication bias in the lymphoma versus metastatic comparison warrants a cautious interpretation of those specific pooled estimates. Additionally, a key limitation of this meta-analysis is the substantial risk of bias across the included studies, as assessed by QUADAS-2, which limits the reliability and generalizability of the pooled estimates. The most prominent concern was in Domain 2 (index test), where most studies derived elastography cut-off values post-hoc using ROC analysis rather than applying pre-specified thresholds. Only a small number of studies [23], used predefined cut-offs. This data-driven optimization likely inflates diagnostic performance, and a sensitivity analysis excluding high-risk studies was not feasible due to the limited number of eligible reports. Bias was also common in Domain 3 (reference standard). Many studies did not clearly report whether outcome assessors were blinded to imaging results, and several used FNAC/FNAB as the reference standard. Given that cytology does not reliably capture tissue architecture, particularly for lymphoma diagnosis and subtyping, this introduces potential misclassification and further compromises diagnostic accuracy estimates [44]. Finally, a scarcity of primary data regarding specific lymphoma subtypes and the primary origins of metastatic nodes precluded more granular subgroup analyses. Overall, these methodological limitations suggest that the pooled results should be interpreted cautiously, as they likely overestimate true clinical performance.

Clinically, the significant stiffness overlap between lymphomatous and metastatic nodes confirms that elastography remains insufficient as a standalone diagnostic tool. Our analysis reveals that no single elastographic modality achieves optimal results for both sensitivity and specificity simultaneously. Consequently, clinicians must interpret elastography findings cautiously, particularly in regions with a high prevalence of lymphoma, and strictly integrate these measurements with conventional imaging characteristics and the broader clinical context to guide management [18, 26].

Future research should prioritize methodological rigor and standardization of elastography protocols. This includes the use of pre-specified elastography thresholds, histopathology from excisional or adequately sampled core biopsies as the reference standard, and rigorous blinding of both index test and reference standard assessments. In addition, standardized ROI definitions and image acquisition techniques are needed to reduce inter-study heterogeneity. Finally, larger prospective multicenter studies are essential to validate modality-specific diagnostic performance and to further evaluate the role of elastography in distinguishing lymphoma subtypes and different metastatic origins.
