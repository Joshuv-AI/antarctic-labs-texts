Vulnerability to bullying victimization: an investigation of genetic and environmental risk factors in a twin-family study
Authors: Dana Martinschledde, Amelie Nikstat, Christian Kandler, Frank Neuner, Benjamin Iffland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Bullying victimization is associated with serious short- and long-term consequences, including mental health problems. The close link between victimization and psychopathology underscores the necessity of examining potential risk factors that may increase vulnerability to victimization. The present study sought to enhance understanding of the mechanisms underlying a transgenerational transmission of the risk for victimization by examining the interplay between genes and environment.Methods: This study extended previous research by employing a nuclear twin family design comprising monozygotic and dizygotic twins, their parents, and siblings. Using data from 1,915 German population-based twin families, structural equation modelling was applied to estimate genetic and environmental components of vulnerability to bullying victimization. Longitudinal path analyses were further conducted to examine how parenting style and children’s problem behaviour interact as risk factors contributing to the likelihood of being bullied.Results: Results indicated that genetic factors accounted for approximately one-third of the variance, which is most likely attributable to genetically predisposed characteristics that augment an individual’s risk of being bullied by peers. Environmental influences not shared by family members accounted for the remaining variance, emphasizing the significance of environmental factors that are unique to each sibling. Furthermore, longitudinal path analyses revealed that the relationship between a positive parenting style and children’s bullying victimization was mediated by children’s externalizing and internalizing problem behaviour.Conclusions: The findings emphasize a complex interplay between genetically influenced risk factors and environmental conditions in explaining the development of bullying victimization. Knowledge on risk factors is crucial for effective preventions and interventions.KEYWORDS: Bullying victimization, heritability, transgenerational transmission, nuclear twin family design, parenting style

Introduction

Bullying victimization is defined as an aggressive behaviour by one or more perpetrators towards a victim of the same age group. It involves unwanted and negative actions that are repeated over time and an imbalance of power or strength between the perpetrator and the victim (Olweus, 1997). A substantial body of research has demonstrated that experiences of bullying victimization in childhood and adolescence are associated with an elevated risk of developing various mental health conditions (Forbes et al., 2020; Kwan et al., 2022; Moore et al., 2017), low quality of life (Takizawa et al., 2014; Wolke et al., 2013), poor academic performance (Hahlweg & Schulz, 2020; Laith & Vaillancourt, 2022), and a paucity of social relationships in adulthood (Halliday et al., 2021; Matthews et al., 2022). Evidence of these negative, persistent consequences highlights the importance of thoroughly examining the etiology of bullying victimization. Twin studies are considered a promising approach in this regard because they allow genetic and environmental sources of interindividual differences in a specific trait (Boomsma et al., 2002).

Specifically, the classical twin design (CTD) is based on the assumption that monozygotic (MZ) twins are genetically identical siblings and also share environmental factors when they were reared together, whereas dizygotic (DZ) twins share, on average, 50% of their genetic makeup, which varies among humans, and also share environmental factors that act to make same similar (Boomsma et al., 2002). By collecting data from both types of twin pairs and comparing their covariances, the variance of a surveyed trait can be divided into three of four possible variance components. Additive genetic components (a2) reflect variance due to the aggregated effects of genetic variants, while non-additive genetic components (na2) encompass variance due to the effects genetic dominance effects within gene loci (d²) or gene x gene interaction effects between different gene loci (i², where na² = d² + i²). Shared environmental components (c2) refer to variance attributable to environmental influences that act to make twins more similar to each other, whereas non-shared environmental components (e², including measurement error) reflect variance due to factors that are specific for each twin and therefore act to make twins less similar to each other (Boomsma et al., 2002).

Previous CTD research examining genetic and environmental pathways to bullying victimization has highlighted the role of genetic and non-shared environmental variance components (Ball et al., 2008; Beaver et al., 2009; Bowes et al., 2013; Brendgen et al., 2014; Connolly & Beaver, 2016; Fisher et al., 2015; Johansson et al., 2022; Shakoor et al., 2015; Törn et al., 2015), whereas shared environmental effects have not been identified as playing a significant role in most studies (e.g. Beaver et al., 2009; Brendgen et al., 2014; Johansson et al., 2022; Törn et al., 2015). Upon closer inspection, however, the ratio of the genetic and non-shared environmental variance components is quite inconsistent in previous CTD studies. Estimates of genetic variance components are ranging from approximately 40% (Beaver et al., 2009; Brendgen et al., 2014; Fisher et al., 2015; Shakoor et al., 2015) to approximately 70% (Ball et al., 2008; Bowes et al., 2013; Connolly & Beaver, 2016; Johansson et al., 2022; Törn et al., 2015), with the remaining variance primarily attributable to non-shared environmental effects. As researchers previously discussed, the substantial variation in these estimates could be attributable to differences in age groups, informants, and survey methods (Veldkamp et al., 2019).

It is also important to acknowledge the limitations of the CTD, as its assumptions may be violated, resulting in biased estimates and limited interpretability of findings (Bleidorn et al., 2018; Instinske & Kandler, 2024). In particular, na2 and c2 cannot be estimated simultaneously in the CTD, which likely results in an underestimation of either shared environmental or non-additive genetic effects. Additionally, the CTD is based on the assumption that spouses do not select each other based on similarity in the examined trait. However, in reality, spouses may frequently exhibit a genetic similarity that exceeds chance levels (Robinson et al., 2017). The CTD's inability to account for assortative mating, due to missing parental data, may therefore lead to an overestimation of shared environmental effects and an underestimation of additive genetic effects (Boomsma et al., 2002). Additionally, the inability to ascertain whether a substantial shared environmental component is attributable to environmental influences shared exclusively by twins, by all siblings, or by all members of a family (Keller et al., 2010) further complicates the interpretation of the CTD's findings. Addressing these limitations is possible through the use of a nuclear twin family design (NTFD), which incorporates additional data from mothers, fathers, and siblings of each twin pair (Bleidorn et al., 2018; Eifler & Riemann, 2022; Nikstat & Riemann, 2020). The NTFD enables the division of c2 into environmental influences shared only by twins (t2) and by all siblings (s2), as well as environmental influences shared by children and either mother or father (m2 and f2). Furthermore, it facilitates the estimation of na2 and the majority of c2 components (except for s2) in the presence of each other (Bleidorn et al., 2018). The NTFD can also model effects of assortative mating (μ) and passive gene-environment correlation, which would indicate that parents create a family environment that correlates with their children's genetic makeup (Plomin et al., 1977). Consequently, the primary objective of the present examination was to address the limitations of previous studies and to provide more detailed and less biased estimates of genetic and environmental effects leading to interindividual differences in bullying victimization by using an NTFD.

Due to the limitations of the previously used CTD designs, it may be assumed that shared environmental effects have been mis-estimated in previous studies. For instance, a large body of research emphasizes the association of parenting styles and experiences of bullying victimization (Chu & Chen, 2025) indicating the significance of experiences that are most likely shared by twins and siblings. Additionally, the high co-occurrence of bullying victimization and maltreating parental behaviour, such as negative, harsh, punitive parenting, proposes an environmental pathway to bullying victimization potentially shared by siblings reared together (Lereya et al., 2015; Tremblay-Perreault & Hébert, 2020). Accordingly, it has been demonstrated that negative parenting practices increase children’s risk for bullying victimization (Charalampous et al., 2018; Lereya et al., 2013; Nocentini et al., 2019).

Especially in childhood and adolescence, family represents a crucial context for learning and developing behavioural regulation and socioemotional functioning (Hahlweg & Schulz, 2020; Phua et al., 2020). If parental behaviour is punitive, overprotective, or neglectful, it may lead to the development of internalizing or externalizing behavioural problems in children (Kuckertz et al., 2018; Lorence et al., 2019; Pinquart, 2017), which, in turn, may increase the risk of being exposed to bullying behaviour by making affected individuals appear as ‘easy targets’ (Hess et al., 2020; Košir et al., 2020; Menesini & Salmivalli, 2017). Hence, it can be assumed that, in addition to a direct effect of parenting styles on bullying victimization, there may also be an indirect effect involving children’s problem behaviour, which links parenting style and victimization. However, to date, no studies have explicitly examined such mediation hypotheses using a longitudinal design. Investigating this would have important implications for understanding the interrelationships of different risk factors.

Accordingly, the present study had two aims. The first objective was to disentangle genetic and environmental components of interindividual differences in the presence of victimization experiences by using a NTFD. In alignment with prior CTD findings (Beaver et al., 2009; Brendgen et al., 2014; Johansson et al., 2022), it was hypothesized (hypothesis 1) that NTFD results would demonstrate (H1a) a substantial additive genetic effect alongside (H1b) an even greater effect of non-shared environment. Contrary to prevailing findings based on CTD, it was expected that the NTFD would unveil at least (H1c) small significant environmental effects shared by all siblings (s2) and/or same-aged twins (t2).

Building upon the hypothesized shared environmental effects and given the availability of data from all family members, a second objective of the present study was to investigate the interrelationships between well-established risk factors for bullying victimization. Specifically, it was hypothesized that (H2a) parenting style is a predictor of bullying victimization and that (H2b) this association is mediated by children’s problem behaviour. To test Hypothesis 2, a path model was developed to assess the interplay between risk factors using data from two time points. The resulting path model incorporated predictive and mediating variables measured in a first assessment wave, whereas bullying victimization was assessed approximately two years later.

Method

Participants

We base our analyses on data from the first two waves of the TwinLife study (Diewald et al., 2021; Hahn et al., 2016), the first panel study of twin families in Germany based on a national probability sample (Lang & Kottwitz, 2017). The cross-sequential study surveys over 4,000 German twin families of four birth cohorts at regular intervals, with each family consisting of a same-sex MZ or DZ twin pair, their biological parents, and, if present, a full biological sibling. Zygosity was assessed with the Zygosity Questionnaire for Young Twins (Goldsmith, 1991) in the two younger cohorts and the Self Report Zygosity Questionnaire (Oniszczenko et al., 1993) in the two older cohorts as well as saliva tests (Lenau et al., 2017). The first assessment wave took place in 2014–2016, followed by the second wave in 2016–2018 which included several items on bullying victimization that were used in the present examination. As individuals under the age of 10 completed a questionnaire with a more limited range of response options adapted for children (Bullying- und Viktimisierungsfragebogen für Kinder, BVF-K; von Marées & Petermann, 2009), it was decided to exclude the youngest cohort (aged 7 at the time of assessment) and siblings under the age of 10 from the analyses.

The TwinLife study was reviewed and approved by the German Psychological Society (Deutsche Gesellschaft für Psychologie; protocol number: RR 11.2009). All participants were informed in writing about the aims of the TwinLife study, the incentives, and that their participation was voluntary and could be withdrawn at any time. In addition, in all face-to-face surveys, participants received a two-page leaflet summarizing the procedures concerning data protection and privacy and their rights to demand the deletion of all data collected in line with German data integrity laws. Written material was sent to participants before all interviews. At the beginning of each interview, informed verbal consent was obtained from participants (and the participants’ legal guardians when the participants were underage) and documented. Following the national legislation and the institutional requirements, written informed consent was not required to participate in this study. Nevertheless, for all supplementary online surveys, written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Instruments

Bullying victimization

Bullying victimization was assessed using the Gatehouse Bullying Scale (GBS; Bond et al., 2007), a self-report questionnaire measuring four subtypes of victimization with a total of 12 items. The GBS distinguishes between direct forms of victimization, including physical threats and verbal abuse, as well as indirect forms of victimization, which contain rumor-spreading by classmates and exclusion from certain activities. In the second assessment wave, the frequency of experiencing each subtype of victimization was reported on a 4-point Likert-type scale (1 = ‘never’, 2 = ‘less than once a week’, 3 = ‘about once a week’, 4 = ‘most days’). Individuals who were attending school at the time of the survey were asked how often bullying victimization had occurred ‘recently’, whereas individuals who were no longer attending school retrospectively reported the frequency of bullying victimization during their entire school career. Following the procedure of similar studies (Bonell et al., 2018; Brendgen et al., 2015), the items of each subtype were summed into a total victimization score, with higher values indicating more frequent and more complex bullying victimization (Cronbach’s α = .58). As the focus of the current study was on the mere presence of bullying victimization, rather than the subjective burden of this phenomenon, ratings of burden were not included in the calculation of the total score.

Parenting style

As parenting styles reported by mothers or fathers may not align with the perception of children, it was decided to assess parental behaviour and involvement using reports of the twins and siblings. In the first assessment wave, all twins and siblings were asked to rate the parenting styles of their mother and father separately by using 13 items each. These items could be assigned to the subscales emotional warmth, negative communication (reversed), control (reversed), monitoring, and inconsistent parenting (reversed). Answers were given on a five-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 = ‘never’ to 5 = ‘very often’. Means for each subscale were calculated, z-standardized, and averaged into an overall score for positive parenting style with higher values indicating a warmer, more supportive, and consistent parenting behaviour (Cronbach’s α = .81). For each twin and sibling, the mean of maternal and paternal parenting style was calculated.

Problem behaviour

The Strengths & Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ; Goodman, 1997) was utilized to assess potential problem behaviour of twins and siblings. As part of the first assessment wave, individuals below the age of 18 completed the child version, while older individuals completed the adult version of the SDQ. Participants were asked to respond to four of the five subscales of the self-report instrument using a 3-point Likert-type scale (0 = ‘not true’ to 2 = ‘certainly true’). Internalizing problem behaviour was assessed using the subscales peer problems and anxiety. Externalizing problem behaviour was measured using the subscales conduct and hyperactivity. Each subscale consisted of five items. The scores for internalizing and externalizing problem behaviour were summed to create an overall problem behaviour score, with higher values indicating greater problem behaviour (Cronbach’s α = .75).

Statistical analyses

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 27) and IBM SPSS AMOS (Version 27). Individuals who did not answer items on bullying victimization were excluded. To minimize the risk of obtaining biased estimates, outliers whose total victimization scores were more than 1.5 times the interquartile range above the upper quartile were not included in the analyses either (Frigge et al., 1989), resulting in the exclusion of 108 firstborn twins, 109 secondborn twins, 55 siblings, 51 mothers, and 32 fathers. To obtain a nuanced view of the prevalence of bullying victimization, frequencies were calculated for each of the four subtypes separately. Following the procedure of similar studies (Due et al., 2019; Ossa et al., 2021), ratings were dichotomized and individuals were considered to have experienced a subtype of bullying victimization if their response to the corresponding item was at least 3 (‘about once a week’) or 4 (‘most days’).

As differences in sex and age may affect covariances among family members, the correction for these effects is a conventional practice in the context of twin studies to avoid biased estimations of model parameters (Bleidorn et al., 2018; McGue & Bouchard, 1984). Across all participants of the present examination, higher age was associated with a greater bullying victimization score (β = .11, p < .001). Females tended to have significantly lower bullying victimization scores than males (β = −.04, p = .005). Therefore, standardized residual scores were calculated and used for all further analyses to control for linear sex and age effects.

After calculating the phenotypic kin correlations, structural equation modelling (SEM) techniques were used to determine the composition of genetic and environmental components explaining the variance in bullying victimization. Specifically, the procedure described by Bleidorn et al. (2018) was applied to the data of the present examination. With different path diagrams for MZ twin families and DZ twin families (see Figure 1), NTFD analyses were performed using Full Information Maximum Likelihood estimation in a multi-group design.

As non-additive genetic effects (i) and sibling-specific shared environmental effects (s) cannot be estimated simultaneously in the present model, the first step consisted of computing two alternative baseline models. In one model, non-additive genetic effects (i) were included and sibling-specific shared environmental effects were set to zero (s = 0), whereas the other model allowed for sibling-specific shared environmental effects instead of non-additive genetic effects (i = 0). All other parameters were set free. A comparison of the Akaike information criterion (AIC) values was then used to determine which baseline model fit the data better, with lower AIC values indicating a better model fit. Next, parameters that did not reach statistical significance in the baseline model were identified. Several nested model comparisons were then performed to check whether these parameters could be fixed step-by-step to zero without leading to a substantial decline in model fit. The χ2 difference test was used to identify the most parsimonious model which did not lead to a significant decrease in model fit compared to the baseline model.

Path and mediation analyses were conducted to examine the potential impact of environmentally influenced factors on the victimization of children. The latter were performed using the PROCESS macro for IBM SPSS Statistics (Hayes, 2022). Specifically, it was tested whether children’s problem behaviour mediated the relationship between parenting style and children’s bullying victimization. Again, standardized residual scores were calculated for all variables to control for age and gender effects. Notably, only data of the second cohort with twins aged 12–14 and siblings aged 10–15 were used to ensure temporal antecedence of predictive and mediating variables in contrast to bullying victimization, which was assessed two years later.

Kin correlations

Table 3 shows the correlations of the total victimization score for all dyads of family members. Since the degree of genetic relatedness is known for each dyad, a closer look at the phenotypic kin correlations can provide first indications of the specific pattern of genetic and environmental effects. In the current study, the correlation between MZ twins was higher than the correlation between other siblings or between parents and their offspring, suggesting the presence of genetic influences (a2 and na²). However, the MZ twin correlation was rather small and less than 1, pointing towards an important role of non-shared environmental influences (e2). DZ twin correlations were not higher than half of the MZ twin correlations, indicating that shared environmental influences were rather negligible. The latter is also underscored by the fact that parent–child and twin-sibling correlations were not larger than the DZ twin correlations, which argues against the presence of twin- or sibling-specific effects of shared environment (t2 or s2). Additionally, the average mother–child correlations tended to be slightly larger and more often statistically significant than the average father–child correlations. As indicated by the non-significant correlation between parents, no evidence for phenotypic assortative mating (μ) was found.

NTFD results

In general, the two alternative baseline models i = 0 (i.e. assuming that na = i, non-additive genetic effects due to gene × gene interactions) and s = 0 showed a good model fit, as indicated by comparative fit indices (CFI) > .95 and root mean square errors of approximation (RMSEA) < .05 (see Table 4 for corresponding fit indices). However, the model in which sibling-specific shared environmental influences were set to zero (s = 0) showed a slightly lower AIC value and was therefore set as the baseline model. It was found that non-additive genetic effects (i), twin-specific shared environmental effects (t), environmental transmission from mother to offspring (m), and influences of phenotypic assortative mating (μ) did not reach statistical significance and could be fixed to zero without leading to a significant decrease in model fit (Δχ2 = 7.65, Δdf = 4, Δp = .121).

Path analysis results

The subsample used for the path and mediation analyses consisted of 1,426 individuals (658 firstborn twins, 654 secondborn twins, and 114 siblings). First, positive parenting style was found to have a significant negative effect on bullying victimization of the child (β = −.09, p < .001). However, the association between these variables was mediated by problem behaviour of the children, as illustrated by a significant indirect effect (β = −.05, 95% CI [−.07, −.03], Z = −4.95, p < .001). When the mediator was included in the model, the direct effect of positive parenting style on children’s bullying victimization was no longer significant (β = −.04, p = .121), suggesting a full mediation. Discussion

The present study utilized a population-based German sample of MZ and DZ twin pairs and their mothers, fathers, and siblings to address two objectives. First, the study sought to gain more detailed insight into genetic and environmental pathways that lead to interindividual differences in bullying victimization. To this end, an NTFD was employed to overcome the limitations of the previously used CTD. Secondly, the study sought to elucidate a potential shared environmental pathway to bullying victimization by investigating the mediating role of children’s problem behaviour in the relationship between parenting styles and children's bullying victimization.

In terms of prevalence, 8.62% of the present sample reported having experienced at least one of four subtypes of bullying victimization. This finding aligns with the results of the German Health Behavior in School-Aged Children (HBSC) study, which reported a prevalence of 8.6% among 15-year-old students (Fischer et al., 2020). Consistent with recent results of Veldkamp et al. (2019), verbal abuse was found to be the most prevalent form of bullying victimization (6.49%), while physical victimization was reported with the lowest frequency (0.19%). Findings revealed no statistically significant gender difference in the overall prevalence of having experienced at least one subtype of bullying victimization. However, while male participants were more likely to report direct forms of victimization, female participants were more often affected by indirect forms of victimization, which is consistent with the pattern widely reported in the literature (Dukes et al., 2010; Fischer et al., 2020; Nansel et al., 2001).

With respect to structural equation modelling, the NTFD results indicated a considerable genetic contribution, accounting for approximately 30% of the variance in bullying victimization, which confirmed hypothesis H1a. The genetic factors were primarily additive, while non-additive genetic effects were found to be non-signficant. After correction for attenuation due to error of measurement (.29/.58), the heritability estimation increases to 50%.

The finding of substantial heritability is consistent with a group of studies that have reported heritability estimates ranging from 32 to 45% (Beaver et al., 2009; Brendgen et al., 2014; Fisher et al., 2015; Shakoor et al., 2015). However, it does not align with investigations that estimated heritability around 70% (Ball et al., 2008; Bowes et al., 2013; Connolly & Beaver, 2016; Johansson et al., 2022; Törn et al., 2015). As previously discussed, the variability in results could be due to methodological differences between studies. For instance, some studies used maternal reports (Ball et al., 2008; Bowes et al., 2013) or peer nomination procedures (Johansson et al., 2022) to ascertain the frequency of bullying victimization, whereas others employed self-reports (Beaver et al., 2009; Shakoor et al., 2015). In addition to the varying age ranges of samples, differences in the conceptualization of bullying victimization may also play an important role. Recently, Eastman et al. (2018) and Veldkamp et al. (2019) distinguished between several subtypes of bullying victimization and estimated heritability for each type separately. With 42% (Eastman et al., 2018) and 70% (Veldkamp et al., 2019), both studies indicated a higher heritability for physical compared to relational forms of victimization (0% and 55%, respectively). Hence, the inconsistency in previous findings may partly result from different selections and combinations of victimization subtypes in questionnaires and sum scores (Johansson et al., 2022).

The present investigation adopted the approach of summing victimization subtypes into a continuous scale, as utilized by the majority of previous studies (e.g. Brendgen et al., 2014; Shakoor et al., 2015), to test the accuracy of their estimates in an extended design. It is noteworthy that the NTFD eliminated some sources of over- and underestimation, thereby revealing a substantial additive genetic effect that was marginally less pronounced than previous estimates derived from the CTD. This finding suggests that the heritability values reported by the majority of studies may require downward revision. However, this suggestion should be interpreted with caution, as summing different subtypes of bullying into a total victimization score also entails certain methodological limitations. The subtypes of bullying victimization differed substantially in their prevalence (0.19–6.49%), meaning that more common forms of bullying may have exerted a disproportionate influence on model estimates compared to rarer subtypes. Moreover, the various subtypes may be driven by distinct social and developmental mechanisms that are differentially influenced by genetic and environmental factors. Consistent with this, prior studies indicate that genetic and environmental estimates vary across bullying subtypes (Eastman et al., 2018; Veldkamp et al., 2019). Consequently, aggregating across subtypes into a total victimization score could conceal genuine genetic and environmental effects.

Given that bullying victimization is neither a personality trait nor a behaviour initiated by the affected individual, the finding of 30% to 50% heritability for bullying victimization may initially appear counterintuitive. Most likely, the additive genetic effect reflects the presence of traits or behaviours that are to some extent heritable and increase an individual's vulnerability to being bullied by peers (Ball et al., 2008; Johansson et al., 2022). As the results of the path analysis revealed a significant direct effect of problem behaviour on bullying victimization, it may be suggested that problem behaviour is one of the characteristics that increase the risk for victimization. By differentiating between internalizing and externalizing problem behaviour, the present study further showed that both types of problem behaviour partially mediated the association between parenting style and children’s bullying victimization. Hence, individuals with low self-esteem, who tend to be anxious, nervous, or withdrawn, may appear as ‘easy targets’ to potential perpetrators and are therefore confronted with an increased risk of bullying victimization (Cosma et al., 2018; Kelly et al., 2015). Conversely, individuals exhibiting restless, aggressive, or poorly controlled behaviour may stand out from their peers, potentially leading to conflicts and, consequently, being more susceptible to bullying victimization (Hahlweg & Schulz, 2020; Reijntjes et al., 2011).

It is noteworthy that internalizing and externalizing problem behaviour have recently been demonstrated to be moderately heritable (Nikstat & Riemann, 2020), which could partially account for the additive genetic effect observed in the present study. Hence, it may be speculated that the results of the path analysis, with effects of problem behaviour outperforming the effects of parenting style, reflect the same mechanisms as the NTFD. That is, rather than elucidating a potential shared environmental pathway to bullying victimization, the path analysis may also highlight the role of additive genetic effects that manifest in children’s problem behaviour. Accordingly, the significance of genetically predisposed risk-elevating characteristics is further supported by the findings of Schoeler et al. (2019), who reported an association between bullying victimization and genetic risk related to mental health vulnerabilities, body mass index (BMI), intelligence, and risk-taking. In this context, it would be promising to further investigate potential evocative gene-environment correlations, which would imply that genetically influenced characteristics of an individual (i.e. problem behaviour) provoke certain reactions from the environment (i.e. bullying behaviour). To examine this type of gene-environment interplay, explicit modelling and further extensions of the NTFD would be required (Bleidorn et al., 2018).

In addition to additive genetic effects, only a small negative (non-genetic) effect from fathers’ vulnerability to bullying victimization on their offspring’s vulnerability was found, accounting for less than 1% of the variance. Non-shared environmental effects accounted for the remaining part of the variance in bullying victimization (approximately 72%), in line with hypothesis H1b, but contradicting H1c. Importantly, this value also includes measurement error, but it still suggests a substantial contribution of non-shared environmental influences on interindividual differences in bullying victimization of about 50% after correction for attenuation due to error of measurement: (.72-[1–.58])/.58 = .52. As previously mentioned, these influences result in a reduction of similarity between twins (Connolly & Beaver, 2016). Non-shared environmental effects typically reflect the impact of social experiences, life events, or peer groups that are unique to each twin (Connolly & Beaver, 2016). Accordingly, school climate and classroom characteristics (Doll et al., 2004; Kasen et al., 2010), teachers‘ responses to bullying (Kochenderfer-Ladd & Pelletier, 2008; Yoon & Bauman, 2014) and peer group norms (Cook et al., 2010; Lodder et al., 2016) have been reported to be significant risk factors for bullying victimization. Such events can be classified into non-systematic (e.g. accidents, illness, trauma) and systematic environmental factors (e.g. peer networks, classrooms, differential parenting, social media) that affect children differently (Brendgen et al., 2005; Plomin & Daniels, 2011). Notably, non-shared environmental effects had a more significant impact than shared environmental influences, as indicated by the non-significant twin-specific and sibling-specific shared environmental effects. Although this finding should be treated with caution, as shared environmental effects may have been obscured by the model specification and methodological shortcomings (e.g. measurements), it is consistent with the results of previous twin studies, which found that the best-fitting models included additive genetic and non-shared environmental effects, while shared environmental effects rarely reached significance (Ball et al., 2008; Beaver et al., 2009; Bowes et al., 2013; Brendgen et al., 2013; Connolly & Beaver, 2016; Törn et al., 2015). Consequently, it can be concluded that the basic pattern of results found with the CTD in previous investigations remained to be the best-fitting solution in the extended design of the current study.

At the same time, however, the absence of significant shared environmental effects appears to contradict – in the first instance – the findings of the path analysis, which underscore the significance of family-level protective factors (i.e. positive parenting) in buffering vulnerability to bullying victimization (in line with H2a). Specifically, the present analysis confirmed an indirect negative effect of positive parenting style on bullying victimization elucidating that children’s behaviour entirely mediated the association between parenting style and bullying victimization of children (confirming H2b). Given that early experiences in the family environment are likely to shape social functioning and behavioural regulation skills, the combination of unfavourable conditions and genetic predisposition could result in the development of problem behaviour, which may in turn increase the risk for bullying victimization (Hahlweg & Schulz, 2020).

Usually, influences such as parenting style or family support are thought to have the same effect on both twins, therefore being considered as shared-environmental influences (Connolly & Beaver, 2016). However, the present study suggests that this may not always be the case. A potential explanation may be that family factors have a differential impact on siblings (Bleidorn et al., 2018). In the context of parenting behaviour, it is conceivable that parents spend different amounts of time with each child or display varying levels of warmth and control (Plomin & Daniels, 2011). Such differences may also depend on child characteristics such as personality, temperament, or behaviour (Plomin, 2024). On the other hand, as Plomin and Daniels (2011) have posited, it is the effect of an event on a phenotype that shapes environmental influences, rather than the event itself. Consequently, children from the same family – even twins – may perceive parental behaviour, the school environment, or even shared peer groups differently (Plomin & Daniels, 2011). That is, even an environmental event that is shared by twins or siblings may produce non-shared environmental effects (Plomin, 2024). For instance, a parental divorce affects both twins, yet one may experience distress and behavioural changes, whereas the other remains comparatively unaffected. Such differences imply that these factors may be more accurately classified as non-shared environmental influences, contributing to sibling dissimilarity (Plomin & Daniels, 2011). Consistent with this reasoning, our data revealed only weak to moderate agreement between twins and between twins and their siblings in the evaluation of parenting style (see Supplement). Because individual reports from each twin and sibling were used to assess parental behaviour and involvement, the mediation analyses reflect child-specific, idiosyncratic relationships that cannot be generalized to all children within a family. In other words, the analyses relate not to actual parenting behaviour but to its subjective perception and evaluation, which in turn are associated with children’s problem behaviour and victimization. Nonetheless, classifying parenting style as a non-shared environmental influence remains speculative, and further research is needed to determine which risk factors for bullying victimization can be attributed to shared versus non-shared environments. In particular, additional multi-informant assessments are required to disentangle shared and differential family effects within sibling pairs.

It is noteworthy that the finding of a small direct effect from fathers to offspring (f) was an exception among the rest of the non-significant shared environmental effects. Furthermore, a small negative passive gene-environment correlation was identified (−.02). This finding suggests the possibility that some parents in the present sample may have transmitted genes to their children that increase the risk of bullying victimization, while concurrently providing an environment that is conducive to the development of vulnerability to bullying victimization (Plomin et al., 1977). The significant environmental transmission from fathers to offspring suggests that the risk-reducing effect of the environment is primarily attributable to the victimization experiences of the father (Bleidorn et al., 2018). Therefore, it could be speculated that if children inherit characteristics that increase their risk for bullying victimization (e.g. problem behaviour), parents may create an environment that works against the formation of these characteristics because their exposure to bullying victimization led to a heightened awareness of risk factors and their consequences (Allison et al., 2014). However, the effect was very small and the possibility of a statistical artifact could not be ruled out. Consistent with Bleidorn et al. (2018) who recently observed an effect of a comparable size and direction in a NTFD, it was decided to refrain from further interpretation of this result until its replication in subsequent studies. It is crucial to note that the absence of statistical significance or small values of environmental transmission from parents to offspring do not imply the absence of these effects. Instead, these findings may support the hypothesis that parental behaviour, even when shared by siblings, can have differential effects on offspring (Plomin, 2024). Consequently, such behaviour would need to be classified as non-shared environment.

The present findings suggest several promising avenues for enhancing the effectiveness of interventional approaches. The results of the path model align with recent research indicating that positive parenting (Zych et al., 2019) can protect children from becoming victims of bullying and buffer the development of internalizing symptoms (Bachler et al., 2018). It is therefore crucial to strengthen parenting competencies, particularly those associated with authoritative parenting styles, which have been shown to provide the greatest protection against victimization (Charalampous et al., 2018). Moreover, mental health professionals should not only address the negative consequences of victimization but also support individuals in managing genetically influenced characteristics – such as problem behaviour – that increase vulnerability to victimization (Bowes et al., 2013). Enhancing the management of such behavioural difficulties may help prevent both future victimization and the chronic progression of associated symptoms (Eastman et al., 2018).

Limitations

Besides its strengths, the NTFD has some limitations that should be considered when evaluating at the results. First, while the NTFD distinguishes between different sources of shared environment, it is not possible to estimate non-additive genetic effects (i2) and sibling-specific shared environmental effects (s2) within the same model. Additionally, while passive gene-environment correlation can be calculated, it is not possible to model other types of gene-environment correlation or interaction. As previously mentioned, further research on evocative gene-environment correlation is necessary to determine if genetically influenced personality traits or behaviour patterns can provoke a certain reaction (i.e. bullying behaviour) from the environment. To estimate more parameters and model additional types of gene-environment correlation or interaction, future studies should aim to extend the NTFD by including partners of twins and potential moderating or mediating variables (Bleidorn et al., 2018).

As participants under the age of 10 completed a different questionnaire to assess bullying victimization, the youngest cohort was excluded from the analyses. The remaining sample of twin pairs was relatively old (12 to 27 years), which precludes the possibility of generalizing the present results to younger children. Future research should compare patterns of genetic and environmental influences between different age groups and males and females to derive implications for the tailoring of interventional approaches.

Furthermore, it should be noted that school-attending participants were asked to report the frequency of bullying victimization experienced during the previous three months, whereas parents as well as older twins and siblings provided retrospective reports covering their entire school career. This discrepancy introduces a temporal heterogeneity in the victimization measure, as the assessment combines reports referring to recent experiences for those still in school with retrospective lifetime reports for those who have completed schooling. Such heterogeneity creates ambiguity regarding the timing of exposure and complicates causal interpretations, particularly in the mediation models linking parenting practices, problem behaviours, and victimization. Moreover, retrospective assessments may be affected by memory or reporting biases and by current psychological functioning, and some reported victimization experiences may precede the measurement of the proposed predictors. Together, these issues may have led to biased parameter estimates in the NTFD and limit the conclusions that can be drawn about directional mechanisms.

Importantly, bullying victimization was assessed with only four ordinally scaled items, which were subsequently summed to a total victimization score. Combined with the use of a non-clinical sample, the limited number of relatively imprecise items resulted in a strongly right-skewed distribution of bullying victimization. Additionally, Cronbach’s α was questionable for all scales, but especially low for the total victimization score (.58), indicating poor reliability. In the present study, however, the assessment of bullying victimization was treated as an event list, with the presence of specific negative experiences being queried. As event lists are not developed according to classical test theory or the effect indicator model (Netland, 2001), special conditions apply to their validation (Sansen et al., 2013). For example, the aforementioned theories are based on the assumption that there are correlations between indicators of a construct, which reflect an underlying latent construct that can be considered the cause of the correlation (Bühner, 2011). However, different individual experiences do not necessarily represent a latent construct and may or may not correlate with each other. Therefore, internal consistency is likely not an appropriate measure of reliability for evaluating event lists (Cleary, 1981; Netland, 2001). Additionally, the aggregation of the heterogeneous victimization subtypes into a total victimization score represents a potential methodological limitation. Hence, it is recommended that future research employ more comprehensive questionnaires to assess bullying victimization with greater precision. Further, it may be beneficial to distinguish between different forms of bullying victimization in future studies because estimates of heritability and environmental impact have been shown to vary as a function of subtype (Eastman et al., 2018; Veldkamp et al., 2019). Additionally, the use of high-risk or clinical samples may further influence the generalizability of the present findings.

Finally, the argument has been posited that twins, due to their distinctive appearance and tendency to stand out in groups, may not be representative of the general population (Bleidorn et al., 2018). A comparison of bullying rates between twins and their non-twin siblings revealed that siblings were subjected to bullying at a significantly higher frequency. This finding suggests that twins’ distinctive appearance and their status as a fixed pair within a dyad may serve as deterrents for potential perpetrators. Consequently, the findings suggest that the limited generalizability of twin data, which is a consequence of their genetically informative design, may be a trade-off for the benefits that accrue from studying data of twin pairs.

Conclusion

The present study was among the first to utilize an NTFD to disentangle genetic and environmental pathways leading to interindividual differences in bullying victimization. By extending the CTD to include data of mothers, fathers, and siblings, different sources of shared environment could be distinguished and passive gene-environment correlation was modelled. Additionally, it was possible to consider a potential influence of assortative mating, which did not reach significance in the current sample. Notably, additive genetic effects accounted for approximately one-third to a half of the variance in bullying victimization, indicating that previous studies using the CTD have slightly overestimated heritability. In the context of bullying victimization, genetic effects are most likely due to certain characteristics that are genetically transmitted from parents to offspring and increase the risk for bullying victimization. Importantly, the fact that previous heritability estimates need to be corrected downwards highlights the role of environmental influences in shaping the vulnerability for victimization. As non-shared environmental effects accounted for the rest of the variance in bullying victimization, factors such as individually unique social experiences or peer groups make a significant contribution to elucidating differences in vulnerability to victimization within twin pairs.

Mediation analyses provided preliminary insight into specific factors that may contribute to the significance of genetic and environmental pathways. Specifically, internalizing and externalizing problem behaviour mediated the relationship between parenting style and bullying victimization, suggesting that problem behaviour is likely to be one of the partly heritable characteristics contributing to the aforementioned significant genetic effect. Interventions targeting problem behaviour might not only prevent children from being bullied but also mitigate the risk of long-term negative consequences.

Furthermore, subsequent studies should employ more extensive designs and incorporate additional variables to ascertain which traits, behaviours, and environmental factors are accountable for the present findings. By doing so, these future studies will be able to provide evidence regarding the replicability of the current findings and also facilitate a more profound comprehension of the particular interplay between genetic and environmental factors that increases vulnerability for bullying victimization.
