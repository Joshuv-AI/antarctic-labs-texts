Advancements in surgical treatments for Huntington disease: From pallidotomy to experimental therapies
Authors: Leo J.Y. Kim, Bornali Kundu, Paolo Moretti, Andres M. Lozano, Shervin Rahimpour
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Huntington disease (HD) is an autosomal dominant neurodegenerative disorder characterized by choreic movements, behavioral changes, and cognitive impairment. The pathogenesis of this process is a consequence of mutant protein toxicity in striatal and cortical neurons. Thus far, neurosurgical management of HD has largely been limited to symptomatic relief of motor symptoms using ablative and stimulation techniques. These interventions, however, do not modify the progressive course of the disease. More recently, disease-modifying experimental therapeutic strategies have emerged targeting intrastriatal infusion of neurotrophic factors, cell transplantation, HTT gene silencing, and delivery of intrabodies. Herein we review therapies requiring neurosurgical intervention, including those targeting symptom management and more recent disease-modifying agents, with a focus on safety, efficacy, and surgical considerations.

Introduction

Huntington disease (HD) is a neurodegenerative disease caused by an autosomal dominant CAG trinucleotide repeat (n ​≥ ​36) expansion in exon 1 of the huntingtin gene (HTT), with an elongating polyglutamine chain at the N terminus of the huntingtin protein [[1], [2], [3]]. Wild-type huntingtin (wtHTT) is ubiquitously expressed and has a complex structure and interactomes that suggest its function as a scaffolding protein, coordinating other protein or cellular functions. In neurons, it controls transcription of several gene products (e.g., brain-derived neurotrophic factors [BDNF]) that regulate fast axonal trafficking, vesicle transport, and synaptic transmission [4]. Mutant huntingtin (mHTT) is a gain-of-function mutation that disrupts these pathways through various mechanisms that result in cytotoxicity. The medium spiny neurons (MSNs) of the striatum are particularly susceptible. The MSNs of the indirect pathway are initially lost, which contributes to hyperkinesia; then, the MSNs in the direct pathway degenerate and lead to hypokinesia [5,6]. Clinically, this dysfunction manifests as chorea and nonmotor features such as cognitive decline, apathy, depression, irritability, anxiety, obsessive/compulsive behaviors, and, rarely, psychosis [7]. Longer CAG repeats are associated with earlier onset and faster clinical progression, with symptoms typically progressing and leading to death 10–15 years after onset [8].

The discovery of the HTT mutation in HD in 1993 fostered multimodal therapeutic development targeting mHTT. Surgical treatment of HD dates back to the birth of stereotactic neurosurgery in 1947. Spiegel and Wycis [9] described a novel stereotactic apparatus to perform a pallidotomy in a patient with HD. The authors then focused on the treatment of choreic movements with surgery targeting both the dorsomedial nucleus (thalamotomy) and the globus pallidus (pallidotomy). After early reports of ablation, stimulation became the dominating method of neuromodulation. More recently, emerging technologies for intrastriatal infusion permit safe delivery of biologic therapies including neurotrophic factors, cell transplantation, HTT gene silencing, and intrabodies. Here, we review these surgical interventions targeting symptom management and disease modification and discuss future directions in the neurosurgical treatment of HD.

Therapeutic Approaches

Symptom management: ablation and stimulation

More than 55 years ago, Spiegel and Wycis [9] injected alcohol into the pallidum to relieve jerking of the head and limbs in a patient with Huntington chorea. The patient improved and lived for 15 years. Since then, pallidotomy has been shown to be an effective treatment for parkinsonism, choreic dyskinesias, and dystonia [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. Cubo et al. [14] performed bilateral pallidotomies of the globus pallidus internus (GPi) in a 13-year-old patient with Westphal variant HD with intractable generalized dystonia. At 3-month follow-up, there was minimal improvement in dystonia and marked worsening of spasticity.

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) is a nondestructive, adaptable, and reversible alternative to ablation surgery (Fig. 1A) [15]. Moro et al. [16] first described DBS of the GPi in a patient with HD. Stimulation in the dorsal portion of the GPi resulted in marked improvement in chorea and dystonia. Notably, these effects were seen at 40-Hz stimulation and resulted in pronounced parkinsonism at 130 ​Hz. Subsequent case reports have recapitulated the finding that DBS reduces hyperkinetic movement disorders (chorea) [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21]]. Gonzalez et al. [22] reported a prospective open-label study of 7 patients undergoing GPi DBS with median follow-up of 3 years. DBS of the globus pallidus externus (GPe) has also been theorized to be an effective alternative target for symptom control in HD [23]. Striatal-GPe neurons (indirect pathway) are more affected in the early stages of HD, leading to a hyperkinetic state (chorea). In the advanced stages of HD, the direct pathway's striatal-GPi neurons degenerate, accounting for rigidity and akinesia. A logical strategy would be inhibiting the overly active GPe. In a transgenic rat model of HD, Temel et al. [24] showed improvement in cognitive and motor symptoms with clinically relevant stimulation of the globus pallidus (equivalent of GPe in primates) (Supplementary Table 1). Wojtecki et al. [25] studied GPi/GPe border zone stimulation (upper two contacts in GPe, lower two contacts in GPi) in 6 patients. In a controlled, double-blinded study, patients were randomly assigned to either 6 weeks of GPi or GPe stimulation followed by 3 months of chronic pallidal stimulation at the site with the best effect–side effect ratio. Both pallidal sites proved to be equally efficacious, with slightly better cognitive outcomes in the GPe group. In total, 36 patients with HD treated with DBS have been reported in the literature (Table 1, Supplementary Table 2) [14,15,[17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22],[25], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30], [31], [32]]. DBS most effectively reduces chorea (∼56%), whereas improvement in dystonia is minor (∼1%), consistent with previous meta-analyses (Fig. 1B–C) [16,33]. Surgical therapies.

Disease modification: infusion and transplantation

In HD, mHTT is the predominant upstream gain-of-function mechanism that disrupts numerous neuronal functions such as synaptic dysfunction, altered axonal trafficking, and extrasynaptic excitotoxicity [32,[34], [35], [36]]. Promising molecular approaches modify HD disease progression by constitutively or temporarily depleting mHTT at genomic, transcriptional, and translational states. Fetal tissue or stem cell (SC) transplantation may further reduce or halt HD progression by promoting restoration of direct or indirect pathways. Stereotactic neurosurgical techniques will be critical in optimizing therapeutic efficacy through safe, reproducible treatment delivery.

HTT gene silencing

Strategies to reduce mHTT have received the greatest investigative attention because of the therapeutic potential to ameliorate the widest breadth of pathological processes [37]. Post-transcriptional interventions using antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), RNAi, and RNA splicing modulators have demonstrated effective decreases of mHTT in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) specimens from preclinical and clinical trials [[38], [39], [40]]. Intrathecal delivery of ASOs through serial lumbar punctures is a U.S. Food and Drug Administration–approved therapeutic modality to treat heritable neurological diseases such as spinal muscular dystrophy in infants and young children (Fig. 2A) [[40], [41], [42]]. ASOs bind specific RNAs according to Watson–Crick pairing to modulate the translation of target gene products through transcript degradation, RNA splicing alteration, RNA-binding protein displacement, and/or regulatory structure disruption (Fig. 2B). Tominersen is an ASO-targeting exon 36 of human HTT mRNA that results in RNase H-mediated degradation of both wild-type and mutant HTT transcripts [40,43]. In Phase 1/2a studies (NCT02519036) of the Ionis/Roche program, intrathecal bolus administration of tominersen through serial lumbar punctures every 4 weeks achieved therapeutic CSF concentrations without any serious adverse events and ∼40% reduction of mHTT in CSF after four doses [45]. On the basis of these data and the open-label extension of the Phase 1/2a trials, a large multinational Phase 3 was initiated with 1–2 intrathecal boluses of tominersen or placebo every 4 weeks, followed by serial lumbar punctures for administration every 8 (Q8W) or 16 (Q16W) weeks. The trial was halted when a planned interim analysis showed patients in the Q8W arm had lower CSF mHTT levels but were doing worse on the composite UHDRS or total functional capacity compared with those in the placebo arm; the Q16W arm showed no significant difference in functional outcome from the placebo arm. Furthermore, the patients in the Q8W arm had higher rates of adverse events, including falls, post-lumbar puncture syndrome, and symptomatic hydrocephalus [46]. Possible reasons for failure included inflammatory response due to excessive reduction of wtHTT protein evidenced by increased CSF pleocytosis and neurofilament light protein. Post-hoc analysis further suggested that younger patients with earlier stages of disease may not only better tolerate the inflammatory response, which decreases with repeated administration, but also benefit from earlier intervention with possibly lower concentrations. Thus, Roche plans to continue the tominersen program in a new Phase 2b clinical trial with younger patients with lower disease burden to explore different doses [47].

Mutant allele-specific ASOs have undergone clinical trials led by Wave Life Sciences studying WVE-120101 (NCT03225833) and WVE-120102 (NCT03225846) [48]. Phase 1/2 trials did not show either agents’ efficacy in lowering the huntingtin gene product after Q4H intrathecal administration. These ASOs were developed without animal model studies where specific mHTT single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) targeted by these agents were not available. An ASO-based drug (TTX-3360, Triplet Therapeutics) targeting the DNA damage response pathway developed to reduce somatic expression of mHTT protein has shown safety in preclinical studies [43,49,50]. New studies in the pipeline include allele-specific ASOs under development by Ionis and the CAG repeat region targeting ASOs by VICO Therapeutics. Wave Life Sciences is further developing a novel ASO using modified chemistry targeting a third SNP (WVE-003) (NCT05032196).

Other mHTT-lowering therapies employ RNAi with small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) or short-hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) transduced by viral vectors for long-term expression in target tissue. Recombinant adeno-associated viral (AAV) vectors are often used because of their ability to harbor large viral genomes, their nonpathogenicity in an immune-privileged tissue such as the brain, and their lack of replicability or integration into the host genome (Fig. 3A–B). In both animal and clinical studies, AAV vectors are introduced via bilateral intraparenchymal infusions. Although the entire brain is affected by mHTT, the striatum is the most common target of delivery because it harbors the greatest extent of disease phenotype. In rodent and nonhuman primate studies, 40–60% reduction of both mHTT and wtHTT was well tolerated, with decreased motor symptoms, increased overall survival, and durable effect 1 year after infusion [51,52]. mHTT protein can be also targeted with intrabodies and antibody infusions. Intrabodies are antibodies or fragments of antibodies that are transduced for binding and inactivating intracellular or extracellular mHTT (Fig. 2, Fig. 3C) [53,54]. Viral vectors transduce intra- or antibody construct into the MSNs for constitutive production, which is then directed to the polyQ domain of the mHTT protein. Studies in rodents have shown that injection of intrabodies into the striatum results in mHTT reduction and improvement in cognition and motor tasks. Although animal models have shown some promise, no corollary drugs have been brought to clinical trial. Finally, DNA mismatch repair proteins such as MSH2 and MSH3 have been implicated in trinucleotide repeat expansion [50]. Their inactivation limits triplet expansion and improves disease phenotype.

HTT gene ablation

The CRISPR-Cas9 system is a widely available genome editing tool undergoing intense clinical trials for applications in human diseases, including sickle cell disease and cancers [[55], [56], [57]]. When bound to a guide RNA (sgRNA), the Cas9 nuclease generates DNA strand breaks to sequences with 3’ protospacer adjacent motifs (PAMs) with exquisite sequence specificity and efficiency. Shin et al. [58] developed two sgRNAs that depend on PAM sites generated by SNP alleles on the mutant chromosome. Using fibroblasts derived from a patient with HD, the group was able to selectively excise DNA spanning the promoter region, transcription start site, and CAG expansion region of the mHTT gene without impacting the normal allele. Building on this work, preclinical studies have shown that intrastriatal administration of lentiviral Cas9/HTT-targeting sgRNA vectors in an HD model induces robust allele-selective HTT inactivation and improved motor deficits [59,60]. Although the results so far have been reassuring, major challenges remain in developing CRISPR-based therapies for HD. Allele-specific sgRNA targets may be limited by existing PAMs in specific haplotypes and off-target effects beyond the mutant HTT locus [56,61,62]. To enhance tissue specificity, CRISPR/Cas9 lipid nanoparticles can be deployed to the brain to efficiently deliver Cas9-sgRNA constructs [63]. Given these theoretical risks, further preclinical studies are needed before advancing to human clinical trials.

Cell transplantation

Fetal tissue and SC transplantation strategies have been proposed to replace MSNs and restore striatal function. These methods aim to produce graft integration into the host's tissue with proper afferent and efferent connections. In excitotoxic and 3-nitropropionic acid animal models, striatal grafts from the fetal ganglionic eminences develop functional connections under appropriate conditions [64,65]. These encouraging rodent models and, later, excitotoxic-lesioned striatum marmoset and macaque models led to small open-label studies in patients with HD [66,67]. In 1992, Sramka et al. [68] published the first report describing graft implantation in the caudate nucleus (injection of 4 grafts along a single trajectory). Since then, at least 70 patients with HD have undergone allograft transplantation, including those in one study of porcine xenograft [69]. Transplant sites include caudate and putamen with variable number of fetuses used (1–8) and various graft locations (e.g., “mesencephalic,” “striatal region,” whole ganglionic eminence, lateral ganglionic eminence). Although it is difficult to draw conclusions from these studies because they were not randomized, were heterogenous in surgical protocols and tissue preparations, and were underpowered, some patients did have clear sustained improvement. Furthermore, postmortem samples revealed normal striatum with appropriate efferent and afferent connections [64]. Bachoud-Levi et al. [70]. Conducted a pilot study in which three of five patients undergoing fetal transplantation of the striatum had sustained motor and cognitive improvements. In a subsequent Phase 2 randomized controlled trial of 54 patients with HD (45 transplanted) [71], they reported 10 procedure-related serious adverse events, including 1 empyema, 3 subdural hematomas (2 requiring drainage), 1 putaminal hematoma, 1 seizure, 1 graft rejection, and 3 striatal cysts, but no clinical benefit. Several limitations for this strategy remain. Specifically, HD affects extrastriatal regions, which were not targeted in the previously mentioned trials; cingulate transplantation has only been explored in mice [72]. Another limitation is the inability to “turn off” or remove the graft in case of unwanted effects (e.g., dyskinesias) [73]. It is also worth noting that tissue graft may contain more than striatal tissue (e.g., pallidum, cortex) [74].

Striatal fetal tissue transplantation also has ethical and logistic concerns given the dependency on primary fetal tissue. Allogenic or syngeneic SC therapy addresses these logistic concerns because SCs can be expanded indefinitely. SC transplantation is not only a potential strategy for replacement of lost neurons and restoration of circuitry but may also be a means of inducing compensatory neurogenesis [75]. Although there are currently no published clinical trials using SC transplantation in patients with HD, studies in excitotoxic, 3-nitropropionic acid and genetic animal models using human embryonic, induced pluripotent, adipose-derived and bone marrow-derived mesenchymal SCs (MSCs) have been performed [[76], [77], [78], [79], [80], [81], [82], [83], [84], [85], [86], [87], [88], [89], [90], [91], [92]]. The use of MSCs engineered to overexpress BDNFs has gained particular support. In a mouse model study by Dey et al. [87], intrastriatal transplantation of mouse MSCs overexpressing BDNF demonstrated a protective effect of NeuN+ and DARP32+ neurons (phenotypic markers of MSNs), which resulted in improved motor coordination. Following these results, Pollock et al. [88] demonstrated that intrastriatal injection of human MSCs overexpressing BDNF reduced striatal atrophy and anxiety-like behavior, induced neurogenesis-like activity, and increased the mean lifespan in YAC128 and R6/2 mouse models. Reassuringly, MSCs have been well tolerated in Phase 1/2 clinical trials in other diseases. Given these results, plans are underway to initiate a Phase 1 clinical trial (HD-CELL) designed to demonstrate the safety of intrastriatal injection transplantation of genetically modified MSCs in patients with HD (patients are from the completed observation study PRE-CELL, NCT01937923) [93]. A limitation of cell replacement therapy is that it does not address constitutive mHTT expression throughout the brain outside of the transplantation site. That is to say, research has predominantly focused on motor symptoms in HD and replacement of MSNs, but HD-associated anxiety, depression, and cognitive impairment may only be resolved with enhancement of off-target neurogenesis.

Connectivity considerations and other potential targets

Resting-state functional MRI investigates spontaneous fluctuations of blood oxygenation level-dependent signal as a surrogate for neural activity [94]. Correlated cortical and subcortical brain regions are assumed to be functionally connected into resting-state networks [95,96]. The method is an emerging tool in identifying functional alterations in neurodegenerative pathologies such as HD [97,98]. In HD, Hohenfeld and colleagues [99] recently reported connectivity alterations in sensorimotor, visual, and subcortical (especially the striatum) networks. Serial diffusion tensor imaging in HD patients demonstrated white matter degeneration associated with symptom development [100]. Dysfunction in brain connectivity may represent the spread of proteinopathies such as HD. In Parkinson disease (PD), the distribution of alpha-synuclein follows dysfunction in basal ganglia circuitry, although this pattern may be more complex in atypical parkinsonism such as progressive supranuclear palsy [97,101,102]. Therefore, understanding the altered brain connectivity in HD may lead to interventions that target vulnerable brain regions to minimize pathologic spread. Similarly, such an intervention targeting vulnerable networks in HD may slow disease progression. In a lesion network mapping study by Laganiere et al. [103], 29 patients with hemichorea with heterogeneity in anatomic location were studied. Lesions showed network overlap in the posterolateral putamen. This previously validated method has identified targets that align with those derived through other methods with known therapeutic efficacy [104,105]. Therefore, neuromodulation of the posterolateral putamen or its associated network may represent a therapeutic target in symptomatic patients. For example, transcranial magnetic stimulation of the supplementary motor area, which is a critical node in this network, has shown promising results for patients with HD with choreiform movements [106].

Surgical considerations for gene and SC therapy delivery

Safety evaluations of dosing and efficacy from the AMT-130 trial are currently underway, and interim results are promising [107,108]. Studies for PD provide the most current insights on the surgical technique and drug delivery efficacy in gene therapy [109]. One of the major practical factors is the volume of viral vector dose, which can determine the method of delivery [110,111]. Although technically possible, manual infusion does not offer the control and consistency of more advanced techniques—namely MRI-guided convection-based delivery using a pressure gradient generated by a pump. This method allows for a consistent rate of infusate to flow into the target parenchyma, with MRI simultaneously providing real-time imaging guidance of the treatment delivery. Modifications to the delivery device for increased efficacy include minimizing dead space with reduced tubing volume, limiting backflow through the stepped configuration of the delivery guide, and using an infusion cannula to enhance delivery accuracy.

More recent trials have used the Clearpoint neuronavigation system (Clearpoint Neuro), which allows precise stereotactic targeting (precision <1 ​mm) of the cannula as well as MRI-guided visualization of the infusate [110,111]. The vector is mixed with gadoteridol, a contrast agent that enhances on standard T1-weighted MRI sequence and indicates whether there is backflow or aberrant flow into perivascular spaces. In addition, the neurosurgeon controls the delivery and can plan for real-time changes in the targeted delivery, ensuring the technical success of an individual patient's treatment. For example, if significant perivascular leakage is seen, the surgeon can advance the delivery system in a stepwise fashion from proximal to distal, which has been shown to result in better drug distribution [109]. Successful infusion parameters for HD trials targeting the striatum can be derived from recent PD trials such that a volume of distribution/volume of infusion (Vd/Vi) ratio should be 3:1 to 2:1 [111]. In other words, a Vd of 1500 ​mm3 requires 500 ​mm3 volume of vector. Infusions can last 8–12 ​h and involve multiple trajectories [109]. Finally, anatomical considerations of the target structure are crucial. The putamen is an elongated structure in the anterior-to-posterior axis, where a parietooccipital approach would provide optimum coverage. Target volume coverage is related to outcomes, and coverage >50% total target volume appears to be optimal [109].

For cell transplant delivery, the Clearpoint system can be used to target the delivery cannula [109]. It is not clear whether there is a benefit to Clearpoint use other than the ability to confirm cannula placement in the target [111]. At the time of infusion, the orientation of the cannula matters such that about 80% of cells are delivered initially in a vertical orientation and the last 20% through a horizontally oriented cannula [70]. Rotating the cannula may help distribute the cell suspension. The tissue preparation is variable, and the inclusion of choroid cells can result in choroid cysts in the recipient patient [112]. Other differences in the surgical procedure are important to distinguish. There are obvious ethical considerations with sham surgery for cell transplantation, given that efficacy timelines can be longer. A graft may take 18 months to become established and integrated in the host while patients in control arms are not offered the surgical intervention for that prolonged amount of time [[113], [114], [115]]. Additionally, a study involving allogeneic cell transplantation may require a control arm receiving immunosuppression, which can have adverse effects [116]. Finally, patients with HD may be more susceptible to subdural hematomas compared with patients with PD, likely related to brain atrophy and increased falls resulting from their chorea symptoms [110]. Additional means of delivering the drug to the brain across the blood–brain barrier, such as focused ultrasound, are also being explored.

Future Management of HD

Advances in molecular characterization of HD pathogenesis, rationally designed therapeutic development, and surgical techniques for neurostimulation and therapy delivery provide opportunities for treatment interventions that could improve clinical outcomes. Previous DBS therapy targeting the striatum demonstrated a relief of hyperkinetic symptoms such as chorea without significant impacts on disease progression; however, these studies were often clinical trials with small cohorts and limited stimulation paradigms. Tissues most vulnerable to HD pathology—particularly the striatum—exhibit somatic instability of the CAG repeat, where the extent of instability is correlated with earlier onset of disease. There is mounting evidence in animal models that DBS may play a role in neuroprotection and disease-related neurotoxicity in certain conditions such as PD. Although there is limited evidence of a similar disease-modifying effect in PD, HD may be unique in the ability to identify gene positivity in the prodromal stages and offer early intervention. Emerging radiographic techniques revealed dysfunctional resting-state networks in the posterolateral putamen in HD patients. Neurostimulation within these networks may help slow disease progression. By integrating genetic profiling of patients and wider stimulation parameters, larger clinical trials may further reveal possible disease-modifying properties of neurostimulation.

The reported cases of HD patients undergoing neuromodulation—albeit limited by sample size—demonstrated the importance of careful patient selection. All of the patients selected for DBS therapy were screened to have mainly motor symptoms that feature hyperkinesia and dystonia without psychiatric components or other medical comorbidities. Unlike in neuromodulation in patients with PD or essential tremors, patients with HD can experience perioperative drowsiness and slower improvement of their chorea over days to weeks [13,16]. Longitudinal studies with longer-term follow up at 48 months showed continued benefit of DBS therapy for hyperkinesia in patients with HD [18,22]. However, there were a subgroup of patients with HD who underwent DBS implantation where the progression of disease accelerated [13,17]. Although these patients’ “off” state symptoms suggest that DBS therapy ameliorated some of the disease progression, further investigation is needed to determine which HD patient subgroups may see long-term benefits of neuromodulation.

As the dominant gain-of-function toxicity, mHTT drives multiple pathogenic processes that underlie HD symptoms. Preclinical data from ablation of mHTT DNA or RNA have been shown to reverse HD pathology, improve HD symptoms, and slow or stop disease progression. From ASOs to genome editing with CRISPR/Cas9, targeted disease-modifying therapies require precise delivery with surgical techniques such as the Clearpoint system or focused ultrasound. Beyond HTT lowering therapies, these surgical techniques broaden opportunities for further disease modification with intrastriatal infusion of neurotrophic factors, cell transplantation, and delivery of intrabodies. Current intrathecal delivery of ASOs or antibodies involves serial lumbar punctures, which may not be tolerated in broader patient populations. Clinical trials with the ASO tominersen resulted in symptomatic hydrocephalus in 5 patients out of 796 participants. Subcutaneous reservoirs with intraventricular or intrathecal catheters have demonstrated efficacy and safety in oncologic treatments of leptomeningeal spread of cancers and pediatric administration of ASOs for spinal muscular atrophy [117,118]. Such surgical implantation may not only facilitate boluses needed for HTT lowering therapies but also treat hydrocephalus. Moreover, advances in stereotactic surgical techniques such as focused ultrasound-mediated pallidotomy or thalamotomy and robot-assisted DBS implantation have reduced operative time and complications [[119], [120], [121]] from anesthesia and surgery. These advances expanded the availability of surgical treatment of movement disorders. With advanced imaging and delivery technologies, clinical trials are well poised to make a significant contribution to the surgical management of HD.
