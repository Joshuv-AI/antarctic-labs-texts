Whole genome sequence-based comparative genomics reveals preliminary genomic features of Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Enteritidis phage type 1 and phage type 4 strains from EnteroBase
Authors: Lucas Vásquez-Herrera, Omar P. Vallejos, José Acevedo-López, Sofía Campos-Gajardo, Alejandro Piña-Iturbe, Daniel Tichy-Navarro, Dwayne Seeram, Anne-Catrin Uhlemann, Pablo A. González, Alexis M. Kalergis, Andrea I. Moreno-Switt, Susan M. Bueno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhole Genome Sequencing (WGS) enables detailed characterization of circulating and emerging bacterial strains. Although tens of thousands of Salmonella genomes have been acquired over the years, analyses of the genomic differences between strains of different phage types are scarce.ResultsWe compared two Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Enteritidis (SEn) phage types, namely phage types 1 and 4 from available databases, using bioinformatic tools and nanopore sequencing of a Chilean PT1 strain. Comparisons between the two phage types show very low genomic divergence and high genomic sequence similarity. Single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) searches identified SNPs specific to each phage type. Although a translocated region was identified in the Chilean PT1 strain analyzed in this study when compared to the genome of a PT4 strain, this was not present in the genomes of other PT1 strains, suggesting a local strain-specific rearrangement. Further analyses yielded no differences in the CRISPR-Cas locus, but a slight difference was observed in Gifsy-2 prophage detection and DNA modification systems between PT1 and PT4 strains.ConclusionsOur findings provide insights into the genomic differences between SEn strains of two different phage types, serving as a basis for future genomic studies, yet further analyses with more diverse geographical locations collected over a longer time span are essential to validate these differences with the potential to establish molecular markers for strain identification and characterization in the context of epidemiological surveillance as a complement to WGS when this technique is not available.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05329-5.

Introduction

During epidemiological outbreaks and surveillance periods, rapid, cost-effective strain subtyping methods are essential for outbreak detection, source attribution, and monitoring circulating lineages. Whole Genome Sequencing (WGS) provides unparalleled resolution for strain characterization, species identification, and detection of virulence and antimicrobial resistance genes. Although its implementation has been widespread becoming the gold standard in several regions, including the USA, the European Union, Australia, and China, its large-scale implementation remains a challenge in low- and middle-income countries, due to the high cost of equipment, the requirement of specialized computational infrastructure, qualified personnel trained in bioinformatics, and pipeline standardization for managing, analyzing and sharing genomic data [1]. In contrast, phage typing was used as a routine method for Salmonella strain classification, based on susceptibility to standardized bacteriophages, allowing the design of typing schemes for Salmonella enterica subs. enterica serovar Enteritidis and Typhimurium [2–4]. Notably, this approach enables discrimination of bacterial strains that share epidemiological and geographic characteristics and was widely implemented due to its low cost and scalability. However, its use has been progressively replaced by WGS because phage typing does not provide high-resolution information and can only be performed on two serovars, S. Typhimurium and S. Enteritidis [5]. Alternative methods such as prophage typing for strain classification have also been used. For example, multiplex-PCR with specific primers, a simple, rapid, and cost-effective method, has been used for strain typing of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus in Iran [6, 7]. For Salmonella, multiplex-PCR [8] and ERIC-PCR (Enterobacterial Repetitive Intergenic Consensus) have been used for strain typing, often accompanied by WGS [9, 10].

Salmonella is a genus of Gram-negative, facultative anaerobic, facultative intracellular bacteria of major public health concern worldwide [11]. Non-typhoidal Salmonella (NTS) remains among the leading causes of foodborne diseases, with hundreds of millions of infections reported annually, resulting in approximately 155,000 deaths [11]. Among the different serovars identified in Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica, Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Enteritidis (SEn) is one of the most prevalent NTS serovars globally [12]. Improved water quality, sanitation, and epidemiological surveillance have controlled infections and mortality caused by this bacterium in developed countries over the years; however, Salmonella remains a significant public health challenge, particularly due to the emergence and spread of antibiotic-resistant strains and the inequity of proper sanitation, food safety measures, and public health awareness between regions [12].

The distribution of SEn phage types varies significantly between countries. While different regions may share similar endemic serovars, the specific phage types circulating within those serovars often differ by geographic location. For example, in the 1990 s, the most common phage type collected from egg-related outbreaks of SEn in the United States corresponded to PT8 (48.2% of strains), followed by PT13a (20.1%) [13]. In Canada, while most PTs showed regional variation, PT8 showed the highest incidence across sample sources (69–96%), indicating that PT8 was widely disseminated in North America [14]. In contrast, PT4 strains were frequently detected in the UK, Germany, the Netherlands, and other parts of Europe, while in Finland, both PT1 and PT4 were common sources of outbreaks in the 90 s [15–18]. The dissemination of strains is a dynamic process that can change over time. Although PT4 was the most common phage type found in the UK; between April 2014 and September 2015, 714 isolates from human cases were reported as PT8 (a PT associated with imported poultry meat and eggs), with 147 falling into an Single Nucleotide Polymorphism (SNP) cluster traced back to 2012 and associated with feeder mice used for reptiles [19]. More recently, between 2020 and 2023 the second most common phage types isolated from human cases in Aotearoa New Zealand were PT9a and PT11. Both belong to Sequence Type 183 (ST183), a lineage associated with hedgehogs and considered endemic in the UK and Germany, being widely disseminated in rural environments [20]. Besides their regional localization, phage types can differ in virulence and host reservoir. PT4 strains have been associated with higher morbidity and mortality in broiler chickens than other phage types [21]. In murine models, phage types PT4, PT8, PT13a, and PT24 cause mortality in mice, with PT4 showing the highest virulence; however, a plasmid-free PT13a strain and two PT24 strains were found to be avirulent in these animals [22]. Consequently, identifying a strain’s phage type can help predict the host origin and the potential severity of resulting infections.

In Chile, phage typing was used to evaluate SEn during surveillance and during major Salmonella epidemics in the 1990s. During this period, two phage types were reported to be more prevalent in the country: phage type 1 (PT1), which was more frequently associated with European lineages and detected in 94% of isolates collected from northern regions [23], and phage type 4 (PT4), a globally distributed phage type predominant in central and southern Chile with 92% of isolates corresponding to this PT [23, 24]. However, the current strain distribution of SEn in Chile is unknown, as no updated data regarding genomic distribution is currently available in the country, which makes the detection of disseminating high-risk lineages and antimicrobial resistance genes difficult [25, 26].

Despite the extensive historical use of phage typing, the genetic basis underlying phage type classification remains poorly understood. Previous studies have focused primarily on bacteriophage characterization in SEn and S. Typhimurium [27, 28], the use of plasmid profiling [29], CRISPR typing [30], pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE) [31–33], or a combination of different methods [34]. However, genetic differences at the strain level between phage types have been somewhat poorly studied, despite thousands of complete genomes being available in various databases [35]. Some reports have shown that genomic elements are associated with phage interactions, with some phage-bacteria interactions being predicted at the strain level using only genomic data [36]. Also, the process of phage type transformation has been reported, in which, due to loss of lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a transformation from PT1, 4, and 6 to PT7 was observed, suggesting that genetic elements could help differentiate phage types [37]. Another report identified a set of SNPs that allowed the identification of strains from PT8, PT13, and PT13a, which were classified into two clades and 4 subclades by phylogeny, but these markers were unable to discriminate individual phage types [38]. With many new genomes being sequenced daily, it will be important to make use of this data to generate specific genomic markers that enable the rapid low-cost identification of disseminating strains with epidemiological potential, as well as the detection of antimicrobial-resistant strains, which can be used as a complement to WGS or in contexts where WGS may not be available.

Here, we aimed to analyze the genomic differences between Salmonella Enteritidis phage types PT1 and PT4, focusing on SNPs, phage defense systems, and CRISPR locus comparisons to identify potential genetic markers for strain identification and characterization in future epidemiological outbreaks.

Materials and methods

Strains and growth medium

Isolates were stored in Luria–Bertani broth (LB) medium made of tryptone 10 g/L (Bacto™, Gibco), yeast extract 5 g/L (Bacto™, Gibco) and sodium chloride 10 g/L (Winkler) supplemented with 20% v/v glycerol in a Cryobank bead system at −80 °C and were reactivated by picking a bead from the stock and growing in 3 mL liquid LB at 37 °C and 200 rpm agitation overnight. One SEn phage type 1 (SEn PT1) isolate was provided by the Public Health Institute of Chile (ISP-Chile), and one SEn strain P125109 phage type 4 (SEn PT4) isolate was acquired from the National Collection of Type Cultures (NCTC 13349, UK).

DNA extraction for sequencing

Genomic DNA from SEn PT1 and PT4 was extracted using the DNeasy UltraClean Microbial Kit (QIAGEN) following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA quantity and purity were assessed by spectrophotometry (NanoDrop, Thermo Fisher Scientific) based on concentration and A260/A280 ratios, and DNA integrity was verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis prior to sequencing at the Sequencing and Omics Technologies Unit, Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile.

Whole genome sequencing (WGS) and genome assembly of SEn strains

Bacterial genomes from SEn PT4 and SEn PT1 were sequenced using the Oxford Nanopore Technologies platform at the Sequencing and Omics Technologies Unit of Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile, utilizing the GrindION platform with MinION Flow Cell R10.4.1 and Ligation Sequencing V14 kit. Quality of the obtained reads was assessed using NanoPlot v1.42.0 [39], Porechop v0.2.4 [40] and NanoFilt v2.8.0 [39]. To corroborate that the reads corresponded to bacteria, Kraken2 v2.1.3 [41] was used with the standard database. Genomic assembly was carried out using Flye v2.9.4 [42], Raven v1.8.3 [43] and Miniasm v0.3 [44]. Assemblies obtained from these tools were evaluated using Quast v5.2.0 [45] and selection of the best assembly was based on size and number of contigs. To polish the selected assembly, the tool Medaka [46] was used to increase the quality of the assembly by creating consensus reads using the Nanopore reads, increasing the precision of the assembly. To evaluate the completion of the assembly, the tool BUSCO v5.7.1 [47] was used to search for conserved orthologous genes. Coverage was evaluated using minimap2 v2.28 [44] and SAMtools v1.20 [48]. For the identification and annotation of genes and other genomic elements, Prokka v1.14.6 [49] was used. Later, protein sequences predicted by Prokka were subjected to functional annotation using EggNOG-mapper v2.1.12 [50]. Finally, Kraken2 was used to give a taxonomic assignment to each contig. These bioinformatic analyses were conducted by the Bioinformatics and Computational Biology Unit of the same institution.

Whole genome comparisons

Assembled genomes for SEn PT1 and PT4 were compared with the reference strain SEn PT4 str. P125109 (Accession number AM933172), the virulence plasmid pSEN from the same strain (Accession number NZ_HG970000), and a PT1 reference strain SEn PT1 [51, 52] to make a circular graph of the alignment and percentage of identity. A direct comparison between the PT1 and PT4 assembled genomes was made using BLASTn and graphed with Artemis Comparison Tool [53] to identify differences in genomic organization between these sequenced strains.

To assess genomic differences between PT1 and PT4 strains, additional genomic sequences were extracted from the EnteroBase database [35] searching for S. enterica assemblies with keywords serovar “Enteritidis” and Phage type “PT4” or “PT1”. SNP comparison was performed using snippy v4.6.0 [54] with the snippy-multi command to generate a script to run all sequences against SEn str. P125109 (Accession number AM933172) as reference. Missing or ambiguous variants were filtered out when coverage was less than × 10 and VCF variant call quality less than 100, following snippy’s default parameters. This analysis included 125 PT4 strains and 82 PT1 strains extracted from EnteroBase, the SEn PT1 and PT4 assemblies we sequenced, an additional SEn PT1 reference strain (3; Accession number CP043433) and S. Typhimurium (STm) str. LT2 (Accession number AE006468) as an outlier control. This made a total of 126 PT4 strains and 84 PT1 strains analyzed. The snippy-core command was used to generate core SNP alignment and a phylogenomic tree was constructed using IQ-TREE3 v3.0.1 [55] with ModelFinder [56] and 1000 ultrafast bootstrap [57] for branch support values. To correct branch lengths to account for recombination, ClonalFrameML was used [58]. The tree generated was graphed using FigTree.v1.4.4 (https://github.com/rambaut/figtree/releases/tag/v1.4.4). Clades were defined through visual inspection of the phylogenetic tree topology. Groups of isolates were assigned to distinct clades based on their clustering by PTs and node support. Finally, an in-house Python script was used to extract the following data from snippy.tab output files: Unique SNPs found within each PT and shared SNPs between PTs. SNPs were considered for further analysis when they were found in more than 60% of samples for a PT (defined as prevalent) but less than 10% in the other (defined as not-prevalent).

To assess whether individual SNPs were significantly associated with phage type (PT), geographic isolation source, or collection timeline, multivariable logistic regression models were constructed using the glm function in R. For each analysis, the presence or absence of a given SNP was treated as a binary dependent variable (1 = SNP present, 0 = SNP absent). Predictor variables included collection year (treated as a continuous numeric variable), country of origin, and PT, with the latter two evaluated as categorical factors. For country, Northern Ireland was used as the baseline since it contained the highest number of isolates on the database (Table 1). The logistic regression models were fitted using the binomial error distribution family. Phage defense systems comparison

To compare different defense systems against phage infection, we used DefenseFinder [59–61] and PADLOC [62, 63] running on a laptop with the default configuration. Both databases were used to increase detection of possible defense systems. Data were graphed with ggplot2 R package [64].

CRISPR locus and prophage comparison

CRISPR array analysis was performed using the CRISPR Comparison Toolkit (CCTK) [65] with the default configuration and following the software’s documentation. Finally, prophages were detected by DBSCAN-SWA [66]. The resulting prophage sequences were reanalyzed using PHASTEST [67] on a local machine with Docker, in “deep” annotation mode, to determine the name and completion (complete or partial) of each found prophage by a score based on number and type (integrase, lysin, tail, terminase, etc.) of phage proteins detected. Data were graphed with ggplot2 R package.

Results

To further examine genetic differences between phage types, we obtained additional sequences deposited in EnteroBase and compared them to the reference strain SEn PT4 str. P125109 at the SNP level. The sequences from EnteroBase corresponded to 125 SEn PT4 strains and 82 PT1 isolates. For 186 isolates the source was recorded as human, with 6 coming from poultry, 2 coming from food, and 13 coming from unknown sources. PT4 isolates were submitted from a variety of countries, 111 from Northern Ireland, 4 were submitted from the UK, 4 from Belgium, 3 from Ireland, 2 from Uruguay, and 1 from the USA, from 1967 to 2015. PT1 isolates were from 1967 to 2017, 36 from Australia and 32 from Northern Ireland, with 9 provided by UK, 3 by Ireland, and 2 from an unknown location (Table 1). Of these isolates, all 125 PT4 strains (100%) were ST11, while 79 (96.3%) PT1 isolates were ST11, 2 (2.44%) were ST1925, and 1 (1.22%) was ST3347 (Table 1). ST assignment was determined according to the Achtman 7 gene MLST scheme used in EnteroBase. Within EnteroBase, genomes are characterized by Hierarchical Clustering (HC) based on core genome multilocus sequence typing (cgMLST), which uses a set number of allelic differences across 1500–3000 core genes for Salmonella, applying a single-linkage clustering criterion. For example, HC20 clusters strains with no more than 20 cgMLST-allele differences. For PT1 and PT4 strains, 73 (89%) and 80 (64%) were classified as HC20_87, with 9 (11%) PT1 strains classified with other HC_20 and 32 (25.6%) PT4 strains being HC20_134418 (Table 1).

The SNP tree (Fig. S1) presented as a cladogram (Fig. 2A), revealed the isolates clustered into four main clades: clade I was composed of 84 PT4 isolates and only 3 PT1 isolates. Clade II was subdivided into a subclade IIa with 33 PT1 isolates and 1 PT4 isolate, and IIb subclade of 7 PT1 isolates. The rest of Clade II was composed of 20 PT4 isolates. Clade III was composed of 35 PT1 isolates with 8 PT4 isolates present. Finally, clade IV was composed of 12 PT4 isolates, and a subclade IVa was composed of 6 PT1 isolates. The bootstrap value of 100 (which means that a clade was retained in all 1000 phylogenetic bootstrap replicates) for the initial bifurcations that form these clades indicate high confidence in these bifurcations during tree construction. Although unique SNPs were found for each PT (1434 for PT1 and 1877 for PT4) almost all were found in less than 10 samples, and none were greater than our set threshold of 60% frequency (data not shown). But we were able to identify 8 unique SNP patterns for each phage type looking into SNPs that were shared between PTs. This included a missense variant (amino acid change) caused by a specific cytosine change to adenine or thymine in the gyrA gene (which confers fluoroquinolone resistance) detected in 77.4% of PT1 strains (C > A(39 strains);C > T(26 strains)), but in only 2.4% of PT4 strains (C > A(2 strains);C > T(1 strain)). Multivariable logistic regression models revealed that for gyrA mutation C > A (Table S3), PT was significantly correlated with the presence of this SNP, with PT4 having a decreased presence of this SNP, meaning that it was more prevalent in PT1 isolates (as indicated by the estimated value). Meanwhile, Australia and the United Kingdom had a significant decrease in the presence of this SNP compared to the baseline, Northern Ireland. The gyrA mutation C > T (Table S4) was not significantly correlated with any factor. For SEN2380A TGTTGGTA > T (Table S5), both phage type and collection year were significantly correlated with the presence of this SNP, with PT4 having an increased presence while collection year was negatively correlated with its presence. Also, Australia had a significant correlation with increased SNP presence compared to Northern Ireland. For aas T > C (Table S6), yiaN G > C (Table S7), tsr G > T (Table S8), ybiO C > T (Table S9), and pegD A > C (Table S10), only phage type and collection time had a significant correlation, with PT4 having an increased presence of these SNPs while collection year was negatively associated with their presence.

The chromosome of Chilean SEn PT1 shows a translocated region compared to SEn PT4

Evaluating variable regions between the Chilean SEn PT1 and SEn str. P125109 PT4 reference strain genomes we observed an inverted translocated region of 99,695 bp in SEn PT1 (Chilean isolate) compared to SEn PT4 (Fig. 3A). This region contains 81 open reading frames, some of which encode metabolism-related genes or operons, such as the ilv operon and wec operon (Fig. 3B). The region was found flanked by rRNA operons (rrn) following the pattern 16S, tRNA-Glu, 23S, 5S, tRNA-Asp and tRNA-Trp upstream, while the pattern 16S, tRNA-Ile, tRNA-Ala, 23S, 5S was found downstream (Fig. 3B). Checking the insertion sites in both strains, the translocated region in PT4 was found between the yieP and mobB genes, while in PT1 it was found between SEN2582 (hyp) and clpB genes. Comparing the sites where the translocated region wasn’t present in both strains, the rrn operon 5S, 23S, tRNA-Glu, and 16S, was found in both sites but in reverse sense (Fig. 3C). The presence of this translocation was further supported by aligning the raw nanopore reads to the SEn PT1 assembly using minimap2, which showed long reads aligning with genes outside and inside the region at the borders, supporting its location (Fig. S2) [68]. Discussion

Here, we compared 84 SEn PT1 and 126 PT4 strains at the genomic level. In this analysis, we observed high genomic similarity between a single Chilean PT1 strain and the reference genome SEn PT4 str. P125109 and reference SEn PT1 (Accession number CP043433) both at the chromosomal level and on the secondary genomic element, confirming it as a plasmid due to its high identity with the Salmonella virulence plasmid. The high completeness and coverage suggest high-quality whole-genome assemblies from both strains. The phylogenetic tree constructed with SNP differences when adding EnteroBase PT4 and PT1 strains was able to separate the strains into different clades, where clades I to IV had different composition of PT1 and PT4 strains. Still, clades were not PT-exclusive, as mixtures of PT1 and PT4 strains within the same clade were observed, suggesting that although SNPs separated them into major clades, they remained sufficiently similar to cluster within the same clades. When analyzing SNPs, we found mutations more present in certain phage types. For example, 77.4% of PT1 strains carried a SNP in the gyrA gene (C > A, 39 strains; C > T, 26 strains). This gene encodes for a subunit of DNA gyrase, a primary target for broad-spectrum antibiotics such as quinolones, and mutations in this protein can confer resistance to these antibiotics [69]. Indeed, of the 82 PT1 strains from EnteroBase, 65 present a quinolone resistance mutation, while only 6 PT4 strains present this resistance, according to the AMRfinder result in the database [35]. This type of antibiotic resistance is an emerging mechanism and has been increasing over the years since its introduction for the treatment of bacterial infections [70]. The association of this mutation with PT1 strains could aid in identifying resistant lineages during outbreaks. This is supported by the multivariable logistic regression models, which showed that the gyrA C > A mutation was negatively correlated with PT4 isolates. However, due to the limited geographic diversity of the available PT1 genomes, this resistance profile may currently reflect only PT1 strains from UK and Australia, which is particularly crucial given the extended temporal window over which these samples were taken (and the latest isolate is from 2017), which may induce a timing and country bias. Following this, the models showed that the gyrA C > A mutation had an increased presence in Australia and the United Kingdom compared to Northern Ireland, possibly suggesting a bias. Although not significant (p-value = 0.05799), a slight positive correlation was observed between the presence of this mutation and the collection year of the isolates, suggesting that this mutation may be increasing over time and raising the possibility that this is an emerging mechanism, although this interpretation is limited by the temporal distribution of the isolates and the lack of more recent evidence. The sequencing of PT1 strains from other countries and recent isolation years will be required to determine the dynamic of temporal dissemination of this mutation and if it’s a characteristic of PT1 strains disseminated globally or only a local characteristic from these countries.

Other SNPs were found to be more prevalent in PT4 strains, such as in genes SEN2380A, a phage tail protein [71], yiaN, a TRAP transport system [72], tsr, a methyl-accepting chemotaxis protein [73], ybiO, a mechanosensitive channel protein [74] and pegD, a putative fimbrial-like adhesion protein [75]. These SNPs were all positively correlated with PT4 isolates but negatively correlated with the collection time. This suggests that these SNPs may be disappearing over time, but the temporal bias of this database limits this conclusion. Since they correspond to phage, surface-exposed proteins and fimbriae, they can modulate phage attachment or entry, potentially contributing to differences in phage susceptibility between PT4 and PT1 strains [76–79]. Nevertheless, further analysis is required to determine the impact of these gene variants on phage susceptibility.

Limitations regarding the dataset composition should be considered when interpreting the generalizability of our SNP findings. First, our reliance on EnteroBase records with PT metadata introduces potential ascertainment bias, since strains lacking phage type annotation were excluded from this study. Second, the dataset exhibits a pronounced geographic skew, with predominant representation of isolates from Northern Ireland, Australia, and the UK, which may not accurately reflect the genomic landscape of SEn in underrepresented or unrepresented global regions. Third, the collection spans an extensive temporal range of five decades (1967–2017), meaning that historical baseline strains may influence overall SNP frequencies in ways that do not reflect current circulating lineages. Finally, the isolates characterized in this study belong overwhelmingly to Sequence Type 11 (ST11), which represents the dominant, highly clonal pandemic lineage responsible for the vast majority of global human SEn infections [80]. Because STs are characterized by the allelic profiles of 7 conserved housekeeping genes [35], our analysis can only capture microevolutionary shifts and SNP variations within this lineage, limiting the generalizability of our results to other more distantly related ST lineages.

During our direct comparison between the single Chilean PT1 strain and PT4 reference, we found a translocated region in the PT1 strain. This region spanned 99,695 bp, containing a high quantity of genes and was flanked by ribosomal operons, which followed a previously reported operon pattern consisting of 16S-23S-5S with a variable region between 16S-23S [81]. Genomic rearrangements due to ribosomal operon recombination have been reported in Salmonella, where inversion and levitation events may occur through recombination of operons in the same or opposite orientation, respectively [81]. Since we only found this translocation in the evaluated Chilean isolate, sequencing additional PT1 clinical isolates will be necessary to determine whether this event represents a rare strain-specific occurrence or a feature of locally circulating PT1 strains.

Some of the genes found in this region are part of operons with different functions, like the ilv operon, whose proteins are involved in amino acid biosynthesis [82]. The wec operon is involved in the biosynthesis of the Enterobacteria Common Antigen (ECA) [83], a conserved antigen between members of the Enterobacteriaceae family with a variety of functions as a membrane structure and virulence factor [84, 85]. ECA has also been reported as important during P22 phage infection in S. Typhimurium 14028 s, where knockout mutants of wecC and wecD genes presented acceleration of lysis after suffering P22 infection, suggesting a protective role of ECA against P22 infection. This was explained due to ECA’s role on the maintenance of membrane integrity and masking of O-antigen [86]. If the translocation identified here alters ECA regulation or expression, it could influence phage susceptibility or virulence in this PT1 isolate. However, transcriptomic analysis and phage infection assays are required to assess if this translocation has any effect on ECA’s biosynthesis, phage susceptibility, or the virulence of this PT1 isolate.

Most bacteria carry defense mechanisms to resist phage infection, including prophages, CRISPR-Cas, etc. [87]. The most common systems detected in both PT1 and PT4 strains (up to 97% of strains from both PTs) by both DefenseFinder and PADLOC are known defense systems found in diverse bacterial species, such as CRISPR-Cas subtype I-E, CBASS I, RM type I and RM type III [88]. DefenseFinder also identified DS-44 in both PT1 and PT4, a predicted defense system with a Mtase domain [89]. Another system, Sucellos, corresponds to a two gene system that encode SclA and SclB proteins which form transmembrane pores, mediating cell suicide [90]. PADLOC, on the other hand, identified a variety of candidate defense systems with similar abundance in both PT1 and PT4, although these haven’t been published yet (Payne, unpublished). Another system identified was VSPR (Very short patch repair system) which repairs base–base mismatches [91]. Although it’s not a defense system, PADLOC still includes it. Another is RM type HNH, which encodes a HNH endonuclease targeting phages [92]. Although some differences were found in DMS systems with enzyme configurations on PT1 strains that are not common for restriction-modification systems [93], previous work suggests that defense systems alone may not be strong predictors of phage susceptibility [36].

CRISPR locus analysis showed that a specific array configuration was highly conserved among both PT4 and PT1 strains with only 8 strains presenting any variation of spacers, suggesting that the CRISPR arrays N°1 and 7 are the most conserved, appearing in 202 isolates. This is consistent with a report in S. Typhimurium where strains of different phage types may present the same CRISPR-locus, while strains of the same phage type may present different CRISPR-locus, making CRISPR-typing unreliable for phage type identification [30]. Finally, prophage abundance was similar between the PT1 and PT4 strains, both containing Gifsy 2, sal3 Fels1 and PCJ13 or Stx phages in similar proportions and states of completion. Although Shigella PCJ13 or Stx phage was detected as incomplete in both PT1 (88.1%) and PT4 (98.4%), it’s assignment may be an artifact due to this prophage having the lowest proteins detected (3 proteins detected), with other possible prophages related to Salmonella, like SEN34, Gifsy-2 and Gifsy-1, having very close score (2 proteins detected, data not shown). Gifsy-2 prophage was detected both intact and incomplete in 97.6% and 85.7% of PT4 strains, respectively, suggesting these strains have two copies of this phage, or one extra element with similar proteins to Gifsy-2. By contrast, Gifsy-2 abundance in the PT1 strains was 95.2% as intact but only 52.4%, suggesting that half of the PT1 strains only have one copy of this prophage element, a slight difference compared to the PT4 strains. Since prophages are an important source of resistance to other phage infections [87], the absence of an incomplete Gifsy-2 prophage could be a difference between PT4 and PT1 strains. Further analyses are required to determine whether this prophage is a determinant of phage type 4 or 1 and its impact on phage susceptibility.

Altogether, these findings provide insight into the genomic differences between 210 SEn strains from two different phage types. While our analysis is limited by sample size and the restricted location of the studied isolates, the results highlight the potential of whole-genome sequencing to uncover phage-type-associated genetic signatures, such as exclusive genomic elements and point mutations in potential receptors for phage infection. These preliminary genetic signatures must be experimentally validated to determine their impact on phage susceptibility, strain phage type, virulence, and functional studies. As WGS becomes more accessible, future studies comprising a larger dataset of a greater range of phage types with broad geographical and temporal range will be essential for establishing generalizable specific genomic characterization between strains and robust and low-cost molecular markers for strain identification, which is an important step towards improving epidemiological surveillance and outbreak response for emerging, high-risk and antimicrobial resistant Salmonella strains as a complement for WGS.
