Isolated Acute Liver Injury Following Mass Envenomation by Wasps
Authors: Hayneil Solanki, Tara Fox, Jerry Chin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAcute liver injury from wasp venom exposure has been reported in the literature, most often in the context of anaphylaxis and multiorgan injury. We present a case of isolated liver injury, a rare presentation, in a healthy 26-year-old man stung by more than 40 wasps. We observed a delayed presentation with conjugated hyperbilirubinemia, significantly raised transaminases, and mild coagulopathy, in the absence of other organ involvement. Complete normalization of liver enzymes was seen 61 days following presentation. Clinicians encountering patients with liver injury in the context of wasp stings should consider wasp venom-associated hepatitis as a possible cause.

INTRODUCTION

Wasps are venomous insects belonging to the order Hymenoptera and harbor the unique ability to sting a subject multiple times during a single attack.1 Wasp venom (WV) is rich in vasoactive substances and can cause mild-to-severe hypersensitivity reactions in susceptible hosts.2,3 While its immediate allergic effects are well appreciated, the nonallergic and direct toxic effects of WV on individual organ systems remain under recognized.2 Acute liver injury is a rare complication of WV exposure and has been described in case reports globally, often in the context of anaphylaxis with hypotension and multiorgan dysfunction.1,2,4,5 We present a case of isolated acute liver injury, a rare consequence of mass envenomation by wasps.

CASE REPORT

A fit and well 26-year-old man sustained more than 40 wasp stings on 2 localized areas on his left thigh, after inadvertently disturbing a wasp nest under a carpet (Figure 1). There was no immediate hypersensitivity reaction. Three days later, he began to experience right upper quadrant pain, anorexia, and was noted to be jaundiced. He presented to hospital 5 days after the index event with worsening jaundice.

Photograph of scars from location of mass envenomation on left thigh, taken 61 days following index event, with permission from the patient.

A thorough history revealed no risk factors of acute hepatitis including recent travel, medication, alcohol or drug use, and prior history of wasp stings. On examination, no signs of chronic liver disease or decompensation such as hepatic encephalopathy were found. Laboratory investigations (Figures 2 and 3) showed a conjugated hyperbilirubinemia (peak level of 21.5 mg/dL), a hepatocellular pattern of injury (peak levels of alanine transaminase 3,970 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase 1,620 U/L, alkaline phosphatase 196 U/L, gamma-glutamyl transferase 173 U/L) and evidence of synthetic dysfunction (peak international normalized ratio 1.7). Renal function was preserved, and there was no evidence of other organ involvement. He was monitored closely over a period of 61 days.

A comprehensive screen for infective (hepatitis A, B, C, and E viruses; human immunodeficiency virus; cytomegalovirus virus; Epstein-Barr virus; and leptospirosis), autoimmune, and other (paracetamol, hemochromatosis and Wilson disease) causes of hepatitis returned negative. Magnetic resonance imaging of the liver revealed no evidence of obstruction or chronic liver disease. Liver biopsy performed 5 days following presentation showed a non-cirrhotic liver with hepatocyte swelling, acidophilic bodies, and inflammatory cells composed of lymphocytes, histiocytes, neutrophils, plasma cells, and a few eosinophils. Mild piecemeal necrosis was seen involving the portal tracts, without bridging or confluent areas of necrosis (Figure 4). No features supporting autoimmune hepatitis were observed.

Liver biopsy with periodic acid-Schiff staining showing piecemeal necrosis (10× magnification).

The patient was managed with supportive care including 3 days of intravenous phytomenadione and 3 days of empiric N-acetyl cysteine infusion. He remained stable without features of acute liver failure; however, due to minimal improvement of his liver function with supportive care, oral prednisone (30 mg daily) was commenced 9 days after hospital presentation and weaned over 40 days. He was monitored in hospital for 11 days and in the outpatient setting closely for 2 months. Coagulopathy was noted to persist for >2 weeks following presentation. Resolution of jaundice and complete normalization of liver function tests was seen 61 days following presentation.

DISCUSSION

Wasps with the ability to sting humans include hornets, yellow jackets, and paper wasps.6 Only female wasps sting, usually as a defense mechanism to a perceived threat.1 Mass envenomation is more likely to occur when a threat is perceived to a wasp colony such as after disturbing a nest.1,6 Multiple stings can expose a single subject to a range of biogenic amines (histamine, serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine), nonenzymatic protein toxins (wasp kinin), and enzymes (phospholipase A, phospholipase B, and hyaluronidase).3

Ex vivo data from the 1980s demonstrated a direct toxic effect of WV on hepatocytes.7,8 In a study on isolated perfused rat livers, a 24 fold rise in alkaline phosphatase, 6 fold rise in alanine transaminase, and 9 fold rise in aspartate aminotransferase was seen from isolated rat livers exposed to venom sac extract from Vespa orientalis.7 In another rat-model study, histology from rat livers with repeated exposure to Vespa orientalis venom showed evidence of hepatocyte necrosis, reduction in number of mitochondria per cell, and fat infiltration.8

Clinical cases of hepatotoxicity following WV exposure have been described in the literature, with a multicenter study of 1,091 cases in China estimating a prevalence of 30.1%.9 The typical presentation is of jaundice, conjugated hyperbilirubinemia, liver enzyme derangement with a hepatocellular pattern, and coagulopathy, as seen in our case.9 A dose-dependent relationship has also been observed with subjects with >10 stings having a higher likelihood of liver injury and coagulopathy.9 Two deaths have been observed in children following mass WV exposure, both due to envenomation by Vespa orientalis.4,5

Hepatotoxicity has largely been reported in the literature in the context of presentation with severe immediate or delayed hypersensitivity reactions to WV and multiorgan failure.4,5 Two recent case reports following mass envenomation by wasps describe liver injury alongside renal failure and rhabdomyolysis.10,11 A case series in China of 103 patients found acute toxic hepatitis following wasp stings in 46.6 percent of patients and other direct toxin-mediated organ injuries, including acute interstitial nephritis and toxic myocarditis.12

We present a rare case of isolated liver injury and propose that this possibly reflects a distinct clinical entity. To the best of our knowledge, only one case of isolated hepatitis has been reported in a 50-year-old man following exposure to Vespa affinis.13 This case had a short latency between toxin exposure and onset of liver injury of 4 hours without any evidence of hypotension, and the subject recovered completely with supportive management within 4 days.13 Our case demonstrates the possibility of a delayed reaction, with an envenomation to jaundice time of >48 hours and persistence of coagulopathy for >2 weeks. Currently, no evidence-based guidelines exist for management of this condition. Our patient recovered fully within 61 days, and although steroids were initiated, their role in accelerating recovery was not clear.

We acknowledge the limitations in this case report including the fact that the species of wasp was not able to be identified, and the uncertain role steroids may have played in this unique setting. Further research to elucidate the mechanism of direct liver injury following WV exposure is required. Similarly, greater experience of therapeutics in patients with a protracted course of illness is needed.

In conclusion, we have reported a case of isolated acute liver injury following multiple wasp stings in a healthy individual with a prolonged recovery period. This likely reflects a different clinical entity to that of a hypersensitivity reaction, which requires greater awareness.
