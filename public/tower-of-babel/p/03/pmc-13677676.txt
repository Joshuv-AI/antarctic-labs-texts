Environmental change increases the contribution of rare microbial species to ecosystem functioning in agricultural soils
Authors: Jiejun Qi, Min Gao, Xinyi Hou, Rong Da, Jiai Liu, Xinye Wang, Chun Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding the responses of soil microbial communities to environmental change, as well as their contributions to ecosystem functioning under disturbed conditions, is of fundamental importance. Yet, the relative roles of rare and abundant microbes in sustaining soil functions under environmental changes remain poorly understood, particularly in agricultural ecosystems. Here, we sampled soils from 50 agricultural fields across eastern China to examine the response patterns of rare and abundant sub-communities, encompassing multitrophic soil organisms, including bacteria, fungi, and protists, to climate warming, dry-wet cycles, and nutrient enrichment, and assessed their relative contributions to ecosystem multifunctionality. We found that, unlike abundant taxa, rare species diversity significantly decreased with dry-wet cycles but increased with nutrient addition. In addition, the community resistance of abundant species was significantly higher than that of rare species under all of treatments. Co-occurrence network analysis showed that species associations increased under environmental changes in both abundant and rare sub-communities, while the shift was more pronounced in rare species. Notably, rare species emerged as the main drivers of ecosystem function, with their contributions becoming more pronounced under environmental changes. Collectively, these results highlight the pivotal role of rare species in supporting and enhancing agricultural ecosystem functioning under global change scenarios.

Introduction

Soil microorganisms are key regulators of biogeochemical cycles and play a vital role in soil functions, including fertility, crop productivity, and stress resistance1. Agricultural ecosystems are increasingly subjected to anthropogenic pressures and climate changes such as warming, drought, excessive fertilization, and the bioaccumulation of pesticides and antibiotics2,3, all of which significantly impact the structure and function of soil microbiomes4. For example, nutrient (N and P) addition regulates microbial diversity via three pathways: i) nutrient addition increases nutrient availability, thereby alleviating microbial resource limitation and influencing microbial diversity5,6; ii) nutrient addition alters microbial interactions, which in turn affects their diversity7; iii) nutrient addition can modify plant-soil feedback (PSF), thereby influencing soil microbial diversity8. In addition, warming-induced diversity loss stems from intensified environmental filtering (e.g., drought and alkalinization) and exacerbated N limitation9. Therefore, understanding how soil microorganisms respond to environmental changes is crucial for the development of sustainable agricultural systems.

Microbial communities typically consist of a few abundant species that dominate in biomass, alongside a large number of rare species present at very low abundances—often referred to as the “rare biosphere”10,11. Abundant and rare taxa generally play distinct roles in shaping microbial community structure and functions12. Owing to their abundance dominance, abundant taxa have historically received more research attention, while the ecological significance of rare taxa has often been overlooked11. Yet, rare taxa act as a reservoir of genetic and functional diversity, with mounting evidence highlighting their essential ecological roles13,14. For example, rare species can contribute to preserving the community’s structural stability during stress or selective pressures15,16, offering a form of “ecological insurance”17. Nevertheless, there is still controversy in existing studies regarding the stability differences between abundant and rare species under disturbances. One perspective suggests that abundant taxa, with broader ecological niches and greater resource flexibility, are more resistant to disturbance13,18. Conversely, some rare taxa may be activated under stress conditions and temporarily increase in abundance, thereby supporting community stability19,20. Currently, the overall response patterns of these sub-communities to environmental change remain unclear, limiting our understanding of microbial community stability and its implications for ecosystem functions.

Revealing the relative contribution of rare and abundant microbial sub-communities to ecosystem functions under environmental changes is essential for predicting the stability of global ecosystems21. Traditionally, abundant species have been considered the primary drivers of microbial biomass and nutrient cycling, thus playing a central role in maintaining ecosystem multifunctionality22. However, recent studies have highlighted the significant contributions of rare species to specialized functions, including sulfate reduction and phenanthrene degradation23,24. Additionally, the significant role of rare species in various soil biogeochemical processes, including nitrification, denitrification, and methanogenesis, has been established.25,26. Notably, some rare taxa exhibit higher metabolic activity per unit biomass than abundant taxa within similarly sized communities, suggesting an over-proportional functional role27,28. Recent studies suggested that the distinct roles in maintaining ecosystem multifunctionality of abundant and rare sub-communities were associated with their different functional traits and ecological niches11,29. Moreover, while it is known that environmental changes can alter BEF relationships, existing evidence is largely based on analyses of the overall microbial community or individual species30,31. How environmental disturbances affect the relationships between the diversity of abundant and rare sub-communities and ecosystem functions remains poorly understood. In particular, the shifting contributions of rare and abundant species to ecosystem functions, from undisturbed (ambient) to disturbed conditions, are still unclear. Elucidating the roles of both fractions under changing environments is essential for predicting ecosystem resilience and sustainability.

In this study, we propose two hypotheses: (1) rare microbial taxa are more sensitive to environmental changes than abundant taxa, and (2) rare taxa play a disproportionately important role in maintaining ecosystem functions under environmental stress. To test these hypotheses, we conducted incubation experiments using soil samples collected from 25 paired maize and rice fields across eastern China to simulate multiple environmental changes, including experimental warming, dry-wet cycling, and nutrient (N, P) addition. The objectives as follows: (1) to reveal the response patterns of rare and abundant species under global changes, and (2) to investigate the connections between rare and abundant species and ecosystem functions under environmental change conditions. This study aims to establish a research framework for understanding the differential responses and functional maintenance of abundant and rare sub-communities under environmental changes.

Results

Resistance of rare and abundant sub-communities to environmental changes

The diversity of the abundant sub-community showed no significant response to environmental changes (Fig. 1a). In contrast, the diversity of the rare sub-community responded strongly, decreasing significantly under the dry–wet cycle treatment but increasing under nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P) addition (Fig. 1b). Additionally, by subsampling an equal number of rare taxa as the abundant ones, we observed that rare microbial diversity continued to exhibit significant variations in response to environmental changes (Fig. S1). A significant response of the diversity of rare sub-community to disturbances was also found in both maize and rice soils (Fig. S2a and S2b). Specifically, rare sub-community diversity significantly decreased under NP enrichment in maize soils, but increased under warming, N, and P enrichment in rice soils (Fig. S2a and S2b). Contributions of rare and abundant species to ecosystem functions under environmental changes

Under control conditions, linear regression revealed no significant relationship between the diversity of abundant or rare taxa and ecosystem functions (Fig. 4a, b). However, we found significant positive relationship between the diversity of abundant and rare species and soil multifunctionality under disturbed conditions, with the relationship being more pronounced for rare species (Fig. 4a, b). In addition, we found that the relationship between abundant species diversity and multifunctionality increased under warming and dry-wet cycles treatments (Fig. 4c, e). Notably, the link between rare species diversity and multifunctionality was enhanced from ambient to disturbed conditions, with steeper slopes and greater explanatory power (Fig. 4d, f). In addition, the same result was also observed in rice soils (Fig. S8). In contrast, except NP enrichment, the relationship between the diversity of rare species in maize soils and ecosystem function was weakened under environmental changes (Fig. S8). Furthermore, environmental disturbances enhanced the correlations between ecosystem multifunctionality and several rare taxa, including Planctomycetes, Proteobacteria, Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, Chytridiomycota, Cryptomycota, Chlorophyta, Ciliophora, Ochrophyta, Peronosporomycetes, and Phragmoplastophyta (Fig. S9b). Variation partitioning analysis (VPA) further revealed that, except warming treatment, the relationship between rare species and ecosystem functions was stronger than that of abundant species across all treatments (Fig. 4g). Also, we found that environmental changes were enhanced the link between rare species diversity and multifunctionality (Fig. 4g). Such correlations were not observed for abundant species (Fig. 4h).

To further test these relationships, we performed structural equation modeling (SEM) to examine whether the stronger contribution of rare species to ecosystem functions persisted under environmental changes when accounting for soil properties and climatic factors. Under control conditions, neither abundant nor rare sub-community diversity was significantly correlated with multifunctionality (Fig. 5a). However, under disturbance conditions, the diversity of both groups was significantly correlated with ecosystem functions, with rare species showing the strongest effects (Fig. 5b). Additionally, available phosphorus not only directly influenced ecosystem functions but also indirectly affected them by altering rare species diversity under disturbed conditions (Fig. 5b). In addition, soil organic carbon was significantly correlated with ecosystem functions in both ambient and disturbed environments (Fig. 5a, b). Finally, we found that the relative abundance of dominant microbial groups within the rare sub-community was positively associated with ecosystem functions, whereas the abundant sub-community showed the opposite trend (Fig. S10a, S10b). Discussion

Agricultural ecosystems are increasingly exposed to environmental changes, which can drive biodiversity loss across multiple trophic levels32,33 and, in turn, impair ecosystem functions and services34–36. By integrating large-scale field sampling with controlled microcosm incubation experiments, we demonstrated that abundant and rare microbial taxa differ markedly in their responses to environmental disturbances and in their contributions to ecosystem functioning. This study integrated large-scale surveys with microcosm experiments revealed the asymmetrical responses of abundant and rare taxa to environmental fluctuations and assessed their relative contributions to ecosystem multifunctionality. Notably, rare taxa were both more sensitive to environmental change and disproportionately important to sustaining ecosystem function. In short, results suggest that rare taxa are disproportionately important to soils under changing conditions.

Elucidating the response patterns of soil microbial communities to environmental changes is essential for understanding the mechanisms that maintain community stability37,38. We found that the abundant sub-community, in contrast to the rare biosphere, exhibited higher resistance to disturbances. This is consistent with the results of previous studies, which shows that abundant taxa exhibit greater resilience to environmental perturbations than rare taxa32,39. The underlying mechanism is that high abundance and broad environmental thresholds increase physiological plasticity and competitive ability, which reduces the risk of local extinction under stress and consequently enhances community stability under global change9,40,41. This functional asymmetry between rare and abundant components may play a critical role in maintaining community stability under global change scenarios.

In addition, our findings found that rare species diversity increased under nutrient addition condition. This is consistent with the results of a long‑term nitrogen and phosphorus addition experiment, which shows that nutrient addition increases microbial diversity42. The underlying mechanism is that nutrient addition increases soil available nutrients, which reduces resource limitation among microorganisms and consequently enhances microbial diversity43. We further demonstrated that abundant species consistently exhibited broader niche breadths than rare species across treatments. According to niche theory, species with wider niches can exploit a greater range of resources, thereby enhancing stability under environmental change10,13. Supporting this, a large-scale survey also revealed that rare taxa tend to occupy narrower niches than abundant taxa in both bulk and rhizosphere soils39. Together, these findings suggest that abundant species sustain stability under disturbances by leveraging their broader access to soil resources.

The biodiversity–ecosystem functioning (BEF) relationship is a central ecological topic and is critical for ensuring ecosystem sustainability29,34,35,44. Our results revealed that rare taxa, rather than abundant taxa, exhibited stronger correlations with ecosystem functions. This agrees with earlier findings, such as studies on cadmium-contaminated soils, where rare taxa dominated the recovery of ecosystem functions following remediation amendments45. Similarly, a large-scale observational survey demonstrated that rare biospheres contributes substantially to numerous individual functions46. The underlying mechanism is that environmental changes activate the microbial “seed bank” (low-abundance or dormant taxa), which provides an “insurance effect” for the community and consequently enhances ecosystem functioning17,47. In addition, rare taxa may sustain ecosystem functions by supplying complementary or unique metabolic pathways—for example, producing vitamins or amino acids required by other microbes and plants11,48.

Biodiversity is widely recognized as a key factor in maintaining and buffering ecosystem functions under environmental changes49,50. However, disturbances can reshape species dynamics and interactions, thereby altering the BEF relationship51,52. In the present study, we found that the positive link between rare species diversity and ecosystem functioning was strengthened under environmental change. This is consistent with recent findings showing that warming amplifies biodiversity effects on phytoplankton biomass production53. The underlying mechanism is that environmental disturbance intensifies stochastic fluctuations within rare microbial subcommunities, which in turn amplifies the complementarity effects among rare taxa, thereby enhancing their relative contribution to ecosystem functioning54,55. Furthermore, increasing evidence suggests that higher network complexity strengthens BEF relationships56. Consistent with this, our findings showed that species associations within rare sub-communities increased under environmental changes, implying that the enhanced contribution of rare taxa to ecosystem functions may partly arise from their increased network complexity. Overall, our findings underscore the critical and often underappreciated role of rare microbial taxa in maintaining ecosystem multifunctionality under global change scenarios. These results highlight the need for policymakers and land managers to prioritize the conservation of rare microbial taxa as a strategy to enhance both the productivity and long-term sustainability of agricultural ecosystems.

Conclusions

We propose a conceptual framework that illustrates the contrasting stability of rare and abundant sub-communities and their respective contributions to ecosystem functions under multiple disturbances in agricultural ecosystems (Fig. 6a, b). Abundant species exhibited greater stability across diversity, community structure, and network properties under disturbance conditions. In contrast, although rare species displayed lower stability, they emerged as the primary drivers of ecosystem functions, with their influence becoming increasingly pronounced in disturbed environments. Overall, these findings advance our understanding of how ecosystem functions are sustained under disturbances and provide a foundation for predicting belowground microbial community responses to global change in agricultural systems. This improved knowledge ultimately enhances our capacity to manage ecological linkages and, in turn, improve agroecosystem services. Finally, our study carries a methodological implication: when employing microcosm incubation experiments to assess soil microbial responses to environmental changes, extended incubation durations should be considered. Methods

Site description and soil incubation

Topsoil samples were collected from 25 long-term cultivation paired maize and rice locations, which extend from 18.25° N to 47.21° N and 102.55° E to 127.15° E. In brief, three plots of 100 m2 were sampled at each site in planting season. From these plots, we collected five soil cores (0–15 cm) from each plot, then combined to one sample. Afterwards, the plant debris and rocks were removed through 2-mm mesh filtration. The climatic information, such as mean annual temperature (MAT), was acquired from the WorldClim database (https://www.world clim.org), which ranged between 2.6 and 25.6 °C.

Soils were subjected to incubation to evaluate the effects of various environmental changes, such as experimental warming, dry-wet cycling and nutrient (N, P) addition. For each site, 50 g of soil was placed in glass bottles with sealing caps, and each treatment contained three replicates. The control treatment was incubated at 20 °C with 60% water holding capacity (WHC) for maize and a flooded state for rice, replicating a more natural condition. The warming treatment maintained similar water conditions as the control but with a temperature increased 5 °C. An increment of 5.0 °C over the ambient temperature is frequently used in experiments testing the effect of warming57,58, and this increment is the predicted climate scenario for the next 100 years59. The soils with dry-wet cycle, under similar temperature conditions as the control, involved cycles of drying and wetting. Each wetting–drying cycle involved wetting until a 60% WHC for dryland and flooded for wetland was achieved and a subsequent natural drying for 14 days. N enrichment treatment was sustained similar temperature and water condition as the control, with additional nitrogen (150 kg N ha–1 year–1) added. P enrichment treatment was conducted at similar temperature and water condition as the control, with additional phosphorus (100 kg P ha–1 year–1) added. NP addition treatment, with similar temperature and water condition as the control, featured the addition of both nitrogen and phosphorus (N: 150 kg N ha–1 year–1 and P: 100 kg P ha–1 year–1). N and/or P in the form of ammonium nitrate (NH4NO3)60 and sodium dihydrogen phosphate (NaH2PO4)61 were dissolved in sterile water, and added during the initial watering. Except for the dry-wet cycle treatment, the moisture content of other treatments was adjusted and maintained during incubation. These bottles were incubated under the different treatments for 170 days. After the experiment was completed, we combined the three replicates into one sample.

Sample collection and DNA extraction

After incubation, the soil samples were collected and stored at −80 °C for subsequent DNA extraction. Total DNA was extracted from soil samples using a FastDNA SPIN Kit for Soil (MP Biochemicals, Solon, OH, USA). Soil bacterial, fungal, and protistan communities were estimated using high-throughput sequencing techniques. 515 F and 907 R to amplify prokaryotic 16S rRNA gene V4- V5 regions, 528 F and 706 R to amplify eukaryotic V4 region. The sequencing process was performed on the Illumina HiSeq2500 platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Utilizing “DADA2” package, amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were trimmed, filtered, and inferred in each sample, with chimeras subsequently removed62. In brief, the “plotQualityProfile” command was run to detect the quality of the amplified sequences63. Subsequently, we used the “removeBimeraDenovo” command to remove chimeras64. Finally, the paired-ends sequences that passed quality control were joined, and the amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were identified. In addition, the ASVs that belonged to mitochondria and chloroplasts were removed for 16S sequences. For protistan community, the ASVs belonged to fungi, invertebrates (Metazoa), and vascular plants (Streptophyta) were removed based on previous studies29,65. Sequence alignment was conducted against the SILVA database (release 138) for bacteria and the Protist Ribosomal Reference database (PR2) for fungi and protists66,67. Finally, the sequences numbers in each sample were refined to the same depth for the bacteria (29688 reads), fungi (5836 reads) and protist (10165 reads).

Definition of rare and abundant sub-communities

Considering the variations in species abundance distributions among organisms, specifically for bacterial communities, ASVs with relative abundance above 0.1% in all samples were defined as “abundant species”, while those with relative abundances below 0.05% were defined as “rare species”16,68,69. For eukaryotic microorganisms (fungi and protist), ASVs with relative abundance above 1% in all samples were defined as “abundant species”, while those with relative abundances below 0.1% were defined as “rare species”70,71. In order to obtain a biodiversity index of rare and abundant species, we calculated the Shannon index of bacterial, fungal, and protistan communities based on the R packages “vegan”72. Then, we standardized the richness index of individual groups (bacteria, fungi, and protists) of rare and abundant communities using a uniform scale from 0 to 134,73. This standardization was obtained based on the following formula: STD = (X - Xmin)/(Xmax - Xmin), where STD represent the standardized variable. In addition, the Xmin and Xmax represent the minimum value and maximum value of target variable X in all samples, respectively. Then, we obtained a microbial diversity index by averaged the standardized richness of bacteria, fungi, and protists.

Ecosystem functioning measures

After incubation, we measured thirteen functions related to C, N and P cycling in all soil samples. These functions encompassed five enzyme activities and eight functional genes. The enzyme activities that related carbon cycling [β-glucosidase and saccharase], nitrogen cycling [N-acetylglucosaminidase], and phosphorus cycling [phosphatase] were measured using fluorometry as described previously74. Assays were conducted using 96-well microtiter plates, with eight replicate wells per sample per assay. Briefly, 125 mL of Tris–HCl buffer was added to 3 g of soil for extracellular enzymes extraction. Then, an eight-channel pipettor was used to add 150 μL of soil suspension liquid and 50 μL of fluorometric substrate to each well of the microtiter plates. The microplates were then cultured in dark conditions at 25 °C. A microplate fluorometer with wavelength filters, in which excitation was 365 nm and emission was 450 nm, was used to determine fluorescence. The enzyme activity was expressed as nmol h−1 g−1 dry soil. The functional genes were associated with organic carbon degradation (Fungicbhif, GH31), N fixation (nifH), nitrification (AOA and AOB), denitrification (nirK and nirS) and organic P mineralization (phoD) were measured by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), performed on the QuantStudio 6 Flex system (Life Technologies). Primer sequences of functional genes were obtained based on previous studies75–80, and standard curves were generated using serial dilutions of linearized plasmid DNA containing specific gene fragments. Functional genes primer sequences information and procedure is presented in Table S1. A 20 μl reaction volume were established for qPCR assays, that containing template DNA 1 μl, each primer 0.5 μl, SYBR Premix 10 μl, and double-distilled water (ddH2O) 8 μl. Each samples contained three repetitions for every functional gene.

Physicochemical analyses

Soil pH, nitrate (NO3−-N), ammonium (NH4+-N) and available phosphorus (AP) were determined based on previous study81. Organic matter content was determined using a loss on ignition (LOI) method, in which the soil was burned at 550 °C for 4 h, and then 0.5 M H2SO4 was used as the extractant82.

Statistical analysis

To compare the stability differences between abundant and rare species, we first calculated the biodiversity resistance for both abundant and rare sub-communities using the approach described by Orwin and Wardle83: RS = 1 − 2 × |D0 | /(C0 + |D0 | ). In the equation, C0 is the value of microbial diversity in the control treatment, and D0 is the difference of microbial diversity between the control and treated soils after the incubation period. This approach captures resistance as a relative deviation (i.e., relative changes) normalized to baseline conditions, thereby enabling meaningful comparisons between abundant and rare species. Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) analysis was used to visualize the change in abundant and rare sub-communities structure under environmental changes. Then, we evaluated the community resistance of abundant and rare sub-communities according to the degree of deviation between control and treated soils. Overall, we used the microbial community similarity (1−Bray-Curtis dissimilarity) between control and treated samples of each pairwise set of soil sampling sites as community resistance, estimated via “vegan” package in R84. This metric represents a standardized measure of relative change (i.e., effect size) between abundant and rare sub-communities, rather than relying on absolute values. The difference in community resistance between abundant and rare species under multiple disturbances was examined via Wilcoxon rank-sum test, using the “wilcox.test” function in the “stats” package in R.

Co-occurrence networks of both abundant and rare sub-communities were constructed for each treatment. Robust correlations based on Spearman’s correlation coefficients (ρ) of > 0.7 or < −0.7 and false discovery rate-corrected p-values < 0.001 were used to construct networks. The topological features of the networks were calculated to estimate the network complexity85,86, including average degree, average path length, network diameter, clustering coefficient, graph density, and modularity87. Through averaging the standardized scores of these topological features, we obtain the network complexity index to explore the changes in microbial interactions under disturbances. Before calculation, the average path length and diameter were transformed (×−1), due to these features indicating the network sparsity. These topological features were calculated using “igraph” package in R. In addition, we calculated a network dissimilarity index to reflect the change of co-occurrence relationships of abundant and rare sub-communities under disturbances based on previous researches88,89.

Niche breadth represents the range of environments that can be adapted by specific microbial taxa. In this study, niche breadth was calculated using Levin’s methods in the ‘spaa’ package. To explore the relative contributions of abundant and rare species to ecosystem functions, we obtained an ecosystem multifunctionality index by averaging the standardized scores of the above 13 ecosystem functions34,73. Subsequently, we compared the relationships between the biodiversity of abundant and rare sub-communities and ecosystem multifunctionality using linear regressions. Variance Partitioning Analysis (VPA) was also used to evaluate the relative contributions of abundant and rare sub-communities to ecosystem functioning. We used Structural equation modeling (SEM) to assess the direct and indirect link between biodiversity of abundant and rare sub-communities, soil properties and ecosystem functions. The model was tested using a χ2 test, standardized root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and comparative fit index (CFI). Generally, CFI values > 0.95 indicated very favorable goodness of fit90. RMSEA values < 0.05 manifested very satisfactory goodness of fit. SEM was performed using ‘lavaan’ package91.
