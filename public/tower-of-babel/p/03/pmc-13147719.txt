AHSP as a novel non-invasive urinary biomarker for prostate cancer
Authors: Chen Wang, Yang-yang Zhang, Hong-qun Wang, Cong Huang, Na Yu, Yan Huang, Ya-nan Zhu, De-lun Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile prostate cancer (PCa) is a common malignant tumor in men, current tumor markers have poor specificity. Our previous exploratory research had identified alpha-hemoglobin–stabilizing protein (AHSP) as a potential novel tumor marker. This study aimed to investigate the expression and clinical significance of AHSP in PCa.Materials and methodsAHSP levels in urine samples from patients with PCa, benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), urothelial carcinoma, or clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) were determined using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Immunohistochemical staining was performed on 101 PCa tissue samples to assess AHSP expression. Urinary proteomic profiling was employed to compare protein expression patterns in urothelial carcinoma and ccRCC to validate the PCa–specific AHSP expression.ResultsImmunohistochemical analysis revealed that AHSP was expressed in 93 (92.1%) PCa tissue samples. High AHSP expression showed a positive correlation with androgen receptor (AR) level (r = 0.353, p < 0.001). The incidence of high AHSP expression increased progressively with higher Gleason scores, and there was a significant difference between the Gleason 6–7 and 8–10 groups (57.4% vs. 83.0%, p = 0.005). AHSP expression was also positively correlated with Synaptophysin (Syn) expression (r = 0.332, p = 0.001), and AHSP was observed in normal neural ganglia. While the sensitivity of AHSP was slightly higher than that of prostate-specific antigen (PSA) (92.1% vs. 89.1%), the difference was not statistically significant. Urinary ELISA analysis demonstrated that the PCa group had the highest mean AHSP level (0.649 ± 0.055ng/ml), which was significantly higher than those in the BPH group (0.456 ± 0.058 ng/ml), the urothelial carcinoma group (0.446 ± 0.052 ng/ml), and the ccRCC group (0.438 ± 0.053 ng/ml) (all p < 0.0001). Notably, in urine samples, AHSP demonstrated significantly higher sensitivity than PSA (96.2% vs. 61.5%, p = 0.011).ConclusionAHSP expression contributes to the diagnosis of PCa and is associated with tumor differentiation. Its positive correlation with AR expression and neuroendocrine differentiation suggests potential clinical relevance for treatment guidance. In urinary assays, AHSP exhibits higher sensitivity than PSA. AHSP exhibits relatively specific urinary expression in PCa, highlighting its potential as a noninvasive tumor biomarker.Keywords: Prostate cancer, Alpha-hemoglobin-stabilizing Protein (AHSP), Androgen receptor, Synaptophysin, Urinary Biomarker, Noninvasive diagnosis

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) represents a major global health concern and is the second most common malignancy in men after skin cancer. In 2020, PCa accounted for approximately 375,000 deaths worldwide [1], and its incidence continues to rise [2]. Although prostate-specific antigen (PSA) screening enables early detection and significantly improves prognosis, its high false-positive rate and limited specificity [2] often lead to overdiagnosis, unnecessary invasive biopsies, and overtreatment of indolent disease. The 5-year survival rate of PCa patients varies markedly across stages: it approaches 100% in stages I–II but drops to approximately 50% in stage IV [3]. Therefore, more effective screening methods capable of detecting early-stage, clinically significant PCa are urgently needed [2].

Recent exploration of urinary biomarkers has shown promise for the early, noninvasive, and precise diagnosis of clinically relevant PCa [4]. Alpha-hemoglobin–stabilizing protein (AHSP) is a molecular chaperone that functions as a scavenger protein. Free α-globin chains tend to precipitate, exerting cytotoxic effects on erythroid cells and disrupting erythrocyte maturation. AHSP binds reversibly to free α-hemoglobin (αHb) to form a stable AHSP–αHb complex, thereby preventing aggregation and precipitation of α-globin and mitigating the deleterious effects associated with severe human disorders such as β-thalassemia [5–8]. Multiple studies have demonstrated a significant association between AHSP and tumorigenesis [9–11]. In studies of acute myeloid leukemia (AML), reduced expression of AHSP has been shown to disrupt normal erythroid maturation and accelerate the malignant transformation of myeloid–erythroid progenitors (MEPs). Recent findings have described a pathogenic cascade in which FLT3 internal tandem duplication (FLT3-ITD) mutations promote leukemogenesis by activating the STAT5/MAPK signaling pathway, while AHSP expression levels are inversely correlated with FLT3 activity [10]. Functional impairment of AHSP markedly enhances FLT3-ITD–driven oncogenic signaling, leading to increased cellular proliferation and inhibition of apoptosis [11]. However, evidence also suggests that AHSP acts beyond hematologic malignancies. Aberrant AHSP expression has been observed across multiple solid tumors, challenging its traditional role in erythropoiesis and establishing it as a new cancer research focus.

Our preliminary study had revealed that urinary proteomic level of AHSP was significantly higher in PCa tissues than that in normal controls. In our present study, we expanded the sample size to measure urinary AHSP levels, thereby validating our previous findings. To assess the specificity of urinary AHSP for prostate cancer, we compared its levels in prostate cancer patients with those in patients with benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), urothelial carcinoma, and clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC)—one of the most common renal malignancies. These analyses aimed to further deepen the comprehensive understanding of AHSP and to facilitate its application in diagnosis and differential diagnosis. The objective of this study was to elucidate the biological and clinical significance of AHSP expression in PCa and to determine its potential as a noninvasive urinary biomarker.

Materials and methods

Data collection

We collected clinical and pathological data from 101 patients diagnosed with PCa and obtained 20 adjacent normal prostate tissue samples between May 2017 and November 2024 at the Department of Pathological Diagnosis, the Third People’s Hospital of Bengbu. These PCa patients aged 54 to 95 years (median: 74 years; mean: 73.9 years). Serum PSA levels were determined in 68 patients. Additionally, 10 patients with urothelial carcinoma (aged from 38 to 83 years; mean:71.7 years) and 20 patients with ccRCC (age 39–82 years; mean:66.5 years) were included for comparative analysis. Telephone follow-up of PCa patients spanned 8 months to 8 years until February 2025, collecting data on general health, recurrence, and survivals.

Immunohistochemistry

AHSP (EPR12319) for immunohistochemistry (IHC) was purchased from Abcam and diluted 1:30–1:40. AR (MXR023), Syn (MX038), and PSA (MAB-0146) antibodies were obtained from Maixin Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Secondary antibodies were purchased from Beijing Zhongshan Jinqiao Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Tissue sections were subjected to antigen retrieval using high-temperature, high-pressure EDTA, followed by incubation with 3% H₂O₂ to block endogenous peroxidase activity. Sections were then incubated overnight with primary antibodies, then for 20 min at 37 °C in a constant-temperature incubator. After color development using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB), the sections were counterstained, mounted with neutral gel, and coverslipped.

For AHSP, PSA, and AR, cells with ≥ 10% staining were considered positive, and ≥ 60% cells in tissues exhibiting moderate to strong staining were defined as high expression. Staining intensity was categorized as weak, moderate, or strong. For Syn, ≥ 5% of tumor cells displaying brown-yellow cytoplasmic staining were considered positive, indicative of focal neuroendocrine differentiation.

Urinary ELISA

A cross-sectional study was conducted involving 41 participants, with preoperative morning urine samples (20 ml each) collected from 26 PCa patients, 5 patients with BPH (as controls; age range: 65–74 years; mean: 69.4 years), 5 patients with ccRCC (age range: 46–54 years; mean: 52.2 years), and 5 patients with urothelial carcinoma (age range: 54–86 years; mean: 67.6 years).

Urinary AHSP and PSA levels were determined using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) following the manufacturer’s instructions for the ELISA kit (Jiangsu Meimian Industrial Co. Ltd., Jiangsu, China; catalog number MM-65437H2 of AHSP; catalog number MM-0421H1 of PSA). Briefly, urine samples were centrifuged for supernatant collection and diluted based on preliminary experiments. Standard solutions were prepared as serial dilutions according to the kit protocol. Next, processed samples and standard solutions (100 µL each) were added to the microplate wells. After sealing and incubating at 37°C for 30 min, wells were washed 5 times with wash buffer to remove unbound substances thoroughly. Then, 100 µL of horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated detection antibody was added and incubated at 37°C for another 30 minutes. After the above steps were repeated for five washes, 100 µl of freshly prepared substrate solution (TMB) was added to each well and incubated at 37°C in the dark for another 10 minutes to develop color. Once a distinct color gradient was observed in the standard wells, 50 µL of stop solution was added to each well, resulting in a color change from blue to yellow. Within 15 min after stopping the reaction, the optical density (OD) of each well was measured at 450 nm (reference 630 nm) using a microplate reader. Finally, the AHSP levels in the urine samples were determined using a standard curve of OD values versus standard concentrations and adjusted for dilution.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Linear regression analysis was performed to validate the accuracy and reliability of protein measurements in urine samples obtained by ELISA. Two-sample independent t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to compare mean protein concentrations between the PCa group and other groups, as well as to evaluate intergroup differences. Chi-square tests were applied to assess differences in categorical variables using 2 × 2 or R×C contingency tables. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was conducted to evaluate associations between variables. Univariate survival analysis was performed using Kaplan–Meier survival curves and the log-rank test. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Research Design

Based on preliminary proteomic studies demonstrating that AHSP was significantly elevated in the urine of PCa patients compared to normal controls, this study further investigated the AHSP expression in the urine samples and cancerous tissues of PCa patients to evaluate its clinical value. The specific research workflow is as follows: urine samples collected from patients with PCa, BPH, urothelial carcinoma, and ccRCC were detected using ELISA for AHSP levels, and the results were compared with PSA to assess the value of AHSP in differential diagnosis.

Furthermore, the tissue samples collected from patients with PCa, paracancerous normal tissue, urothelial carcinoma, and ccRCC underwent immunohistochemical test for AHSP expression across different tissues. The PCa group was compared with various control groups to verify whether there was elevated AHSP expression. Additionally, the potential correlations of AHSP with AR, Syn, PSA, and ACSM1 (from previous research) in the PCa group were analyzed to further explore the potential of AHSP and PSA as complementary markers for prostate cancer detection. Results

Immunohistochemical findings

In the PCa group (n = 101), the positive expression rate of AHSP in PCa tissues was 92.1% (93/101) (sensitivity: 92.1%). Among the negative cases (n = 8), 4 showed minimal or weak expression, and 4 were completely unstained. Among 20 normal prostate tissue samples, 18 showed no AHSP expression, while the remaining 2 showed gland expression (in 20–30% of cells) and lacked diffuse expression, resulting in a positive rate of 10.0% (2/20) and a negative rate of 90.0% (18/20) (specificity: 90.0%).

The high-expression rate of AHSP in PCa tissues was 69.3%, whereas no high expression was observed in normal tissues (Fig. 2). Correlation analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between high AHSP expression and high AR expression in the PCa group (r = 0.353, p < 0.001). High AHSP expression was also positively correlated with Syn expression (r = 0.332, p = 0.001). Notably, AHSP staining was observed in normal ganglion cells and nerve fiber, suggesting a potential association between AHSP expression and neuroendocrine differentiation, which may inform therapeutic strategies.

Univariate survival analysis of Kaplan–Meier curves (log-rank test) indicated that AHSP expression was not significantly associated with cancer-specific survival in this cohort (p > 0.05), although further investigations with larger samples are warranted to clarify the prognostic significance of AHSP. While AHSP was not expressed in some clear cells, it was expressed in some normal tissues and cells including striated muscle, normal renal tubules, erythrocytes, smooth muscles, and ganglion cells. AHSP exhibited low expression in ccRCCs, suggesting that it might be either absent or minimally expressed in tumors of clear cell lineage.

In the cross-sectional comparison, AHSP expression varied among different tissues: it was highly expressed in PCa, showed low expression in ccRCC, and was positive in all urothelial carcinoma cases (10/10). The rate of high AHSP expression was significantly higher in PCa than those in ccRCC (69.3% vs. 30.0%, p = 0.001).

The positive expression rate of PSA was 89.1% (90/101) in PCa tissues, showing a trend towards a correlation with AHSP expression (r = 0.181, p = 0.070); specifically, 81.8% (9/11) of PSA-negative tissues expressed AHSP, while 75.0% (6/8) of AHSP-negative tissues expressed PSA, indicating that the two proteins can complement each other. The high expression rate of PSA was 88.1% (89/101). High AHSP expression in PCa was positively correlated with serum PSA levels (r = 0.281, p = 0.020). Comparison of AHSP positivity with serum PSA levels showed that among 13 patients with serum PSA < 10.0 ng/ml, 11 had positive AHSP expression in PCa tissues, suggesting that AHSP may serve as a valuable supplement for tissue diagnosis in cases of serum PSA false negatives.

Urinary ELISA results

ELISA demonstrated that the urinary AHSP level was 0.649 ± 0.055 ng/ml in the PCa group, which was significantly higher than that in the BPH group (0.456 ± 0.058 ng/ml), the urothelial carcinoma group (0.446 ± 0.052 ng/ml), and the ccRCC group (mean 0.438 ± 0.053 ng/ml) (all p < 0.0001) (Table 2). Notably, urine AHSP level exhibited certain specificity in the PCa group (Fig. 3). No significant differences in urine AHSP level were observed among the BPH, urothelial carcinoma, and ccRCC groups (P > 0.05), with the lowest level detected in the ccRCC group. Thus, urinary ELISA confirmed that AHSP was specific in PCa, consistent with the previous proteomic testing results in urine samples of PCa.

Compared to that in BPH patients, urine PSA levels were below the threshold (1.794 pg/ml) in 10 out of 26 PCa patients, resulting in a sensitivity of 61.5% (16/26) and a high false-negative rate (38.5%). Notably, urine AHSP detection demonstrated higher sensitivity than PSA (96.2% vs. 61.5%, p = 0.011) and superior specificity. For BPH patients, AHSP level measurement showed greater advantage; it can serve as a complement to PSA and may even be a better alternative to PSA in urine testing.

Cross-sectional comparison by urinary proteomics

Urinary proteomics performed on 16 patients with urothelial carcinoma, 15 patients with ccRCC, and 5 healthy individuals (serving as controls) showed that differential AHSP was undetectable in the urine samples of all 15 patients with ccRCC, which further confirmed the relative specificity of urinary AHSP for PCa.

Among 16 patients with urothelial carcinoma, 8 showed detectable AHSP intensity (range: 575.032–19,414.400), while AHSP was low in the remaining 8 cases. The difference was not statistically significant when compared with the healthy control group (P > 0.05).

Discussion

AHSP is a highly abundant erythroid-specific protein that functions as a molecular chaperone. It reversibly binds free α-globin and participates in hemoglobin assembly, protecting free α-hemoglobin (αHb) from precipitation in both solution and living cells, and plays a crucial role in regulating αHb stability and HbA assembly [5–8]. Recent studies have revealed that AHSP also exerts unique functions in tumors [11, 13]. Our present study demonstrated that AHSP expression in PCa tissues, as well as its concentration in urine, was significantly higher than in normal controls. Further analysis revealed that AHSP expression levels in PCa tissues were markedly higher than those in other cancer types. Specifically, AHSP levels in the urine samples of PCa patients exceeded those in patients with BPH, urothelial carcinoma, and ccRCC. Notably, AHSP was absent in clear cells but showed prominent expression in eosinophilic cells in ccRCC. These results suggest that AHSP may have potential value in differentiating PCa from other urological malignancies, particularly ccRCC. For suspected PCa cases, AHSP can be utilized for risk stratification to improve the accuracy of initial screening. Thus, AHSP shows potential as a non-invasive urine biomarker for prostate cancer, easing patient discomfort and expanding early screening to facilitate timely treatment and improve cure rates.

Although immunohistochemistry showed some AHSP expression in the urothelial carcinoma group, urine ELISA indicated significantly lower AHSP levels in this group than in the PCa group. Therefore, future diagnostic use of AHSP would not rely solely on its positive/negative status, but rather on interpreting urine AHSP concentration differences in combination with other clinical and pathological indicators.

Studies have shown that AR is a key factor in the development, progression, and prognosis of PCa [14–16]. Our present study showed a significant correlation between AR and AHSP, which further supported the potential of AHSP as a prostate biomarker of targeted therapy. Our results also showed that the expression of AHSP was significantly correlated with ACSM1 [12], and both were related to AR, suggesting that these two factors may have a synergistic effect in PCa.

Moreover, we observed a strong correlation between AHSP and Syn, with both proteins expressed in ganglion cells. Syn is an established biomarker essential for the diagnosis of neuroendocrine tumors [17, 18]. Thus, AHSP may be involved in the neuroendocrine differentiation of PCa and potentially serve as a biomarker for neuroendocrine-type PCa, which suggests a potential therapeutic relevance of AHSP in neuroendocrine PCa, warranting mechanistic studies in this subtype.

Analysis of AHSP expression relative to Gleason scores revealed significant differences among groups. The proportion of high AHSP expression exhibited an overall upward trend with increasing Gleason scores, with the rate in the 8–10 group significantly exceeding that of the 6–7 group; moreover, AHSP expression was associated with PCa differentiation, indicating higher expression in higher-grade tumors.

Moreover, age was a significant risk factor for PCa [19, 20]. Previous studies have demonstrated a strong correlation between PCa incidence and age [21, 22], with PCa being the most common malignancy among men aged 70 years and older [1]. Our study demonstrated that high AHSP expression was significantly more prevalent in older patients, suggesting that elevated AHSP may be associated with advanced age. A recent study has also shown that AHSP gene expression was correlated with prognosis in AML [10]. Accordingly, we investigated the potential relationship between AHSP and PCa prognosis. Although our study demonstrated a correlation between AHSP expression and Gleason score, Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed no significant association between AHSP expression and PCa prognosis (p > 0.05). Therefore, its utility as a prognostic biomarker requires further validation through large-scale studies.

When PSA and AHSP expression results were inconsistent in PCa tissues, AHSP serves as a valuable diagnostic aid, facilitating its clinical translation. AHSP has demonstrated superior sensitivity and specificity. While normal prostate glands are essentially AHSP-negative, positive glandular expression strongly supports a PCa diagnosis. Conversely, PSA is expressed in both normal and cancerous prostate glands—with higher levels in PCa—resulting in lower diagnostic specificity than AHSP. Urine assays indicated that AHSP possessed higher sensitivity than PSA. Furthermore, AHSP effectively compensated for PSA false positives and false negatives, ensuring accurate diagnosis. Finally, serum PSA levels showed a positive correlation with tissue AHSP expression.

The precise mechanisms by which AHSP functions in PCa, particularly its role in promoting tumor cell proliferation, remain to be elucidated. Given the strong correlation between AHSP and AR, future studies should focus on the interaction between AHSP and AR. Integration of AHSP with established biomarkers such as PSA could further optimize patient management.

Research limitations

Our present study had certain limitations. Due to financial constraints, the sample size of urine samples was small, and rare renal malignancy subtypes were not included. Therefore, future large-scale studies are warranted to further validate the potential of AHSP as a diagnostic biomarker for PCa.

Conclusion

Multiple lines of evidence from this study confirmed that AHSP exhibits high expression in PCa with considerable sensitivity and specificity. AHSP expression is significantly positively correlated with AR and Gleason scores. Comparative analyses revealed that urinary AHSP levels were highest in PCa patients compared to ccRCC and urothelial carcinoma, a difference that was statistically significant and diagnostically valuable. Histologically, AHSP showed strong positive expression in PCa tissues but little or no expression in normal tissues, which is expected to enhance the precision of pathological diagnoses. Elevated urine AHSP levels in PCa patients also support its utility in non-invasive diagnostic and screening. Overall, AHSP represents a promising novel biomarker for non-invasive detection of PCa; notably, AHSP demonstrates superior sensitivity to PSA in urine assays.
