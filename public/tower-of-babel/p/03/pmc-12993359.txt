Temporal trends in cervical cancer incidence and mortality in economically emerging countries, 1992–2021: an age-period-cohort analysis
Authors: Changchang Dun, Yuteng Su, Partha Basu, Xuelian Zhao, Shangying Hu, R Muwonge, Youlin Qiao, Fanghui Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe economically emerging countries contributed to over half of the global cervical cancer (CC) burden and are good examples showing how rapid economic growth and health policy change affect the trends of CC burden. This study aimed to analyse the time trends of CC burden across Brazil, Russia, India, China and South Africa (BRICS) and associations with age, period and birth cohort from 1992 to 2021.MethodsData on CC incident cases, deaths, age-standardised incidence rates (ASIRs) and age-standardised mortality rates (ASMRs) were sourced from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 to demonstrate the temporal trends of CC burden for BRICS countries from 1992 to 2021. An age-period-cohort model was used to determine the net drift, local drift, longitudinal age curves, as well as period and cohort relative risks regarding CC incidence and mortality.ResultsBetween 1992 and 2021, the ASIR of CC decreased from 16.5 to 15.6 per 100 000 women (−5.5%) and the ASMR declined from 10.1 to 6.7 per 100 000 women (−33.7%) in BRICS. Brazil reported continuous decreases in both CC incidence (net drift: −1.1%, 95% CI −1.1% to −1.0%) and mortality (net drift: −1.7%, 95% CI −1.80% to −1.6%). India also had reduced CC incidence and mortality but experienced worsening period effects from 2012 onwards. Russia and China experienced decreasing trends in mortality yet increasing trends in incidence, and there were apparent unfavourable trends among young and middle-aged women in Russia. South Africa maintained the highest CC incidence (40.0 per 100 000 women, 95% CI 35.0 to 45.6) and mortality (21.3 per 100 000 women, 95% CI 18.8 to 24.1) across the BRICS and had the steepest growth of CC incidence and mortality rates with increasing age.ConclusionDespite an overall decreasing trend of CC burden in BRICS, substantial heterogeneity exists across nations. Identifying country-specific priority groups and tailoring interventions is essential, and the patterns observed have implications for public health strategies applicable not just to BRICS nations but also to many other emerging economies facing substantial CC burden.

Introduction

Cervical cancer (CC) is the fourth leading cause of cancer incidence and mortality among females globally, resulting in over 660 000 incident cases and over 340 000 deaths in 2022.1 In 2021, the WHO launched a global initiative to eliminate CC as a public health issue, aiming to reduce the incidence to below 4 cases per 100 000 women years.2 CC is a typical example of inequalities in disease with vast disparity in burden by the country’s economic level. So far, significant progress in CC elimination has been observed in Australia, North America and some of the North European countries.3 Emerging countries, featuring rapid economic growth and accounting for over half of the global population, have experienced significant social transitions in the past decades while continuously maintained a comparatively high CC incidence and mortality over time. As a disease amenable to prevention strategies, understanding the time changes in CC burden is crucial for evaluating the ongoing interventions and informing strategy adjustments.

Therefore, in this study, we chose Brazil, Russia, India, China and South Africa (BRICS) as five key representatives for the evaluation of CC burden in emerging countries, given their rapid economic development, demographic scale and increasing role in shaping global health agendas. Previous studies have explored temporal trends of CC incidence and mortality across the entire population or for specific age groups in BRICS countries, yet these studies did not adequately differentiate between cohort and period effects.47 Existing age-period-cohort analyses for BRICS countries, however, have used different data sources, making it difficult to compare and analyse the driving factors for the observed trajectories.812 Hence, the primary objective of this study was to examine the time trends in CC burden across BRICS from 1992 to 2021 based on the Global Burden of Disease, Injuries and Risk Factors Study (GBD) 2021 dataset. The secondary objective was to analyse the driving factors for the observed CC incidence and mortality trajectories using the age-period-cohort (APC) framework to put forward targeted resource allocation and policymaking.

Methods

Data source

The GBD 2021 is an open database providing estimation for the burden of 371 diseases and injuries by age and sex across 204 countries and territories. The methodologies used in GBD 2021 have been detailed in earlier publications.13 14

Time trend analysis

We applied total number of incidences, total number of deaths, age-standardised incidence rates (ASIRs), age-standardised mortality rates (ASMRs), as well as relative proportion to describe the temporal trends of CC in BRICS from 1992 to 2021. Relative proportion in this analysis indicated the proportion of CC cases or deaths among all cancers. North America was chosen as the reference because it represents a high-performing setting with long-standing CC screening and early adoption of human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination, thereby providing a well-established benchmark for assessing progress and gaps in BRICS countries. Furthermore, the time trends in the age distribution of CC incidence and mortality were analysed for five BRICS countries from 1992 to 2021, stratified by six age groups (15–34, 35–44, 45–54, 55–64, 65–74 and 75+years).

APC analysis

We used the APC model to discern the age, period and birth cohort effects on CC incidence and mortality. The APC framework cannot only produce net drift and local drift metrics, but also analyse the independent contribution of age, period and birth cohort effects. Briefly, the net drifts represent the overall annual percentage change in the age-standardised rate over time, which indicates the overall log-linear trend by period and cohort. The local drifts represent the annual percentage change of rate within each age category over time, which indicates the age-specific log-linear trends by period and cohort. The age effects reflect the biological processes of ageing. The period effects refer to differences in exposures to environmental and social risk factors over time affecting all age groups. The cohort effects are related to various experiences of life events shared by each generation.

For APC model input data preparation, we reorganised the incidence and mortality data into 6 consecutive 5-year periods from 1992–1996 to 2017–2021, with 2002–2006 years as the reference period and 14 consecutive 5-year age intervals from 20 to 24 years to 85–89 years (the categories of under 19 years and 90 plus years were ruled out due to the small number of CC incident cases and deaths). 19 consecutive cohorts were accordingly identified based on the collinearity relationship between APC (ie, birth cohort=period age), spanning from those born from 1905–1909 (median, 1907) to 1995–1999 (median, 1997), with the 1950–1954 birth cohort (median, 1952) as the reference group. The selection of reference period and birth cohort is arbitrary and does not influence the interpretation of the results.

Statistical analysis

The net drift (% per year) is expressed as the annual percentage change of incidence or mortality while the local drift (% per year) is expressed as the annual percentage change of age-specific incidence or mortality. The impact of age on CC incidence and mortality was illustrated by fitted longitudinal age curves. Period effects and cohort effects were demonstrated by calculating period relative risks and cohort relative risks, which are derived from the ratios of age-specific rates in relation to the reference period or cohort. There is an identification problem due to the exact linear relationship between the three variables, age, period and birth cohort (age=period-birth cohort), which makes it statistically challenging to estimate the independent effects of age, period and birth cohort.15 To address this problem, we produced the estimable APC parameters and functions by adopting a method that avoids imposing arbitrary constraints on the model, which have been described in detail elsewhere.16 17 Parameter estimation was conducted using the Age-Period-Cohort Web Tool developed by the US National Cancer Institute.17 The statistical significance of these parameters was determined using the Wald χ² test, with a significance level set at 0.05. Data visualisation was performed using R software (V.4.4.0) with packages dplyr (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=dplyr) and ggplot2.18 We reported this study in compliance with the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting.19

Patient and public involvement statement

No patients or the public were involved in the analysis.

Results

Time trends in CC incidence and mortality across BRICS, 1992–2021

Table 1, figure 1 and online supplemental tables 1-6 show population, number of CC incident cases and deaths, ASIRs, ASMRs, as well as net drifts estimated from APC model. Brazil has reported continuous reduction in CC incidence and mortality since 1992, with a net drift of incidence at −1.1% (95% CI −1.1% to −1.0%) per year and a net drift of mortality at −1.7% (95% CI −1.80% to −1.6%) per year. From 1992 to 2021, Russia experienced the most marked increase in ASIR within BRICS, with an estimated 50.0% increase from 12.9 per 100 000 persons in 1992 to 19.4 per 100 000 persons in 2021. India showed the largest reduction in CC burden, with fastest net drift of incidence at −1.3% (95% CI −1.5% to −1.1%) per year and net drift of mortality at −2.0% (95% CI −2.2% to −1.9%) per year, as well as a 42.3% decrease in relative proportion of CC new cases and a 43.7% decrease in relative proportion of CC deaths. China has the lowest ASIR and ASMR of CC among BRICS nations during the study period. The net drift of incidence was 0.9% per year (95% CI 0.8% to 1.0%) and the net drift of mortality was −1.1% (95% CI −1.2% to −1.0%) per year, indicating an uptrend in CC incidence yet a downtrend in mortality from 1992 to 2021 in China. Despite having the least absolute number of CC incident cases (4600 in 1992 and 11900 in 2021) and deaths (2300 in 1992 and 6000 in 2021), South Africa maintained the highest ASIR and ASMR since 1997, the highest relative proportions since 2004 and the fastest annual increase (net drift at 1.6%, 95% CI 1.0% to 2.2%) for CC incidence and mortality (net drift at 1.2%, 95% CI 0.7% to 1.7%).

Time trends in CC incidence and mortality in BRICS across different age groups

Figure 2A,B and online supplemental tables 7 and 8 show the local drifts in CC incidence and mortality across BRICS. In Brazil, local drifts of incidence and mortality were below zero for most age groups, except for women aged 20–24 to 35–39 years groups who experienced increasing trends in CC incidence. In Russia, local drifts of incidence and mortality displayed single-peaked curves, with increasing trends for women aged 20–24 to 50–54 years and peaking at the 35–39 years age group. All age groups in India had local drifts below zero for incidence and mortality, interpreted as a substantial reduction in CC burden during 1992–2021 across all ages. In China, CC incidence demonstrated increasing trends from 20–24 to 75–79 years groups and flat trends from 80–84 to 85–89 years groups, whereas CC mortality showed decreasing trends among all age groups. In South Africa, along with increasing age, the declining trends of incidence and mortality gradually mitigated from 20–24 to 30–34 years groups, and afterward there was an increasing annual percent change in each age group.

Figure 2C,D show the temporal changes in age distribution of CC new cases and deaths across BRICS. For Brazil, the age distribution of CC incident cases across 1992–2021 was relatively stable, but there was a steady shift of CC mortality from the younger stage (15–34 years) to elder stage (>65 years). For Russia, the CC incident cases and deaths gradually transitioned from older women (>65 years) to younger and middle-aged women (15–54 years). For India and China, there was an emerging transition of CC incidence from young-aged women (15–34 years) to middle-aged and elder women (>45 years), and this shift was even more evident for mortality in these two countries. For South Africa, there existed fluctuations in the age distribution mainly as a result of social changes, but there was a gradual transition from younger (15–34 years) women to older women (>65 years) for CC incidence and mortality overall.

APC effects on CC incidence and mortality across BRICS

Age effects displayed similar trends in Brazil, Russia and India, with middle-aged women showing higher incidence than younger and older women. In China, the risk of CC incidence increased gradually with age. South Africa maintained the highest CC incidence among all age groups, and the rates increased markedly among older women. All countries showed similar results for the age effects on CC mortality except for Brazil, in which the mortality risk continued to rise with increasing age (figure 3A,D and online supplemental figures 9 and 12).

For CC incidence, Brazil was the only country showing continuous decrease in period rate ratios after 1997. India presented downward period effects from 1992–1996 to 2007–2011 followed by uptrends from 2012 onwards, indicating a worsened situation in the last 10 years for the population. Russia and China showed adverse trends in period effects overall, indicating a substantial rising risk of CC incidence for these two countries across the study period. In South Africa, the incidence rate ratios rose from the period of 1992–1996 to 2002–2006 and kept steady after 2007. Period effects on CC mortality in all BRICS countries showed reducing trends except for South Africa, which possessed increasing mortality rate ratios from 1992 to 2021 (figure 3B,E and online supplemental tables 10 and 13).

Birth cohort rate ratios of CC incidence in Brazil first presented substantial decline and then showed slight rising risks with a turning point at the 1965–1969 birth cohort. Russia possessed a bimodal cohort effect curve with 1910–1914 birth cohort and 1980–1984 birth cohort population having the two highest incidence risks. Across all birth cohorts within BRICS, India’s curve presented the most notable achievement, indicating a significant decline in CC incidence among females born from 1905 onwards. In comparison, the birth cohort effects continued to rise in China from 1992 onwards. In South Africa, the cohorts born between 1905 and 1974 experienced a rising burden, but successive birth cohorts showed a downward trend in CC incidence. Similar patterns were identified for the birth cohort effects on CC mortality in BRICS countries, except that China had constant decreasing birth cohort effects since the 1905–1909 birth cohort (figure 3C,F and online supplemental tables 11 and 14).

Discussion

Notable differences were found between BRICS countries in both rates and time trends. Brazil achieved remarkable improvements in controlling CC burden. The strong political commitment and a comprehensive healthcare framework established the necessary structural and technical foundations to combat CC in Brazil. Since the 1980s, Brazil reformed its healthcare model by implementing the Unified Health System, which is by far one of the largest and most complex public health systems globally.20 Another contributor to the reduced CC burden might be the Viva Mulher CC control programme launched in 1995, which substantially expanded the Pap smear coverage across Brazil.21 Continuous efforts by the government and Ministry of Health in the recent decade were another reason for Brazil’s distinguishing improvements.22 23 CC screening coverage among women aged 25–64 in Brazil has maintained above 80% since 2006, and the first-dose HPV vaccination rate reached 73.6% for girls and 25.7% for boys in 2019.20 24

Russia had a comparatively high CC incidence and mortality within the WHO European Region, mainly resulting from a historical absence of effective CC control actions.25 There were unfavourable situations in 30–45 years women for incidence and mortality, which implicated a need for Russia to enhance CC diagnosis, treatment and health education for middle-aged females. Evidence suggested that the strong decline in fertility since the early 20th century was responsible for the favourable cohort effects of women born in 1915–1950, as with the number of parities decreasing, the risk for CC also reduced.26 27 The subsequent adverse cohort effects mainly resulted from the post-war increase of HPV infection among young generations during 1960–1990.8 The social upheaval associated with the collapse of the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics in 1991 could be responsible for the lasting unfavourable period effects during 1992–2016. Since 2000, Russia has begun to adopt a series of public health policies to control CC.25 28 However, in the 2021 Russian Ministry of Health guideline, Pap cytology, instead of HPV testing, was still recommended for CC screening and HPV vaccine has not been included in the national immunisation programme (NIP) yet.29 In the face of the rising trends in CC burden, Russia has an urgent need to enhance its CC prevention system, as according to Vaccarella et al, the risk of CC among Russian women could reach levels comparable to those currently observed in some sub-Saharan African countries, if no action is taken.28

India had an increasing total number of CC new cases and deaths during 1992–2021 but the temporal trends in both ASIR and ASMR were encouraging. However, the achievement in CC burden in India was primarily attributed to the changing pattern of risk factors, including later age at marriage and childbearing, declines in multiparity, improved access to healthcare, shrinkage of prostitution and wider use of condoms due to very effective AIDS control measures, rather than the existence of effective CC prevention system.3032 Although the Indian government has initiated a national CC screening policy since 1975, only 2% of women aged 30–49 years have ever undergone screening in the 5 years up to 2019.33 34 Additionally, there was a pause of HPV vaccination pilot projects in India due to unrelated adverse events.35 The confusions created around the stoppage of the HPV vaccination pilots are still influencing the decision by the Ministry of Health to introduce the vaccine into the NIP. This is despite the facts that a new indigenously produced HPV vaccine has been approved by the regulators in India and recommended by the National Technical Advisory Committee on Vaccination.36 Therefore, despite the current favourable incidence and mortality trends, concerns about future trends are reasonable as with national economic growth, India is likely to shift from conservatism to sexual openness, which would result in an increase in CC incidence and mortality, like what has occurred in many other nations.37 The slight uptrends in period effects for CC incidence and mortality in the recent decade in India may also indicate this.

Two events have profoundly influenced the CC disease burden in China in the past 30 years. The first is the reform and opening-up policy in 1979, which has led to substantial social change in China and promoted a shift towards more open sexual attitudes among individuals. This was a possible reason for the increasing period effects on CC incidence after 1997 and the faster rising cohort effects after the 1957 birth cohort. Another important contributing factor is the national ‘Two Cancers (breast cancer and CC) Screening Program’ launched in 2009 to provide free CC screening for women from rural areas (expanded to both urban and rural women in 2019).38 This programme, coupled with the medical advancements in treatment in China over the past few decades, has resulted in a relatively stable trend of CC mortality despite the continuous increases in CC incidence. China is now shifting its focus from sole economic growth to coordinated health-based social development. In 2016, the Chinese government announced the Healthy China 2030 declaration, followed by the Healthy City Innovation Pilot Programmes in 2021 aiming at the elimination of CC through joint efforts of the entire society.39 40 We could observe how strategic prevention measures derived from this programme would affect the future trends of CC in China, which can provide references for policymaking in other similar settings.

The steep increases in CC incidence and mortality observed between 1997 and 2007 in South Africa could be related to the establishment of multi-ethnic democracy in 1994, after which the previously marginalised ethnic groups who contributed the majority of CC burden began to have access to reproductive health resources and receive the CC diagnosis.7 41 Of note, South Africa has the highest number of HIV-infected population worldwide.42 In this study, we found that the previous gradually increasing period effects for CC incidence and mortality were flattened and even reversed after 2007, which was consistent with the nationwide rollout of the antiretroviral therapy programme in 2004.43 The South African government has made continuous efforts in the post-apartheid era (after 1994), including the launch of the national CC screening policy, progression toward racial equity, enhancement in healthcare resources accessibility and promotion of condom use.10 44 45 All of these could have contributed to the continuous enhancement in cohort effects in the successive birth cohorts after 1977. As of now, South Africa has included primary HPV testing into the updated CC prevention policy and has incorporated HPV vaccination into the NIP.46 47 However, facing a huge gap between the current high CC burden and the WHO elimination goal, strong political will and concerted efforts were further required in South Africa.

Overall, findings from the APC model in this study could be linked to each country’s health policy and distinct priorities under its unique national situations. Age effects highlighted the age groups most affected by CC, which could inform the CC screening age range and resource allocation priority. Period effects are typically associated with public health measures and socioeconomic development, such as the conduction of national CC screening programmes, establishment of healthcare system and advancement in CC treatment modalities. Of note, period effects usually lead to an early reduction in CC mortality while there is a delay in CC incidence decline. Cohort effects could offer insights into the influence of exposures or policy shifts affecting specific generation, such as the introduction of HPV vaccination into NIP or abrupt changes in social and health policy. For BRICS, the importance of a coordinated primary healthcare system was highlighted by the Brazilian experience, which served as a reference for other countries. For Russia, the higher risk among middle-aged women indicated the need for strengthened CC control actions among these populations. India needs to focus on the construction of a comprehensive CC prevention system to get prepared to address potential future risks. For China, sustained efforts in implementing high-quality screening and increasing HPV vaccination rates are essential to address the rising incidence of CC. The persistently very high CC burden and high HIV prevalence in South Africa suggest that multisectoral actions and integrated approaches co-managing HPV and HIV infections are warranted.

To the best of our knowledge, this study was the first to comprehensively assess the temporal trends of CC incidence and mortality across the BRICS with the use of GBD data. Another merit was that we used the APC model to examine the shifts in incidence and mortality risks, which could provide deeper insights for CC epidemiology and policymaking for economically emerging countries. However, this study has several limitations. First, this study inherited the limitation of the GBD framework due to insufficient primary data in certain isolated and impoverished regions in BRICS countries. Although GBD methods have the advantage of making informed forecasting when there is a lack of original data, they can also introduce biases. Therefore, we compared GBD 2021 with another most established global cancer database, WHO Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN) 2022, in terms of CC incidence and mortality in BRICS countries, and found only slight differences existed between these two online databases (online supplemental table 15). We also performed a systematic search of PubMed and Embase to identify publications reporting the CC epidemiology based on each BRICS country’s national cancer registry data.468 11 We found that although the numeric from the national cancer registry can never be completely identical with GBD 2021, the overall CC trends were generally consistent, which indicated the robustness of findings from this study. Second, CC incidence and mortality data for women under 20 and over 90 years of age were excluded from this study because there was a small number of incident cases and deaths in these age categories, and as such our analysis cannot help to explain patterns among these groups of women. Finally, we used a 5-year interval to explore the age, period and cohort effects, which was necessary to accommodate the 5-year data format of age in GBD reports, yet some subtle variations might be smoothened.

Conclusion

Findings from our study suggested that with effective mitigation strategies, CC can be largely prevented as exemplified by Brazil. The age-specific, period-specific and cohort-specific estimates have implications not only for BRICS but also for other economically emerging countries with similar health settings. To reach the goal of CC elimination, strengthened public health policies and targeted resource allocation to different priority groups are needed for each country.
