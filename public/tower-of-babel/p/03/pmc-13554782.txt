Valve Deterioration After Supra-Annular Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement
Authors: Ying-Hwa Chen, Hsiao-Huang Chang, Wei-Ting Wang, Hsin-Bang Leu, I-Ming Chen, Su-Man Lin
Published: 2026

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) in patients with small aortic annuli (SAA) has been associated with higher residual transvalvular gradients, increasing the risk of prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM), structural valve deterioration (SVD), and adverse outcomes. In the randomized SMART Trial, supra-annular self-expanding valves (SEVs) have demonstrated hemodynamic advantages over balloon-expandable valves in patients with SAA.1 However, data on mid- to long-term durability across annular sizes—especially in an Asian cohort where smaller annuli are more prevalent2—remain limited. This study evaluated mid-term clinical outcomes, bioprosthetic valve dysfunction, SVD, and bioprosthetic valve failure (BVF) in an Asian cohort undergoing TAVR with SEVs, stratified by annular size.

From May 2010 to December 2023, consecutive patients with symptomatic severe aortic stenosis undergoing TAVR with CoreValve/Evolut platforms in Taiwan were enrolled. Patients were stratified by computed tomography–derived annular diameter into SAA (≤23 mm) and large aortic annulus (LAA; >23 mm). Clinical follow-up included scheduled visits and echocardiography at 1 month, 6 months, 1 year, and annually thereafter. Valve-related outcomes were defined according to Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 criteria.3 SVD and BVF were assessed using standardized definitions, and only patients with at least 2 postprocedural echocardiograms were included. A competing risk analysis was conducted using the Fine-Gray method to estimate the probability of SVD in the event of death that would impede the development of SVD. Predictors of SVD were identified using a multivariable analysis, with significance set at P < 0.05.

Among 370 patients (excluding valve-in-valve and redo-TAVR), 191 (51.6%) had SAA and 179 (48.4%) had LAA. Mean age was 81 years, 57% were female, and the median Society of Thoracic Surgeons score was 6.4% (IQR: 3.6-9.7). Bicuspid valves were present in 12.2%. Patients with SAA were more often female, had smaller body surface area, and had fewer comorbidities. Procedural characteristics were comparable, with predominantly transfemoral access under conscious sedation.

At 30 days, mean pressure gradients (MPGs) (7.6 ± 3.9 vs 8.0 ± 3.9 mm Hg), indexed effective orifice area (1.11 ± 0.25 vs 1.09 ± 0.24 cm2), and rates of moderate/severe PPM (16.2% vs 18.0%) were comparable between SAA and LAA groups. An elevated postprocedural MPG ≥20 mm Hg was rare (1.1%) and did not differ between groups.

Over a median follow-up of 3.5 years (IQR: 1.8-5.9 years; maximum: 13 years), 168 patients (45.4%) died, with 57 (33.9%) from cardiovascular causes. Kaplan-Meier estimates of all-cause mortality at 1, 3, 5, and 7 years were 10.9% (95% CI: 7.6-14.0), 26.5% (95% CI: 21.6-31.1), 40.3% (95% CI: 34.2-45.8), and 55.8% (95% CI: 48.7-61.9), respectively. There were no significant differences in all-cause or cardiovascular mortality between SAA and LAA groups.

Bioprosthetic valve dysfunction occurred in 80 patients (20.9% SAA vs 22.3% LAA), predominantly due to nonstructural dysfunction such as PPM. Valve thrombosis occurred in 2 patients within the SAA cohort and infective endocarditis in 2 patients within the LAA cohort, each representing a rare incidence of 0.5%. Moderate and severe SVD occurred in 4 (1.1%) and 10 (2.7%) patients, respectively. The exposure-adjusted SVD incidence was 1.0 per 100 patient-years, with similar rates of moderate (0.3 per 100 patient-years) and severe SVD (0.6 vs 0.8; P = 0.754) in SAA and LAA groups. BVF incidence was 0.8 per 100 patient-years, also comparable between groups (0.6 vs 1.0; P = 0.549). Aortic reintervention was required in 6 patients (1.6%) (1% vs 2.2%; P = 0.942), at a median of 46 days (IQR: 21-251) after diagnosis of severe SVD or endocarditis. Mortality did not differ between patients with and without SVD.

SVD was associated with younger age, bicuspid valves, higher 30-day postprocedural gradients (11.9 ± 5.8 mm Hg vs 7.6 ± 3.7 mm Hg; P < 0.001), and a higher prevalence of MPG ≥20 mm Hg (7.1% vs 0.8%; P = 0.002). On multivariable analysis, higher post-TAVR MPG (subdistribution hazard ratio: 1.18; 95% CI: 1.05-1.34; P = 0.008) and younger age (subdistribution hazard ratio: 0.92; 95% CI: 0.86-0.98; P = 0.014) independently predicted SVD, whereas annular size did not. A postprocedural MPG >12.9 mm Hg was identified as the optimal threshold for predicting SVD (Figure 1B).

This study demonstrates that supra-annular SEVs provide favorable mid-term durability in an Asian cohort, with low rates of SVD, BVF, and reintervention. Notably, outcomes were comparable between patients with small and large annuli, suggesting that SEVs may mitigate the adverse hemodynamic profile traditionally associated with SAA.

Prior studies consistently implicate suboptimal post-TAVR hemodynamics as a principal determinant of long-term outcomes. Large registries demonstrate that PPM4 and elevated postprocedural gradients5—rather than annular size—independently predict SVD, with balloon-expandable valves and smaller prostheses4,6 conferring higher risk. Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of optimizing postprocedural valve hemodynamics to enhance durability.

In contrast to prior reports,5,7 no survival difference was observed between patients with and without SVD, possibly reflecting systematic echocardiographic surveillance and timely reintervention.

Several limitations warrant consideration. This single-center observational study included a limited number of SVD events, reducing statistical power. SVD incidence may be underestimated due to deaths of unknown cause during the COVID-19 period. The nonrandomized design also introduces potential selection bias.

In conclusion, in this Asian cohort undergoing TAVR with supra-annular SEVs, mid-term valve durability was favorable and comparable between small and large annuli. Postprocedural transvalvular gradient—rather than annular size—was the principal determinant of SVD. Early detection and timely reintervention may mitigate the adverse impact of SVD on survival.
