Bitter hop nutraceutical stimulates fat mass loss while preserving muscle mass in a cohort of individuals with overweight or obesity receiving diet and exercise advice
Authors: Edward Walker, Kim Lo, Isabel Smirk, Marry Rose Spence, Hannah Dinnan, Sheridan Martell, Dominic Lomiwes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiet and exercise advice are routinely the primary interventions given for weight loss; however, frequently result in poor weight loss outcomes. While modern pharmaceuticals are highly effective, there exists a need for a non-pharmaceutical adjunct therapy for lifestyle change-mediated weight loss. Recently, the gastrointestinal delivery of a bitter hop-based nutraceutical has been shown to stimulate increases in glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and cholecystokinin (CCK). Our aim was to determine if this same bitter hop nutraceutical is an effective adjunct therapy during prescribed weight loss programs.MethodsThis was a randomized, double-blind, parallel treatment study. One hundred and fifty adult males and females were recruited and required to undertake a 24-week weight loss program consisting of monthly lifestyle advice group seminars. Daily treatments of either placebo or 500 mg of a bitter hop extract (BHE) were given to the participants, who were instructed to take half the daily dose at least 1 h before the two major meals of the day. Body weight, bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) of body composition, and subjective appetite and craving measures were taken every four weeks.ResultsOne hundred and twenty-eight participants completed the program. The BHE treatment group exhibited a greater reduction in weight (3.8%) and fat mass (3.9 kg) after 24 weeks when compared to placebo control (p < 0.05), and a total reduction of 4.3% body weight and 4.5 kg fat mass when compared to baseline values (p < 0.05). There was a proportionally similar reduction in BIA-estimated visceral fat area in the BHE treatment group relative to the placebo control (p < 0.05), and neither group exhibited any loss in BIA-estimated skeletal muscle mass.ConclusionThese data suggest that appetite suppressing nutraceuticals may be a useful adjunct therapy for individuals receiving lifestyle change advice for the purpose of weight loss. Introduction

Nutraceutical and dietary supplement use during weight loss efforts is highly prevalent, with studies suggesting 15% of people have incorporated them into their weight loss routines at least once [1]. The wide use of such supplements is in part driven by the broad spectrum of weight loss-associated bioactivity claimed, ranging from the enhancement of fat loss by stimulating metabolism [2], reduction in food intake via the suppression of appetite [3], and inhibition of fat storage by increased lipolysis and decreased adipocyte differentiation [4], in addition to the reduction of muscle loss during caloric deficit and suppression of food craving experience during dietary change [5]. While potentially a useful adjunct therapy, the assessment of nutraceutical effectiveness is often challenging due to limited information on mode(s) of action, a lack of placebo controlled clinical research, the use of proprietary formulations that lack ingredient standardization, and minimal data supporting use cases and protocols in a medical setting [6]. Despite these challenges, nutraceutical formulations have potential as an effective adjunct therapy alongside diet and exercise for those either unsuitable, unwilling, or unable to take glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) based weight loss medication.

The importance of effective non-pharmaceutical interventions has become increasingly apparent during the age of GLP-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1 RA) therapies. While highly effective for weight loss, the long-term use of GLP-1 RAs presents several challenges and concerns [7]. GLP-1 RA intervention is associated with significant muscle mass loss that may have unfavorable metabolic effects [8], weight loss from GLP-1 RA treatments is only sustained while treatment continues [9], and GLP-1 RA treatment induces downregulation of endogenous anorexigenic hormones that may facilitate post treatment weight regain [10]. Nutraceutical interventions that can address these issues by acting either as an alternative to GLP-1 RA treatment for weight loss, a post therapy cycle to prevent weight regain, or as an endogenous GLP-1 stimulator to reverse the suppression of endogenous GLP-1 levels, may prove useful as part of an integrated therapeutic approach. Indeed, a growing number of products reports to be GLP-1 activators, “nature's Ozempic,” or aim to support GLP-1 weight loss programs. These products include the plant alkaloid berberine [11], gut targeted pre- and probiotics such as Akkermansia muciniphila [12,13], and more general nutritional support such as protein-type supplements [14], with each having a different mode of action and different levels of clinical support for their reputed bioactivity. One promising nutraceutical formulation that may simulate weight loss and regulate endogenous GLP-1 levels is based on extracts from the flowers of the Humulus lupulus plant, commonly known as bitter hops.

Hops, the bittering component in beer, contains highly bioactive compounds that have shown promise as potential weight loss inducing agents. Various forms of hop extracts have been assessed for weight loss-related bioactivity, including isomerized forms found in beer [15], oxidized forms found in old hops [16], and non-isomerized non-oxidized forms found in raw hops before they undergo the brewing process [17]. Isomerized hop extracts have shown directed weight loss potential in people via activation of fatty acid oxidation and reducing visceral fat mass [18], whereas unmodified hop acids have acute effects on eating behavior by decreasing appetite [3,17,19].

Amarasate® is a gut-targeted nutraceutical formulation rich in unmodified hop bitter acids and is designed to trigger the ‘Bitter Brake® appetite suppression mechanism, whereby a high dose of intensely bitter compounds is delivered directly to the small intestine and trigger gut enteroendocrine cells to release appetite-suppressing hormones such as cholecystokinin (CCK), GLP-1, and peptide tyrosine tyrosine (PYY) [3,19]. This ‘bitter brake’ mechanism has also been shown to reduce hunger and cravings during acute fasting and to decrease voluntary food intake [3,17]. Given its potent short-term weight loss-associated bioactivity, this nutraceutical may be useful as an adjunct therapy to lifestyle modifications. By combining this hop-acid rich nutraceutical with diet and exercise, it may be possible to aid and maintain weight loss in individuals with obesity who are otherwise resistant to conventional lifestyle modification.

Here we assess the bitter hop-based extract (BHE) Amarasate for its capacity to aid in weight loss in participants receiving group monthly lifestyle advice, and report on changes in body weight, changes in estimates of body composition, and changes in subjective measures of appetite and food craving.

Materials and methods

Participants

One hundred and fifty males and females (eligibility: age 18–45 y, BMI 25–35 kg/m2) were recruited through poster and electronic advertisement in Auckland, New Zealand. The exclusion criteria were: current participation in a weight loss program or use of weight loss medication (including GLP-1 RA based treatments); diagnosed diseases of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract; previous GI surgery; undergoing food restriction or any condition that may alter body composition within a short period of time; recent weight loss/gain (5 kg within the last 6 months); use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or current glucocorticoid use; history of ischemic gut, diabetes, celiac disease; history of alcohol or drug abuse; any known medical conditions/medications that may affect the gut or appetite; known allergy, intolerance, or sensitivity to any ingredients in the study product; or inconsistent menstrual cycle such as presents during perimenopause. Participants had their medical history taken by researchers to determine compliance with study criteria and were required to report any diagnosed history of eating disorders to research staff. Participants were also required to be non-smokers, ascertained healthy by self-report. Participants were tested for diabetes as assessed by HbA1c blood measures and excluded if diabetic. All subjects provided written informed consent prior to the clinical trial and were allowed to withdraw at any time for any reason.

The clinical research protocol and related documents were approved by the New Zealand Health and Disability Ethics Committee (HDEC) (2024 EXP 18664). All studies were carried out in accordance with the requirements set out in the Declaration of Helsinki and the ethical guidelines required by the HDEC, the trial was registered at the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ANZCTR- ACTRN12625001011471), and was conducted at The New Zealand Institute for Plant and Food Research Limited (now The New Zealand Institute for Bioeconomy Science Limited), Auckland, New Zealand.

Study design, supplements and protocol

The study design was a randomized, double-blind, parallel, 6-month (24 week) weight loss study to assess the effect of a BHE on weight loss achieved. Full treatment dose of either 500 mg daily dose of the Amarasate bitter hop flower extract suspended in a canola oil excipient or a matched placebo control, which were protected from gastric acid digestion by encapsulation using a hydroxypropyl methylcellulose delayed release capsule (DRcaps®, Capsugel, Morristown NJ, USA), and divided into four capsules for each daily dose, were given using a 3 week dose escalation protocol of increasing the dose by 125 mg per week up the maximum dose of 500 mg. Compliance was determined by monthly assessment of remaining treatment capsules and self-reported by participants. The Amarasate BHE was prepared using a food safe supercritical CO2 extraction process from a specific cultivar of Humulus lupulus. All treatment groups were instructed to take half of the total daily dose at least 1 h before the two main meals of the day and on a relatively empty stomach. Participants were required to attend study visits every 4 weeks where they had their body weight and composition measures taken, had questions related to the treatment protocol answered, and went to a dietician-led 1-h group seminar giving general lifestyle advice (exercise and diet) to support weight loss. This advice included a recommendation for protein consumption at every meal, limiting of unhealthy snack foods, consuming nutrient dense foods, reducing energy dense foods, and undertaking daily exercise. Study visit occurred during weekday evenings, and participants were requested not to eat for 4 h before the visit, not to consume large quantities of liquid during or immediately before the visit, have an empty bladder during testing, and not to undertake intense exercise the day of the study visit to ensure accuracy of study measures. While attending study visits, participants were required to complete various study documents, including the short form of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) based assessments for appetite, food cravings, and side effects, and were asked if they believed they experienced any treatment related side effects. Additionally, before the intervention began, participants were given a seminar explaining how to correctly use the test capsules, what side effects may occur and suggested a course of action if they did. Randomization was conducted using a sequence generated by an online random treatment allocation application www.GraphPad.com. Weight and estimates of body composition

Body weight was determined using a calibrated AND HW-220KGL digital commercial scale (A&D HOLON Holdings Company, Limited. Tokyo, JAPAN), and height was measured using a wall mounted pull down tape measure. Height and body weight measures were taken by study researchers at the initial in person screening visit and then remeasured at the baseline visit if the participant was enrolled in the study, with further weight measures being taken every 4 weeks during study visits. Weight measures were taken with shoes and overgarments (coats, jumpers, sweaters, jackets etc.) removed and presented as change from baseline values. Estimates of body composition were assessed by researchers at study visits every 4 weeks by bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) using an InBody S10 6 frequency 8 electrode bioelectrical impedance body composition analyzer (InBody, Gangnam-gu, Seoul, 06106 Korea). Body composition measures tracking trending changes in estimates of total body fat, total muscle mass, and visceral fat area are presented as change from baseline in order to monitor composition changes occurring during the intervention period.

Activity and food intake assessments

Participant activity level was evaluated using the short form IPAQ questionnaire. This questionnaire was presented to participants every four weeks during their study visits and assesses their average activity level over the last 7 days. Food intake was determined using a participant filled in 4 day food diary (3 week days and 1 weekend day), that was completed at baseline before any intervention started, at the mid-point of the study (week 12), and at the end of the study immediately before treatment stopped (24 weeks), and reviewed by research staff with the participant present to improve accuracy of the reporting. Energy intake was determined by cross referencing dietary intake with the New Zealand Food Composition Database (foodcomposition.co.nz).

Subjective assessment of appetite, food cravings, and side effects

Subjective assessments of appetite, food cravings, and gastrointestinal side effects were assessed using VAS based questionnaires. These questionnaires were presented to participants every four weeks at their study visits and assessed average subjective feelings over the previous 7 days. These recall based VAS assessments have previously been validated to be accurate representations of daily appetite changes [7].

Safety parameters

Venous blood samples were taken from participants within 1 week prior to starting the trial and then again during the last week of the 24-week intervention period. Participant blood samples were tested at the local diagnostic lab (Awanui Labs, Auckland, New Zealand), for a variety of blood safety makers including: renal function tests for sodium, potassium, calcium, chloride, urea, and creatinine; liver function tests of bilirubin, alkaline. phosphatase, alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), protein, albumin, globulin.

Statistical methods

The study sample size to adequately power the clinical trial was calculated using published data from previous 24-week nutraceutical weight‐loss interventions using body weight loss as the dependent variable. Power estimates showed 50 participants per group were sufficient to detect a difference in the primary outcome measure of body weight, based on a power level of 0.8 and a dropout rate of 20%. This estimate, combined with regulatory requirements to meet health claim standards in the Australian region led to a recruitment target of 75 participants per treatment group for a total of 150 participants.

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 11 and results expressed as means ± SEM for each of the intervention groups. Body weight, estimates of body composition, and VAS based assessments of subjective feelings were assessed using mixed model effects. Residual plots were inspected to confirm that the model assumptions of normality and constant variance were met. Where a treatment by time interaction was evident, a subsequent pairwise comparison was conducted using Tukey's post-hoc testing to determine any significant differences for weight, body composition measures, and VAS ratings between the two intervention groups and from baseline measures within an intervention group. Statistical significance for all parameters was set at p < 0.05 with a significant difference between groups denoted by ∗ = p < 0.05 and within groups relative to the week 0 baseline values denoted by † = p < 0.05 (version 11; GraphPad Software).

Results

Participants and compliance

One hundred and fifty participants were randomized and 128 participants completed the weight loss trial protocol and were included in the final analysis. Of the 22 participants that did not complete the study, 12 were on the placebo treatment and 10 were on the BHE treatment. Two participants, one from each group, withdrew from the study due to side effects from the treatment capsule. Five participants from the BHE treatment and one from the placebo treatment took only half the prescribed dose of the intervention for the majority of the study due to treatment tolerability. One additional participant in the BHE treatment group took only one quarter of the prescribed dose for most of the study due to treatment tolerability. Of the six participants in the BHE treatment group that did not take the full treatment dose, three reported diarrhea only, two reported diarrhea and nausea, and one reported only nausea. The participants who withdrew from the study due to side effects reported diarrhea and malaise for the BHE and the placebo treatment, respectively.

Visceral fat area

Participants were monitored for changes in estimates of visceral fat area by BIA, which is presented as difference from baseline values (Δ) and tracked as a trend over time. The two treatment groups exhibited different profiles for estimates of visceral fat loss that closely aligned with the respective changes in overall body fat. Visceral fat loss in the placebo group reached a maximum of 14 cm2 at the 12-week timepoint before returning to an overall loss of 9 cm2 at 24 weeks as per BIA. Whereas, the mean change (Δ) in visceral fat area for the BHE treated group was 33 cm2 at the 24-week time point, with a relatively consistent rate of loss over the entire treatment period, and equating to a 24 cm2 visceral fat loss for the BHE relative to the placebo control at the end of the intervention period, as per BIA. Gastrointestinal side effects

Mean (Δ) in VAS for GI side effects of nausea, urge to vomit, bloating, cramp or discomfort, and heartburn are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. Subjective VAS ratings of GI side effects did not show evidence for a treatment by time interaction in any of the conditions evaluated, however, acute changes were notable at the week 4 time point. An acute increase in nausea and cramp or discomfort ratings occurred at the 4-week time point for the BHE treatment group that then decreased towards baseline values at week 8 (supplementary Fig. 1 a & d). When compared to the response to placebo treatment, the week 4 changes in cramp or discomfort were the greatest in magnitude, with a smaller difference observed in the nausea ratings.

Energy intake and exercise assessments

Energy intake was as assessed by analysis of 4-day diet diaries taken at baseline (week 0), at the study mid-point (week 12), and at the end of the study (week 24). When compared to baseline values, the BHE treatment group decreased their daily energy intake by 748kj and 545kj more than the placebo treatment group at the 12- and 24-week time points respectively (p < 0.05). Assessment of physical activity was determined using the short form IPAQ questionnaire. There was no overall evidence for a treatment by time interaction, however, physical activity levels did increase to a peak at the 8-week time point in both the BHE and the placebo treatment groups (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Discussion

Nutraceutical and supplement usage during diets is commonplace and often recommended as an adjunct therapy during managed weight loss interventions [1]. However, as data supporting their efficacy for enhancing or maintaining weight loss are often lacking, it is uncertain to what degree each of these dietary interventions help [6,20]. This current study shows for the first time that a bitter hop-based nutraceutical product can aid in weight and fat mass reduction achieved during a weight loss program, and that this weight loss is achieved without reduction in muscle mass, as per BIA.

The overall body weight loss in the placebo group was in line with expectations given the limited degree of intervention offered, with only a monthly 1-h group seminar consisting of general diet and exercise advice given. Interestingly, the focus of the lifestyle advice, including protein at every meal (including snacks), limiting unhealthy snacks, and increasing exercise, showed notable efficacy for total fat loss over that of muscle mass in the placebo group. It is possible that the lifestyle advice, while brief, may have indicated a significant degree of behavioral change for the participants, and although beneficial, was too difficult for many participants to adhere to for the entire duration of the study. Further to this, BIA showed both groups having a similar profile of change in body composition during the first 12 weeks, although a greater level of absolute weight loss was more common in individuals treated with BHE. The continued improvements in body composition in the BHE treated group during the last 12 weeks of the intervention, may have been due to improved compliance with the suggested lifestyle changes. Indeed, the consistent and favorable reductions in hunger and food cravings observed in the BHE group across the entire trial period, would likely be associated with better capacity to comply with dietary change and may in part explain the greater reduction in energy intake and the greater weight loss reported. The changes in subjective hunger and craving measures observed in the BHE treatment group are unlikely to be associated with treatment induced side effects, as the side effects were small in magnitude and only present at the week 4 time point, whereas the reduction in hunger and cravings ratings is consistent throughout the study.

The weight and BIA determined body composition outcomes reported for the BHE treated group would be considered at the high end of what has previously been reported for nutraceutical interventions, with clinical research assessing other noteworthy weight loss supplements and gut targeted interventions typically showing at best moderate efficacy. Berberine is a benzylisoquinoline alkaloid that is often used as a weight loss aid and primarily acts as a metabolic regulator that triggers AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activity that mimics metformin's mode of action [21]. Berberine supplementation is shown to reduce body weight by an average weight loss of 2.1 kg in a recent meta-analysis [22], although data from an individual study in that analysis indicated weight loss similar to what is reported here [23]. Other supplements that target the gut for weight loss, such as prebiotics and probiotics, show limited weight loss potential. A meta-analysis of probiotics showed consistent yet limited weight loss (averaging under 1 kg), combined with a tendency to favor the loss of body fat over that of muscle mass. Interestingly, probiotics have been linked to increases in gut PYY levels that may offer muscle mass protection [24], suggesting some limited mode of action crossover between them and the BHE tested here. Treatment of individuals with obesity with the probiotic Akkermansia muciniphila, a mucin degrading gut bacterium that stimulates GLP-1 secretion in vitro and in animal models, has shown slightly greater absolute weight loss of 1.7 kg after 12 weeks of supplementation in a small proof of concept trial [25]. This same trial also reported 1.5 kg of weight loss was fat, again supporting fat mass loss in gut interventions that stimulate endogenous peptide hormone release. Prebiotic supplementation also results in similar small, yet consistent, changes in body weight (again averaging less than 1 kg) and, like prebiotics, is likely more suitable for longer-term use rather than for a targeted weight management program.

The peak body weight loss in the BHE group of just over 4% (3.8 kg) during the 24-week intervention compares favorably with some pharmaceutical based treatments. Real-world evaluations of weight loss achieved by pharmaceutical treatments show weight loss ranging from a few percent to in excess of 10% over a similar duration depending on the study and drug being examined. A meta-analysis of previous-generation weight loss medications reports approximate weight loss of 3% for the lipase inhibitor orlistat and 5% for both the serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor sibutramine and the cannabinoid receptor inverse agonist rimonabant, placing the results reported here in line with these pharmaceutical interventions [26]. Current weight loss medications based on stabilized GLP-1 exhibit greater weight loss potential, with the GLP-1 RA liraglutide achieving over 5% weight loss in real world applications [27], increasing to 10% for similar assessments evaluating semaglutide [28]. The dual GIP/GLP-1 RA tirzepatide achieves even greater weight loss [29]. While the overall magnitude of weight loss is notably greater for real world use of semaglutide when compared to the BHE tested here, it is noteworthy that many studies report a significant loss in lean mass [30]. Additionally, semaglutide-induced weight loss is rapidly regained once treatment ceases [31], a phenomenon that may relate to the inhibitory effect GLP-1 RAs have on endogenous GLP-1 production [10]. For individuals who require either less weight loss or a maintenance of muscle mass, a nutraceutical product, such as the one tested here, may be a viable treatment option.

Amarasate, the specific BHE present in the test capsules, has been shown to exhibit appetite-suppressing activity by increasing blood concentrations of the anorexigenic gut peptides GLP-1, CCK and PYY [19]. These peptide hormones are released during intestinal exposure to bitterness, and in addition to inducing satiation and enhancing postprandial satiety, they may also have a role in reducing food cravings [32,33]. Interestingly, a growing body of research supports these same gut peptide hormones having bioactivity that may alter hedonic responses to food and modify post-prandial homeostatic regulation of metabolism. GLP-1 is a target for reward system-related disorders, such as those present during overeating, highlighting a potential role in food addiction [33]. CCK is stimulated by consumption of dietary fat as well as biasing metabolism towards fat oxidation, suggesting a role in fat mass loss [34,35]. PYY has been associated with activation of muscle stem cells, and its plasma level predicts responses to exercise, indicating possible muscle sparing effects [24,36]. When combined, these bioactivities suggest that changes in gut hormones may be in part responsible for the preferential loss of fat mass observed here and suggest the importance of gut peptide signaling during weight loss. Interestingly, previous work examining hop bitter acid oxidation products showed targeted abdominal fat loss occurring within a similar time frame to what is reported here, along with a small but significant change in body weight that occurred with no reported muscle mass loss [16]. The authors of this previous work put forward PPARα (Peroxisome Proliferator-Activated Receptor gamma) activation as the putative mode of action for this fat loss, and it is possible that the BHE treatment given in this study was also able to directly trigger abdominal fat oxidation by this same mode of action. Although, given relative greater bias towards overall fat mass loss as reported by BIA in this study, combined with the gut targeting of the intervention, and other known fat-related bioactivity, it is also possible that the visceral fat mass loss is unrelated to any possible PPARα activation. Interestingly, the putative mode of action triggered by the BHE tested here, activation of gastrointestinal bitter taste receptors and subsequent release of peptide hormones, is of increasing interest in the pharmaceutical space. Indeed, a synthetic bitter agonist was shown to induce significant weight loss in rodents [37], and in a recently published study, another synthetic bitter compound was shown to selectively reduce fat mass in rodents and led to weight loss in a small-scale pilot clinical trial [38].

Limitations

This study has several limitations regarding weight management that were made for both practical and safety reasons. Firstly, for safety reasons, we recruited only individuals with overweight and obesity who had no obesity-associated comorbidities. The decision to recruit only healthy individuals with overweight or obesity limits the extension of these results to weight loss in those with comorbidities, meaning that additional follow up research should be done to ensure this intervention is suitable for specific at-risk groups. Further to this, individual participants were defined as healthy by self-report, required to be non-smokers, and only screened for diabetes (HbA1c assessment). It is likely that undiagnosed/unreported concurrent obesity related illnesses were present in the study population, and that these may have affected results observed in participants.

Ethical review and cultural consultation placed a requirement for same culture, same gender staff to be available to undertake hip/waist circumference measurements. Given the culturally diverse local population, the investigators could not guarantee this would be possible and these useful measures were therefore not taken during the study. Additionally, the recruitment for this study was targeted towards individuals who are overweight due to limited appetite control, and results of this study may be more relevant to those that overeat due to homeostatic hunger cues, and potentially less relevant to those who experience conditions such as emotional eating. This study also limited participants ages to between 18 and 45 to reduce the risk of inadvertent inclusion of females undergoing perimenopause and included an over representation of female participants due to no bias being placed on the biological sex of recruited participants, a common occurrence in weight loss trials [39]. Given the lower number of males in the study, there may be a degree of gender bias present in the results, and it may be worthwhile conducting a future weight loss study in a male population. It would additionally be worth conducting a study targeted towards post-menopausal females, to confirm the results presented here also apply to that important group.

Body composition data was collected during study visits using professional grade BIA, and while this allowed standardization to best practice pretest conditions and a high frequency of testing, this assessment methodology reports poor correlation with dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA) for segmental analysis (arms, legs), and for absolute fat mass and absolute lean mass values when pretest conditions are not controlled [[40], [41], [42]]. The specific BIA technology used here does however show high levels of correlation with DXA when tracking trends in whole-body fat and lean mass changes over time [42], and for overall fat and overall lean mass when pretest conditions are controlled [43]. Given these limitations, body composition data was presented as change from baseline, and only estimates of whole-body fat mass, and whole-body muscle mass, along with visceral fat area were reported. In future studies, it would be useful to conduct additional DXA and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) body composition analysis to confirm the results present here and to allow for accurate determination of segmental muscle and fat changes.

The energy intake data reported here was obtained from 4-day diet diaries, and while commonly used, these assessments are prone to underreporting, potential bias, and should be considered estimates of energy intake.

Finally, this is a relatively small trial for a weight loss outcome, and as there were participants lost to follow up in both groups, we cannot exclude the possibility that these individuals had less favorable changes in weight and body composition. We suggest conducting a confirmatory clinical trial with a larger number of study participants, possibly in a real-world clinical setting that follows participants for an extended period of time. These points should be addressed in future work.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we determined the efficacy of a BHE to regulate weight loss achieved during a 24-week weight loss program. The results presented here support the effectiveness of this BHE in aiding weight loss achieved when dieting as part of a lifestyle advice approach. These findings contribute to a growing body of work supporting the use of BHEs for weight management, further supporting this BHE as an adjunct therapy during weight loss interventions, and additionally shows that longer term BHE treatment results in decreased hunger and food cravings that are maintained for the duration of the treatment and that are unlikely to be related to any GI side effects experienced.
