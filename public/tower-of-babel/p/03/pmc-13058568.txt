The Effect of Single and Double Shift Works on Salivary Cortisol Levels in Nurses
Authors: Fadime Ulupınar, Sibel Meler, Engin Şebin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThis study aimed to elucidate the effects of single versus double‐shift work schedules on salivary cortisol concentrations among nurses.DesignThis research was designed as a comparative descriptive study utilizing repeated measures to assess changes over time and shift types.MethodsA total of fifty‐two female nurses, working in rotating shifts, participated in the study. Saliva cortisol samples were collected from all participants before, after, and at midnight for both a single and a double shift. To assess interactions between time (07:30–08:30, 15:30–16:30, 23:00–24:00) and shift type (single and double), a repeated‐measures two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used.ResultsAnalyses encompassing both time and shift type demonstrated significant main effects for both variables. Salivary cortisol concentrations were maximal between 07:30 and 08:30, while the trough levels were recorded at midnight. Inspection of the main effect associated with shift type revealed that double‐shift workers exhibited higher mean cortisol concentrations. Specifically, cortisol levels were nearly two‐fold higher at midnight in double‐shift workers compared to single‐shift workers. Additionally, a significant interaction effect between time and shift type was found.ConclusionThe pronounced effects of double shifts, as evidenced by significant changes in cortisol levels, warrant urgent consideration by healthcare policymakers and administrators, balancing operational efficiency with nurses' well‐being and patient safety.Patient or Public ContributionNo patient or public contribution.

Introduction

Despite acknowledging that nurses assigned to nocturnal shifts are frequently exposed to adverse health impacts, the necessity for uninterrupted patient care remains a non‐negotiable aspect of nursing practice (Bani Issa et al. 2020; Lin et al. 2014; Yuan and Fang 2024). A multitude of studies have rigorously explored the adverse effects of nocturnal shift work and extended duty periods on nurses' health status and occupational performance (Dall'Ora et al. 2016; Matheson et al. 2014; Tahghighi et al. 2017). In many healthcare systems, nurses are often required to undertake extended shift duties, which may span over long hours and frequently extend into the night, with rest or sleep often being restricted during these periods. This scenario exposes nurses to the combined challenges of nocturnal shifts and prolonged working hours. Nurses involved in shift work may be deployed to either steady or rotational schedules within the same week, potentially resulting in an incongruity between biological rhythms and social patterns (Shao et al. 2010; Yuan and Fang 2024). Fluctuations in the circadian rhythm of hormonal markers such as cortisol and melatonin offer a physiological paradigm for assessing the perturbations instigated by single and double‐shift work engagements (Bani Issa et al. 2020; Grosser et al. 2022).

Cortisol, often referred to as the “stress hormone,” is a key regulatory hormone that exhibits a predictable diurnal pattern in the human body (Kirschbaum and Hellhammer 1994; Korompeli et al. 2009). In a healthy sleep–wake cycle, cortisol levels typically peak in the early morning hours, preparing the body for the day's challenges by increasing alertness and energy levels, and gradually decline throughout the day, reaching their lowest point around midnight (Björntorp and Rosmond 2000; Metzenthin et al. 2009; Zhao et al. 2020). This circadian rhythm of cortisol is controlled by the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and plays a vital role in a variety of biological functions, including metabolism regulation, immune function, and stress response (Hellhammer et al. 2009; McGregor et al. 2016). However, disruptions to the normal sleep routine, such as those experienced by night shift workers, including nurses, can significantly alter this rhythm (Sladek et al. 2019). Exposure to stress and light during the night can lead to elevated cortisol levels, contributing to sleep disturbances and reduced sleep quality (Björntorp and Rosmond 2000; Grosser et al. 2022).

Salivary cortisol has been widely used in the literature as a non‐invasive biomarker to assess stress‐related responses and circadian rhythm alterations, particularly in nurses and other shift‐working populations (Inder et al. 2012; Lee et al. 2024). Previous studies have demonstrated that salivary cortisol reliably reflects diurnal cortisol variation and is sensitive to work‐related stress, sleep disruption, and circadian misalignment (Grosser et al. 2022; Kirschbaum and Hellhammer 1994; Korompeli et al. 2009). Owing to these characteristics, salivary cortisol has been extensively applied in occupational and healthcare research to capture physiological stress responses under real‐world working conditions, especially in studies requiring repeated measurements across different time points and work schedules.

Disruptions to the circadian rhythm, such as those caused by night shifts and extended working hours, can lead to several significant health issues in nurses (Bani Issa et al. 2020; Dall'Ora et al. 2016; Lin et al. 2014). A disturbed circadian rhythm is characterized by difficulty falling asleep, frequent sleep disruptions, excessive daytime sleepiness, and a general unrefreshed feeling (López‐Soto et al. 2019; Matheson et al. 2014). The cumulative effect can lead to chronic fatigue, which can impair cognitive functions, such as memory, attention, and decision‐making skills, posing significant risks to both nurses and their patients due to the increased potential for errors in medication administration or other essential tasks (Razavi et al. 2019; Tahghighi et al. 2017). Additionally, long‐term disruption of the circadian rhythm can lead to alterations in hormonal regulation, including disruptions to cortisol rhythms as discussed earlier (Grosser et al. 2022; Hardeland 2008). These disturbances can lead to metabolic disorders like obesity, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases due to the alteration of metabolic and cardiovascular rhythms (Björntorp and Rosmond 2000; Farhood et al. 2019; McGregor et al. 2016).

The disruption of the circadian rhythm has been associated with various health consequences, including potential risks such as certain types of cancer, particularly breast cancer (Cipolla‐Neto and Amaral 2018; Farhood et al. 2019). This association is postulated to be a result of disturbed endocrine secretions, as diurnal hormones have been demonstrated to exhibit anticancer properties (Farhood et al. 2019). Given the potential health implications of shift work, it is crucial to understand how different work schedules impact physiological markers such as cortisol levels among nurses. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the effect of single‐ and double‐shift work on salivary cortisol levels, contributing to the broader understanding of how shift work can influence health outcomes in healthcare professionals.

Methods

Study Design

This comparative descriptive study was conducted at an academic medical institution within a non‐profit health system in Turkey between October 2023 and February 2024. The participants worked in a cyclical shift pattern: after completing a single shift (08:00–16:00), a nurse would then move on to a double shift (16:00–08:00), followed by a 24‐h rest period. Conversely, upon completing a double shift, they would transition to a single shift after another 24‐h rest period. Saliva samples were collected from all participants before, after, and at midnight for both a single and a double shift. Pre‐samples were gathered 30 min prior to the start of each shift: between 07:30–08:00 for a single shift and 15:30–16:00 for a double shift. Post‐samples were collected 30 min after the end of each shift: 08:00–08:30 following a single shift and 16:00–16:30 after double shifts. This design ensured the randomness and balance between single and double‐shift schedules. For the midnight measurements (23:00–24:00), samples were collected at the midpoint of their shift for those working double shifts and during a routine daily period when they were not on duty for those working single shifts. Midnight samples were collected between 23:00 and 24:00 under two distinct conditions depending on shift type. For nurses working double shifts, this time point corresponded to the midpoint of their active work period. In contrast, for nurses working single shifts, the midnight sample was obtained during off‐duty hours, representing a routine nighttime period outside working hours.

Participants

The required sample size was determined using G*Power 3.1.9.4 to ensure sufficient statistical power to detect significant differences. A priori power analysis was conducted for a repeated measures ANOVA with within‐subject factors. Based on an expected medium effect size (f = 0.25), an alpha level of 0.01 (α = 0.01), and a desired statistical power of 0.90 (1‐β = 0.90), the analysis indicated that a minimum of 50 participants would be necessary to achieve adequate power for detecting significant effects with our study design, which included 2 factors (single and double shift) and 3 measurement points (07:30–08:30, 15:30–16:30, 23:00–24:00). To account for potential data loss, a target sample size of 55 participants was set. Ultimately, the study included 52 female nurses who met the inclusion criteria. They all volunteered without receiving any monetary compensation. The participants were aged 20 to 40 years and were all employed in shift‐oriented units, primarily delivering direct patient care. The inclusion criteria required that participants be registered nurses with at least 1 year of work experience and have a healthy body mass index (BMI) within the standard range. Exclusion criteria included non‐nursing health professionals, nursing personnel whose primary roles did not involve direct patient care (e.g., leadership, case management, educators), and advanced practice nurses. Furthermore, individuals with a confirmed history of psychiatric illnesses or those on medication regimes that included cardiologic, anti‐depressant, tranquilliser, hypnotic, anti‐Parkinson, or glucose‐lowering pharmaceuticals were also excluded. In addition, participants with a known history of endocrine disorders affecting cortisol secretion, including Cushing's syndrome, were excluded based on self‐reported medical history. To ensure the study's results reflected real‐world conditions as closely as possible, participants were asked to maintain their regular dietary and caffeine consumption habits during the study. Before commencing, all participants provided informed consent, thereby affirming their voluntary participation.

Salivary Sample Collection

Cortisol can be measured using various biological matrices, including blood (serum or plasma), urine, and saliva. In the present study, salivary cortisol was preferred because it offers a non‐invasive, practical, and reliable method for repeated sampling under real‐world working conditions, particularly during shift work, without introducing additional procedural stress. Furthermore, salivary cortisol is considered to reflect the unbound, biologically active fraction of circulating cortisol, making it a well‐established biomarker for assessing stress‐related and circadian rhythm alterations in occupational settings (Inder et al. 2012; Lee et al. 2024). To determine cortisol levels, saliva samples were collected from nurses for both single and double shifts using sterile saliva collection tubes and swabs. Before each collection, nurse participants were asked to abstain from eating, drinking, or smoking for at least 30 min to maintain the sample's integrity. Samples were secured 30 min before their shift's commencement and 30 min post‐conclusion. During the collection process, participants first rinsed their mouth with water and waited for about 2 min. They were then instructed to sit comfortably, tilting their heads forward slightly. Using a sterile swab, they gently rubbed their cheek's interior for approximately 30 s. The swab was subsequently placed into a saliva collection tube without making contact with external surfaces and was sealed securely. Post‐collection, to avoid hormonal degradation, samples were promptly stored in a cool, dark place. Each sample was labelled with the date, collection time, and a participant identifier before being stored in biohazard bags. Accompanied by comprehensive documentation detailing the participant's shift and any peculiarities during collection, these samples were then transported to the laboratory under temperature‐controlled conditions for in‐depth analysis. The salivary cortisol concentrations were quantified utilizing enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits commercially procured from Cayman Chemical (Cortisol Express, Ann Arbor, MI, USA).

Statistical Analysis

Data processing and statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics v25 for Windows (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism version 9.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). All data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD). To assess interactions between time (07:30–08:30, 15:30–16:30, 23:00–24:00) and shift type (single and double), a repeated‐measures two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used. Sphericity assumptions were checked using Mauchly's test, and corrections were applied when assumptions were violated. In particular, if the epsilon (ε) value was less than 0.75, the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied. Conversely, the Huynh–Feldt correction was used for degrees of freedom when ε exceeded 0.75.

Results

The study included 52 nurses. The mean ± standard deviation values for their respective ages, work experience, and Body Mass Index (BMI) were 27.9 ± 5.9 years, 6.9 ± 4.5 years, and 22.0 ± 1.4 kg/m2, respectively.

A two‐way repeated‐measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) including both time and shift type demonstrated significant main effects for both variables (Figure 1). Salivary cortisol concentrations were maximal between 07:30 and 08:30, while the trough levels were recorded at midnight. Inspection of the main effect associated with shift type revealed that double‐shift workers exhibited higher mean cortisol concentrations. Specifically, cortisol levels were nearly two‐fold higher at midnight in double‐shift workers compared to single‐shift workers. Additionally, a significant interaction effect between time and shift type was found.

Mean salivary cortisol concentrations at 07:30–08:30 (before the single‐shift and after the double‐shift), 15:30–16:30 (after the single‐shift and before the double‐shift), and 23:00–24:00 (midnight during the off‐time for the single‐shift workers and mid‐work for the double‐shift workers).

Discussion

This study primarily sought to evaluate the influence of single versus double‐shift schedules on salivary cortisol concentrations in nurses. Among research endeavours investigating the repercussions of shift work, our endeavour is distinctive due to its focus on the Turkish nursing milieu where the double‐shift model is notably widespread. Our most pivotal observation—a near two‐fold surge in cortisol levels at midnight for double‐shift nurses compared to their single‐shift peers—highlights the considerable physiological strain imposed by prolonged work durations. This spike in cortisol, a hormone fundamentally associated with stress regulation and circadian rhythm modulation, indicates not only a perturbation in the diurnal rhythm but might also insinuate elevated stress burdens and their concomitant negative health implications. In sum, our findings underscore the imperative of re‐assessing nursing operational paradigms, particularly in settings like Turkey, where the rigours of double shifts could be amplifying health inequities among medical practitioners.

In numerous studies across Asian and Middle Eastern cultures, it has been consistently observed that women continue to shoulder a substantial share of domestic responsibilities, regardless of their professional engagements (Lin et al. 2022; Sladek et al. 2019). In such socio‐cultural milieus, female nurses, despite their taxing nocturnal shifts, are often additionally burdened with the expectations of fulfilling household duties. This dual role—of a healthcare professional during the night and a primary caregiver or homemaker during the day—may impose profound stress, which our study suggests may manifest as disrupted cortisol rhythms. Elevated cortisol levels, as widely acknowledged in literature, are emblematic of augmented stress and potential disturbances in the circadian rhythm (Lin et al. 2022; McGregor et al. 2016; Metzenthin et al. 2009). The exigencies of nocturnal shift work, combined with daytime domestic responsibilities, can compound disturbances in the sleep–wake cycle (Grosser et al. 2022; Sladek et al. 2019). This, in turn, could lead to a chronic misalignment in the circadian rhythm, further heightening cortisol production and intensifying stress responses (Minelli et al. 2021; Tsai et al. 2019). Such patterns, particularly prevalent among female nurses in Asian and Middle Eastern contexts, might exacerbate their susceptibility to health complications associated with chronic stress and sleep deprivation.

Our investigation reveals the pronounced physiological strain inherent to double‐shift nursing, an observation that's alarmingly under‐recognized in many healthcare institutions. The marked midnight cortisol elevation among these nurses, when compared with their single‐shift colleagues, is a clear affirmation of findings from previous research highlighting shift work's detrimental impact on circadian rhythm (Grosser et al. 2022; Hennig et al. 1998; Lin et al. 2022). Cortisol, beyond its pivotal role in stress response mechanisms, also stands as a crucial regulator of our internal sleep–wake cycles. Elevated levels, particularly during the midnight hours, signal a significant misalignment in the body's natural rhythms. As these imbalances persist for extended periods, they could precipitate a cascade of adverse health outcomes, ranging from cardiovascular diseases and cognitive decline to metabolic syndromes and sleep disturbances (Björntorp and Rosmond 2000; Hellhammer et al. 2009; McGregor et al. 2016).

The consistent cortisol elevations, combined with the circadian misalignments our study underscores, are not isolated physiological aberrations (Grosser et al. 2022; Hellhammer et al. 2009; Sladek et al. 2019). They're harbingers of broader health concerns that could be looming on the horizon. Chronic stress, which is physiologically manifested through sustained cortisol spikes, brings with it a plethora of negative health outcomes: from weakened immune responses and heightened susceptibility to infections to escalated risks for conditions like hypertension, diabetes, and even mental health disorders (Björntorp and Rosmond 2000; McGregor et al. 2016). Nurses, as frontline caregivers, are entrusted not only with their patients' well‐being but also with the broader healthcare system's operational efficiency (Matheson et al. 2014). Ensuring nurses' health isn't compromised is paramount. Our findings, while focused on Turkey, provide a global clarion call. They emphasize the need to critically revisit and realign nursing operational norms, especially in settings where the rigours of double shifts could be covertly magnifying health disparities.

Our study, while centered on nursing practices, offers insights that reverberate beyond this specific investigation. In healthcare frameworks where prolonged shifts are the norm rather than the exception, a plethora of other medical professionals, be they doctors, technicians, or support staff, might be unwittingly navigating similar physiological terrains of stress and circadian disruption (Bani‐Issa et al. 2020; Ibar et al. 2021). As healthcare globally transitions toward models of patient‐centric care, an intertwined responsibility emerges: ensuring that caregivers themselves aren't mired in cycles of chronic stress and fatigue (Lin et al. 2015; Nagai et al. 2011). Only by addressing these interconnected dynamics can healthcare truly advance toward an integrated, holistic model in which patient and practitioner well‐being are viewed as two sides of the same coin.

Limitations

In interpreting the findings of our study, several notable limitations warrant attention. Primarily, the cross‐sectional nature of our research captures only a specific time‐point of cortisol levels in nurses, with longitudinal studies potentially offering more insight into the prolonged effects of work shifts on cortisol concentrations. Additionally, while our research emphasized work shifts, external factors—ranging from individual health behaviours and diet to sleep patterns and personal stressors—might also play a role in influencing cortisol levels but were not deeply considered. The sociocultural dynamics of Turkey, with its unique blend of cultural and socioeconomic contexts, were touched upon in our study, but a deeper, more nuanced exploration could further enrich the discourse. Lastly, while our research offers critical insights into the Turkish nursing landscape, transferring these findings to other nations or cultural backgrounds requires careful extrapolation, considering the distinctiveness of the Turkish context. In essence, while our study illuminates the implications of shift patterns in Turkey's nursing sector, these limitations suggest the need for more expansive research to more comprehensively understand the relationship between work shifts, cortisol levels, and health outcomes.

Conclusion

In light of our study into the influence of work schedules on salivary cortisol concentrations among nurses, this study unveils compelling insights into the physiological impacts of shift work on healthcare professionals. It is evident that the double‐shift system exerts notable physiological strain on practitioners, as demonstrated by the nearly two‐fold increase in midnight cortisol levels compared to those working single shifts. The observed perturbations in cortisol levels signal potential disruptions in the diurnal rhythm and raise concerns about heightened stress levels and their subsequent health repercussions among nurses. The strain of prolonged work durations is not merely a professional challenge but resonates with broader health equity concerns, as shift work intersects with personal stressors, health behaviours, and socio‐cultural obligations that impact overall well‐being.

Future research should adopt a more nuanced approach by incorporating pre‐ and post‐shift assessments for nurses, enabling a more detailed understanding of physiological changes during single and double shifts. Moreover, increasing the frequency of salivary cortisol measurements, potentially every 2 h, could provide a more precise depiction of circadian disruptions caused by night shifts. Additionally, integrating clinical data, such as stress‐related psychometric instruments and depression scales, could strengthen the correlation between biochemical markers and psychological stress levels, providing a holistic view of the impact of shift work on nurses' health.

While our study presents a foundational understanding within its scope, it is paramount that future research endeavours delve deeper, embracing a longitudinal approach and more comprehensive assessment methodologies. This would facilitate a richer comprehension of the persistent effects of shift work on physiological and psychological health. In summary, our study underscores the pressing need to recalibrate nursing operational paradigms. The findings call on policymakers, healthcare administrators, and nursing communities to reconsider long‐standing practices, weighing operational efficiency against the tangible health risks they pose to medical practitioners. Beyond operational efficiency, it's about safeguarding the well‐being of those at the forefront of healthcare provision.
