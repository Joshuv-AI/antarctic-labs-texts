Filling the Responsibility Gap: Agency and Responsibility in the Technological Age
Authors: Yong-Hong Xia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The rise of automation, such as artificial intelligence, has significantly reduced human agency in artifact-involving actions, thereby giving rise to a moral dilemma known as the responsibility gap. In this dilemma, moral undesirable consequences arise from technology, yet no one is held accountable because individuals lack control over their actions and their outcomes, as well as the ability to predict these outcomes, rendering them morally blameless. This paper proposes a framework based on externalist agency and distributed responsibility, aiming to address this problem by assigning accountability to artifacts. Externalist agency holds that agency is a gradient concept rather than an all-or-nothing one. Between fully autonomous agency and non-agency, there are three intermediate gradients: distributed agency, constrained agency, and derivative agency. Distributed responsibility holds that responsibility should be allocated according to these gradients of agency. For both humans and artifacts: if an agent possesses derivative agency, forgiveness is warranted; if it possesses distributed agency, punishment is justifiable. This paper further suggests that punishment for artifacts can take the form of punishment for technological lineage—that is, modifying the design of technologies to guide their evolution toward safer and more ethical directions.

Introduction

Technological artifacts have traditionally been assumed to be passive and neutral tools, based on the belief that humans maintain full control over them and can autonomously use them for good or ill (Jaspers & Bullock, 2010; cf., p. 100; Winner, 1978, p. 25). Accordingly, the consequences of artifact-involving action（henceforth AIA） are considered the sole responsibility of users, not of the artifacts themselves (Pitt, 2014). However, as technology continues to evolve, this assumption is increasingly being challenged. Consider three scenarios of accidental injury: with a gun, responsibility unambiguously falls on the user; with an automated production line, responsibility becomes dispersed among operators, managers, and designers; with an autonomous robot, responsibility becomes nearly impossible to determine. This progression illustrates that the more complex the structure and function of a technological artifact are, the less human agency can be exerted on it, and the less moral responsibility seems to be attributable to humans (Simmler, 2024), potentially resulting in a situation where no human agent can be held accountable (Nersessian & Mancha, 2020).

This dilemma, in which innocent individuals are harmed by technology, but no one is held morally responsible, is known as the responsibility gap problem (Matthias, 2004; Danaher, 2022; Sparrow, 2007). It challenges traditional moral responsibility theories, which hold that individuals are only responsible for consequences they can predict and control (Aristotle & Ross, 2009). In AIA, every consequence arises from complex technological interactions, rendering these consequences difficult to predict and control by individual human agents. This complicates the attribution of responsibility, undermines the system of moral punishment, and threatens social justice.

This paper proposes a novel approach to the responsibility gap by advancing a gradient model of agency grounded in externalist assumptions. It treats agency not as a fixed internal capacity of the human subject, but as one that is shaped by material dependencies. Whereas traditional views regard agency as all-or-nothing and confined within the individual, this model reconceives agency as a continuum defined by degrees of material involvement, which opens conceptual space for attributing agency to non-human objects. When high-gradient human agency cannot fully account for AIA, low-gradient material agency may help fill the explanatory gap. This, in turn, raises the possibility of distributed responsibility, where non-human material agents might share responsibility for the outcomes of action.

To explore this idea, the article is structured as follows: Section “The responsibility gap problem” analyzes the roots of the responsibility gap; Section “Externalist agency: action with artifacts” develops an alternative externalist model of agency, while Section “Distributed responsibility” investigates the prospects of distributed responsibility by considering how material objects might be integrated into systems of action and responsibility. Section “Advantages of distributed responsibility” offers a further defense of this framework by outlining its advantages. Building on these steps, the paper seeks to address the responsibility gap and reconstruct the systems of moral responsibility.

The Responsibility Gap Problem

The term “responsibility gap” was coined by Matthias (2004) to describe the challenges posed by the application of artificial intelligence (AI) to traditional systems of moral responsibility. Although the operational rules of AI are initially set by humans, machine learning enables these rules to evolve during operation, leading to scenarios where no one can fully control AI behaviors. As a result, when AI errors cause harm, it becomes difficult to attribute responsibility to operators, designers, or manufacturers, leaving victims without recourse for accountability. Danaher (2016) further describes this predicament as giving rise to a “retribution gap,” where a strong desire for punishment arises, but no deserved subject can be identified.

As AI becomes increasingly prevalent, the responsibility gap problem grows more acute, particularly contexts like autonomous weapons and autonomous driving systems (Champagne & Tonkens, 2015; Nyholm, 2018; Sparrow, 2007; Wood, 2023). For example, when autonomous weapon systems mistakenly harm civilians, their operation beyond human prediction and control renders responsibility attribution deeply ambiguous, giving rise to what is known as a responsibility gap. Sparrow (2007) emphasizes that identifying a specific individual accountable for civilian deaths is fundamental to the principles of just warfare. Danaher (2016) also notes that humans are natural retributivists, insisting that harm must be met with punishment. This signifies a “harm-attribute-punishment” justice process, where someone can be attributed and punished when innocent harm occurs. However, the responsibility gap disrupts this justice process, leading to a dilemma: should society prioritize justice by curtailing technological progress, or accept a compromise of justice for the sake of innovation?

Addressing the responsibility gap problem requires an exploration of its root causes. A prevailing view attributes the gap to the complexity and opacity of automation technology, which render the outcomes of AIA unpredictable and uncontrollable (Gunkel, 2020; Königs, 2022; Matthias, 2004; Wood, 2023). Another perspective frames it as a “many-hands problem,” where responsibility is not absent but dispersed among multiple actors (de Jong, 2020; Taylor, 2021; see also Jonas, 1984). While these views differ in focusing either on things (technological system complexity) or humans (labor division complexity), I argue that the root cause lies in the joint interaction between humans and artifacts, rather than in many hands or many things alone (cf. Coeckelbergh, 2020).

The responsibility gap can be analyzed on two levels. From a descriptive level, it represents an absent state of responsibility; from a normative level, it reflects a state of moral injustice.1 Through the lens of these two levels, we will see that the responsibility gap involves the relationship between humans and artifacts, rather than just humans or artifacts alone.

First, let’s analyze the origins of the absence of responsibility. According to Aristotle’s doctrines, moral responsibility applies only to voluntary actions, which require both free will and awareness of the consequences (Aristotle & Ross, 2009, pp. 38–41). This yields two conditions for moral responsibility: the control condition, which means that the agent must have control over the behavior leading to an adverse outcome, and the epistemic condition, which requires that the agent can foresee the potential negative consequences of their decisions (Fischer & Ravizza, 1999, p. 13; Rudy-Hiller, 2022).

Although Aristotle did not explicitly use the concept of agency, he effectively equated voluntariness with agency. Consequently, the two conditions above serve not only as prerequisites for voluntariness and moral responsibility but also as fundamental requirements for agency. It follows that the necessary condition for a person to bear moral responsibility for an action and its consequences is having sufficient agency over them (Alvarez, 2024). Without agency, there can be no responsibility; where agency is absent, responsibility cannot exist. Conversely, this also clarifies a key principle of responsibility allocation: the degree of agency determines the degree of responsibility.

In AI applications, responsibility gaps frequently arise in the absence of human agency (Coeckelbergh, 2016). Sparrow’s (2007) trilemma shows that programmers cannot foresee consequences, operators lack complete control, and machines cannot be punished. The crux of the trilemma is the loss of human agency in AIA (cf. Simpson & Müller, 2016). More accurately, it is due to human agents in AIA feeling an increasing loss of control over the AIA (Hindriks & Veluwenkamp, 2023; Nyholm et al., 2023), i.e., a loss of their sense of agency. The more autonomous machines become, the less agency there is for designers or operators, and the more it appears that no one is responsible for the damage done by machines.

While a lack of agency at the descriptive level entails a corresponding lack of responsibility, such a condition does not necessarily constitute an injustice at the normative level. Consider two scenarios that illustrate how injustice can arise. In the first scenario, Sam walks by the lake, and Alice walks past him and sneezes loudly. Startled, Sam stumbles, falls into the water, and drowns. Since her sneeze was an involuntary bodily reaction rather than a deliberate action, she had no control over the incident and thus should not bear moral blame. In this case, the absence of agency and responsibility does not constitute moral injustice—it is simply misfortune. In the second scenario, Alice’s self-driving car malfunctions, causing Sam to fall into the lake and drown in fright. Here, traditional theories of moral responsibility suggest no one to be blamed since no one controlled the car; but according to our moral intuitions, it is unjust to blame Sam’s bad luck, and someone (or something) should bear responsibility.

The difference between these two scenarios is that the first death stems from an uncontrolled natural event (i.e., a sneeze), while the second is caused by the malfunction of an artifact (i.e., a self-driving car). According to Judith Shklar (1990, p. 73), if the cause of harm can be attributed to luck and necessity, then such harm is considered a misfortune; if it can be attributed to human choice or technology, then it is an injustice. We can accept misfortunes caused by natural events, but it is difficult to accept the injustice caused by the malfunction of artifacts. The responsibility gap is considered an injustice, not a misfortune, because it is caused by the malfunction of technological artifacts rather than natural processes.

Based on the above analysis, we can identify two necessary conditions for the responsibility gap: (1) humans have diminished agency over the consequences of actions, and (2) these consequences are caused by losing control over technological artifacts. The former leads to the absence of responsibility, while the latter makes this absence an injustice. In this sense, the root of the responsibility gap involves not only humans, who can no longer predict or control outcomes, but also artifacts, whose involvement is causally implicated in those outcomes and creates a moral demand for the assignment of accountability. In this sense, the responsibility gap can only be addressed through a framework of analysis that integrates both human and non-human entities.

Externalist Agency: Action with Artifacts

Given that human agents lose full agency in the context of the responsibility gap, and that the gap itself is intimately tied to the involvement of artifacts, this leads to a straightforward inference: artifacts have, at least partially, substituted for the lost portion of human agency and thereby causally contributed to outcomes of AIA. As a result, the agent of an AIA should include not only humans but also artifacts. However, this view may be controversial. From the perspective of contemporary action philosophy, agency refers to the ability to perform intentional actions or voluntarily initiate actions; thus, it is often bound to inherent capacities such as intentionality, higher-order desires, or rational deliberation. This internalist view conceptualizes agency as an inner life, independent of worldly control over the consequences of actions. According to this view, agency may need to meet epistemic conditions, but it does not have to satisfy control conditions, as the control over the consequences of AIA has nothing to do with the inner life of an agent. Accordingly, there is no difference between human agency in bodily actions and that in AIA, and therefore, agency is not absent, and thus its purported absence cannot account for the absence of responsibility. In this section, I will propose an externalist view of agency in opposition to this internalist view of agency, which rejects the anchoring of agency to the subject’s internal capacities and argues instead that external resources are an indispensable constituent of agency.

The internalist view of agency has dominated the contemporary philosophy of action. Anscombe (2000) and Davidson (1963) argue that actions are events caused by mental intentions, which give reasons for the actions, making them intelligible. Therefore, for them, agency is essentially the capacity to form action intentions, allowing the agent’s mental states to rationally bring about actions. Their views represent the standard account of action and agency. Frankfurt (1971) and Bratman (1987) later refined this standard account, adding that agency involves second-order volitions (reflective desires guiding first-order desires) or long-term plans that ensure consistency and self-governance. Despite differences, internalist views agree that agency resides in the internal capacity to form intentions and deliberate, independent of external constraints.

However, this view seems disconnected from reality, particularly in extreme cases where external constraints render action impossible. Imagine a person who is imprisoned, with their body completely restrained, unable to move or speak. Intuitively, we would say that this person has lost their agency. Yet, according to the internalist perspective, since their mental capacities remain intact, including their internal intentions and capacity for rational deliberation, they are still considered to possess agency. This contradiction highlights a fundamental dilemma in the internalist view. We must recognize that if an individual lacks the external resources to realize their intentions, internal capacities alone are insufficient to exercise agency.

One might raise the following objection: the restricted individual still possesses full agency; it is simply that someone or something is preventing them from exercising it. This objection reflects a strongly internalist intuition—namely, that agency is an inherent and enduring attribute of the individual, which is not nullified by external constraints. However, this intuition fails to distinguish between potentiality and actuality. Agency is not merely a potential capacity; it is an actual power to turn intentions into actions and to take responsibility for their outcomes. While intentions, motivations, and judgments are indeed necessary components of agency, they do not suffice on their own. These internal states can constitute a causally complete action only when supported by enabling conditions such as external resources, social institutions, and environmental affordances. If these external conditions are completely missing, an individual may still “want” to act, but they will be practically unable to convert those intentions into real-world actions. They may still be able to think, imagine, or remember—but their thoughts cannot lead to real actions, so they lose their connection with the world. Without the ability to act, we cannot talk about assigning responsibility. In this way, their agency is strongly limited. (See later discussion of constrained agency).

This example demonstrates the limitation of internalism: if an individual lacks the external resources to fulfill their intentions, relying solely on internal capacities is insufficient to exercise agency. In contrast, Aristotle represents an ancient externalist view of agency. This limitation illustrates the need for an externalist account, such as that represented by Aristotle. While he views agency as the capacity for voluntary actions, he carefully considers the external constraints that render the action involuntary, defining the latter as those “of which the moving principle is outside, being a principle in which nothing is contributed by the person who acts—or, rather, is acted upon, e.g. if he were to be carried somewhere by a wind, or by men who had him in their power.” (Aristotle & Ross, 2009, p. 38) Aristotle believed that agency means that the causal flow in the real world can be controlled by the agent. At the same time, by extending actions beyond the body, Aristotle also expanded the scope of the agent. For him, when you use a stick to push open a door, the stick’s movement is not merely a consequence of the action—as internalists would argue—but rather, the stick’s movement is considered part of your action (Charles, 2017). Therefore, for Aristotle, action often means acting with artifacts.

Although Aristotle introduced external factors into the model of action, both he and mainstream contemporary philosophers of action regard action as behavior stemming from internal rational cognition. This implies that while he recognizes that the control conditions for agency and responsibility depend on the support of external resources, he does not seem to clearly demonstrate that the epistemic conditions for voluntary action also rely on external resources. Knowing the consequences of actions does not depend solely on the internal mental structure of an individual; instead, it is deeply embedded in external resources. Theories such as distributed cognition and extended cognition emphasize that cognitive processes often unfold across individuals, other people, and environmental media. The generation and maintenance of knowledge depend on the networked configuration of social and material components (Hutchins, 1995; Hutchins, 1995). External cognitive tools such as paper and pen, calculators, and even digital devices are not merely passive auxiliary means but structurally constitute a part of the cognitive system (Clark & Chalmers, 1998). Knowledge of the external world is both a product of internal mental processing and a process co-sustained by external resources.

Therefore, human knowing and control of the consequences of their actions are often inseparable from the support of various material and social resources. To describe this dependence, I propose a 3 × 3 analytical framework of agency, based on Aristotle’s control and epistemic conditions. In this framework, neither control nor knowledge is treated as all-or-nothing. Instead, each condition is divided into three types: autonomous, distributed, and absent. Control may come from the agent alone, from interaction with others or with artifacts, or it may be missing altogether. The same applies to knowledge: it can be formed independently, co-produced through information exchange with others or artifacts, or absent in some situations. Nine types of agency

The nine grids described by Fig. 1 can be grouped into five gradients of agency, ranging from high to low: autonomous agency, distributed agency, constrained agency, derivative agency, and non-agency.

The first and highest gradient is autonomous agency (corresponding to Grid 1: Autonomous Cognition × Autonomous Control). This form represents full self-determination and self-awareness, where both the agent’s cognition and control are fully independent. For example, when an author completes a piece of writing alone, they not only control the writing process by themselves but also have a clear anticipation of their creative goals. This is a typical case of autonomous agency. The internalist view of agency often focuses on this ideal type. However, such a view does not account for most real-world cases of agency.

The second gradient is distributed agency. In this case, the agent’s control or cognition is no longer fully independent, but instead emerges through coupling with other agents or artifacts. This category includes three specific combinations: (1) when the agent retains autonomous cognition while control is distributed (Grid 2: Autonomous Cognition × Distributed Control); (2) when the agent maintains autonomous control over the consequences of the action, yet achieves cognition in a distributed manner (Grid 4: Distributed Cognition × Autonomous Control); (3) and when both control and cognition are distributed (Grid 5: Distributed Cognition × Distributed Control).

This type of agency covers most situations of AIA. For example, when a navigation app guides us to walk to a destination, the agency here corresponds to “Distributed Cognition × Autonomous Control” because the app provides key route information, which represents distributed cognition, while we control our walking pace and direction to maintain autonomous control. When we drive an electric vehicle with an advanced driver-assistance system (ADAS), the agency is “Distributed Cognition × Distributed Control” as ADAS both shares environmental detection tasks as part of distributed cognition and aids in speed adjustment to participate in distributed control. When a surgeon works with a surgical robot, the agency fits “Distributed Control × Autonomous Cognition”: the surgeon independently plans the operation to exercise autonomous cognition, and the robot assists in precise execution to achieve distributed control.

Within this agency structure, agency is neither a purely human attribute nor a simple function of objects; instead, it manifests through the collaborative action between humans and objects. Compared to the traditional framework of autonomous agency, distributed agency better explains the structure of action under automated conditions.

The third gradient is constrained agency, which refers to an agency structure where one of the autonomous agency conditions is present, but the other is completely absent. For instance, take the example mentioned earlier in this paper: a conscious person who is tightly restrained and unable to move. This person still has autonomous cognition but completely lacks control (Grid 3). In this case, their agency is clearly constrained. There is also a second scenario: when control remains autonomous but cognition is absent (Grid 7). Here, the agent can act physically but without awareness. For instance, a distracted driver may follow a navigation app to reach a destination without realizing that the route has deviated. The driver’s movements are still autonomous, based on muscle control, but their cognitive engagement is missing. In both situations, agency is limited because one essential condition—either cognition or control—is entirely absent.

The fourth gradient is derivative agency, which refers to a limited form of agency often described in an anthropomorphic way. Although artifacts in this category do not have full agency, their scaffolding role in action cannot be ignored. Several well-known cases from Science and Technology Studies (STS), especially theories of material agency, fall under this type.

The first form of derivative agency is “Distributed Control × No Cognition” (Grid 8). In this case, artifacts can exert practical control over actions through coupling with humans, but they themselves can neither assist human cognition nor achieve autonomous cognition. For instance, Latour (1992) mentioned that speed bumps “prescribe” drivers to slow down when approaching. In Latour’s view, speed bumps perform a function of “translation”: they implicitly “invite” certain behaviors from humans while “discouraging” others, thereby exerting agency in human actions. This translation function is, in fact, a form of distributed control over actions. The second form is “Distributed Cognition × No Control” (Grid 6). Here, artifacts are integrated into human extended cognitive systems and contribute to human cognition, yet they themselves do not hold the authority to manipulate actions. For example, in Verbeek’s (2006) theory of technological mediation, medical imaging equipment cannot directly manipulate actions, but they alter human perception and decision-making by processing and presenting information. Derivative agency, in this sense, offers a way to incorporate theories of material agency into a more reasonable analytical model. It not only avoids anthropomorphizing artifacts as fully autonomous agents but also transcends the neutral view of regarding them as mere tools, thereby revealing the essence of artifacts’ agency.

The fifth gradient is no agency, which signifies the complete absence of agency. This is different from derivative agency, where at least one condition is present; instead, it involves a double absence, making it impossible to qualify as an agent in any sense. For example, fully non-functional artifacts—such as a powerless robot, a malfunctioning sensor, or a damaged traffic light—can neither act on the environment nor provide information. They are simply inactive objects within an action network. A human can also be in a non-agentic state. If a person has no control over an action and no access to relevant knowledge, their role is no longer agentic but merely that of a passive patient within the action network. Non-agency serves as the zero point of the entire framework. It helps define and locate other types of agency along a continuous spectrum.

The above 3 × 3 gradient model of agency reveals one key insight: fully autonomous agency is an extremely rare phenomenon. In specific action practices, an individual’s grasp of control and epistemic conditions is often not independent; instead, it is highly dependent on the support of external resources. Among the different types of agency, distributed agency and derivative agency are especially relevant to this paper. Both emphasize that the epistemic or control conditions depend on external resources; thus, agency is distributed and external rather than internal. This shifts the focus away from the traditional internalist views of agency and opens up an externalist approach to understanding how humans and artifacts act together. From this perspective, we can more reasonably analyze how responsibility is shared or assigned in complex action networks.

Distributed Responsibility

Given that artifacts exert a non-negligible form of agency in AIA, a possible approach to addressing the responsibility gap becomes clear. If human agency alone can no longer fully explain the causes of actions, then perhaps material agency can be introduced to supplement this explanation. This leads to a key question: can material objects be made to (jointly) bear responsibility for the consequences of actions, thereby helping to fill the responsibility gap? Hanson (2014) recognizes this possibility. He argues that in the age of automation, most actions involve both human and non-human components. As a result, moral responsibility should be assigned not only to individual humans but also to the collective of human and non-human agents. This model of responsibility can be called distributed responsibility, which is not only a form of collective or shared responsibility among humans (Smiley, 2023), but also one that extends responsibility to include objects and systems (Floridi, 2016).

However, simply stating that responsibility should be assigned to the collective of humans and artifacts is not enough to solve the responsibility gap problem. We also need to identify who is responsible and to what extent. This requires estimating the approximate responsibility share of each relevant human and non-human agent to develop practical accountability strategies. According to the responsibility principle introduced in Section “The Responsibility Gap Problem” determine each agent’s gradient of agency based on the type of epistemic and control conditions it meets in a given action, thereby further determining its proportional responsibility. At the two ends of the spectrum, responsibility distribution is straightforward: a fully autonomous agent bears full responsibility, while a non-agent bears none. However, in cases of distributed or derivative agency, humans and artifacts jointly act through various forms of collaboration. To prevent responsibility gaps, responsibility must no longer be assigned only to human individuals—it must also be extended to material agents.

But how can we assign responsibility to material agents? Holding someone accountable does not always mean punishing them. Sometimes, once they recognize their responsibility, we may choose to forgive them instead. This idea offers a way to think about assigning responsibility to technological artifacts. We can do so through two possible strategies: forgiveness and punishment. While punishment is certainly essential for upholding justice, when it fails—or is no longer possible—we must turn to forgiveness instead (Ci, 2006, pp. 187–188).

Considering it’s challenging to punish non-human elements, a natural strategy is to accept the retribution gap and forgive the involved human and non-human agents. Forgiveness, as the opposite of revenge, has always been a desirable strategy. From Nietzsche’s perspective, forgiveness transcends the slave-like resentment and revenge of “an eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth,” laying the groundwork for a move from weakness to strength (Nietzsche & Large, 2007, pp. 13–15). From Hannah Arendt’s perspective, forgiveness prevents us from being trapped in risky and luck-based causal chains, thereby ensuring the possibility of future free actions (Arendt, 1998, pp. 239–241). Although the forgiveness discussed by both them is forgiving humans, the logic of forgiving artifacts is similar (Bennett, 2010, p. 38). In this sense, acknowledging the existence of accountability gaps and relinquishing the impulse for retribution can be beneficial and morally reasonable (cf. Kraaijeveld, 2020; Munch et al., 2023).

Compared to forgiveness, punishment strategies are much more common. Legal punishment—often formal and systematic—and moral condemnation—often subtle and widespread—are two typical forms. However, such punishments usually target humans, whether individuals or groups, rather than technological artifacts. At first glance, punishing artifacts may seem unrealistic, since objects cannot feel pain. But if we see punishment not as revenge, but as a way to reform the punished subject, then punishing artifacts becomes more reasonable—because artifacts can be modified and improved. We can regard a machine as an object that continuously evolves, and it has its own phylogenetic history. For instance, as Simondon pointed out, technical objects that persist are always more concretized and individuated than their predecessors (Simondon et al., 2017, pp. 25–28). If we adopt this perspective, artifacts have their own genealogies. Just as nature imposes punishment on species through natural selection, shaping the direction of their evolution, we too can impose selective punishment on technological artifacts, thereby determining their future lineage. This selection involves eliminating the harmful and preserving the good, achieved by resetting the technological codes and moral scripts of technological artifacts. In doing so, immoral or harmful technological artifacts are phased out, while ethical and beneficial ones are retained. This selection of technological lineage can be termed punishment for technological lineage.

But when should we apply punishment strategies, and when should we turn to forgiveness? Here, I propose a basic framework: if an agent shows distributed agency in a joint action, a punishment strategy is appropriate; if an agent only exhibits derivative agency, a forgiveness strategy may be more suitable. In cases of distributed agency, the agent retains autonomy in either control or cognition, and its role is active and irreplaceable. Therefore, whether the agent is human or non-human, it should be held accountable through punishment when responsibility is assigned. In contrast, if the agent’s role is merely derivative—meaning the joint action depends more on the other party’s agency—then it lacks the capacity to predict or control its own actions. In such cases, applying a forgiveness strategy aligns better with our sense of justice. Distributed responsibility

In AIA, we can identify five distinct scenarios based on different combinations of distributed and derivative agency. In each case, the agencies of humans and artifacts are complementary and often inversely related. In the remainder of this section, I will examine each scenario separately to explain how moral responsibility is distributed between human and non-human agents.

In the first and second scenarios, human agency takes the form of distributed agency—either “Distributed Control × Autonomous Cognition” or “Autonomous Control × Distributed Cognition.” Meanwhile, artifact agency is derivative—either “Distributed Control × No Cognition” or “No Control × Distributed Cognition.” In these situations, agency is mainly concentrated on the human side, and artifacts play only a supporting role in the causal chain. The accountability strategy in these cases is to punish humans and forgive artifacts. This approach partly reflects the traditional view of technological neutrality or instrumentalism, which holds that tools are not moral agents and that their value depends on how humans use them. However, artifacts are not entirely without agency. They still have limited, derivative agency, which means we can assign them some causal responsibility, but we choose to forgive rather than punish—because their agency is not complete. For example, if a person commits murder with a gun, the person’s agency is distributed agency (“Distributed Control × Autonomous Cognition”), while the gun’s agency is derivative agency (“Distributed Control × No Cognition”). Therefore, regarding the fatal result, human agency plays a dominant role, so we punish the human user; whereas the gun’s agency is merely derivative, so we do not impose punishment on it.

In the third scenario—where both human and material agency are distributed agency (i.e., “Distributed Control × Distributed Cognition”)—humans and objects jointly determine the outcome of the action through distributed cognition and control. This means the share of agency is roughly balanced between humans and objects: neither fully possesses cognition nor control, and both play an indispensable role in the occurrence of the action. In such cases, responsibility cannot fall on just one party. A strategy of joint punishment should be used, based on each party’s specific role and contribution. This means holding humans accountable for their normative and supervisory duties, and also applying punishment for technological lineage to the artifact. A clear example is the case of autonomous vehicles. The driving process reflects distributed agency shared between the driver, the system developers, and the vehicle’s intelligent system. The system can independently plan routes, make decisions, and take actions—such as emergency braking—showing a degree of cognitive and control capacity. At the same time, the driver must still intervene in complex situations, using information from both the system and the environment. Developers, through designing the system’s algorithms, also shape how it makes decisions (e.g., when to brake or steer). Therefore, when an accident occurs, both human agents (developers and drivers) and machine agents contribute causally. Responsibility should not be attributed solely to the driver or developer, but should also be traced back to the autonomous driving system itself. Here, accountability for the autonomous driving system can take the form of punishment for technological lineage: by repairing or improving its algorithms, we ensure that updated versions better comply with safety and ethical standards.

In the fourth and fifth scenarios, human agency is derivative—either “No Control × Distributed Cognition” or “Distributed Control × No Cognition”—while artifact agency is distributed—either “Autonomous Control × Distributed Cognition” or “Distributed Control × Autonomous Cognition.” These cases are often found in highly automated systems or science fiction settings, such as intelligent manufacturing systems and autonomous robots. In such situations, humans mostly assist machines in either cognition or control, playing roles such as supervisors, monitors, or assistants. Their agency is therefore derivative. In contrast, machines play a more prominent role in the decision-making and execution of action; yet they still rely on humans in either cognition or control, so their agency falls under the category of distributed agency.

Thus, the reasonable accountability strategy for these two scenarios is to forgive humans and punish artifacts. A good example is the use of industrial robots. In highly automated settings, human operators only perform supervisory or auxiliary roles, with limited agency. Therefore, even if they fail to intervene in an accident in a timely manner, they should be forgiven. Although a robot’s behavior is based on an AI model developed by humans, the complexity of the system means developers cannot fully predict or control it. As long as developers have complied with development standards, they should not be regarded as the primary responsible parties. Instead, responsibility should fall on the robotic system itself. This can be done through punishment for technological lineage, such as upgrading models or optimizing sensors, to ensure the system improves and avoids similar failures in the future.

Advantages of Distributed Responsibility

By incorporating artifacts into the framework of action and responsibility, the aforementioned distributed responsibility system reveals the dependence of human actions on artifacts and addresses the responsibility gap problem by holding artifacts accountable. Below, I will further elaborate on the advantages of the framework proposed in this paper compared to existing agency and responsibility theories that aim to address the responsibility gap problem.

Balancing Internalism and Material Agency Theory

The externalist view of agency represents a more inclusive perspective. A fundamental tenet of internalism holds that artifacts lack an “inner life”—such as intentionality and free will (cf. Mitcham, 2014)—and therefore cannot be moral agents or bear moral responsibility (Heersmink, 2017; Parthemore & Whitby, 2014). The framework proposed in this paper does not deny the special status of agents with an inner life; that is to say, only such agents can possess autonomous agency. However, there are multiple gradients of agency between autonomous agency and non-agency—a dimension overlooked by internalism. Distributed agency and derivative agency can also directly influence the consequences of actions, thereby exercising a certain degree of moral agency. In practice, human actions are always mediated by external tools and systems. Without these external supports, many actions would be impossible. For this reason, it is unreasonable to exclude artifacts from the category of agents. A more plausible claim, accordingly, is that humans and artifacts jointly perform moral actions and, by extension, are jointly assigned responsibility.

On the other hand, compared with theories of material agency, the framework proposed in this paper offers a more nuanced analysis of agency—while also avoiding contradictions with our everyday intuitions. Actor-Network Theory (ANT) and its subsequent developments generally argue that human moral actions are always shaped by artifacts (Latour, 2002; Introna, 2014), that artifacts possess a definite moral status (Fisher & Gamman, 2018; Kroes & Verbeek, 2014), and that artifacts should therefore bear moral responsibility. However, these analyses often overlook the gradients of agency. They do not clearly define different levels of material agency, and they fail to distinguish between three types of agency: (1) the agency of everyday artifacts, (2) human autonomous agency, and (3) machine agency in science-fiction or post-singularity contexts.

Without clear distinctions between these forms of agency, Actor-Network Theory (ANT) often faces the following criticisms. First, it may lead to functionalism or even behaviorism: the view that an entity qualifies as an agent simply because it performs functions or exhibits movement patterns analogous to those of an agent. Second, it risks falling into ontic relativism—treating different types of agents as ontologically identical, which in turn renders responsibility allocation arbitrary and meaningless. Third, it may succumb to anthropomorphism: equating material agents with humans, a stance that contradicts our everyday common sense.

Compared to ANT, the framework proposed in this paper offers three key advantages by differentiating between various types of agency. First, externalism addresses the criticism of functionalism or behaviorism leveled at ANT. This is because externalism defines agency based on an entity’s cognitive and control capacities in action (capacities derived not only from internal properties but also from external resources), rather than merely on its exhibition of certain behaviors. A puppet, for instance, may exhibit behaviors like humans, but it lacks both autonomous cognition and genuine control over action; thus, it can only be categorized as non-agent. Second, our framework avoids ontic relativism: by distinguishing between the agency of everyday machines (derivative or distributed agency) and that of machines in post-singularity or science-fictional contexts (autonomous agency), it enables a more rational assessment of the agency of different artifacts and the allocation of corresponding moral responsibility. Third, it evades the frequent criticism of anthropomorphism directed at ANT. By explicitly differentiating material agency from human autonomous agency, the framework preserves the basic common-sense understanding that humans are distinct from artifacts.

Balancing Neglect and Over-accountability

The distributed responsibility framework proposed here avoids two extremes. On the one hand, it does not ignore human responsibility. In today’s risk society, responses to responsibility gaps increasingly prioritize strict responsibility implementation—for example through taking retrospective moral answerability (Kiener, 2022, 2024) and vicarious responsibility grounded in moral entanglement (Goetze, 2022). Building on this trend, one potential criticism of the distributed responsibility framework is that it may overlook humans’ responsibility for designing and using artifacts (Johnson, 2006; Johnson & Noorman, 2014). However, this framework can strengthen the human sense of responsibility rather than weaken it. Traditionally, moral responsibility was thought to apply only to outcomes that one could directly predict and control. But the ideas of distributed and derivative agency, together with distributed responsibility, show us that no action is ever fully transparent or controllable for humans. Instead, these ideas empower us to courageously take responsibility in an uncertain world. Climate change offers a good example. It results from the complex interaction between humans and non-human systems. In the early stages of the Anthropocene, people could neither fully predict the consequences of carbon emissions nor control them. According to classical responsibility theory, this would mean humans bear no moral responsibility. But in the framework proposed here, climate change is shaped by human distributed agency and the Earth’s derivative agency. Therefore, humans must bear primary responsibility. This shows that distributed responsibility does not weaken human accountability. In fact, in some cases, it may even enhance it (Verbeek, 2014; cf.; Mitcham, 2014).

On the other hand, this framework can also avoid over-accountability for humans (and things) in some cases. As previously noted at the normative level, forgiveness is beneficial in certain situations; the following will elaborate on its benefits for technological decision-making and innovation from an instrumental perspective. For example, Danaher (2022) argues that when faced with complex decisions, responsibility gaps allow potential stakeholders to focus on making more accurate and long-term professional decisions without being overly concerned about the responsibilities they might bear. In my view, excessive accountability will lead to a phenomenon that could be called the reverse tragedy of the commons of responsibility: If every individual is held accountable for uncertain risks and chance events, two consequences may follow: first, no one will be willing to take risks—for fear of direct responsibility; second, individuals may even discourage others from taking risks—due to the potential for joint liability. Addressing existing societal risks has always required innovation—both technological and institutional. For example, to tackle climate change, we may need geoengineering. Yet al.l innovation inherently carries potential risks, such as the risk of R&D failure and the risk of harm from application. Thus, a certain degree of risk acceptance is necessary. However, the reverse tragedy of the commons of responsibility would render technological innovation impossible. Forgiveness, however, can prevent this reverse tragedy. By refraining from excessive blame, we create an environment where technological innovation is not stifled by the fear of responsibility. In particular, allowing for forgiveness in cases where responsibility gaps arise can facilitate the development and application of emerging technologies. In this way, forgiveness serves not only as a way to resolve moral dilemmas but also as a safeguard for humanity’s innovation systems.

Implementing Accountability for Artifacts

This framework also translates the idea of holding artifacts accountable into practical action, preventing it from becoming an abstract and empty conceptual game. Many existing proposals have mentioned holding artifacts accountable as a feasible solution. Floridi and Sanders (2004) once drew an analogy between human punishment mechanisms and the governance methods for artificial agents: in human society, rule-breaking behaviors may be met with monitoring and reform, isolation, and even the death penalty as punishment; for artificial agents, these correspond to system monitoring and maintenance (reform), isolation from Cyberspace (removal from normal operations), and complete deletion (destruction without backup). Similarly, Hanson (2009) proposed a strategy he calls “disassembling the alliance.” In his example, when a driver commits a DUI offense, revoking their driver’s license effectively breaks the alliance between the driver and the car.

However, the previous solutions mostly target the tokens of artificial agents, rather than the types of technological artifacts. Yet in society, technologies are replicable and widely deployed, and many of their ethical risks arise not from one failed situation, but from how an entire class of technologies operates and spreads. As a result, punishing individual tokens—through monitoring, isolation, or deletion—cannot fully address the structural problems at the type level. In this sense, what truly needs to be regulated and reprimanded is not a single failed program or device, but the technical paradigm itself, and the social logic behind its deployment. This is the idea of punishment for technological lineage. If a particular type of technology consistently causes unacceptable outcomes, we must go beyond regulating single devices. It becomes necessary to restrict—or even halt—the evolution and spread of that entire class of technology. In this way, punishment becomes not just a technical fix, but a form of historical and institutional governance that shapes how technologies are reproduced and evolve over time.

Conclusion

Human action is rarely isolated; it is mostly AIA in reality. Technological artifacts have always supported, constrained, and transformed human action. In an age of automation, it has become increasingly difficult for humans to fully perceive and control the outcomes of their actions. Meanwhile, artifacts are gaining more agency within AIA. If we continue to treat humans as the only agents and bearers of responsibility, responsibility gaps will emerge, threatening social justice. This paper argues that to fill these responsibility gaps, we must incorporate artifacts into the network of action and responsibility, treating them as agents and bearers of responsibility.

Although the idea of artifact agency is not new, meaningful discussions on how to hold artifacts accountable are still limited. This paper advances work in these two areas, providing preliminary answers to two questions: What is the agency of the artifact? And how to hold artifacts accountable? First, it proposes a gradient model of agency to situate and detail the types of artifact agency. Second, based on this model, it develops a distributed responsibility system to clarify accountability strategies for humans and artifacts across different scenarios. This framework expands the boundaries of morality to encompass not only humans, but also the various things that play an active role in our world.
