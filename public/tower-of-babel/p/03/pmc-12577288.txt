Global status and attributable risk factors of esophageal, stomach, colorectal cancers in adolescents and young adults: global burden of disease 2021 study analysis
Authors: Huanyu Wei, Lei Yuan, Yanxia Li, Yutong Xia, Hongli Miao, Xinrui Xu, Linfei Li, Lei Shen, Mengyao Ji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsDigestive tract cancers, encompassing esophageal, stomach, colorectal cancers, account for about 20% of all cancer cases worldwide. This study seeks to examine the global epidemiological trends of these cancers in adolescents and young adults (AYA) from 1990 to 2021, as well as the related risk factors, to offer valuable insights for the formulation of health policies and the efficient distribution of public health resources.MethodsData from the Global Burden of Disease 2021 (GBD 2021) were utilized to conduct a comprehensive assessment of the global incidence, death, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) of digestive tract cancers within the AYA population. The correlation between cancer burden and socioeconomic development was examined by Socio-Demographic Index (SDI) regions. Use comparative risk assessment from GBD to evaluate risk factors for cancer deaths and DALYs. Assess incidence trends with BAPC model.ResultsThe global burden of digestive tract cancer increased at different rates from 1990 to 2021. In 2021, colorectal cancer in AYA accounted for 70,201 incidences, 26,222 deaths, and 1,538,837 DALYs worldwide. In contrast, esophageal and stomach cancer had lower burdens in 8,164 and 42,039 incidences, respectively. While the incidence and death rates of esophageal and stomach cancers have declined, colorectal cancer have shown a significant increase in AYA. The age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) of colorectal cancer correlates positively with SDI. In AYA, level 2 risk factors for colorectal cancer include alcohol use, dietary risks, high body mass index, high fasting plasma glucose, low physical activity, and tobacco. For esophageal cancer, the risk factors are alcohol use, dietary risks, and tobacco, and for stomach are dietary risks and tobacco. By 2040, the ASIR of the three digestive tract cancers in AYA will all decrease according to the BAPC model prediction.ConclusionThe study of digestive tract cancers in AYA serves to identify risk factors within the realm of public health, facilitate the rational allocation of medical resources. Moreover, it can attend to the physical and mental health requirements of AYA individuals and enhance the cognitive framework regarding cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14880-9.Keywords: Esophageal cancer, Stomach cancer, Colorectal cancer, Socio-demographic index, Epidemiology

Introduction

According to the latest estimates from the World Health Organization (WHO), colorectal cancers rank as the third most prevalent cancer by incidence and the second most prevalent by death, accounting for 9.6% of all new cases in 2022 [1]. Stomach cancer is the fifth most commonly diagnosed cancer (6.8%), followed by esophageal cancer (4.6%) [1]. Collectively, these three digestive tract cancers represent 20% of all cancer cases globally. Notably, the epidemiological patterns of cancer incidence and death exhibit significant geographical and temporal variations [2]. In regions with high Human Development Index (HDI), the incidence and death rates for colorectal cancers are generally higher compared to those in low HDI regions [1]. Consequently, understanding the epidemiological characteristics of these cancers is crucial for assessing their public health impact across diverse populations.

The epidemiological trends of these three digestive tract cancers has been mentioned in other studies, the burden of colon rectal cancer and pancreatic cancer increased, while the burden of stomach cancer and liver cancer decreased [3]. Over the past three decades, the burden of all three cancers has increased in the whole population, correlating positively with socioeconomic development, thereby presenting substantial challenges for global cancer prevention and treatment [3, 4]. Numerous studies have focused on the widespread emergence of digestive tract cancers, predominantly investigating these diseases across all age groups. In 2021, new incidence cases of colorectal cancer in AYA were as high as 70,201, causing 26,222 deaths. It is particularly noteworthy that the increasing incidence of early-onset esophageal, stomach, colorectal cancers in AYA has emerged as an important area of recent research [5–7].

The GBD 2021 database, updated post-COVID-19 pandemic, provides new insights as the pandemic has had a profound impact on global public health resources. As such, the analysis of data from the 2021 database may yield different findings compared to previous years [8, 9]. The GBD 2021 database encompasses comprehensive epidemiological data on 371 diseases and 88 attributable risk factors across 204 countries and regions, spanning from 1990 to 2021 [10]. In this study, we leverage the most recent GBD 2021 database to explore global and regional epidemiological trends of digestive tract cancers—esophageal, stomach, colorectal cancers—among adolescents and young adults. This study aims to investigate the global distribution and temporal regional trends of these diseases in the AYA population, alongside a risk factor analysis, to inform the development of targeted prevention and control strategies.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

For our analysis, we utilized data from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 (GBD 2021) database, which encompasses global information on 371 diseases and injuries, sourced from a variety of methods, including epidemiological surveys, disease surveillance systems, cancer registries, and more. The methodologies for data collection and processing have been described in previous publication [11]. The data for our study includes the number of incident cases, deaths, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) for esophageal, stomach, colorectal cancers from 1990 to 2021, along with their corresponding age-standardized rates (ASRs). These data were extracted using the GBD visualization platform (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/).

To assess the socioeconomic development levels across regions, the SDI was employed. The SDI ranges from 0 to 1 and is calculated as the geometric mean of three key indicators: fertility rate, mean years of education, and per capita income. As a composite indicator of determinants of health structure, SDI is critical to understanding the environmental and socioeconomic risk factors for digestive system cancers in AYA. We examined the correlation between disease burden and SDI to determine the context of the distribution of risk factors. Based on SDI values, countries and territories are classified into five categories: low, low-middle, middle, high-middle, and high SDI. These classification data are provided by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation and cover the period from 1990 to 2021.

Meanwhile, the GBD 2021 database provides a comprehensive assessment of the specific effects of risk factor exposure, and this study summarizes the level 2 risk factors associated with digestive tract cancer: (1)Esophageal cancer: Alcohol use, Dietary risks, Tobacco; (2)Stomach cancer: Dietary risks, Tobacco; (3)Colorectal cancer: Alcohol use, Dietary risks, High body − mass index, High fasting plasma glucose, Low physical activity, Tobacco.

Statistical analysis

The GBD 2021 database uses statistical modeling techniques such as Cause of Death Ensemble Modeling (CODEm) and BayesianMeta-Regression Model (DisMod-MR2.1) to address incomplete or missing data across regions. In our study, we relied on GBD-derived estimates and uncertainty intervals, which already reflect corrections for data sparsity, especially in low-SDI regions. Furthermore, this study also employed the Bayesian Age -Period-Cohort (BAPC) model to project the ASIR of digestive tract cancers from 2022 to 2040.

Our study followed the demographic reporting standards recommended by the International Agency for Research on Cancer-the World Health Organization (IARC-WHO) [12, 13]. Three key indicators—incidences, deaths, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), all expressed as absolute numbers—were used to assess the global and regional disease burden of the three types of digestive tract cancers. ASRs, including ASIR, age-standardized mortality (ASDR) and age-standardized DALY (ASDALYR), are calculated based on the standard population of the GBD in accordance with the guidelines of the IARC-WHO. To evaluate temporal trends, the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) in ASRs per 100,000 population was calculated. Positive EAPC values with uncertainty intervals (UIs) above zero indicate an increasing trend, while negative values indicate a significant decrease [14]. A two-tailed P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In addition, we strictly defined the group of AYA aged 15 to 39 years, and all subgroup analyses were conducted with a consistent 5-year age interval. These standardization strategies ensured the comparability and repeatability of our findings across regions and over time.

In our study, we report the 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) provided by the GBD 2021 database for all estimates of incidence, death, and DALYs. The inclusion of 95% uncertainty intervals in our analysis enhances the transparency and robustness of our findings.

Results

Esophageal cancer

Between 1990 and 2021, the number of incident cases, deaths, and DALYs due to esophageal cancer in the AYA population was highest in middle SDI regions, with the lowest observed in both high SDI and low SDI countries (Fig. 1A). The global incidence of esophageal cancer in AYA decreased from 9,359 (95% UI 8252–10,441) to 8,164 (95% UI 7,401–8,961), and the ASIR decreased from 0.47 (95% UI 0.41–0.52) to 0.27 (95% UI 0.24–0.30) (EAPC = −1.06, 95%UI −1.38–0.74). While global incidence declined, a noticeable upward trend was observed in low SDI countries (EAPC = 2.06 95%UI 1.98–2.13), whereas a clear downward trend was seen in high-middle SDI countries (EAPC = −2.39). In 2021, East Asia recorded the highest number of incident cases in the AYA population, totaling 3,281 (95%UI 2,680–3,967) (Table 1). Among these, People's Republic of China reported the highest number of new cases (3,177 95%UI 2574–3866), followed by Republic of India (1,219, 95%UI 929–1,311) and Islamic Republic of Pakistan (323, 95%UI 213–405) (Fig. 2A, Table S1).

Stomach cancer

As the fifth largest cancer, the global incidence of stomach cancer in AYA has declined over the past 30 years, from 53,639 (95%UI 46,761–58,447) in 1990 to 42,040 (95%UI 35,999–47,271) in 2021 (EAPC = −1.06, 95%UI −1.24–0.87). In High-middle SDI regions we observed the highest ASIR (2.17, 95%UI 1.72–2.56) and in low-middle SDI countries the lowest ASIR (0.84, 95%UI 0.74–0.96), which is slightly different from esophageal cancer (Table 4). Among them, People's Republic of China, Republic of India and Republic of Indonesia had the highest number of cases, reporting 19,028 (95%UI 14,865–23,249), 3,661 (95%UI 3,182–4,491), and 1,093 (95%UI 1,045–1,047) cases, respectively (Fig. 3A). In high-middle SDI regions, ASIR, ASDALYR, and ASDR were all the highest compared to the other SDI regions. Although ASIR is not the lowest in high SDI regions, there is a clear decreasing trend in ASDR (Fig. 1B).

Colorectal cancer

From 1990 to 2021, the global number of incident cases of colorectal cancer in the AYA population increased from 41,384 (95%UI 37,653–44,500) to 70,201 (95%UI 64,050–76,855), with the ASIR rising from 2.03 (95%UI 1.84–2.20) to 2.29 (95%UI 2.07–2.71) (EAPC = 1.57, 95%UI 1.48–1.65). The APC of colorectal cancer incident in AYA population showed an increasing trend in different years, but fluctuated between 2000 and 2012, sometimes it increases and sometimes it reduces (Figure S3A, Table S6). The global incidence of colorectal cancer in AYA showed an overall increasing trend, with the most significant rise observed in low SDI regions (EAPC = 3.02, 95%UI 2.97–3.07) (Table 7). After 2015, the ASIR in high-middle SDI regions gradually exceeded that in high SDI regions, with the ASIR in high-middle SDI countries reaching 3.93 (95%UI 3.35–4.61) in 2021(Fig. 1B). East Asia recorded the highest number of incident colorectal cancer cases and the highest ASIR in AYA, with 29,326 (95%UI 24,051–35,501) cases and an ASIR of 5.13 (95%UI 4.19–6.25), respectively (Table 7). Among the 204 countries, People's Republic of China accounted for the largest number of new colorectal cancer cases in AYA in 2021 (28,516 95%UI 23,133–34,697), followed by the United States (4,829 95%UI 4,614–5,020) and Republic of India (4,074 95%UI 3,410–5310). Together, these three countries accounted for approximately 53.3% of all new AYA incidence cases globally (Fig. 4A, Table S1).

To investigate the impact of socioeconomic development, measured by the SDI, on the incidence and mortality trends of digestive tract cancers, we generated scatter plots to visualize the dynamics of SDI and ASRs (ASIR, ASDR, and ASDALYR) across 21 global regions over the past three decades.

In the case of esophageal cancer, the correlations between SDI and the ARSs of ASIR, ASDR, and ASDALYR in the AYA population were not consistently positive or negative. Specifically, when SDI was below 0.4 or above 0.6, ASRs showed a decreasing trend with higher SDI. Attributable risk factors

To systematically evaluate how socioeconomic development influences the global burden of digestive tract cancers, we employed the SDI as a key covariate in our risk factor analysis. The SDI serves as a composite indicator integrating income per capita, educational attainment, and fertility rates, making it an essential metric for disentangling the complex interplay between development and disease epidemiology.

Based on the GBD 2021 data, we analyzed the Level 2 attributable risk factors for esophageal cancer, stomach cancer, and colorectal cancer in the AYA population. Three major Level 2 risk factors were identified for esophageal cancer mortality: alcohol use, dietary risks, and tobacco. Among these, alcohol use emerged as the most significant contributor to esophageal cancer mortality in high-SDI regions. In regions with high-middle and middle SDI, tobacco use was the predominant factor contributing to esophageal cancer mortality. Conversely, dietary risks were the largest contributing factor in low-middle and low-SDI regions (Fig. 10A).

A Level 2 risk factors contributing to esophageal cancer related death in AYA. B Level 2 risk factors contributing to stomach cancer related death in AYA. C Level 2 risk factors contributing to colorectal cancer related death in AYA

For stomach cancer, dietary risks were the most important attributable risk factor, significantly contributing to stomach cancer deaths in AYA. However, in high-SDI and high-middle SDI regions, tobacco exposure also played a significant role in stomach cancer deaths (Fig. 10B).

For colorectal cancers, six Level 2 risk factors were identified: alcohol use, dietary risks, high body mass index (BMI), high fasting plasma glucose, low physical activity, and tobacco use. Among these, dietary risks were the most significant contributor to colorectal cancer mortality, both globally and across regions with different SDI levels. The contributions of alcohol use and high BMI to colorectal cancer mortality increased progressively with higher SDI levels (Fig. 10C).

Dietary risks emerged as a common Level 2 risk factor for all three cancers studied, with alcohol use and tobacco also being critical contributors. The contribution of these risk factors to cancer DALYs closely mirrored their impact on cancer mortality (Fig. 11A-C).

A Level 2 risk factors contributing to esophageal cancer related DALY in AYA. B Level 2 risk factors contributing to stomach cancer related DALY in AYA. C Level 2 risk factors contributing to colorectal cancer related DALY in AYA

GBD 2021 database focuses on quantifiable behavioral, metabolic and environmental risk factors and does not fully incorporate genetic factors. Environmental factors are present in the GBD database, but according to our analysis, they are less strongly associated with digestive tract cancer in AYA populations than tobacco and other factors. Although genetic and environmental factors were not emphasized in our study, we cannot ignore the role of these two factors in cancer development.

Prediction

Using data from 1990 to 2021, we applied the BAPC model to predict the ASIR of esophageal, stomach, colorectal cancers in the AYA population. The model projects a decline in the ASIR of these cancers in the AYA population over the next two decades. Since 1990, the ASIR of esophageal and stomach cancers in the AYA population has exhibited a downward trend (Fig. 12A-B). In contrast, the incidence of colorectal cancers in this population has increased over the 30-year period from 1990 to 2021. However, the BAPC model predicts a reversal of this trend, forecasting a decline in the ASIR of colorectal cancers over the next 20 years (Fig. 12C).

Discussion

In this study, we report on epidemiological trends and attributable risk factors for three major digestive tract cancers: esophageal cancer, stomach cancer, colorectal cancer. The key findings are as follows: First, colorectal cancer has the highest global burden in the AYA population, and over the past three decades, the incidence and mortality of esophageal and stomach cancer have decreased in the AYA population, and the incidence of colorectal cancer has significantly increased. Second, after considering population growth and population changes, ASIR for colorectal cancer increased significantly during these three decades, while ASDR and ASDALYR decreased, and ASIR, ASDR and ASDALYR for esophageal and stomach cancer all decreased. Third, ASIR in colorectal cancer showed a significant increasing trend with the increase of SDI. Forth, we found that the incidence of cancer was positively associated with age in the AYA population, highlighting the gradually increasing burden of digestive tract cancer with age, and the significantly higher incidence in men than in women. Fifth, we explored the Level 2 risk factors and found dietary risks is a common Level 2 risk factor for the three cancers. Finally, we predicted with the BAPC model that these three gastrointestinal cancers would continuously decrease in ASIR in the AYA population over the next two decades.

In the past three decades, there have been notable changes in the incidence and mortality of esophageal, gastric, and colorectal cancers among AYA people. For esophageal and stomach cancers, the incidence and mortality have slightly decreased. This is mainly attributed to several factors. Firstly, the diet has become more balanced, with less consumption of pickled and smoked foods rich in nitrites, and an increase in fresh fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and high—quality proteins, which enhance immunity [15–19]. Secondly, the public's awareness of health has improved, leading to a reduction in smoking and alcohol abuse, as well as better food hygiene. Thirdly, medical technology has advanced, with endoscopy enabling earlier diagnosis and multidisciplinary treatment (MDT) improving treatment outcomes. Finally, better public health conditions, such as improved water quality and stricter food hygiene supervision, have reduced exposure to carcinogens [20, 21].

However, the incidence of colorectal cancer has significantly increased. The reasons include the westernization of diet, with more intake of high—calorie, high—fat, high—protein, and low—fiber foods, which produce harmful substances in the intestine [22–24]. The sedentary lifestyle and lack of exercise slow down intestinal peristalsis, and frequent staying up late disrupts the body's metabolism and immune function. Moreover, the rising obesity rate, due to unhealthy diet and lack of exercise, promotes tumor growth through the secretion of certain cytokines and hormones [25–28]. Environmental pollutants and plastic additives may also contribute to the increased risk [29–31].

Despite the rising incidence, the mortality of colorectal cancer has decreased. This is because of the popularization of screening methods like fecal occult blood tests and colonoscopies, which allow for early detection [32]. Advanced treatment methods, such as minimally invasive surgeries, new chemotherapy drugs, targeted therapies, and immunotherapies, have improved treatment effects. The wide application of the multidisciplinary treatment model also helps to develop personalized treatment plans, thus extending the survival time of patients.

The observed differences in incidence and death rates of digestive tract cancers across regions can be attributed to a combination of cultural, economic, and healthcare system factors. In regions with higher incidence of esophageal and stomach cancer, such as East Asia and South Asia, traditional diets often include high consumption of pickled and smoked foods, which are rich in nitrites and other carcinogens. In contrast, regions with lower incidence, such as Western countries, have seen a shift towards healthier diets with increased consumption of fresh fruits and vegetables. The prevalence of smoking and alcohol consumption varies significantly across regions. In low and middle SDI regions, smoking and alcohol use remain high due to cultural acceptance and limited public health interventions. In contrast, high SDI regions have implemented stricter anti-smoking and alcohol control policies, leading to a decline in these behaviors [33, 34].

Regions with high SDI values often have better access to healthcare and public health infrastructure. This includes improved sanitation, better food safety regulations, and more effective cancer screening programs [35]. In contrast, low SDI regions may lack these resources, leading to higher cancer incidence and death rates. The increasing incidence of colon and rectal cancer in high SDI regions is partly attributed to the rising prevalence of obesity due to sedentary lifestyles and high-calorie diets. In contrast, low SDI regions may not yet have reached the same levels of obesity, but they are experiencing rapid changes in diet and lifestyle that could lead to future increases in cancer incidence.

The ASIR of digestive tract cancers do not have a simple linear relationship with the SDI. The ASIR fluctuates in a specific range, for example, when SDI is in the range of 0.4–0.6. We believe that this may be because the region is in the economic transition period at this stage, and the lifestyle changes rapidly, such as the popularization of fast food and the increase of unhealthy living habits, which offset some of the benefits brought by medical progress and hindered the downward trend of ASIR [36, 37].

We focused on level 2 risk factors for digestive tract cancer in AYA populations and found that alcohol use was an important risk factor for esophageal and colorectal cancer, and tobacco was an important risk factor for esophageal and stomach cancer. We recommend that legislation be passed to limit the number and density of alcohol and tobacco outlets in areas with high rates of digestive tract cancer among AYA, especially in areas where AYA congregate. At the same time, alcohol and tobacco taxes can be raised to raise their prices and reduce consumption. A total smoking ban should be implemented in public places such as schools and parks to reduce the exposure of young people to second-hand smoke. Law enforcement inspections of bars, restaurants and liquor stores should also be strengthened to ensure that alcohol is not sold to minors [38, 39]. Dietary risks is important risk factors for colorectal cancer, so we advocate for schools and communities to promote healthy eating programs that encourage increased intake of fruits, vegetables and whole grains, build more sports facilities, and encourage participation among AYA [40, 41]. This is also the original intention of our study on the global prevalence trend and related risk factors of digestive tract cancer in AYA population, which provides an important reference for the formulation of effective health policies.

There are several limitations when using the GBD database for AYA digestive tract cancer burden surveys. Firstly, the data issues include incomplete information from low SDI regions and uneven coverage, with developed countries being over-represented while developing regions and special groups are under-studied. Secondly, the GBD 2021 database primarily focuses on quantifiable behavioral, metabolic, and environmental risk factors, omitting genetic factors and potentially missing some clinically relevant factors in its analysis. Furthermore, while the BAPC model is a well-established method for analyzing long-term trends in epidemiological data, future work could explore alternative statistical approaches to further validate the robustness of our findings. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution when applied to specific populations and regions. Future research should aim to address these limitations by improving data quality, enhancing representation of under-studied groups, and incorporating a broader range of risk factors.

Conclusion

In this study, we draw the following conclusions: Colorectal cancer has the highest global burden. The incidence and mortality of esophageal and stomach cancer decreased, while that of colorectal cancer increased significantly. The ASIR of colorectal cancer increases with SDI. Dietary risks are a common Level 2 risk factor for the three cancers, and the incidence of these three cancers decreases in AYA in the future.

The global burden of esophageal cancer, stomach cancer, colorectal cancer is a pressing health issue of great significance. These cancers not only cause immense suffering and loss of life for countless individuals and their families but also impose a substantial economic burden on healthcare systems worldwide. Addressing this burden is crucial for improving public health outcomes, reducing health disparities, and alleviating the social and economic impact of these diseases. It is imperative that we take immediate action, including promoting preventive measures, enhancing early detection and screening programs, and improving access to effective treatment and care. By working together, we can make significant progress in combating these cancers and saving lives.
