Community health survey development, administration, social determinants and mental health in African immigrants and refugees in Portland Oregon
Authors: Vincent Chirimwami, Egide Dukuzumuremyi, Joy Mulumba, Noni Canavan, Mark Van Ryzin, Andrew W. Bergen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCommunity-based organizations interested in members’ health may lack data to develop culturally-appropriate, evidence-based interventions. Community leaders perceived the need for a health survey, developed and administered in partnership with research scientists, to address mental health concerns of black African immigrants and refugees (principally depression).ObjectivesThe goals of the project were: development of a community health survey for African immigrant and refugee populations; translation, piloting and administering the survey, and descriptive analyses and dissemination. Consultation with a community Advisory Board throughout the study was an overarching goal.MethodsA working partnership between members of the African Family Holistic Health Organization, including an Advisory Board, and members of Oregon Research Institute was formed. Recommended protocols to measure demographics, social determinants of health, everyday discrimination, perceived stress, mental health and substance use, including combustible tobacco use, were included after multiple consultations with the Advisory Board. The survey was translated and piloted in multiple African languages. Survey participants were recruited individually and at community-based organization events and completed the survey. Descriptive and bivariate analyses and dissemination to multiple groups was performed.ResultsThe survey (Demographics and Disability, Social Determinants of Health, Substance Use), was administered electronically and completed by N = 385 (73% ≤40 years, 61% female) participants in the 2nd quarter of 2023. Participant symptoms of anxiety and depression were elevated relative to US 2022 National Health Interview Survey data (overall and compared to individuals identifying as non-Hispanic Black), due to increased reporting of mild symptoms, and in participants who were younger, food insecure, English proficient or with greater levels of perceived discrimination or stress. Measures of ever smoking were low (even a puff, 11%), with greater ever smoking observed in participants who were male, with some college, English proficient, or with greater levels of perceived discrimination or stress.ConclusionsSurvey development and administration in multiple languages benefited from validated tools, repeated consultations of the research team with Advisory Board members, and community-based organization staff contributions. Community concerns about mental health appear justified, though increased symptoms were mild and subclinical. While absolute levels of ever smoking are low, tobacco use associations suggest opportunities for prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24599-8.

Introduction

Community-based health research is a translational paradigm where community-based organizations and researchers engage with each other in collaborative inquiry, intervention and implementation in a way that responds to the needs of both groups [1]. This is especially relevant when the community is from a group previously under-represented in research, with less access to health resources, and who may be subject to societal and/or research marginalization through structural racism and racial stigma [2]. Community-based organizations prefer researchers to have an understanding of the community’s values [3], and to engage with the community before data is collected from community participants and the research aims are completed [4].

Demographics and social determinants of health (SDoH) influence health-related quality of life, physical and mental health conditions, and access to health care [5–8]. E.g., age, sex, race, ethnicity, education, family income and urbanization are associated with anxiety and depressive symptoms, where individuals who are younger, female, self-identify as Black non-Hispanic or as White non-Hispanic, or have lower levels of education, income and urbanicity are more likely to experience symptoms, than individuals who are older, male, self-identify as Hispanic and Asian non-Hispanic, or have higher levels of education, income and urbanicity [9]. The best known associations of individual SDoH and tobacco-related health disparities in the US population are those of education and poverty with smoking prevalence [10]. Employment status, health insurance status, race, sex, and urbanicity are additional correlates of tobacco related health disparities [11]. The Social Vulnerability Index (four sub-indices consisting of socioeconomic, transportation, minoritized group, and household type and density measures collected by the US Census [12]) remains associated with smoking prevalence after adjustment for individual SDoH [13].

Research on perceived discrimination highlights the disproportionate burden faced by non-Hispanic Black individuals in the United States. In the 2001–2003 Collaborative Psychiatric Epidemiology Surveys, Kim et al. reported mean scores for perceived discrimination of 19.82 (SD = 8.12) for non-Hispanic Black individuals and 17.12 (SD = 6.78) for non-Hispanic Whites using the nine item Everyday Discrimination Scale [14, 15]. This study also confirmed, in general, the measurement equivalence of the Everyday Discrimination Scale across race and ethnicity groups. In the Nashville Stress and Health Study, everyday discrimination was associated with higher levels of anxiety and depressive symptoms [16, 17]. Research focusing on African immigrants and refugees in the US is more limited. However, examining discrimination among African immigrants, Saasa found that increased perceived discrimination was associated with higher levels of social exclusion across multiple dimensions [18]. This suggests that African immigrants and refugees may experience higher levels of discrimination, associated stress and physical and mental health outcomes compared to the general U.S. population, as has been observed in the African American population [14, 19]. E.g., frequent everyday discrimination was associated with increased cardiovascular risk (even after adjusting for other cardiovascular risk factors such as older age and lower educational attainment) in a community cohort of sub-Saharan Black immigrants recruited in Washington DC [20]. Higher levels of perceived stress [21] in the Africans in America cohort were associated with reduced sleep quality and lack of health insurance [22]. These findings highlight the importance of considering racial and immigrant status when assessing experiences of discrimination and stress, as these factors can significantly influence physical and mental health outcomes [19].

What do we know about the prevalence of mental health and tobacco use in African ancestry immigrants and US-born African Americans? In the largest review of studies of health disparities between US-born and non-US-born Black participants (N = 208 studies published between 1985 and 2022), in general, most (~ 80%) of studies report that non-US-born Black participants have better health than US-born Black participants, where most (~ 70%) of studies focused on physical health outcomes [23]. Nevertheless, mental health outcomes constituted the largest single group of studies (n = 40), where n = 26 used diagnostic interview data, one used medical chart review data, and the remainder used validated self-report scale data), while substance use outcomes consisted of 24 studies [23]; most studies used data from the National Survey of American life, a nationally representative survey including African Americans, Afro-Caribbean Americans and Non-Hispanic Whites [24]. No clear pattern of mental health disparities across the range of mental health outcomes studied was observed between US-born and non-US-born Black groups, however, for the most commonly studied outcome (depressive symptoms), non-US-born Blacks reported lower or similar levels than US-born Blacks [23]. Note that in the US population, Black and White non-Hispanic groups report higher prevalence of anxiety and depressive symptoms than do Asian or Hispanic groups [9]. The authors emphasized that the cultural validity of mental health measures required examination and called for more research to understand construct validity and nativity differences in mental health outcomes. For substance use or use disorders (marijuana use, 16 studies; alcohol use, 8 studies; and, tobacco use, 4 studies focused on cancer or maternal health risks), prevalence was lower in the adult non-US-born Black population than the adult US-born Black population [23]. For tobacco use in particular, smoking prevalence was higher in US-born participants and the expected education and income gradients were observed only in US-born participants [25]. Further, Black participants born in the US or Europe were more likely to be current smokers of cigarettes or cigars than Black participants who were immigrants from Africa or the West Indies [26]. In the general population, Black and White non-Hispanic groups report higher prevalence of combustible tobacco product use and cigarette smoking than do Asian or Hispanic groups [27].

The African Family Holistic Health Organization (AFHHO) is an immigrant and refugee-led non-profit community organization grounded in a vision of a collective caring and economically thriving Sub-Saharan African community. AFHHO’s mission is to build healthier lives for Portland’s African Diaspora communities through a transformative, relationship-centered, and holistic approach to health. AFHHO partners with communities to identify unique needs, foster resilience, and create a foundation for healthier families and stronger communities. The most prevalent community health concern was mental health, principally depression, although AFHHO leadership is also aware of concerns regarding tobacco use. Prior African immigrant and refugee community-based participatory research in Portland, Oregon identified stress, health care access, and health care provider discrimination as American life and health care challenges [28]. AFHHO leadership desired to understand whether community health data indicated a need for culturally compatible provision of mental health services, and, AFHHO and Oregon Research Institute (ORI) affiliated scientists were interested in the possible influence of SDoH on mental health. The objectives of the study were to collect, analyze and disseminate selected demographics, SDoH and mental health data and relations via a community survey developed in a community-researcher partnership. Lessons learned during community engagement, survey development, administration, and analysis may be relevant to other refugee and immigrant organizations and their partners. Study results provide data on mental health and combustible commercial tobacco product use in this African refugee and immigrant community and identify opportunities for prevention and treatment.

Methods

Partnership, survey development and ethical approval

The AFHHO ORI partnership

The partnership formed via interaction of the first author with AFHHO leadership and members and ORI scientists. As a member of the AFHHO’s Men Wellness and COVID-19 educational groups since 2018 and as a member of ORI’s Health Equity Research Group since 2020, he was aware of general health concerns of AFHHO members, which focused on mental health and potential future drug use in younger members, and ORI colleagues’ interest in addressing health disparities in Oregon. The opportunity to begin assessing potential health disparities in the AFHHO membership, an interest of ORI colleagues, coalesced after discussions with the Executive Director of AFHHO and ORI colleagues around plans to develop, administer, analyze and disseminate results of a community health survey; this led to the submission of a proposal by the AFHHO and ORI Community and Engagement Services (ORI-CES). The 2021–2023 focus of the Oregon Health Authority (OHA) Tobacco Prevention and Education Program on community-based organizations and tobacco cessation and prevention was an attractive focus given ORI’s experience studying tobacco use behaviors and developing novel tobacco use treatments. We agreed ORI researchers would obtain ethical approvals, design the survey, analyze the data, provide the AFHHO with the final report for dissemination, and pursue further dissemination; the AFHHO would provide administrative and governance support for survey development and administer the survey.

Advisory board

AFHHO established an Advisory Board (Board) of ten community members (Board members) to provide oversight and guidance [29] to promote a shared vision for addressing the health needs of the African refugee and immigrant community [30]. The Board, composed of community representatives, included five young adults, three adult women, and two adult men, ensuring diverse perspectives. Each board member brought prior experience with AFHHO ranging from three to six years. Board members consulted with ORI researchers and AFHHO leadership at each step of the project to review and discuss project progress, challenges, and future plans.

Survey development team

The survey development team (team) consisted of four members of ORI, including one affiliated with the AFHHO and with ORI, with a range of prior experience in health research including measurement, analysis, prevention and treatment of mental health and substance use. The team had specific expertise in refugee community communication, educational equity, behavioral and substance use assessment, statistical analysis methodologies, computer assisted survey coding and development, and formal dissemination of analysis findings. The team and Board planned to meet with each other at each milestone in survey development, administration, analysis and dissemination.

Survey development

The team met virtually to select, discuss and finalize the inclusion of measures and worked collaboratively via Google Docs to edit, document and revise survey questions, while survey coding, logic and survey text and changes used Qualtrix, with survey versions in Word also used for review by the team and Board members. Initial survey development discussions among the team included the need to balance the number of measures necessary to cover the social determinants and health data collection needs of the study, the broad cultural diversity of the Black African immigrant and refugee membership, and practical concerns about participant burden and survey administration. Discussion led the team to focus on commonly used validated instruments, including those from the PhenX resource [31]. Measures were proposed by team members, discussed and adopted using consensus. The survey measures were presented to Board members twice at approximately three month intervals of active development for review and feedback.

Survey team and board consultations

At the initial meeting, the team and Board members discussed the opportunity to conduct a survey to understand the health needs of the community and to provide context for targeted interventions to address refugee and immigrant health challenges. At the following meeting, proposed measures were discussed. Board members and ORI researchers met one more time after translation of the survey and then Board members took the draft survey online. At each of these last two meetings, Board members provided critical recommendations on the content and proposed administration of the survey, and ORI researchers incorporated specific suggestions to improve the survey. ORI researchers met with Board members multiple times during data analysis to obtain feedback and before dissemination to the ORI Health Equity Research Group, OHA program officers, and to the National Cancer Institute’s Cancer Center Cessation Initiative Consortium.

Meetings between the partners on survey development focused on likely community member sensitivity to issues of privacy, identity and identifiability, as well as expected lexical differences between African ethnic groups, e.g., regarding terms of measurement. This led the partners to modify the initial set of demographic questions by using ranges instead of asking for exact numbers of years, and to drop questions about height and weight due to concerns about translation and generalizability. Concerns were raised about the potential intrusiveness of questions about disability and gender identity; the existence of OHA standards for questions in these areas helped the partners include such questions. Concerns about the sensitivity of participants in response to socioeconomic measures which would have required multiple questions about family size, family income, and residence characteristics led ORI researchers to drop socioeconomic measures. We discussed including a small area deprivation index as a proxy for participant socioeconomics, however, lack of participant residence data and a reluctance to collect that information rendered this option moot. In contrast, questions on tobacco use were praised by Board members for the coverage of this health behavior.

Ethical approval

We presented a draft of the Informed Consent to the Board based on the ORI Minimal Risk Consent, revised to reflect the context and goals of the health survey. Members of the Board discussed and revised the language to protect the anonymity and confidentiality of participants. The revised Informed Consent was reviewed by the team, and the survey and Informed Consent were submitted to the ORI Institutional Review Board (IRB) for review; the survey and Informed Consent received expedited approval. Subsequent to expedited approval, the team discussed how to protect personal health information by passing anonymized IDs between the recruiting agency and ORI-CES. The Informed Consent was revised to reflect this anonymized code transfer between the recruiting agency and ORI-CES, and the survey was revised to improve the coding of questions related to health behaviors. Both Informed Consent and the survey were resubmitted for review to the ORI IRB under a Protocol Modification, and after a final revision of the consent, the survey and Informed Consent received approval in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Survey measures

Proposed survey measures and consultations

Measures were proposed by individual members of the survey development team, endorsed by other team members in a consensus process, and then reviewed with Board members. Consultation on measures and the survey included discussions regarding reasons for being proposed, concerns regarding cultural generalizability or participant sensitivity, availability of participant, some of which resulted in modifications or exclusion of some proposed measures or questions. See Electronic Supplementary Material for details on measures.

Social determinants of mental health framework

The theoretical foundation underlying the social determinants of mental health reflects a shift from an individual-centric to a structural and relational understanding of mental health disparities [32]. While the social determinants of health (SDH) broadly refer to the conditions in which individuals are born, live, work, and age [33], scholars have argued that the social determinants of mental health (SDMentH) require a distinct conceptualization that foregrounds perception, appraisal, and indirect causal pathways [34]. Handerer et al [35] emphasize that, unlike SDH—which often operates through direct mechanisms such as environmental exposure or physical deprivation—SDMentH is primarily mediated through psychological processes shaped by individual interpretation, social norms, and cultural context. This distinction supports an epistemological framework in which mental health outcomes emerge from the interaction between structural factors and subjective experiences, highlighting the need for multilevel, multifactorial models. In parallel, Cairney et al [36] applied intersectionality theory to demonstrate how overlapping social positions, such as gender, income, and marital status, interact in complex, nonlinear ways to influence mental healthcare utilization. Their findings underscore that single-axis variables do not adequately explain structural inequities in mental health; instead, they must be understood through dynamic constellations of disadvantages. Together, these perspectives form a robust theoretical basis for conceptualizing SDMentH as an emergent phenomenon resulting from both macro- and individual-level appraisal processes [35, 36].

Demographics and disability

With critical input from Board members, the survey provided ranges for participants to answer questions about age (age strata were 18 to 26, 27 to 40, 41 to 64, and ≥65), and years living in the US. After discussion about how to ask questions about sex and gender identity in a culturally appropriate manner, we included questions from draft OHA Sex and Gender Identity (SOGI) recommendations. After discussion, we revised the question soliciting an unstructured response regarding “race, ethnicity, tribal affiliation, country of origin, or ancestry” included in Oregon’s implementation [37] of Federal data collection standards [38] for Race, Ethnicity, Language And Disability status (REALD) to retain “ethnicity”, and added language to prompt members whose self-identified ethnicity includes more than one ethnicity to include all self-identified ethnicities. We included the REALD “language(s) used at home” question in the survey’s Demographics section, and the English proficiency question in the survey’s SDoH section. We included all disability questions [39] from the Race, Ethnicity, Language, and Disability form (MSC 0074 rev. 12/2020) from the Equity and Inclusion Division, Oregon Health Authority, Oregon Department of Human Services [37], and added a question soliciting an unstructured response regarding other disabilities at the end of the Demographics Section. Definition of a disability includes the presence of a disability, description of a functional limitation, or the interaction between the individual with a disability and their environment [40]. Our coding of disability followed the approach used by Cornelius et al in their survey of tobacco product use in adults in the US [27]. I.e., for our analysis, those who responded “No” to all nine questions were coded as not living with a disability. We also include questions regarding participant length of residence, marital status, and African region of origin.

SDoH

We included questions regarding individual and interpersonal levels of influence including the domains of behavior and the physical/built environment (e.g., employment, occupation, physical activity, education), the sociocultural environment (e.g., English proficiency, food insecurity), health care (insurance coverage, access and utilization), and measures of perceived discrimination [14] and stress [21]. See Electronic Supplementary Material for details on SDoH measures.

Mental health

We included two mental health questionnaires, i.e., screening measures to be used in non-psychiatric care settings that measure anxiety symptoms [41] and depressive symptoms [42]. Anxiety symptoms were measured using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7 (GAD-7), which contains seven items and stratifies scores of 0-4, 5-9, 10-14 and 15-21 into minimal or none, mild, moderate and severe anxiety symptoms, respectively; 89% of patients with a structured clinical interview diagnosis of GAD had GAD-7 scores ≥10 and 82% of patients without a diagnosis of GAD had scores <10 [41] (see Electronic Supplemental Material for GAD-7 questions and [41] for more details on GAD-7 validation). The GAD-7 has been evaluated for differential item functioning or item bias in South African White and African working adults using nested logistic regression (trait level, group membership and interaction) and other tests [43]. Two questions (GAD-7 questions 1 and 3) showed differential item functioning by model likelihood ratio test significance; however, changes in item functioning were considered to be negligible or minor for both questions and all estimated parameters [43].

Depressive symptoms were measured using the Patient Health Questionnaire 8 (PHQ-8), which contains eight items (Electronic Supplementary Material) and stratifies scores of 0-4, 5-9, 10-14 and 15-19 and 20-24 into none or minimal, mild, moderate, moderately severe and severe depressive symptoms, respectively [42, 44]. The PHQ-8 was derived from the PHQ-9 due to the very low endorsement of the ninth question (suicidality/self-harm) and the inability to provide adequate intervention when used as a screening questionnaire [45]. A PHQ-8 score ≥10 has excellent sensitivity and specificity for major depressive disorder (MDD) [45] and in a large nationally representative study, 96.4% of respondents with PHQ-8 scores ≥10 had a diagnosis of MDD, other depressive disorder or anhedonia [42]. In Africa, the PHQ-9 has been used most often in Ugandan populations, where a systematic review found 51 studies, five involving refugees; in one study the Cronbach α was calculated as 0.95, perhaps due to the high levels of depressive symptoms [46].

Substance use

The last section of the survey asked questions about tobacco, alcohol and cannabis use. Tobacco questions were drawn from the Population Assessment of Tobacco and Health [47], and the Tobacco Use Supplement of the Current Population Survey [48] (see Electronic Supplementary Material). Integrating tobacco behaviors from multiple surveys into questions regarding age of initiation, frequency and quantity required novel skip logic coding. Questions about knowledge of and use of other commercial tobacco products completed the tobacco subsection. For information on questions about alcohol and cannabis use, quantity and frequency, see the Electronic Supplementary Material.

Survey administration and analysis

Survey translation

The cultural diversity of the AFFHO and other immigrant and refugee organizations is reflected in the number of African and European languages that are used at home by the membership. In order to reach members whose regular language is not English, the Informed Consent and survey were translated into seven African languages (Swahili, Somali, Amharic, Tigrinya, Kirundi, Kirwanda, Oromo) by The Spanish Group (Irvine, CA). The final English survey text and keeping items in the same order as the English version were important to code the multiple language versions of the survey. Board members and members of the African community piloted all language versions of the survey before development was considered complete.

Participant recruitment

We utilized three principal modes of recruitment: a AFHHO community outreach specialist (COS) sent the IRB-approved recruitment text via email including an online link to the survey to community members in the AFHHO clients’ database; community organization leaders contacted their respective members and send the recruitment text via multiple channels; and, community-based organizations, including the Center for African Immigrants and Refugees Organization (CAIRO), invited community members through multiple channels (word of mouth, WhatsApp, text message, and social media (Facebook and Instagram) to come to their offices for a survey-taking event. CAIRO’s mission is to advocate for and increase equity by providing equitable opportunities through programs, services, and community organizing for African immigrant and refugee families to thrive. At the survey-taking event, case managers provided participants with the link to the survey that they took using the organization’s computers or participants’ cell phones. Case managers also assisted participants who needed help with taking the survey. Specific language in the Informed Consent specified that if a participant received help with completing the survey, the survey participant would remain anonymous to the investigating team and the person who helped the participant would maintain confidentiality. We partnered with other community-based organizations who invited members of their community to such events.

The recruitment process started in March 2023 and ended on July 31, 2023. Most community members responded favorably when the COS contacted them directly. Contact by community organization leaders via WhatsApp, text message, or email solicited a response from those who did not respond to the COS contacts. The community event was found to be the most effective recruitment method [49].

Survey administration and informed consent

All participants logged onto the survey via a url provided by community organization staff and provided their informed consent digitally. To provide informed consent and access the online survey, a participant had to enter a code number that a COS assigned to them. We used ORI’s licensed Qualtrics data collection systems to collect responses to survey questions.

Statistical analysis

After data were collected, we examined the reliability of multiple item measurement scales and distributions of key outcomes (e.g., anxiety and depressive symptoms, and substance use). We evaluated the relations between selected SDoH and key outcomes. Selected SDoH were (dichotomous coding): age (≤40, 41+); sex (female, male); disability (below); marital status (married versus divorced, widowed, separated, never married, and a member of a non-married couple); highest level of education (either some college, college graduate, some graduate school, or advanced degree versus grade school, some high school and high school or GED); current employment status (either working now versus temporarily laid off, sick leave, or maternity leave,looking for work, unemployed, retired, disabled, permanently or temporarily, keeping house, and student); health insurance (covered, not covered); food insecurity (In the last 12 months, how often did you and/or other people in your household ever cut the size of your meals or skip meals because there wasn’t enough money for food: almost every month, some months but not every month or only 1 or 2 months versus not at all); English proficiency (Would you say you speak English: very well or well versus not well, not at all); everyday discrimination (< median versus > median); and, perceived stress (< median versus ≥ median).

For analysis of demographics and SDoH and symptoms of mental health, we used the chi-square test; for the Discussion, we also calculated relative risks. For analysis of demographics, SDoH and cigarette smoking behaviors, we utilized the Fisher Exact test. To compare levels of anxiety or depressive symptom categories in AFHHO-ORI survey respondents to those measured in US adults [50], we utilized the IPUMS 2022 NHIS database and online analysis software [51] to extract and tabulate counts of: US adults and US Non-Hispanic Black (NHB) adults with none/minimal, mild or moderate & severe anxiety (GAD-7 scores of 0-4, 5-9, 10-21) or depressive (PHQ-8 scores of 0-4, 5-9, 10-24) symptoms, without and with person-weights, stratified by age (18-26, 27-40, 41-64 and 65+) and used proportional fitting to age-adjust AFHHO-ORI mental health symptom category data to US and US NHB adult age strata proportions. We performed three comparisons of mental health symptom categories between US and US NHB versus AFHHO-ORI data: unadjusted, AFHHO-ORI age-adjusted, and both NHIS person-weighted and AFHHO-ORI age-adjusted comparisons; we evaluated differences by chi-square.

Discussion

The goals of the study were to foster a partnership between a community of African immigrants and refugees and a group of researchers, develop and pilot a health survey using validated measures, invite Black African immigrant and refugee community-based organization members to complete the survey, and to perform and disseminate initial analyses to Board members and to relevant external groups. Our approach involved multiple consultations between community members represented by an Advisory Board and the survey research team to: propose, explore, and agree upon data collection measures and analysis; review results and interpretation; and, agree on dissemination to community and scientific stakeholders. The study enabled community members to lead (first author), shape (Board members) and conduct (AFHHO staff) their first externally funded public health project. The primary finding was that AFHHO-ORI survey respondents endorsed mild, i.e., subclinical, mental health symptoms at a higher level than observed in the 2022 US or US NHB populations.

During accomplishment of these goals, lessons were learned that may assist others engaged in community-based participatory research with African immigrants and refugees. The primary lesson learned is the importance of consultation with members of the community to be surveyed. For example, cultural, lexical and privacy concerns were raised in our consultations prior to survey administration and influenced whether and how the survey asked questions as diverse as gender identity, anthropometrics, years post-migration, and family income. Our main finding of elevated mild anxiety and depressive symptoms led to discussion of cultural and lexical issues around mental health measures with community members, and to more detailed examination of mental health symptom data in national and other African immigrant and refugee datasets for further insight.

Community mental health symptom levels and differences with US population levels were discussed with Board members; discussion focused on lexical and cultural issues. Board members pointed out that there are no words in Swahili or Somali for mental health or for anxiety, instead, the concept associated with “worry” is closer to “fear that something will happen to themselves or another person”, i.e., similar to the seventh GAD-7 question, but focused on another person. If respondents were concerned about the possibility of harm occurring to themselves or to another person, this might have led to increased scores in more than one GAD-7 question and might account for some elevation of anxiety symptoms. I.e., community levels of mental health symptoms may be due to cultural differences in interpreting questions about feelings, or, reluctance to report feelings associated with stigmatized behaviors [52]. Concern for others resulting in elevated mild anxiety symptoms might explain the slight but non-significantly greater elevation of mild anxiety symptoms compared to mild depressive symptoms within respondents compared to 2022 US and NHB populations, i.e., it may have been somewhat protective towards the development of depressive symptoms. Qualitative cognitive interviews with community respondents to assess health literacy and understanding of survey questions might help interpret these responses [53].

When compared with 2022 NHIS data [50, 51], we observed a significantly higher prevalence of mild anxiety & depressive symptoms in AFHHO-ORI survey respondents. The mild symptom category remained significantly more common among respondents than in either US or US NHB populations after adjustment of AFHHO-ORI respondent data to NHIS age proportions or age adjustment and use of NHIS design weights. Two nested studies of African immigrants from five West African countries living in the Baltimore-Washington DC area (the African Immigrant Health Study, AIHS) have administered the PHQ-8: in N=317 participants (mean (SD) age 47 (11) years and 60% female), 74.2%, 16.8% and 8.8% endorsed minimal or no, mild or moderate to severe depressive symptoms (PHQ-8 ≥10) [54]; and, in analysis of the N=148 participants aged 50+ years from the same sample (mean (SD) age 62 (8) years and 62% female), 8.1% endorsed moderate to severe depressive symptoms [55]. In post-hoc comparison of AFHHO-ORI respondent and AIHS summary data, AFHHO-ORI respondents endorsed mild depressive symptoms more often than did AIHS participants (24.6% versus 16.8%, p=.049). We observed significantly increased mild anxiety symptoms in younger versus older individuals in AFHHO-ORI respondents, as in the US population [9]; mild depressive symptoms were more common, but not significantly so, in younger versus older AFHHO-ORI respondents (also observed in the AIHS [54, 55]). Board members pointed out community members might respond differently when answering questions in a survey versus being asked by a healthcare provider. An ORI team member suggested increased mild symptoms of mental health could also be due to under-reporting of moderate or severe symptoms. Analysis of community data and comparison with the characteristic patterns of item level and total scores of the GAD-7 and PHQ-8 [56], may generate quantitative hypotheses regarding African immigrant responses for testing in additional community datasets.

Increased prevalence of mild anxiety and depressive symptoms among AFHHO-ORI survey respondents were supported by associations with food insecurity and English proficiency. Meta-analyses of US studies of anxiety or depression among those with food insecurity found significant odds ratios between two and three [57], a narrative synthesis of African studies found that food insecurity is associated with poorer mental health [58], and a narrative synthesis of studies of African immigrants in the US found that food insecurity is associated with risk of mental disorders [59]. In post-hoc analysis of our community data, odds ratios (95% CI) for mild, moderate or severe symptoms versus minimal symptoms in food insecure versus food secure respondents were 3.1 (1.9-5.1) for both anxiety and depressive symptoms, suggesting the food insecurity-mental health association in the Portland community sample is similar to that observed in US, African and African immigrant population samples; estimation of relative risks indicates a two-fold relative risk (data not shown) of mild or greater mental health symptoms when food insecurity is present.

Indicators of acculturation, including English proficiency, have previously been examined for their association with physical and mental health in US immigrants. Limited English proficiency in a sample of African immigrant adults (N=763) with mean residence of 5.5 years in the US has been associated with poor self-rated health [60]. In the same sample, dietary acculturation was also associated with poor self-rated health [61]. In the same survey, in a diverse sample of immigrant adults (N=5,982), in bivariate analyses, four English language-related variables (proficiency, comprehension, and use at home or at work) were associated with modest increases (odds ratios of 1.22 to 1.43) in poor self-rated health; however, when multivariate adjustment and all acculturation variables (length of residence, five (5) language-related variables, and dietary change) were included in analyses, language-related variables were no longer significant, suggesting that residence and dietary measures of acculturation were the most important influences on self-rated health, though authors also suggested that length of acculturation might indicate a longer period of exposure to racial or immigrant discrimination leading to psychosocial stress [62]. In post-hoc analyses of our community sample, analysis of any symptoms versus none or minimal symptoms in English proficient versus non-proficient respondents demonstrate two-fold relative risks (data not shown) of mild or greater mental health symptoms in those with proficiency in English, a marker for acculturation.

Associations of perceived discrimination and stress with mental health symptoms had the largest chi-squares (comparing distributions of none, mild and moderate & serious symptoms) and relative risks (comparing none versus any symptoms) observed in the study. AFHHO-ORI respondents with perceived discrimination or stress scores ≥ median were four to five times more likely to endorse anxiety or depressive symptoms than respondents with scores below the median (data not shown). We believe the finding of association of perceived stress with anxiety symptoms in the AFHHO-ORI sample is a novel finding in the African immigrant and refugee literature. The biopsychosocial mechanism that links perceived discrimination and the stress response was recently demonstrated in a sample of African American and White adults with similar exposures to perceived discrimination; African American but not White race moderated the association between perceived discrimination and cortisol concentration [63].

The biopsychosocial stressors of perceived discrimination and effects on mental health have been studied in African Americans but less so in African immigrant and refugee populations [19]. Soto et al [64] compared the diagnostic criteria of Generalized Anxiety Disorder (the basis of the GAD-7 questionnaire) to the symptoms of racial battle fatigue, a model for the effects of racial discrimination [65], and suggested mechanisms for the development of GAD in exposed individuals: the chronic stressor of discrimination may condition the individual to respond with worry (anxiety), and chronic exposure may deplete resilience resources. Analysis of the National Survey of American Life (2001-2003) with discrimination exposures coded as race-based and non-race-based and with the outcome of GAD, demonstrated: higher exposures to race-based discrimination in African American and Afro-Caribbean American individuals, and higher exposures to non-race-based discrimination in White non-Hispanic individuals; increased lifetime odds for GAD endorsed by individuals from each ethnicity with either form of discrimination; increased odds for GAD in African-American individuals exposed to race-based discrimination; and, higher lifetime odds of GAD endorsed by women than by men, for both forms of discrimination [64]. Together these results point to the adverse effects of discrimination on the risks for GAD in individuals from multiple ethnicities exposed to discrimination, though they do not shed light on subclinical anxiety states (e.g., GAD-7 scores between 6 and 10). Recent analyses of the same dataset emphasized regional and local differences in perceived discrimination exposure and resilience factors associated with differences in observed GAD and major depression [66–68]. Mechanisms other than the type and frequency of exposure to discrimination may be relevant to understanding the development of mental distress and illness in African American and African immigrant and refugee populations, e.g., the influence of internalized historical representations of slavery and colonialism [69], and vicarious (partner-experienced) discrimination [16, 17].

The low absolute rates of combustible tobacco cigarette use (11%, 3.7%, and 2.9% for even one or two puffs, ≥100 cigarettes lifetime, and every day or some day smoking) and the very low level of smoking heaviness (data not shown), suggests that AFHHO-ORI respondent cigarette smoking prevalence is consistent with previous studies of African immigrants. These studies have found that Black immigrants and refugees are less likely to engage in substance use compared to their native-born Black American counterparts. Analysis of current smoking in 2000-2016 NHIS data reported current smoking prevalence of 4.3% in African immigrants, lower than the 18.6% in African Americans [70]. Analysis of 2006-2015 Tobacco Use Supplement to the Current Population Survey data demonstrated that current cigarette smoking was significantly lower among African-born Black immigrants (4.7%) and West Indies-born Black immigrants (4.9%) than African Americans (17.4%) and European-born Black immigrants (17.7%) [26]. In 2021 NHIS data [29], 11.5% of US adults (11.7% of African American US adults) reported currently smoking cigarettes (≥100 cigarettes lifetime and every day or some day smoking), and in the 2018-2019 Tobacco Use Supplement, 11.2% of Oregon adults reported being current smokers [71]; both estimates are several times that of respondents to the AFHHO-ORI survey. 2019 NHIS data revealed that US men were more likely to currently use cigarettes than US women, although the absolute difference was modest (13.1% versus 10.1%). In AFHHO-ORI respondents, males were much more likely (19.3% versus 5.7%, a 3.4-fold relative risk) than females to endorse ever smoking (even one or two puffs); current smoking rates in the AFHHO-ORI respondents limited ability to assess associations with SDoH.

The majority of community smokers across the three definitions of ever smoking reported being younger, male, not endorsing a disability, having some college, possessing health insurance, food insecure, and with good English proficiency. Associations of ever smoking (even one or two puffs) suggest that males, those with greater acculturation, and those exposed to psychosocial stressors are at elevated risk for tobacco exposure (English proficiency RR=2.8-fold, some college RR=3.3-fold, everyday discrimination RR=2.3-fold, and perceived stress RR=3-fold), and may be at risk for future tobacco product use, given their initial exposure (even one or two puffs). These associations are consistent with the association of discrimination scores and increased risk of nicotine or tobacco product use in US young adults [72]. Ever-smoking (even one or two puffs) among younger community members might serve as a single question screener to assess the likely risk of exposure to psychosocial stressors that might result in progression to current smoking in the future.

Limitations

While our IRB did not contain community members, our Advisory Board reviewed drafts of both the Informed Consent and the survey. Different recruitment methods may have recruited respondents from different samples of the overall community. In descriptive, bivariate and age-adjusted analyses, we did not adjust for or impute missing data. Aversion to collecting household residence (housing, income, address) related data limited our ability to examine socioeconomic factors influencing mental health. The necessity to limit survey length meant that some measures were not included. A French translation was not prepared or used due to a focus on African languages. The cross-sectional design of the survey limited interpretation.

Conclusions

The goals of the study were: to develop a health survey to assess Black African refugee and immigrant demographics, SDoH, mental health and substance use; administer the survey to community-based organization members; and, analyze selected demographics and SDoH for association with mental health and combustible tobacco use, consulting throughout with community members represented by an Advisory Board. Survey measures and the Informed Consent balanced concerns about the time necessary to complete the survey, participant identifiability, demographic measure survey standards, lexical issues related to measurement questions, and existing community-based organization member data. The survey and Informed Consent were translated into seven African languages and administered to Black African refugees and immigrants in partnership with community-based organizations in the spring and summer of 2023. Initial analyses of mental health supported the community’s primary health concern regarding mental health, identified selected measures that could be used for screening, and demographics and SDoH that might guide preventive interventions.
