Implementation of a Gestational Diabetes Virtual Care Clinic: A Before–After Comparative Study

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is an increasingly common diagnosis during pregnancy and has a substantial effect on maternal and fetal morbidity as well as implications for future health. GDM is significantly associated with a range of pregnancy complications when adjusted for confounders [1]. GDM poses a significant health concern globally, defined as diabetes first onset or recognized during pregnancy, with untreated cases leading to adverse maternal and neonatal health outcomes [2]. In Ireland, the prevalence of GDM has been increasing in recent years, mirroring global trends. Several studies [3] have reported Irish prevalence rates, estimating that approximately 12% of pregnancies are affected by GDM. Untreated GDM can result in complications for both mother and baby, including macrosomia, neonatal hypoglycaemia, preterm birth, pre-eclampsia, increased risk of birth injury, and heightened risk of type 2 diabetes development later in life [3, 4]. Women with overt diabetes in pregnancy present a significantly higher risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes compared with GDM [5]. The risk of postpartum diabetes is significantly elevated, nearly sixfold higher than GDM and 25-fold higher than normoglycaemia in pregnancy [6]. Women with a history of GDM have a nearly tenfold increased risk of developing T2DM, in comparison with those with a normoglycaemic pregnancy [7].

The management of GDM involves a multidisciplinary team comprising specialist diabetes midwives, dietitians, endocrinologists, and obstetricians. Treatment for GDM emphasises lifestyle modifications, incorporating medical nutritional therapy (MNT) and physical activity. Pharmacological intervention, such as insulin therapy or metformin, may be necessary in some cases to maintain euglycaemia [8–10]. Advancements in healthcare technology, such as Bluetooth-enabled glucometers, telemedicine, and virtual clinics, have emerged as efficient tools for GDM management. These technologies enable remote monitoring of blood glucose levels in real time, enhancing self-management skills and treatment adherence [11]. Digital health strategies, including apps, tele-education, and remote monitoring, have emerged as promising alternatives [12, 13]. A retrospective Canadian study was conducted among individuals with GDM attending interdisciplinary diabetes in pregnancy clinics in Calgary, Alberta. The study highlighted the potential of virtual care to enhance GDM management [14]. Digital health technologies have significantly advanced maternal and fetal health outcomes by enabling real-time monitoring, personalised care, and improved access to critical health interventions [15, 16]. These innovations empower both patients and healthcare providers to make informed decisions, resulting in better health outcomes [17]. Various digital tools support these improvements, each addressing specific maternal healthcare needs. Mobile applications and telehealth platforms enhance communication between patients and healthcare providers, promoting active patient engagement [17–19]. Despite their potential benefits, there is a paucity of evidence regarding clinical outcomes following the implementation of virtual clinics for GDM management. The aim of this study was to assess the effectiveness of a multidisciplinary group educational intervention of lifestyle modification via telemedicine virtual clinics delivered by a specialist diabetes midwives and dietitians and to review the maternal outcomes of women with GDM following a change in clinical practice.

Methods

The National Maternity Hospital (NMH) Dublin, Ireland is a tertiary-level unit in Dublin with 6000–9000 deliveries per year. Screening for GDM was based on risk factors, with pregnant women screened for GDM between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation with a glucose challenge test (GCT) 50 g. If positive, women are then invited to complete a 3-h glucose tolerance test (GTT) 100 g as per American Diabetes Association (ADA) Standards of Care in Diabetes recommendation [20].

Between 2019 and 2021, there was a significant change in clinical practice for managing GDM and neonatal hypoglycaemia within this hospital. In 2019, women diagnosed with GDM were notified of their diagnosis via telephone. All women were invited to attend a 2-h, in-person lifestyle group session with a diabetes specialist midwife and a dietitian. Following this, women were reviewed weekly. During these visits, fasting and postprandial venous blood samples were taken frequently and women provided the midwife with self-recorded blood glucose levels. Glucose targets were < 5.3 mmol/l before meals and < 7.8 mmol/l at 1 h post prandial. When glucose targets were abnormal in at least three subsequent occasions, women were referred to the endocrinology team for consideration for pharmacological medical treatment.

This practice transitioned in March 2021 to a new virtual care model led by the advanced midwife practitioner with expertise in diabetes and pregnancy, partly driven by the COVID pandemic. Women were still informed of their diagnosis by phone but were now invited to a 1-h webinar with a specialist diabetes midwife and a dietitian. Women received this education from the comfort of their own home. Follow-up care was provided through telehealth clinics using the “Attend Anywhere” platform. Importantly, the new system integrated Bluetooth-enabled blood glucose monitors that synchronize to an app, with data automatically uploaded to a cloud-based system. This eliminates the need for self-reporting, allowing healthcare providers to access real-time glucose data during virtual consultations. Glucose targets were the same: < 5.3 mmol/l before meals and < 7.8 mmol/l at 1 h post prandial. All participants continue with weekly contact with diabetes in pregnancy service as it was the same prior to the intervention including diet or the pharmacologically treated. The contacts were with the Bluetooth-enabled blood glucose monitors that synchronize to an app, with data automatically uploaded to a cloud-based system. If pharmacological treatment was required (glucose targets abnormal in at least three subsequent occasions), it was initiated the same day by an advanced midwife practitioner and then escalated to the in-person diabetes in pregnancy multidisciplinary clinic, which included an endocrinologist and gynaecologist. Our safety protocols meant that if a woman was not contactable on two occasions (phone/text/email), they were considered as did not attend and booked for the next in-person clinic. We did not experience significant language issues with the appointments. There was no change in the make-up of the team before and after the intervention, the service was provided with the same multidisciplinary team (MDT) members before and during study, and there was no staff allocation elsewhere. This was a retrospective study analysis of healthcare record data at two specified time points: January to June 2019 and January to June 2021. As the virtual service was part of standard practice it was not necessary to sign an informed consent.

The data collection for this study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee National Maternity Hospital, Holles Street, Dublin, Ireland (EC24:2020). The data used in this study was de-identified. The study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and its later amendments.

Outcomes for review in this study were based on a core outcome set for studies of GDM prevention and treatment [21]. The sample analysed were all women diagnosed with GDM at two specified time points: face to face (January–June 2019) and virtually (January–June 2021) excluding patients that suffered a first trimester miscarriage or that did not deliver in the hospital as birth. The data collected and analysed were from patient healthcare records. For the purposes of this study, the following definitions were used. Macrosomia was defined as birth weight > 4.5 kg. Preterm birth was defined as birth before 37 weeks’ gestation. Low birth weight was defined as birth weight < 2.5 kg.

A series of univariate statistical analyses were performed as appropriate using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software (IBM Statistics 25.0). Demographic data was analysed through descriptive analysis to assess frequencies of characteristics. Associations between categorical variables and year of GDM diagnosis were assessed using chi-square tests for independence. Yates’ correction for continuity, Pearson’s chi-square and Fisher’s exact test were used as appropriate. Associations between continuous variables and year of GDM diagnosis were analysed using non-parametric tests (Mann–Whitney U test). A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Chi-square tests were applied to compare change in proportions between 2019 and 2021, unless any expected cell count was lower than 5 in which case Fisher’s exact test was applied, or if a larger than 2 × 4 table Pearson’s chi-square test was applied with p values derived from Monte Carlo permutations. Continuous variables were compared between years using Mann–Whitney tests. Binary logistic regression or multinomial logistic regression was performed for neonatal outcomes both unadjusted (mirroring the chi-square tests) and adjusted for the following covariates: treatment mode (medication versus not), number of raised glucose values on oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), age (in years), BMI (in kg/m2), parity (numeric), and weeks’ gestation at diagnosis of GDM (numeric).

Results

A total of 583 women with GDM were included: 2019 group (n = 227) and 2021 (n = 356) group; no significant clinical differences were seen between groups. Participant ethnicity was predominantly white in both cohorts, comprising 84.1% in 2019 and 76.7% in 2021. In 2019, other participants were identified as Asian (9.7%), Arab (3.5%), Hispanic (1.8%), and Black 0.9%, while in 2021, participants identified as Asian (14.9%), Hispanic (2.8%), Arab (2.2%), and Black (2.2%). Median age was 35 years (IQR 32–38). Median BMI was 28 kg/m2 (IQR 24–32) Two-thirds of women were in their second or subsequent pregnancy with approximately one in four women having had a diagnosis of GDM in a previous pregnancy on average; women were diagnosed with GDM at 28 weeks’ gestation (2019; Md = 27 weeks, 2021; Md = 28 weeks). Half of women in this study had at least one identified risk factor for GDM with obesity (BMI > 30 kg/m2) the most noted individual risk factor across the total cohort (n = 93 16.0%).

We reviewed attendance to education between groups in 2021 and 2019. A higher rate of attendance via the virtual platform was identified in the 2021 group than 2019 group (99.4% vs 95.6%, p = 0.001). The rate of missed appointments/did not attend (DNA) decreased following the transition to virtual care, from 10.4% (81/778 appointments) in 2019 to 9.5% (112/1174 appointments) in 2021. We did not have accurate data on the reduction in face-to-face appointments. In addition, women in the 2021 virtual education group experienced faster initiation of treatment within first 5 days (42.6% vs 58.8%, p < 0.001) and 10 days (77.3% vs 94.9%, p < 0.001) of diagnosis. There was no significant difference in the final method of treatment between diet and physical activity (71.8% vs 74.2%, p = 0.565), metformin (15.4% vs 10.4%, p = 0.093) and insulin (15.0% vs 15.7%, p = 0.906) use between 2019 and 2021. There were no significant differences in assessed birth outcomes including rates of preterm birth, shoulder dystocia, or low birth weight. The median gestation at birth was 39 (IQR 38–39) weeks in both groups (p = 0.733) and the median birth weight was 3.445 kg (2019: Md = 3.500 kg, IQR 3.115–3.830 kg; 2021: Md = 3.403 kg, IQR = 3.082–3.770 kg, p = 0.087). More than half of women with GDM achieved a spontaneous vaginal birth in both groups (55.9% vs 53.0%, p = 0.778).

Discussion

The findings of this study provide evidence supporting the implementation of a virtual care model for the management of GDM now being introduced in other maternity hospitals in Ireland. The transition from an endocrinology-led, in-person clinic to a registered advanced midwife practitioner (RAMP)-led, multidisciplinary-provided virtual care model resulted in improved attendance at education, faster initiation of treatment and continuity of care. These results align with previous research highlighting the benefits of telemedicine and remote monitoring in diabetes care [22], which demonstrated an increased ability to assess glycaemic control and make clinical adjustments to therapy while enhancing self-management and led to increased engagement. This convenience reduces the need for frequent in-person visits, which can be challenging due to time constraints and multiple hospital appointments for women in pregnancy. It also reduces travel, related costs and DNA rates [23].

The key finding of this study was the statistically and clinically significant increase in attendance at virtual GDM education sessions versus in-person education sessions, with 14 additional attendances due to the virtual nature of the session. The accessibility of virtual education likely contributed to this improvement. Higher attendance rates may have led to increased patient engagement, improved understanding of GDM management and adherence to treatment plans. Another significant outcome was the faster initiation of pharmacological treatment in the virtual care group. The use of Bluetooth-enabled glucometers and real-time data monitoring allows healthcare professionals to assess blood glucose levels promptly and initiate therapy without delay as shown previously [24].

The results are seen from self-monitoring of blood glucose three times daily in the lifestyle group and this is sufficient clinical data with which to assess glycaemic control in pregnancy for women managed on MNT and exercise. The ability to make timely treatment decisions is critical in managing GDM and the RAMP and diabetes midwifery team are well positioned within the MDT to translate this into clinical practice. This suggests that both in-person and virtual care models can achieve similar clinical interventions when appropriate monitoring and clinical interventions are implemented [25].

Although previous studies emphasized the importance of physical visits in managing GDM, virtual care is gaining increasing acceptance as a feasible and efficient option. Virtual models have been shown to effectively manage glucose levels, reduce hospital visits, and increase patient satisfaction [26]. The use of real-time data collection tools allows healthcare providers to adjust treatment in a timely manner, improving overall outcomes. Several studies have similarly found that telemedicine enhances self-management skills, optimises blood glucose control, and minimises complications [27, 28].

Telemedicine models for gestational diabetes care have demonstrated clear economic advantages, reducing both healthcare system and patient costs without compromising outcomes. Studies show significant reductions in face-to-face visits and direct healthcare expenditure, with one program reporting a 56% reduction in clinic visits and 16% lower costs [29]. Digital and remote-monitoring models also lessen indirect costs for women, including travel, parking, childcare, and time off work, resulting in substantial per patient savings [30]. Recent reviews confirm that remote monitoring improves efficiency and glycaemic management while maintaining safety and satisfaction, supporting its cost-effective integration into routine maternity care [31, 32].

Our findings reinforce that the effectiveness of a virtual care model is enhanced when its content is evidence-based and delivered by professionals. As in previous studies that we referenced, digital health tools, including tele-education, can improve attendance at education in women with GDM and we are suggesting that such tools should complement education. Future research should focus on virtual care model resources, long-term cost-effectiveness and staff levels.

Study Limitations

This was a retrospective study which allowed for analysis and comparison of routinely collected clinical data between two defined time points following the introduction of a change in clinical practice. A significant limitation was that we do not have accurate data on the decrease in face-to-face appointments as this change was conducted during the Covid pandemic and this data was not accurately collected. This model needs access and adaptability to technology and may not be generalizable to all contexts. We could see potential benefit in process indicators: reduction in number of visits to the hospital and potential benefit in terms of resources. Our study showed similar improvements to studies referenced above. Future prospective studies would be strengthened by the inclusion of additional maternal outcomes including hypertensive disorders, gestational weight gain and glycaemic control, as well as patients’ perspectives on the virtual care model.

Conclusions

Our virtual model facilitated increased attendance at education, faster initiation of treatment, continuity of care with integration of telemedicine and remote monitoring technologies in gestational diabetes management. Further prospective research should focus on expanding the scope of maternal health outcomes, assessing long-term benefits and evaluating patient satisfaction with virtual care models.
