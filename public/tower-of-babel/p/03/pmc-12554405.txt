Age and Health Status Benefits Following Initiation of Atrial Fibrillation‐Specific Treatments
Authors: Ryo Nakamaru, Nobuhiro Ikemura, Shun Kohsaka, Takehiro Kimura, Yoshinori Katsumata, Takahiko Nishiyama, Ikuko Ueda, Kojiro Tanimoto, Koji Negishi, Seiji Takatsuki, John A Spertus, Masaki Ieda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtrial fibrillation (AF) is managed with various strategies, including rate and rhythm control, to improve patients' health status. Although its incidence increases with age, how age affects health status improvements following AF treatment remains unknown.MethodsUsing data from a multicenter registry for outpatients with newly recognized AF, baseline and 1‐year health status was assessed with the Atrial Fibrillation Effect on Quality‐of‐life quesTionnaire (AFEQT). The associations between age and 1‐year changes in AFEQT‐overall summary and subdomain scores with rate or rhythm control strategies were estimated using generalized additive models. To address nonlinear relationships, we developed multivariable linear regression models with age range categories (<65, 65–75, and ≥75 years).ResultsAmong 2786 patients (31.7% women); 946 were <65, 1019 were ≥65 to 75, and 821 were ≥75 years, with rate control pursued in 23.4%, 42.0%, and 66.4%, respectively, and their mean 1‐year improvements in the AFEQT overall summary were 11.1 (SD: 16.6), 8.0 (17.0), and 4.7 (18.2), respectively. After multivariable adjustment, older age was independently associated with less AFEQT overall summary improvement, regardless of treatment strategy. Patients aged ≥75 years had a smaller AFEQT overall summary improvements than those aged <65 years (difference between groups −3.86 points [−5.29 to −2.42]), primarily driven by smaller improvements in AFEQT daily activities scores with similar improvements in the AFEQT symptoms or treatment concern domains.ConclusionsAlthough older adults with AF exhibited smaller gains in functional limitation following disease‐specific treatment, they experienced measurable improvements in overall health status regardless of treatment strategies.Keywords: AFEQT, age, atrial fibrillation, health‐related quality of lifeSubject Categories: Atrial Fibrillation

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common cardiac arrhythmia in adults. 1 AF impairs the health status (symptoms, function, and quality of life) of patients, 2 leading clinical practice guidelines to emphasize optimizing health status as a primary treatment goal. 3 , 4 Currently, various treatment strategies, including rate‐controlling medications and restoration of sinus rhythm, can improve patients' health status, as observed in pivotal trials demonstrating population‐averaged benefits in selected patient populations. 5 , 6 However, translating these benefits into routine clinical practice can be challenging given the potential heterogeneity of treatment benefit by individual patient characteristics. 7

In older adults, assessing patients' health status to better guide treatment strategies can be challenging, given their clinical complexity and higher burden of comorbidity. 8 , 9 Moreover, older adults are often at increased risk for pharmacological intolerance 10 , 11 or procedure‐related complications, 12 , 13 and are often excluded from randomized controlled trials. Better understanding the association of age with health status outcomes after AF treatment is needed to inform routine clinical practice.

To address this knowledge gap, we aimed to investigate the association of age with health status improvements after different treatment strategies in a multicenter AF registry. 14 AF incidence increases with age, contributing to high mortality and morbidity 8 , 9 ; therefore, a better definition of the association could provide a richer understanding of the anticipated health status benefits of alternative AF‐specific treatment strategies to better inform decision‐making.

METHODS

Data Sharing Statement

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Data Sources

The KiCS‐AF (Keio Interhospital Cardiovascular Study‐Atrial Fibrillation) registry has been previously described. 7 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Between September 2012 and June 2018, the registry collected information on patients referred to outpatient clinics from 11 institutions in the Tokyo metropolitan area with newly diagnosed or worsening AF. Based on predefined criteria, the registry collected data on demographics, vital signs, medical histories, medications, ECGs, echocardiograms, and blood test results at registration.

Yearly follow‐up examinations were conducted for 5 years through chart reviews, correspondence, and telephone interviews. Study coordinators updated the patients' cardiovascular events, documented changes in laboratory results and medications, and transcribed the Atrial Fibrillation Effect on Quality of Life Questionnaire (AFEQT) that was completed by patients at baseline and 1 year after. 18 Data quality assurance was achieved through systematic validation to identify outliers and ensure data completeness. The clinical research coordinators at each institution addressed all data queries. Additionally, the senior study coordinator (I.U.) and investigator (S.K.) performed onsite audits to ensure consecutive patient enrollment. The institutional review boards of the participating sites approved the study, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Assessment of Health‐Related Quality of Life

All participants were requested to answer the AFEQT at registration, before the initiation of rhythm or rate control therapy, and 1 year after registration. This 20‐item questionnaire evaluates 4 domains of AF‐related quality of life: symptoms, daily activities, treatment concerns, and treatment satisfaction, using a 7‐point Likert response scale. 18 The AFEQT‐overall summary (OS) score, derived from the first 3 domains, ranges from 0 to 100. A score of 0 indicates the worst symptoms, physical limitations, and treatment concerns, whereas a score of 100 indicates the best AF‐specific health status. We used a culturally and linguistically translated version of the AFEQT for the Japanese participants. A recent analysis showed that a 5‐point change in the AFEQT‐OS score corresponded to one European Heart Rhythm Association functional status class improvement, representing a clinically important within‐patient difference. 19

Study Population

For this analysis, we extracted participants with available data on AFEQT‐OS scores, both at registration and at 1‐year follow‐up, to assess the 1‐year change. We divided them into 3 age categories (<65, 65–74, and ≥75 years) based on prior literature (Figure S1). 20 , 21 All participants were assigned either the rhythm or rate control strategy within 1 year of registration, defined as (1) catheter ablation: those who underwent pulmonary vein isolation using radiofrequency or cryoballoon ablation. Patients with persistent AF underwent additional nonpulmonary vein trigger ablation (eg, left posterior wall, superior vena cava, or coronary sinus), linear ablation (eg, cavotricuspid isthmus ablation or lateral mitral isthmus ablation), continuous fractionated atrial electrogram ablation, or focal premature atrial contraction ablation at the discretion of the treating physician 22 ; (2) pharmacological rhythm: those with antiarrhythmic drug therapy who but did not undergo catheter ablation; and (3) rate control: those with either beta blockers, calcium channel blockers, and digitalis without antiarrhythmic drugs or catheter ablation. Treatment allocation was directed by the treating physician based on the patient's condition and preference. The registry did not specify target heart rates or medication doses. 14

Statistical Analysis

For descriptive purposes, continuous variables are summarized as mean±SD or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables are summarized as percentages across the 3 age categories. To assess generalizability, patient characteristics between those with and without missing AFEQTs, after excluding deaths, were compared. Continuous variables and their group differences were assessed using Student's t test or the Mann–Whitney U test based on their distributions, and the chi‐square test was used to compare categorical variables. A positive change in AFEQT scores (score at 1 year—score at baseline) indicates a health status improvement and a negative change signifies a decline.

Initially, we compared the unadjusted baseline AFEQT scores and their changes within 1 year across 3 age categories. To further assess the clinical importance of health status improvements, we also compared the proportion of patients achieving a clinically meaningful improvement in AFEQT‐OS within 1 year among the age groups. The thresholds of clinically meaningful improvement were defined based on prior literature, as described. 19 One approach applied a uniform threshold of a 5‐point improvement across the overall population. Alternatively, an age‐specific approach was applied with thresholds set at 7.6 points for patients aged <75 years and 3.4 points for those aged ≥75 years. 19 Furthermore, to explore potential clinical determinants of the clinically meaningful improvement, we conducted multivariable logistic regression analyses stratified by age groups (<75 and ≥75 years). The dependent variables were whether a patient achieved a clinically meaningful improvement, based on the aforementioned age‐specific cutoffs. The models were adjusted for the following variables, selected based on prior literature 16 : baseline AFEQT‐OS, sex, body mass index (≥25 kg/m2 or not), estimated glomerular filtration rate (≥60 mL/min per 1.73 m2 or not), anemia (defined as <13 g/dL for men and <12 g/dL for women), previous history of heart failure, hypertension, diabetes, stroke, and vascular disease, AF type (paroxysmal versus nonparoxysmal), treatment strategy (rate versus rhythm control), the use of oral anticoagulation, and participating institutions (high volume hospitals [performing over 250 catheter ablation cases per year] versus others).

Next, we evaluated the association between age (as a continuous variable) and changes in AFEQT‐OS and domain scores (eg, daily activities, symptoms, and treatment concerns) within 1 year using generalized additive models with smooth curve fitting. We included nonlinear terms in the models and assessed their statistical significance. Linear versus nonlinear associations were evaluated through a visual inspection of the estimated curves and comparative analysis of the model fit using Akaike information criterion values. We applied cubic regression splines to age and AFEQT scores to allow for flexible modeling of their effects. The optimal number of basis functions was selected based on generalized cross‐validation, and the effective degrees of freedom were used to evaluate the complexity of the fitted splines. Furthermore, to address nonlinear relationships and enhance clinical interpretation, we developed multivariate linear regression models with categorized age ranges (<65, 65–75, and ≥75 years). As a sensitivity analyses, we also evaluated adjusted relative change in AFEQT‐OS (ie, percent change within 1 year) across the age categories to enhance clinical interpretability. All models were adjusted for the variables described.

Finally, we conducted subgroup analyses based on (1) different treatment strategies and (2) AF type (paroxysmal AF versus nonparoxysmal) to examine the differential association between age and health status benefits by treatment strategies or AF type. 5 When conducting subgroup analyses, all covariates, other than treatment strategy or AF type, were included.

The following variables had moderate missing data: hemoglobin level (5.1%) and estimated glomerular filtration rate (4.7%, Table S1). Multiple imputation using chained equations was performed to maximize the inclusion of all participants. The imputation model included baseline covariates, treatment strategy, and AFEQT‐OS scores at baseline and 12 months. The number of burn‐in iterations between imputations was 50, and the number of imputations was 20 in the current multiple imputation by chained equations. Statistical significance was set at 2‐sided P values <0.05, and all statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.3; R Project for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

Patient Characteristics

Among the 3033 consecutive patients presenting with AF in this registry, 2786 (84.3%) patients with available data on AFEQT‐OS scores at baseline and the 1‐year follow‐up were included (Figure S1). Table S2 compares the characteristics of patients with and without missing AFEQT data. Patients without AFEQT data were largely comparable to those in the analytical cohort; however, patients with missing AFEQT data were less likely to have hypertension, coronary artery disease, paroxysmal AF, or a rhythm control strategy. The mean age of the studied cohort was 67.7±11.1 years, with 946 (34.0%) <65 years, 1019 (36.5%) were 65–74 years, and 821 (29.5%) were ≥75 years old. Patients aged ≥75 years old were more likely to be women, had more comorbidities, lower body mass indices and estimated glomerular filtration rate levels, and higher CHA2DS2‐VASc scores. Rate control was pursued in 23.4% (N=221), 42.0% (N=428), and 66.4% (N=545) of patients aged <65, 65 to 74, and ≥75 years, respectively.

Unadjusted changes in AFEQT‐OS and subdomain scores are summarized in Table 2. Overall, the AFEQT‐OS score improved in all age groups, although these improvements were attenuated with advancing age (11.1 [16.6], 8.0 [17.0], and 4.7 [18.2] in patients aged <65, 65–75, and ≥75 years old, respectively). Clinically Meaningful Improvement of AFEQT‐OS

When applying a clinically meaningful threshold of 5 points of improvement in AFEQT‐OS, the proportion of patients with clinically meaningful improvement decreased with advancing age (58.6%, 50.6%, and 43.6% in patients aged <65, 65–75, and ≥75 years old, respectively Table S4). Furthermore, even when the age‐specific cutoff was applied, the proportion of patients achieving clinically meaningful improvement decreased with advancing age (Table S4). Multivariable logistic regression analyses identified heart failure, female sex, paroxysmal AF, and rhythm control as independent predictors of clinically meaningful improvement in patients aged <75 years, whereas anemia was negatively associated with clinically meaningful improvement in those aged ≥75 years (Table S5).

Age and Adjusted Changes in AFEQT Scores

The generalized additive model showed a nonlinear relationship between age and change in AFEQT‐OS over 1 year; advancing age consistently attenuated the improvement, with a further decline observed in patients aged >65 years, reaching a plateau in those aged approximately 75 years of age (Figure 1A). Nonlinearity was verified using fit statistics (Table S6). The multivariable linear regression models also revealed lesser improvements between age categories (65–74 versus <65 years old, difference between groups −1.47 points [95% CI, −2.67 to −0.28]; ≥75 versus <65 years old, −4.22 points [−5.65 to −2.79]) (Figure 2 and Table S7). In contrast to the absolute changes, there were no differences in the relative changes in AFEQT‐OS among the categories (65–74 versus <65 years, difference between groups −0.65% [−5.66 to 4.36]; ≥75 versus <65 years, −5.43% [−11.4 to 0.59]) (Figure S3).

DISCUSSION

Previous trials have demonstrated the overall health benefits of AF‐specific management; however, older patients are often excluded from randomized controlled trials. Because the incidence of AF increases with advancing age; understanding the health status outcomes following AF‐specific management across a broad age spectrum is important. In the context of large, prospective, observational study, we found that although older adults exhibited smaller gains in functional limitation following disease‐specific treatment, they experienced measurable improvements in overall health status regardless of treatment strategies. Notably, their improvements in symptoms and treatment concerns were comparable to younger individuals. These findings provide novel insights into the influence of age on health status outcomes after AF treatment and can support shared decision‐making discussions with future patients.

This study builds on previous research investigating the association between age and health status in patients with AF. Our findings diverge from earlier reports describing a relatively preserved health status in older adults, 23 despite significant symptom burden and physical limitations. This discrepancy can be partially explained by the inclusion of a greater number of individuals with impaired health status in our study, owing to the enrollment of newly diagnosed or referred patients with AF. However, the present study, in the context of patients living at least a year after presentation, extends this prior work by describing longitudinal health status changes following AF treatment. It also highlights that physical functioning appears to improve less in older, as compared with younger patients. A previous report on ischemic heart disease revealed that although older patients with chronic coronary syndrome exhibited consistent improvement in angina frequency, they experienced less improvement in physical function and quality of life following invasive management than that seen in younger patients. 24 The consistent differential relationships between age and individual components of health status emphasize the need for comprehensive health status assessments in older adults with cardiovascular disease to better inform the management in older adults.

The overall health status benefits after AF‐specific treatment were observed across all age strata. Although older adults exhibited smaller improvements in functional limitation, their gains in symptom were comparable to those of younger individuals. This trend is consistent with findings in patients with coronary artery disease following coronary interventions. 24 , 25 Patient symptom burden and functional independence are crucial to determining the best treatment options, particularly in older adults. 24 , 26 Among patients aged <75 years, posttreatment health status benefits were consistent across the AFEQT subdomains, suggesting that older adults are more likely to experience less functional improvement and may warrant supplemental treatments, such as cardiac rehabilitation, to further improve their health status. Older adults are more prone than the younger population to a decline in physiological reserve 27 , 28 and comorbid conditions. 8 , 9 , 29 Indeed, we identified the history of stroke and anemia as negative predictors for overall health status improvements, emphasizing that multidisciplinary care and optimal managements of underlying comorbidities, in addition to initial AF treatment, are necessary to improve their overall health status. Importantly, older adults with AF had comparable treatment benefits regarding symptoms and treatment concerns relative to younger patients, indicating that age was not a negative predictor of health status benefits from AF treatment. This was surprising given that older adults are more likely to have comorbidities that affect treatment effects, suggesting that age may not be a deterrent to AF‐specific treatment in contemporary clinical practice, particularly applying catheter ablation. Meanwhile, our previous study indicated that evaluating the severity of health status through routine history‐taking can be challenging, 30 particularly for older adults. Therefore, these findings highlight the potential for disease‐specific questionnaires to supplement clinical care and support the assessments of treatment response.

The present study also highlighted that patients aged ≥75 years old were less likely to experience health status benefits with pharmacological rhythm control. Although pharmacological rhythm control can effectively maintain sinus rhythm in patients with AF; caution is warranted to older adults' increased susceptibility to drug intolerance. 11 Nevertheless, given the heightened risk of procedure‐related complications associated with advanced age, 12 , 13 shared decision‐making is essential when pursuing either treatment strategy. Further investigations are needed to identify optimal AF‐specific treatment strategies for improving the health status of older adults.

Limitations

Our study has some limitations. First, as a nonrandomized observational study, it inherently possesses limitations despite using the optimal method to describe current treatment patterns and care outcomes. Therefore, a selection bias may be associated with the treatment decisions taken by physicians, which may have influenced the changes in health status. Notably, factors that significantly influence outcomes in older adults with AF, such as frailty, were not evaluated, which may have affected our findings. Second, we did not assess age‐related conditions, including frailty, that may influence health status and treatment response in older adults with AF. In addition, the lack of data on AF‐specific treatment success (eg, Holter ECG or heart rate after rate control) may have introduced further bias. Third, we restricted participation to newly diagnosed or referred patients undergoing initial evaluation for AF in cardiology clinics to capture pretreatment data; however, we were unable to obtain data on the details of the care provided before registration. Fourth, AFEQT scores were collected at only 2 time points: at registration (before the initiation of any AF‐specific treatment) and at the 1 year after registration. Therefore, the observed change in AFEQT score might underestimate the actual benefit derived from treatment, particularly if patients experienced a rapid decline in health status before the intervention that was subsequently reversed. This limits our ability to fully characterize the treatment‐related trajectories of health status. Finally, as with our previous reports, 15 , 16 the potential for generalizability of our results to the Japanese population needs to be examined further due to patient enrollment from the KiCS‐AF registry. However, our registry included a substantial number of patients from numerous centers across Japan. Therefore, our findings reflect current practice patterns in Japan.

Conclusions

Although older adults with AF exhibited smaller gains in functional limitation following disease‐specific treatment, they experienced measurable improvements in overall health status regardless of treatment strategies. Notably, their improvements in symptoms and treatment concerns were comparable to younger individuals. These findings suggest that age may not be a deterrent to AF‐specific treatment and highlight the need for further research to enhance physical function and to improve treatment benefits in older adults.
