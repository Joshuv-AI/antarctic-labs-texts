Response to the Letter to the Editor “Opinion on nerve-sparing radical hysterectomy”
Authors: Kenro Chikazawa, Ken Imai, Tomoyuki Kuwata, Ryo Konno
Published: 2025

Thank you for reading our paper and for your interest in our work. We would like to take this opportunity to clarify several points that may have been misunderstood.

First, although the procedure described in our paper closely resembles a modified radical hysterectomy, we consider it to be fundamentally different. In a modified radical hysterectomy, if the deep uterine vein is not transected, the bladder vein is not addressed. In other words, the posterior layer of the vesicouterine ligament remains untouched. In contrast, our nerve-sparing technique involves partial transection of the posterior layer of the vesicouterine ligament. Additionally, it allows for a more extensive resection of the vaginal and paracervical tissues. Thus, this procedure should not be considered a modified radical hysterectomy; rather, it may be more accurately described as a selected nerve-sparing Okabayashi radical hysterectomy [1].

Second, the longer operative time and greater blood loss observed in our study are primarily attributable to the fact that most radical hysterectomies at our institution have been performed by fellows under supervision since 2016. As stated in our manuscript, “all radical hysterectomies in this study were supervised by an attending physician.” As demonstrated by the SHAPE trial and JCOG1101, there is a clear trend toward surgical de-escalation, and the number of candidates for radical hysterectomy has significantly decreased [2,3]. Consequently, we strive to provide skin-to-skin operative experience to our fellows whenever possible during these increasingly rare procedures [4,5]. Naturally, this contributes to longer operative times and increased blood loss. Similar operative durations were reported in another study conducted at a teaching hospital in Japan [6]. Data from a multicenter prospective study in Japan also revealed a wide range of operative times and blood loss [7]. Importantly, our study did not demonstrate any deterioration in oncologic outcomes. Our cases of classic Okabayashi nerve-sparing radical hysterectomy, which were mostly performed by attending surgeons, had comparable operative times to those reported by Komatsu et al. [8]. Furthermore, studies by Fujii et al. [9] and Komatsu et al. [8] stated that nerve-sparing surgeries were “performed by one of the authors,” implying that these procedures were conducted by experienced attending surgeons who fully understood the surgical techniques. These surgeries were associated with shorter durations and less blood loss. This distinction is critical in comparing their findings with ours, which included a large number of surgeons in training, including fellows. Therefore, our study may better reflect current clinical realities, where opportunities to perform a radical hysterectomy are becoming increasingly limited.

Regarding the differences observed in Table 2 of the original manuscript, in the revised procedure (simplified nerve-sparing radical hysterectomy), as we aimed to perform transection limited to the vesical veins following the concept proposed by Yabuki et al. [10,11], it became necessary to perform a more complete pelvic lymphadenectomy along the internal iliac vein to clearly identify and isolate these veins. This technical refinement naturally led to a more thorough clearance of lymphatic tissue in this region and facilitated division of the cardinal ligament at the true pelvic wall, resulting in a wider resection of both the parametrium and the vagina.

In contrast, in the earlier cases (classic Okabayashi nerve-sparing radical hysterectomy), the pelvic lymphadenectomy was less complete, particularly around the internal iliac vein. Although the paravaginal and pararectal spaces were sufficiently developed, the dissection along the internal iliac vein was incomplete. The cardinal ligament was transected as laterally as possible at that time, but compared with the revised procedure, it was relatively closer to the cervix.

Therefore, the differences observed in Table 2—greater resected parametrium, longer vaginal length, and a higher number of lymph nodes—reflect the more meticulous and comprehensive pelvic lymphadenectomy along the internal iliac vein achieved in the simplified nerve-sparing radical hysterectomy, rather than the effect of transecting the posterior layer of the vesicouterine ligament itself.

In the classic Okabayashi nerve-sparing radical hysterectomy group, 2 of 76 cases (2.6%) were performed laparoscopically, whereas in the simplified nerve-sparing radical hysterectomy group, 20 of 38 cases (52.6%) were laparoscopic procedures, with a significant difference between the groups (p<0.001). This difference in surgical approach might have contributed to the more complete pelvic lymphadenectomy and better visualization of deep pelvic structures, such as the internal iliac vein, in the simplified group. We acknowledge this as a limitation of our study, as you suggested.

Third, our evaluation of bladder function differs from previous reports. We used “postoperative days before independent urination” as our endpoint, rather than “time to achieve a post-void residual (PVR) volume less than 50 mL.” This indicates the time until the patient was completely free from the need for clean intermittent self-catheterization, even on rare occasions in daily life. We believe that achieving a PVR of <50 mL several times is insufficient. Our endpoint was defined as the time at which the patient no longer required one or 2 daily episodes of clean intermittent self-catheterization, even during periods when residual urine volume tends to be highest, such as in the morning or before bedtime. Naturally, this requires more time to achieve; however, from a quality-of-life (QoL) perspective, this is a more clinically meaningful endpoint. In this respect, our study is novel and aligns with the growing emphasis on QoL in contemporary clinical research. Recent Japanese studies evaluating nerve-sparing radical hysterectomy have employed urodynamic assessments [12]. We believe that it may be time to update the evaluation method for bladder function, moving beyond merely recording several instances of PVR <50 mL, and toward assessments that better reflect patients’ real-life experiences [12]. Regarding bladder function preservation, in both groups, only 2.6% of patients required long-term medication for bladder symptoms at the last follow-up, as reported in the original paper. In recent studies on nerve-sparing radical hysterectomy, urinary symptoms were reported in 27.4% of the patients [13], and 8.82% required intermittent self-catheterization [14]. Furthermore, a recent retrospective Japanese study reported that 30%–40% of patients required pharmacologic therapy for urinary symptoms after nerve-sparing radical hysterectomy [15]. Our results based on the Okabayashi method appear to be comparable to these reports. As stated in the original paper, the median follow-up period was 60 months in the classic Okabayashi nerve-sparing radical hysterectomy group and 38 months in the simplified nerve-sparing radical hysterectomy group. Bladder function was evaluated at the last follow-up visit for each patient. In recent Japanese studies assessing postoperative bladder function after nerve-sparing radical hysterectomy, the follow-up duration was approximately 6 months in both retrospective studies [6,14,15] and even in a prospective study protocol [12]. Therefore, our follow-up period can be considered sufficient for evaluating long-term bladder function recovery.

Finally, we present intraoperative photographs taken after a classic Okabayashi nerve-sparing radical hysterectomy, which clearly demonstrate the preservation of the bladder branches arising from the pelvic plexus and connecting to the hypogastric nerve (Fig. 1). As previously described in anatomical studies, only course III branches remain in these procedures. In contrast, simplified nerve-sparing radical hysterectomy preserves both courses II and III, indicating a greater extent of nerve preservation [16].

We hope that this letter serves as a comprehensive response. Regarding the association between complete transection of the posterior layer of the vesicouterine ligament and postoperative urinary dysfunction, the results of the ongoing JACC trial may be informative. This prospective, multicenter study, currently ongoing within the Japanese Gynecologic Oncology Group, evaluates Okabayashi nerve-sparing radical hysterectomy performed via laparoscopy (a non-randomized confirmatory trial of minimally invasive LRH [new-Japanese LRH] in patients with early-stage cervical cancer: https://www.eagot.org/_Contents/board_view.php?idx=16&page=1&bd_code=cervical). The trial includes patients with cervical tumors less than 4 cm in diameter, predominantly International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics stage IB1, who are generally considered candidates for nerve-sparing surgery in Japan.

Importantly, the JACC protocol explicitly includes assessment of the complete transection of the posterior layer of the vesicouterine ligament, with intraoperative photographic documentation and grading (A–C) by central review. In other words, this study is prospectively collecting data to determine whether a more complete transection of the posterior layer is associated with a higher incidence or severity of postoperative urinary dysfunction.

Although the JACC trial was not primarily designed to evaluate nerve-sparing techniques, its results will nonetheless provide critical information for reconsidering the appropriate extent and approach of radical hysterectomy in this patient population. Additionally, the findings may help refine the current understanding and role of Okabayashi radical hysterectomy involving complete transection of the posterior layer of the vesicouterine ligament, within the context of surgical de-escalation in modern gynecologic oncology.
