High glucose fluctuation levels modulate monocyte cytokine expression via oxidative stress mechanism
Authors: Shiqi Sun, Zhiheng Sun, Qi Huang, Jiazhong Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the impact of glycemic variability on cytokine expression (MCP-1, IL-6, TNF-α) and oxidative stress in monocytes, and explored the protective effect of the antioxidant α-lipoic acid (ALA). ‌Human THP-1 monocytes were cultured under four conditions: stable normoglycemia (5 mmol/L), persistent hyperglycemia (20 mmol/L), low-amplitude glucose fluctuation (alternating between 5 and 20 mmol/L), and high-amplitude fluctuation (alternating between 5 and 30 mmol/L). The effects of ALA supplementation were assessed in parallel. ‌Compared to the normoglycemic control, intracellular oxidative stress was significantly elevated in all hyperglycemic groups (p < 0.05), with the highest levels observed in the high-amplitude fluctuation group (p < 0.05 vs. low-amplitude fluctuation). Both sustained and fluctuating hyperglycemia upregulated the mRNA and protein expression of MCP-1, IL-6, and TNF-α (p < 0.05 vs. normal control). ALA treatment markedly suppressed this cytokine overexpression (p < 0.05). Furthermore, ALA significantly attenuated oxidative stress (p < 0.05) and restored antioxidant enzyme activity across all hyperglycemic groups. ‌In this monocyte model, high-amplitude glucose fluctuations induced greater oxidative stress and inflammatory cytokine dysregulation than sustained hyperglycemia, with the severity correlating with the fluctuation magnitude. The antioxidant α-lipoic acid attenuated these effects, suggesting a potential role for oxidative stress in this process. Further research is needed to elucidate the precise causal mechanisms linking glucose fluctuations to monocyte dysfunction.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Diseases, Endocrinology, Physiology

Introduction

In vitro studies have demonstrated that exposure of monocytes to hyperglycemic conditions significantly alters their functional phenotype. Monocytes, as key mediators of innate immunity, become hyperactivated under high glucose, leading to enhanced production of pro-inflammatory cytokines—including MCP-1, IL-6, and TNF-α—and increased generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS)1. MCP-1 drives monocyte recruitment into arterial walls, initiating atherogenesis. IL-6 and TNF-α promote endothelial dysfunction, insulin resistance, and plaque instability—key processes in diabetic macrovascular disease. This inflammatory shift is not merely a consequence of metabolic overload but is actively driven by defined molecular pathways. Specifically, chronic hyperglycemia activates four major biochemical cascades: the formation of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs), protein kinase C (PKC) isoforms, the polyol pathway, and the hexosamine pathway. Critically, Brownlee’s seminal work established that mitochondrial overproduction of superoxide serves as the unifying mechanism linking all four pathways, thereby amplifying intracellular oxidative stress and contributing to diabetic vasculopathy2,3.

ROS generated under these conditions act not only as cytotoxic byproducts but also as signaling molecules that activate redox-sensitive transcription factors such as nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) and activator protein-1 (AP-1), as well as mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), including p38 and JNK4,5. These pathways collectively drive the transcriptional upregulation of inflammatory mediators and adhesion molecules (e.g., ICAM-1, VCAM-1), facilitating monocyte adhesion to endothelial cells—a pivotal early event in atherogenesis6–8. Indeed, elevated MCP-1 expression in monocytes under hyperglycemia enhances chemotaxis and subendothelial infiltration, accelerating foam cell formation and vascular inflammation9. Once recruited into the vessel wall, activated monocytes differentiate into macrophages, internalize oxidized LDL, and perpetuate local inflammation through sustained cytokine release—thereby fueling plaque progression and instability in diabetic macrovascular disease10.

While multiple cell types—including endothelial cells, vascular smooth muscle cells, and adipocytes—are involved in diabetic complications, monocytes represent a particularly critical target for investigating glycemic variability–induced damage. First, circulating monocytes are among the first immune cells to encounter fluctuating glucose levels in vivo, making them sensitive sentinels of metabolic stress1,10. Second, monocyte activation directly initiates and propagates vascular inflammation: their adhesion to endothelium precedes structural vascular injury, and their differentiation into tissue macrophages drives chronic inflammation in both micro- and macrovascular beds6,7,10. Third, clinical studies consistently show that elevated circulating levels of MCP-1, IL-6, and TNF-α—predominantly derived from activated monocytes/macrophages—correlate strongly with the severity of diabetic complications9,11. Given these considerations, we focused on monocytes as a reductionist yet pathophysiologically relevant model to dissect the direct effects of glucose fluctuations on oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling, independent of secondary paracrine influences from other cell types. This approach allows clearer attribution of observed effects to intrinsic monocyte responses, which can later inform more complex co-culture or in vivo studies.

While most prior in vitro models have employed constant high glucose (typically 20–25 mmol/L) to mimic sustained hyperglycemia1,12, this approach fails to reflect the dynamic glycemic excursions commonly observed in diabetic patients. Clinical evidence increasingly indicates that acute glucose fluctuations—characterized by rapid swings between hyper- and normoglycemia—may exert more deleterious effects on vascular cells than stable hyperglycemia13–16. For instance, Quagliaro et al. demonstrated that intermittent high glucose induces greater mitochondrial superoxide production and NF-κB activation in endothelial cells compared to constant high glucose17. Similarly, Otsuka et al. reported that transient hyperglycemia significantly enhances monocyte–endothelial adhesion in vivo7.

To better recapitulate the clinical reality of glycemic variability, we designed an in vitro fluctuating glucose model using the human monocytic THP-1 cell line. In addition to a normal glucose control (5 mmol/L) and a sustained high glucose group (20 mmol/L), we implemented two oscillating regimens: low-amplitude fluctuation (5 ↔ 20 mmol/L) and high-amplitude fluctuation (5 ↔ 30 mmol/L). Glucose levels were cycled every 2–3 h during the daytime (three cycles over 15 h), followed by 9 h of normoglycemia overnight, totaling 24 h of exposure—mimicking postprandial spikes and fasting troughs in poorly controlled diabetes17. This design allows direct comparison of the relative impacts of glycemic stability versus variability on monocyte oxidative stress and inflammatory responses.

Furthermore, to test whether oxidative stress is causally involved in glucose fluctuation–induced monocyte dysfunction, we co-treated cells with α-lipoic acid (ALA; 62.5 µmol/L), a potent mitochondrial-targeted antioxidant known to scavenge ROS, inhibit NF-κB activation, and ameliorate diabetic complications in preclinical and clinical settings18–20. By integrating this pharmacological intervention, our study aims to delineate the mechanistic role of oxidative stress in mediating the differential effects of constant versus fluctuating hyperglycemia on monocyte biology.

Materials and methods

Main materials and reagents

THP-1 cells (human acute monocytic leukemia cell line) were obtained from the Preservation Center of Wuhan University (Wuhan, China; Cat. No. GDC0067). RPMI 1640 medium and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were purchased from Gibco™, Thermo Fisher Scientific (Grand Island, NY, USA; Cat. Nos. 11875093 and 10099141, respectively). D-glucose was acquired from Amresco LLC (Solon, OH, USA; Cat. No. 0230-500G). ALA was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA; Cat. No. T1383).

The ROS Detection Kit based on the DCFH-DA probe was provided by Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China; Cat. No. S0033S). Malondialdehyde (MDA) levels were measured using an MDA Assay Kit (thiobarbituric acid method) from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China; Cat. No. A003-1–2). Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Grand Island, NY, USA; Cat. No. 15596026). Reverse transcription was performed with the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; Cat. No. K1622). Real-time PCR was carried out using SYBR Green Real-time PCR Mix from TOYOBO Life Science (Osaka, Japan; Cat. No. QPK-201).

Sandwich ELISA kits for human IL-6, MCP-1, and TNF-α were purchased from MultiSciences (Lianke) Biotech Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China; Cat. Nos. EK106/2–96, EK124/2–96, and EK182/2–96, respectively). All primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China).

Cultivation and passaging of THP-1 cells

THP-1 cell line was retrieved and thawed in a sterile workbench. After resuscitation, the cells were suspended in RPMI 1640 medium containing 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and seeded into culture flasks with 5mL of medium. The flasks were then placed in a 5%CO2 incubator at 37℃ with 90% humidity for cultivation. The cells were passaged every 2–3 days.THP-1 cells in the logarithmic growth phase (approximately 80–90% confluence, with viability > 95% as determined by MTT assay) were used for experiments. THP-1 cells were used in their undifferentiated, monocytic state for all experiments. Cells were not treated with PMA or other differentiating agents. Briefly, a single-cell suspension was seeded into a 96-well plate at a density of 10³−104 cells per well, with a total volume of 200µL per well (considering the well volume of 370µL in a standard 96-well plate). The cells were cultured for a period determined by the experimental objectives. Subsequently, 50µL of MTT solution (2 mg/mL) was added to each well, and the cells were further incubated for 3 h. After incubation, the culture medium was aspirated, and 150µL of DMSO was added to each well. The plate was then placed on a microplate shaker for 10 min to dissolve the formazan crystals. Finally, the optical density (OD) was measured at a wavelength of 570 nm using a microplate reader, and the cell viability was calculated. Once the cell viability reached 95%, the cell density was adjusted to 1 × 105cells/mL for subsequent experiments14.

Experimental grouping and treatment

Well-confluent THP-1 cells were first synchronized by incubation in phenol red–free RPMI 1640 medium for 6 h at 37 °C under 5% CO₂. Following synchronization, cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 1.5 × 10⁵ cells per well and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS under the designated glucose conditions. The culture medium was replaced every 24 h to maintain consistent treatment exposure and support cell viability. The experiment was divided into two main categories: Control Groups: Normal Control Group: 5mmol/L glucose; High Glucose Group: 20 mmol/L glucose; Low-level Fluctuation Group: Alternating 5mmol/L and 20mmol/L glucose; High-level Fluctuation Group: Alternating 5mmol/L and 30mmol/L glucose; Drug Treatment Group: On the basis of the control groups, 62.5µmol/L ALA, agents capable of inhibiting ROS production was added, an antioxidant working at the mitochondria level15. To control for osmotic effects, mannitol was added to the media of the normal glucose (5 mmol/L) and sustained high glucose (20 mmol/L) control groups to match the osmolarity of the highest glucose concentration (30 mmol/L) used in the fluctuation experiments. After 24 h, various indicators were measured. For the stable control groups (5mmol/L and 20mmol/L glucose), the medium was replaced once every 24 h. For the fluctuation groups, the glucose concentration was manually adjusted in a cyclical manner to simulate postprandial spikes: cells were exposed to 20 mmol/L (low-fluctuation) or 30 mmol/L (high-fluctuation) glucose for 3 h, followed by 5 mmol/L glucose for 2 h. This 5-hour cycle was repeated three times during a 15-hour daytime period. A 9-hour nighttime period of constant 5 mmol/L glucose followed, completing a 24-hour regimen. To control for osmotic effects, mannitol was added to the media of the normal glucose (5 mmol/L) and sustained high glucose (20 mmol/L) control groups to match the osmolarity of the highest glucose concentration (30 mmol/L) used in the fluctuation experiments. Cells were exposed to their respective glucose conditions (as described for each group) for 24 h per day16. This daily exposure protocol was repeated for a total of 14 days to model chronic effects. After the final 24-hour exposure period on day 14, cells and supernatants were collected for analysis.

ALA was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a 100 mmol/L stock solution, which was stored at −20 °C. For treatment, the stock was diluted in complete culture medium to a final working concentration of 62.5 µmol/L. The final concentration of DMSO in the culture medium was 0.0625% (v/v). A vehicle control group (cells treated with 0.0625% DMSO in normal glucose medium) was included in all experiments, and no significant effects of the vehicle were observed on any measured parameters.

This model was designed to simulate the oscillating hyperglycemic conditions (e.g., postprandial spikes) observed in diabetic patients, rather than hypoglycemic episodes. The chosen glucose concentrations (5mmol/L as normoglycemia, 20mmol/L and 30mmol/L as hyperglycemia) are based on established in vitro models of diabetic hyperglycemia.

Measurement of ROS and MDA

ROS levels were detected using the ROS Detection Kit with the DCFH-DA probe. Briefly, the DCF fluorescence was measured by flow cytometry (BD FACSCanto™ II, BD Biosciences, USA) at an excitation wavelength near 502 nm and an emission wavelength near 530 nm, allowing for the quantification of intracellular ROS levels. MDA content was measured using the MDA Assay Kit. The thiobarbituric acid (TBA) method was employed, and absorbance was read at 532 nm using a spectrophotometer. The absorbance values were converted to MDA concentrations using a standard curve. The serum MDA content was calculated as follows: (Absorbance of Sample - Absorbance of Sample Blank)/(Absorbance of Standard - Absorbance of Standard Blank) *10.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from THP-1 cells using Trizol reagent, and its concentration and purity were determined spectrophotometrically. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Gene-specific primers for HO-1, MCP-1, IL-6, TNF-α, and the housekeeping gene β-actin were used for amplification. The primer sequences, expected amplicon sizes, and thermal cycling parameters are summarized in Table 1. All reactions were performed in triplicate on a real-time PCR system using SYBR Green Real-time PCR Mix. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method, with β-actin as the internal reference.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Cell culture supernatants were collected after the final treatment period and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris. The concentrations of MCP-1, IL-6, and TNF-α in the clarified supernatants were quantified using commercially available sandwich ELISA kits, according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1, Agilent, USA), and cytokine concentrations were determined by interpolation from standard curves prepared on the same plate.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (Version 13.0, URL Link: https://www.ibm.com/products/spss-statistics). Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. For data meeting the assumptions of normality and equal variance, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed for group comparisons. When ANOVA indicated significant differences, Tukey’s post hoc test was used for pairwise comparisons. For data not meeting parametric assumptions, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for overall group comparisons, followed by the Mann-Whitney U test for pairwise comparisons. The Bonferroni correction (P < 0.05/n) was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation for parametric data, or as median (interquartile range) for non-parametric data. A two-tailed P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses adhered to the underlying assumptions of the respective statistical methods.

Results

Effects of fluctuating and sustained hyperglycemia on HO-1 mRNA expression in THP-1 cells

Compared to the normoglycemic control group, HO-1 mRNA expression levels in THP-1 cells were significantly elevated in the sustained hyperglycemia, low-amplitude fluctuation, and high-amplitude fluctuation groups (all P < 0.05). Over time, HO-1 mRNA expression in all groups showed an increasing trend, indicating a time-dependent effect. Further analysis revealed that HO-1 mRNA levels in both fluctuation groups (low- and high-amplitude) were significantly higher than those in the sustained hyperglycemia group (both P < 0.05). Moreover, expression in the high-amplitude fluctuation group was significantly higher than in the low-amplitude fluctuation group (P < 0.05) (Table 2).

Discussion

In recent years, research has demonstrated that fluctuating hyperglycemia, as opposed to sustained hyperglycemia, plays a more prominent role in promoting the onset and progression of chronic vascular complications in diabetic patients, thereby becoming a research focus15. Atherosclerosis, characterized by chronic inflammatory responses, is the primary pathological change in diabetic macrovascular complications. This study primarily aimed to observe the effects of varying concentrations of sustained and fluctuating hyperglycemia on the expression of MCP-1, IL-6, and TNF-α in monocytes, while also examining the production of ROS and changes in antioxidant enzyme activity, and further exploring the underlying mechanisms. In inflammatory responses, ROS serves as a crucial signaling molecule capable of triggering a series of signaling pathways. ROS can activate the NF-κB signaling pathway, leading to an increase in the expression of inflammatory genes. Additionally, ROS can activate the MAPK signaling pathway, which includes subfamilies such as ERK, JNK, and p38, promoting the production and release of inflammatory mediators. These inflammatory mediators then mediate inflammatory responses through specific receptors, forming a complex regulatory network4.

This experiment revealed that both fluctuating and sustained hyperglycemia significantly upregulated the mRNA levels and protein expression of MCP-1, IL-6, and TNF-α in monocytes. Compared to the sustained hyperglycemia group, the fluctuating hyperglycemia group produced more ROS, which paralleled the marked increase in intracellular MCP-1, IL-6, and TNF-α expression. Furthermore, oxidative stress and these expression levels of MCP-1, IL-6, and TNF-α in monocytes increased over time, demonstrating a time-dependent effect, Specifically, at 6 h, the levels in these groups further increased, and reached their highest levels at 24 h. Pretreatment with the antioxidant ALA significantly reduced the mRNA levels and protein expression of MCP-1, IL-6, and TNF-α, with statistically significant differences. Therefore, this study suggests that acute fluctuating hyperglycemia exerts similar or even more severe oxidative stress damage compared to chronic sustained hyperglycemia, leading to abnormal expression of cytokines. The mechanism may involve increased production of ROS and decreased antioxidant enzyme activity at high glucose concentrations. High glucose rapidly induces intracellular oxidative stress, increasing ROS generation through the PKC pathway. ROS, in turn, can activate pathways such as PKC and MAPK, which are known to enhance the inductive effect of high glucose on inflammatory cytokines5. Additionally, ROS serves as a signaling molecule akin to a second messenger, activating numerous redox-sensitive signaling pathways, including NF-Κb and activator protein-1 (AP-1). The activation of these pathways results in abnormal cytokine expression, contributing to the development of atherosclerosis and related diseases such as insulin resistance, diabetes, and its complications. Therefore, inhibiting oxidative stress in monocytes in diabetes is crucial for the treatment of diabetes and its complications.

When we further compared the fluctuation groups to the high glucose group, we found that both the low-level and high-level fluctuation groups displayed significantly higher levels of oxidative stress and cytokine expression. This suggests that, in addition to the damaging effects of constant high glucose, fluctuations in glucose levels exacerbate oxidative stress, even at relatively low levels of fluctuation. Moreover, the high-level fluctuation had significantly higher oxidative stress and cytokine expression levels than the low-level fluctuation group. This finding underscores the importance of minimizing glucose fluctuations, as even small increases in fluctuation magnitude can lead to significant increases in oxidative stress. our results demonstrate that both high glucose levels and glucose fluctuations contribute to increased intracellular oxidative stress, with the latter effect being more pronounced at higher levels of fluctuation. These findings have important implications for the management of conditions such as diabetes, where minimizing glucose fluctuations may be crucial for reducing oxidative stress and preventing associated complications.

Numerous studies suggest that an increase in ROS occurs in the primary cell types involved in atherosclerosis, namely vascular endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, and monocytes. This increase in ROS may be the initiating factor in the development and progression of diabetic vascular complications20. The earliest event in diabetic macroangiopathy is the recruitment of monocytes into the arterial endothelium, where they adhere to endothelial cells and migrate downwards. This process is influenced by multiple factors, with MCP-1 being the most important due to its potent chemotactic effect on monocytes. Research has shown that increased ROS can upregulate MCP-1 expression in vascular smooth muscle cells21. In this study, we found that fluctuating hyperglycemia increased the content of MDA in monocytes more than sustained hyperglycemia, suggesting that fluctuating hyperglycemia exerts stronger oxidative stress damage on monocytes than sustained hyperglycemia, as MDA is a crucial indicator of cellular oxidative stress. Many publications report that greater fluctuations in blood glucose levels lead to more severe damage to the body. However, the specific mechanism of how blood glucose fluctuations damage monocytes is not entirely clear. Nevertheless, the significant decrease in oxidative stress markers observed in the drug group treated with the antioxidant ALA compared to the high glucose group indicates that oxidative stress may be one of the underlying mechanisms. This finding also provides a theoretical basis for the clinical use of antioxidants in diabetes treatment.

The antioxidant ALA was chosen in this study due to its distinctive pharmacological profile, which aligns closely with the pathophysiology of diabetes. While antioxidants such as vitamin C (water-soluble) and vitamin E (lipid-soluble) act primarily within specific cellular compartments, ALA exhibits amphiphilic properties, enabling it to function in both aqueous and lipid environments. This broad solubility supports its role as a versatile antioxidant capable of mitigating ROS across diverse cellular domains, including the cytosol and membrane structures. Moreover, ALA demonstrates a pronounced ability to target mitochondrial oxidative stress—a critical consideration given that hyperglycemia-driven superoxide generation predominantly arises from the mitochondrial electron transport chain, a central tenet of Brownlee’s unifying hypothesis for diabetic complications. By directly neutralizing mitochondrial ROS and facilitating the regeneration of endogenous antioxidants such as glutathione, vitamin C, and vitamin E, ALA represents a particularly suitable agent for examining glucose-induced oxidative injury18,19. Its documented efficacy in attenuating oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in both preclinical models and clinical settings further justifies its use in this mechanistic investigation20.

In terms of oxidative stress indicator detection, this experiment also included HO-1 because, although HO-1 is widely distributed throughout the body, its expression level is relatively high in monocytes and macrophages22. Upregulation of HO-1 expression can counteract glycosylation in monocytes, reduce the release of inflammatory mediators and adhesion molecules23, and directly inhibit the production of oxygen free radicals in monocytes, thereby counteracting oxidative stress effects and reversing stress-induced damage in monocytes24. Damage to monocytes can lead to increased expression of adhesion molecules such as intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) and vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1), which enhances monocyte adhesion ability. Once monocytes enter the subendothelium, they phagocytize lipids to form foam cells. ICAM-1 also mediates the accumulation of lymphocytes at sites of damage, jointly promoting the development of diabetic vascular complications. Studies have shown that the binding of AGEs to their receptors can lead to intracellular oxidative stress, which subsequently activates NF-κB to induce overexpression of genes encoding cell surface adhesion molecules such as ICAM-1, VCAM-1, and MCP-1, resulting in enhanced monocyte adhesion to endothelial cells and the initiation of inflammatory mechanisms underlying vascular disease25. By regulating the expression of MCP-1 and cellular adhesion molecules, NF-κB is involved in the entire process of monocyte infiltration and migration, playing a crucial role in the development of diabetic vascular complications. In type 2 diabetes patients, acute blood glucose fluctuations of any degree can significantly elevate MCP-1 levels in monocytes. As a primary leukocyte adhesion molecule, MCP-1 plays a role in cell adhesion, atherosclerosis, and thrombosis11.

The marked upregulation of HO-1 mRNA in fluctuation groups—correlating with ROS and MDA levels—likely represents an adaptive cellular defense against oxidative stress. HO-1, a stress-responsive enzyme, degrades heme into anti-inflammatory and antioxidant products (biliverdin, CO), thereby counteracting redox imbalance. Its stronger induction under high-amplitude fluctuations further supports the notion that glycemic variability imposes greater oxidative burden than sustained hyperglycemia.

Our data demonstrate a strong association between glucose fluctuation magnitude, ROS levels, and inflammatory cytokine expression, which was ameliorated by the antioxidant ALA. This suggests oxidative stress as a key mediator, though the precise sequence of events (e.g., ROS leading to inflammation vs. inflammatory signaling amplifying ROS) and the upstream sensors (e.g., RAGE, mitochondrial dysfunction) require further elucidation.

In summary, the results of this study initially indicate that human peripheral blood monocytes exhibit increased ROS production in a high-glucose environment, accompanied by an increase in serum MDA levels, suggesting evidence of pronounced oxidative stress and oxidative stress-induced damage. Furthermore, the expression of HO-1 mRNA in THP-1 cells is significantly upregulated compared to the control group, and this expression level is closely related to the level of oxidative stress in the body. Monocyte damage plays a crucial role in diabetic macroangiopathy. While previous literature has largely attributed inflammation and enhanced adhesion capabilities as potential damage mechanisms10. this study’s in vitro cellular experiments suggest that oxidative stress may also be one of the mechanisms involved, and this is further confirmed by drug intervention experiments. However, the exact mechanism of monocyte damage in diabetic patients remains to be verified through further experimental investigations.

Blood glucose fluctuations have a more potent damaging effect on human peripheral blood monocytes compared to sustained hyperglycemia. Monocytes, as crucial components of the immune system, play a key role in resisting infections and clearing pathogens. Therefore, monocyte damage caused by blood glucose fluctuations may increase the infection risk for diabetics and decrease their immune defense capabilities. Further research has found that blood glucose fluctuations also significantly disrupt cytokine expression, further exacerbating the inflammatory response in diabetics and accelerating the occurrence and progression of complications. This discovery not only reveals the important role of blood glucose fluctuations in the pathophysiological process of diabetes but also provides new insights for formulating diabetes treatment strategies. In clinical practice, besides intensifying blood glucose management, personalized treatment, and reducing blood glucose fluctuations, attention should also be paid to immune function.

This study has several limitations. First, it utilizes an in vitro monocyte cell line model, which, while controlled, cannot fully replicate the complex paracrine and systemic interactions present in vivo. Second, the glucose fluctuation regimen, though designed to mimic postprandial swings, may not capture the full spectrum of glycemic variability in diabetic patients. Third, while ALA ameliorated the observed effects, our study does not delineate the precise upstream sensors (e.g., mitochondrial dysfunction, RAGE activation) linking glucose fluctuations to oxidative stress. Future studies using primary human monocytes, co-culture systems, and animal models are needed to validate these findings and establish their direct pathophysiological relevance. Additionally, investigations incorporating intermediate amplitudes of glucose fluctuation in these models would help more precisely define the threshold and dose-response relationship linking glycemic variability to monocyte dysfunction. Finally, clinical studies correlating metrics of glucose variability with monocyte activation markers in patient cohorts would provide critical translational validation.
