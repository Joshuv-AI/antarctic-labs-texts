Speeding Up Hartree–Fock in JuliaChem with Density Fitting
Authors: John J. Hayes, Mark S. Gordon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this work, the density fitting (DF) approximation is added to the restricted Hartree–Fock (RHF) implementation in the JuliaChem computational chemistry code. Utilizing a DF algorithm that uses symmetry and integral screening, a significant reduction in time to compute the Fock matrix is achieved. The symmetry and screening DF-RHF techniques were adapted to be performed on graphics processing units (GPUs), which are well suited to perform the matrix multiplications that comprise the bulk of the Fock build time in DF-RHF. The JuliaChem DF-RHF GPU algorithm employs a novel approach that automatically switches between two DF-RHF algorithms depending on the number of basis functions in the calculation. The JuliaChem GPU DF-RHF implementation demonstrates up to 2× speedup for Fock build times compared to the existing best-in-class GPU DF-RHF implementation by operating directly on screened intermediate matrices. Due to the high portability of the Julia language code, the JuliaChem CPU and GPU DF-RHF implementations could be benchmarked on a variety of CPU and GPU architectures from multiple hardware vendors.

Introduction

Computational Chemistry in Julia

Computational chemistry codes are often written in “low-level” languages, such as FORTRAN and C++, as they offer high computational performance through fine-grained control over computer resources. However, low-level languages can be challenging to learn and use efficiently. Alternatively, high-level languages, such as Python, have syntax and programming patterns that are easier to learn but are difficult to make performant. The Julia − programming language was designed to address this gap between ease of use and performance.

The original Julia language paper was published in 2012 by Bezanson et al. In the years since, Julia has been used in fields such as machine learning, computational biology, high-energy physics, climate modeling, and many more. − There have been several packages built in Julia for computational chemistry, such as Molly.jl for molecular dynamics, DFTK.jl for experimenting with and prototyping density functional theory methods, and Fermi.jl for electronic structure theory, which has Hartree–Fock as well as several post-Hartree–Fock methods implemented.

In this work, the authors added new features to JuliaChem, , an electronic structure theory software package written in the Julia programming language. JuliaChem was implemented to demonstrate the viability of using Julia for the development of computational chemistry codes.

The most recent addition to the JuliaChem restricted Hartree–Fock (RHF) implementation was an improved load-balancing scheme that makes use of a “hybrid Message Passing Interface (MPI)/task-based” parallel algorithm. The parallel RHF implementation in JuliaChem is made possible by the Julia language features for multithreading (using multiple CPU cores in one process) and an open-source package MPI.jl for shared memory and distributed parallelism (running multiple processes on the same CPU or running on multiple nodes of a computer cluster). Making use of hybrid MPI/task parallelism, the authors displayed several novel techniques for calculating the RHF Fock matrix: collapsing the two-dimensional symmetric matrix to a single [index parallelizable] loop, a new scheme for dividing the shell quartets within the single-index loop into batches for load-balancing with minimal synchronization overhead, and a hybrid MPI/task-based distribution of batches of shell quartets. The JuliaChem RHF self-consistent field (SCF) method shows iteration speedups of up to 26% on systems that have up to ∼1000 basis functions relative to the MPI/OpenMP algorithm in the widely used electronic structure theory software package GAMESS. ,

In the present work, the authors explore expanding JuliaChem to make use of graphics processing units (GPUs), as they have become the computational backbone in many high-performance computing (HPC) systems, especially those obtained by the Department of Energy in its Exascale Computing Project. Examples are Aurora at Argonne National Laboratory and Frontier at Oak Ridge National Laboratory, both of which can provide >1018 floating-point operations (FLOPs) per second.

Restricted Hartree–Fock

In electronic structure theory calculations, RHF is the foundation of many of the most popular ab initio methods, e.g., second-order Møller–Plesset perturbation theory (MP2), coupled-cluster theory (CC), configuration interaction theory (CI), and others that can capture the electron correlation effects of a chemical system. The scaling is O(N 5) for MP2 and O(N 7) for the most popular CC method, CCSD­(T), where N is the number of basis functions. The scaling for the CI methods can be exponential. The formal scaling of post-Hartree–Fock methods can make using them for many systems of chemical interest intractable using computing resources that are available to most investigators. Therefore, highly accurate, lower-scaling approximations are needed to reduce computational bottlenecks. A commonly used approach is the density fitting (DF) approximation (also often called the resolution-of-the-identity (RI)).

DF approximates the four-center two-electron repulsion integral (ERI) tensor as a contraction of three-center two-electron and two-center two-electron integrals using an auxiliary basis set (see eqs and below). Applying the DF approximation does not reduce the scaling exponent of post-HF methods, but the approximation can reduce the prefactor (the constant factor in front of the O(N x )) of the scaling. For example, the prefactor can be reduced by up to 2 orders of magnitude for DF-MP2 compared with full MP2. The “relative error of 0.2% of the correlation energy appears acceptable since it is very much smaller than typical errors (about 15% for the cc-pVTZ basis set) or intrinsic errors of the MP2 approximation.”

Once the primary bottlenecks of a method are resolved, to improve performance further, one must address smaller bottlenecks. One such bottleneck is calculating the reference wave function for post-Hartree–Fock methods. The same DF approximation can be applied to Hartree–Fock (HF), and while DF does not change the scaling order of magnitude, DF can, for small- and medium-sized systems, improve the prefactor. On the other hand, RHF without DF will have better performance for large molecules due to the inability of the standard formulation , of DF-RHF to adequately make use of index screening during the calculation of the exchange contribution to the Fock matrix. A local orbital DF-RHF has been discussed as a means to reduce the scaling of the DF-RHF exchange component.

The DF approximation is expected to result in errors in the SCF energy relative to standard RHF “of about two orders of magnitude smaller than changes in the SCF energy introduced by a change of the basis set.” Compared to the energy errors due to the DF approximations in post-HF methods and changes in the basis set, DF-RHF gives nearly free speedups in terms of energy accuracy when DF-RHF is used as the reference wave function in place of RHF.

Due to the potential speedup of DF-RHF relative to full RHF for applicable chemical system sizes, DF-HF has been implemented in many computational chemistry codes for approximating Hartree–Fock. For example, DF-HF has been implemented in MOLPRO for RHF and restricted open shell HF (ROHF), in addition to a localized orbital DF, which “can be used to speed up the” calculation of the exchange terms. ORCA has a full DF-RHF routine (RI-JK), as well as one that calculates only the Coulomb term using DF (RI-J), and one which calculates Coulomb with DF and calculates exchange numerically (RIJCOSX). , PSI4 has a DF algorithm and emphasizes DF as an excellent choice as an initial guess or substitution for a full RHF calculation “unless either absolute accuracy is required [≥CCSD­(T)] or a JKFIT auxiliary basis is unavailable for the orbital basis/atoms involved.” , Additionally, the GTFock DF-RHF implementation is available in PSI4. Q-CHEM has implemented standard DF-RHF and a technique (called (occ-)­RI-K) for reducing the scaling of the exchange term by only including terms related to occupied orbitals. PySCF has implemented DF for CPU DF-RHF and for GPU via the extension GPU4PYSCF. Recently, Stocks, Palethorpe, and Barca introduced a multi-GPU implementation of DF-RHF in the EXESS code, which showed high strong scaling with 2–4 GPUs.

This work aims to (1) apply the DF approximation to the existing JuliaChem RHF SCF implementation using a similar hybrid MPI/task parallelism as the RHF routine when evaluating the two- and three-index integrals, thereby demonstrating the efficacy of DF-RHF in Julia for many applications; (2) explore making use of a variety of computer architectures, including Intel, ARM, and AMD CPUs, and AMD and NVIDIA GPUs, in a Julia computational chemistry code; and (3) inform future high-performance implementations of GPU-based DF-RHF and DF-ROHF methods in GAMESS and the related GPU library GMSHPC (previously called LIBCCHEM).

Implementation

The DF-RHF implementation in JuliaChem can be grouped into three components: (a) the calculation of the (P|Q) and (pq|P) integrals and the contraction of the (P|Q) and (pq|P) integrals into BpqQ , (b) the contractions to calculate the Coulomb and exchange components of the DF-RHF Fock matrix using BpqQ , and (c) combining the DF-RHF Fock matrix with the existing JuliaChem SCF routine.

The matrix–matrix and matrix–vector multiplications to calculate Fpq in JuliaChem DF-RHF are performed using the Julia Basic Linear Algebra Subprograms (BLAS) library. − The specific BLAS implementation can be chosen by the user at runtime based on the hardware being used, using the Julia BLAS implementation interoperability feature (libblastrampoline). In general, the BLAS implementations available in Julia are multithreaded (i.e., a single operation can use multiple CPU cores). JuliaChem makes use of multithreaded BLAS capabilities for several operations and uses single-threaded operations for others. Julia provides a method for changing the number of threads used by BLAS during runtime.

The CPU and GPU implementations share similar logic; therefore, the CPU implementation is described first, followed by a section that describes the key changes needed to create an efficient GPU implementation.

Calculating DF-RHF Integrals and BpqQ

The (P|Q) and (pq|P) DF-RHF integrals are calculated using a task-based approach similar to that described in the previous JuliaChem paper using the integral library LIBINT. In contrast to direct RHF where the 4–2-ERIs are calculated on each iteration, for DF-RHF, the (P|Q) and (pq|P) integrals are calculated and stored in CPU memory to be used for calculating BpqQ . The calculation of the (P|Q) and (pq|P) integrals is partitioned on the P index of both (e.g., thread 1 is assigned P = 1:10, thread 2 is assigned P = 11:20, thread 3 is assigned P = 21:30), and all (P|Q) and all (pq|P) integrals are calculated for the given value of P.

The (P|Q) and (pq|P) integrals are calculated only during the first iteration and then stored in CPU random-access memory (RAM). The integrals are used to calculate BpqQ (eq ) using a BLAS matrix multiplication. BLAS matrix multiplications require the input matrices to be in RAM; storing the integrals on disk would result in a performance bottleneck during storage and retrieval. BpqQ is stored in memory for all DF-RHF SCF iterations.

[J –1/2] PQ is computed using the Cholesky factorization of (P|Q). For numerical stability, it is necessary to perform the Cholesky factorization on a single rank (or GPU) and broadcast the result to all the other ranks (or GPUs). This broadcast of [J –1/2] PQ does not cause a significant bottleneck. The time to broadcast [J –1/2] PQ is included in the time to compute [J –1/2] PQ listed in the benchmark timings in Section .

For a DF-RHF calculation conducted using multiple MPI ranks, distributing the calculation of the (pq|P) integrals among the MPI ranks would be ideal. Complications arise when trying to compute the subset of BpqQ given to each rank. All (pq|P) values are needed to calculate any value of BpqQ . Therefore, all (pq|P) values need to be stored on all ranks. The subset of (pq|P) calculated on one rank would have to be transferred to all other ranks, and those ranks would then contract that subset of (pq|P) into the subset of BpqQ needed on that rank. One such method was explored in which the (pq|P) integrals were partitioned evenly among the ranks. An algorithm to distribute the integrals and pass them via MPI to the other ranks was implemented; however, no performance improvement was obtained. The limiting factor to an approach in which the (pq|P) calculations are distributed among the ranks is the internode communication overhead of the computer cluster being used.

In practice, with the speed of current node interconnect technology, for molecular system sizes for which DF-RHF provides speedups over RHF, it is faster to recompute all (pq|P) on all ranks and eliminate MPI communication of (pq|P) or BpqQ . This approach of recomputing the (pq|P) integrals on all ranks is used in JuliaChem.

In the case that the DF-RHF calculation is performed on one MPI rank, BpqQ is calculated using a multithreaded BLAS triangular matrix–matrix multiply (trmm) routine, making use of the PQ symmetry of [J –1/2] PQ . For DF-RHF calculations on multiple MPI ranks, the calculation of BpqQ is distributed using a “static” approach; that is, the work is evenly partitioned among the MPI ranks along the Q index. Any other choice of index load-balancing would require passing large amounts of data over MPI when calculating Fpq . The partitioning of BpqQ onto multiple ranks produces a matrix shape that does not match the data pattern required for using the BLAS triangular matrix–matrix multiply. A strategy to exploit the PQ symmetry of [J –1/2] PQ in calculating BpqQ for multi-rank (or multi-GPU) jobs was not explored, and a general matrix–matrix multiply (gemm) is used.

Evenly load-balancing the Q indices of BpqQ among the ranks is more important than any potential benefit of dynamically allocating segments of BpqQ during its calculation. If one rank were given significantly more Q indices than another while calculating BpqQ , the partial Fock matrix calculations (eqs –) on that rank would take longer than the other ranks, causing uneven load-balancing on every SCF iteration.

The (pq|P) and BpqQ matrices are stored in two-dimensional arrays as described in Huang et al., with one dimension for pq and one dimension for Q. Since Julia is a column-major language, the pq dimension must be stored in the columns of the array because the contraction of the intermediate WpiQ must have contiguous data regions corresponding to a primary basis set index for performant matrix–multiplication operations; therefore, the p index is stored in the column dimension. After BpqQ is calculated, the memory allocated for storing (P|Q), [J –1/2] PQ , and (pq|P) can be released or reused.

Calculating DF-RHF F pq

Two CPU DF-RHF Fock build algorithms are implemented in JuliaChem. The first is a nonscreened, nonsymmetric version (called dense DF-RHF), which makes use of multithreaded BLAS function calls. The dense DF-RHF algorithm calculates each term in eqs – as a single matrix–matrix or matrix–vector BLAS multiplication operation and does not make use of the pq symmetry in Jpq and Kpq . The second algorithm (called screened DF-RHF) makes use of the pq symmetry and Schwarz screening. Kpq and Jpq and the corresponding intermediates are calculated using single-threaded BLAS operations in parallel in the screened algorithm.

For sufficiently small molecular systems, less than approximately 300 basis functions depending on the CPU used, the dense algorithm is faster than the more complex screened algorithm; for small systems, the performance gained by using multithreaded BLAS calls outweighs the benefits of screening and symmetry, which require many parallel single-threaded BLAS operations. For larger systems, using the screened method is faster because of: (1) the reduction in the memory scaling of a screened B(pq)′Q , (2) the reduction in floating-point operations by using screened operations, and (3) the use of pq symmetry to calculate the Fock matrix. A table of benchmark results of symmetric vs nonsymmetric (dense) K pq calculations for small to medium molecules is shown in Table S11 in the Supporting Information.

Screened CPU DF-RHF Method

When calculating (pq|P) in the screened CPU method, JuliaChem screens shell pairs using eqs –. For all shell pairs that pass the screening threshold, the basis function pairs in each unscreened shell pair are assessed. The screening results are stored in Boolean matrices (one for the shell pairs and one for the basis pairs), which are then used in the integral routine to check if a shell triplet (ab|C) should be calculated; if the triplet is calculated, the corresponding basis function triplets (pq|P) are also checked and stored only if they also survive screening. A lookup table is created to map between the screened index (pq)′ and the unscreened index pq for mapping back values from the screened format to the full unscreened format of the Fock matrix (e.g., J(9)′→J1,12 , B(9)′1→B1,121) . A graphical representation of the data layout and mapping between pq and (pq)′ is shown in Figure . In all arrays in which (pq)′ screening is used, the pq indices are laid out in one dimension. B(pq)′Q is stored as a two-dimensional array with Q in the rows and (pq)′ in the columns. d(pq)′ is stored as a one-dimensional array.

Screened data mapping. An illustration of how pq indices in two dimensions are mapped to one dimension in the screened (pq)′ index space. Any screened value in the two-dimensional matrix is marked with a gray background. For example, (1,2) is index 6 in one-dimensional column-major pq indexing. Because (5,1) is screened, (1,2) maps to index 5 in the screened (pq)′ index space. The screened indices corresponding to a given q are kept contiguous (each column q is given a different background color). Storing in B(pq)′Q , J(pq)′ , and d(pq)′ in this screened manner reduces storage size and enables a reduction in the total floating-point operations required to calculate the DF-RHF Fock matrix.

The pq index is symmetric and therefore could be stored in a triangular format so that no repetitive data are stored (e.g., only p ≥ q is stored). A triangular storage algorithm was explored, but complications arise in the calculation of the VQ and WpiQ intermediate matrices, which result in poor performance. Optimized custom linear algebra routines could perhaps be implemented to perform such screened and triangular matrix operations, but such operations are not available in the linear algebra libraries of Julia.

To exploit the pq sparsity of B(pq)Q , when calculating the intermediate WpiQ , the contraction must be done as many matrix multiplications, one for each primary basis index p. The WpiQ values corresponding to the Q and i indices for a given p are kept contiguous (p is the last column of the matrix) such that the matrix multiplication to calculate WpiQ can be performed in one operation for a given p. For each p, a specially manipulated coefficient array Cri′ , in which only the r basis indices that survived the Schwarz screening with basis index p are stored (matching the layout of the screened (pq)′ dimension of B(pq)′Q ), is used to compute WpiQ for the given p (see eq ).

The Kpq matrix is partitioned into a grid of n-by-n square blocks. Due to symmetry, only blocks in the lower triangle and along the diagonal need to be computed (Figure b). The algorithm used to calculate Kpq is described in the GTFock paper, with two differences. First, GTFock uses a batched BLAS library, which handles the processing of the Kpq blocks in parallel. The Julia standard linear algebra libraries do not have methods for the batched gemm (general matrix multiply) functionality. Batched BLAS is a nonstandard add-on to the BLAS specification and is not included in all BLAS implementations. Julia BLAS is interoperable between multiple BLAS implementations. JuliaChem DF-RHF was designed to follow this interoperable pattern and only uses standard CPU BLAS routines to increase the types of hardware it could support without the need for any hardware-specific code.

Parallelizing DF-RHF symmetry operations. A graphical representation of the symmetric regions calculated for Jpq (a) and Kpq (b). For Jpq (a), only columns in the lower triangle are calculated (column data are contiguous in column-major languages). Each colored column corresponds to an index p. Each column is calculated using a single-threaded matrix–vector multiplication in parallel. For Kpq (b), matrix–matrix multiplications are performed to calculate blocks of the lower triangle (with overlap into the upper triangle on the diagonal). When the number of basis functions is not divisible by the block size, two strategies are employed. For the CPU implementation, each rectangular block (light blue) is combined with the full-sized (dark blue) boxes above it and calculated using a rectangular matrix multiplication. The green block on the diagonal is calculated separately. The GPU implementation combines all of the rectangular blocks (light blue) and the remaining square block on the diagonal (green) and performs one rectangular matrix multiplication. The symmetrical Jpq and Kpq matrices are summed into the Fpq matrix, and the Fpq values in the lower triangle are copied to the upper triangle of Fpq .

The parallel processing of the Kpq blocks was implemented using a simple Julia parallel loop over the blocks in the lower triangle. Blocks are calculated by using single-thread BLAS matrix multiplications. Each thread computes a single block; as a thread becomes available for more work, it is given the next unprocessed block. The calculation of subsequent blocks is repeated until all blocks have been calculated (and copied to the symmetric block on the other side of the matrix). Finding the best choice of block size is a compromise between having more blocks (less redundant calculations along the diagonal) and having matrix multiplications large enough to saturate the CPU core. If the number of blocks to be calculated is not divisible by the number of threads, then some threads are left without work to do, reducing CPU utilization. The Perlmutter CPU nodes at NERSC have 64 cores per CPU and therefore have a core-count-to-block-count mismatch problem. The problem of batching blocks is not relevant to the GPU implementation because of the different parallelism paradigms for GPUs.

An alternate way to increase CPU utilization could be to perform the Jpq work on those idle threads, but this possibility was not explored in this work; for larger systems, the contribution of Jpq to the total time to compute Fpq is relatively small.

A useful tool has been created by the Julia open-source community: the BLASBenchmarksCPU.jl that allows one to run benchmarks of the various Julia BLAS implementations. BLASBenchmarksCPU.jl was used to determine the block sizes used in the benchmarks listed in the Section .

Now, consider the Jpq contribution to the Fock matrix. Jpq and VQ can be calculated using standard matrix–vector multiplications. To make use of pq symmetry, the operations are performed using in-memory Julia subarrays (called “views” in Julia) of B(pq)′Q (and d(pq)′ for the calculation of VQ ) where p ≥ q (see Figure a for visualization). In the first SCF iteration, two look-up tables are created with values for each primary basis index p: one table gives the start index in the screened, one-dimensional (pq)′ index space; the second look-up table gives the number of unscreened pairs for the given p. With the start index and the number of unscreened (pq)′ indices, the memory locations in and can be determined for the matrix–vector multiplications necessary to calculate VQ and J pq .

Each subarray matrix–vector multiplication is performed using a single Julia thread. Each thread is given a subset of the p indices. Thread utilization is not an issue (as it is in the parallel calculation of Kpq ) because the number of basis functions is typically much larger than the number of threads. So, the remainder when dividing the number of basis functions p by the number of threads is small relative to p. Therefore, the time a thread may sit idle waiting for the other threads to finish calculating the last few p indices for Jpq is negligible compared to the total time to compute Jpq .

The last step is to add Jpq and Kpq into Fpq . Then, Fpq for each MPI rank is summed in a multi-rank calculation. Adding J(pq)′ to Fpq requires mapping the one-dimensional representation of J(pq)′ back to the two-dimensional unscreened representation and adding the values to the appropriate indices of Fpq . Fpq is then passed back to the calling JuliaChem SCF routine, as described in the JuliaChem RHF papers. ,

The overhead caused by utilizing screening in the DF contractions is most pronounced for small systems. Overhead due to the screened algorithm is most impactful in the calculation of WpiQ . The overhead occurs because the calculation requires many small subarray matrix multiplications (one for each p), which may not saturate the CPU core being used.

GPU DF-RHF Implementation

The CPU DF-RHF implementation was adapted to explore if a similar approach could improve the time to solution for the Fock build step of existing GPU DF-RHF implementations. , Calculating the DF-RHF Fock matrix on GPUs in JuliaChem follows the same general approach as the CPU method, as it contains two separate implementations: (1) screened for larger systems and (2) dense for smaller systems. Creating GPU code based on the JuliaChem CPU code was straightforward for DF-RHF because the backbone of the code is tensor contractions using linear algebra libraries. These libraries are provided by CUDA.jl , for NVIDIA GPUs, with an interface similar to the Julia CPU linear algebra libraries. For the GPU implementation, arrays that correspond to the CPU arrays were created and allocated for the integrals (P|Q) and (pq|P), as well as the intermediates, Jpq , Kpq , and Fpq . JuliaChem does not have GPU ERI capabilities; therefore, the integrals are computed on the CPU. (P|Q), (pq|P), the core Hamiltonian, and the MO coefficients are copied from the CPU to the GPU memory. Times to transfer the integrals to the GPU are shown in Table S3 in the Supporting Information. Calculating ERIs on GPUs using Julia will be the subject of future work.

The linear algebra calls were converted from BLAS to CUDA BLAS (cuBLAS). After the Fock matrix is calculated on the GPU, it is copied back from the GPU to the CPU and returned to the CPU SCF routine. JuliaChem can use multiple GPUs on a node, allocating a portion of the Fock build to each GPU in a manner similar to how the CPU algorithm distributes the auxiliary indices to the MPI ranks. The partial Fock matrices from the GPUs are summed on the CPU before being passed back to the SCF routine. The only use of Julia multithreading in the GPU DF-RHF Fock tensor contractions is to execute operations on multiple GPUs in parallel.

Similar to the CPU algorithm, Schwarz screening can be used in the dense DF-RHF GPU algorithm to calculate BpqQ ; B(pq)′Q is calculated and then fully decompressed to BpqQ . The operation and memory scaling of the dense DF-RHF algorithm listed in Figure do not include screening. The dense DF-RHF GPU calculations reported in Section did not use screening for (pq|P) or BpqQ .

A key addition to the GPU implementation, which is not present in the CPU implementation, is an “adaptive” algorithm that automatically chooses to use either the dense or screened algorithm depending on the number of basis functions. For sufficiently small systems (less than 500 basis functions), the overhead of doing screened tensor contractions (which requires many small matrix multiplications for the calculation of WpiQ ) is sufficiently high that computing each of the arrays (VQ , WpiQ , Jpq , and Kpq ) as single dense operations is faster because those operations better saturate the GPU resources. JuliaChem provides an input file parameter to change the threshold number of basis functions that determines if the dense or screened implementation is used by the adaptive algorithm (the default is 800 basis functions); this parameter is used for tuning on different GPU architectures.

The screened GPU implementation includes the use of batched matrix multiplications for the calculation of WpiQ . CUDA.jl includes a “grouped batched” gemm functionality that allows the submission of many matrix multiplications in one function call (grouped by multiplications with the same input and output matrix shapes) and handles the parallel execution of the matrix multiplications. Grouped batched gemm is used to calculate the many matrix multiplications needed to calculate WpiQ in the screened algorithm. Batching is particularly important for smaller systems, for which asingle gemm for calculating W for a p does not saturate the GPU resources.

The GPU algorithm automatically chooses the block sizes for the calculation of Kpq , ensuring that the number of operations per block exceeds some threshold. If the block sizes are too small, then the GPU is not saturated by matrix multiplication. The threshold value is configurable, such that the algorithm can be tuned to various GPUs by the user. When the number of basis functions is not divisible by the number of blocks, the remaining region not covered by the square blocks is calculated as one rectangular matrix multiplication (see Figure b).

Following the design of the dense CPU algorithm, the dense GPU algorithm does not use pq symmetry when calculating the Fock matrix. The dense GPU algorithm is faster than the screened GPU algorithm for systems larger than that for the corresponding CPU comparison. Including an option to use symmetric operations in the dense GPU algorithm could provide additional Fock build speedups with respect to the screened GPU algorithm for medium-sized systems, especially with the calculation of Kpq . This option will be explored in the future.

CUDA kernels were written (using CUDA.jl) to calculate the mapping between screened indices (pq)′ and nonscreened indices pq and to perform the copying of data from one storage pattern to the other, thereby creating the (pq)′ to pq mapping table on the GPU. This is followed by the creation of a screened density array d(pq)′ , copying the screened J(pq)′ to the Fock matrix, and copying the Kpq data from the lower triangle to the upper triangle.

Continuing the theme of making use of the portability of the Julia language and the availability of open-source packages to utilize various GPU hardware, a proof-of-concept GPU implementation was developed to allow the use of AMD GPUs. The refactoring process was straightforward because CUDA.jl and AMDGPU.jl are developed in conjunction with one another by the Julia open-source community to have very similar, if not identical, application programming interfaces (APIs) for GPU operations. There are only a few places where code specific to the different GPU architectures was necessary (e.g., custom GPU kernels for mapping screened to unscreened indices and allocation of GPU memory). As a result, only a small percentage of the code for the GPU implementation is specific to a particular hardware architecture. The AMD proof-of-concept does not include batching of the matrix multiplications for WpiQ .

Results and Discussion

Computational Approach

A set of benchmarking calculations for this study was conducted on several high-performance computing systems: (1) The Iowa State University Nova cluster, which features Intel Skylake Xeon Platinum 8260 processors (24-core, dual-socket, 2.40 GHz). These nodes are comparable to (but not identical to) the Intel Xeon Skylake 8180 processors used in the previous JuliaChem paper and to the Intel Xeon Skylake Platinum 8160 hardware employed in benchmarking the GTFock DF-RHF. (2) CPU and GPU calculations were conducted on the NERSC Perlmutter system: CPU-only calculations were run on dual-socket AMD EPYC 7763 nodes, while GPU-accelerated calculations used Perlmutter GPU nodes, each equipped with a single AMD EPYC 7763 CPU and four NVIDIA A100 GPUs (40 GB memory each). (3) Proof-of-concept implementations utilizing AMD GPUs were benchmarked on the Frontier system at Oak Ridge National Laboratory, featuring nodes with a 64-core AMD EPYC 7713 “Trento” CPU (2.0 GHz) and four AMD MI250 GPUs (64 GB memory each). (4) CPU-only calculations were performed on the Aurora Supercomputer at Argonne National Laboratory: Each Aurora node has two Intel Xeon CPU Max 9470C Series CPUs (52 cores per CPU). (5) CPU and GPU benchmarks were conducted on an NVIDIA Grace Hopper system, which integrates a 72-core ARM-based Neoverse-V2 CPU and a single H100 GPU (96 GB memory). In the results presented below, these systems referred to as Skylake, Perlmutter CPU, Perlmutter GPU, Frontier, Aurora, and Grace Hopper, respectively.

All calculations on dual-socket nodes (Skylake, Perlmutter CPU, Aurora) were performed by using two MPI ranks per node. Multithreaded BLAS operations do not perform well when BLAS is configured to use all cores on multiple CPU sockets. Binding each MPI rank to the cores of a single socket and setting the number of BLAS threads for the run to the number of cores on a single socket gives the best performance.

Different BLAS packages were used to calculate the DF-RHF tensor contractions, depending on the hardware being used. BLASBenchmarksCPU.jl was used to choose the Julia BLAS package for each system. MKL.jl was used for Intel CPUs (Skylake and Aurora); BLISBLAS.jl was used for AMD hardware (Perlmutter, Frontier) nodes; LinearAlgebra.jl (the default for Julia) was used for Grace Hopper nodes. CUDA.jl was used for all of the NVIDIA GPU calculations. The proof-of-concept AMD implementation relies on AMDGPU.jl. AMDGPU.jl provides a Julia interface to make use of the ROCm (Radeon Open Compute) functionality, such as rocBLAS.

The DF-RHF screening threshold σ2 (eq ) was set to 10–12 for all calculations. The JuliaChem RHF integral screening thresholds and techniques are the same as those described in the previous paper.

The reported Fock build times for GPU calculations do not include the time to copy (pq|P) or (P|Q) from the CPU to the GPU. The other GPU codes considered in this work calculate ERIs on the GPU and, therefore, do not require this copy step. CPU to GPU copy times on a Perlmutter GPU node for the polyglycine tests are provided in Table S3 in the Supporting Information. Times for copying the updated coefficient matrix to the GPU(s), copying the Fock matrix back to the CPU, and summing the partial Fock matrices for multiple GPU runs are included in the reported Fock build benchmark times. JuliaChem can be configured to run multi-GPU calculations either on one MPI rank using multiple GPUs, with parallelism handled by Julia parallel tasks, or by running the calculations using one GPU per rank. All benchmark calculations reported in this work use the multi-GPU task-based method, as MPI communications would add unnecessary overhead.

The full results of all benchmarks, scripts used to run benchmarks, and accompanying scripts for parsing the results are included on the JuliaChem.jl github.com page (https://github.com/juliachem-jl/JuliaChem-Papers). Tables of Fock build times and other relevant data from the plots and figures below are provided as Supporting Information.

CPU DF-RHF Energy Correctness

To ensure that JuliaChem DF-RHF meets the expected threshold of energy correctness with respect to RHF, calculations were performed on the S22(3) test set using JuliaChem RHF and the DF-RHF screened algorithm with the cc-pVDZ/cc-PVDZ-JKFIT and cc-PVTZ-JKFIT basis set pairs, with an SCF energy convergence threshold of 1 × 10–8 h. Polly et al. explored the errors that occur due to using the DF-RHF approximation and found that SCF energy differences are much less significant than other typical errors in HF and post-HF methods such as basis set errors, “which can be up to 4 kcal mol–1 [6.4 × 10–3 h].” The range of DF-RHF absolute energy errors compared to RHF for cc-pVDZ/cc-PVDZ-JKFIT and cc-pVTZ/cc-PVTZ-JKFIT are 3.45 × 10–5 h to 2.61 × 10–3 h and 5.24 × 10–6 h to 7.14 × 10–4 h, respectively. These RHF vs DF-RHF absolute energy differences fall into the expected magnitudes, and the magnitude of the errors decreases with an increase in basis set size as expected.

One can use DF-RHF orbitals as an initial guess for a full RHF calculation, so this option has been implemented in JuliaChem. DF-RHF as an initial guess method can attain full RHF accuracy while improving computational efficiency. At each iteration, an SCF energy and root-mean-squared density (RMSD) convergence check is performed; if the changes in SCF energy and RMSD are lower than the user-specified guess convergence values, RHF is used for all subsequent iterations until SCF convergence is achieved. DF-RHF as an initial guess for RHF in JuliaChem was benchmarked for energy differences with respect to RHF, with the core Hamiltonian as a guess. JuliaChem switches from using DF-RHF to using RHF when energy and root-mean-squared density (RMSD) differences between two iterations reach thresholds chosen by the user at runtime. For the S22(3) test set, switching threshold parameters of 10–3 h for the iteration change in energy and 10–2 e/a03 (electron charge density per unit volume in atomic units) for iteration RMSD changes, and an overall SCF convergence threshold of 10–8 h were used; the range of absolute SCF energy errors relative to JuliaChem RHF without DF-RHF as the guess for cc-pVDZ/cc-PVDZ-JKFIT and cc-pVTZ/cc-PVTZ-JKFIT are 7.00 × 10–10 h to 4.78 × 10–8 h and 5.00 × 10–10 h to 3.31 × 10–7 h, respectively. These energy differences are near or below the order of magnitude of the overall SCF convergence threshold.

Benchmarks run with other DF-RHF guess switching threshold parameters were tested; the results can be found in the full benchmark set on the JuliaChem GitHub. Using an energy switching threshold of 10–6 h or smaller can cause SCF energy differences on the order of the DF-RHF vs RHF error (up to 10–4 h for the S22(3) test set); these energy differences occur because the calculation converges too close to the DF-RHF result, and subsequent RHF iterations are not able to converge to the correct SCF result. Optimizing the best choice of these switching threshold parameters was not explored in this work.

CPU DF-RHF Results

The DF-RHF CPU implementations in JuliaChem were benchmarked against the existing RHF implementation using the S22(3) test set with the 6-311++G­(2d,2p) basis set and aug-cc-pVTZ-JKFIT for the auxiliary basis set. The range of speedups (defined as the average RHF Fock build time divided by the average DF-RHF Fock build time) for Skylake nodes is 12–50×; for Aurora, the range is 8–48×; for Perlmutter CPU nodes, the range is 4–10×. DF-RHF speedups versus RHF on Perlmutter AMD CPUs are lower than the speedups on Intel CPUs on Skylake and Aurora. The most significant cause is the performance of the BLAS implementations available in Julia that can be used on AMD CPUs, compared to BLAS implementations that can be used on Intel CPUs. BLAS functions optimized for Intel CPUs are provided by the Intel Math Kernel Library (MKL) using the MKL.jl library. BLISBLAS.jl and LinearAlgebra.jl (which uses OpenBLAS) both perform similarly for JuliaChem DF-RHF on AMD hardware. BLISBLAS.jl and LinearAlgebra.jl perform single CPU core matrix multiplication on a Perlmutter CPU node much more slowly than the same matrix multiplication on a single CPU core of a Skylake node by using MKL.jl. A benchmark set of matrix multiplication times using matrix sizes and shapes relevant to the JuliaChem DF-RHF main bottleneck (calculation of the exchange blocks), on Perlmutter CPU, Skylake, and Aurora nodes is provided in Tables S1 and S2 in the Supporting Information.

For a matrix multiplication C = A × B, matrix A has dimensions M by K, B has dimensions K by N, and C has dimensions M by N. Square matrix multiplication (M = N = K) on one CPU core was used to find the maximum achievable FLOP rate per CPU core for a given CPU using Julia BLAS on the CPUs used to benchmark JuliaChem. The maximum FLOP rates per CPU core were 45.8 GFLOPS, 82.0 GFLOPS, and 94.2 GFLOPS for Perlmutter, Skylake, and Aurora CPUs, respectively (see Table S1 in the Supporting Information for FLOP rates at different matrix sizes).

Nonsquare matrix multiplications on one CPU core were benchmarked to find the FLOP rates per CPU core for matrix multiplications of the typical matrix shapes for the main bottleneck of DF-RHF: calculating the exchange contribution to the Fock matrix. The matrix dimensions used follow the size of one exchange lower triangle block (Figure b) for the linear alkanes in the GTFock test set (molecule details are shown in Table ). For the smallest matrix multiplication (M = N = 51, K = 78,975), the FLOP rates per CPU core are 9.9 GFLOPS, 27.7 GFLOPS, and 28.3 GLOPS for Perlmutter, Skylake, and Aurora CPUs, respectively. For the largest matrix multiplication (M = N = 301, K = 346,801), the FLOP rates per CPU core are 30.2 GFLOPS, 35.7 GFLOPS, and 45.8 GFLOPS for Perlmutter, Skylake, and Aurora CPUs, respectively (see Table S2 in the Supporting Information for the FLOP rates of all matrix sizes that were benchmarked).

As a result of the slower performance of the BLAS implementations of nonsquare matrix–matrix multiplication operations optimized for AMD CPUs, DF-RHF calculations show worse performance than might be expected on Perlmutter CPU nodes. Although a Perlmutter node (64 cores per CPU) has approximately 2.6 times as many cores per CPU as a Skylake node (24 cores per CPU), for the matrix multiplication benchmark, the Skylake node, using MKL, has a 2.8 times faster maximum FLOP rate per CPU core than Perlmutter CPU nodes, using BLISBLAS for the smallest matrix multiplication test (M = N =51, K = 78975). Comparable performance is observed using OpenBLAS on Perlmutter CPU nodes; the results of these benchmarks are shown in Table S2 in the Supporting Information. Using MKL on Perlmutter CPU nodes did not improve the performance.

As a result of the BLAS performance, DF-RHF exchange performance on Skylake nodes is comparable to or better than the results on Perlmutter CPU nodes for small systems (# of primary basis functions <500). For the largest matrix multiplication benchmark, Skylake nodes have a 1.2 times faster FLOP rate per CPU core. With similar FLOP rates per core but more cores per CPU, Perlmutter CPU nodes perform DF-RHF exchange faster than Skylake nodes for larger molecular systems.

Another reason for suboptimal DF-RHF speedups compared to RHF (on any type of CPU) is a mismatch between the number of CPU cores and any possible division of the lower triangle of the exchange matrix into square blocks (Figure b), leading to suboptimal load balancing. The number of lower triangle blocks is equal to b × (b + 1)/2, where b is the number of blocks by which the width and height of the matrix are divided. For example, a 10-by-10 grid of blocks results in 55 blocks in the lower triangle; on a 64-core CPU, nine cores will be unused during the exchange calculation. An 11-by-11 grid of blocks results in 66 blocks in the lower triangle; on a 64-core CPU, 62 cores are unused, while the final 2 blocks are calculated on 2 cores. For Perlmutter CPUs using 55 blocks, 9 of 64 cores are not used for the entire exchange calculations; for Skylake CPUs using 55 blocks, all cores are used for the first 48 blocks; for the remaining 7 blocks, only 7 of 24 cores are used. Increasing the number of blocks would reduce idle core times; however, the FLOP rates for matrix multiplications on small matrices are lower than those for larger ones. Increasing the number of blocks beyond 55 did not show improvements for the systems benchmarked in this work.

Underutilization of CPU cores reduces the performance of the exchange matrix calculation, which is the most time-consuming part of the DF-RHF Fock build. In contrast, the RHF routine readily parallelizes to any number of cores. The JuliaChem RHF parallel implementation distributes the work to cores by assigning subsets of the shell quartet indices; the number of shell quartet indices is typically much larger than the core count of a CPU; therefore, the amount of time a core waits for other cores to finish the last shell quartets is minimal in comparison to the total Fock build time for an SCF iteration.

There are several possible strategies to improve the DF-RHF performance: (1) inclusion of a batched gemm (general matrix multiply) procedure (from some new implementation in the Julia BLAS library or by including some other external linear algebra package into JuliaChem), (2) using a more complex way of dividing the matrix multiplication using rectangles of differing sizes such that the number of rectangles is equal to the number of threads, (3) partitioning BpqQ for each thread in a similar manner to that done for MPI ranks (increasing the total number of blocks calculated in parallel), (4) writing a custom multithreaded matrix multiply kernel for the specific case of symmetry and sparsity of the exchange matrix, or (5) using multiple multithreaded BLAS when there are available cores. Investigation of alternative methods of calculating the exchange matrix for the nondivisible block size is in the planning stage.

The numerical suffix at the end of each molecule name is the number of atoms in the system (more details are provided in Table ). # Basis functions is the number of basis functions of the molecule. # Nodes is the number of nodes used for the benchmark calculation. Memory (MB) is the amount of RAM required for storing the matrices used to calculate the Fock build. Build times for calculating the (pq|P), [J –1/2] PQ , B(pq)′Q , Kpq , and the DF-RFH Fock build using pq symmetry are averages of multiple runs.

As the system size grows, the GTFock and JuliaChem Fock build times on the Skylake nodes begin to converge. This convergence in performance is expected. While GTFock and JuliaChem use different versions of BLAS, the matrix multiplication implementations in both BLAS libraries are highly optimized for the CPUs being used and will reach similar computational throughput thresholds for those CPUs as the molecular system size grows (corresponding to matrix multiplications of larger matrices). Also, as the molecular system size grows, an increasing amount of the Fock build time is spent performing BLAS matrix multiplications for the calculation of the exchange matrix (eq ). For example, for Alkane-62, 37% of the Fock build time is used to calculate the exchange matrix; for Alkane-362, 69% of the total Fock build time is used to calculate the exchange matrix. GTFock uses a batched-BLAS matrix multiplication routine for load balancing the exchange calculation. JuliaChem CPU DF-RHF performs the load balancing without a batched-BLAS routine. When calculating large matrix multiplications, the throughput for both the GTFock batched-BLAS-based exchange calculation and the JuliaChem exchange calculation is governed by the underlying BLAS matrix multiplication. For example, when both GTFock and JuliaChem use symmetry to calculate the DF-RHF Fock matrix (on similar but not identical Intel Skylake CPUs), the Fock build time for the smallest alkane system tested (Alkane-62, 510 basis functions) is 0.12 s and 0.076 s for JuliaChem and GTFock DF-RHF, respectively; for the five largest alkane systems, Alkane-122 to Alkane-362, the average Fock build times of JuliaChem DF-RHF are 0.79 s, 3.14 s, 5.35 s, 3.18 s, and 5.83 s (Table ), while the GTFock Fock build times are 0.67 s, 2.76 s, 4.54 s, 2.89 s, and 5.33 s.

As mentioned above, due to the lack of Schwarz screening in the calculation of Kpq at a sufficiently large molecular size, standard RHF with Schwarz screening will outperform DF-RHF. For the GTFock DF-RHF and RHF implementations, the molecular size at which RHF begins to match or outperform DF-RHF using the cc-pVDZ/cc-pVDZ-RIFIT basis is between 300 and 400 atoms, depending on molecular geometry. The DF-RHF performance of JuliaChem DF-RHF is comparable to that of GTFock DF-RHF, and the system sizes where RHF outperforms JuliaChem DF-RHF are similar. This comparison was not performed against JuliaChem RHF because the GTFock RHF performance is better than JuliaChem RHF due to the higher-performing 4–2-ERIs used in GTFock.

When running the GTFock test set on Perlmutter CPU nodes, lower performance relative to the CPU core count is observed for the JuliaChem DF-RHF Fock build when calculating the smallest systems in the GTFock benchmark test set. This low performance with respect to the CPU core count, as described in detail above in the S22(3) test set analysis, is caused by the lower BLAS FLOP rates for small nonsquare matrix multiplications on AMD CPUs and the CPU core to Kpq blocks count mismatch.

The screened CPU DF-RHF Fock build shows good strong scaling for multiple CPUs when operating on molecular systems which saturate the CPU. For example, Alkane-242 has 1.9, 2.7, and 3.4× speedup over a single-node calculation when using 2, 3, and 4 nodes, respectively. Figure contains a plot of the strong scaling results on 1–4 CPUs for a representative selection of the GTFock test set using the cc-pVDZ/cc-pVDZ-RIFIT basis set pair on Perlmutter CPU nodes. The DF-RHF algorithm is readily run in parallel on multiple nodes because tensor contractions for the DF-RHF Fock build can be partitioned on the auxiliary basis index Q of the intermediate matrices. The strong scaling speedup is highest for the larger molecules. The strong scaling performance improvement with increasing system size is due to the increasing time spent performing the matrix–matrix multiplications for calculating Kpq . With each additional node used for a DF-RHF calculation, the time spent communicating the partial Fock matrices between nodes increases, thereby decreasing the overall parallel efficiency.

GPU DF-RHF Energy Correctness

The screened GPU SCF energies were compared with the screened CPU DF-RHF energies of the EXESS polyglycine test set using the cc-pVDZ/cc-pVDZ-RIFIT basis set pair; the SCF energy differences are between 2.00 × 10–10 h and 6.15 × 10–8 h. These differences are near or below the threshold set for SCF energy convergence, 10–8 h, demonstrating that the GPU DF-RHF implementation has energy correctness similar to that of CPU DF-RHF relative to RHF.

GPU Performance Benchmarks

The screened algorithm (using Schwarz screening and pq symmetry) and the dense algorithm (no screening and no pq symmetry used) were benchmarked on a Perlmutter GPU node using one GPU (Figure ). Below 500 basis functions, the dense algorithm, which calculates each of the matrices in eqs – with one matrix multiplication and does not use pq symmetry, outperforms the screened algorithm. The screened algorithm is slower for these small systems due to the overhead related to calculating WpiQ using a screened B(pq)′Q , which requires a matrix multiplication for each primary basis index p. The dense and screened algorithms have similar Fock build times for 500–600 basis functions on Perlmutter. The screened algorithm for ≥700 basis functions shows significant performance improvements, up to a 2× Fock build time speedup, and uses much less memory. For example, for Glycine7, the screened algorithm uses approximately 2.5 times less memory than the dense algorithm.

With the performance crossover point between the screened and dense algorithms established on the Perlmutter GPU nodes, the adaptive algorithm could be configured for optimal performance and comparison with other codes. Below 500 basis functions, the JuliaChem adaptive algorithm is configured to use the dense algorithm, and above 500 basis functions, the screened algorithm is used. This crossover point varies based on the gemm performance of the GPU used; all benchmarks below will indicate the crossover point used.

Figure shows the Fock build times of the JuliaChem adaptive algorithm compared with the DF-RHF implementations of GPU4PYSCF (an extension to PySCF, a computational chemistry package written in Python and C) and EXESS (a GPU-focused computational chemistry package written in C++). Fock build times on one NVIDIA A100 GPU for EXESS were taken from the benchmark outputs supplied in the Supporting Information of Stocks et al. The Fock build times for GPU4PYSCF were taken from calculations performed by the current authors (GPU4PYSCF outputs are provided as Supporting Information) on a Perlmutter GPU node using one A100 GPU.

JuliaChem outperformed GPU4PYSCF for all 18 glycine molecules. For systems with less than 750 basis functions, JuliaChem DF-RHF Fock build times are between 2.1 and 25.3× faster than GPU4PYSCF; for systems with greater than 750 basis functions, JuliaChem adaptive GPU Fock build times are between 1.8 and 2.1× faster than GPU4PYSCF. The larger molecule tests demonstrate the competitiveness of the JuliaChem and GPU4PYSCF DF-RHF algorithms. For larger molecules, most of the time to solution for the Fock build is taken up by doing BLAS operations, which are provided by CUDA. The JuliaChem screened algorithm makes better use of the available GPU resources and therefore has a faster time to solution.

EXESS outperforms JuliaChem for small systems (0–500 primary basis functions), for which the times are very short. This occurrence is due partly to the Julia language overhead when calling CUDA library functions and partly to the fact that the JuliaChem dense GPU algorithm does not use pq symmetry. Between 500 and 750 basis functions, the Fock build times for JuliaChem and EXESS are similar. On Perlmutter, above 500 basis functions, JuliaChem switches from the dense algorithm to the screened algorithm. At 700 basis functions (Glycine9), EXESS and JuliaChem GPU DF-RHF both take, on average, ∼0.04 s to calculate the Fock matrix. At 750 basis functions, JuliaChem begins to outperform EXESS. The speedups compared to EXESS above 750 basis functions are between 1.3 and 2.1×. These speedups are due to two factors. First, JuliaChem operates on a screened B(pq)′Q , which speeds up the calculations of WpiQ , VQ , and Jpq . Second, if there is not sufficient memory on the GPU to store the entire unscreened BpqQ , EXESS stores B(pq)′Q in GPU memory and loops and decompresses segments of B(pq)′Q to the BpqQ format, which will fit into GPU memory, and performs dense matrix multiplications on those segments of BpqQ to calculate that segment’s contribution to Fpq . In contrast, JuliaChem operates directly on B(pq)′Q and therefore does not spend time decompressing B(pq)′Q .

JuliaChem does not yet have GPU RHF, so a performance crossover analysis could not be conducted.

The adaptive GPU algorithm shows good strong scaling speedup for the Fock build step. Figure contains a plot of the strong scaling results for a demonstrative selection of the polyglycine molecules tested. The strong scaling speedup is highest for the largest molecules for the given underlying algorithm used by the adaptive algorithm. The dense algorithm is used for Glycine6, while the screened algorithm is used for Glycine10, Glycine15, and Glycine18. The strong scaling performance improvement with increasing system size is due to the increasing time spent performing the matrix–matrix multiplications for calculating Kpq . For large system sizes, calculating K pq takes up over 75% of the total Fock build time; this step can be parallelized onto multiple GPUs with little overhead by distributing work along the auxiliary indices, resulting in strong scaling speedups approaching the ideal 1× additional speedup per GPU added.

The proof-of-concept JuliaChem DF-RHF for AMD GPUs was benchmarked on an AMD MI250 GPU on the Frontier supercomputer. Fock build times are reported in Figure . Although the MI250 has a higher theoretical maximum performance (23.9 TFLOPS) than an NVIDIA A100 (19.50 TFLOPS), the Fock build times on AMD MI250 GPUs are slower than those on NVIDIA A100 GPUs. For example, for Glycine18, the average Fock build time on an A100 is 0.41 s and the average Fock build time on an MI250 is 0.55 s. Peak performance FLOP rates are often calculated by performing a matrix multiplication on two large square matrices (i.e., a multiplication of a matrix of dimension M-by-K multiplied by a matrix of dimension K-by-N, where M = K = N) using general matrix multiply (gemm).

GPU Fock builds the performance frontier. Fock build time scaling on a Frontier node using one AMD MI250 GPU compared with one Perlmutter node using one NVIDIA A100 GPU. Polyglycine molecules ranging from 100 to 1,375 primary basis functions (cc-pVDZ/cc-pVDZ-RIFIT basis set pair) were used. The slower Fock build times compared to an NVIDIA A100 GPU are due to the performance of the rocBLAS gemm operations on nonsquare matrix multiplications used in calculating the exchange matrix. Fock build times are displayed on a log scale.

The most time-consuming step in DF-RHF is the matrix multiplication for calculating the exchange matrix. However, the dimensions of the input matrices are not square (and K is much greater than M and N), and the left matrix in the multiplication matrices is transposed. The NVIDIA cuBLAS gemm kernel shows better performance for these nonsquare matrix multiplications. AMD rocBLAS displayed poor performance for the case in which M is not equal to N, and M and N are very small in comparison to K. To avoid these poor-performing matrix multiplications, the number of basis functions must be divisible by the Kpq block width. Only using divisible block widths limits the ability to scale the number of Kpq blocks linearly with increasing system size. For example, for a system of size 1,755 basis functions, the best performance might come from using 55 lower triangle blocks, but this would leave a small rectangle remaining outside the square. Calculating the remaining rectangle for some systems in the polyglycine test took nearly as long as the rest of the symmetric Kpq block matrix multiplications. Improving performance for the nondivisible case would require creating a custom gemm kernel for the nonsquare matrix occurrence on AMD GPUs or significantly adapting the JuliaChem DF-RHF GPU exchange algorithm. Neither of these possibilities was explored in this work.

Conclusions

In this work, the authors successfully integrated the density fitting (DF) approximation into the restricted Hartree–Fock (RHF) implementation in JuliaChem. By exploiting Fock matrix symmetry, using Schwarz screening, and implementing a flexible parallel algorithm, the JuliaChem DF–RHF implementation achieves substantial reductions in Fock matrix build times across a wide range of chemical systems and hardware platforms.

On GPUs, JuliaChem introduces a novel adaptive algorithm that dynamically selects between dense and screened DF-RHF algorithms based on the molecular system size. The GPU algorithm achieves up to 2× speedups over existing state-of-the-art GPU DF-RHF codes by operating directly on a Schwarz-screened intermediate matrix, removing the need to compress and decompress data on the GPU.

The DF-RHF methods developed here lay the groundwork for creating high-performance CPU and GPU DF-RHF implementations in existing popular GPU-enabled electronic structure packages such as GAMESS. , Insights gained in this work will guide future investigations by the authors in improving the performance of calculating the DF-RHF exchange matrix.

The work presented in this paper continues to demonstrate the competitiveness of the Julia language as a high-performance programming language for computational chemistry. JuliaChem, enabled by the Julia language features and the packages available in the Julia ecosystem, serves as a flexible and easy-to-use platform for algorithm development and experimentation on a wide array of cutting-edge HPC systems built with many different CPU and GPU hardware architectures. The use of Julia as the implementation language proves to be advantageous for rapid code development, cross-platform portability, and integration with high-performance linear algebra libraries, thereby leading to competitively performant code. The GPU implementation, in particular, benefits from the Julia GPU package ecosystem, enabling support for both NVIDIA and AMD GPUs with minimal architecture-specific code. The authors intend to expand JuliaChem by adding more methods, such as perturbation theory, coupled-cluster, and open-shell methods, and adding more capabilities, such as native Julia CPU and GPU ERIs, reduced-precision methods, and integration with other electronic structure theory codes.
