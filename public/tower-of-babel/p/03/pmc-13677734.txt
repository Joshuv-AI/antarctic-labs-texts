Structure and mechanism of inhibition of lysine demethylase 2A (KDM2A) by compound 183c
Authors: Pavel Mader, Linshan Liu, Frédéric Vallée, Francois Denis, Victor Pau, Daniel Mao, Lauren Caldwell, Kin Chan, Igor Kurinov, Yael Mamane, Paranjay Parikh, Zemin Zhang, Michel Gallant, Mike Tyers, W Cameron Black, Michael Zinda, Daniel Durocher, Jeff Wrana, Michal Zimmermann, Frank Sicheri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLysine demethylase 2A (KDM2A) is a key epigenetic regulator and promising target for inhibition since its loss is selectively lethal in cancers reliant on Alternative Lengthening of Telomeres (ALT). Here we investigated the mechanism of compound 183c, a potent KDM2A inhibitor described in the patent literature. Biophysical studies showed that 183c requires the co-factor 2-oxoglutarate (2OG) for binding. A 2.2 Å crystal structure of the KDM2A–183c–2OG ternary complex explained the selectivity of 183c and revealed how 183c occupies the H3K36me2 substrate pocket, with a crucial group mimicking dimethylated lysine 36. In proliferation assays, several ALT-positive cell lines were selectively sensitive to 183c. Comparing ALT-positive SAOS2 and ALT-negative SJSA1 cells, both showed repression of E2F-regulated genes, but only SJSA1 activated EMT, DNA repair, and mTOR pathways, indicating distinct adaptive responses. These structural and cellular insights establish a foundation for designing improved KDM2A inhibitors for ALT-positive cancers.Subject terms: Chemical tools, Drug discovery and development

Introduction

Histone lysine-specific demethylase 2A (KDM2A, also known as FBXL11 or JHDM1A) is a conserved member of the Jumonji-C domain (JmjC)-containing family of histone demethylases, which utilize Fe2+, O2, and 2-oxoglutarate (2OG) to remove methyl marks from lysine residues1. Besides KDM2A, the JmjC family comprises additional related lysine demethylases, including the closely related KDM2B, and the KDM3-9 subfamilies, MINA and NO661. KDM2A is a multi-domain enzyme that, in addition to the catalytic JmjC domain, also contains a zinc-finger (ZF-CXXC) DNA binding domain coupled with a PHD domain, a central F-box domain and a C-terminal leucine-rich repeat (LRR) domain1–3 (Fig. 1a). Using the ZF-CXXC/PHD module, KDM2A binds to linker DNA in unmethylated CpG islands on heterochromatin, and this interaction is further supported by direct binding to heterochromatin protein 1 (HP1) and recognition of the nucleosome acidic patch2,4–7. Here, KDM2A specifically demethylates di- or mono-methylated lysine 36 on histone H3 (H3K36me1 or H3K36me2) to maintain an inactive chromatin state and repress transcription of loci including centromeric satellite repeats and rDNA genes3,5,8. The demethylase function of KDM2A has been implicated in a variety of cellular processes, including cell division, development, stem cell maintenance and oncogenesis (reviewed in ref. 9)5,10–12.

Importantly, KDM2A has emerged as a novel oncology drug target. Both oncogenic and tumor suppressor roles for KDM2A have been proposed in various contexts9 and KDM2A appears to be overexpressed in samples of select human tumors (e.g., breast or non-small cell lung cancer13,14). Most notably, KDM2A inactivation is synthetic lethal in tumor models that utilize alternative lengthening of telomeres (ALT)15. ALT is used by ~4-8% of human tumors to counteract replicative senescence due to progressive telomere shortening (while most of the remaining tumors use telomerase reactivation) and is enriched in cancers of mesenchymal origins, such as soft-tissue and bone sarcomas, pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors, or gliomas16–27. While genetic inactivation of KDM2A is tolerated by telomerase-expressing cell lines, it is lethal in both engineered and cancer-derived ALT+ models in vitro and in vivo27. Both the H3K36 demethylase and DNA-binding activities KDM2A are required to support the survival of ALT+ cells. KDM2A physically associates with ALT+ telomeres and its inactivation elevates telomeric H3K36me2 levels, disrupts telomere clustering in ALT-associated PML bodies (APBs) that are required for ALT-mediated telomere elongation, and induces mitotic segregation defects, cell cycle arrest and mitotic catastrophe27.

Multiple pharmacological tool inhibitors of JmjC demethylases have been identified, although the conservation of the catalytic domain makes selectivity for individual enzymes challenging (reviewed in refs. 28,29). Structurally enabled drug design can aid the development of more selective and drug-like lead molecules. Our research aimed to identify a selective inhibitor for Lysine Demethylase 2A (KDM2A). We initially selected 183c and the closely related stereoisomeric compounds 183a, 183b, and 183 d from patent WO2016112284A1 (Genentech and Constellation Pharmaceuticals) because they were among the most potent reported inhibitors of KDM2B, the demethylase most closely related to KDM2A within the JmjC family. Given the high sequence identity shared by the catalytic domains of KDM2A and KDM2B, we reasoned that these compounds would be strong candidates for cross-reactivity with KDM2A and, therefore, suitable starting points for structural and mechanistic investigation. Subsequent biochemical profiling confirmed that 183c was highly potent against both KDM2A and KDM2B, and we therefore prioritized this compound for further study. Compound 183c (Fig. 1b), chemically named 2-(6-((3 R,4 R)-1-cyclobutyl-4-ethylpiperidine-3-carbonyl)-2-methoxynaphthalen-1-yl)acetonitrile, is characterized by a substituted naphthalene core linked to a substituted piperidine ring via a ketone group. Importantly, the piperidine moiety contains two chiral centers, located at carbons 3 and 4, both possessing the (R) absolute configuration (3 R,4 R). While the compound’s inhibitory activity against KDM2B was reported, its binding mode remained undescribed. Here we report the crystal structure and mechanism of action of the KDM2A inhibitor 183c.

Results

Chemical synthesis of 183c

Compound 183c was obtained through an 11-step synthetic sequence that included palladium-catalyzed hydrogenation, yielding a racemic mixture of the cis piperidine diastereoisomers ( ± 183c). This racemate mixture was subjected to chiral supercritical fluid chromatography (SFC), providing enantiomerically pure 183c (see Supplementary Information file for details).

Activity and structure analysis

We evaluated the inhibitory profile of 183c using an AlphaScreen assay against the representative family members KDM1A, both KDM2A and KDM2B, KDM3A, KDM4A, KDM5C, KDM6A and KDM7B, using the services at BPS Biosciences Inc (San Diego, California). Potent inhibition by 183c was observed against KDM2A (IC50 = 11 nM), KDM2B (IC50 = 13 nM) and KDM7A (IC50 = 9.6 nM), while residual inhibitory activity was detected against KDM1A and KDM4A (20% and 43% at 10 µM, respectively) and no activity was detected against KDM5C, KDM3A and KDM6A (Fig. 1c). The potent activity of 183c against the three family members paralleled their evolutionary relatedness with KDM2A sharing 80% and 49% sequence identity with and KDM2B and KDM7B, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1). These results identify compound 183c as an attractive lead for the development of a potent and selective inhibitor against KDM2A.

Following biochemical characterization, we initiated structural studies of KDM2A in complex with 183c. Despite repeated attempts, no interpretable difference density for 183c was observed in the resultant crystal structures. To elucidate the binding determinants of compound 183c, differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF) was performed (Fig. 1d), revealing that 2OG is critically required for the binding of 183c to KDM2A. Specifically, 183c alone induced a marginal ΔTm of 0.3 °C, while 2OG alone induced a ΔTm of 3.6 °C. Strikingly, the combined 183c and 2OG induced a massive ΔTm of 17 °C (Fig. 1d), demonstrating a strong dependency on 2OG for the binding of 183c to KDM2A. Based on this finding, we revised our structure determination strategy to include both 2OG and 183c in KDM2A crystal soaking experiments. The modified approach yielded a crystal structure of KDM2A in complex with both 183c and 2OG at 2.2 Å resolution (PDB ID: 9PHV)–see Table 1 for x-ray data collection and refinement statistics and Fig. 2a for a representative unbiased electron density map.

Binding mode details

The catalytic domain of KDM2A consists of 12 β-strands and 19 α-helices with the active site situated at the center of a jellyroll motif that engages a Ni2+ ion, 2OG, and 183c, all in close proximity (Fig. 2b). The nickel ion is coordinated by the invariant residues His 212, His 284, and Asp 214 from the bottom of the pocket and carboxyl oxygen atoms of 2OG from the top off the pocket. Compound 183c, in turn, is situated in front of 2OG in a manner that partly exposes it to solvent.

Overall, binding of 183c is dominated by hydrophobic interactions with only two detected hydrogen bonds (Fig. 2b, c). Firstly, the N1-cyclobutyl-4-ethyl piperidine moiety of 183c projects most deeply into the active site, where it engages in hydrophobic interactions with a pocket lined by the side chains of Tyr 199, Leu 201, Val 220, and Asn 298. Additionally, the N1 atom of the piperidine engages in a weak 3.2 Å H-bond with the carboxylic oxygen of 2OG, while the ethyl group on carbon 4 orients towards solvent. Secondly, the substituted naphthalene moiety engages in hydrophobic/π-stacking interactions with Phe 324 and His 212 side chains (see also Fig. 2d) within a pocket lined by Ile 144, Ser 145, Thr 209, Phe 211, Val 213, and Asp 214. The ketone carbonyl oxygen, which links the piperidine and naphthalene moieties, forms a hydrogen bond with the main chain amide nitrogen of Phe 215. Lastly, the methoxy and acetonitrile substituents on the naphthalene moiety also orient towards the solvent. In addition to partly lining the 183c binding pocket and contributing a direct H-bond to 183c, the negatively charged 2OG molecule appears to shield an unfavorable charge interaction between the positively charged Ni2+ and the amino group of 183c. The combination of these effects likely accounts for the dependency on 2OG for the binding of 183c to KDM2A. We note that while the identity of the nickel ion was confirmed by X-ray fluorescence experiments performed at the synchrotron, the physiologically relevant ion is Fe2+3,30,31.

183c is a structural mimic of KDM2A’s natural substrate

Comparison of the KDM2A structure bound to 183c with the previously reported structure of KDM2A bound to a natural H3K36me2 substrate peptide (PDB 4QX7) reveals striking similarities suggestive of structural mimicry (Fig. 3). In particular, compound 183c occupies the spatial footprint corresponding to approximately three amino acid residues of the H3K36me2 peptide. Specifically, the 1-cyclobutylpiperidine moiety of 183c is positioned within the same sub-pocket that accommodates the dimethylated lysine 36 side chain of the H3K36me2 peptide, whereas the 2-(2-methoxynaphthalene-1-yl)acetonitrile moiety of compound 183c occupies the region bound by Lys 37 and Pro 38 of the H3K36me2 peptide. Furthermore, both 183c and the H3K36me2 peptide form a hydrogen bond with 2OG with their respective nitrogen atoms (piperidine’s N1 atom of 183c and methylated Lys 36 side chain nitrogen of the H3K36me2 peptide), which occupy similar positions (Δ = 1.8 Å). Despite these similarities, differences in interaction are also observed. Included is a hydrogen bond between 183c and the main chain amide nitrogen of KDM2APhe 215 that is not exploited by the substrate peptide, and the lack of hydrogen bonds to the side chain of KDM2ASer145 and the main chain oxygen of KDM2AGln116 that are only exploited by the substrate peptide. We posit that mimicry of the latter H-bonding interactions may afford an opportunity to further enhance inhibitor potency.

Rationalizing the specificity of 183c

To rationalize the observed binding preference of 183c across the 8 tested JmjC lysine demethylase family members and to predict the specificity profile against the remaining 19 of 27 human family members, we carried out a sequence comparison of residues predicted to line the 183c binding pocket (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). All 13 amino acids lining the 183c binding pocket except Val213 are identical amongst KDM2A, KDM2B and KDM7B (Supplementary Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 4a), which likely explains the strong cross reactivity of 183c across these three proteins (Fig. 1c). The single binding pocket difference between these enzymes is a modest substitution of Val213 in KDM2A for Ile in KDM2B and KDM7B and the side chain of Val213 is oriented away from 183c (Fig. 4b). An additional difference between KDM2B, KDM7B and KDM2A, in the vicinity of the 183c binding pocket is the polypeptide sequence flanking Phe324 (Supplementary Fig. 2a). This sequence in KDM2A (PNKFR in KDM2A, QPKFR in KDM2B, PDFLK in KDM7A and ADLFR in KDM7B) undergoes a large conformational shift (5.1 Å) between apo and inhibitor-bound states to allow a π-stacking interaction between the conserved Phe 324 sidechain with the substituted naphthalene of 183c (Fig. 2d). If the energy required to induce this conformation change is affected by the sequence of this loop, we reason that it could contribute to the specificity of 183c across related family members. Since the JmjC family member KDM7A differs in its binding pocket from KDM7B and KDM2B only in the composition of the Phe324-containing loop, we predict that KDM7A (PHF8) will also show strong but not necessarily identical sensitivity to 183c.

In contrast, far fewer of the 13 residues lining the 183c binding pocket of KDM2A are conserved in KDM1A (0/13), KDM3A (3/13), KDM4A (4/13), KDM5C (2/13) and KDM6A (3/13). In the case of KDM4A, the difference in residues in the vicinity of the 183c binding pocket, correlates with a highly altered 3D structure (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b, Fig. 4c). Based on the considerable divergence in binding pocket residues in most other family members, ranging from 7 of 13 residues conserved in PHF2 to only two of 13 residues conserved in KDM5B, we predict that the majority of lysine demethylase family members beyond KDM2A/B and KDM7A/B will be highly insensitive to inhibition by 183c. We note that the structure of KDM2A bound to 183c does not allow simple rationalization of the residual sensitivity of KDM4A and KDM1A to 183c at high inhibitor concentrations. Interestingly, while 183c occupies the position of the natural peptide substrate in KDM2A, in other previously described inhibitor binding modes, such as compound 76 binding to KDM4A, the inhibitor instead occupies the position of the cofactor 2OG32 (Supplementary Fig. 3). Together, these structures illustrate how small-molecule inhibitors of the lysine demethylase family can act through fundamentally different mechanisms.

Cellular efficacy of 183c

To assess the cellular efficacy and selectivity of compound 183c, we evaluated its activity in a clonogenic assay using a panel of human cancer cell lines. We tested 183c against four ALT-negative (ALT-; control group) and five ALT-positive (ALT + ) cell lines (Fig. 5a, b). Quantification of N = 2–3 independent experiments revealed that three out of five ALT+ cell lines exhibited, on average, increased sensitivity to 183c compared to the ALT-negative controls. Of note, the SAOS2 cell line showed the most potent growth inhibition by 183c as compared to other ALT+ cell lines (Fig. 5a, b). Indeed, analysis of sensitivity using an Incucyte growth assay showed potent inhibition of SAOS2 cell proliferation with an IC50 of 3±2 nM (Fig. 5c).

To further elucidate the cellular response to compound 183c, we assessed by RNA-Seq the effects on gene expression in ALT-positive (SAOS2) versus ALT-negative (SJSA1) osteosarcoma cell lines. Sequencing libraries for 12 samples (three replicates per cell line per condition) passed quality control (Supplementary Fig. 4a), and principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear separation between treated and control samples within each cell line (Supplementary Fig. 4b–c). When all samples were analyzed together, the greatest variation corresponded to cell line differences (PC1, 41.32%), followed by treatment effects of 183c (PC2, 16.06%), indicating a consistent transcriptomic response to 183c (Supplementary Fig. 4d).

To identify the specific genes and pathways affected by 183c, we performed differential gene expression (DEG) analysis. In the ALT-positive SAOS2 cell line, treatment with 183c resulted in 117 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (log2|FC | > 1), with most genes (96%) downregulated (Fig. 6a–c). Motif enrichment analysis of the promoters of genes that were significantly downregulated by 183c in SAOS2 cells revealed three significant enrichments for target genes of the E2F-2 transcription factor family, the KRAB domain repressor ZNF253, and the LRF (Leukemia/lymphoma-Related Factor) motif (Fig. 6b). This suggests that the repressive effects of 183c may be mediated, in part, through pathways regulated by these transcription factors. Similarly, SJSA1 cells showed a robust yet distinct transcriptional response to 183c, with a larger number of genes affected by 183c (234 genes with log2|FC | > 1) (Supplementary Fig. 5a). In particular, far more genes were induced by 183c (67 genes versus 5, Supplementary Fig. 5a-c). Motif analysis showed greater diversity in motifs associated with 183c-regulated genes in SJSA1 cells (Supplementary Fig. 5b), but interestingly, E2F2 motifs, including E2F2, E2F3 and E2F4, were associated with downregulated genes, indicating a shared repressive mechanism. Upregulated genes, on the other hand, were enriched for motifs associated with ZIC4, CTCF, and ETF, suggesting cell-type-specific regulation of transcriptional programs by 183c (Supplementary Fig. 5b).

To comprehensively assess the cellular responses of the SAOS2 and SJSA1 cell lines to 183c treatment, we performed hierarchical clustering on all DEGs and annotated the genes into 11 categories. The resulting heatmap demonstrates that the genes cluster into 11 distinct, functionally relevant categories, reflecting tightly coordinated transcriptional changes (Fig. 6c, Supplementary Fig. 5c). Analysis of these clusters identified several core functional groups that were mostly downregulated in the SAOS2 cell line, including the genes related to metabolic enzymes, ubiquitin/proteostasis, intracellular signaling networks, secreted signaling molecules, and cell cycle/DNA repair. The SJSA1 cell line shares similar overall downregulation of metabolic enzymes but did not undergo this global collapse; instead, it displayed a pattern of active compensation, characterized by targeted upregulation in functional categories associated with structural/cytoskeletal reorganization, ubiquitin/proteostasis, transcriptional regulation, intracellular signalling, and cell cycle/DNA repair, that are essential for survival efforts against cytotoxic stress. In particular, this adaptive profile includes: the upregulation of ALG10B (Alpha-1,2-glucosyltransferase B) to maintain functions of cell surface proteins and CPS1 (Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I) to enhance detoxification of catabolic ammonia; the enhanced genomic integrity, evidenced by the coordinated upregulation of key cell cycle regulators, including the DNA polymerase subunit POLE, the spindle scaffold WDR62, the anaphase promoting subunit ANAPC1, and the kinetochore protein KNTC1; and the augmented MAPK cascade through heightened induction of MAP3K1, MAPKAPK2, along with MAP3K10 (Fig. 6c, Supplementary Fig. 5c).

Cross-comparison of DEGs revealed dramatic cell-type differences: 31 genes (10%) overlapped (p = 1.922781e-31), whereas 86 and 203 genes were uniquely regulated in SAOS2 and SJSA1, respectively (Fig. 6d). Interestingly, among these 31 overlapping genes, 28 shared E2F motifs, indicating regulation of E2F transcription may represent a common target imposed by 183c (Fig. 6e). These data point to hypotheses that could explain the differential sensitivity of SAOS2 and SJSA1 to 183c: e.g. the resistance to 183c could be acquired by SJSA1’s adaptation, whereas analysis of the 86 SAOS2-specific DEGs, such as the unique and most strongly downregulated gene SERP2 (a critical ER chaperone), suggests the differential response may be due to the failure of the 183c-sensitive SAOS2 cell line to rescue the cellular stress imposed by the treatment.

Next, to delineate the transcriptional states specifically induced by 183c in different cellular contexts (ALT+ and ALT-), we directly compared the transcriptomes of 183c-treated SAOS2 and 183c-treated SJSA1 cells, without normalization to their respective DMSO controls (Supplementary Fig. 6a–b), which preserves intrinsic differences between ALT+ and ALT- cell lines. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) further showed that 183c-treated SJSA1 cells were selectively enriched for hallmark pathways associated with epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), DNA repair, apoptosis, mTOR signaling and downregulation in glycolysis and hypoxia (Supplementary Fig. 6c). Together, these results demonstrate that 183c triggers both a common E2F-mediated repressive program, as well as a cell–type–specific adaptive transcriptional response, the latter being prominent in ALT-negative SJSA1 but largely absent in ALT-positive SAOS2 cells. This adaptive program may contribute to the divergent phenotypic outcomes observed upon 183c treatment between these two osteosarcoma models. In further support of target engagement in cells, 183c treatment of SJSA1 cells induced a statistically significant global increase of the H3K36me2 mark by day 6 ( ~ 4-fold increase as assessed by immunofluorescence (IF) (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Discussion

We have characterized compound 183c as a potent inhibitor of KDM2A with strong cross reactivity against the closely related members KDM2B and KDM7B in vitro, but almost no detectable cross reactivity against the more distantly related members KDM4A, KDM5C, KDM3A, KDM6A and KDM1A. We show that 183c is active in cells as evidenced by its ability to more effectively kill ALT-positive cell lines relative to ALT-negative cell lines, supporting a therapeutic hypothesis developed by genetic means, suggesting that KDM2A may be an Achilles heel for the treatment of ALT-positive cancers. At the same time, our data revealed that sensitivity to 183c can vary among ALT-positive cell lines, with some models (e.g., SAOS2) being clearly hypersensitive as compared to others. Further development of KDM2A inhibitors should therefore include studies to identify biomarkers stratifying ALT-positive tumors based on their dependence on KDM2A activity. These could include, e.g., differing metabolic states, compensatory chromatin modifications, or drug uptake/metabolism.

The pronounced dependence of 183c binding on 2OG suggests that this compound does not behave as a classical 2OG-competitive JmjC inhibitor. Instead, our DSF, structural, and substrate-overlay analyses support a model in which 183c binds primarily in the substrate-binding pocket and is strongly favored by the 2OG-bound state of KDM2A. In the ternary complex, 183c sits adjacent to 2OG and makes a weak hydrogen bond through the piperidine nitrogen, while comparison with the H3K36me2-bound structure shows that 183c closely mimics key features of the natural methyl-lysine substrate. Thus, 183c is best viewed as a 2OG-dependent substrate mimic that is mechanistically distinct from canonical cofactor-competitive JmjC inhibitors. We note that Gerken and co-workers previously reported a potent KDM2/7-specific inhibitor series, exemplified by (S,S)-6, that acts through a similar 2OG-dependent, peptide-substrate-competitive mechanism33. Although it represents a distinct chemotype, it likely engages the same binding pocket as 183c. This mechanism may also have physiological implications. Because intracellular 2OG levels can vary with metabolic state, hypoxia, and oncogenic rewiring, the extent of KDM2A engagement by 183c may depend on cellular context34,35. While our current study did not directly test how changes in intracellular 2OG influence cellular or in vivo sensitivity to 183c, the observed 2OG dependence raises the possibility that metabolic state could modulate target engagement and pharmacological response. This will be an important question for future studies aimed at defining the translational potential of this chemotype.

Our RNAseq analysis revealed that compound 183c elicited a reproducible transcriptional response characterized by repression of E2F-regulated targets and induction of cell–type–specific adaptive programs. While both ALT-positive (SAOS2) and ALT-negative (SJSA1) osteosarcoma cells exhibited suppression of proliferative E2F-driven genes, only SJSA1 cells activated compensatory pathways associated with EMT, DNA repair, and mTOR signaling. These findings suggest that 183c engages a shared repressive mechanism but triggers distinct downstream transcriptional adaptations depending on telomere maintenance status, potentially explaining the differential phenotypic sensitivity observed between ALT-positive and ALT-negative osteosarcoma models. Though we note that these results are consistent with recent findings showing that KDM2A cooperates with E2F1 to regulate the transcription of meiosis-related genes in mouse sperm36, we cannot exclude that partial inhibition of KDM2A homologs (e.g. KDM2B/KDM7A/KDM7B) contributed to the observed transcriptional changes in our experiments. Future studies with more selective compounds tested at pharmacologically relevant concentrations are needed to delineate the exact functional contribution of KDM2A inactivation vs. inhibition of related enzymes, as well as direct and indirect effects on gene expression.

The X-ray crystal structure of 183c bound to KDM2A enables a rationalization of its specificity across the JmjC-containing family of histone demethylases and provides a platform for further modification to enhance potency, specificity and pharmacodynamic properties. Based on sequence conservation in the vicinity of the inhibitor binding pocket, 183c is expected to show strong cross reactivity against the three family members KDM2B, KDM7A, and KDM7B and little to no cross reactivity against the majority of 27 other JmJ family members. Directions for future optimization of potency include exploiting vacant H-bonding partners in the inhibitor binding pocket. For example, the addition of a polar group to the ethyl substituent of 183c to capture an H-bond with Ser145, as exploited by natural substrate peptides and the addition of a polar group to the acetonitrile nitrogen to capture an H-bond with the Lys 37 side chain. Directions for optimization to reduce cross reactivity against KDM2A, KDM7A, and KDM7B (PHF8) include extending 183c in the direction of Val213 to generate a steric clash with the larger substituted Ile residue and extending 183c in the direction of the Phe324 containing loop, as it is highly variable in sequence. Although the crystal structure was determined with Ni²⁺ rather than the physiological Fe²⁺, the overall ternary binding mode is expected to be preserved. In our structure, 183c does not directly chelate the metal, but instead binds in the adjacent substrate pocket while 2OG sits between the metal center and the piperidine nitrogen of 183c. This arrangement suggests that the proposed shielding of an unfavorable electrostatic interaction should also apply in the Fe²⁺-bound enzyme. We nonetheless note that Ni²⁺ and Fe²⁺ differ in coordination chemistry and their effects on JmjC demethylase function37, so subtle quantitative differences in geometry or interaction strength cannot be excluded. In the absence of a cleaner compound, we posit that 183c can serve as a useful cellular probe for interrogating KDM2A/B and KDM7A/B biology.

Methods

Chemistry

See Supplementary Information for synthetic route, purification procedures and confirmatory 1H NMR and high-resolution liquid chromatography mass spectrometry analysis.

Protein expression and purification

The catalytic domain of KDM2A used for crystallization was expressed in E. coli as two polypeptides referred to as the N-terminal domain (NTD) and the C-terminal domain (CTD). An N-terminal His-tag followed by a TEV cleavage site and residues 2-383 (NTD) of KDM2A was cloned into one multiple cloning site, and residues 450-517 (CTD) were cloned into a second multiple cloning site of the pETDuet-1 vector and transformed into BL21 (DE3) cells. The cells were incubated in Terrific Broth (TB) medium in the presence of 100 μg mL−1 ampicillin at 37 °C and shaking at 200 rpm. When the OD600 reached 1.0, the overexpression of His-TEV-KDM2A was induced by the addition of isopropyl-1-thio-D-galactopyranoside (IPTG), at a final concentration 0.5 mM, and incubated overnight at 16 °C with shaking reduced to 180 rpm. The next day, cells were harvested by centrifugation at 7000 × g (10 min, 4 °C) and the cell pellets were flash frozen in liquid N2 and stored at −20 °C.

The cell pellet from 4 L of culture was thawed and resuspended in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris pH 8, 200 mM NaCl, 5 mM imidazole, 1% glycerol). The cell suspension was supplemented with 1 mM PMSF, 1 mM TCEP, and 500 U of DNAse I and the cells were lysed by high-pressure homogenizer and sonication. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 20,000 × g (30 min, 4 °C) and the resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm filter and applied onto a 5 mL HisTrap HP column (GE) equilibrated in Buffer A (20 mM Tris pH 8, 200 mM NaCl, 5 mM imidazole, 1 mM TCEP, 1% glycerol). The column was then washed with 20 column volumes (CV) of Buffer A, and subsequently with 10 CV of wash buffer containing 30 mM imidazole (20 mM Tris pH 8, 200 mM NaCl, 30 mM imidazole, 1 mM TCEP, 1% glycerol). Finally, the protein was eluted using a linear gradient into the elution Buffer B (20 mM Tris pH 8, 200 mM NaCl, 300 mM imidazole, 1 mM TCEP, 1% glycerol). All wash fractions and elution fractions were analyzed by SDS-PAGE. Fractions containing the KDM2A fragments were pooled for further processing.

TEV protease (stock conc. 5 mg mL−1) was added to the eluted protein and incubated overnight at 4 °C during dialysis against buffer D (20 mM Tris, pH 8, 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP, 1% glycerol). TEV cleavage was confirmed by SDS-PAGE (uncut band of His-TEV-KDM2A-NTD at 48.4 kDa and cut KDM2A-NTD at 45.2 kDa, the KDM2A-CTD was unchanged at 7.7 kDa).

The cleaved sample was then applied to a 5 mL HisTrap HP column (GE) equilibrated in Buffer A. The column was washed with 10 CV of Buffer A, and the protein was eluted using a linear gradient into the elution Buffer B. Note that the cleaved sample eluted earlier than the uncleaved sample owing to the loss of the His-tag. Flow-through and elution fractions were analyzed by SDS-PAGE for the presence of cut KDM2A-NTD (45.2 kDa) and KDM2A-CTD. Elution fractions containing cut KDM2A-NTD and KDM2A-CTD were pooled. As the final purification step, the protein was applied on 16/600 Superdex 75 (GE) column equilibrated in 20 mM Tris, pH 8, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP, 1% glycerol. Typical purification yield was 8 mg of protein per 1 L of culture. The purity of the protein assessed by SDS-PAGE was over 95%. The identity of the protein was confirmed by intact mass LC-MS (Agilent-Q-TOF 6545).

Protein crystallography

Crystals of KDM2A (2-383, 450-517) were grown in hanging drops at room temperature by mixing 1 µL of 10 mg ml−1 with 1 µL of mother liquor containing 0.2 M Trimethylamine N-oxide, 0.1 M TrisCl, pH 8.0, 6–10% glycerol and 18.5–20% PEGMME 2000 for 5 days. The crystals were soaked overnight in mother liquor containing 1 mM 2-OG and 1 mM 183c (final 2% DMSO). The crystals were cryo-protected in a solution of 25% PEG MME 2000, 20% glycerol, 0.2 mM TMANO and 0.1 M TrisCl, pH 8.0 and plunge frozen in liquid nitrogen.

Single crystal X-ray diffraction data were collected at a wavelength of 1.4831 Å at beamline 23-ID-C at Advanced Photon Source, Argonne National Laboratory. Data indexing and integration were performed using XDS38. Molecular replacement was performed with Phenix Phaser39 using the previously determined structure of human KDM2A (PDB 2YU2) as a search model. Structure refinement was performed using Phenix.refine39, and interactive model building was carried out with Coot40. All crystallography software used in this project was obtained via SBGrid41. Data collection and refinement statistics are summarized in Table 1. The final model geometry was assessed with MolProbity as implemented in Phenix. Ramachandran statistics indicated that 98.15% of residues fall within favored regions and 1.85% residues are in allowed regions with no outliers. The MolProbity clashscore was 5.03, and the percentage of rotamer outliers was 0.89%9.

RNAseq

Total RNA was isolated from SAOS2 and SJSA1 cell lines following treatment with 183c or DMSO for 3 days. Each treatment group included three technical replicates, resulting in a total of 12 samples. RNA was extracted using the Quick-RNA MicroPrep Kit (Zymo Research, Cat. #R1050) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The sample RNA quality was accessed by Agilent Fragment Analyzer using High Sensitivity RNA Analysis Kit. The sequencing library was prepared from high-quality total RNA using TaKaRa SMART-Seq mRNA LP (Cat# 634768) according to manufacturer protocol. Briefly, 100 ng of the total RNA was used for full-length cDNA generation with 7 PCR cycles. The quality and quantity of the purified full-length cDNA were measured by Agilent Fragment Analyzer and Qubit 2.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), respectively. Then 1.5 ng of the full-length cDNA was used to generate the final barcoded sequencing libraries according to manufacturer's protocol. The barcoded cDNA libraries were checked with Agilent Fragment Analyzer for fragment size and quantified with qPCR using Collibri Library Quantification Kit (ThermoFisher) on a BioRad CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System. Quality-checked libraries were loaded onto an Illumina NextSeq2000 running SR100 cycles. Real-time base call (.bcl) files were converted to FASTQ files using Illumina bcl2fastq V2.20 conversion software.

RNA analysis

Following sequencing, Illumina universal adapters and polyA sequences were trimmed with TrimGalore (v0.6.10). The data were aligned to the human genome (GRCh38) with STAR (v2.7.9a), and mapped reads were quantified with FeatureCounts (v2.0.3). The aggregated counts matrix was loaded into R (v4.4.1) for downstream analysis. Differential gene expression analysis was performed with DESeq2 (v1.46.0), and results were filtered to include those with FDR adjusted p value of <0.05 and a Log2 Fold Change of >1 or <−1. A gene set enrichment analysis was performed based on the filter-passing genes with the R package fgsea and pathways obtained from MSigDB. Gprofiler was used for motif analysis. These results were visualized with the R packages ggplot2 and ggVennDiagram. The RNAseq data are deposited under GEO accession number GSE311698.

In vitro demethylase activity assays

Demethylase activity assays were performed by BPS Bioscience as follows: All the enzymatic reactions were conducted in duplicate at room temperature for 60 minutes in a 10 µl mixture containing assay buffer, histone H3 peptide substrate, demethylase enzyme, and the test compound. These 10 µl reactions were carried out in wells of 384-well Optiplate (PerkinElmer). The dilution of the control compounds was first performed in 100% DMSO. Each intermediate compound dilution (in 100% DMSO) was then directly diluted 30× fold into assay buffer for 3.3× concentration (DMSO). Enzyme-only and blank-only wells had a final DMSO concentration of 1%. From this intermediate step, 3 µl of compound was added to 4 µl of demethylase enzyme dilution and incubated for 30 minutes at room temperature. After this incubation, 3 µl of peptide substrate was added. The final DMSO concentration was 1%. After enzymatic reactions, 5 µl of anti-Mouse Acceptor beads (Revvity, diluted 1:500 with 1× detection buffer) or 5 µl of anti-Rabbit Acceptor beads (Revvity, diluted 1:500 with 1× detection buffer) and 5 µl of Primary antibody (BPS, diluted 1:200 with 1× detection buffer) were added to the reaction mix. After brief shaking, the plate was incubated for 30 minutes. Finally, 10 µl of AlphaScreen Streptavidin-conjugated donor beads (Revvity, diluted 1:125 with 1× detection buffer) were added. In 30 minutes, the samples were measured in AlphaScreen microplate reader (EnSpire Alpha 2390 Multilabel Reader, Revvity).

Data analysis was performed as follows: enzyme activity assays were performed in duplicate at each concentration. The AlphaScreen intensity data were analyzed and compared. In the absence of the compound, the intensity in each data set was defined as 100% activity. In the absence of enzyme, the intensity in each data set was defined as 0% activity. The values of % activity versus a series of compound concentrations were then fitted using non-linear regression analysis in Prism (Prism 11.0.0) of a sigmoidal dose–response curve generated with the equation Y = B + (T-B)/1 + 10((LogEC50-X)×Hill Slope), where Y = percent activity, B = minimum per cent activity, T = maximum per cent activity, X = logarithm of compound and Hill Slope = slope factor or Hill coefficient. The IC50 value was determined by the concentration causing a half-maximal percentage activity.

Differential scanning fluorimetry

The thermal shift in stability upon 183c binding to human KDM2A (amino acids 2-383 and 450-517) in the presence and absence of 2OG was assessed using DSF. Assays were performed using 5 µM KDM2A protein in a buffer comprising 100 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, supplemented with SYPRO Orange Dye (Invitrogen, Cat. # S6650; 1:1000 dilution from stock). The final concentration of the cofactor 2OG was 500 µM (a saturating concentration). The final concentration of the test compound 183c was also 500 µM. The combined sample containing both ligands was prepared using these same concentrations. All samples contained 1% (v/v) DMSO. Samples were prepared in technical triplicate with a final volume of 20 µL per well. Assays were conducted in a 384-well plate (Roche LightCycler 480 Multiwell Plate 384, white, Ref 04 729 749 001) sealed with an adhesive foil (LightCycler 480 Sealing Foil, Ref 04 729 757 001). The plate was subjected to a linear temperature gradient from 20 °C to 85 °C using a Roche LightCycler 480 II instrument. SYPRO Orange fluorescence was monitored using the 465 nm excitation and 580 nm emission filter combination. The raw data were plotted as relative fluorescence intensity versus temperature. The melting temperature Tm for each condition was determined as the peak of the first derivative plot, d(fluorescence)/dT versus temperature. The thermal shift ΔTm was calculated by subtracting the Tm of the unbound protein control from the Tm of the protein-ligand sample.

Cell culture

HELA, HOS, HS683, SJSA1, HS729T, SAOS2, and U2OS cell lines were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). CAL72 cells were purchased from the German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures (DSMZ). TM31 were obtained from RIKEN BioResource Research Center. SJSA1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Corning 10-040-CV) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; VWR 97068-085), 100 U mL−1 penicillin and 100 μg mL−1 streptomycin (Pen/Strep; Corning 30-001-CI). TM31, HELA, HOS, and HS729T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (Corning 10-014-CV) supplemented with 10% FBS and Pen/Strep. SAOS2 and U2OS cells were cultured in McCoy’s 5 A medium (Cytiva SH30200.FS) supplemented with 10% FBS and Pen/Strep. All cell lines were maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Clonogenic assay

Cells were seeded on 6-well plates at the following densities (cells/well): HELA, HOS, SJSA1, U2OS, CAL72, SAOS2–500; HS683, HS729T–1000; TM31–1200. Compounds were added the next day, and cells were grown until visible colonies formed in DMSO-treated control wells ( ~ 1.5–4 weeks). The compound-containing medium was refreshed every 3–4 days. At the end of the assay, medium was removed, plates were rinsed with PBS and colonies were stained with 0.4% (w/v) crystal violet in 20% MeOH/ddH2O for 30 min. Plates were then rinsed 2× in ddH2O, air-dried and scanned on a GelCount instrument (Oxford Optronix) to quantify colony numbers.

Incucyte growth assay

500 SAOS2 cells were plated per well of a clear 96-well plate, and serial dilutions of the compound were added the next day. Cell growth was monitored by daily scans on an Incucyte S3 instrument (Sartorius) until DMSO-treated wells reached confluence ( ~ 11–14 days). The compound-containing medium was refreshed every 3–4 days. The dose-response effect on cell growth was quantified using Incucyte software (Sartorius).

IF staining

Cells were fixed in 4% PFA (Cedarlane 1001359) for 1 hour at room temperature, followed by three washes with 1× PBS (WISENT INC. 311-425-LL). Cells were then permeabilized using PBS containing 0.5% Triton X-100 (Sigma CAS-9002-93-1) for 10 minutes at room temperature. After permeabilization, cells were blocked in IF buffer (1xPBS supplemented with 0.5% Triton X-100, 0.05% Tween-20 (Sigma P9410), and 2% BSA) for 30 minutes at room temperature. Cells were then incubated with primary antibody against H3K36me2 (CST2901T) diluted in IF buffer. Following primary antibody incubation, cells were washed three times with IF buffer for 5 minutes each at room temperature. Samples were then incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (Invitrogen SA5-10038), followed by three additional washes with IF buffer for 5 minutes each. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Thermofisher, 1:2000) for 5 minutes at room temperature. Cells were then washed twice with ddH₂O for 5 minutes each and mounted using Immuno-Mount (Thermo Fisher). Slides were allowed to dry overnight at room temperature before imaging.
