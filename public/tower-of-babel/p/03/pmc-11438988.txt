Distant metastasis patterns among lung cancer subtypes and impact of primary tumor resection on survival in metastatic lung cancer using SEER database
Authors: Tian Xie, Bing-Mei Qiu, Jing Luo, Yi-Fei Diao, Li-Wen Hu, Xiao-Long Liu, Yi Shen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research aimed to systematically uncover the metastatic characteristics and survival rates of lung cancer subtypes and to evaluate the impact of surgery at the primary tumor site on cancer-specific survival in DM lung cancer. We used the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database (2010–2019) to identify primary lung cancers with DM at presentation (M1). Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival curves were generated and compared utilizing log-rank tests. Cox regression methods were employed to determine hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals related to CSS factors. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was applied to reduce bias. We analyzed 77,827 M1 lung cancer cases, with 41.22% having DM at presentation. Bone metastasis was most common in ADC, ASC, SCC, LCC; brain in LCNEC; liver in SCLC. Lung was common in TC + AC and SCC. Long-term survival was best in TC + AC and worst in SCLC (p < 0.001). Male gender, age < 50, primary tumor site (main bronchus, lower lobe), large tumor diameter, ADC/SCLC/SCC pathology, and regional lymph node involvement were significant risk factors for multiorgan metastasis. Age ≥ 50, male, large tumor diameter, positive lymph nodes, and multiorgan metastases were associated with lower CSS. In contrast, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, systemic therapy, and surgery were associated with higher CSS rates. Primary tumor resection improved survival in lung cancer patients (excluding small cell lung cancer, SCLC) with single organ metastases (KM log rank p < 0.001, HR = 0.6165; 95% CI (0.5468–0.6951)), especially in brain (p < 0.001, HR = 0.6467; 95% CI (0.5505–0.7596)) and bone (p = 0.182, HR = 0.6289; p < 0.01), but not in liver or intrapulmonary metastases after IPTW. Significant differences in DM patterns and corresponding survival rates exist among lung cancer subtypes. Introduction

Lung cancer ranks second position in incidence and the first in mortality among all cancers1. Distant metastasis serves as the primary contributor to mortality in the majority of cancer cases2. Previous studies suggest that approximately 40% of newly diagnosed lung cancer individuals exhibit metastatic disease at the time of diagnosis3. Hence, conducting studies on metastatic lung cancer is of critical importance. The latest histological classifications of lung cancer can be classified into adenocarcinoma of the lung (ADC), squamous cell carcinoma of the lung (SCC), large cell carcinoma (LCC), small cell lung cancer (SCLC), adenosquamous carcinoma (ASC), large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC), and carcinoid tumor (TC + AC). An increasing amount of experimental and clinical evidence is challenging the classification of NSCLC subtypes4–6. With the introduction of early detection technologies for lung cancer, the classification of different subtypes has become more beneficial for diagnosis and treatment7. Lung cancer frequently metastasizes to the liver, bone, brain, and lung, leading to poor prognostic results8. Data concerning the metastatic patterns and survival of lung cancer with distant metastases (DM) have primarily been obtained through retrospective studies; however, population-based data in this field require further analysis to enhance clinical understanding. Some previous studies lack reliable metastasis data9, while others have described metastasis in lung cancer subtypes, mainly non-small cell lung cancer10. Unfortunately, few studies have focused on SCLC, LCNEC, and carcinoid tumors, and those that do often include a limited number of patients and lack studies on specific lung cancer subtypes11.

Previous relevant prospective studies have demonstrated that aggressive local treatment (surgery, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy) can enhance the prognosis for Individuals with metastatic non-small cell lung cancer12,13. Surgery remains the primary method of local treatment. Several small retrospective studies have shown that some patients with metastatic lung cancer may gain advantages from primary site surgical intervention14–17. Nonetheless, a scarcity of robust evidence exists owing to the restricted patient population encompassed in these investigations.

The objective of this investigation was to assess the patterns of distant metastasis (DM) in diverse lung cancer variants and their impact on patient survival, utilizing the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. Additionally, this study sought to examine the risk factors that impact cancer-specific survival among individuals with metastatic lung cancer and to explore the benefits of surgery for primary tumor resection in patients with DM lung cancer.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

Data were obtained from the National Cancer Institute’s SEER 17-registry custom database18,19. Since 2010, data on the location of metastases have been added to the SEER database. The time of diagnosis for the selected patients was limited to 2010–2019. Eligible patients were selected using SEER*Stat 8.4.0 software (https://seer.cancer.gov). The primary site was selected as “lung and trachea” for the cases. Lung cancer was the only identified primary tumor, and the diagnosis was confirmed by microscopy. Based on the histological classification, lung cancer instances were categorized as: adenocarcinoma (ADC; histological codes 8140, 8141,8144, 8230, 8245, 8250–8255, 8260, 8265, 8323, 8333, 8480, 8481, 8490, 8507, 8551, 8570, 8571, 8574–8576), adenosquamous carcinoma (ASC; histologic code 8560), squamous carcinoma (SCC; histologic codes 8052, 8070–8075, 8083–8084), large cell carcinoma (LCC; histologic codes 8012, 8014, 8082, 8123, 8310), small cell lung cancer (SCLC; histologic codes 8041–8045), typical carcinoid tumor (TC; histology code 8240), atypical carcinoid tumor (ATC; histology code 8249), large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC; histology code 8013), and others. DM at onset was categorized as M0 (absence of DM) and M1 (presence of DM). Throughout this duration, 295,725 cases of lung cancer were identified. The 8877 cases without metastasis information and survival months were removed. Of the 286,848 cases, 118,225 (41.22%) had DM at presentation. In addition, patients with “blank” metastatic sites, unknown tumor size and lymph node status, and patients whose cause of death was not cancer were excluded (n = 40398). Our analysis encompassed 77,827 patients, from whom we extracted the following variables: age at diagnosis, race, sex, tumor location, histological classification, tumor size, distant metastases, DM site (liver, lung, bone, brain), regional lymph node condition, primary tumor site surgical intervention, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, cancer-specific survival (CSS) time, CSS status, and cause of death.

Statistical analysis

We conducted a comparison of clinical parameters between groups using the chi-square test and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Kaplan-Meier analysis and Cox regression analysis were performed to compare differences in tumor-specific mortality between M1 lung cancer subgroups. We computed hazard ratios (HR) along with their respective 95% confidence intervals (CI). A p-value was deemed statistically significant when it was less than 0.05. Statistical evaluations were conducted utilizing R software, version 4.2.2, with univariate analysis by the R package CBCgrps20, survival analysis and multifactor analysis were performed using the R packages survivor and tidyverse. In order to mitigate the impact of confounding variables, we employed Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting (IPTW) was used. IPTW was performed using the “RISCA,” “foreign,” “tableone,” and “survey” R packages.

Results

Characteristics of M1 lung cancer

At the time of initial diagnosis, SCLC exhibited the highest distant metastasis (DM) rate among histological subtypes (56.74%), succeeded by LCNEC (47.52%), ADC (43.36%), LCC (40.61%), ASC (36.69%), SCC (26.54%), and TC + AC (9.18%). A significant portion of lung cancer patients identified with distant metastases were over 50 years old and exhibited large tumor sizes. In metastatic lung cancer cases, the highest percentage of patients present with a primary tumor location in the upper lobe of the lung. Adenocarcinoma emerged as the predominant histological subtype, followed by small cell lung cancer and squamous lung cancer. Over two-thirds of the cases did not undergo surgical resection, and regional lymph node involvement was observed in most instances. Table 1 outlines the fundamental characteristics of the patients.

Metastatic patterns in lung cancer subtypes

In adenocarcinoma (ADC), squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), adenosquamous carcinoma (ASC), and large cell carcinoma (LCC), bone metastases were the predominant site, comprising 35.15%, 33.26%, 38.51%, and 33.54%, respectively. The highest prevalence of brain metastases was observed in large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC) at 31.25%. Carcinoid tumors (typical and atypical combined, TC + AC) most frequently metastasized to the lungs, accounting for 32.95% of cases. Small cell lung carcinoma (SCLC) showed a significantly higher incidence of liver metastases at 35.35% compared to other subtypes (Fig. 1). Regarding multi-organ metastases at diagnosis, 38.25% of patients with distant metastasis had involvement of multiple organs, with the highest rates found in SCLC (42.30%), followed by ADC (39.52%), ASC (38.67%), LCNEC (37.21%), LCC (35.95%), SCC (29.10%), and TC + AC (28.71%). Liver was the sole metastatic site in 21.43% of SCLC and 21.45% of TC + AC cases, which was notably higher than other subtypes. Among single organ metastases, ADC and ASC had the highest percentages of bone metastases at 22.53% and 26.30%, respectively. Lung was the predominant single organ metastasis site in SCC, while brain was most frequent in LCC and LCNEC (Fig. 2).

M1 lung cancer survival rate analysis

Upon stratification by histological type, Kaplan-Meier (KM) curves revealed Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) exhibits the lowest cancer-specific survival (CSS) rates, followed by squamous cell carcinoma (SCC); whereas thymic carcinomas and adenocarcinomas (TC + AC) show the highest CSS. In terms of median survival times among different lung cancer subtypes with metastases, large cell carcinoma (LCC) has the shortest median survival time of 3 months. Metastatic squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) shows a median survival of 4 months, large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC) and adenosquamous carcinoma (ASC) both have 5 months, small cell lung cancer (SCLC) has 6 months, and adenocarcinoma (ADC) has 7 months. In contrast, metastatic typical and atypical carcinomas (TC + AC) exhibit the longest median survival time observed at 58 months, which is statistically significant (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3). Multivariable Cox regression assessment, accounting for variables including age, race, sex, and histological classification, primary tumor location, tumor size, regional lymph node status, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, systemic therapy, and surgical treatment, demonstrated that patients aged 50 years or older (HR = 1.2788; 95% CI (1.2321–1.3273)), male (HR = 1.1981; 95% CI (1.1798–1.2167)), those with large tumor diameter (HR = 1.0013; 95% CI (1.0012–1.0015)), positive lymph nodes (HR = 1.2585; 95% CI (1.2346–1.2830)), and multi-organ metastasis (HR = 1.4168; 95% CI (1.3945–1.4395)) experienced a negative impact on CSS in M1 lung cancer. Conversely, patients receiving radiotherapy (HR = 0.8536; 95% CI (0.8406–0.8668)), chemotherapy (HR = 0.3493; 95% CI (0.3435–0.3552)), systemic therapy (HR = 0.8004; 95% CI (0.7814–0.8199)), and surgery (HR = 0.5419. 95% CI (0.5136–0.5718)) had a positive impact on M1 lung cancer CSS, with a decreased mortality risk in comparison to those who remained untreated, and the most significant reduction in death risk observed in patients receiving chemotherapy, followed by surgery. Furthermore, multivariate analysis indicated that SCC, SCLC, LCC, LCNEC, and ASC adversely affected CSS in M1 lung cancer compared to ADC, with the highest death risk observed in SCLC (HR = 1.6295; 95% CI (1.5952–1.6646)). On the other hand, the mortality risk was notably reduced for the TC + AC group (HR = 0.2100; 95% CI (0.1769–0.2493)). Individuals with M1 lung cancer and primary tumor site in the main bronchus experienced an increased death risk compared to lung cancer with a primary location in the peripheral lung lobes (Table 2).When stratifying by age, sex, race, local lymph node involvement, and the number of organ metastases, unadjusted KM survival curves demonstrated superior survival outcomes for lung cancer patients under 50 years of age, of non-White, female, without lymph node involvement, and with single-organ metastasis (Fig. 4A-E). IPTW adjusted Kaplan-Meier analyses yielded consistent findings (Fig. 4F-J), with log-rank p-values below 0.001. Baseline data prior to and following IPTW adjustment can be found in Supplementary Table 1. The multivariate Cox proportional hazard regression model also corroborated these results. When stratified by chemotherapy, radiotherapy, systemic therapy, and surgical intervention, unadjusted Kaplan-Meier survival curves revealed enhanced survival rates for DM lung cancer patients who received chemotherapy, radiotherapy, systemic therapy, and surgical treatment (Fig. 5A-D). Upon IPTW correction, Kaplan-Meier analyses indicated that individuals with DM lung cancer who underwent chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy experienced improved survival outcomes, with log-rank p-values below 0.001. However, no significant difference emerged for surgical intervention (log-rank p = 0.5335; Fig. 5E-H). Baseline data before and after IPTW adjustment is delineated in Supplementary Table 2. The multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis confirmed improved survival outcomes for DM patients with lung cancer who underwent chemotherapy, radiotherapy, systemic therapy, and surgical treatment both before and after IPTW adjustment.

Multi-organ metastasis risk factors and the impact of primary tumor resection on survival outcomes in lung cancer patients (excluding SCLC) with single- and multi-organ distant metastases

Table 3 outlines the relationships between single-organ and multiple-organ metastatic occurrences and assorted clinical aspects. Male sex, age below 50 years, primary tumor location (main bronchus and lower lobe of the lung), larger tumor diameter, pathological subtypes (ADC/SCLC/SCC), and regional lymph node involvement were identified as significant risk factors for multi-organ metastasis. Furthermore, we assessed the influence of initial tumor location surgery on survival results for patients with single- and multi-organ distant metastatic (DM) lung cancer (excluding small cell lung cancer, SCLC). Unadjusted KM curves and multivariate Cox proportional hazard regression models revealed that individuals who received surgical intervention at the initial tumor location for single-organ (log-rank p < 0.001, HR = 0.5275; 95% CI [0.8877–0.9275], p < 0.001) and multi-organ (log-rank p < 0.001, HR = 0.6982; 95% CI [0.6173–0.7896], p < 0.001) metastases experienced improved survival outcomes in the context of distant metastatic lung cancer (Fig. 6A and C). Intriguingly, no significant survival benefit was observed in patients with multiple-organ distant metastases who underwent primary tumor site surgery following IPTW adjustment (log-rank p = 0.358, HR = 0.8630; 95% CI [0.6961-1.0700], p = 0.179) (Fig. 6D). In contrast, a significant survival benefit was evident for patients with single-organ distant metastases who underwent surgical intervention at the initial tumor location (log-rank p < 0.001, HR = 0.6165; 95% CI [0.5468–0.6951], p < 0.001) (Fig. 6B). Impact of primary tumor site surgery on cancer-specific survival in patients with single organ metastatic lung cancer (excluding SCLC) at various metastatic sites

Upon conducting a more granular analysis of metastatic sites in lung cancer individuals (excluding small cell lung cancer, SCLC)with single-organ metastases, both KM survival curves and multivariate Cox proportional hazard regression analyses of the unadjusted cohort revealed that individuals with bone metastases who underwent surgery at the primary tumor site (log-rank p < 0.001, HR = 0.5773; 95% CI [0.5022–0.6638], p < 0.001), those with brain metastases (log-rank p < 0.001, HR = 0.6047; 95% CI [0.5466–0.6691], p < 0.001), those with liver metastases (log-rank p < 0.001, HR = 0.4827; 95% CI [0.3712–0.6276], p < 0.001), and those with intrapulmonary metastases (log-rank p < 0.001, HR = 0.50664; 95% CI [0.45448–0.5648], p < 0.001) all demonstrated enhanced survival outcomes (Fig. 7A-D). Notably, after applying inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) adjustment, no significant survival benefit was observed for lung cancer individuals(excluding small cell lung cancer, SCLC) with liver metastases who received surgical intervention at the primary tumor location (log-rank p = 0.952, HR = 0.9242; 95% CI [0.7011–1.2182], p = 0.575) (Fig. 7G), and those with intrapulmonary metastases (log-rank p = 0.124, HR = 0.65316; 95% CI [0.53090–0.8036], p = 0.113) (Fig. 7H). However, individuals with brain metastases who received primary tumor site surgery exhibited a significant improvement in survival outcomes (log-rank p < 0.001, HR = 0.6467; 95% CI [0.5505–0.7596], p < 0.001) (Fig. 7F). In contrast, Kaplan-Meier curve analysis of individuals with bone metastases receiving surgery at the primary tumor location revealed no significant difference (log-rank p = 0.182), while multivariate Cox proportional hazard regression model analysis indicated a survival advantage for these patients (HR = 0.6289; 95% CI [0.50230–0.7873], p < 0.01) (Fig. 7E). Discussion

Lung cancer, being the leading cause of cancer-related fatalities, necessitates a comprehensive understanding of the timing and progression of its metastatic dissemination for effective diagnosis and treatment21. The majority of metastases originate directly from primary lung cancer, and the pathological subtypes significantly influence the metastatic site and prognosis22. Prior research has indicated that the incidence of diagnosed distant metastases at lung cancer onset is approximately 40% 23. The present study’s findings reveal variations in distant metastatic sites among different lung cancer pathological subtypes. Bone metastasis emerged as the prevalent site of metastatic occurrence for most pathological subtypes, while small cell lung cancer frequently metastasized to the liver, carcinoid tumors predominantly metastasized to the lung, and large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma of the lung mainly metastasized to the brain. Among single-organ metastases, ADC and ASC primarily metastasized to bone, TC + AC and SCC to the lung, LCC and LCNEC to the brain, and SCLC to the liver. Corresponding literature has also reported the predisposition of SCLC for liver metastasis24. Each lung cancer histological subtype exhibits a distinct metastatic pattern, providing valuable information to assist clinicians in directing their investigation for metastatic conditions after a histopathological confirmation.

As per the 2021 WHO classification, pulmonary neuroendocrine tumors are classified into typical carcinoid tumors (TC), atypical carcinoid tumors (AC), large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma of the lung (LCNEC), and small cell lung cancer (SCLC). Lung carcinoid tumors originate from neuroendocrine cells in the bronchopulmonary mucosa and comprise a minor proportion of lung neoplasms (1–2%), but are on an increasing trend25,26. In our study, we found that the metastasis patterns of large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC) are distinctly different from those of lung carcinoid tumors. LCNEC commonly metastasizes to the brain, whereas lung carcinoid tumors frequently metastasize within the lungs. In contrast to other types of lung cancer, M1 pulmonary carcinoid tumors have a more favorable long-term survival and prognosis. Although small cell lung cancer (SCLC) has the lowest cancer-specific survival rates, its median survival time is longer compared to large cell lung cancer (LCC) and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). This phenomenon may be attributed to the relatively low incidence of LCC, which limits the scope of specialized research and drug development. In contrast, SCLC, known for its high sensitivity to chemotherapy and radiotherapy, benefits from a variety of established treatment protocols and emerging therapies, such as immunotherapy and targeted therapies against specific molecular markers27. Despite SCLC being considered a highly aggressive cancer type with generally poor prognosis, it demonstrates an inherent high sensitivity to chemotherapy drugs early in treatment. This high responsiveness can significantly slow disease progression shortly after the onset of therapy, thereby extending median survival times28.

Prior research has suggested that central lung cancer tends to have a higher probability of causing regional lymph node metastasis29,30, and has a higher proportion of pulmonary metastases31. A large cohort study on lung adenocarcinoma found that central lung cancer has a greater propensity for early bone metastasis32. However, the impact of the primary site, particularly in cases of metastasized lung cancer, on prognosis and treatment planning is not well understood. Our examination revealed that individuals with main bronchial lung cancer experienced elevated CSS mortality rates in comparison to those with lung cancer at alternative locations, and were more likely to have metastases to multiple organs. This highlights the importance of considering the primary site of DM lung cancer in treatment planning and prognostication. Previous research has identified that age, gender, race, and lymph node involvement hold a significant correlation with the survival outcomes of individuals afflicted by metastatic lung cancer33–36. In our study, we utilized the most recent data on patients with metastatic lung cancer and, after balancing confounding factors using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW), observed similar findings: patients younger than 50 years, non-white, female, with no evidence of lymph node involvement, and those with single-organ metastatic lung cancer exhibited improved survival outcomes.

The decline in mortality from non-small cell lung cancer between 2013 and 2016 is attributed to the approval of targeted therapies and the development of new tests and immunochemical therapies that have improved long-term survival7,37. Primary and metastatic tumor resection are essential therapeutic approaches for individuals with lung cancer and can prolong patient-specific survival. Our investigation revealed that stage M1 lung cancer patients who received chemotherapy, radiotherapy, systemic therapy, and surgery experienced a significantly decreased risk of cancer-specific survival (CSS) mortality. Intriguingly, after applying inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) adjustment, KM and Cox regression analyses identified a survival advantage in patients with single-organ metastatic lung cancer who received primary tumor site surgery. Conversely, no substantial difference was observed in patients presenting with multi-organ metastases.

A retrospective analysis of 27 small sample cases demonstrated that patients with bone metastases from lung cancer might potentially achieve enhanced survival outcomes through surgical intervention38. An examination of 28 past cases disclosed that patients with brain metastases from non-small cell lung cancer can achieve better survival after receiving surgical treatment39. Similarly, another retrospective study of 37 small sample cases indicated that surgery on the primary site of lung cancer brain metastases can improve median survival time40. The prospective studies are consistent with our research12,41,42. However, there are fewer reports on the benefits of surgical treatment for lung cancer liver metastases, and there is controversy43. There is also limited research on lung cancer lung metastases. A retrospective study reported that lung cancer patients with lung metastases in the same lobe as the primary lesion are good candidates for surgery, while no surgical benefit was observed in patients with metastases in different lobes on the same side44. Following IPTW adjustment, our research discovered that lung cancer individuals (excluding SCLC) with brain metastases who received surgical treatment at the primary tumor location exhibited a significant enhancement in tumor-specific survival. Similarly, individuals with bone metastases derived from lung cancer (excluding SCLC) also benefited from surgical intervention. However, patients with liver and intrapulmonary metastases originating from lung cancer did not demonstrate a significant advantage following surgical treatment.

Our study represents the largest investigation to date, offering insights into lung cancer subtypes and the location of distant metastases. We also analyzed the differences in prognosis among various M1 lung cancer subtypes. This large-scale, retrospective clinical investigation examined the influence of factors such as age, gender, race, lymph node involvement, number of distant metastatic organs, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, systemic therapy, and surgical treatment on M1 lung cancer patients’ prognosis. The study delved deeper into the influence of primary tumor site surgery on prognostic survival for individuals with both single and multiple organ metastases and the survival implications of different metastatic organs in patients with single-organ metastases using IPTW.

Despite the study’s significance, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, even when employing inverse probability propensity score analysis, it is challenging to eliminate selection bias inherent in retrospective patient data collection. Several potential factors not recorded in the SEER data may influence outcomes, including the ECOG status of the patients, their specific nutritional and physical status, comorbidities, and detailed information on radiotherapy. Second, the SEER database does not report other distant metastatic sites, constraining the study’s scope. While the SEER database contains information on primary site surgery, the absence of specific information regarding whether surgery was performed in conjunction with metastatic site surgery and the lack of data on the number of metastases and metastatic lymph nodes preclude a determination of the relationship between lymph node and organ metastases and assessment of whether a patient has oligometastatic disease. Additional randomized controlled trials are necessary to further explore the association between surgical intervention and survival rates in patients with lung cancer exhibiting distant metastasis.

Conclusions

Our findings demonstrate that different lung cancer subtypes have distinct preferences for distant metastases and varying long-term prognoses, which is crucial for patient counseling and mortality risk stratification. Central lung cancer is more prone to have multi-organ metastases and a higher risk of death. Resection of the primary tumor in lung cancer patients(excluding SCLC) with single-organ metastases improves the patient’s survival, particularly in cases with brain metastases and bone metastases. Thus, greater attention should be given to the surgical treatment of patients with brain metastases and bone metastases from lung cancer, and more randomized controlled trials should be conducted.
