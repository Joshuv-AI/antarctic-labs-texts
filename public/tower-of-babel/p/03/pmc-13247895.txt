Cardiac computed tomography-derived left atrial volume index as a predictor of major adverse cardiovascular events after transcatheter aortic valve replacement
Authors: Jiaming Li, Jun Shu, Jin Zhang, Yao Zhao, Jian Yang, Hongliang Zhao, Minwen Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeft atrial volume index (LAVI) has been recognized as a significant indicator of left heart remodeling and diastolic dysfunction. This study aimed to investigate the association between LAVI assessed by coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) in patients with severe aortic stenosis (AS) after transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR).MethodsThis retrospective single-center study enrolled patients with severe AS undergoing TAVR between February 2020 and June 2024. All patients underwent CCTA examination prior to TAVR. Left atrial (LA) volume was automatically quantified from CCTA images, and the LAVI was computed by indexing to body surface area (BSA). Univariate and Firth-penalized Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were used to determine the predictors of MACE. Additionally, restricted cubic spline analysis was performed to explore the relationship between LAVI and MACE.ResultsA total of 206 patients (113 males, 93 females; mean age 68.11±8.01 years) were included in the final analysis. The incidence of MACE was 13.1% over a median follow-up time of 581 [interquartile range (IQR), 378–990] days. The LAVI was significantly higher in patients with MACE than in those without MACE [70.14 (54.81–84.12) vs. 51.19 (39.67–64.45) mL/m2, P<0.001]. After adjustment for clinical confounders and European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation (EuroSCORE II), LAVI ≥53.32 mL/m2 [hazard ratio (HR) =4.249, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.585–11.394, P=0.001] and male sex (HR =2.864, 95% CI: 1.164–7.051, P=0.012) still independently predicted MACE. Restricted cubic spline results showed a nonlinear relationship between LAVI and MACE (P for nonlinearity =0.032).ConclusionsPreprocedural CCTA-derived LAVI may aid risk stratification for MACE after TAVR in severe AS. Male patients warrant closer postprocedural attention.

Introduction

Aortic stenosis (AS) affects 2–5% of adults over 65 years and is the leading cause of sudden cardiac death in valvular heart disease populations (1,2). For patients with severe AS who are at high surgical risk or deemed inoperable, transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has emerged as the standard treatment, driven by its minimally invasive profile and established clinical benefits (3,4). Recent guideline expansions now include younger and lower-risk patients as TAVR candidates, creating new demands for refining preoperative risk prediction (5). While current models rely heavily on clinical and hemodynamic parameters, they inadequately account for structural cardiac remodeling (6,7). Consequently, this limitation in risk prediction necessitates the development of advanced imaging biomarkers to better stratify patients at elevated risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs).

Previous studies have shown the prognostic importance of left atrial (LA) enlargement in various diseases such as heart failure (8), systemic hypertension (9), stroke (10), myocardial infarction (11), and type 2 diabetes (12). In patients with AS, LA enlargement directly reflects the long-term excessive filling pressure on the left ventricle (13). Clinical observations reveal that significant LA enlargement commonly occurs in severe AS, especially in the absence of mitral valve disease or atrial fibrillation, where it serves as a robust marker of disease severity and duration (14). Moreover, according to the staging system, stage 2 of AS is characterized by LA damage, with progressive increases in left atrial volume index (LAVI) observed in stages 3 or 4 (15). A higher LAVI has been associated with adverse clinical outcomes in these valvular heart diseases patients (16). However, whether LAVI can predict outcomes in post-TAVR patients remains unclear.

Traditional echocardiography provides a preliminary assessment of LA volume, but its two-dimensional nature and operator dependence limit measurement precision (17). In contrast, coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) is a standard imaging method for TAVR candidates (18), providing critical information for prosthesis sizing, annular assessment, and access route planning (19,20). Importantly, deriving LAVI from this existing CCTA dataset adds no additional radiation, contrast agent, or cost, transforming a structural assessment into a functional prognostic marker. Nevertheless, LAVI derived from CCTA remains underexplored in the TAVR patient population. Therefore, this study aims to investigate whether CCTA-derived LAVI independently predicts MACE after TAVR, and to establish its potential role as an opportunistic risk stratification tool within the standard pre-TAVR imaging workflow. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-1-0032/rc).

Methods

Study population

This retrospective cohort study analyzed consecutive patients with severe AS who underwent TAVR at Xijing Hospital between February 2020 and June 2024. Inclusion criteria were: (I) age >18 years; (II) severe AS defined by at least one criterion: aortic valve area <1 cm2, peak aortic jet velocity >4 m/s, or mean pressure gradient >40 mmHg (18,21,22). Exclusion criteria were: (I) incomplete medical records; (II) poor imaging quality; (III) MACE within 30 days post-TAVR; (IV) lost to follow-up; (V) history of other cardiac surgery. Patients experiencing MACE within 30 days post-procedure were excluded from the analysis because early events are often related to procedural complications rather than baseline cardiac remodeling status reflected by LAVI. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the ethics committee of Xijing Hospital, Fourth Military Medical University (No. KY20253499-1). The institutional review board waived the need for informed consent due to the retrospective nature of study.

CCTA image acquisition

All CCTA images and coronary artery calcium score (CACS) were acquired using two scanners: a second generation dual-source computed tomography (CT; Somatom Definition Flash, Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany) and a third generation dual-source CT (Somatom Force, Siemens Healthineers, Germany). All patients underwent non-enhanced CT scans prior to CCTA for quantitative CACS assessment. The non-contrast scan range extended from 1cm below the tracheal bifurcation to the diaphragm, while the CCTA scan covered the entire heart. CACS quantification was performed using prospective electrocardiogram gating at 55–65% of the R-R interval with a 3-mm slice thickness. For CCTA, prospective or retrospective electrocardiogram gating was selected based on the patient’s heart rate after breath-hold, with image acquisition at 30–70% of the R-R interval. According to vascular and cardiac function, 50.0–60.0 mL of iodinated contrast agent (iopromide, 370 mgI/mL; Bayer Healthcare, Berlin, Germany) was administered intravenously via the median cubital vein at 4.0–5.0 mL/s, followed by a 40 mL saline flush. Real-time bolus tracking was performed with the region of interest positioned in the descending aorta. Scan parameters were configured as follows: (I) the second generation dual-source CT, detector collimation 128×0.6 mm, slice thickness 0.75 mm, gantry rotation time 0.28 s, tube voltage fixed at 100 kV, and reference tube current maintained at 300 mAs under automatic tube current modulation; (II) the third-generation dual-source CT, detector collimation 192×0.6 mm, slice thickness 0.75 mm, rotation time 0.25 s, tube voltage ranging 80–120 kV. Tube current was dynamically adjusted by the CARE Dose 4D system (Siemens Healthineers, Germany) according to patient’s body size.

CCTA imaging analysis

CCTA images were evaluated in axial and multiplanar reconstruction views. Two radiologists (each with >10 years of cardiovascular imaging experience) independently evaluated all coronary segments ≥1.5 mm using a 16-segment model (23). Discrepancies were resolved by consensus with a third senior radiologist (with 28 years of cardiovascular imaging experience). CACS was measured on electrocardiogram-gated non-contrast CT scans using the Agatston method, performed using the ‘Calcification Score Module’ of Syngo.via software. CACS were classified as: 0, 1≤ CACS <100, 100≤ CACS <400, and ≥400 (24). Coronary artery disease (CAD) severity was assessed on CCTA and classified according to the Coronary Artery Disease Reporting and Data System (CAD-RADS) (25).

LAVI measurement

The LAVI was determined through a two-step process. First, the LA volume was automatically calculated by dedicated cardiac function analysis software (Shukun Technology Co., Beijing, China) during the analysis of CCTA images within the workstation’s ‘CT Cardiac Function’ sub-module. Subsequently, LAVI was derived using the formula: LAVI (mL/m2) = LA volume (mL)/body surface area (BSA, m2) (see Figure 1).

Follow-up

Follow-up data in the present study were collected through telephone interviews by trained research personnel blinded to participants’ clinical information. The median follow-up was 581 days [interquartile range (IQR), 378–990 days]. MACE, including cardiac death, heart failure rehospitalization, stroke, nonfatal myocardial infarction, and unstable angina, were assessed.

Statistical analysis

Participants were divided into two groups based on the presence or absence of MACE after TAVR. The normality of the present study data was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation, and groups were compared using independent t-tests. Non-normally distributed continuous data were expressed as median (IQR), and groups were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are summarized as counts (percentages) and compared using Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests. The optimal threshold of LAVI for predicting MACE was determined using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves analysis based on the Youden index. Kaplan-Meier curves were used to present the cumulative incidence of MACE, with between-group comparisons performed by the log-rank test. Univariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was used to assess the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for factors associated with MACE after TAVR. Then, potential predictors with P<0.05 in univariable analysis were considered for multivariable analysis. Given the limited number of events (n=27), multivariable analysis was performed using Firth penalized Cox regression with the coxphf package in R to avoid overfitting. To assess robustness, a sensitivity analysis was performed with LAVI modeled as a continuous variable in the Firth penalized Cox regression. Restricted cubic spline analysis with three knots was performed using the rms package in R to explore the non-linear relationships between LAVI and MACE. A two-tailed P value <0.05 was considered significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 23.0, SPSS Inc.) and MedCalc (version 20.100, Ostend, Belgium) software.

Results

Study cohort selection

Detailed information about the study cohort enrollment process is presented in Figure 2. Among 359 patients who underwent TAVR for severe AS, 153 were excluded due to: (I) inadequate image quality (n=13); (II) prior surgical history (n=38); (III) incomplete clinical records (n=85); (IV) MACEs occurred within 30 days (n=5); (V) lost follow-up (n=12). At last, a total of 206 AS patients were included in the final investigation of the study. The mean age of this population was 68.11±8.01 years, and 113 (54.9%) were male.

Follow-up and baseline characteristics

During a median follow-up of 581 days (IQR, 378–990 days), 27 patients (13.1%, 27/206) had MACE, including 13 cardiac deaths, 9 heart failure rehospitalization, 2 stroke, 2 nonfatal myocardial infarctions, 1 unstable angina. All participants were divided into two groups based on post-TAVR MACE occurrence. Baseline characteristics of all patients are shown in Table 1. The MACE group had a significantly higher proportion of males than the No-MACE group [21 (77.8%) vs. 92 (51.4%), P=0.01]. Creatinine and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) levels were significantly greater in the MACE-positive group than in the MACE-negative group (all P<0.001). Echocardiographic results revealed that left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV) and left ventricular end-systolic volume (LVESV) were significantly higher in the MACE group [LVEDV: 147.0 (102.8–193.3) vs. 102.5 (82.0–138.0) mL, P=0.003; LVESV: 62.0 (43.5–122.0) vs. 45.0 (34.0–67.5) mL, P=0.011], whereas left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was significantly lower [48.0% (33.0–55.0%) vs. 55.0% (50.0–58.0%), P=0.005]. There were no significant differences in the age, body mass index (BMI), BSA, European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation (EuroSCORE) II, medical history, peak/mean gradients, or mitral regurgitation severity between the MACE and non-MACE groups.

Association of MACE with LAVI

A threshold of LAVI ≥53.32 mL/m2 (exploratory cutoff derived from ROC analysis) predicted MACE occurrence post-TAVR (see Figure 3A). As determined by log-rank testing of Kaplan-Meier estimates, dichotomized LAVI (≥53.32 mL/m2) showed significant associations with time to MACE (log-rank test, P<0.001; Figure 3B), where higher values were associated with increased risk. Univariable Cox regression analysis for MACE showed that male sex (HR =2.555, P=0.043), EuroSCORE II (HR =1.135, P=0.039), and LAVI ≥53.32 mL/m2 (HR =4.752, P=0.002) were associated with MACE after TAVR (Table 3). In Firth penalized Cox regression after adjusting for EuroSCORE II, only male sex (HR =2.864, 95% CI: 1.164–7.051, P=0.012) and LAVI ≥53.32 mL/m2 (HR =4.249, 95% CI: 1.585–11.394, P=0.001) independently predicted the incidence of MACE post-TAVR (Table 3). Meanwhile, with LAVI treated as a continuous variable and after adjustment for male sex and EuroSCORE II, the association with MACE remained significant (HR =1.010, 95% CI: 1.003–1.017, P=0.047). No significant interaction between dichotomized LAVI and sex was detected (P=0.697). Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a significant nonlinear relationship between LAVI and MACE (P for nonlinearity =0.032; overall LAVI effect P=0.031; Figure 4).

Subanalysis of heart-failure-related MACE events

A total of 22 heart-failure-related events were included in the subanalysis. After Firth penalty Cox regression, EuroSCORE II (HR =1.169, 95% CI: 1.033–1.323, P=0.025) and LAVI (HR =1.012, 95% CI: 1.004–1.020, P=0.034) were identified as predictors of heart-failure-related events following TAVR (see Table S1).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study investigating the association between preprocedural CCTA-measured LAVI and post-TAVR MACE. The main findings of the present study are as follows: (I) the survival probability in patients with LAVI ≥53.32 mL/m2 was significantly lower than in those with LAVI <53.32 mL/m2 (P<0.001, log-rank test). (II) Firth penalized Cox regression analysis demonstrated that only male sex and high LAVI (≥53.32 mL/m2) were independent predictors of post-TAVR MACE. LAVI exhibited a higher HR (HR =4.249) than male (HR =2.864) indicating that LAVI is an efficient predictor of MACE in patients undergoing TAVR. (III) The restricted cubic spline analysis showed a non-linear relationship between LAVI and the risk of MACE, supporting a threshold effect. (IV) LAVI and EuroSCORE II are valuable prognostic factors for heart-failure-related events following TAVR.

In the present study, the incidence of MACE following TAVR in patients with severe AS was 13.1%, a finding consistent with previously reported outcomes (26). Notably, we observed that AS patients with LAVI ≥53.32 mL/m2 had a significantly increased risk of post-TAVR MACE. This observation is in line with the work of Yang et al., who reported significantly higher mortality in patients with the highest LAVI category (>48 mL/m2) (27). The slightly higher LAVI threshold identified in our cohort (≥53.32 vs. >48 mL/m2) likely stems from differences in the imaging modalities used to quantify LAVI. Furthermore, previous studies have established that increased LA pressure and enlarged LA size are independent risk factors for adverse events after TAVR (28,29), which further supports our finding that LAVI is closely associated with post-TAVR prognosis. Beyond clinical events, LAVI has also been linked to favorable changes in laboratory markers after TAVR, including improvements in B-type natriuretic peptide levels (30). Taken together, these findings indicate that preprocedural assessment of LAVI carries meaningful clinical value for risk stratification in patients with severe AS undergoing TAVR.

To further clarify the relationship between LAVI and post-TAVR MACE, we performed restricted cubic spline analysis to reveal a significant nonlinear relationship between LAVI and MACE (P for nonlinearity =0.032, overall LAVI effect P=0.031). In this analysis, knots were placed at 34.29, 53.21, and 83.15 mL/m2. Of note, the second knot corresponded closely with the ROC-derived optimal cutoff of 53.32 mL/m2. This alignment points toward a threshold effect in our cohort, though larger studies will be needed to confirm the finding. Collectively, these findings imply that the dichotomization of LAVI can serve as a useful marker for predicting post-TAVR MACE.

The composite MACE endpoint in this study included cardiac death, heart failure rehospitalization, stroke, nonfatal myocardial infarction, and unstable angina. These outcomes are linked to LA remodeling through distinct mechanisms. LA enlargement primarily reflects chronic diastolic dysfunction and elevated filling pressures (31), which directly contribute to heart failure and cardiac death (32,33). Conversely, the relationship between LA enlargement and thromboembolic events such as stroke or myocardial infarction is likely more indirect, potentially mediated by atrial fibrillation or other factors (34,35). Due to the small number of stroke, nonfatal myocardial infarction, and unstable angina events in our cohort, we performed an exploratory subanalysis restricted to cardiac death and heart failure rehospitalization to investigate the mechanistic differences. The results showed that LAVI remained a significant predictor of these heart failure-related events, suggesting that LAVI has similar predictive value for heart failure events after TAVR. However, the value of LAVI in predicting other MACE events requires further evaluation in larger cohorts.

Interestingly, our results also identified male sex as a significant risk factor for post-TAVR MACE. Previous studies have reported that females exhibit higher 1-year survival rates post-TAVR compared to males (36-38). Furthermore, men and women demonstrate distinct responses to pressure overload caused by AS: women tend to develop a more concentric left ventricular hypertrophy, whereas men often present with a more eccentric pattern (39). This latter pattern may contribute to ventricular dilation, fibrosis, reduced ejection fraction, and the progression of heart failure (40). Morris et al. discovered women showed an earlier improvement than men in ejection fraction after TAVR (41). In our study, no significant interaction was detected between LAVI and sex. However, the limited number of events means that our analysis was underpowered to detect such an interaction. Despite this limitation, our findings reinforce the notion that male patients may warrant closer surveillance in post-TAVR risk management. This supports the potential value of sex-specific risk stratification and more intensive long-term monitoring to improve outcomes in high-risk men. The observed sex-related difference in risk warrants further investigation in larger cohorts, with sex-stratified analyses to determine whether LAVI independently predicts adverse events in both male and female subgroups.

Although echocardiography remains the most widely used clinical tool for assessing LAVI (42), it has inherent limitations. Measurements are operator-dependent and rely on two-dimensional planar views, which may underestimate true LA volume. CCTA, by contrast, enables three-dimensional reconstruction and thus offers more accurate and reproducible volumetric quantification. Prior work has shown that CCTA-derived LAVI correlates well with echocardiographic measurements (43). Beyond volume assessment, CCTA provides essential information on the arterial access route and permits detailed anatomical evaluation of the aortic valve apparatus, including annular sizing for prosthesis selection, optimal valve positioning, and assessment of risks such as annular injury or coronary occlusion. Importantly, deriving LAVI from preprocedural CCTA adds no extra cost, radiation exposure, or contrast load. It therefore serves as a practical and effective approach for integrating anatomical and functional risk stratification within a single imaging examination.

Limitations

There are some limitations in this study that should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective single-center study inherently introduces potential biases. Prospective, multicenter studies with standard inclusion criteria are needed to minimize selection bias. Second, the relatively small number of MACE events limited statistical power and precluded adjustment for multiple covariates. Although we used Firth penalized Cox regression to mitigate overfitting, the wide CIs reflect imprecision. Future studies with larger event numbers are needed to confirm our findings. Third, the ROC-derived cutoff was optimized for this dataset and may introduce optimistic bias, limiting generalizability. Although restricted cubic spline analysis supported a threshold effect, this specific cutoff should be considered exploratory and requires external validation in independent cohorts. Fourth, the composite MACE endpoint included heterogeneous outcomes (all-cause death, cardiac death, heart failure rehospitalization, non-fatal myocardial infarction, and stroke). LA remodeling is most directly linked to heart failure events, but the number of heart-failure-related events in our cohort was too small to perform a well-powered subanalysis. Future studies with larger event counts should specifically examine the association between LAVI and heart-failure-related outcomes or other MACE events. Fifth, LAVI measurements in our study were obtained only through automated CCTA analysis. We did not perform a comparison between CCTA-derived LAVI and echocardiographic LAVI. Future studies should include both modalities to evaluate their agreement and incremental prognostic value. Finally, although male sex emerged as an independent predictor, the exploratory interaction analysis between LAVI and sex was underpowered (only 27 events). Therefore, we cannot rule out effect modification by sex, and larger studies are needed to determine whether the prognostic value of LAVI differs between males and females.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the present study demonstrates that preprocedural LAVI is an independent predictor of MACE after TAVR. LAVI can be easily measured using CCTA, which may help improve risk stratification in patients with severe AS undergoing TAVR. Male TAVR patients require more attention after TAVR compared with female patients. Further larger studies are warranted to validate our results.
