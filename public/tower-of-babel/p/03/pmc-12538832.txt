Validation of the Arabic version of the 16-item free and cued recall test among patients with schizophrenia
Authors: Najla Sam, Chadia Haddad, Benjamin Calvet, Oussama Dahdouh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSeveral subjective scales have been used to measure verbal memory in patients with schizophrenia, such as the 16-item free and cued recall test. This study aimed to validate the Arabic version of the 16 item free and cued recall test among patients with schizophrenia.MethodsA cross-sectional study was performed among 172 patients with schizophrenia and 60 healthy controls at the Psychiatric Hospital of the Cross, Lebanon. The 16 item free and cued recall test was used to assess verbal memory in patients with schizophrenia.ResultsThe internal consistency of the Free and Cued Recall test was 0.722, and the factor analysis revealed a 2-factor solution accounting for 79.35% of the variance. A significant difference between schizophrenia patients and healthy controls was found across all recall items, except for cued recall 3 and delayed cued recall, with patients scoring lower overall. Additionally, the test positively correlated with BACS verbal memory (r = 0.352, p < 0.001) and working memory (r = 0.421, p < 0.001), as well as the MOCA test (r = 0.474, p < 0.001). Test-retest reliability indicated moderate stability, with Pearson correlations ranging from 0.33 to 0.79 and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) between 0.46 and 0.59.ConclusionThe Free and Cued Recall test exhibited satisfactory psychometric properties, including adequate internal consistency, good construct validity, and strong concurrent validity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07417-8.

Background

Cognitive impairment in schizophrenia has gained increasing attention, despite longstanding emphasis on positive and negative symptoms of the disorder [1–3]. These impairments span various domains, including memory, attention, executive functions, and processing speed, often ranging in severity from moderate to profound [4, 5]. They follow a consistent trajectory, impacting patient outcomes more significantly than the core psychotic symptoms [1]. Studies have identified cognitive dysfunction as a critical determinant of functional status, influencing daily activities such as interpersonal communication, problem-solving, and independent living [6].

Memory deficits, in particular, are a hallmark of cognitive dysfunction in schizophrenia, often presenting early in the disease course. Verbal memory is among the most impaired domains of cognitive function in schizophrenia. It is impaired in first episode and chronic schizophrenia and appears to be present throughout the course of illness without being fully accounted for by changes in clinical state or medication effects [7]. These deficits impact verbal learning and recall abilities, contributing to broader challenges in functional and social interactions [7]. Recognizing these impairments is crucial for understanding the overall cognitive burden experienced by patients.

Neuropsychological assessments serve as a cornerstone for objectively evaluating cognitive performance [8]. Numerous tools have been developed to assess verbal memory, including the Hopkins Verbal Learning Test (HVLT) [9], California Verbal Learning Test (CVLT) [10], Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT) [11] and the Brief Assessment of Cognition in Schizophrenia (BACS) scale [12]. Among these, the 16-item Free and Cued Recall Test (RL-RI 16) is notable for its ability to assess verbal memory in-depth by examining retrieval strategies and encoding processes, offering additional insights into cognitive functioning [13]. The RL-RI 16 test has gained prominence due to its structured approach, making it particularly useful for both clinical practice and research settings. Highlighting its advantages, the RL-RI 16 employs controlled cues to differentiate between memory storage and retrieval difficulties, providing nuanced diagnostic information [13]. Language and cultural factors significantly influence cognitive assessments. Many tools, including the RL-RI 16, were originally developed in English and later adapted to other languages, such as French [13]. However, versions of the 16-item Free and Cued Recall Test tailored to Arabic-speaking populations remain underexplored. This represents a significant gap, given the widespread use of the test in memory assessments and its potential for cross-cultural applications. While studies have validated the RL-RI 16 in several languages, including English and French, no robust adaptations or validations exist for Arabic-speaking patients with schizophrenia.

To address this gap, the present study focused on the adaptation and validation of the Arabic version of the 16-item Free and Cued Recall Test among patients with schizophrenia. Therefore the study objective is to validate the 16-item Free and Cued Recall test in Arabic language and evaluate its psychometric properties that could provide clinicians and researchers in Arabic-speaking contexts with a reliable tool to assess memory function. Such an instrument is essential for improving patient care, developing tailored interventions, and advancing research on cognitive impairments in schizophrenia.

Methods

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional study was conducted among 172 patients diagnosed with schizophrenia and schizoaffective disorder from the psychiatric hospital of the cross-Lebanon (HPC). The study included 60 healthy controls who were matched with patients based on age, sex, and educational background. Those who met the DSM-5 criteria for schizophrenia and schizoaffective disorders, were between the ages of 18 and 60, had completed more than five years of school, were on antipsychotic medication, and were clinically stable were eligible to be included. Current substance use problems, brain injury, neurological illnesses, and any other mental or psychiatric conditions are the exclusion criteria. Healthy individuals were chosen from the HPC hospital staff who satisfied the following requirements: none of the participants had any neurological or mental health conditions that could affect their cognitive function, brain damage, or a current substance use disorder. The methodology used in this study is part of a broader project on schizophrenia and cognition, which have been published in previous studies [14, 15]. In the previous study validating the BACS battery, we compared the overall performance of the BACS with that of a standard cognitive test battery [14]. We also included a control group to compare the cognitive performance of patients with schizophrenia against healthy controls [14]. The control group in the current study is the same as that in the BACS study and consists of 60 participants, with identical means and standard deviations. The clinical group, however, has been augmented with 52 additional participants.

Sample size calculation

Based on a study by Comrey & Lee (2013) [16], 5–10 observations per item are needed for the scale validation process. Therefore, the minimal sample size required to validate the RL/RI-16 (16 items) was 160 participants.

Translation procedure

The questionnaire was translated from French to Arabic following established guidelines, including forward and backward translation. A mental health professional carried out the forward translation, and another specialist carried out the reverse translation. After comparing the two French versions, any differences were identified and resolved by the authors’ agreement. The translated version of the instrument was utilized in the study after it was pre-tested on individuals with schizophrenia, and no significant problems were found. This approach ensured linguistic and conceptual equivalence, thereby maintaining the validity of the scale in the new language.

Measures

In this study, the questionnaire was administered in Arabic, which is Lebanon’s native language. Participants’ clinical information (diagnosis, length of illness in years, length of hospitalization in years, number of hospitalizations, and family history of mental disorders) and sociodemographic information (age, sex, marital status, educational attainment, and monthly income) were assessed in the first section.

The following measurements were included in the questionnaire’s second section:

16-item Free and Cued Recall test

The 16-Item Free and Cued Recall Test is a cognitive assessment designed to evaluate verbal memory through structured recall tasks [13]. It begins with an encoding phase where the examiner presents cards with four items, prompting participants to identify them using category cues. This is followed by immediate cued recall, allowing participants multiple opportunities to retrieve items they may have missed. After a brief distractor task (20 s), participants engage in free recall for 2 min, followed by cued recall for any unrecalled items, with this process repeated three times [13]. A recognition test assessed the ability to distinguish target items from distractors. Finally, a delayed recall phase occurs 20 min later, measuring retention without prior verbal tasks. Overall, the test effectively gauges encoding, storage, and retrieval of verbal information, reflecting both immediate and delayed recall performance [13].

The self-assessment scale of cognitive complaints in schizophrenia (SASCCS)

Schizophrenia patients’ perceptions of their cognitive impairment are measured using the 21-item SASCCS scale [17], which has been validated in Arabic [15]. The items are assessed on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (very often), and the total score was calculated by summing all the items [17]. Higher scores indicate more complaints of cognitive impairment [17]. The Cronbach alpha calculated in our study was 0.896.

Brief assessment of cognition in schizophrenia (BACS)

The BACS is a neuropsychological test that evaluates cognitive function in individuals with schizophrenia [18] and has been previously validated in Arabic among chronic patients with schizophrenia [14]. It consists of six subscales: semantic fluency for verbal fluency, digit sequencing for working memory, list learning for verbal memory, Tower of London for executive function, and symbol coding for attention and processing speed [18]. The convergent validity of the RL/RI-16 test was evaluated using two tests from the BACS scale (digit sequencing and list learning).

Positive and negative syndrome scale (PANSS)

The PANSS is a 30-item questionnaire with three subscales: overall psychopathology (16 items), negative symptoms (7 items), and positive symptoms (7 items) [19]. It has been previously validated in Arabic in a study done among 400 participants (200 patients with schizophrenia and 200 controls) [20]. A rating ranging from 1 (no symptoms) to 7 (very severe symptoms) is assigned to each item [19]. Higher scores denote more severe symptoms, and the overall score is determined by adding up all of the responses [19]. The Cronbach alpha value calculated in our study was 0.863.

Montreal cognitive assessment (MoCA)

The MoCA is a rapid screening tool designed to detect mild cognitive dysfunction [21]. The MOCA was validated into Arabic language among patients with schizophrenia [22]. It evaluates several cognitive domains including attention, concentration, executive functions, memory, language, vasoconstriction skills, conceptual thinking, calculations, and orientation [21]. The test takes approximately 10 min to administer and has a total possible score of 30 points, with scores of 26 and above considered normal [21]. The MoCA includes tasks such as alternating trail making, visuoconstructional skills (cube and clock drawing), naming animals, memory recall, attention (digit span and vigilance), serial subtraction, sentence repetition, verbal fluency, abstraction, delayed recall, and orientation [21]. Each task is scored individually, contributing to the overall assessment of cognitive function. The Cronbach alpha calculated in our study was 0.793.

Data analysis

Data analysis was done using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 25. A descriptive analysis was performed where categorical variables were expressed as absolute frequencies and percentages and quantitative variables as means and standard deviations.

A bivariate analysis was conducted using the Student’s independent t-test to compare continuous variables in two groups. The distribution of normality of continuous variables were assessed by visualizing histograms with normal curves and examining skewness and kurtosis values, which were all less than |1.96|, indicating approximate normality. The majority of continuous variables met the assumptions for parametric testing. For variables showing deviations from normality, we conducted non-parametric analyses using the Mann-Whitney U test, and the results were consistent with those obtained from the t-tests. The Chi-square test was used for dichotomous and multinomial variables comparison between groups.

An Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was conducted to identify the factor structure of the 16-item Free and Cued Recall test using principal components analysis with Varimax rotation. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s test of sphericity were ensured to be adequate. The retained number of factors corresponded to Eigenvalues higher than one. Cronbach’s alpha was calculated for the 16-item Free and Cued Recall test scale to assess its internal consistency reliability. The intraclass correlation coefficient were used to assess test-retest.

The relationships between the 16-item Free and Cued Recall test total scores and clinical variables have been investigated using bivariate correlations (Pearson correlation). The convergent validity of the 16-item Free and Cued Recall Test was assessed specifically using only two subtests from the BACS, verbal memory and working memory, rather than the full BACS battery, utilizing an expanded sample from the previous BACS validation study [14]. We specifically used these two subtests from the BACS battery, as they correspond directly to the memory functions assessed by the RL-RI 16 and are therefore appropriate for validating its convergent validity. Additionally, independent cognitive measures such as the MoCA and the SASCCS scales were included to further strengthen the assessment of convergent validity. The MoCA is primarily a screening tool, it includes a verbal memory component, which is why we incorporated it to provide an additional measure of memory function in our validation process.

In addition, a multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA) was carried out taking the 16-item Free and Cued Recall test subscales as the dependent variables. The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the total sample (N = 232)

A significant difference was found between schizophrenia patients and healthy controls in all the items of the Free and Cued Recall test except in the cued recall 3 and the delayed cued recall. The schizophrenia group scored significantly lower mean than the healthy controls in the 16-item Free and Cued Recall test. Also, a significantly higher average score cognitive complaints (SASCCS scale) was found among patients with schizophrenia as compared to health controls. Considering the BACS subscales related to verbal memory and working memory a significantly lower mean scales were found among patients with schizophrenia as compared to healthy control group (Table 2).

Among patients with schizophrenia, age was not significantly correlated with any of the memory indices (p > 0.05). A statistically significant negative correlation was observed between the duration of illness and the total score across the three recall trials (r = −0.159, p = 0.041). Additionally, a significant negative correlation was found between the duration of hospitalization and delayed recall performance (r = −0.165, p = 0.032) (Supplementary table S1). Also, no significant sex differences were observed across any of the memory indices (p > 0.05). Education level was significantly associated with memory performance in patients, with those having secondary or university education showing higher total scores and better performance in the three recall trials compared to those with lower education levels (p < 0.001 for both) (Supplementary table S2).

In contrast, among healthy controls, females performed significantly better than males on both the total score (p = 0.035) and the total of the three recall trials (p = 0.040). Education level was significantly associated with delayed recall (p = 0.015), with university-educated participants performing best. Family history of psychiatric illness was significantly associated with delayed recall (p < 0.001) (Supplementary table S2).

Validity of internal structure

Among patients with schizophrenia, a factor analysis was run to test the construct validity of the Free and Cued Recall test using the principal component analysis as the extraction method. All items of the test could be extracted from the list, and the scale converged on a 2-factor solution using the Varimax rotated matrix with an eigenvalue greater than 1, accounting for 79.35% of the variance (Bartlett sphericity test p < 0.001, KMO = 0.624) (Table 3). The Cronbach alpha value for the scale was 0.722.

Among healthy control, two distinct factors emerged, accounting for 67.33% of the total variance (Factor 1 = 52.52%, Factor 2 = 14.80%). The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy was 0.609, indicating acceptable factorability, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (p < 0.001), supporting the suitability of the data for factor analysis. Internal consistency for the test was modest, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.542 overall (supplementary table S3).

Concurrent validity

Among patients with schizophrenia, the Free and Cued Recall test total score was positively correlated with the BACS verbal memory (r = 0.352, p < 0.001), BACS working memory (r = 0.421, p < 0.001) and the MOCA test (r = 0.474, p < 0.001).

Moreover, a significant negative correlation was found between the Free and Cued Recall test total score the SASCCS scale (r=−0.165, p = 0.032). When considering the association between the PANSS scale and the Free and Cued Recall test, no significant association was found except between the total delayed recall and the general psychopathology PANSS scale (r=−0.221, p = 0.005) (Table 5).

Among healthy controls, performance on the 16-item Free and Cued Recall test showed significant positive correlations with objective cognitive functioning, particularly verbal and working memory, as measured by the BACS. In contrast, no significant associations were found between the 16-item Free and Cued Recall test and subjective cognitive complaints as measured by the SASCCS, nor with age (all p > 0.05) (Supplementary table S4).

Discussion

In the current study, the 16-item free and cued recall test was translated into classical Arabic language and validated among a sample of Lebanese patients with schizophrenia. The validated version demonstrated a good internal consistency, stability over time and good construct and convergent validity showing that the test is a useful instrument for evaluating verbal memory in patients with schizophrenia.

The schizophrenia group scored significantly lower mean in all the items of the free and cued test than healthy controls, highlighting a pronounced deficiency in verbal memory among the schizophrenia group. The significant impairments in verbal memory observed in the schizophrenia group, as evidenced by their lower scores on the Free and Cued Recall test compared to healthy controls, underscore a critical aspect of cognitive dysfunction in this population [23]. Specifically, the markedly lower mean scores in free and delayed recall indicate persistent challenges in both encoding and retrieving information. This aligns with prior research, such as Green’s (2006) meta-analysis, which highlights similar deficits in verbal memory among individuals with schizophrenia [24]. Additionally, the higher cognitive complaints reported on the SASCCS scale suggest that patients are aware of their memory difficulties, which can affect their quality of life and treatment engagement [25]. Given the mean duration of illness (21.60 years) and its potential impact on cognitive functioning, these findings emphasize the need for targeted cognitive interventions, like cognitive remediation therapy, to improve memory function and overall functioning in daily life for individuals with schizophrenia [26]. In our sample, sex was not significantly associated with memory performance among patients with schizophrenia. In contrast, healthy control females performed significantly better than males on the test. Education level was a significant predictor of memory performance in both groups, with higher educational attainment associated with better recall. Additionally, in healthy controls, delayed recall performance was positively associated with a university education (p = 0.015) and with having a family history of psychiatric illness (p < 0.001). These findings suggest that demographic influences on memory performance may differ in nature or magnitude across clinical and non-clinical populations.

A good internal consistency was found (Cronbach’s alpha = 0, 72), comparable to other findings (Green et al., 2000) [24], suggesting that the 16-item free and cued recall test is a reliable measure for verbal memory. Based on EFA results, the factor analysis found a 2-factor structure for the free and cued recall test contrasting with previous findings that proposed a different structure. In earlier studies, the scale’s validation indicated a more complex multi-factor arrangement, which often included both free and cued recall within a single dimension [27]. Our analysis revealed a clear distinction between the two components, the first one capturing free recall performance and component 2 representing cued recall performance, accounting for a cumulative 79,35% of the variance. This suggests a more streamlined conceptualization of recall abilities specific to patients with schizophrenia, enhancing the understanding of memory constructs within this population. Notably, this two-factor solution contrasts with previous validation studies, which proposed a single-factor or more integrated model combining free and cued recall as part of a unified episodic memory construct. In their original conceptualization, both recall types were considered closely interdependent, often analyzed under a general memory index. The emergence of distinct factors in our sample may reflect the specific cognitive profile of individuals with schizophrenia, where impairments in strategic retrieval (free recall) and cue utilization (cued recall) are differentially affected. This divergence highlights the importance of examining factor structures within clinical populations separately, as they may exhibit patterns that differ from normative or elderly cohorts used in earlier validation studies. Future research with larger samples and cross-cultural comparisons is warranted to further explore the stability and generalizability of this two-factor structure (28). Although the KMO value of 0.624 is on the lower end of the acceptable range, this may be due to the relatively small sample size compared to the number of variables analyzed. Future studies with larger samples are needed to confirm the stability of this factor structure.

The test-retest reliability of the 16-item Free and Cued Recall test demonstrated moderate reliaility, with Pearson correlation coefficients ranging from 0.33 to 0.79 and Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) between 0.46 and 0.59. These findings suggest a reasonable level of stability in test performance over a two-week interval, although the ICC values indicate that the reliability may be somewhat lower than desired for a cognitive assessment tool. Importantly, this constitutes a novel contribution to the psychometric evaluation of the test, as the original French validation of the RL/RI-16 did not report test-retest reliability data. Their study focused primarily on the development of normative data, the influence of sociodemographic variables on recall performance [29]. The moderate ICC values, particularly in the context of psychological testing, can be interpreted as indicative of variability in the performance of patients with schizophrenia [30]. This population often exhibits fluctuations in cognitive abilities due to factors such as medication changes, variations in symptom severity, or other situational influences that can impact recall performance. Therefore, assessing cognitive functions such as verbal memory twice and expecting consistent results can be challenging, especially among patients with schizophrenia, whose cognitive performance may fluctuate over time. Such factors may contribute to the observed variability in scores across test administrations.

The range of Pearson correlation coefficients suggests that while some aspects of recall demonstrate stronger reliability, others, particularly in the delayed recall assessments, are less stable [31]. This inconsistency may highlight the complexity of memory processes in individuals with schizophrenia, where certain recall types may be more susceptible to cognitive fluctuations. It also points to the need for careful interpretation of scores, as changes may not solely reflect true differences in cognitive ability but could also stem from contextual factors affecting test performance. Despite these moderate results, the overall findings support the continued use of the Free and Cued Recall test as a valid measure for assessing memory in schizophrenia. However, practitioners and researchers should be aware of the potential for variability and consider additional assessments or longer retest intervals to enhance reliability. Future studies could benefit from exploring the underlying factors contributing to these fluctuations, thereby refining the tool and improving its psychometric properties in diverse contexts.

The Free and Cued Recall test demonstrated positive correlations with the BACS verbal memory and BACS working memory, as well as with the MoCA test providing evidence for concurrent validity [18]. Among healthy controls, the 16-item Free and Cued Recall test demonstrated significant positive correlations with BACS verbal memory and working memory scores, supporting convergent validity in both clinical and non-clinical samples. However, these findings provide preliminary evidence of convergent validity and should be interpreted with caution due to the overlap in samples with the BACS study [14] which introduces non-independence of measures.

No significant correlations were observed with subjective cognitive complaints (SASCCS) or with age, suggesting that subjective and objective memory assessments may diverge even in healthy populations. These findings align with previous research that has shown similar associations between objective cognitive measures and memory performance in individuals with schizophrenia [2, 27]. The alignment of our findings with previous research underscores the established relationship between memory performance and overall cognitive functioning in individuals with schizophrenia. Numerous studies have documented similar associations, indicating that deficits in memory, particularly verbal memory, are linked to broader cognitive impairments, as shown in research by [2]. These studies emphasize that cognitive assessments should encompass various domains, as improvements in memory are often correlated with enhancements in attention and executive function. Moreover, the consistent findings across research suggest that memory deficits can serve as predictive markers for cognitive health and functional outcomes, highlighting the importance of integrating memory evaluations into clinical practice. This comprehensive approach can ultimately lead to more effective treatment strategies and improved quality of life for individuals with schizophrenia.The moderate positive correlations observed between the 16-item Free and Cued Recall test and both the BACS working memory subtest (r = 0.421) and the MoCA (r = 0.474) support its convergent validity within a clinical population. While some psychometric guidelines (e.g., Carlson & Herdman, 2012) propose that correlations below 0.50 may be considered insufficient for strong convergent validity, it is important to contextualize these thresholds within the complexities of cognitive functioning in schizophrenia. Memory performance, especially in tasks involving free and cued recall, often reflects interactions between multiple cognitive domains—attention, encoding, retrieval, and executive function. Consequently, perfect overlap with broader cognitive screening tools is neither expected nor necessarily desirable. The correlations we observed are consistent with prior studies validating cognitive tools in schizophrenia, where moderate but statistically significant associations are commonly interpreted as evidence of meaningful convergence. Nonetheless, we acknowledge this as a limitation and suggest that future research should explore convergent validity further using more domain-specific memory assessments to refine the psychometric evaluation of the Arabic version.

A significant negative correlation was observed between the Free and Cued Recall test and the SASCCS scale, indicating a reverse relationship between subjective and objective measures of cognitive function [32]. This suggests that patients may report lower subjective cognitive functioning even when their objective recall abilities are comparatively intact. The significant negative correlation between the Free and Cued Recall test and the SASCCS scale suggests a complex relationship between subjective and objective measures of cognitive function in individuals with schizophrenia. Specifically, this finding indicates that patients who report higher cognitive complaints indicating a perception of cognitive dysfunction tend to score lower on objective memory assessments, reflecting actual verbal memory deficits. However, it also raises the possibility that some patients may perceive their cognitive abilities as poorer than they are, even when their objective recall abilities are comparatively intact. This discrepancy could stem from various factors, including heightened self-awareness of cognitive challenges, anxiety about memory performance, or the impact of psychiatric symptoms on self-perception [33]. Consequently, the findings underscore the importance of considering both subjective reports and objective assessments when evaluating cognitive function in schizophrenia, as the subjective experience of cognitive impairment may not always align with actual performance, complicating treatment and support strategies [33].

Additionally, no significant association was found between the Free and Cued Recall test and the PANSS scale [34]. This lack of correlation raises important questions about the relationship between cognitive assessments and general psychopathology measures in this population. Overall, these findings highlight the complexity of cognitive functioning in schizophrenia and underscore the importance of using both subjective and objective measures to gain a comprehensive understanding of cognitive abilities in this population.

Limitation

Our study has several limitations. Its cross-sectional design does not allow for the inference of concurrent validity and limits the ability to draw causal inferences. This design prevents the establishment of causal relationships between variables, as it does not allow for the assessment of temporal sequences or changes over time. Therefore, longitudinal studies are needed to better understand the directionality and causality of the observed associations. Given that the population consists of chronically hospitalized patients with schizophrenia whose cognitive function may be severely impaired, selection bias is a possibility. Furthermore, the results cannot be generalized to the broader population due to the small sample size and the fact that patients were recruited from a single site. Expanding the sample size and including participants from outside the hospital setting, as well as from other Arabic-speaking countries, would significantly enhance the generalizability of our findings. Also, a limitation of the present study is the potential for circular validation due to the partial overlap in samples with a previous study that validated the BACS battery. Specifically, the current study used the BACS List Learning subtest to examine the convergent validity of the RL-RI 16, while the previous BACS validation study included the RL-RI 16 as part of the comparative battery. This introduces a degree of non-independence in the measures. Information bias may have occurred, as participants might not have provided accurate details during face-to-face interviews. The factor analysis indicated factors comprising only two items, which may lead to instability and could affect the evaluation of verbal memory. Specifically, the KMO value of 0.624, although within the acceptable range, was relatively low and may have impacted the stability of the factor model. Additionally, exploratory factor analysis conducted among healthy controls also revealed a two-factor structure. Although the cumulative variance explained was slightly lower, the factor structure remained consistent with that of the schizophrenia group, distinguishing between free recall and cued recall components. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure was 0.609, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (p < 0.001), indicating that the data were suitable for factor analysis. This consistency across groups suggests that the test reliably captures two separable memory processes regardless of diagnostic status, although the internal consistency among healthy controls was more modest (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.542), possibly due to lower variability in scores. The test-retest reliability values observed in this study were low and lacked sufficient stability for clinical application. Therefore, further studies are warranted to confirm our findings.

Conclusion

This study presents the first psychometric validation of the Arabic version of the 16-item Free and Cued Recall Test (RL-RI 16) in a clinical population of patients with schizophrenia. The results demonstrated that the 16-item free and cued recall have a good psychometric features, including high internal consistency, strong construct validity, adequate concurrent validity and the test-retest reliability was found to be low to moderate. Therefore, although the test shows promise as a useful instrument, its suitability for routine clinical use and research investigations should be considered with caution. The study’s novel contribution lies not only in the linguistic adaptation but also in the cultural and clinical contextualization of a widely used neuropsychological tool, making it accessible to Arabic-speaking mental health professionals and patients. Further investigation is necessary to confirm our results and investigate other facets of the validity, reliability, and cut-off variables of the 16-item free and cued memory test.
