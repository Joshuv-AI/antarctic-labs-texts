The escalating burden of osteoarthritis in East and Southeast Asia: an information-analytical study from the GBD 2021
Authors: Jinlei Zhou, Yuan Zhang, Shanggao Xie, Yanlei Li, Qice Sun, Xujie Tang, Tingxiao Zhao, Xinji Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReport on the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR), and age-standardized disability-adjusted life years (ASDR) of osteoarthritis and their trends at the global, regional, and national levels using data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 study.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study to assess the burden of osteoarthritis in relation to age, sex, and socio-demographic indices (SDI) using data from the GBD 2021 study. The ASIR, ASPR, and ASDR at global, regional, and national levels were extracted. The estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) from 1990 to 2021 was then calculated using SDIs, complemented by cluster and frontier analyses.ResultsIn 2021, there were 606.98 million cases of osteoarthritis globally, with the ASIR increasing from 489.78 to 535 cases per 100,000 individuals between 1990 and 2021 (EAPC 0.33 [95% CI: 0.31–0.35]). This considerable rise was evident across all age groups (especially within the middle-aged group aged 30–60 years), gender groups, SDI quintiles and GBD regions. The East and Southeast Asian regions are concurrently experiencing a notable surge in the ASIR, ASPR, and ASDR associated with osteoarthritis. Frontier analyses indicate that Japan and Singapore possess the highest unrealized health potential.ConclusionBy 2021, the global burden of osteoarthritis had shifted substantially toward East and Southeast Asia, with an especially pronounced surge among middle-aged adults (30–60 years). These observations underscore the imperative for clinicians to recognize osteoarthritis as an emerging midlife health challenge in these regions, calling for earlier diagnosis, tailored preventive strategies, and revised clinical guidelines to address this evolving demographic landscape. Key Points• This study reveals a sharply escalating burden of osteoarthritis among middle-aged adults (30–60 years) in East and Southeast Asia, underscoring an urgent need for clinicians to strengthen early detection and optimize management strategies in this population.• The migration of osteoarthritis’s epicenter to Asia underscores the pressing need for region-specific clinical guidelines and interventions, particularly in nations with substantial unmet health potential such as Japan and Singapore.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10067-025-07740-1.Keywords: Asia, Disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), Epidemiology, Incidence, Middle aged, Osteoarthritis, The Global burden of disease (GBD)

Introduction

Osteoarthritis represents the most prevalent form of arthritis, with aetiology attributed to biochemical alterations and biomechanical stresses affecting the articular cartilage [1, 2]. Osteoarthritis is the leading cause of chronic pain and long-term disability in adults, exerting a significant socio-medical and economic impact [3, 4]. The estimated cost of osteoarthritis treatment is approximately 1–2.5% of the gross national product. In 2016, healthcare spending on osteoarthritis in the United States reached a considerable $80 billion [1]. It is therefore imperative that a comprehensive assessment of the burden and trends of osteoarthritis be conducted in order to facilitate a more nuanced understanding of its impact on population health.

A recently published Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2020 study indicates that the global burden of osteoarthritis is increasing in general and is concentrated in the high-income Asia–Pacific region [3]. This finding is inconsistent with the conclusions of the 2017 GBD study, which concluded that the burden of osteoarthritis is concentrated in high-income North America [5]. Furthermore, the GBD 2020 study did not elucidate trends in the burden of osteoarthritis across countries or regions.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to analyze the global burden and trends of osteoarthritis from 1990 to 2021; to stratify the global trends by age group, gender, and socio-demographic index (SDI); to analyzing burdens and trends at both national and regional levels.

Method

Data sources

All data utilized in this study were directly sourced from the latest 2021 GBD study. The 2021 GBD study encompasses data on 371 diseases and injuries, as well as 88 major risk factors, across 204 countries and territories [6, 7]. Estimates in the GBD 2021 database are derived from vital registration systems, vital registration samples, cause-of-death autopsies, survey record data from the Global Health Data Exchange, and the GBD Population Health Data Repository[6]. The existing literature provides comprehensive descriptions of the methods used to estimate the burden of fatal and non-fatal diseases [8, 9]. A more detailed description of the GBD protocol can be found on the web (https://www.healthdata.org/research-analysis/about-gbd/protocol). Ethical review was not required for this study since the GBD 2021 database is publicly accessible.

In the GBD 2021 study, osteoarthritis is defined as osteoarthritis that has been radiologically confirmed as Kellgren-Lawrence grade 2–4 and is accompanied by symptoms [10–13]. Kellgren-Lawrence grade 2 indicates the presence of one defined osteophyte in the joint; grade 3 indicates several osteophytes and narrowing of the joint space; and grade 4 adds a bony deformity to the criteria of grade 3. Symptomatic osteoarthritis necessitates the manifestation of pain for at least one month within the past twelve months.

This study utilized the Global Health Data Exchange GBD Results Tool to query the data (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/). We designated “osteoarthritis” as the cause of the disease and selected “prevalence,” “incidence,” and “disability-adjusted life years (DALYs)” as the evaluation indicators. Subsequently, the retrieved data were collected according to different years, levels of socio-economic development, countries and regions, genders, and various age groups. All raw data collected have been summarized in Supplementary Table 2.

Statistical analysis

The number of incidence cases, prevalence cases, DALYs, and their respective age-standardized rates (ASRs) were directly obtained from the GBD 2021. All ASRs are reported per 100,000 population. The 95% uncertainty intervals (UI) were defined by the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the ordered 1,000 estimates derived from GBD's algorithm.

We calculated the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) to track the trend in ASR between 1990 and 2021, employing a linear regression model to determine the 95% confidence interval (CI) for the EAPC [14]. A linear regression line was constructed using the natural logarithm of the fitted rate, represented by the equation y = α + βx + ε, where x denotes the calendar year and y is the logarithm of the rate, and the calendar year was designated as the dependent variable, and the EAPC was calculated as 100 * (exp(β)—1). If both the EAPC value and the lower limit of the 95% CI are greater than 0, the ASR is considered to be increasing; conversely, if both the EAPC value and the upper limit of the 95% CI are less than 0, the ASR is considered to be decreasing. Then, we aggregated EAPCs for ASPR, ASIR and ASDR from a global sample of countries and conducted a comprehensive spatial clustering analysis to illustrate and quantify the geographic variations and trends in the burden of osteoarthritis [15]. Furthermore, frontier analyses were conducted based on three ASRs and SDIs with the objective of evaluating the untapped health potential of each country or region in accordance with its level of development.

The study was analyzed using the R programming language (version 4.3.2) and a number of R software packages, including ggplot2, dplyr, purrr, tidyr and ggsci.

Results

Global trends

The global incidence of osteoarthritis has risen markedly from 1990 to 2021, with rates escalating from 489.78 per 100,000 population [95% UI: 433.1–541.5] in 1990 to 535 per 100,000 population [472.38–591.97] in 2021, reflecting an EAPC of 0.33 [95% CI: 0.31–0.35]. Consequently, the ASPR of osteoarthritis surged between 1990 and 2021, increasing from 6,393.12 per 100,000 population [5,683.2–7,059.53] in 1990 to 6,967.29 per 100,000 population [6,180.7–7,686.06] in 2021, with an EAPC of 0.34 [0.31–0.37]. The ASDR attributable to osteoarthritis escalated between 1990 and 2021, rising from 222.8 per 100,000 population [106.65–450.29] in 1990 to 244.5 per 100,000 population [117.06–493.11] in 2021, with an EAPC of 0.37 [0.33–0.4]. Compared to data from 1990, the global incidence of osteoarthritis, the number of prevalent cases, and the DALYs in 2021 have all increased by a factor of 1.33 (incident cases: 46,632,144 in 2021 vs 20,900,510 in 1990; prevalent cases: 606,989,319 in 2021 vs 256,076,700 in 1990; DALYs: 21,304,566 in 2021 vs 8,918,857 in 1990) (Supplementary Table 1). The global subscale in Supplementary Table 2 offers detailed data on osteoarthritis prevalence, incidence, and DALYs for each year from 1990 to 2021.

SDI trends

In 2021, the ASIR of osteoarthritis showed the most pronounced increase in the middle SDI quintile, with an EAPC of 0.48 (95% CI: 0.44–0.51; from 475.42 per 100,000 population [419.58–527.87] in 1990 to 536.49 per 100,000 population [473.16–595.02] in 2021), followed by the low-middle SDI quintile (EAPC: 0.42 [0.4–0.44]), the high-middle SDI quintile (EAPC: 0.38 [0.35–0.42]), the low SDI quintile (EAPC: 0.29 [0.28–0.31]), and finally the high SDI quintile (EAPC: 0.21 [0.17–0.24]). The rate of increase in the ASIR of osteoarthritis within the middle SDI quintile was more than twice that observed in the high SDI quintile. The ASPR of osteoarthritis exhibits a similar trend. Consequently, osteoarthritis ASDR increased substantially across all countries with varying SDI quintiles, with the most significant rise observed in the middle SDI quintile, exhibiting an EAPC of 0.57 (95% CI: 0.53–0.61; from 208.27 per 100,000 population [100.47–419.68] in 1990 to 240.41 per 100,000 population [115.09–483.99] in 2021), followed by the low-middle SDI quintile (EAPC: 0.5 [0.48–0.53]), the high-middle SDI quintile (EAPC: 0.4 [0.36–0.44]), the low SDI quintile (EAPC: 0.37 [0.35–0.39]), and the high SDI quintile (EAPC: 0.27 [0.21–0.33]) (Table 1).

54 GBD regional trends

As shown in Fig. 2 and Table 1, the most significant increases in ASIR for osteoarthritis between 1990 and 2021 were observed in East Asia (from 487.3 cases per 100,000 population [95% UI: 428.32–544.04] in 1990 to 554.47 cases per 100,000 population [486.91–619.37] in 2021, with an EAPC of 0.58 [0.5–0.65]), Southeast Asia (from 376.04 cases per 100,000 population [332.26–418.37] in 1990 to 437.13 cases per 100,000 population [386.13–485.01] in 2021, with an EAPC of 0.51 [0.5–0.51]), and the Basic Health System regions (from 470.87 cases per 100,000 population [415.49–524.65] in 1990 to 531.94 cases per 100,000 population [467.55–591.54] in 2021, with an EAPC of 0.49 [0.45–0.53]). Similarly, the regions with the most substantial increases in ASDR for osteoarthritis between 1990 and 2021 include East Asia (from 211 per 100,000 population [102.08–424.58] in 1990 to 245.04 per 100,000 population [117.45–492.41] in 2021, with an EAPC of 0.66 [0.58–0.75]), the South-East Asia Region (from 179.5 per 100,000 population [102.08–424.58] in 1990 to 245.04 per 100,000 population [102.6–431.19] in 2021, with an EAPC of 0.61 [0.58–0.63]), and Southeast Asia (from 163.3 per 100,000 population [78.14–329.58] in 1990 to 196.18 per 100,000 population [93.56–393.42] in 2021, with an EAPC of 0.61 [0.61–0.62]). It is important to note that the South-East Asia Region, as designated by the World Health Organization, reflects a framework grounded in health policy and regional cooperation, and consequently includes South Asian countries such as India and Bangladesh. In contrast, the term "Southeast Asia" is typically understood as a geographic and cultural construct, often aligned with the United Nations' geographical divisions, and excludes South Asian nations. In addition, cluster analysis of the EAPC visualized in Fig. 3 demonstrates a significant increasing trend in the burden of osteoarthritis from 1990 to 2021 in the Asian region, particularly in East Asia. Conversely, there is a notable decreasing trend in the burden of osteoarthritis in the European and American regions, especially in North America.

Global trends by sex

The ASIR for osteoarthritis increased globally in both men and women from 1990 to 2021. The EAPC was 0.28 (95% CI: 0.26–0.3; from 408.56 per 100 000 population [95% UI: 361.23–452.94] in 1990 to 445.74 per 100 000 population [393.68–493.98] in 2021) for males and 0.36 (95% CI: 0.32–0.39; from 568.6 per 100 000 population [502.58–627.86] in 1990 to 621.32 per 100 000 population [548.49–686.94] in 2021) for females. Consequently, the ASPR for osteoarthritis also rose in both males (EAPC: 0.29 [0.27–0.32]) and women (EAPC: 0.38 [0.34–0.43]) between 1990 and 2021. The DALYs for osteoarthritis also increased globally in both males and females from 1990 to 2021, with an EAPC of 0.31 (95% CI: 0.29–0.34; from 182.42 per 100,000 population [87.58–369.04] in 1990 to 200.52 per 100,000 population [95.88–404.87] in 2021) for males and 0.42 (95% CI: 0.37–0.47; from 257.65 per 100,000 population [123.39–520.4] in 1990 to 284.14 per 100,000 population [136.29–573.11] in 2021) for females.

As shown in Table 1, in 2021, the global number of osteoarthritis cases in women was 27.7 million (59.41%), the prevalence was 368 million (60.73%), and the DALYs for osteoarthritis were 13.02 million (61.12%). Global trends by age group

Globally, the most substantial increase in ASIR for osteoarthritis between 1990 and 2021 was observed in individuals aged 45–49 years, with an EAPC of 0.47 (95% CI: 0.40–0.55; from 1,295.42 per 100,000 population in 1990 [95% UI: 1,007.99–1,618.39] to 1,442.25 per 100,000 population in 2021 [1,115.85–1,797.5]). This was followed by those aged 35–39 years (EAPC: 0.46 [0.37–0.55]) and 40–44 years (EAPC: 0.46 [0.38–0.53]). A similar pattern is observed in the growth trends of both ASPRs and DALYs (Supplementary Table 1). Figure 7 illustrates that the ASIR for osteoarthritis among individuals aged 50–64 has reached its zenith from 1990 to 2021. Additionally, the ASDR and ASPR for osteoarthritis exhibit a progressive upward trajectory with advancing age over the same period.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the inaugural study to delineate the burden and trends of osteoarthritis by age, sex, and SDI across all populations from 1990 to 2021 at the global, regional, and national levels. As shown in Supplementary Table 1, the annual incidence, annual prevalence, and annual DALYs of osteoarthritis have increased dramatically globally over the past three decades, reaching 46.63 million incidence cases (a 123.11% increase), 606.98 million prevalence cases (a 137.03% increase), and 21.3 million DALYs (a 138.87% increase) in 2021. The burden of osteoarthritis is increasing across all age subgroups, in all five SDI quintiles, and in all 54 GBD regions, in both sexes.

The ASIR for osteoarthritis increased nearly twice as fast in the middle-aged cohort (30–60 years) as compared to the older cohort (60 years and older), which has traditionally been the focus of previous studies on controlling the burden of osteoarthritis [3, 16, 17]. This comparison suggests that osteoarthritis is progressively affecting younger individuals, highlighting an urgent need to focus on controlling the burden of osteoarthritis in the middle-aged population. It was observed by Jaimie that although the prevalence of osteoarthritis rises with age, 3.5% of adults in the working age demographic have some form of osteoarthritis [3]. Furthermore, the rate of increase in both the ASPR and ASDR followed a similar pattern, indicating that targeting middle-aged patients with osteoarthritis has been an ineffective strategy. The development of osteoarthritis in middle age is associated with advancing age, elevated body mass index (BMI) and regular physical exertion, particularly kneeling or squatting, during the working years [18–20]. Therefore, global health agencies should enhance awareness of osteoarthritis and advocate for more robust early prevention and treatment strategies targeting middle-aged populations across all countries and regions.

From 1990 to 2021, the osteoarthritis ASIR, ASPR, and ASDR in the middle SDI quintile grew twice as fast as those in the high SDI quintile. The two regions exhibiting the most rapid concurrent growth in ASIR, ASPR, and ASDR for osteoarthritis are East Asia (including Mongolia and China) and Southeast Asia (including Thailand and Myanmar). The markedly elevated growth rate indicates that these regions are wholly inadequately equipped to cope with the exponentially increasing prevalence of osteoarthritis. On the one hand, the necessity of heavy physical occupational activities for livelihoods in developing Asian countries, particularly in rural areas, has been identified as a contributing factor to the prevalence of osteoarthritis [21]. On the other hand, the BMI of the Asian population is also steadily increasing. Despite the efficacy of joint replacement surgery in treating severe osteoarthritis, it remains inaccessible to the majority of the population in these regions due to cognitive and financial limitations. Despite the established efficacy of joint replacement surgery in treating severe osteoarthritis, factors such as limited health literacy, inadequate understanding of available treatment options, misconceptions about surgical effectiveness, cultural apprehensions regarding invasive procedures, and financial constraints may collectively discourage patients in certain Asian populations from pursuing this intervention. Moreover, in 2021, the highest ASIR, ASPR, and ASDR values are observed in East Asia, particularly in the Republic of Korea. The above findings are inconsistent with those of the 2017 GBD study, suggesting that the burden of osteoarthritis has shifted and is now largely concentrated in East and Southeast Asia, with the burden continuing to rise [5].

The ASIR, ASPR, and ASDR have declined in only three of the world's 204 countries: Israel, the Netherlands, and Denmark. However, the fastest growing countries for ASIR, ASPR, and ASDR were not located in the Asian region, but in Equatorial Guinea in west-central Africa. This finding is consistent with the 2017 GBD study, suggesting some dispersion in the distribution of the burden of osteoarthritis [5]. Frontier analyses indicate that specific ethnic groups on certain islands exhibit the highest unrealized health potential at their respective levels of development. For example, Japan in East Asia and Singapore in Southeast Asia, despite the absence of ASIR data, have been demonstrated to exhibit the greatest potential for health improvement in terms of ASPR and ASDR. This is related to the more frequent kneeling position in Japan [22]; but Singapore lacks studies on osteoarthritis-related risk factors.

To the best of our knowledge, this study offers a comprehensive description and analysis of the latest burden and trends of osteoarthritis from 1990 to 2021 at the global, regional, and national levels. Our findings advocate for increased research on osteoarthritis within middle-aged populations, specifically those aged 30 to 60 years, and underscore the necessity to enhance osteoarthritis guidelines and interventions tailored for East and South-East Asian countries. However, we acknowledge that variations in reporting practices across countries constitute a major limitation of this study. First, data heterogeneity arises because many low- and middle-income countries lack comprehensive civil registration and health information systems, leading to underreporting, misclassification, or incomplete documentation of osteoarthritis diagnoses, particularly in rural or medically underserved regions. Second, diagnostic criteria differ across settings; reliance on radiological confirmation (Kellgren-Lawrence grade ≥ 2) combined with symptomatic assessment may be applied inconsistently. Third, coding and classification practices vary. Although GBD employs a standardized framework, national health systems use different coding systems (e.g., ICD versions) and may not consistently distinguish OA from other rheumatic diseases. Fourth, estimation and modeling uncertainties exist. The GBD relies on complex modeling to infer prevalence in regions with limited data. While uncertainty intervals are reported, these estimates are more robust in countries with comprehensive data infrastructures than in those with sparse or unreliable data [23–25]. Therefore, we recommend that the estimated results for regions with limited data be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

By 2021, the global burden of osteoarthritis disease had shifted and was largely concentrated in East and Southeast Asia, with the burden continuing to grow. Strong recommendations to improve the prevention and treatment of osteoarthritis among individuals aged 30 to 60 years.
