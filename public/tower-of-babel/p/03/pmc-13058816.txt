The AI Innerve Algorithm: Automatic Reproducible Reading for Intraepidermal Nerve Fiber Density Analysis, a New Tool for Small‐Fiber Neuropathy Diagnosis
Authors: C Labeyrie, A Lorenzo de Brionne, O Trassard, V Blot, O Petit, D Hervault, D Medernach, O Morassi, C Guettier, D Adams, N Brunel, C Adam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo measure intra‐observer and interobserver correlations and reading times for visual and artificial intelligence algorithm‐assisted reading (innerve) of intraepidermal small nerve fiber density (IENFD) in skin biopsies.MethodsWe retrospectively selected 40 skin biopsy slides with PGP9.5/col4 double‐immunofluorescence labeling. The slides were scanned and read by two experienced observers (O1 and O2), on two occasions (visual reading 1 ‐VR1‐ and 2 ‐VR2‐). They were then read after predetection with the Innerve algorithm (assisted reading ‐AR‐). IENFD and reading time were evaluated to assess reliability and time saved.ResultsIntra‐observer reliability was evaluated by calculating intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). The ICC obtained were 0.95 (O1)/0.99 (O2) for the two VR and 0.96 (O1)/0.99 (O2) for VR1/AR. Inter‐rater ICC between O1 and O2 was 0.88, 0.87, and 0.93 for VR1, VR2, and AR, respectively. AR reading time was 1.96 times faster for O1 and 1.03 times slower for O2. Subgroup analyses revealed an interobserver ICC significantly > 0.7 (p < 0.05), with ICC > 0.9 regardless of sex and skin pigmentation.DiscussionInnerve is an innovative and reliable AI tool for IENFD determinations on skin biopsies, making such analyses—which were considered time‐consuming and difficult—easier for small‐fiber neuropathy diagnosis.Keywords: artificial intelligence, neuropathic pain, neuropathology, skin biopsy, small‐fiber neuropathy

Introduction

Small‐fiber neuropathy (SFN) results from damage to the Aδ/C peripheral nerve fibers causing pain, hyposensitivity to pain or temperature, and/or dysautonomic symptoms. Diabetes is the most common SFN cause, and other causes can progress to more severe neuropathy, such as familial amyloid neuropathy [1].

Standard nerve conduction studies cannot detect small‐fiber lesions; skin biopsy is the reference diagnostic test [2]. C fibers terminate as free endings in the skin epidermis, intersecting with the basal membrane, and can be labeled by anti‐PGP9.5 to quantify intraepidermal nerve fiber density (IENFD) on pathology examinations according to published guidelines and validated norms for age and sex, at the ankle [2, 3, 4].

This quantification is restricted to specialized laboratories, because of (i) the complexity of the immunofluorescence staining, (ii) the time‐consuming nature of the visual reading during which the pathologist counts fibers one‐by‐one under the microscope on three sections per level, and (iii) the difficulty of interpretation taking into account validated standards, clinical results, and other paraclinical assessments of small‐fiber function. These technical obstacles limit the widespread use of this technique despite its potential to reduce diagnostic uncertainty concerning SFN in patients suffering from compatible but nonspecific symptoms, such as chronic pain.

We have developed Innerve, an algorithm for digitized slides that automatically annotates small fibers, allowing pathologists to rapidly check and correct annotations to determine IENFD.

This study evaluated whether Innerve enables reproducible and faster IENFD determination by comparing two independent readers on digitized slides, performing two conventional visual readings (manual annotation of the fibers, one‐by‐one) and a third time using slides previously annotated by Innerve.

Methods

Study Participants

Patients who had undergone skin biopsies for clinical purposes at Bicetre Hospital (2019–2021) were selected to form a representative cohort, balanced for age, sex, skin pigmentation assessed on hematoxylin‐eosin‐saffron slides, and IENFD. Biopsies were performed at the ankle or thigh with a 3‐mm punch. Samples were fixed in paraformaldehyde and frozen.

Skin Biopsy Specimen Staining, Scanning and Innerve Processing

Frozen skin biopsy samples were reprocessed according to EFNS/PNS guidelines [2] with double‐immunofluorescence labeling for PGP9.5/anti‐col4 on three sample cuts per slide (Appendix S1). Slides were scanned (Pannoramic 250 Flash III 3DHistech scanner), anonymized, and analyzed with the Innerve algorithm (Figure 1A), regardless of staining or scanning quality and papillomatous complexity.

Innerve Algorithm

The training dataset comprised 5632 manually annotated fibers, regardless of staining or scanning quality, or papillomatous complexity. Details of the process used and an example of digitized slide processing are available in Appendix S1. A patent has been published online (WO2023139328A1 Method for automatically detecting a biological element in a tissue sample).

IENFD Determination by Visual and Assisted Reading

Two experts trained in IENFD visual reading for standard care acted as blind readers for the Innerve study: observer 1 (O1; CL) and observer 2 (O2; OM). IENFD was first evaluated by visual counting on digitized slides without Innerve annotations, with Caseviewers software (3DHistech). This first visual reading (VR1) was followed, 30 days later, by a second visual reading (VR2) and then, 30 days later, by an assisted reading (AR) with Innerve annotations for IENFD calculation by checking Innerve annotations to eliminate false positives and to add false negatives (Figure 1B).

Statistical Analysis

We calculated the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) (two‐way random method) to assess the reliability of IENFD estimation across observers, both with and without Innerve assistance. We performed a subgroup analysis for the pigmentation, sex, and IENFD groups (0%–30%, 30%–60%, 60%–100%, > 100% of the norm for age and sex).

We performed F tests for ICC significantly greater than 0.7. The results were considered significant if the p‐value was < 0.05. Bland–Altman plots were used to evaluate the degree of agreement: x‐axis mean density/slide (across 6 readings); y‐axis = deviation from the mean value.

Ethics

The Innerve study was authorized by the competent ethics committee (Institutional Review Board Mondor. IRB#00011558).

Results

Demographic and Clinical Data

We included 40 patients with a median age of 51.5 years in this study (Appendix S2). The sex ratio (M/F) was 18/22 and 12/40 (30%) patients had dark skin. The IENFD values obtained for VR1 (observer 2) were evenly distributed between poor [< 3/mm, n = 10 (25%)], moderately low [3–5.99/mm, n = 9 (22.5%)], slightly low [6–8.99/mm, n = 9 (22.5%)] and close to or above the norm [> 9/mm, n = 12 (30%)]. Skin biopsies were performed for ATTRv follow‐up in 20 patients and for clinically suspected SFN in the other 20.

Intra‐Observer and Interobserver Reliability for IENFD, With and Without Innerve Assistance

The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was > 0.9 for intra‐observer reliability between the two visual readings VR1/VR2 and between these two readings and the Innerve‐assisted reading (AR) (Table 1).

For interobserver reliability analysis, we obtained ICC values of 0.88 [95% CI: 0.53–0.96] for VR1, 0.87 [95% CI: 0.43–0.95] for VR2, and 0.93 [95% CI: 0.83–0.97] for AR. Assisted reading with Innerve yielded an intra‐observer ICC > 0.9 regardless of pigmentation and sex and improved the interobserver ICC. Innerve AR improved interobserver ICC regardless of density, but with a lower statistical power (probably due to small sample sizes, Data S1).

The Bland–Altman plot representation of IENFD VR and AR for each observer (Figure 1C) revealed a lower level of dispersion of inter‐observer and intraobserver IENFD values at lower densities, as expected given the smaller number of events to be counted. This finding was supported by the analysis of ICC by density group, in which ICC values were highest for the lowest densities (Data S1, statistically nonsignificant trend).

Time Saved

For VR1, mean reading time was 13.5 min/slide for O1 and 10 min/slide for O2. The difference in mean reading time between VR1 and AR (AR‐VR reading time) was −6.82 min for observer 1 (1.92 times faster with AR) and + 0.45 min for observer 2 (1.03 times slower) (Figure 1D). The median AR time for O2 of slides with IENFD > 9/mm (which took the longest to read, i.e., 10 slides) was 15 min for the first half read, and 12 min for the second half, indicating a training bias in the interface.

Discussion

International guidelines recommend determining IENFD on skin biopsy for the diagnosis of SFN [2]. This method has been shown to be reliable across pathology laboratory or observer [3, 5], with few exceptions [6]. Its widespread adoption is limited by the high level of expertise required, including visual counting which is technically demanding, time‐consuming, and prone to artifacts, which can compromise intra‐observer and interobserver reproducibility. Few solutions, such as fluorescence image processing or reader‐assistance software, have been proposed to address these challenges [7].

In our study, the mean visual reading time per observer was remarkably low due to extensive training and the particular conditions of attention required: for instance, a novice observer from our team took a mean of 48 min per slide, whereas two experienced observers estimated their visual reading time under a fluorescence microscope at about 17 min per slide (unpublished personal data).

AI algorithms have demonstrated substantial potential in pathology, particularly in oncology, achieving performance comparable to expert visual reading [8]. In neuromuscular diseases, AI has begun to assist in imaging analysis, such as automated predictions for CMT1a on muscle MRI [9]. Developing an AI algorithm for IENFD was particularly challenging due to the large size of digitized slides and rare small nerve fibers only a few pixels wide, requiring sophisticated deep‐learning methods to reconstruct the 3D structures of rare small fibers in 50 μm‐thick skin sections.

Our Innerve algorithm‐assisted reading showed high reliability regardless of observer and patient sex, age, or pigmentation. Expert observers maintained excellent intra‐observer and interobserver reliability with Innerve, while the time required was halved for the observer familiar with the platform (CL), highlighting the importance of interface training. However, while our initial results are promising, larger multicenter and external validation studies are required, with particular attention paid to the scanning of slides, which is probably a crucial step when using Innerve.

Despite strong international recommendations, skin biopsy remains underutilized, often compared with other small fibers assessment methods such as LEP, Sudoscan and QST [10, 11]. The main obstacles are the technical difficulty and time‐consuming nature of IENFD determination. Innerve offers the potential to simplify and accelerate this process, particularly for slides with high fiber counts, though human supervision remains essential for borderline or technically suboptimal cases.

The future development of Innerve should explore its reliability for other laboratory‐based biopsy examinations, observers, and its predictive values for normal/abnormal IENFD relative to unassisted visual reading, as recommended by experts for the use of AI in pathology [12]. Advanced functions should include the calculation of IENF parameters such as length, numbers of branches, and axonal swelling. Innerve is a revolutionary neuropathological tool paving the way for AI assistance in complex assessments.
