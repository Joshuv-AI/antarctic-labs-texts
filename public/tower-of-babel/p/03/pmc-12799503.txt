Neuropeptide Y1 receptor antagonist alleviated osteoarthritis by restoring chondrocyte autophagy through PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway
Authors: Song Liu, Jianqun Wu, Yucong Lin, Haifeng Liang, Yu Cai, Le Wang, Zhao Wang, Hongxun Sang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteoarthritis (OA) is a debilitating joint disorder affecting millions worldwide, characterized by progressive cartilage degradation and chronic pain. Emerging evidence suggests that neuropeptide Y (NPY) and its Y1 receptors are involved in OA pathogenesis, although the underlying molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood. This study investigates the role of NPY/Y1R signaling in OA progression through PI3K/AKT/mTOR-mediated regulation of chondrocyte autophagy.MethodsHuman cartilage samples were collected from ten OA patients (3 male,7 female, 63–75 years old) undergoing total knee arthroplasty and graded using the Kellgren–Lawrence system. Primary chondrocytes were isolated from neonatal C57BL/6 mice and treated with NPY (0.01–5 μM) or interleukin-1β (IL-1β, 10 ng/mL) to mimic OA-like degeneration. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and KEGG pathway analysis were performed to identify NPY-regulated signaling pathways. In vivo, OA was induced in 8-week-old male C57BL/6 mice via destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) or intra-articular injections of NPY (5 μM, every 4 weeks). Mice were treated with the Y1R antagonist (0.1 μM, weekly) or vehicle control. Pain behavior was assessed using von Frey filaments and CatWalk gait analysis. Cartilage degeneration was evaluated via histology (Safranin O/Fast Green, OARSI scoring), immunofluorescence (COLII, MMP13, LC3-II, p62), and micro-CT (subchondral bone remodeling, osteophyte formation). The activation status of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and autophagy-related markers was determined via Western blotting and immunofluorescence assays under both in vitro and in vivo conditions.ResultsNPY and Y1R expression were significantly elevated in human OA cartilage compared to normal tissue. In vitro, NPY (5 μM) suppressed chondrocyte proliferation, reduced COLII expression, and increased MMP13 production. RNA-seq revealed NPY-mediated activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and inhibition of autophagy-related genes. NPY treatment enhanced the phosphorylation levels of PI3K, AKT, and mTOR, while concurrently decreasing LC3II expression and increasing p62 accumulation. The Y1R antagonist reversed these effects, restoring autophagy and attenuating cartilage degradation. In vivo, NPY injections induced OA-like changes, including cartilage thinning, osteophyte formation, and mechanical allodynia. Y1R antagonist treatment mitigated these effects, improving gait parameters and reducing subchondral bone sclerosis. Immunofluorescence confirmed that Y1R inhibition decreased PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling and enhanced autophagy in chondrocytes.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that NPY/Y1R signaling exacerbates OA progression through PI3K/AKT/mTOR-mediated suppression of chondrocyte autophagy. Pharmacological inhibition of Y1R emerges as a novel therapeutic strategy, effectively targeting both cartilage degeneration and pain, with potential disease-modifying effects on osteoarthritis progression.The translational potential of this articleThis study highlights the NPY Y1 receptor as a promising therapeutic target for OA by demonstrating its role in modulating chondrocyte autophagy via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. The results support the development of Y1R antagonists as novel OA therapeutics. This work bridges molecular discovery to potential clinical application, offering hope for a transformative approach to OA management.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a global public health concern, with a disease burden surpassing that of several other chronic conditions, including many cancers and hypertensive heart disease [1]. Epidemiological studies demonstrate that OA affects over 10 % of the global population, with prevalence rates approaching 100 % in individuals aged 65 years and older [1,2]. Current clinical treatments primarily focus on pain relief and inflammation control using non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), with total joint replacement as the final option [2,3]. Sympathetic neuropeptides have emerged as significant mediators in joint homeostasis, demonstrating essential trophic effects that are vital for maintaining joint tissue integrity [2,4]. Neuropeptide Y (NPY) is widely expressed in the central and peripheral nervous systems and acts as a local modulator of pain and inflammatory processes in OA. NPY-positive primary afferent C-fibers have been in painful knee joints. Serum NPY levels have been found to correlate with anatomical severity and clinical symptoms of knee OA. In addition, the pathophysiological role of NPY in infection, inflammation, and autoimmunity has been suggested by its presence and variable concentrations in OA joint fluid, supporting its role as a potential regulator of pain transmission and perception in OA [5]. NPY and its Y1 receptors are localized in key pain-signaling centers throughout the nervous system [6]. However, paradoxical results of the direct effects of NPY on chondrocyte also exist. A recent study has shown that, intra-articular NPY administration directly promotes chondrocyte hypertrophy and cartilage matrix degradation. However, inhibition of NPY2R but not NPY1R-specific antagonist, can remarkably ameliorate NPY-mediated effects [7]. Notably, cold stress or NPY treatment mainly upregulates Y2R expression, while Y1R levels remain stable. This suggests that the functions of Y1R and Y2R might depend on different pathological environments, like stress versus chronic degeneration.

NPY effectively facilitated late hypertrophy/mineralization of ATDC5 cells via Y1 receptor signaling, rather than via Y2 receptor signaling [8]. However, the level of NPY in the joint fluid of human OA patients is related to pain and the degree of degeneration [5]. Y1R has been widely confirmed to be involved in pain transmission, consistent with the stimulatory effect of Y1R in the dorsal root ganglia and spinal cord [9]. Y2R is mainly distributed on presynaptic membranes and negatively regulates NPY release, thereby exacerbating pain [10]. Therefore, we hypothesize that it might be possible to alleviate the pain and degeneration of the OA knee joint by activating the Y1 receptor.

Autophagy, a cellular self-degradation process, is a critical mechanism for maintaining cartilage homeostasis and preventing OA [11,12]. In OA, impaired autophagy leads to chondrocyte apoptosis and accelerated slow degradation. Conversely, enhancing autophagy has been shown to protect chondrocytes and mitigate cartilage degeneration. The PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway serves as a central regulator of chondrocyte autophagy, modulating critical aspects of cartilage homeostasis [13,14]. This pathway functions as a key molecular switch that controls autophagy: activation of PI3K and AKT promotes mTOR activity, which suppresses autophagy by inhibiting the formation of autophagosomes and the degradation of damaged cellular components. In chondrocytes, dysregulation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is strongly associated with reduced autophagic flux, resulting in chondrocyte apoptosis and cartilage degeneration in OA. Conversely, inhibition of mTOR or upstream components of the pathway enhances autophagy, thereby promoting chondrocyte survival and preserving cartilage integrity [15]. NPY may further modulate autophagy via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. Targeting NPY/Y1R signaling to restore autophagy has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for OA.

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the dose-dependent effects of NPY on behavioral outcomes in a mouse model and on chondrocytes in vitro. In addition, we hypothesized that Y1 receptor antagonists could block the effects of NPY and restore autophagy by inhibiting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, thereby preventing OA.

Materials and methods

Collection of human samples

Ten patients (3 male,7 female, 63–75 years old) undergoing total knee arthroplasty in our institution were enrolled. Cartilage tissues were harvested and divided into Grades III-IV in reference to Kellgren–Lawrence grade system using preoperative X-rays. Based on the severity of cartilage degeneration and the weight-bearing area, normal and OA cartilage tissues were selected [16]. The study was reviewed and approved by the institutional ethics committee, and all patients consented to this project (No.2025024).

Primary chondrocyte culture

The cartilaginous portions of metatarsal bones isolated from newborn C57 BL/6 (less than 6 days) were dissected, rinsed in phosphate-buffered saline, and then pretreated with 0.25 % trypsin for 30 min at 37 °C, followed by incubating in 0.2 % collagenase II for 3 h. The cell suspension was gently aspirated and passed through a 70-μm nylon mesh filter, followed by sequential washes in phosphate buffer solution (PBS) and serum-free DMEM before final cell counting. Chondrocytes were seeded at a density of 5 × 105 cells/mL in a 100-mm dish in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium with 1 % penicillin– streptomycin, 50 μg/mL ascorbic acid, and 10 % fetal bovine serum (Gibco). The culture medium was replaced every 48 h.

Animals

A total of 56 C57 BL/6 mice (eight-week-old, male) were used and housed 4 to 6 per cage in a 12-h light/dark cycle. The animals were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Guangzhou Medical University (No.2025-12052), followed the guidelines for the treatment of animals of the International Association for the Study of Pain, and conducted in full compliance with the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care.

Intra-articular drug administration

For intra-articular injection of NPY induced OA mice were intra-articular injections of NPY (Tocris Bioscience, 1153) as 5 μM,10 μL every four weeks, then mice were randomly assigned to treatment groups receiving either PBS (NPY group), Y1 receptor antagonist (Tocris Bioscience, BIBO3304, NPY + anti-Y1R group), and Y2 receptor antagonist (Tocris Bioscience, BIIE0246, NPY + anti-Y2R group) (n = 5) as 0.1 μΜ, 5 μL with consecutive intra-articular drug injections weekly; For induction of OA by destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) surgery, the medial meniscotibial ligament was dissected carefully after visualization using micro scissors. Sham surgery was performed with visualization of the ligament only. 1 week after surgery, mice were randomly assigned to treatment groups receiving either PBS (DMM group) or Y1 receptor antagonist (anti-Y1R group) (n = 6). Intra-articular injections were performed in anesthetized mice. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (1.5–2 mL/min) and treated aseptically throughout the experiments. The solution was injected through the patellar ligament by using a 27G needle with the knee flexed at a 90-degrees angle.

Micro-CT analysis

After dissection and fixation, knees were scanned with micro-CT (SkyScan 1172; Bruker) using an x-ray voltage of 100 kV and current of 200 μA and 6 μm voxel size. Analysis of the medial compartment of the subchondral bone about 200 slices were selected as region of interest (ROI) and were performed using with the CT analyzer (Materialise) with 85–255 Hounsfield. The following parameters of subchondral bone were analyzed: bone volume per tissue volume (BV/TV) and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp). We reconstructed the CT osteophytes by reconstruct the entire knee joint CT, and removing the normal patellar, tibia, femoral and menisci completely by CTAn, the remaining bone tissue formed around them is regarded as osteophytes [17].

Gait analysis

Gait was analyzed in detail using the CatWalk system (Noldus CatWalk XT). Mice were acclimated to a glass plate and allowed to walk freely for at least 30 min in a darkened room before testing. Briefly, a light beam from a fluorescent lamp completely reflected internally was aimed through the glass plate. When the paw touches the glass plate, the light beams were reflected downwards. This results in a sharp bright image of the paw print and is recorded with a video camera. Hind paw footprint area and pressure distribution were quantitatively assessed using the CatWalk XT automated gait analysis system [18].

Mechanical threshold testing

To evaluate sensitivity to a non-noxious mechanical stimulus, we used an incremental series of 8 von Frey filaments (Stoelting, Inc, Wood Dale, IL) of logarithmic stiffness (0.08–6g). The 50 % withdrawal threshold was determined using the up–down method. Each filament was applied perpendicular to the central plantar surface of the hind paw skin with sufficient force to cause a slight bending of the filament. A positive response was defined as a rapid withdrawal of the paw within a count of 5 s, as silently counted by the experimenter. Mechanical sensitivity von Frey filaments (Stoelting Co.) were applied to the left hind paw plantar surface until withdrawal or 5 s. The 50 % withdrawal threshold in grams was calculated using the up-down method.

Pathological staining and OA-score

Briefly, samples were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde/PBS for 24 h and decalcified in 10 % EDTA, pH 7.4 for 4 weeks. Samples were embedded in paraffin and 6 μm sagittal sections. The paraffin slides were dewaxed through pure xylene for 10 min three times and dehydrated at different concentrations of ethanol. The slices were then stained by fast green dye solution for 6 s and stained by Safranin O for 90 min for Safranin O and Fast Green; The slices were then stained by hematoxylin solution for 5 min and stained by eosin for 2 min for hematoxylin-eosin staining. The images were finally captured by a light microscope (Olympus, Japan) and scored according to the OARSI guidelines [19]. HC was defined as the non-mineralized cartilage layer above the tidemark, while CC comprised the mineralized tissue between the tidemark and subchondral bone line at standardized weight-bearing zones; Sagittal sections were divided into anterior, middle, and posterior regions. Three measurements per region (3 points/section) using ImageJ were averaged across 3 non-consecutive sections per joint to minimize regional bias.

Western blot

Chondrocytes were lysed in RIPA buffer, protease inhibitor and phosphorylation inhibitor mixture (Beyotome, P1006). Proteins were separated by 10 %–12 % sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacryl amide gel electrophoresis; separated proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore) and were probed with the following primary antibodies: COL II (Abcam, ab34712), MMP13(Thermofisher, PA5-27242), PI3K (Starter, S0B0265), p-PI3K (Absin, abs130868), AKT (Starter, S0B0114), p-AKT (Absin, abs130890), mTOR (2983S, Cell Signaling Technology), p-mTOR (5536S, Cell Signaling Technology), NPY1R (Abcam, ab91262), NPY (Cell Signaling Technology, 11976S), NPY2R(Bioss, bs0937R), GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology, 2118S). Finally, the blots were visualized by an electrochemiluminescence detection system (Millipore) with a horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibody. A representative blot from 3 independent experiments is presented for each protein.

Immunofluorescence

To detect COL II, MMP13, P62, and LCII expression in the cartilage, knee tissues were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for 4 h and decalcified in 10 % EDTA for 24 h and 20-μm-thick sections were obtained. After rehydration, then were permeabilizated by 0.3 % Triton for 20 min and blocked using 5 % goat serum for 5 min at room temperature. Afterward, sections were incubated with primary antibodies NPY, NPY1R, NPY2R, COLII, MMP13, LC3II (Proteintech, 14600-1-AP), P62 (Proteintech, 18420-1-AP), p-PI3K, p-AKT, p-mTOR at a dilution of 1:200, overnight at 4 °C. For secondary reactions, species-matched secondary antibody at a dilution of 1:300 was used for 2 h at 37 °C. Finally, sections were counterstained with DAPI before imaging. The percentage of NPY, NPY1R, NPY2R, MMP13, LC3II, P62, p-PI3K, p-AKT, and p-mTOR positive cells was calculated as the number of positive cells per grid divided by the total number of cells of DAPI per grid quantified by Imaris. For each treatment group, 3 cartilage sections from 3 different sample were analyzed and averaged.

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was measured with a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, DOJINDO, CK04) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, chondrocytes at different concentrations of NPY (0.01–5 μM) were prepared in 96-well plates and cultured for 48 h and 96 h. A 10-μL bolus of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by reaction for 2 h at 37 °C in the incubators. Absorption was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

Chondrocyte were treated with 10 ng/L IL-1β, 5 μM NPY or PBS, and harvested after 48 h. RNA was lysed with Trizol reagent and quantified with ND-2000 (NanoDrop Technologies). A sequencing library was constructed using BGISEQ-500 (igeneCode Biotech, Beijing), and qualified reads were mapped to the mouse reference genome. Differential expression analysis between groups was performed using the DESeq2 R package. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as having a log2 fold change ≥2 and a P value ≤ 0.05. Heat maps were generated using the heatmap package. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and KEGG pathway analysis were performed using the cluster Profiler R package.

Statistical analysis

All experiments in vitro were repeated at least three times. SPSS 22.0 software (Chicago, IL, USA) was used to perform the statistical analysis. The normality of data was verified with Shapiro–Wilk test. All normally distributed values were expressed as means±SDs. Student's t test was used to compare two groups, while one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to compare three or more groups followed by a Bonferroni test. A P value < 0.05 was considered as statistically significance.

Elevated Expression of NPY and Y1R in OA patients

Safranin O–fast green and hematoxylin–eosin staining were performed on normal and OA cartilage areas from OA patients after total knee replacement. The cartilage in the normal area appeared intact without exfoliation, whereas OA cartilage exhibited extensive exfoliation, corresponding to a higher OARSI score (Fig. 1A and B). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed significantly elevated expression levels of both NPY and Y1 receptor in osteoarthritic regions compared to normal articular cartilage. In contrast, Y2 receptor expression showed no significant difference between OA and normal tissue (Fig. 1A and C). In mice with DMM-induced OA, mild osteoarthritic changes were observed 4 weeks post-surgery, including proteoglycan loss and longitudinal fissures in the cartilage layer (Fig. 1D and F). NPY and Y1 receptor expression levels were elevated compared to the sham group, while Y2 receptor expression remained unchanged (Fig. 1E and G). Following 24 and 48 h of IL-1β stimulation (10 ng/mL), primary mouse chondrocytes exhibited significantly upregulated Y1 receptor expression, while Y2 receptor levels remained unchanged (Fig. 1H and I). Further exploring the effect of NPY on autophagy of chondrocytes and its relationship with the PI3K/AKT and mTOR signaling pathway, we confirmed that NPY inhibited the autophagy in chondrocytes, including the decrease in the expression of autophagy-related proteins LCII/I and the increase in the expression of P62(Fig. 5A and B). Also, those treatments, such as 1 μM or 0.1 μM concentration of NPY, promoted the expression of p-PI3K/PI3K, p-AKT/AKT, and p-mTOR/mTOR via Western blot (Fig. 5C and I). Pretreatment with a Y1 receptor antagonist effectively relieved the NPY-induced autophagy impairment, including up-regulation of LCII/I proteins and down-regulation of P62, however, pretreatment with a Y2 receptor antagonist had no significant effect on the impairment of autophagy (Fig. 5E and F). Pretreatment with Y1 receptor antagonist effectively suppressed the NPY-induced upregulation of p-PI3K/PI3K, p-AKT/AKT, and p-mTOR/mTOR. In contrast, pretreatment with a Y2 receptor antagonist did not affect these signaling pathways (Fig. 5D and J). MDC staining indicated that NPY inhibited chondrocyte autophagy, as evidenced by a reduced number of autophagosomes (Fig. 5G and K). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that NPY treatment reduced COL II expression and decreased the levels of the autophagy marker LC3II. Additionally, Y1 receptor antagonist pretreatment effectively reversed the NPY-induced suppression of chondrocyte autophagy, whereas the Y2 receptor antagonist had no significant effect (Fig. 5H–L). Discussion

Building upon limited existing research regarding NPY and its Y1 receptor in OA, this study demonstrates that NPY exacerbates OA pathogenesis by inhibiting chondrocyte autophagy. Furthermore, we confirm that NPY Y1 receptor antagonist attenuates OA pain and progression by enhancing chondrocyte autophagy through the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, revealing its therapeutic potential as a novel OA treatment target.

Knee pain is a primary symptom of OA and may be associated with nerve infiltration into the synovial lining and subchondral bone of the knee joint [20,21]. Elevated NPY levels serve as an independent marker of disease activity in rheumatoid arthritis and correlate with accelerated disease progression [22,23]. NPY can bind to free nerve endings in articular cartilage, synovium, and subchondral bone [24,25]. Anatomical studies have shown that NPY-positive sympathetic nerve fibers are present in cartilage, and chondrocytes themselves can also secrete NPY [26]. Subchondral bone remodeling can significantly increase nerve invasion and contribute to knee pain. Osteoclasts secrete nerve growth factor 1, which promotes axonal growth of sensory nerves in subchondral bone [27]. Dorsal root ganglion hyperexcitability and pain hypersensitivity are closely correlated with the degree of subchondral bone remodeling during OA progression [27,28]. Neuropeptide deficiency leads to stronger subchondral bone sclerosis [29]. In our study, the expression levels of NPY and its Y1 receptor were significantly elevated in OA chondrocytes. Treatment with a Y1 receptor antagonist effectively reduced OA-related pain and subchondral bone degeneration. NPY may stimulate osteoblastic differentiation via activating canonical Wnt signaling and enhancing the angiogenic capacity of bone marrow stromal cells (BMSCs) [30]. These osteogenic and angiogenic activities contribute to bone repair by human pluripotent stem cell-derived osteogenic cells, leading to subchondral bone stiffening and vascular invasion into cartilage channels. The newly formed blood vessels can penetrate calcified cartilage through fibrovascular channels. As OA progresses, these vascular channels may also extend into non-calcified cartilage. Vascular ingrowth is often accompanied by nerve innervation, which contributes to the onset of OA-related pain [31]. Y1 receptor antagonism has been shown to suppress osteogenic differentiation and angiogenesis [30]. Inhibiting subchondral bone remodeling and sclerosis may help reduce pain and slow OA progression. Y1R antagonists have also demonstrated anti-osteoporotic effects and the ability to modulate gut microbiota in ovariectomized rats [32].

NPY plays a critical role in immunomodulation, as evidenced by attenuated inflammation observed in Y1 receptor knockout mice [33]. In healthy human joints, the synovium is richly innervated by both sympathetic and sensory nerve fibers [34]. In rheumatoid arthritis, studies in both human and animal models have revealed a depletion of neuron-derived neurotransmitters in synovial tissue, attributed to the loss of sympathetic innervation [35]. Moreover, the extent of this neurotransmitter depletion correlates with the severity of inflammation. Comparative studies have reported a reduction in sympathetic innervation in the synovial tissue of patients with rheumatoid arthritis, whereas this phenomenon is not observed in OA. The proinflammatory neuropeptide CGRP has also been implicated in joint inflammation. As previously mentioned, neuropeptides such as substance P (SP), vasoactive intestinal peptide, and NPY play key roles not only in nociception, but also in bone remodeling, inflammatory regulation, and angiogenesis [36,37] Co-transmitters such as neuropeptide Y, adenosine triphosphate, and its metabolite adenosine also act as mediators of the sympathetic nervous system. Local pro-inflammatory responses triggered by injury or pathogens activate the sympathetic nervous system, which subsequently induces various immunoregulatory mechanisms. These mechanisms may exert either pro- or anti-inflammatory effects depending on neurotransmitter concentrations and the specific pathological context [37].

NPY can directly induce chondrocyte hypertrophy by upregulating the expression of Col10a1, MMP-13, and ADAMTS-5 [7]. Chen et al. reported that NPY significantly increased Col2a1 expression in the murine chondrogenic cell line ATDC5, and that inhibition of Y1R partially attenuated NPY-induced chondrocyte hypertrophy and mineralization [8]. Administration of recombinant NPY to rat chondrocytes and metatarsal bones inhibited growth plate chondrogenesis and impaired longitudinal bone growth [38]. In the present study, high concentrations of NPY significantly promoted chondrocyte degradation, while Y1 receptor inhibition effectively mitigated both chondrocyte degeneration and OA progression. Similarly, treatment with other neuropeptides, such as alpha-calcitonin gene-related peptide and substance P, has also been shown to reduce the expression of chondrogenic markers in OA chondrocytes [39].

However, the specific NPY receptor responsible for OA development remains controversial. Both Y1 and Y2 receptors have been reported to play roles in bone homeostasis. Wu et al. found that intra-articular injection of NPY induced OA characterized by cartilage degradation and fibrillation. Co-treatment with a Y2 receptor antagonist—but not a Y1 receptor antagonist—significantly alleviated these pathological changes, suggesting that Y2R may play a critical role in NPY-induced chondrocyte hypertrophy and cartilage matrix degradation [7]. In contrast, NPY has also been shown to promote the proliferation of both chondrocytes and articular cartilage, potentially via autocrine mechanisms mediated by Y1R [26]. Y2R is predominantly expressed in presynaptic membranes and the hypothalamus, while Y1R is widely distributed in osseous tissues, including chondrocytes, osteoblasts, and bone marrow cells [20]. In this study, we observed a significant upregulation of Y1R expression in OA chondrocytes, with no detectable change in Y2R expression levels. These findings support a critical role for Y1R in mediating the deleterious effects of NPY on chondrocytes and promoting OA progression. Conditional deletion of Y1R specifically in chondrocytes may be required to further validate this conclusion.

Autophagy, a cellular self-protection mechanism that removes damaged proteins and organelles, plays a critical role in maintaining chondrocyte function [40], It can delay chondrocyte senescence and hypertrophy, thereby mitigating chondrocyte degeneration and OA progression [14,41] NPY, through Y1R, has been shown to activate the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, thereby suppressing autophagy in chondrocytes. This is evidenced by reduced expression of autophagy markers such as LC3-II and elevated levels of p62. Inhibition of Y1R can counteract these effects by downregulating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, thereby restoring autophagic flux and reducing collagen II degradation, ultimately contributing to cartilage protection. However, previous studies have reported that inhibition of NPY2R, but not NPY1R, significantly ameliorated NPY-induced effects [7]. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting Y1R to regulate the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and restore chondrocyte autophagy, thereby alleviating OA progression. Our current findings that Y1R inhibition downregulates the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and enhances autophagy are consistent with previous reports. For instance, one study demonstrated that LY294002, a PI3K inhibitor, enhances autophagy and protects chondrocytes from apoptosis by inhibiting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway in an OA model [42]. Similarly, mTOR inhibition with rapamycin has been shown to promote autophagy and reduce cartilage degradation in a rat OA model [43]. However, the precise molecular mechanisms through which Y1R regulates autophagy, and its potential interactions with other signaling pathways, remain to be fully elucidated.

This study has the following limitations. Firstly, insufficient validation of receptor-specific mechanisms, while the study highlights the critical role of Y1R (over Y2R) in OA progression, it lacks direct mechanistic validation of Y1R specificity. More persuasive research, as conditional Y1R knockout chondrocytes or Y1R overexpression models is needed. The potential involvement of other NPY receptors (e.g., Y2R or Y5R) cannot be ruled out, which may weaken the conclusion that Y1R is the sole mediator. Second, the use of primary chondrocytes from newborn mice lacks of scientifically rigor instead of OA mice or clinical OA samples. Last, the study focuses narrowly on PI3K/AKT/mTOR while ignoring potential interactions with other OA-relevant pathways (e.g., MAPK, NF-κB, or Wnt signaling). This limited scope may miss important compensatory or synergistic mechanisms in OA pathogenesis.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that elevated levels of NPY in OA may contribute to disease progression by inhibiting chondrocyte autophagy. Inhibition of the NPY Y1 receptor effectively alleviated cartilage degeneration by enhancing autophagy in OA chondrocytes, suggesting that Y1 receptor antagonists may serve as a promising therapeutic strategy for OA.
