Development and validation of the Scholarship of Teaching and Learning Assessment Questionnaire (SoTLAQ) among health professions educators
Authors: Fatemeh Mohseni, Reyhane Golbaf, Reihaneh Hasani, Shaqayeq Mehrpour, Maryam Nasimi, Mahboobeh Khabaz Mafinejad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith the increasing recognition of the role of the scholarship of teaching and learning in enhancing educational quality and fostering the professional growth of health professions educators, the availability of a rigorously validated instrument to measure their awareness, attitudes, and experiences has become essential. This study aimed to design and validate a questionnaire to assess the awareness, attitudes, and experiences of faculty members involved in health professions education regarding their involvement in scholarship of teaching and learning activities.MethodsThis exploratory mixed-methods research was conducted in accordance with the AMEE Guide No. 87 framework, a structured approach for instrument development and validation, to develop and validate the Scholarship of Teaching Assessment Questionnaire (SOTLAQ). Participants were recruited from faculty members at Tehran University of Medical Sciences (TUMS), Iran, using purposive sampling for the qualitative phase and convenience sampling for the quantitative phase. The initial item pool was generated from a literature review, semi-structured interviews, and a focus group discussion. The extracted codes were assessed through expert panel deliberations involving six experts in the scholarship of teaching. Eight experts assessed content validity, while face validity was evaluated through cognitive interviews with five faculty members to ensure item clarity and transparency. Construct validity was examined using exploratory factor analysis, and reliability was determined by calculating Cronbach’s alpha based on responses from 165 faculty members.ResultsFrom the semi-structured interviews (176 codes), the literature review (37 codes), and the focus group (21 codes), a total of 30 merged codes were initially identified. Following the expert panel review, a 24-item questionnaire was finalized. The content validity index (CVI) and content validity ratio (CVR) exceeded 0.75. Exploratory factor analysis identified five factors, including “perceived awareness,” “perceived skills,” “perceived motivation and support,” “perceived enablers and drivers,” and “perceived barriers and challenges,” which explained 63.3% of the variance. The overall Cronbach’s alpha was 0.906.ConclusionThe SoTLAQ questionnaire is a valid and reliable tool for assessing key dimensions that affect faculty engagement in the scholarship of teaching activities. This questionnaire may be useful in planning educational interventions, empowerment programs, and policymaking in the field of medical education. However, further validation in diverse institutional settings and different countries is recommended before its widespread use.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09514-y.Keywords: Scholarship, Teaching, Teacher, Medical education, Questionnaire

Introduction

Improving the quality of education is one of the main goals of universities [1, 2]. To achieve this goal, scholarship of teaching and learning (SoTL), as an evidence-based approach to improving teaching methods, curriculum design, and student learning, is one of the key solutions [3, 4]. Scholarship of teaching, which was introduced by Boyer, was later developed and commonly referred to in the literature as the ‘Scholarship of Teaching and Learning (SoTL),’ highlighting an explicit focus on students’ learning as well as teaching process [4, 5]. Unlike educational research, which is primarily aimed at producing generalizable knowledge, Scholarship of Teaching and Learning (SoTL) is a systematic, evidence-based approach in which faculty members critically examine teaching practices and students’ learning processes in real-world settings, using scientific and valid methods. The main goal of SoTL is to improve the quality of teaching and learning process by generating reliable evidence and sharing findings with the academic community [6]. Scholarship of teaching and learning not only helps to understand learning processes better, but also provides a basis for educational innovations and responsiveness to learners’ needs [1, 7]. Faculty members play a pivotal role in achieving this goal [8]. Their direct interaction with students and their awareness of the strengths and weaknesses in the educational environment provide a valuable opportunity to conduct SoTL that continuously improves the quality of teaching [6]. Faculty members’ participation in the SoTL activities leads to the development of educational programs and positive changes at the university [1].

Despite the importance of SoTL, faculty members’ awareness, attitudes, and experience are associated with challenges [9]. Understanding these challenges and faculty members’ needs is crucial for designing and implementing effective educational interventions that promote SoTL among faculty members [6]. Ghasemi et al.’s study emphasizes the importance of faculty professional development, emphasizing their educational roles in the university [2]. Also, the results of Keshmiri’s study show that SoTL empowerment programs, if planned based on needs and addressing existing challenges, can serve as a foundation for improving faculty members’ capabilities in line with educational changes [1]. These findings suggest that a clear understanding of the current situation, in terms of faculty members’ awareness, attitudes, and experience in the field of SoTL, is an essential prerequisite for designing and implementing such programs.

Furthermore, Simpson et al. emphasize the importance and status of Scholarship of Teaching and Learning (SoTL) activities for the advancement of faculty members, stating that establishing clear standards for the design, implementation, and documentation of the SoTL can help enhance the status of SoTL in universities [10]. In this regard, it is also crucial to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and experience of faculty members regarding these standards and how to design, implement, and document SoTL. The study by Zibrowski et al. shows that faculty members identify factors such as lack of time, prioritization, and motivation as substantial barriers to activity in this field [11]. Understanding these barriers from the faculty members’ perspective can help design effective interventions to address them in the academic environment.

Considering the emerging challenges in SoTL—including issues related to data collection and analysis— there is a need identified in the literature for valid and reliable instruments to assess relevant constructs (e.g., educators’ awareness, attitudes, and experiences) [3]. To the best of our knowledge, no dedicated tool has been developed to assess health professions educators’ awareness, attitudes, and experiences regarding SoTL. In this study, awareness refers to educators’ self-reported familiarity with and perceived understanding of SoTL (i.e., awareness of concept and key features such as its purpose, process, and evaluation criteria), not just evaluating knowledge factual knowledge [12]. Attitudes refer to educators’ perspectives regarding favorable or unfavorable evaluation of engaging in SoTL [13]. Experiences refer to educators’ prior exposure to and involvement in SoTL-related activities, which may shape their subsequent engagement through influence on perceived control and readiness to act [13]. Understanding these dimensions among faculty members is an important prerequisite for designing and implementing effective educational interventions and fostering a culture of SoTL in universities. Accordingly, and based on this identified gap, the present study was conducted to address the need for a comprehensive instrument by designing and validating a questionnaire for assessing educators’ awareness, attitudes, and experiences regarding SoTL. The questionnaire can be used to identify faculty development needs and to inform the design and evaluation of interventions aimed at strengthening SoTL engagement in academic institutions (e.g., pre-/post-intervention assessment) [14]. This use aligns with the ISSOTL Grand Challenge #5, which emphasizes understanding and strengthening the practice, use, and growth of SoTL [15].

Methods

Design

The Assessment of Scholarship of teaching and learning Questionnaire (SoTLAQ) was developed following the seven-step process recommended by Artino et al. in AMEE Guide No. 87 (Fig. 1) [16]. This exploratory mixed-methods study aimed to design and evaluate the validity and reliability of a tool measuring the awareness, attitudes, and experiences of health professions educators regarding the Scholarship of Teaching and Learning.

The steps of instrument development and validation

Questionnaire development

Step 1: literature review

A systematic search was conducted across ProQuest, ERIC, Scopus, Web of Science (WOS), and PubMed databases to identify English-language literature on survey faculty members’ awareness, attitudes, and experiences in the SoTL, up to January 20, 2023. The search utilized keywords including “scholarship of teaching and learning” OR “educational scholarship” OR “scholarship of teaching” AND “questionnaire” OR “survey” OR “tool” OR “scale” AND “teacher” OR “academic staff” OR “faculty member” OR “educator.” The first researcher (FM) analyzed the full texts using a conventional content analysis approach [17]. Initial codes (i.e., labeled meaning units) were inductively extracted from the included full texts. Subsequently, the extracted codes were reviewed and refined through two sessions involving two other researchers (MKM and MN). Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Step 2: qualitative study

In this phase, data were initially collected through in-depth, semi-structured interviews. A criterion-based purposive sampling strategy was used [18]. Eligible participants were: (I) being a faculty member at Tehran University of Medical Sciences (TUMS), (II) having experience in conducting at least one SoTL activity. TUMS was selected as the study setting because it is a major medical university with established educational development structures and a pool of educators with SoTL-related experience, enabling identification of information-rich participants. Also, to ensure that participants had sufficient experience in conducting SoTL activities, SoTL office database of Education Development Center (EDC) was used to identify individuals with key experience matching the inclusion criteria. Willingness to participate was assessed and informed consent was obtained.

An initial semi-structured interview guide was drafted based on the literature review and the study objectives (awareness, attitudes, and experiences regarding SoTL). The draft was reviewed, refined, and finalized in an expert panel consisting of six medical educationists who had scholarly expertise in this field (Appendix 1). A total of seven health professions educators participated in the study. During each interview, the researcher asked participants to explain their experiences related to SoTL activities. Interviews continued until data saturation was achieved; no new information was provided in the last two interviews. Immediately after each interview, transcripts were prepared and then coded using directed content analysis in three categories: factors influencing awareness, attitudes, and experiences during the SoTL activities.

Furthermore, to complement data collection, a focus group session was held with five experts in the field of the Scholarship of Teaching and Learning. Participants were selected through criterion-based purposive sampling [18] from the Educational Development Center (EDC) database of instructors and participants involved in SoTL-related programs; eligible experts were invited through targeted invitations, participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained. The focus group discussion was conducted in Persian (Farsi), the native language of the participants, to facilitate clear and rich expression of their experiences. During the session, the moderator asked semi-structured, open-ended questions that encouraged participants to express their perceptions to share their perspectives regarding SoTL. Although a formal focus group interview guide was not used, the discussion was guided by predefined key topics related to the study objectives. At the beginning of the session, the moderator explained the session objectives and rules (e.g., confidentiality, respectful discussion, active listening, and turn-taking). With participants’ consent, the session was recorded, and the transcript was prepared. Two researchers (MN and FM) subsequently classified the codes using a directed content analysis approach.

Step 3: data integration

The codes extracted from the interviews and focus group in the qualitative study (Step 2) were compared and integrated with the codes identified in the literature review (Step 1). Subsequently, codes with similar meanings were merged, and duplicate codes were removed.

Step 4: item development

Six experts in the field of SoTL were recruited as members of the expert panel to develop items for the questionnaire. A two-hour panel session was held, during which the moderator presented the codes extracted from Step 3, and feedback was gathered to develop the most appropriate items.

Step 5: content validity

To assess content validity, eight experts who had expertise in the field of SoTL from different universities in Iran were selected and provided with explanations about the research objectives and data collection process. The SoTLAQ, along with a content validity assessment form, was sent to the experts via email. The Content Validity Index (CVI) was calculated using the Waltz and Bausell formula [19]. Experts rated the relevance and clarity of each item on a four-point Likert scale: “Highly relevant” (4), “Relevant” (3), “Somewhat relevant” (2), and “Not relevant” (1). Items with a CVI value above 0.79 were considered to have confirmed content validity [19]. Additionally, the Content Validity Ratio (CVR) was calculated using Lawshe’s formula [13]. Experts were asked to classify each question into one of three categories: “Essential,” “Useful but not essential,” and “Not essential.” Items with a CVR value below 0.75 were removed from the questionnaire [20].

Step 6: cognitive interviews

To collect evidence supporting the construct validity of the SoTLAQ, cognitive interviews were conducted with five faculty members from TUMS selected through convenience sampling. For qualitative data collection, the thinking-aloud and concurrent verbal probing techniques were employed. In each interview, participants were asked to read each item aloud and express their understanding of its meaning. Their comprehension of each item was then assessed to evaluate clarity and semantic relevance. Items exhibiting ambiguity, bias, or linguistic issues were revised. Based on received feedback, items assessing similar conceptual areas were merged, and unclear or inappropriate items were reviewed and rewritten. The cognitive interview process continued until the fifth participant provided no more feedback. This iterative process improved the linguistic clarity and conceptual accuracy of the questionnaire, ultimately enhancing the construct validity of the tool.

Step 7: pilot testing

To assess construct validity and reliability, the SoTLAQ was designed as an online tool using the e-Poll platform, and the link was distributed among faculty members at TUMS. Since this tool was developed for the first time in the field of Scholarship of teaching and learning, and new components were identified, exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted to determine the questionnaire’s factor structure [21]. The sample size was considered adequate based on commonly recommended subject-to-item ratios for exploratory factor analysis (typically 5–10 participants per item). To accurately determine factor loadings, the questionnaire was randomly completed by 165 faculty members. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25 [22]. Principal component analysis with Varimax rotation was used for EFA. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) index was calculated to assess sampling adequacy, with values above 0.7 indicating suitability for factor analysis. Furthermore, Bartlett’s test of sphericity was performed, with a p-value of less than 0.05 considered as the acceptance criterion. In the final version, items with factor loadings greater than 0.40 were retained. After identifying factors through EFA, Cronbach’s alpha was calculated to evaluate reliability and internal consistency. Values above 0.7 indicated acceptable reliability [23]. The final version of the SoTLAQ is provided in Appendix 2.

Scoring of the final questionnaire

Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (Strongly disagree) to 5 (Strongly agree). Higher scores indicate higher levels of the construct assessed by the questionnaire. Items 22–24 are negatively worded and therefore reverse-scored (1 = 5, 2 = 4, 3 = 3, 4 = 2, 5 = 1).

Results

The initial codes extracted from semi-structured interviews (176 codes), literature review (37 codes), and focus group (21 codes) were reduced to 30 final codes for the development of the preliminary questionnaire items after merging similar codes. Following review by the expert panel, these codes formed the basis for drafting 24 questionnaire items.

During the content validity assessment, the CVI index indicated that all 24 items of the SoTLAQ scored above 0.75 in terms of relevance and clarity. Similarly, for the CVR index, all items scored higher than 0.75. Consequently, all 24 items were retained at this stage. The CVI and CVR indices results are presented in Table 1. Discussion

This study aimed to design and psychometrically evaluate the Assessment of Scholarship of Teaching and Learning Questionnaire (SoTLAQ), a tool to assess the awareness, attitudes, and experiences of health professions educators regarding the SoTL. This instrument was developed in accordance with the seven recommended steps outlined in AMEE Guide No. 87 [16]. The final questionnaire consists of 24 items on a 5-point Likert scale. The resulting questionnaire demonstrated acceptable content and construct validity, as well as reliability, making it a potentially helpful tool for assessing educators’ awareness, attitudes, and experiences in the field of the Scholarship of Teaching and Learning based on self-reported ratings.

Exploratory factor analysis revealed that the instrument comprises five main factors: Perceived Awareness (5 items), Perceived Skills (3 items), Perceived Motivation and Support (8 items), Perceived Empowerment and Drivers (4 items), and Perceived Barriers and Challenges (5 items). These five factors effectively represent the three dimensions of awareness, attitude, and experience among educators in the Scholarship of Teaching and Learning. The factor structure obtained through exploratory factor analysis reflects the multifaceted and complex nature of faculty participation in SoTL activities. Internal consistency of the questionnaire was acceptable (α = 0.906).

Building on the literature that conceptualizes SoTL as a systematic, evidence-informed inquiry into teaching and student learning with the aim of improving practice and sharing outcomes beyond the local context, our findings can be interpreted within this framework. Consistent with prior SoTL literature, our first three domains align with core elements commonly described for engaging in SoTL—understanding the concept and its boundaries (Perceived Awareness), having capability for systematic inquiry (Perceived Skills), and sustaining engagement through motivational/supportive conditions (Perceived Motivation and Support) [4–6]—whereas enablers/drivers and barriers/challenges reflect contextual determinants that may facilitate or hinder participation in SoTL.

One key dimension influencing how faculty members engage in the Scholarship of Teaching and Learning is perceived awareness. This domain highlights that conceptual clarity about what SoTL is—and how it differs from educational research—is a prerequisite for meaningful participation. Kreber’s study showed that a significant barrier to participation in SoTL activities is the lack of distinction between educational research and SoTL, as many faculty members equate educational scholarship with teaching evaluation or classroom analysis, neglecting its research-based, systematic, and documentation aspects [24].

Moreover, limited familiarity with evaluation standards for SoTL (e.g., Glassick’s criteria) may reduce faculty members’ confidence in how SoTL work is judged and documented [25]. Harris’s study also indicated that even in universities where developing SoTL is a strategic goal, poor awareness of evaluation criteria and formal structures for these activities remains a serious obstacle to widespread faculty participation [26].

The perceived skills subscale reflects the practical abilities required to translate interest in SoTL into an operational project. According to Watt et al., faculty members lacking methodological skills in designing educational studies, analyzing educational data, and writing teaching and learning research are less likely to engage in SoTL projects [27]. Brown et al. also emphasize that without skills in documenting and disseminating educational activities, even the most innovative ideas will lack systematic impact [28]. Furthermore, this finding supports the need for structured, skills-based faculty development program and mentorship to build capability for SoTL work. Knorr’s study demonstrated that access to skill development workshops, educational study design tools, and active mentoring are key factors in developing faculty members’ technical capabilities in SoTL [29].

The third factor identified in this study, labeled “Perceived Motivation and Support,” plays a crucial role in explaining faculty members’ participation in SoTL activities. This domain suggests that engagement in SoTL depends not only on individual interest but also on feedback processes and the departmental/organizational climate that supports sustained effort. Self-Determination Theory, proposed by Deci and Ryan, posits that intrinsic motivation flourishes when three key psychological needs—autonomy, competence, and relatedness—are satisfied [30]. Dasgupta et al. demonstrated that faculty development programs based on this theory can enhance faculty member engagement in SoTL projects [31]. Steinert et al. emphasize that continuous feedback, incentive policies, and faculty perceptions of organizational support are among the most critical factors for sustained involvement in educational activities [32]. The study by O’Sullivan and Irby highlights the necessity to redefine professional development frameworks based on intrinsic motivation and contextualization, showing that professional programs should align with the individual needs and identities of faculty members [33].

The “Perceived Enablers and Drivers” subscale captures enabling conditions that make SoTL feel feasible (e.g., clear guidelines, opportunities, and recognition), thereby helping translate awareness and skills into action. Tuan’s study showed that faculty members’ positive perceptions of university educational infrastructures—such as learning opportunities, supportive structures, and adequate resources—are directly associated with research participation [34]. Harris emphasizes the importance of “perceived empowerment” as an essential component in her conceptual model and believes that having skills or resources alone does not lead to success, but rather, one must believe in the possibility of success to lead to action [26]. In his self-efficacy theory, Bandura points out that individuals’ belief in their ability to perform a task plays a key role in initiating and completing that task [35]. Accordingly, empowerment may function as a bridge between capability and actual participation in SoTL.

The factor “Perceived Barriers and Challenges,” pertains to cognitive and structural inhibitors hindering faculty participation in SoTL. This domain indicates that even motivated and skilled educators may not engage in SoTL when contextual constraints (particularly time and insufficient managerial/structural support) are prominent. Watt et al. found that the most reported obstacle by faculty engaged in teaching was lack of time due to clinical duties and teaching responsibilities, alongside lack of motivation stemming from structural disregard of educational activities [27]. Steinert et al. reported that inefficient perceptions of the value of SoTL, the absence of successful role models in universities, and the neglect of educational achievements in promotion criteria are major challenges [32]. Harris, in his model, categorized organizational barriers (lack of support, absence of formal structures), individual barriers (lack of confidence in ability), and systemic barriers (unequal valuation) as inhibiting components for SoTL [26].

The five factors identified in this study are categorized into three main domains: “awareness,” “attitude,” and “experience.” This categorization supports an interpretable, practice-oriented structure for understanding readiness to engage in SoTL. The “perceived awareness” factor reflects awareness of the concepts in the SoTL, related regulatory requirements, and their differences from educational research. The other three factors, “perceived motivation and support,” “perceived empowerment and drivers,” and “perceived barriers and challenges,” represent attitudinal and contextual components, which include faculty members’ perception of the value, feasibility, and the degree of environmental support or inhibition for conducting the SoTL. Finally, the “perceived skills” factor refers to the experiential and practical dimension, encompassing skills such as targeted resource searching, understanding educational literature, and writing SoTL projects.

The SoTLAQ may be used in future research as a practical instrument to assess health professions educators’ self-reported awareness, attitudes, and experiences regarding SoTL and to inform targeted faculty development and institutional support strategies. Although the current study provides initial evidence of content validity, construct validity (exploratory factor analysis), and internal consistency, further psychometric evaluation is recommended in other institutions and larger, diverse samples, including confirmatory factor analysis to verify the factor structure and additional validity evidence and stability over time (test–retest reliability).

Strengths and limitations

One of the strengths of this study was the development and validation of an instrument informed by both qualitative and quantitative data. Item generation also involved direct input from faculty members, which helped ensure content relevance and contextual appropriateness for the target community. In addition, the use of exploratory factor analysis on independent samples supported the instrument’s structural validity. However, this study had limitations. Sampling from a single university may reflect context-specific validation within one academic institution and may limit the generalizability of the findings to other settings. In addition, although SoTL is a cross-disciplinary construct, this instrument was developed and validated specifically among health professions educators; therefore, further validation is recommended before using it in non-health disciplines [16]. Furthermore, institutional educational culture (e.g., faculty development programs and promotion criteria) may affect responses; thus, use in other settings may require additional validation. Moreover, although we used appropriate methods to evaluate validity and reliability, this questionnaire is self-reported. Therefore, responses may be subject to bias (e.g., participants may overestimate or underestimate their awareness/skills, or answer in a socially desirable way, or show acquiescence bias), and the results should be interpreted as perceptions rather than objectively verified knowledge or behavior.

Conclusion

The questionnaire developed in this study is a valid and reliable instrument for measuring the key dimensions of participation of health professional educators in the SoTL. The five-factor structure—including perceived awareness, perceived skills, perceived motivation and support, perceived empowerment and environmental drivers, and perceived barriers and challenges—provides a comprehensive picture of the factors influencing faculty members’ educational scholarship activities. The results indicate that advancing the SoTL requires the simultaneous development of cognitive, technical, and motivational capabilities, as well as the provision of supportive infrastructures and the identification and reduction of structural and mental barriers. This instrument can be widely applied in designing needs assessment planning, empowerment workshops, and educational policy-making in the field of SoTL.
