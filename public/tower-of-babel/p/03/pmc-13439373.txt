Effect of common children’s beverages on surface properties of single-shade and restorative material: an in vitro study
Authors: Manal Almutairi, Ihab Mousa, Raghad Alqahtani, Rahaf Alqahtani, Khozama Alhazzani, Zain Hafiz, Majedah Alhomaidhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Single-shade composite restorations can adapt to the tooth structure, improve esthetics, and reduce reliance on shade selection. The objective of this study was to assess the effect of four different children’s beverages on the color changes, gloss, and surface microhardness of single-shade resin composites compared to universal composite. Forty-eight specimens were prepared from each Shade A2 universal (Filtek Z250) and single-shade resin composite material (Vittra APS Unique). Then, the specimens were divided into four subgroups: Distilled Water (control group), Pepsi Cola Drink, Orange Juice, and Chocolate Milk, all at 24 °C. These specimens were immersed in their respective beverages for 30 min daily. Color changes, gloss, and microhardness values were evaluated at baseline before immersion and at the 1st, 7th, and 30th days of immersion. Data were collected and statistically analyzed using two-way variance analysis (ANOVA) and Tukey’s post-hoc test (p < 0.05). The results showed color change was significantly greater in Vittra APS than in Filtek Z250 across all time intervals, especially in Pepsi Cola after 30th days. For gloss, Vittra APS and Filtek Z250 at all time periods for four storage media showed statistically significant differences. Microhardness of Vittra APS was significantly affected by all time periods and all media except water. Filtek Z250 showed significant differences across all storage media at baseline and after 1st day. Overall, the single-shade composite showed more color change, gloss loss, and hardness reduction—especially in storage media like Pepsi Cola and Orange Juice—compared to the universal composite.

Introduction

Resin-based composites as direct restoration have become a preferred dental restorative option due to their enhanced aesthetics, adhesive properties, and minimal tooth removal requirements1. Esthetic indications of resin composite restorations in the anterior region, such as fracture of the incisal edge and closure of diastemas, are often preferred due to their superior optical properties, translucency effect, and high polishability2. It has been recommended to use layering techniques for color matching; this method works extremely well, but it takes a lot of professional skills and more clinical time3. Clinicians have challenges when dealing with the layering of resin composite restorations in the cosmetic area, since it might be difficult to achieve color matching between the tooth and the dental structure4. Therefore, single-shade composite restoration can adapt to tooth structure and improve esthetics, reducing reliance on shade selection. This effect is referred to as the “Chameleon effect”5. However, multiple studies assessing the discoloration of Single-shade composites have demonstrated that significant discoloration from beverages like coffee and tea, despite their chameleon effect, and any discoloration in the resin composite would affect the esthetic and appearance6.

In 2022, a study was conducted by Ersöz and colleagues in an in vitro experiment. The result showed that the color stability of single-shade resin composites showed less color change was seen in resin composites having silane-coated fillers in their structure7. On the other hand, a recent study in 2023 by Rohym et al. was done to assess and compare two single-shade resin composites’ color stability and found that a new chromatic material technology used in single-shade resin composites lacks esthetic success due to surface roughness and dramatic color change6. In 2023, Elmalawany et al. evaluated the effect of multiple beverages, with and without brushing, on the surface mechanical properties of two resin composites and concluded that beverages and brushing harmed the surface properties of both dental resin composites8. Another study in 2022 by Ebaya et al. investigated and compared the surface roughness and color stability of two different single‑shade composite restorations, methacrylate resin-based composite and ormocer‑based composite. Universal composites demonstrated acceptable surface smoothness and good color matching with various tooth colors; the aging process adversely affected their color stability and surface properties9.

This research was conducted due to a lack of studies investigating the effect of using the most common coloring beverages and the importance of using single-shade restorations in children due to their advantages. Thus, this study aimed to investigate the effect of four common children’s beverages on the color, gloss, and microhardness of single-shade resin composites compared to universal composite. The null hypothesis states that there was no significant difference in the effect of these four common children’s coloring beverages on the color, gloss, and microhardness of single-shade resin composites and universal composite.

The sample size was calculated by using G*Power version 3.1.9.4 (Heinrich Heine University of Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany) with a power of 90%. According to the power analysis, the total sample size was determined to be 48 specimens from each resin composite, 12 units from each subgroup, with a supposed significance level of 0.05 and an effect size of 0.455.

Forty-eight specimens of each single-shade resin composite material (Vittra APS Unique) and shade A2 universal (Filtek Z250) were made by filling a silicon mold set on a glass slide with the uncured material. The specimens were 10 mm in diameter and 2 mm thick, and were covered with a Mylar® strip matrix (Crosstex International, Inc., Hauppauge, NY, USA). The mold was covered with a second strip and a glass slide. As directed by the manufacturers, the specimens were cured on both sides using a photo-polymerization unit (Bluephase, Ivoclar Vivadent, Schaan, Liechtenstein) with an intensity of 900 mW/cm2 radiance as measured with a radiometer (Kerr manufacturing product). The light-curing tip was positioned at a distance of approximately 1 mm from the sample surface for uniform curing. Each side of the mold was light-cured for 40 s to ensure thorough polymerization. In the studies that were performed, only specimens free of macroscopically visible defects were used. Then all specimens were polished using aluminum oxide disks (Sof-Lex, 3 M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA) and an electric handpiece operated at 15,000 rpm for 10 s on each disk, medium, fine, and superfine, respectively. Uniform, dry, and intermittent pressure was applied. Each specimen received polishing with a new disc to avoid contamination or wear inconsistencies. All of the specimens were kept in an airtight container filled with distilled water at 37 °C for 24 h in order to finish the polymerization process10. One operator handled every polishing operation. The specimens were stored in distilled water for 24 h before being wiped dry with tissue paper. For each group, baseline values have been measured for each specimen’s color, gloss, and surface microhardness.

Each specimen’s color was measured using a LabScan XE spectrophotometer (HunterLab, Reston, VA, USA) to determine the baseline color values. The spectrophotometer was calibrated with its own calibration device before obtaining the color measurements of the specimens, which were taken at the center of each specimen. A clinical spectrophotometer was expressed using International Commission on Illumination System D65 (CIE L*a*b*), where the L* parameter specifies the degree of lightness, a* indicates redness or greenness, and b* indicates yellowness or blueness. Three areas—one in the middle and one on each side—were measured for every specimen using a standard white background. The color changes (ΔE*) were calculated11,12 as follows:

For the gloss, all specimens were measured using a gloss meter (Novo Curve; Rhopoint, Bexhill-on-Sea, UK). This device employs a measurement technique that evaluates specular gloss at a 60° incident angle over a 2 mm × 2 mm area. It utilizes a source‐filter photocell combination that is spectrally adjusted to acquire CIE luminous efficiency based on CIE source C, with an exposure time of 2 s, repeated two times. The average reading was noted in gloss units. Before the measurements, the device was calibrated with reference to the gloss units (93.3 GU) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The position of each specimen was maintained using a black opaque plastic mold placed over the specimens during the measurements to eliminate the influence of the ambient light and maintain the position of the specimen during repeated measurements.

Then, the specimens were subjected to initial surface microhardness measurements using a hardness tester (Innovatest Europe BV, Maastricht, Netherlands). The microhardness parameters: Vickers diamond hardness was measured from the upper surface of specimens using a 300 g load applied for 15 s14. The indentations were measured by a built-in graduated microscope with an × 40 objective lens. To ensure measurement accuracy and account for potential surface variations, three indentations per specimen were performed at different locations, following standard Vickers microhardness testing protocols. The microhardness value for each specimen was calculated as the mean of these three measurements. This approach enhances the reliability of the results by minimizing localized inconsistencies and ensuring a representative assessment of each composite material’s surface hardness.

After that, the 48 Specimens of each type of resin composite were randomly allocated into four subgroups (n = 12), according to storage media to be investigated: 50 ml of distilled Water (control group), 50 ml of Pepsi Cola Drink (AlJomaih Beverages Co. Ltd., Dammam, KSA), 50 ml of commercial Orange juice (Al Rabie Saudi Foods Co., KSA), and 50 ml of Chocolate milk (Almarai Co., Riyadh, KSA), all at 24 °C.

The specimens were immersed in their corresponding storage media, daily renewed for 30 min, then rinsed with distilled water and kept in closed containers in distilled water at an incubator at 37 °C. Each sample group was dried with a paper towel to proceed with the measurement. All the statistical analyses were done using IBM SPSS Statistical software for Windows version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics (mean and standard deviation) were used to describe the color change, gloss, and microhardness values. An independent t-test and one-way ANOVA followed the two-way analysis of variance. A p-value of ≤ 0.05 was used to report the statistical significance of the results.

Color measurement results

Table 2 shows there was a statistically significant difference between the color change values of the resin composite at the end of the 1st, 7th, and 30th days (p < 0.05). Among the resin composite Vittra APS, the single-shade composite group showed statistically significantly more color changes after 30 days. It was shown that Pepsi Cola, after 30th days, had the highest significant difference by a two-way ANOVA test, as shown in comparison with other media. Finally, we ran a repeated measurement test for single-shade resin composite with repeated measurement test showed statistically significantly more color changes in all time periods(p < 0.05).

Universal resin composite (Filtek Z250) of the multi-shade composite resins showed less color change than the single-shade composite group (Vittra APS) in all time periods(p < 0.05). With repeated measurement for the Universal resin composite (Filtek Z250) group to compare the four storage media, distilled water, Orange juice, Pepsi Cola, and Chocolate milk, over the three selected times, we found by repeated measurement RM-test that the means and SD of all storage media, except water, were statistically significantly increased (p < 0.05). For the multiple comparison test used, we found that the Orange juice media had the highest significant difference compared to other media over the three times.

Gloss measurements results

Results of gloss measurements are presented in Table 3. Two-way ANOVA test for the single-shade composite group (Vittra APS), at all time periods for four storage media, showed a statistically significant difference, p < 0.05. The multiple comparison test showed that Chocolate milk was significantly lower than water at baseline and after 1st day. However, at 7th days, Chocolate milk was significantly lower than Pepsi Cola, while at 30th days, Chocolate milk was significantly lower than Orange juice. We ran repeated measurement tests over time, which showed water, Orange juice, and Pepsi cola had a significant change over time with p < 0.05, while Chocolate did not show a significant change.

For the Universal resin composite (Filtek Z250) group, at all time periods except after 1st day in four storage media, results showed a statistically significant difference. Based on the multiple comparison test, at baseline, Chocolate milk was significantly higher than distilled water. At 7th days and 30th days, water was significantly lower than Pepsi Cola and Chocolate milk. We ran a repeated measurement test in all time periods, which revealed that distilled water, Orange juice, Pepsi Cola, and Chocolate milk showed a significant change with p < 0.05.

Discussion

The esthetic and functional longevity of single-shade restorative materials is a crucial concern in pediatric dentistry, as these materials are often exposed to colored, acidic, and sugary beverages that are commonly consumed by children15. Several studies have explored the effects of such beverages on the surface properties of these materials, particularly focusing on color stability, surface roughness, and microhardness16. Multiple studies found that the optical properties of direct restorative materials used in paediatric patients were notably affected by prolonged exposure to drinks commonly consumed by children17. In 2023, Prasada highlighted that single-shade resin composites have the potential to adapt to color changes, which can be beneficial for patients with a high propensity for consuming colored beverages15. However, despite these advancements, the ability of single-shade composites to maintain their color stability over time remains a challenge, especially when exposed to commonly consumed beverages such as cola and fruit juices, such as orange juice18. Therefore, we aimed to assess the effect of four different children’s beverages as storage media on the color stability, gloss, and surface microhardness of single-shade composite (Vittra APS) and compare it with the universal resin composite (Filtek Z250) group.The results of this study demonstrate significant differences in the color change, gloss, and microhardness stability of single-shade composite (Vittra APS) and universal resin composite (Filtek Z250) over time when exposed to various storage media. Therefore, we reject the null hypothesis of this study.

A composite restoration’s aesthetics are significantly influenced by color. The resin matrix’s composition, filler particle size, degree of polymerization, and coloring additive presence all affect the restoration’s color stability13,19. The primary cause of color change in resinous materials is the adsorption or absorption of coloring agents, such as those present in the daily diet.

In the present study, when the color change for resin composite specimens was analyzed, the single-shade resin composite (Vittra APS) exhibited greater color changes compared to the universal resin composite (Filtek Z250), particularly in media such as Pepsi Cola and Orange juice. Vittra APS showed significant discoloration after 30 days, especially when stored in Pepsi Cola, which produced the highest color change among the other tested storage media. Aligning with findings of Elwardani and coworkers, who found that exposure to Cola and Orange juice resulted in the greatest changes in both surface roughness and color stability, emphasizing the role of beverages in material16. According to Thakkaret al., the type of beverage, acidic or pigmented, also plays a significant role in discoloration20. Also, with sugar and sugar-free, for example, a study by Ponnala et al. shows that sugar-containing milks and juices caused more significant color changes than sugar-free options, particularly in materials, which exhibited a higher susceptibility to staining21. Fazlioglu et al. in 2022 demonstrated that significant color change in single-shade composites, with supra-nanohybrid resins showing a greater tendency for discoloration compared to other composites22. This agrees with another study findings, which showed that single-shade composites exhibit greater surface roughness and discoloration than multi-shade composites, thus highlighting the trade-off between clinical convenience and material durability23. These findings have clinical significance: children who regularly drink these kinds of beverages may eventually experience more esthetic issues with their dental restorations over time. These findings suggest that single-shade composites are more prone to color degradation, likely due to the nature of their resin and filler composition, which may be more susceptible to staining agents5.

The gloss parameter, which is associated with the color advantage, has a significant impact on the survival rates of aesthetic restorations since it needs to resemble the teeth’s natural appearance. In this regard, the loss of gloss has a detrimental effect on the resin composites’ appearance, creating disharmony between the restored and adjacent teeth24. In this study, both resin composites demonstrated significant changes in gloss across the different media, with Chocolate milk consistently resulting in lower gloss values, particularly for single-shade composite resin (Vittra APS) and Universal resin composite (Filtek Z250); however, they exhibited relatively better gloss retention, particularly in water. This suggests that while both materials are affected by environmental factors, the universal resin composite (Filtek Z250) maintains its esthetic properties to a greater extent than the single-shade composite (Vittra APS). According to Ozera et al., the gloss of the resin composites was significantly impacted by the type of drink. They discovered that Coca-Cola makes the three resin composites—Z350XT, IPS Empress Direct, and Charisma Diamond—less glossy. Compared to other resin composites, Z350 was more affected by cranberry juice25. Another study measured the effect of artificial aging of both the same resin composite restorations, including the single-shade resin composite and multi-shade resin composite. El-Rashidy et al. found discoloration changes and gloss reduction after immersion26. The gloss instability of composites after immersion in acidic solutions could be explained by phosphoric acid’s weakening of the organic matrix, which impacted light incidence and reflection and promoted a decrease in brightness27.

Similarly, the microhardness of the surface of composite restorations can be influenced by the type of beverages used for immersion and the duration of immersion28. However, the effect of the interaction between resin composite and various beverages is influenced by a complex interplay of multiple variables29. Also, the microhardness of these materials diminishes upon immersion of these drinks, as the acidic components penetrate the resin matrix, leading to softening and weakening of the material’s structure; therefore, a reduction in microhardness compromises the material’s resistance to wear and its overall durability30. Our study showed that microhardness values for both materials decreased over time, with chocolate milk and Pepsi Cola media showing the most significant reductions. The single-shade composite (Vittra APS) showed a noticeable decline in hardness after storage in these media. In contrast, universal resin composite (Filtek Z250) demonstrated relatively stable microhardness, particularly in distilled water, indicating that its composition may offer better durability under certain conditions. These findings are supported by a similar study with Bengal and colleagues in 2024, which also observed that beverages significantly affect resin composites, increasing surface roughness and reducing microhardness31. This advises that frequent consumption of beverages may compromise the long-term performance of dental restorations.

In this study, each step was made to standardize the methodology: Miller strip, the standardized finishing and polishing techniques, the specimens’ thickness of 2 mm for the light cure unit, and the curing duration was set to 40 s with 20 s exposure time for the top and 20 s for the bottom, respectively32. The methods used in this study were those of earlier investigations. Before being immersed in the tested solutions to finish polymerization, the specimens were submerged in distilled water for 24 h33. And a single operator carried out every attempt to guarantee that the methodology and all of the steps were standardized34. This study has its own limits, even though it has standardized the methodology that helps with the evaluation of materials utilization. This study’s limitations include it’s in vitro design, which may not fully replicate real oral conditions. The use of only four storage media limits the applicability of the findings to other staining agents, and the 30-day observation period may not capture long-term effects. The use of flat restorative material surfaces that did not replicate clinical settings, the sample size, the lack of a pH measurement for the beverage, and the use of distilled water rather than artificial saliva were other study shortcomings. The samples used in this investigation did not undergo any aging processes. Furthermore, the oral environment is impacted by a number of variables, including saliva, normal dietary habits, and hygiene practices, all of which may have had an effect on results. Additionally, this study’s immersion temperature was restricted to 24 °C. Variations in beverage temperature are necessary to provide a more thorough understanding of the tested solutions’ effects on restorative materials and to more accurately reflect long-term clinical settings.

Overall, these findings emphasize the importance of material selection in clinical practice. While single-shade composites like Vittra APS may offer initial aesthetic appeal, they are more vulnerable to color change and degradation over time, particularly when exposed to staining agents. Regular composites like Filtek Z250, however, showed superior resistance to color change and mechanical degradation, making them a more durable option for long-term use in environments that expose restorative materials to staining substances.

Conclusions

Under the conditions of this study, the single-shade composite resin (Vittra APS) showed greater color changes, gloss reduction, and hardness loss compared to the universal resin composite. These results highlight the importance of selecting materials based on both esthetic and mechanical durability in clinical applications.
