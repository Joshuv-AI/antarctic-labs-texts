Analysis of land-use and livelihood transformations in peri-urban areas: Insights from North-Western Ghana
Authors: Alfred Toku, Samuel Twumasi Amoah, Nelson Nyabanyi N-yanbini, Anthony Kwabena Sarfo, Prosper Tornyeviadzi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper questions claims that peri-urbanization perpetuates the shrinking of livelihood opportunities. A critical analysis of the peri-urbanization process presents a more nuanced transformation of livelihood opportunities. Drawing on concurrent triangulation mixed-method, primary data was sourced from non-farm workers using survey questionnaires, informant interviews, and observation from 225 respondents in a Ghanaian peri-urban community -Bamahu. Digital image software, Erdas Imaging 2015 and Landsat satellite were used to acquire land use and land cover data to affirm spatial differences. Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics complemented with thematic analysis for qualitative data. The findings showed significant land use and land cover alterations from 2000 to 2019. These changes resulted in the springing up of non-farm livelihoods that populated formal and informal non-farm opportunities. The analysis provides evidence that supports two key contributions to peri-urbanization and livelihood transformation debates. Empirically, it brings in-depth insights from original fieldwork data to highlight specific contextual realities by not only revealing the spatial alterations but also peering into how emerging non-farm livelihoods trade-off with existing farm-based livelihoods. Theoretically, it extends the debate by reframing peri-urbanization as a process of transforming livelihoods that thrive due to infrastructural development, new skills, and a ready market for goods and services. These findings have important implications for opening up a theorization of the peri-urbanization phenomenon. This paper concludes that the emergence of these livelihoods is crucial and should be adequately integrated into urban policies and plans to ensure inclusive cities.Keywords: Peri-urbanization, Land use diversification, Livelihood transformation, Non-farm opportunities, Inclusive cities

Introduction

The emergent developments in peri-urban areas attributed to rapid urbanization processes have gained prominence in urban development discourses in many cities worldwide [1,2]. There are two important strands of the debate. The first is a recognition that the expanded form of urbanization is associated with the conversions of agricultural land uses to other land uses particularly in peri-urban areas which often come along with loss of livelihoods, pressure on ecosystems, changes in household fuels, and the emergence of slums and shanty towns [[3], [4], [5]]. It is argued that negative characteristics in many growing urban areas are felt more in the peri-urban areas. Thus, these areas are perceived to be at the expanding urbanization's receiving end. As a result, Bonye et al. [6] and Toku [7] observed that households engage in a mix of non-farm and on-farm livelihoods as a response to the agricultural land use change instigated by urbanization. The second aspect of the discourse considers how peri-urbanization boosts economic productivity through the concentration of capital, the ribbon development of small businesses, infrastructural development, and service provision in the city [see Refs. [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. Relatedly, developments in peri-urban areas generate opportunities such as small businesses, service-oriented jobs, and commercial, and manufacturing employment which residents including indigenous communities engage in to earn income [12]. Among other things, the inevitable diversification of livelihood options to address the challenges of the growing urban and peri-urban populations is key. The extent literature [World Bank [13]; Owusu Ansah & Chibu [14] and De Bruin & Holleman [15], affirmed a backing for peri-urban development through livelihood transformations emanating from peri-urbanization to offer pathways for poverty reduction and economic growth packages. Yet, literature on the nature and patterns of peri-urban livelihood transformations in households has not kept pace with these dynamics [16]. A more nuanced understanding of these dynamics is particularly critical as discussed in this current study.

This paper examines how spatial alterations associated with peri-urban developments and emerging non-farm livelihood trade-offs shape existing farm-based livelihoods. Following the aforementioned, we question claims that peri-urbanization mainly perpetuates the shrinking of livelihood opportunities. We argue that peri-urbanization rather transforms livelihoods into more formal and informal non-farm employable opportunities that thrive due to the development of infrastructure, new skills, and a ready market for goods and services. Based on the spatial alterations and emerging non-farm livelihood trade-offs with existing farm-based livelihoods, it could be concluded that the emergence of new forms of livelihoods in peri-urban communities in Ghana is crucial and should be adequately integrated into urban policies and plans to ensure inclusive cities.

This paper makes two key contributions to the peri-urbanization and livelihood transformation debates. First, at the empirical level, it brings in-depth insights from original fieldwork data to highlight some specific contextual realities by not only revealing the spatial alterations as observed in previous studies [[17], [18], [19], [20]] but also peering into how emerging non-farm livelihoods trade-off with existing farm-based livelihoods. Second, at the theoretical level, it extends the debate by reframing peri-urbanization as a process of transforming livelihoods into more formal and informal non-farm employable opportunities that thrive due to the development of infrastructure, new skills, and a ready market for goods and services. The paper is organized as follows: it begins by introducing an expanded form of urbanization as a threat to the countryside's dominant economic activity (agriculture) globally, in Africa, and in Ghana as well as the changing phase of peri-urban regions and livelihood transformations due to the assimilation of ‘new urban residents’ and associated activities. This was followed by literature and the methodology, which draws on primary data, secondary data from respondents, and the United States Geological Survey Department for spatio-temporal data and analysis. The subsequent section presents the results and discussions. The final section is devoted to policy recommendations and concluding thoughts emanating from the literature and the study results.

Literature

Understanding urbanization, peri-urban engulfment, and livelihood changes

Globally, several studies on the extent of peri-urban engulfment and development emanate from population growth, expanded urbanization, and industrial development, which have an expressly dire effect on peri-urban livelihood alterations [[21], [22], [23], [24]]. Shifting from agricultural ventures to more non-farm economic activities remains a public outcry in the developing world due to the underlying land-use transformation. It is argued in extant literature that peri-urban areas are undergoing several kinds of transformations in their livelihood structures, with both urban and rural features co-existing [25,26]. In the works of Varkey & Manasi [24], many peri-urban regions witnessed the conversion of arable lands to accommodate new industrial development. Similarly, Peperah [27] and Issifu [28] revealed the continuous network of suburban communities as the core of a backward-forward linkage. Such linkages result in anthropogenic developments that threaten existing farm-based livelihoods [20,21]. Hence, peri-urban dwellers access diverse income sources for creating non-farm employment opportunities due to investment and reinvestment in non-farm ventures that have come about due to peri-urbanization [29]. Aligning with Amoateng et al. [30], peri-urban areas were described as environments with the characteristics of non-farm job creation hinging on transportation, housing, environmental alterations, and others. For instance, the USAID [31] report indicated that non-farm income activities correlate positively with the overall household wealth of the underprivileged population residing in the countryside. This is glaring because non-farm prospects are not climate-dependent. Therefore, poverty alleviation through employment generation suggests a strong relationship between expanded urbanization, peri-urbanization, and livelihood transformation [32,33]. Such positive outcomes are reflected in peri-urban dwellers, who are typically rural, elderly men, vulnerable women, and children [34]. This, therefore, joins the embedded debate in this paper that, in many developing regions, livelihood opportunities have expanded despite literature pointing to the downside of inevitable urban expansion challenges on peri-urban livelihoods.

Conceptualizing peri-urban non-farm livelihood transformations: theory and practice

A livelihood comprises farm and non-farm abilities, assets, and actions necessary for a decent or good standard of living [23,35]. Rural or peri-urban livelihood is a function of available natural or man-made resources utilized by dwellers to achieve the highest level of utility. The Department for International Development [36] framework was adapted as a model fit to operationalize the concept of peri-urban non-farm livelihood transformations due to peri-urbanization and sub-urban development This paper captured in the wider framework of sustainable livelihoods the transforming structures, processes, and vulnerability context as outcomes of peri-urbanization and sub-urban development (see Fig. 1). This is because the wider environment affects not only the assets to which peri-urban dwellers have access but also what can be achieved with those assets in the context of peri-urbanization. In this paper, the framework has four main fixed components: livelihood assets; formal opportunities; informal opportunities; and livelihood outcomes or results, which are linked to all the activities or livelihood options in the peri-urban community - Bamahu. In the context of this paper, livelihood assets dwell on all the significant resources that individuals pull together from formal and informal non-farm and farm opportunities available. These assets include natural capital, physical capital, financial capital, human capital, and social capital assets [37]. Further, formal and informal non-farm opportunities are taken by individuals to improve and maintain their well-being as well as manage their insolvency depending on the livelihood assets available. Also, livelihood outcomes and results are the direct and indirect effects of non-farm, formal, and informal opportunities that individuals and households have embraced to enhance their well-being.

This paper situates the argument in the 2000 DFID livelihood framework by incorporating the transformative effects of peri-urbanization on livelihood assets, strategies, and outcomes, including formal and informal non-farm livelihood opportunities. Peri-urbanization is dependent on overall population growth and the outcomes of land use. This is because there is a consistent spill-over of the population and the built-up area into these zones, and the backward and forward linkages of their economies need attention [38]. These transformations have had impacts on livelihood assets, considering the current state of land use, land-related actors, and the relative values placed on land and land resources. This has not affected their dominant livelihood opportunities only but has widened the creation of new livelihood portfolios that trade off the existing ones. These new livelihood creations are underscored by infrastructural expansion, population growth, and convergence that use the available capital in the peri-urban economy.

For instance, there is a shift from subsistence farming to commercial agriculture albeit in limited quantities due to a lack of collateral to attract loans and reduced farm sizes due to peri-urban land-use changes. The peri-urban dwellers tend to capitalize on existing and emerging non-agricultural activities that aid their survival and diversify their income sources [39]. However, peri-urban transformations impact the livelihood assets to create embedded livelihood outcomes in a long- and short-term period to fasten household needs (e.g., food, shelter, and health care; education; the market; and environmental conservation) to aid their survival.

Therefore, utilizing the Sustainable Livelihoods Framework to improve comprehension of livelihood change in peri-urban enclaves marked by rising population growth and tradeable services. This theory was utilized to analyze the processes, consequences, and interactions in peri-urban areas that have experienced expansion and development in recent years. The framework is a planning tool used to evaluate the sustainability of new emerging opportunities resulting from shifts in land-use patterns in peri-urban areas. These opportunities involve both agricultural and non-agricultural activities that aim to improve the well-being of the local population. The outcomes of livelihoods can be connected to the creation of new skills, increased income and food security, the expansion of the local market, and the prevention of outmigration.

Materials and methods

Description of the study area

This research was conducted in Bamahu, one of the fastest-growing peri-urban areas in the Wa Municipality. Wa is 66.3 % urbanized, compared to the national urbanized share of 50.9 % and the regional urbanized share of 16.3 %, and has expanded to engulf adjoining settlements, including Bamahu [[40], [41], [42], [43]]. Bamahu, a peri-urban community, has a total population of 4472 [44] and a total land area of 8719092.68 square meters (8.72 km2), which is about 2 % of the municipal land area (579.86 km2). For this paper, “Bamahu" is defined to encompass the Simon Diedong Dombo University of Business and Integrated Development Studies [SDD UBIDS], the Blue Hills Hotel area, and the township of Bamahu towards the bridge that separates Bamahu from the Kumpala community and the boundary point that separate the Bamahu from Kpongu community (see Fig. 2). The Bamahu community was selected based on its notable spatial and livelihood changes resulting from urban expansion and the establishment of growth centers [24,[43], [45], [46], [47], [48], [49]]. Land-use changes resulting from this have impacted current livelihoods admits their survival. The location of the Bamahu community along the Wa-Kumasi trunk route, the availability of land for agricultural purposes, and residential and industrial land-use expansion naturally triggered growth and development in the area [50]. All these developments that leapfrogged since the establishment of the growth pole (the University for Development Studies – Bamahu campus now the Simon Diedong Dombo University of Business and Integrated Development Studies – Bamahu campus) till date have changed the land-use pattern from agricultural to commercial, residential, and civic land-use. The Bamahu community has since become a business incubation hub for residents and foreigners within and without the Bamahu and the Wa Municipal. As a multicultural city with a unique point of transportation that links the southern part of the Upper West Region, the municipality has grown outwardly to affect livelihood portfolios in adjoining communities. The migrant population has dominated Bamahu due to the presence of the university, which attracts commercial and residential facilities. Since the majority of the population in Bamahu are students, the economic composition of the suburb has changed since 2006, when the then Wa-campus of the University for Development Studies (UDS) was moved to its permanent campus in Bamahu [51].

Consequently, attention was drawn to Bamahu as the most lucrative location for commercial properties for retailing and other commercial establishments (hotels, hostels, guest houses, restaurants, and supermarkets) with other accompanying menial jobs. This has since outbid the dominant livelihood option (agriculture) in Bamahu, which is driving all the livelihood choices and strategies. As one of the most lucrative locations among the peri-urban areas of Wa Municipality for farming in the past, Bamahu was selected because, it is now recognized as a commercial and residential hub for many migrant students, workers, and businesses. In recent times, the existence of land and the nature of land-use change in Bamahu highlight the shift in land uses which draws attention to the need for infrastructural development, which expands non-farm livelihoods and widens the choices and strategies of the peri-urban residents [45,46].

Sources of data and methods of data collection

This research adopted a concurrent triangulation mixed-method design as a cross-sectional study aimed to solicit different but complementary data that better described the research problem [52]. For triangulation, both qualitative and quantitative techniques and analytical procedures were used concurrently for a detailed objective result separately and merged for interpretation. The study design counted on questionnaire administration, in-depth interviews, and observation complemented with geospatial techniques to produce the needed data to achieve the objective of this paper [53,54].

Data collection took place from January to December to capture data during the off-farming season and farming season. The questionnaire was administered to the owners of business ventures and non-farm workers at the household level to obtain primary data on the changing patterns of livelihoods, income levels, and livelihood portfolios in peri-urban Bamahu. Separate in-depth interviews lasting 47 min on average were carried out with officials from the Wa Municipal Assembly, the National Board for Small-Scale Industry, and the Department of Community Development to solicit relevant secondary data. The study sought to explore the nature, outcomes, and experiences of an emerging livelihood portfolio in peri-urban Bamahu due to peri-urbanization.

Sectional heads in Bamahu, Tindana (landlords), and settlers or tenants were also interviewed separately for 28 min to add additional information that aided the understanding of the study. Additional information was obtained from separate focus group discussions lasting up to 90 min from women groups, business owners, and residents in Bamahu on the nature of livelihood change and the survival choices and strategies adopted. All these engaging research participants offered insights and clues that shaped their opinions and understanding of the nature of livelihood transformations in Bamahu due to the extent of growth, land use, and land-cover change and the springing up of new livelihood choices and strategies among peri-urban dwellers in Bamahu. This paper uses infographics to visualize spatial-temporal data. Landsat images were acquired from secondary sources (the United States Geological Survey Department) to estimate the extent of peri-urban growth since 2000 and to predict the trend and the direction of land use change.

Sample size determination and sampling

When the true population is unknown coupled with limited literature in the area of interest the sample size is required to estimate the proportion with the desired level of precision from pilot studies. The sample size is given with a confidence interval of 95 %, where σ is the population standard deviation (the mean of the squared deviations between the data values and the population means) and Z represents the Z-value (measurement using statistics to explain how a value relates to the mean of a set of values) at a confidence interval (shows how often the true population proportion who would respond is within the confidence interval) of 95 %. Considering .05 error of margin (a statistic that represents the degree of random sampling error in the survey), thus (△), Ƥ represents the proportion of a parameter obtained from a pilot study undertaken by the researchers in the study area with the characteristics of interest. Following, there were no records of former farm workers and now non-farm workers in the Bamahu community. The pilot study revealed that 68 % of non-farm workers had previously been farm workers.

The minimum sample size required is rounded up to 196 non-farm peri-urban workers. Assuming a 25 % drop-out rate in the survey, the study added the calculated percentage to the sample size such that (196 + 49 = 245) non-farm workers needed to be sampled. However, 225 respondents were covered in the study who previously owned a farm and have now opted to engage in a non-farm livelihood portfolio. Additional key informant interviews were carried out with the Assembly Member, the community head of small-scale businesses, and heads of farmer associations. Due to the nonexistence of the sample frame, the authors used the convenience sampling technique to select peri-urban non-farm workers. A convenience sampling technique was used to reach out to participants who were readily available and met all the required inclusive characteristics thus, must be a non-farm worker, must be a small business owner, and must be part of a residence in the Bamahu community in the last 5–10 years. In cases where we had no small business owner or an employee who would lead the team to the owner of a household, we skipped it. Also, a purposive sampling technique was used to select relevant key stakeholders for the study, including business owners, officials of the National Board of Small-Scale Industry, the Wa Municipal Assembly, and Rural Enterprise Development who had relevant information on the nature of non-farm businesses, and the levels of non-farm business development in peri-urban Wa (Bamahu).

Methods of data analysis

The data were analyzed using Statistical Package for Service Solution version 20. Appropriate statistical analysis was done using descriptive statistics; percentages and frequency counts to analyze survey data on income levels, while Exploratory Factor Analyses (EFA) were used to show the dominant non-farm livelihoods adopted by the populace due to per-urbanization in Bamahu. From the EFA, a data suitability test was carried out with a Cronbach's alpha (internal reliability) of .7402 for the accepted reliability of the research instruments. Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin Measure of Sampling Adequacy is .564, suggesting sample adequacy for the analysis of the data set. Bartlett's Test of Sphericity of 626.356, a Determinant of 7.081E-04, and a Level of Significance of p-value < .001, indicating no duplicate in the study population. In the spatial data acquisition and analysis, the methodological approach was on image acquisition, processing, and analysis with multi-temporal LANDSAT 7 (Enhanced Thematic Mapper) images acquired from the United States Geological Survey Department for 2000, 2010, and 2019. Digital image software, Erdas Imagine 2015, was used for image processing, analysis, and interpretation of the spatial data as shown in Table 1. Bands were stacked to form a multispectral image using the layer Stack Spectral tool in Erdas Imagine 2015.

Results and discussion

Socio-demographic characteristics of respondents

Demographic data showed that the largest category of respondents was within the age group 46–59 years (47 %), followed by people aged 36–45 years (37 %) as indicated in Table 2. About 57 % of the respondents were males, while females accounted for 43 %. The majority of the respondents had no formal education (57 %), with only (33 %) having basic formal education (29 % primary and up to high school level (4 %). Crop farming was the dominant livelihood option (64 %). The resurgence of other land-based livelihoods from animal rearing, charcoal production, and weaving have dominated the remaining 36 %. The study area, which used to be entirely rural, is now changing as a result of peri-urban growth and development and its emerging livelihoods.

This included commercial activities such as groceries, fuel or gas attendants, mobile money vendors, cleaning and security services influenced by the existence of clinics, schools, retail shops, banking services, and fuel or gas stations among others. These new livelihoods emanate from the fast-growing population in Bamahu and its associated specific development pole following the establishment of the then University for Development Studies (now the Simon Diedong Dombo University of Business and Integrated Development Studies). Therefore, the respondents are cashing in on these emerging opportunities as a way of meeting the diverse needs of the rapidly increasing population at the individual and household levels. The study revealed that these complementary private and public services and their opportunities have recently shaped the survival of many peri-urban dwellers. This corroborated the assertion of Buor & Konkor [48] and Khen et al. [55] that in many developing regions livelihood diversification is important and revered as a survival strategy. The reasons are that existing land-based activities (farming) for peri-urban households are bid out with increasing commercial and residential demands from the concentrated population.

In recent years, the Bamahu community has had a mix of rural and urban characteristics in terms of the opportunities that support their survival (Table 2). In terms of income, the greater majority of the respondents (45 %) indicated that they earned between 700 and 900 Ghana cedis [per month] which is by far better than their subsistence farming activities offered. Among the few educated who were managers and owners of shops, (4 %) indicated that they get more than 1000 Ghana cedis [per month] in income from the newly adopted livelihood portfolios. Although there were a lot of small and medium-scale livelihoods other than farming, the respondents contend that these were more rewarding throughout the year than being under the sun for peanuts (a.k.a., farming). Despite the constraints of a poor asset base, lack of credit facilities, and infrastructure, it became evident that the welfare of the people hinged on the livelihood portfolios underscored by peri-urbanization dynamics.

Land-use and land cover change in Bamahu from 2000 to 2019

In the year 2000, [see Fig. 3 (Panel A)] there were only a small number of built-up regions represented by isolated patches symbolized in red in the land use categories. Since the year 2000, the predominant land cover in Bamahu has been grasslands and farmlands. This was due to the combination of a low human population density and slow expansion and dispersion of the Wa Township towards the outskirts. The rate of peri-urbanization in Bamahu was impacted by the restricted availability of peri-urban land and the lack of significant changes in land use and land cover in 2010. The land use and land cover (LULC) in 2010 [see Fig. 3 (Panel B)] primarily consisted of subsistence agriculture, fishing, and other low-skilled farming activities, such as charcoal production and communal gathering of resources such as fruits, nuts, and leaves (e.g., shear, mangos, and dawadawa, among others), which were essential for survival. It was revealed in Fig. 3 (Panel B) that gradually there were shrinking farmlands and grasslands because Wa Township began to expand to engulf its boundaries and immerse some peri-urban communities like Bamahu in the Wa Municipality. The study area began to show signs of land-use changes because building and development began to take over grasslands and farmlands with accompanying livelihood portfolios. The springing up of this building and development (i.e., residential, commercial, and civic land-use) could be linked to the establishment of the SDD-UBIDS. The university became functionally possible when these sectors (i.e., clinics, residential structures, commercial hubs, banking services, and groceries) were operational. Hence, there are changes in land use that triggered the emergence of new livelihood opportunities.

The situation deteriorated in 2019 [see Fig. 3 (Panel C)]. In addition to the status changes of the former University for Development Studies campus to a fully-fledged university which gained autonomy in 2019, there have been many more attractions in the study community, including hotels, guest houses, private schools, banks, wholesale and retail centers, and the growth of the local market. According to Khan et al. [55], the move of the campus to Bamahu in 2005 also stimulated expansion in the area that was formerly characterized by concentrated or dense agricultural zones or state farms. The map clearly illustrates the encroachment of urban development over agricultural and grassy areas, leaving just a few scattered areas for farming. The investigation uncovered that the housing rental market in Bamahu is experiencing growth due to the increasing population need and demand. This has led to the establishment of commercial facilities to cater for potential consumers, including students, service professionals, and migrant settlers. As a result, there have been alterations in the land cover and spatial density of Bamahu, which has attracted a large number of developers and speculators to the area. Land speculators have become more prevalent, and there are problems with land litigation largely due to multiple land sales. Nevertheless, the establishment of churches, prayer camps, and other commercial and municipal facilities has added to the demand for land and changes in land use, resulting in the growth of new livelihoods. An observation was made that the Wa-Kumasi Road passing through Bamahu is progressively transforming the previously agricultural community into a hub for transportation, particularly during school sessions and much more so during holiday periods. This is due to the relocation of several transport operators who bring their commercial vehicles to Bamahu to serve the growing population.

This statement confirms the claim made by Lambin & Geist [56] that the changes in land use, both direct and indirect, are happening at an unprecedented rate on the outskirts of several cities in the developing regions of the world. According to studies conducted by Bonye et al. [6], Toku [7], Korah et al. [19], Osumanu et al. [42] and Buor & Konkor [48], peri-urban areas in Wa see significant growth and offer significant non-farm livelihood opportunities for survival. The university's establishment had a profound impact on the previously agrarian economy, leading to a shift towards a commercialized economy and a rise in non-farm economic activities. This was supported by the leader of the Small Business Owners' Association in Bamahu:

“Due to the high demand from students and migrants, my colleagues and I, who are involved in the grocery and other businesses, have decided to boost our investments. This will allow us to maximize our profits and generate more job opportunities”.

The Rural Enterprise Development Director had this to say,

“The development of small businesses in the Wa Municipality has gravitated toward the peri-urban communities where there is available potential that attract the growing population, their increasing attendant needs, complemented with tradeable services that are beneficial to the populace”

This indicates that the Bamahu community's strategic location and territorial nature have resulted in constant pressure to adapt from nearby villages such as Danko, Konfabiala, Piisi, Kumpala, and the Wa Township. As a result, a wide variety of individuals with a wide range of interests are drawn to the expanding non-farm development. This was corroborated by Mancebo [57], who noted that the type of expansion in peri-urban areas is influenced by planning biases, residential choice, and the location of specific activities. Additionally, Webster [58] claims that peri-urban landowners' economic reasoning promotes peri-urbanization and the growth of new, non-farm livelihoods. Thus, Irwin & Geoghegan [59] ; Woldegebriel & Girma [60] and Iddrisu et al. [61] suggested that peri-urban inhabitants' income levels rise as a result of increased economic gains from shifting their livelihood from agriculture to other non-farm portfolios.

Extent of land cover changes in Bamahu between 2000 and 2019

The spatial analysis in Table 3 shows significant land cover changes from 2000 to 2019 in Bamahu. Whereas some land cover changes were reducing steadily over the years, others were increasing inevitably due to population growth and peri-urbanization demands. Though some percentage of farmland still exists, the farming activities are predominantly for subsistence. The reduction of farmland through the bidding-out process of emerging non-agricultural activities has affected the economy of Bamahu. As supported by the statistics in Table 3, the reduction rate is substantial (866,023.139 m2), equivalent to about a 30 % reduction in farmlands. This further affects various economic activities that are linked to agriculture, such as farming, buying and selling farm produce, and the general income and food security of the enclave. It was revealed that between 2000 and 2019, grassland drastically reduced from 4,843,940.379 m2 to 1,595,040.846 m2. This indicates that the ‘forested’ savannah is swiftly declining in Bamahu because of increasing developing areas and its novel emerging land use in the commercial and residential sectors of the economy. It was revealed that building and development has increased significantly from 968,788.076 m2 in 2000 to 5,083,710.748 m2 in 2019 and is estimated to be 78 % compared to the previous 30 %. It is therefore not surprising that the new emerging land uses have presented new livelihood opportunities that have made life comfortable for greater sections of the population in Bamahu underscored by peri-urbanization. These are by far the elements in peri-urban transformations that bespeak the driving forces of livelihood diversifications in many growing areas.

Similarly, in the study community, the land cover changes, and the supporting statistics show that the trend of agricultural livelihood opportunities is heading towards extinction giving rise to non-farm livelihood opportunities that aid the survival of the populace. The increasing expansion of non-farm livelihood opportunities resulting from peri-urbanization is outbidding farm livelihood opportunities in Bamahu. In line with the assertion of Seto & Kaufmann [62], their study concluded that many economic activities have engulfed agrarian communities at the fringes that have shaped land use and available livelihoods. This has led to the alteration of the landscape and green space deterioration through agricultural land conversion to residential and commercial land use emanating from peri-urbanization. Put differently, in the work of Lerise et al. [63], land use for non-agricultural activities (residential, industrial, and commercial purposes) tends to dominate agricultural lands and their associated livelihood portfolios. The springing up of these new livelihoods permits peri-urban dwellers to adopt several alternative non-agrarian ventures that improve income by ensuring livelihood security. These new livelihoods include becoming a cleaner service, security guard, engaging in small businesses (grocery), and food vending, among others. As supported by Abdulai et al. [64], and Iddrisu et al. [61] large tracts of agricultural lands are indiscriminately converted for a physical infrastructure provision that has come with its ardent challenges and prospects. Hence affirming that existing livelihood systems change peri-urban households [18,43].

Dominant livelihood opportunities due to peri-urbanization in Bamahu

The diversification of livelihoods differs among individuals depending on the available livelihood capitals that influence their ability to sustain themselves. The Bamahu community's way of life is being influenced by the growing peri-urbanization, which is causing income and food instability among some residents. This is mainly due to the increasing population concentration and the ongoing conversion of agricultural land into residential, civic, and commercial areas.

A 48-year-old woman revealed that:

“It is difficult for us to cope with the effects of urbanization in Bamahu. Now all our trees are being used for the production of charcoal, our farmlands, and our communal trees like dawadawa, mangoes, and shea are also being used for building construction, including the university campus that used to be a state farmland”, he concluded

A 65-year-old male farmer also indicated that:

“The conversion of lands has led to the loss of farmlands and diversification into non-farm income-generating ventures. I must state that it was difficult, but the changes have paid off in our lives”

The loss of farmlands has necessitated the increasing diversification into non-farm income-generating livelihoods that generate more income and augment isolated agrarian activities for survival. That many forms of induced urbanization growth result in improved tradable services that support the survival of the growing converged population. In the study area, therefore, many of these tradable services are intensified due to the ready market, which is largely composed of students and the service worker population.

A 43-year-old security personnel who also works as a cleaner revealed that:

“The establishment of the then University for Development Studies has occupied vast arable lands, including my family’s land, with no minimal payoffs [compensation]. With farming opportunities becoming secondary for me, I was recruited to become security personnel for one facility in Bamahu, and at the same time, I worked as a cleaner with the university during the day. Although farming used to be good, I have gained the respect of my family for accepting to work in the formal sector through which I am earning salaries at the expense of the minimal income received from farming all season previously”

It became evident that farming has become more of a part-time job for some dwellers in Bamahu as a subsistence, basically to feed themselves. This is to substantiate that non-farm livelihood options have emerged in the study area due to urban growth and altered land-use patterns to widen investment opportunities that create non-farm opportunities derived from the establishment of non-profit social and economic infrastructure. The development of non-profit social and economic infrastructure is critical for several urban and peri-urban areas. These institutions have secondary functions that act as magnets, drawing in a wide range of tradeable services. These institutions function as economic centers, acting as the core of development. The literature from Bonye et al. [6], Woldegebriel & Girma [65], Gallant et al. [66] and Busck et al. [67] reveals a shift in land use from a predominantly agricultural landscape to a diverse landscape that redirects livelihood capitals towards non-farm ventures.

Tracing from the second component (see Table 2), it was revealed that the fuel/gas attendant and food vendor had a factor loading of .509 and .512. Similarly, Asare's [70] studies in Ghana supported a higher share of income for the peri-urban farm population, who diversified into non-farm livelihood activities from the various sectors of their economy. “This fast-food joint has been helpful to me and the entire Bamahu community. We have employed more than 25 young men and women from the community who help in the production process, packaging, and delivery of our food in the entire Bamahu Township and its environs.”

The result is consistent with the claim by Bryceson [71] and Mandere et al. [72] that many peri-urban dwellers without access to farmlands due to their far locations engage in available dominant non-farm income-generating activities for survival. These activities offered diverse and stable income sources to boost their living standards and enhance income security. Empirical data from the field substantiates this claim, as the majority of the respondents (88.7 %) believed that they were earning a constant income now rather than before, although 11.3 % had divergent views of still engaging in agriculture on a subsistence basis. This was explained by the findings of Adomako [73], who found that the implications of land-use changes initiated by sprawl are greatly felt negatively by individuals whose livelihood depends on agriculture. Hence, the development of a monetized service economy in these peri-urban zones serves as an opportunity to diversify due to the trend of emerging non-farm portfolios.

However, it was revealed that a greater majority (85 % of the respondents) settled on the operation of grocery stores (provision shops).

This was revealed by the 52-year-old shop owner;

“I am better off than my senior brother who is still farming very far away from Bamahu … I get a ready market for the products I stock in my shop; it is only when students are on break that affects my sales but indeed my all-year-round benefits from this shop are far better than what I used to receive when I was a farmer”

This suggests that some livelihood opportunities depended on the extent of peri-urbanization and urban growth in the Bamahu. This growth's nature and pattern influenced the development of tradeable services that provide employment opportunities for the peri-urban population. On the other hand, students make up the majority of Bamahu's population, making it a significant market for commodity trade and service provision to manufacturing and retail establishments.

Income-generating activities before and after peri-urbanization in Bamahu

Essential choices and strategies with which many people try to make ends meet to improve their living standards are referred to as livelihood diversification. They are the adaptive processes that allow households to either keep the existing ones or maintain livelihood-changing portfolios [74]. Regardless of the trend of peri-urban growth in Bamahu, there are still traces of agricultural activities that have been the bedrock of the economy. It was revealed that the greater majority of the respondents (53 %) engaged in farming before peri-urbanization surfaced in the growing area, with a total average income of 1271 Ghana cedis per year. This indicates that it was a major source of income for the various households due to several branches of the likelihood portfolio it renders to the people. Although other jobs contributed to household income, they were not the mainstay of the economy of Bamahu or the municipality at large. In the era of peri-urbanization, farming has seen a decline in terms of the number of people involved, available arable spaces, and the total average income gained from it. It was revealed that 16 % of the respondents engaged in farming as a coping or supplement livelihood option in the face of peri-urbanization in Bamahu. The total average income from agriculture has fallen from 1271 Ghana cedis to 480 Ghana cedis (see Table 5).

The analysis revealed an increase in opportunities for making a living as Bamahu has experienced growth and accompanying alterations in land use thus, shifting from agricultural to commercial, public, and residential functions. The data indicate a rise in non-farm livelihoods, hence expanding the available options and strategies for survival as well as expected income.

The livelihood opportunities had an average total annual income 5780 Ghana cedis now compared to the average total annual income of 1674 Ghana cedis before the emergence of non-farm livelihood. This affirmed the idea of Alobo Loison [75] that many peri-urban dwellers have diverted their traditional sources of livelihood (farming) to a new emergent one due to peri-urbanization and its associated opportunities. In line with the assertion of Belay & Bewket [76], many households’ livelihoods are enhanced in terms of availability and access, thereby improving income and food-generating activities. It was revealed that a greater majority of the respondents (57 %) were informally employed under non-farm opportunities (e.g., food vendors, mobile money operators, mobile credit vendors, provision store attendants, and smock weavers). Subsequently, 34 % of the respondents contacted indicated that they had formal non-farm opportunities (e.g., security service, institutional laborers, cleaners, and fuel/gas attendants) (see Table 6).

This was what a 44-year-old man owner of ‘Watanga’ Shop outlined in a key informant interview;

"Even though we are not salary workers, we earn between 30 and 45 Ghana cedis a day from my provision shop … Since school authorities have leased our farmlands, driving the motor [vehicle] at night when students are on campus has also proven beneficial. Our relocation to far-off lands necessitated significant capital to begin farming again, prompting some of us to allocate our limited financial resources to other non-farm ventures”

This corroborated what transpired in an interview with a 43-year-old respondent;

"If you do not have money to build a hostel or any other residential facility for renting, you will become miserable since the farming occupation and its opportunities have declined, and those who farm need to borrow land from other families in faraway locations, which increases the cost of production … It is, therefore, important for me and my family to rest on the new opportunities that are non-agricultural."

It was deduced that peri-urban Bamahu has been at the receiving end of peri-urbanization where there are visible pressures on infrastructural expansion, and diverse non-farm livelihood activities such as sand winning and infrastructural expansion, weaving, and other tradeable opportunities all outbid agricultural activities [27,77]. Hence, adopting these non-farm activities increased the annual average income of the respondent, suggesting an increase in the current annual income received from these emergent economic activities other than agriculture. This corroborates the literature that poverty alleviation has been abridged due to peri-urbanization and peri-urban non-farm development [43,48]. As supported by Abass et al. [78], livelihood diversification is central to poverty reduction efforts for the majority of subsistence farmers in peri-urban areas.

This substantiates the findings of Alobo Loison [75] that peri-urban dwellers' rapid diversion from their traditional sources of livelihood stabilizes their income, controls outmigration, and improves food security through environmental conservation. This justifies the assertion that in many peri-urban environments, dominant economic activity (traditional farming activities) is always inescapably conflicting with alternative economic, residential, and recreational interests aimed at providing diverse non-farm livelihood options [65,79,80]. In the same vein, some scholars, such as Smith et al. [81], Simon [82], Paavola [83], and Bonye et al. [84], idealized that peri-urban land-use alteration towards service provision is evident in the global south. However, scholarly studies by Mehar et al. [85] outlined that, coupled with farmer's climatic shocks, there is an unsustainability of livelihoods that are linked to agriculture, which propels farmers to search for alternative non-farm opportunities that have come with peri-urbanization.

Concluding remarks

This paper provides rigorous empirical evidence to question the assertions that peri-urbanization perpetuates the shrinking of livelihood opportunities. Mainly, such claims mask and under-appreciate the complex and dynamic potentials of livelihood transformations that can contribute to poverty alleviation – as the case of Bamahu demonstrates. This study's findings show significant spatial alterations, as there has been a 424.7 % increase in the extent of building and development, resulting in a corresponding reduction of 29.8 % of savannah lands and 67.1 % of grasslands. These spatial alterations have influenced livelihood activities as people gradually transitioned from farm-based to non-farm-based livelihood activities. Relatedly, it was revealed that farm-based livelihood activities in Bamahu have drastically decreased between 2000 and 2019 while at the same time, there has been significant livelihood diversification and the emergence of new livelihoods. These emerging livelihoods were largely in commerce, light manufacturing industries, and services.

Despite the significant loss of farmlands and its impact on households' incomes, a kind of trade-off became evident when the research participants were moving from purely agrarian to non-agrarian activities. Dominant among the new emerging livelihoods were food vending, mobile money operations, grocery stores, local fabric weaving (smocks), and mobile credit vending. Apart from these, there was a gradual shift towards more formalized and service-oriented livelihoods, such as teaching in private and public schools, security services, institutional laborers and cleaners, and fuel and gas attendants. These findings lend credence to the effect that beyond spatial alterations that are usually associated with peri-urbanization, there is also the emergence of diverse non-farm livelihoods trade-off with existing farm-based livelihoods. While accepting the complex nature of peri-urbanization, the paper recognizes its essential role in creating opportunities for people to meet their own basic needs and those of the growing population. It is therefore critical to explore the dynamics of the livelihoods and the necessary conditions that can help peri-urban residents particularly the poor and the marginalized to seize the opportunities. This study's findings have important implications for opening up a theorization of the peri-urbanization phenomenon. This paper argues that the emergence of non-farm and farm-based livelihoods is crucial and should be adequately integrated into urban policies and plans to ensure inclusive cities. Having the SDGs in mind, city authorities and all relevant stakeholders have a critical responsibility to provide an enabling environment that will support the growth of both the farm and non-farm livelihoods.
