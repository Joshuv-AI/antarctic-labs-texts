The Effect of Empiric Initiation of Treatment on Outcomes Among Patients Presenting With Extrapulmonary Mycobacterium Tuberculosis Infection in a Community Hospital Setting
Authors: Talha Awwal, Faisal R Kh, Hanya Awaba, Waseem Anwar, Jinma Ren, Muhammad A Safdar, Saqib Walayat, John Farrell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExtrapulmonary tuberculosis (EPTB) accounts for ~20% of Mycobacterium tuberculosis infections in Pakistan. Diagnostic delays are common and may worsen outcomes. We evaluated whether empiric treatment initiation improves outcomes among patients with EPTB at a large public hospital in Islamabad, Pakistan.MethodsRetrospective cohort of consecutive EPTB cases at Pakistan Institute of Medical Sciences (January–June 2016). Patients were categorized as clinically diagnosed (symptoms/radiology) or laboratory-supported (histology, microbiology, or biochemical tests). Standard Directly Observed Therapy Short-course (DOTS) based regimens were used. Outcomes were treatment success, failure, loss to follow-up, and mortality; groups were compared using chi-square testing.ResultsOf 317 EPTB patients, 275 completed treatment and were analyzed. Mean age was 34.4 years; 57% were male. The most common sites were pleural (28.7%) and lymphatic (20.3%) TB. Clinically diagnosed patients had higher cure rates than laboratory supported diagnosis group patients (87.6% vs. 79.6%; p = 0.221) and significantly lower mortality (5.3% vs. 9.3%; p = 0.041). Among patients who completed treatment, 82.9% achieved treatment success; overall cure rate was 71% including non-completion and loss to follow-up.ConclusionsEarly empiric therapy may be associated with improved survival and higher cure rates compared with delaying treatment pending laboratory confirmation. Context-appropriate EPTB guidelines emphasizing prompt clinical treatment are warranted in high burden settings.Keywords: Tuberculosis, Extrapulmonary tuberculosis, Empiric therapy, Treatment outcomes, Diagnostic delay, Pakistan

Introduction

Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTC) infection is a leading cause of death worldwide.1 The World Health Organization (WHO) declared MTC infection a global health emergency in 1992. At that time MTC was prevalent globally, in virtually every country.2 MTC control efforts in prosperous economies have led to a dramatic decrease in the prevalence of MTC infections, but the same cannot be said about middle income and low-income economies where MTC infections remain highly prevalent. MTC infection is the fifth and sixth leading cause of mortality in lower middle-and low-income economies, respectively.3 Pakistan has a high burden of MTC infections. In 2018, the incidence of MTC infection was 265/100000 which is among the highest in the world.3 While the global mortality rate for MTC infection has fallen by >50% between 2000 and 2018 (due in large part to improved availability of anti-tuberculous drugs and antiretroviral medications for HIV coinfected patients worldwide), death from MTC infection in Pakistan has declined more modestly between 2000 and 2018, and remains >20/100,000 population (an 8% case fatality rate).3 This is due largely to limited access to specialized health care in Pakistan, while access in more prosperous countries has improved over the same time period.4,5 The National Tuberculosis Control Program (NTCP) in Pakistan provides free medication under the Directly Observed Therapy Short course (DOTS) program. But missed MTC infections from lack of notification, and barriers to patient access to care such as profound poverty, safety (e.g., dangers resulting from the war against terrorism), and refugee migration are key barriers to TB elimination in Pakistan. Individuals infected with Mycobacterium tuberculosis may either be asymptomatic (e.g., latent TB infection (LTBI)) or develop active tuberculosis.1,6 The majority of patients with active tuberculosis present with signs and symptoms of pulmonary infection, but a minority of patients with active disease (19–39%), present with either primary extrapulmonary tuberculosis (EPT) or EPT in the setting of active pulmonary infection.6,7 An epidemiologic study of newly diagnosed cases of tuberculosis in the United States suggested that the proportion of EPT is on the rise (16% in 1993 vs. 21% in 2013).8 Although nationwide descriptive data on Extra pulmonary tuberculosis in Pakistan have been published, studies specifically evaluating the impact of empiric initiation on clinical outcomes remain lacking.9 Mycobacterium tuberculosis may spread to any organ of the body through lymphatic or hematogenous dissemination and lie dormant for years at a particular site before causing disease.7 Manifestations may relate to the system involved, or may be nonspecific and non-localizing, such as prolonged fevers and night sweats.7 Conventional mycobacterial cultures require extended incubation time, and although many rapid molecular diagnostics have been approved these assays have limited applicability to the diagnosis of EPT.10,11 Although most individuals infected with MTC are completely asymptomatic, and among symptomatic individuals MTC infection typically presents as a primary pulmonary infection; MTC can affect almost any organ in the body, and physicians often rely on clinical judgment to identify and treat EPT appropriately. Tuberculosis also may present as a disseminated process (also known as miliary disease), or as EPT concurrent with pulmonary involvement.7 The most common sites of EPT are lymph nodes, pleura, genitourinary tract, bones and joints, meninges and the central nervous system, peritoneum and other abdominal organs.6 The aim of this retrospective study was to understand and evaluate the process of diagnosis and treatment and examine outcomes of patients with extrapulmonary tuberculosis in and around Islamabad, a high burden region for MTC infections in Pakistan.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Pakistan Ministry of Health Services National Tuberculosis Control Program (NTCP). Consecutive patients evaluated and treated for extrapulmonary tuberculosis at the Department of Infectious Diseases, Pakistan Institute of Medical Sciences (PIMS), Islamabad, between January 1 and May 31, 2016 were eligible for inclusion. Patients with concurrent pulmonary TB were excluded.

Extrapulmonary TB was defined as TB involving any nonpulmonary site, including lymph nodes, pleura, abdomen, genitourinary tract, musculoskeletal system, or meninges. All patients received free anti-tuberculous therapy through the NTCP under the Directly Observed Therapy, Short-course (DOTS) program. Standard first-line therapy consisted of isoniazid (5 mg/kg), rifampicin (10 mg/kg), ethambutol (15 mg/kg), and pyrazinamide (25 mg/kg), followed by continuation therapy with isoniazid and rifampicin for 6–15 months based on disease site and clinician judgment. Drug susceptibility testing was not performed.

Diagnosis was based on WHO recommendations and categorized into two groups:

Laboratory-supported diagnosis, defined by microbiologic, histologic, or biochemical evidence (e.g., lymphocytic exudative effusions, CSF analysis, histology with caseating granulomas, or QuantiFERON-TB™ Gold), and

Laboratory-supported diagnosis, defined by microbiologic, histologic, or biochemical evidence (e.g., lymphocytic exudative effusions, CSF analysis, histology with caseating granulomas, or QuantiFERON-TB™ Gold), and

Clinical diagnosis, defined by compatible symptoms and radiologic findings without laboratory confirmation.

Clinical diagnosis, defined by compatible symptoms and radiologic findings without laboratory confirmation.

Appropriate samples were obtained according to disease site. Biopsies and aspirates were examined by smear, culture, and histopathology when feasible. Radiologic criteria were used for diagnosis of peritoneal, musculoskeletal, and CNS TB when invasive sampling was not possible. In the absence of culture confirmation, diagnosis relied on clinician judgment, exclusion of alternative diagnoses, and response to therapy.

Patients were followed primarily as outpatients, with follow-up extending through May 2017. Outcomes were classified as treatment success (symptom resolution or treatment completion), failure (persistent symptoms or non-adherence), loss to follow-up, or death.

Demographic and clinical data were obtained from hospital records and NTCP registries. Statistical analysis was performed using SAS 9.4 and Microsoft Excel 2010. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline demographic and clinical characteristics. Categorical variables were compared between diagnostic groups using the chi-square test, with statistical significance defined as a two-sided p-value <0.05. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.

Results

We evaluated 317 EPTB cases from medical records and outpatient reports between January 2016 and May 2016. Of 317 patients, 42 were excluded from the analysis: 16 patients were lost to follow up, and 26 patients failed to complete treatment (Fig. 1). The majority of patients that failed to complete treatment belonged to the laboratory diagnosis group (18/26–69%), while 31% (8/26) belonged to the clinically diagnosed group. Final analysis was carried out on 275 patients. Demographics were similar between patients diagnosed by clinical symptoms and lab tests (see Table 1). The overall male to female ratio was 1.3:1. The mean age of patients was 34.4 (standard deviation 17.2) years with a range of 12–90. The mean age for males in the study was 30 years, compared to 37 in females. All patients were born in Pakistan: 30.3% (78/275) were permanent residents of Islamabad, 14.2% (39/275) from peripheries and towns surrounding Islamabad including, 25.4% (70/275) from Rawalpindi, 15.6% (43/275) from other cities of Punjab province, 10.5% (29/275) from Khyber Pakhtunkhwa Province, and 5.8% (16/275) from Azad Jammu Kashmir. 31.6% (87/275) of patients had a known history of TB exposure from a household contact. 2.9% (8/275) of patients were HIV positive at the start of treatment for MTC infection. HIV serocon-version was not detected for any patients in our cohort while undergoing treatment for MTC infection, and neither obvious risk factors for HIV infection nor HIV associated manifestations or AIDS defining diseases were reported during treatment. As depicted in Fig. 2, the most common sites of EPT were the pleura and lymph nodes, which accounted for a combined 49.8% (137/275) of cases, followed by musculoskeletal (14.9%), peritoneal (11.3%), and CNS (10.9%), respectively. Among patients with MTC infection of the lymph nodes, the cervical (63%) region was most commonly affected, followed by mediastinum (13.8%), sub-mandibular (6.9%) and supraclavicular (6.9%). The most common site involved in musculoskeletal TB was the lumbar spine, i.e. Pott’s disease (32%). 5.4% (15/275) patients with EPT had multi-organ involvement. Patients were distributed to two groups: 1) laboratory supported diagnosis of MTC infection (162 pts), and 2) diagnosis based on clinical criteria and/or imaging suggestive of MTC infection (113 pts). 79.6 % (129/162) of patients with laboratory evidence of MTC infection had clinical resolution of symptoms on follow-up. Among patients diagnosed based on clinical criteria, 87.6% (99/113) had clinical resolution of symptoms on follow-up. As shown in Fig. 3, patients with clinical diagnosis seemed more likely to be cured or have no residual symptoms after treatment than those with lab diagnosis. This was not statistically significant however there was a trend for higher rate of improvement in clinical group (OR = 1.8, 95% CI: 0.7–4.8 p = 0.221). The cure rate was 82.9% (228/275) amongst those patients who completed follow up. Mortality was 5.3% in the clinical diagnosis group compared to 9.3% in the laboratory-confirmed group (Odds ratio = 0.29, 95% CI: 0.086–0.950, p = 0.041). The success of treatment and mortality was similar across genders (Table 2). 83% of males and females underwent successful treatment while 17% failed treatment. Similarly, overall mortality was 8% (7.6% for males and 7.7% for females) (Table 2). The average age of death among men (N = 12) was 58 years, as opposed to 27.7 years in women (N = 9).

Discussion

TB is one of the leading causes of mortality and morbidity in the developing world. Globally, there were an estimated 10 million new cases of TB disease in 2018, of which 7 million were reported to the WHO, including 4.48 million (64%) with a documented HIV test result (477,461 were HIV positive).3 The absolute number of TB deaths (excluding TB deaths among HIV positive people) fell from 1.8 million in 2000 to 1.4 million in 2015.12 The global reduction in the total number of TB deaths between 2015 and 2018 was an additional 11%.3 Pakistan is among the list of six countries that account for 60% of the global TB burden.12 While pulmonary TB has a classical presentation with productive cough ± hemoptysis, weight loss, fevers and night sweats, the presentation of EPT varies from case to case, depending on the site involved and duration of disease. EPT can go undiagnosed until the disease has progressed to an advanced stage or disseminated. The primary site of infection of tuberculosis is lung parenchyma and the hilar lymph nodes. It spreads by lymphatic system or blood system and can infect any organ. Following infection Mycobacterium tuberculosis remains dormant in the host for a variable amount of time and reactivates when the conditions are optimal. Clinical presentation depends on the site of infection, the bacterial load and the immune status of the host.7 Tuberculosis can also involve more than one organ. 5.5% of the EPT cases in our study cohort had multiorgan involvement. Our results showed a higher number of male cases as compared to female cases 1.35:1. This ratio is inconsistent with other studies of MTC infection and EPT from around the world. A previous study conducted by Chandir et al. showed that more women were affected by MTC disease compared to men, and female gender is one of the recognized risk factors for EPT in the USA.8,13 Data from European Union and Turkey also indicate similar results.1,12 Although socioeconomic status, body mass index and other variables were not studied in these cases, we postulate that we have a larger number of cases in men mainly because of under reporting of the disease in women and more occupational related exposures to disease in men. Because poor workers and laborers coming to cities to find work often commonly live in close quarters with other laborers and work in overcrowded workplaces and factories which increases their risk of exposure to disease, a larger number of men (who are disproportionately represented among laborers) may be exposed to infected patients in occupational settings. Unfortunately, we did not have access to exposure data, the diagnosis of EPT was based on several approaches referenced earlier. Most of the patients in our study (59%) were diagnosed with EPT based on laboratory criteria (i.e., either microbiologic, histologic, or biochemical tests). Biochemical tests included pleural, pericardial, cerebrospinal fluid, ascitic fluid analysis (elevated protein, low glucose, lymphocytic pleocytosis), and QuantiFERON-TB™ Gold (Qiagen). The most common site involved was pleural followed by lymph nodes. Studies from India, Nepal, and Netherlands show that the lymphatics are the most common site of EPT infections 6,7,14–16 129 (79.6%) patients in this group had clinical resolution of symptoms on follow-up, which is well below the WHO target of 90% treatment success rate.17 However, treatment rates of EPT are not well documented in general and according to Kulchavenya et al. the actual success rate is 81.4% 18 113 (41%) patients in our study were diagnosed based on clinical criteria which included clinical signs and symptoms and radiological evidence of disease. 99 (87.6%) of these patients had resolution of symptoms on follow-up, which is much closer to the 90% target success rate recommended by WHO and reported by and others 17,19,20 26 patients (9.45 %) failed to complete the treatment and 16 were lost to follow up. Losses were mostly due to patients coming from farfetched areas of Punjab, and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and then relying on their local primary care doctors after the diagnosis was made. Out of these 26 patients, 18 (70%) belonged to the lab diagnostic group while 8 belonged to the clinical group. It remains unclear why there was a difference in success rate and mortality in the two groups. Future studies should be designed to assign this difference. Treatment starting at an earlier stage of disease and persistent adherence to therapy in the clinical group can also explain the difference. Treatment failures could also be due to resistant bacteria, missed diagnosis, and non-tuberculous mycobacteria infections that were misdiagnosed as MTC infection. Eight (2.9%) of the patients were HIV positive. Although there is a strong correlation between HIV and EPT,21 it cannot be demonstrated in our cohort because screening for HIV infection is not mandatory for EPTB patients in Pakistan. The HIV status in the rest of the patients was either negative or unknown. The average age of death in males was 58 years versus 27.7 years in females. The ratio of success of and failure of treatment was similar across both genders, and overall mortality ratio was also similar between genders (Table 2). The lower average age of death in women appears to be a reflection of the study population, which clearly skews young for woman in our cohort, but could also be related to delays in seeking medical care in younger female patients due to social barriers such as traditional gender based childcare expectations and responsibilities. The limitations of our study were the retrospective design, limited population sample, and possibility of recall bias (e.g., time from symptom onset to start of treatment was not available), since some study data was collected by interviewing the patients at the end of the survey period. Moreover, treatment was variable (as some retreatment patients may have been started on streptomycin) and onset of symptoms to start of treatment was unclear and variable duration of treatment could have also influenced outcomes. Our study suggests that greater effort needs to be made towards early clinical recognition and treatment of EPT, particularly among Pakistani women, as this may improve survival and decrease morbidity. The documentation of cases of mortality in young women with EPT in Pakistan is extremely alarming. Outreach to women across Pakistan must become a priority of the health ministry, and more women need to be included in future studies to identify the unique risks and most effective diagnostic and treatment interventions for women with EPT. Finally, National guidelines need to be established to diagnose and treat EPT earlier as early diagnosis and initiation of appropriate treatment are the keys for reducing morbidity and mortality in patients with EPT. EPT is an important cause of morbidity and mortality in Pakistan, yet neither a national containment strategy nor treatment guidelines have been developed for Pakistani patients with EPT. A comprehensive large-scale study is needed to determine the true burden of disease, optimal treatment period, and effectiveness of various antimicrobial drug combinations.

Conclusions

Early empiric therapy for extrapulmonary tuberculosis (EPTB) in high-burden, resource-limited settings may be associated with improved outcomes compared with delaying treatment for laboratory confirmation. Although the difference did not reach statistical significance, a trend toward improved outcomes was observed among patients treated based on clinical diagnosis. These findings suggest that prompt empiric treatment in appropriate clinical contexts may be beneficial and warrant further investigation in larger prospective studies.
