Differential MicroRNA Expression in Chronic Spontaneous Urticaria in Relation to Treatment Response
Authors: Youngsoo Lee, Jin Young Noh, Jiwon Yoon, Boyoun Choi, JungMo Kim, Hyun Goo Woo, Young-Min Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeChronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) is a mast cell-driven skin disorder characterized by a complex pathogenesis involving immune dysregulation and inflammation. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) have emerged as critical post-transcriptional regulators in various diseases. This study aimed to investigate differentially expressed serum miRNAs in CSU and its association with treatment response.MethodsThirty CSU patients (37.9 ± 9.8 years, 15 females) and 10 normal controls were enrolled. Patients were stratified by clinical response to H1-antihistamines (H1AHs) and omalizumab. Serum miRNAs were profiled using the Affymetrix GeneChip® miRNA 4.0 Array. Differentially expressed miRNAs were identified, and bioinformatic analyses were performed to predict target genes and assess pathway enrichment.ResultsEighteen miRNAs were differentially expressed between CSU patients and normal controls, with 10 upregulated and 8 downregulated in CSU. Compared to H1AH responders, 23 miRNAs were downregulated in H1AH nonresponders. In omalizumab-treated patients, 4 miRNAs, hsa-miR-503-5p, hsa-miR-1282, hsa-miR-93-5p, and hsa-miR-638, were reduced in complete responders. Bioinformatic analysis identified 3 hub genes (MYC proto-oncogene [MYC], cyclin D1 [CCND1], ribonucleotide reductase regulatory subunit M2) in H1AH nonresponse, and 14 key target genes, including heat shock protein family A (Hsp70) member 8 (HSPA8), CCND1, and E2F transcription factor 3, in omalizumab complete responders. Notably, hsa-miR-93-5p and its predicted target CCND1 were associated with both H1AH nonresponsiveness and omalizumab complete response.ConclusionsDistinct serum miRNA signatures are associated with treatment responses in CSU. In particular, miR-93-5p with network targets, including MYC, CCND1, and HSPA8, indicates shared regulatory pathways underlying H1AH refractoriness and omalizumab responsiveness. These findings offer mechanistic insights into CSU pathogenesis and support a personalized approach to treatment.

INTRODUCTION

Chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) is a debilitating skin disorder characterized by spontaneous, recurrent episodes of wheals, angioedema, or both, lasting for 6 weeks or longer.1 Although its clinical presentation is well defined, CSU exhibits considerable heterogeneity in disease course, severity, and treatment response.23 The underlying pathogenesis involves a complex interplay of immune dysregulation, type I and type IIb autoimmunity, mast cell and basophil activation, and aberrant cytokine and chemokine signaling.34 Despite advances in therapeutic strategies, including a stepwise approach from H1-antihistamines (H1AHs) to omalizumab and immunosuppressants, a substantial subset of patients remains refractory.25 This underscores the need for a deeper understanding of disease mechanisms and the development of more targeted therapeutic interventions.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are a class of small non-coding RNAs, typically 18–25 nucleotides in length, that have emerged as key post-transcriptional regulators of gene expression.6 By binding to complementary sequences in target messenger RNAs (mRNAs), miRNAs induce translational repression or mRNA degradation, thereby exerting fine control over protein synthesis.7 They play crucial roles in immune cell differentiation, cytokine signaling, apoptosis, and inflammation. Dysregulated miRNA expression has been implicated in a range of immune-mediated disorders, including asthma, atopic dermatitis, allergic rhinitis, and systemic autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus and rheumatoid arthritis.891011 These findings suggest that miRNAs may contribute to disease pathogenesis and serve as potential diagnostic biomarkers or therapeutic targets. Given the central role of mast cells and immune dysregulation in CSU, miRNA-mediated regulation of gene networks may underlie the observed variability in disease phenotypes and treatment outcomes.

Several recent studies have investigated circulating miRNA profiles in CSU, identifying dysregulated miRNAs, such as miR-125a-5p,12 miR-155,1314 and miR-221,13 which are implicated in immune-related pathways including CCL17-mediated signaling, the Janus kinase/signaling transducer and activator of transcription pathway, and autoimmune mechanisms. However, miRNA expression patterns related to treatment response in CSU remain largely unexplored. Profiling miRNA expression based on the clinical response to H1AH or omalizumab may not only help identify predictive biomarkers for therapeutic stratification but also offer insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying CSU persistence or resolution.

Therefore, this study investigated differentially expressed serum miRNAs in patients with CSU, assessed their association with treatment response, and explored their functional relevance through integrated target gene prediction and pathway enrichment analyses.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study participants

Patients with CSU and normal controls (NCs) were recruited from the Department of Allergy and Clinical Immunology at a university hospital in Korea between January and May of 2018. Inclusion criteria for CSU were recurrent episodes of urticaria, with or without angioedema, persisting for 6 weeks or longer. Exclusion criteria included the use of systemic immunosuppressants within 3 months prior to enrollment. NCs had no history of allergic diseases, atopy, or other chronic medical conditions. Disease activity was evaluated using the Urticaria Activity Score over 7 days (UAS7).

Patients were stratified based on their clinical response to H1AH and omalizumab. H1AH nonresponders were defined as individuals who failed to achieve complete symptom control despite up-dosing H1AHs to 4 times the standard dose for ≥ 4 weeks. Of the 17 H1AH nonresponders, 15 were subsequently treated with omalizumab and evaluated for treatment response after ≥ 3 months. Omalizumab response was classified as follows: complete responders (UAS7 = 0 within 12 weeks), partial responders (≥ 50% reduction in UAS7 with residual symptoms), and nonresponders (< 50% reduction in UAS7 or minimal clinical improvement).

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Ajou University Medical Center (AJOUIRB-MDB-2017-221), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Sample collection and processing

Serum samples were collected from both 30 CSU patients and 10 NCs, and they were stored at −70°C until analysis. For CSU patients, samples were obtained at baseline prior to H1AH dose escalation or initiation of omalizumab treatment. Total miRNAs were extracted from 100 μL of serum using the QIAGEN miRNeasy serum/plasma kit. Synthetic Caenorhabditis elegans miR-39-3p (Cel-39-3p) was spiked into each sample as an internal control for normalization.

MiRNA profiling

The expression of miRNA was analyzed using the Affymetrix GeneChip® miRNA 4.0 Array (Homo sapiens), which includes 2,578 probes for mature human miRNAs. Microarray hybridization and scanning were conducted at Macrogen, Inc. (Seoul, Korea) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Raw data were processed using Transcriptome Analysis Console software (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), which included background correction and normalization. Differentially expressed miRNAs (DEMs) were identified using a fold difference ≥ 2 and P < 0.05 for comparisons between CSU patients and NCs. For analyses based on treatment responses to H1AH and omalizumab, a less stringent threshold of fold difference ≥ 1.5 and P < 0.05 was used to detect potentially meaningful differences while minimizing the risk of type II error, given the smaller subgroup sizes. Because of the exploratory nature of the study and limited sample size, false discovery rate correction was not applied. The reported P values are nominal and should be interpreted descriptively.

Target gene prediction and functional analysis

Validated target genes for each DEM were retrieved using the multiMiR R package (version 2.4). Only target genes with experimentally validated interactions supported by at least 2 curated databases (e.g., miRTarBase, TarBase, and miRecords) were retained for downstream analyses.

Using Cytoscape v3.10.1, miRNA-mRNA interaction networks were visualized. Functional enrichment analysis (Gene Ontology [GO] terms and KEGG pathways) was performed using g:Profiler (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/gprofiler) with Benjamini-Hochberg correction (adjusted P < 0.05). Protein-protein interaction networks were explored with STRING v12.0 (https://string-db.org), applying a combined confidence score ≥ 0.40 to retain medium- or higher-confidence associations.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean. Normality was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Group differences were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test or one-way analysis of variance, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics v29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and a P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Demographic, laboratory, and clinical characteristics

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the 30 CSU patients and 10 NCs are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. There were no significant differences in age (37.9 ± 9.8 vs. 37.7 ± 10.2 years) or sex distribution (50% females in both groups). However, total serum immunoglobulin E (IgE) levels were significantly higher in CSU patients compared to NCs (376.8 ± 508.0 vs. 76.0 ± 56.4 kU/L, P = 0.003).

The CSU group was further stratified by treatment response: 13 patients were classified as H1AH responders and 17 as nonresponders. Among the H1AH nonresponders, 6 patients achieved complete response (UAS7 = 0), whereas 9 showed partial improvement (UAS7 reduction ≥ 50%, but not reaching a score of 0) and one patient showed no response to omalizumab. Owing to the limited sample size, the partial and nonresponders were combined into a single group designated as noncomplete responders in the analysis (Supplementary Table S2). No significant differences were observed among these subgroups with respect to age, sex, total IgE levels, atopy status, disease duration, UAS7 scores, or the presence of angioedema.

Among these, 8 miRNAs (hsa-miR-16-5p, hsa-miR-3613-5p, hsa-miR-320a, hsa-miR-486-5p, hsa-miR-92a-3p, hsa-miR-320c, hsa-miR-4330, hsa-miR-320d) were significantly downregulated, whereas 10 miRNAs (hsa-miR-4440, hsa-miR-4497, hsa-miR-7108-5p, hsa-miR-297, hsa-miR-6800-5p, hsa-miR-4793-3p, hsa-miR-4467, hsa-miR-101-5p, hsa-miR-6821-5p, hsa-miR-1268b) were significantly upregulated in CSU patients compared to NCs (Table 1). Functional annotation of hub genes in omalizumab complete responders

A total of 14 hub genes were identified from miRNAs downregulated in omalizumab complete responders. GO Biological Process enrichment identified significant terms related to cell-cycle regulation, stress responses, and signal transduction (Supplementary Fig. S1B). STRING protein-protein interaction analysis revealed 3 distinct clusters (Fig. 4B). Cluster 1, centered on HSPA8 (Hsc70), included DNAJC10, ACTR2, and OCRL, and was enriched for cellular stress responses and chaperone-mediated folding. Cluster 2, comprising CCND1, E2F3, CDKN1A, and WEE1, represented canonical cell-cycle checkpoint control. Cluster 3, including SMAD7 and SKI, related to transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) signaling and transcriptional repression. Notably, CCND1 was shared between H1AH-refractory and omalizumab-responsive networks.

DISCUSSION

This study investigated miRNA profiles in CSU with a focus on their association with H1AH refractoriness and omalizumab responsiveness. Compared to healthy controls, we identified 18 DEMs in CSU, with 23 miRNAs significantly downregulated in H1AH nonresponders. Among these, 9 miRNAs, including miR-93-5p, were validated across multiple prediction databases, leading to 8 hub genes. STRING analysis revealed functional interactions among MYC, CCND1, and RRM2, suggesting their potential involvement in sustained mast cell activation in H1AH-refractory CSU.

For omalizumab response, 4 downregulated miRNAs were identified in complete responders, with miR-93-5p and miR-503-5p prioritized for their extensive target networks. Notably, miR-93-5p was consistently downregulated in both H1AH nonresponders and omalizumab complete responders, suggesting its involvement in FcεRI downstream pathways. Network analysis identified MYC, CCND1, and HSPA8 as key targets of miR-93-5p, implicating this miRNA in the regulation of cell-cycle progression, cellular stress adaptation, and protein homeostasis, processed critical for mast-cell activation and survival. Clinically, these findings suggest that reduced circulating miR-93-5p may reflect a highly activated mast-cell state, serving as a potential biomarker of H1AH treatment responsiveness. Moreover, the association of miR-93-5p with pathways such as PI3K/Akt and chaperone-mediated stress regulation implies that modulation of these networks could influence omalizumab efficacy by attenuating mast-cell reactivity.15 However, reliable tools to stratify CSU patients based on response potential are still lacking.

Mast cells play a central role in CSU pathogenesis through persistent activation of multiple receptor-mediated signaling pathways. Previous studies have demonstrated that miRNAs regulate various aspects of mast cell biology, including differentiation, proliferation, and effector functions.16 For example, miR-221/222 modulate the cell cycle via NFAT and NF-κB pathways,17 miR-539 and miR-381 influence c-Kit-mediated proliferation,18 and miR-146a promotes apoptosis by suppressing NF-κB signaling.19 Other miRNAs, such as miR-221, miR-142-3p, and miR-101-5p, have been implicated in mast cell degranulation through FcεRIα- or PAF-mediated pathways.1620 However, the miRNA-mRNA regulatory networks identified in our study differ in part from those reported previously, likely due to methodological and biological heterogeneity across studies, including variations in sample type (serum vs. plasma, tissue or extracellular vesicles [EVs]), analytic platforms, and patient stratification criteria.

Our findings extend these observations by suggesting that downregulation of specific miRNAs in H1AH nonresponders may enhance mast cell proliferation and mediator release. Zhang et al.12 found elevated serum miR-125a-5p, a molecule upregulated in allergic diseases and autoimmune conditions, such as psoriasis and rheumatoid arthritis, which in CSU was associated with higher IgE, C-reactive protein, anti-thyroid peroxidase, and interleukin (IL)31 levels.13 Lin et al.21 reported increased expression of miR-2355-3p, miR-2355-5p, and other miRNAs in patients with active wheals and anti-FcεR antibodies. Differences between our findings and those of the earlier reports may reflect variations in ethnicity, study design, analytical platforms, or the type of biological samples analyzed.

In H1AH nonresponders, MYC, CCND1, and RRM2 have emerged as hub genes. MYC is a master transcription factor that regulates cell proliferation, survival, and metabolism downstream of FcεRI crosslinking, c-Kit, Toll-like receptor 4, PAF, and cytokine signaling, through pathways such as PI3K/Akt, MAPK/ERK, and NF-kB.22 CCND1, a MYC target, promotes cell cycle progression at G1/S checkpoint and enhances mast cell proliferation.23 RRM2, another gene regulated by MYC, supports DNA synthesis and granule biogenesis and mediator secretion.24 Upregulation of these genes may sustain mast cell proliferation, survival, and effector functions, contributing to the persistent activation observed in H1AH-refractory CSU. Interestingly, in omalizumab complete responders, CCND1 appeared within a cell cycle regulatory cluster along with CDKN1A, E2F3, and WEE1, genes involved in cell cycle checkpoint control. This may indicate a restored feedback mechanism following mast cell stabilization by omalizumab. These findings suggest that omalizumab, beyond its role in blocking FcεRI-mediated activation, might contribute to the re-establishment of cell cycle regulatory pathways, potentially limiting mast cell expansion and promoting disease remission in CSU.

A recent study analyzing EV-derived miRNAs identified distinct signatures between omalizumab responders and nonresponders,25 whereas our serum-based analysis revealed a unique expression pattern. This discrepancy underscores the need for larger, comparative studies to evaluate the utility of EV- versus serum-derived miRNAs as predictive biomarkers. In our network analysis, HSPA8 emerged as the most central hub gene in omalizumab complete responders. As a constitutively expressed heat shock protein 70 family, HSPA8 maintains protein quality control, supports antigen processing, and modulates immune responses.26 Elevated Hsp70 levels and anti-Hsp70 antibodies have been reported in CSU, particularly in moderate-to-severe disease.2627 HSPA8 is known to function not only as a molecular chaperone maintaining proteostasis but also as a cytokine-like mediator capable of inducing tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 production in monocytes.26 Given that mast cells are metabolically active and highly sensitive to stress signals, the predicted upregulation of HSPA8, based on the miRNA profile, may indicate a baseline cellular state that is more amenable to anti-IgE-mediated mast cell stabilization. This suggests that HSPA8-associated pathways could serve as the biomarkers of omalizumab responsiveness and potential targets for modulating mast cell activity in CSU.

In addition, SMAD7 and SKI, which are negative regulators of TGF-β signaling, were predicted in omalizumab complete responders. Their predicted upregulation may represent restored TGF-β-mediated immune regulation, enhancing regulatory T cell function and suppressing IL-6 and TNF-α. Notably, while TGF-β mRNA expression in CSU patient PBMCs has been correlated with disease activity, soluble TGF-β protein levels did not differ between patients and controls nor correlate with UAS7.28 This suggests that transcriptional upregulation of TGF-β does not necessarily translate to measurable plasma protein levels, likely due to complex activation and latency mechanisms. In this context, the presence of SMAD7 and SKI in our omalizumab responder network may reflect restored TGF-β signaling regulation rather than absolute cytokine abundance, contributing to immune homeostasis and favorable anti-IgE treatment response.

Finally, the KIF23-DCTN5-UBFD1 module in omalizumab complete responders suggests coordinated regulation of intracellular transport, vesicle trafficking, and protein degradation.293031 KIF23, a kinesin motor protein, has been implicated in immune cell proliferation and cytokine signaling in asthma and autoimmune conditions, though its role in CSU remains unexplored.29 DCTN5, part of the dynactin complex, may regulate granule mobilization and FcεRI-related signaling in mast cells.30 UBFD1 is involved in endoplasmic reticulum-associated degradation and proteostasis maintenance, potentially contributing to stress resolution.31 In refractory CSU, mast cells are highly secretory and stress-sensitive. The presence of this module in omalizumab complete responders suggests a shift toward a more quiescent and homeostatic mast cell phenotype that supports successful anti-IgE treatment.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the sample size was relatively small, particularly within treatment responder subgroups, post hoc power analysis indicated that the key miRNAs showing differential expression, miR-16-5p, miR-101-5p, and miR-93-5p, were detected with adequate statistical power. Specifically, miR-16-5p and miR-101-5p (CSU vs. NC) exhibited large, standardized effects (Cohen’s d ≈ 5.1 and 1.8, respectively; power > 0.95), while miR-93-5p showed consistent large effects in both H1AH (d ≈ 1.0; power ≈ 0.84) and omalizumab response comparisons (d ≈ 1.2; power ≈ 0.78). These results suggest that the principal signals were robustly detected despite the limited sample size. However, smaller or subtler differences in miRNA expression may have remained undetected. Therefore, larger multicenter validation cohorts are warranted to confirm reproducibility across populations and to delineate the longitudinal dynamics of miRNA signatures in relation to treatment outcomes. Second, the reliance on bioinformatic predictions for miRNA-mRNA interactions, while appropriate for initial screening, requires experimental validation through functional assays to establish causal links with target gene expression and mast cell activation. Third, only UAS7 scores were available to assess disease activity, other patient-reported outcome measures, such as the urticaria control test or chronic urticaria quality of life questionnaire were not collected, which may limit the comprehensiveness of disease control assessment. Fourth, the cross-sectional design precluded assessment of longitudinal changes in miRNA profiles, leaving it unclear whether these are stable markers or vary with disease activity and treatment response. Finally, potential confounders, including comorbidities, medication history, and genetic variation, were not fully controlled and may have influenced miRNA expression.

In conclusion, this study provides preliminary insights into the molecular mechanisms of CSU, implicating miR-93-5p, MYC, and HSPA8 in H1AH refractoriness and omalizumab response. The identified miRNA expression patterns may help stratify patients into treatment-responsive and nonresponsive groups, enabling more personalized therapy while avoiding ineffective interventions. Dysregulated miRNAs may also reflect broader changes in immune cell dynamics, cytokine signaling, and mast cell activity, all of which are central processes in CSU pathophysiology. These findings lay the groundwork for future predictive biomarker development and may inform novel therapeutic targets for CSU management.
