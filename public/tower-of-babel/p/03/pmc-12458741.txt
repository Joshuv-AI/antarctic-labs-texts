Use, knowledge and perception of large language models in clinical practice: a cross-sectional mixed-methods survey among clinicians in Switzerland
Authors: Simon Bruno Egli, Armon Arpagaus, Simon Adrian Amacher, Sabina Hunziker, Stefano Bassetti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesLarge language model (LLM)-based tools offer potential for clinical practice but raise concerns regarding output accuracy, patient safety and data security. We aimed to assess Swiss clinicians’ use, knowledge and perception of LLMs and identify associated factors.MethodsAn anonymous online survey was distributed via 34 medical societies in Switzerland. The primary outcome was frequent use of LLMs (at least weekly use). The secondary outcome was higher knowledge regarding LLMs (score above the median in an 11-item test). Qualitative analysis explored clinicians’ perceptions of LLM-related opportunities and risks.ResultsAmong 685 participants (response rate 29.0%), 225 (32.8%) reported frequent use of LLMs, 25 (3.6%) reported having used a specific medical LLM and 42 (6%) reported the availability of workplace LLM guidelines. The median knowledge test score was 6 points (IQR 4–8 points). Multivariable analysis showed that younger age, male sex and research activity were significantly associated with frequent use and higher knowledge. Qualitative analysis identified administrative support, analytical assistance and access to information as key opportunities. The main risks identified were declining clinical skills, poor output quality and legal or ethical concerns.DiscussionThe study highlights a notable adoption of LLMs among Swiss clinicians, particularly among younger, male and research-active individuals. However, the limited availability of workplace guidelines raises concerns about safe and effective use.ConclusionThe gap between widespread LLM use and the scarcity of workplace guidelines underscores the need for accessible educational resources and clinical guidelines to mitigate potential risks and promote informed use.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Medical Informatics, Implementation Science

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are generative artificial intelligence (AI) models trained to process and generate language. Since the release of ChatGPT by OpenAI (San Francisco, California, USA) in November 2022, the risks and benefits of LLMs in medicine have been discussed extensively.1 2 LLM applications in clinical practice include evidence-based decision support, streamlining administrative tasks and patient communication.3 4 Furthermore, the importance of generative AI for medical education and preparing clinicians for its adoption has been emphasised.5

However, using LLMs has potential pitfalls, such as fabricating information (ie, ‘hallucinations’) and incorporating biases inherent to the training data.6 7 LLMs generate natural-sounding responses based on probabilistic patterns in large datasets rather than actual understanding. The term ‘stochastic parrot’ highlights this ability to mimic human language without genuine comprehension.8 Patient safety and data security are paramount in healthcare, further complicating the implementation of new technologies.9

Many clinicians and patients already use LLMs for medical or paramedical purposes. However, significant gaps remain in guidelines and regulatory frameworks for healthcare professionals using LLMs in clinical medicine and research. Continuous training for clinicians and medical students is crucial to help them appreciate both the opportunities and the risks posed by generative AI in medicine. This could empower them to play an active role in its development, evaluation and implementation.10 To achieve these goals, it is essential to provide resources that support informed decision-making, guide future digital health policies and enable successful integration of LLMs into medicine.

In this context, research on clinicians’ adoption of generative AI is critical. Our survey among Swiss clinicians aimed to identify factors associated with their use, knowledge and perception of LLMs. Additionally, it explored their views on potential opportunities and risks linked to integrating LLMs into clinical practice.

Methods

Survey administration and participants

We conducted a nationwide anonymous online survey among clinicians in Switzerland. We identified 51 societies representing all medical specialties and contacted each to disseminate the survey to members via email, newsletter, website or LinkedIn. A reminder was sent if no response was received. Participating societies are listed in online supplemental file 1.11

Data were collected using umfrageonline.ch, an online tool that facilitates survey distribution and completion while guaranteeing data privacy per the European Union’s General Data Protection Regulation. Clinicians working in Switzerland who fully completed the survey were eligible. The response rate was calculated by dividing completed surveys by the total survey views.

Questionnaire design

Best Practices for Survey Research guidelines12 and Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology criteria13 guided questionnaire development, following a three-step process:

Initial draft: two investigators (SBE and AA) prepared a draft based on the existing literature on LLMs in clinical practice, including a knowledge test on basic LLM principles and limitations. This test was iteratively refined for clarity and differentiation of knowledge levels, then piloted with 49 fifth-year medical students and non-medical professionals, following public involvement principles (online supplemental file 2).14

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was frequent general LLM use for any purpose. Frequency was assessed on a categorical scale: multiple times a day, daily, at least once per week, at least once per month, less often than once per month, never. For dichotomisation, frequent use was predefined as at least weekly use.

The key secondary outcome was higher knowledge of LLMs, assessed using an 11-item test, with scores ranging from 0 to 11 points. Higher knowledge was predefined as a score above the median for dichotomisation.

Baseline characteristics

Demographic and professional characteristics assessed were age, sex, language, workplace (hospital, private practice, other), hospital type, position, medical specialty, research activity and availability of workplace guidelines.

Qualitative assessment

Two free-text questions invited participants to share their views on LLMs’ potential opportunities and risks in clinical practice.

Data analysis

We used a mixed-methods approach with quantitative and qualitative analyses. Baseline characteristics and factors potentially related to clinicians’ perceptions of LLMs were stratified according to the primary and the secondary outcome. Categorical variables were compared with Pearson’s χ2 test or analysis of variance as appropriate. Continuous variables were analysed using t-tests after assessing normality via visual inspection of Q–Q plots. We transformed three continuous variables (attitude towards general use, perceived current usefulness, importance of AI knowledge) into deciles to achieve normal distribution. Additional analyses included: (1) univariable logistic regression to assess associations between factors and outcomes and (2) multivariable models adjusted for age, sex and university hospital status. A two-tailed p value<0.05 indicated significance. Quantitative analyses were performed in STATA V.15.0 (Stata, College Station, Texas, USA).

Qualitative analysis of answers to free-text questions followed an inductive thematic approach. Two investigators initially reviewed 10% of responses to establish a consensus on overall themes. Categories were iteratively refined into three key themes per question and represented in a figure.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of the 51 medical societies contacted, 34 (66.7%) agreed to distribute the survey. The survey was viewed 2360 times and data were collected from 817 participants between 18 April and 1 October 2024. Of these, 685 completed the survey (response rate 29.0%) and were included in the final analysis. Mean age (±SD) was 46.06 years (± 11.99), 398 (58.1%) of the participants were male and 511 (74.6%) were German speaking (table 1).

Primary outcome: frequency of general LLM use and multivariable associations with frequent use

Of the 685 participants, 475 (69.3%) had used an LLM for general purposes at least once and 225 (32.8%) were frequent users (at least weekly use) (figure 1).

Comparison of frequency of general and medical use with dichotomisation into frequent (at least weekly) and infrequent (less than weekly) users.

Demographic factors associated with lower odds of frequent use were older age (47.13±11.90 years vs 43.86±11.91 years, adjusted OR 0.97, 95% CI 0.96 to 0.99, p<0.001) and female sex (adjusted OR 0.50, 95% CI 0.35 to 0.71, p<0.001) (table 1). Professional factors associated with lower odds of frequent use were working in private practice (adjusted OR 0.63, 95% CI 0.41 to 0.96, p<0.04), a tertiary hospital (other than university hospitals) (adjusted OR 0.58, 95% CI 0.38 to 0.9, p<0.02) and a primary hospital (hospital category as reported by participants, a link to the official categorisation by the Federal Office of Public Health was provided for guidance) (adjusted OR 0.5, 95% CI 0.28 to 0.9, p=0.02).

Professional factors associated with higher odds of frequent use were working in workplaces other than hospitals or private practices (adjusted OR 2.78, 95% CI 1.39 to 5.58, p=0.01), position as a consultant or head clinician (adjusted OR 2.53, 95% CI 1.16 to 5.51, p=0.02), research activity (adjusted OR 2.92, 95% CI 2.05 to 4.17, p<0.001) and availability of LLM guidelines in the workplace (adjusted OR 5.72, 95% CI 2.82 to 11.6, p<0.001).

A positive attitude towards LLM use, perceived usefulness of LLMs and perceived importance of AI knowledge were associated with more frequent use.

Frequency and purpose of medical use of LLMs

Among the 685 participants, 390 (56.9%) reported using an LLM for medical purposes at least once and 149 (21.8%) were frequent medical users (figure 1). Using LLMs as a knowledge resource was the most frequent purpose (182 participants, 26.6%), followed by scientific work (148 participants, 21.6%), teaching (143 participants, 20.9%), administration (142 participants, 20.7%), clinical reasoning and decision-making (102 participants, 14.9%) and communication with patients (98 participants, 14.3%) (figure 2A).

Use of LLMs as a knowledge resource was most prevalent among participants working in emergency and intensive care (37 participants, 32.7%). A higher proportion of participants from internal medicine-related specialties reported using LLMs for clinical reasoning and decision-making (52 participants, 20.3%) compared with those from other specialties (online supplemental file 5).

Models used for general and medical purposes

In total, 453 of 685 participants (66.1%) had used ChatGPT (OpenAI), 89 (13%) Bing Chat or Copilot (Microsoft, Redmond, Washington, USA), 49 (7%) Gemini or Bard (Google, Mountain View, California, USA) and 73 (10.7%) any other LLM (including Llama (Meta, Menlo Park, California, USA), Claude (Anthropic, San Francisco, California, USA), Le Chat (Mistral AI, Paris, France) and Perplexity (Perplexity AI, San Francisco, California, USA) (figure 2b). For medical purposes, 362 (52.8%) used ChatGPT (OpenAI), while no other LLM had been used by more than 40 participants. Notably, only 25 (3.6%) indicated having used any medical LLM (eg, OpenEvidence (Xyla, Cambridge, Massachusetts, USA), Med-PaLM (Google, Mountain View, California, USA), Meditron (École Polytechnique Fédérale de Lausanne, Lausanne, Switzerland).

Knowledge of LLMs and multivariable associations with higher knowledge

The median knowledge test score was 6 (IQR 4–8) points. Demographic and professional factors associated with lower odds of higher knowledge were older age (48.06±11.95 vs 43.68±11.62 years, adjusted OR 0.96, 95% CI 0.94 to 0.97, p<0.001), female sex (adjusted OR 0.32, 95% CI 0.23 to 0.46, p<0.001) and working in a tertiary hospital (other than university) (adjusted OR 0.64, 95% CI 0.41 to 0.98, p<0.05) (table 2).

Attitude towards the use of LLMs and multivariable associations with a positive attitude

Median VAS Score for the attitude towards the use of LLMs was 63 (IQR 45–76) points. Demographic and professional factors associated with lower odds of a positive attitude were older age, female sex and working in emergency and intensive care (online supplemental file 4).

The availability of LLM guidelines in the workplace was the only factor associated with a positive attitude.

An evaluation of participants’ perceptions of LLMs in clinical practice revealed an anticipated positive dynamic of their usefulness, a relatively high perceived importance of AI knowledge and, conversely, a low perceived preparedness for AI use (online supplemental file 6).

Qualitative analysis of LLMs’ opportunities and risks in clinical practice

The qualitative analysis showed that 446 of 685 participants (65%) identified at least one opportunity and 468 (68.3%) at least one risk associated with the use of LLMs in clinical practice. Three main themes emerged for both opportunities and risks, as summarised in figure 3.

Opportunities and risks of large language models as perceived by clinicians. Main themes were derived from free-text responses by iterative inductive thematic analysis.

The most frequently mentioned theme was organisation (63% of responses). Respondents emphasised the potential of LLMs to streamline documentation, simplify appointment scheduling and assist with administrative and routine tasks. Data assessment emerged as the second theme (49%), with responses focusing on the possible use of LLMs for analysis and interpretation, supporting clinical reasoning and decision-making, and bolstering research. The third key theme was information (30%), where participants underscored the role of LLMs in expediting knowledge availability and transfer, improving medical education, advancing patient education and facilitating patient–clinician interactions. Also, expected overarching benefits of LLM use were highlighted, including increased efficiency, improved quality, reduced errors and enhanced safety.

Concerns about user-related factors were the most frequently mentioned risks (54%). Respondents expressed worries about a potential decline in clinical knowledge, skills, linguistic competency and ingenuity, which could lead to clinician dependence on LLMs. Additionally, they raised concerns about the negative impact of LLMs on the patient–clinician relationship. Concerns about the models themselves ranked second (51%). Participants worried about the quality of model output, citing false information, hallucinations and bias that could result in incorrect diagnoses and treatment recommendations. Additionally, they voiced concerns about a lack of reproducibility and explainability of model output. The third theme focused on security, legal and ethical issues (35%), including data privacy risks, misuse and manipulation. Some respondents also considered the current legal and ethical frameworks for the use of AI in clinical practice to be inadequate. Finally, overarching perceived risks included insufficient control and unclear accountability.

Discussion

This comprehensive online survey examined Swiss clinicians’ use, knowledge and perception of LLMs one and a half years after the release of ChatGPT. Key findings were that many surveyed clinicians reported using LLMs for general and medical purposes, with age, male sex and research activity significantly associated with frequent use and higher knowledge. Conversely, guidelines for the use of LLMs were reported to be rarely available in clinicians’ workplaces. Combined with the qualitative analysis results – which identified administrative and analytical support as well as improved access to information as perceived opportunities, and declining clinical skills, poor output quality and legal or ethical concerns as perceived risks—this survey provides valuable insights into LLM adoption in clinical practice.

Over two-thirds of clinicians reported using LLMs for general purposes and over half for medical purposes, significantly more than UK general practitioners (20%)15 and US internal medicine residents (26%).16 Moreover, 22% were weekly or more frequent users, suggesting growing clinical adoption despite a lack of workplace guidelines. Interestingly, LLMs were most often used as a knowledge resource, while use for clinical reasoning and decision-making (eg, differential diagnosis) was less common, contrasting with other studies. Although the importance of medical LLM development has been emphasised early on,17 most clinicians reported using standard models, predominantly ChatGPT. This poses challenges due to the general purpose LLMs’ high variability, unreliability and tendency to convincingly present false information.7 Presumably, more reliable medical LLMs, such as OpenEvidence, are available,18 which incorporate retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)—a process in which the LLM retrieves relevant information from external sources (eg, PubMed) prior to generating a response. By grounding outputs in external, verifiable information, RAG helps reduce hallucinations.19

Younger age and male sex were associated with frequent use, higher knowledge and a positive attitude towards LLMs. Previous studies on the effect of age and sex on digital health technology adoption have shown mixed results.20,23 A recent Swiss survey found that younger individuals more often reported frequent LLM use and substantial knowledge.24 Economic research also noted an age-related digital divide in financial technology adoption and a generative AI sex gap.25,27 Consumer surveys suggested that privacy concerns and trust issues, perceived benefits and self-assessed knowledge influence these differences, with women often expressing greater concerns about personal data use.26 28 We hypothesise that younger and male clinicians’ positive attitude towards LLMs leads to more frequent use and deeper engagement with LLMs, fostering higher knowledge.

Furthermore, research activity was associated with frequent use and higher knowledge of LLMs but surprisingly not with a positive attitude. Although prior evidence linking clinicians’ research activity to the adoption of digital health technologies is limited, it is plausible that researchers are early adopters of LLMs due to their potential utility in scientific tasks.

The availability of workplace guidelines was associated with frequent use, higher knowledge and a positive attitude. Specifically, the presence of guidelines may indicate a supportive institutional environment for LLM adoption, enhance clinicians’ trust in the technology, provide clarity on appropriate use cases and contribute to a more positive perception.

Qualitative analysis identified three main themes for perceived opportunities and risks, aligning with those reported in previous studies.2 29 30

Administration and documentation support was the most common perceived opportunity, likely reflecting clinicians’ high administrative burden. The frequent mention of LLMs as a knowledge resource is notable, as few clinicians currently use medical-specific LLMs for information. However, the uncritical use of general purpose LLMs for clinical questions poses risks, as incorrect or ‘hallucinated’ information can be misleading. Medical LLMs, not yet approved by regulators, should be used cautiously with thorough background checks.

Key risks included loss of clinical skills, incorrect responses and data privacy violations. These findings suggest that while clinicians are experimenting with LLMs, many are aware of the current limitations. Whether this translates to responsible use is unclear.

Limitations

This survey, distributed via medical associations, included clinicians from various specialties but may not fully represent the Swiss medical community. Recruitment strategy may have introduced selection bias. However, it remains unclear whether clinicians with a favourable or unfavourable attitude towards LLMs were more inclined to participate in the survey. The survey focused exclusively on the clinicians’ perspective, excluding patient input. Finally, the knowledge assessment was based on a self-designed knowledge test which, despite iterative development and pre-testing, may not fully reflect participants’ actual knowledge.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that age and sex may influence the adoption of LLMs among clinicians and highlight a significant gap between widespread use and the availability of workplace guidelines. Notably, few clinicians reported using specialised medical LLMs that incorporate RAG to mitigate hallucinations. Easily accessible educational resources for clinicians at all levels and regularly updated workplace guidelines are warranted to facilitate LLM implementation, address knowledge disparities and prevent harm due to uninformed use. Training should focus on anticipated opportunities such as specific medical LLMs and provide strategies for maximising benefits while minimising risks. With LLMs becoming increasingly popular among medical students, their adoption is likely to accelerate with the next generation of clinicians.31 Educational efforts should emphasise responsible use during medical training. Further research is needed to monitor the adoption of LLMs and assess the impact in clinical practice.
