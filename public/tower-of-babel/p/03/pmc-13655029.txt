A High-Resolution Power Grid Frequency Dataset from Five Solar Sites Across England in April 2023
Authors: Maitreyee Dey, Clarke V Simmons
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFrequency is the pivotal indicator of power grid stability as it conveys the instantaneous balance between electrical supply and load demand. Around the world, the power frequency standard is either 50 or 60 Hz and maintaining it within a very narrow range is vital for reliable system operations. This paper presents the Power Grid Frequency Data (PGFD): a high-resolution UK grid frequency dataset collected from five solar sites across England over one month (April 2023). Unlike typical grid frequency data recorded at one-second intervals, this dataset captures frequency dynamics every 10 ms for a 100x improvement in temporal resolution. The dataset contains 1.3 billion data records, and it will help researchers: detect anomalies, transient events, frequency oscillations, assess frequency stability, better understand grid behaviour, and improve frequency control strategies. By making this dataset openly available, this work aims to support the energy transition to a low-carbon emissions future.

Background & Summary

Power frequency is the pivotal control parameter for alternating current (AC) electrical power grid systems. Commonly referred to as grid frequency, electrical frequency, AC frequency, or line frequency, it is nominally either 50 or 60 Hertz (Hz), depending on geographic region. Maintaining power frequency within a narrow (typically ±1%) tolerance is essential to proper electrical network operation. Frequency stability is a major operational concern for system operators, as frequency deviations and instability can disrupt performance, cause outages, and even damage equipment1,2.

Grid frequency is inherently linked to the dynamic balance of electricity supply and load demand. When power generation exceeds demand, the frequency increases; conversely, when demand exceeds generation, frequency drops. Any imbalance will cause a shift in frequency until a new equilibrium is reached.

The time derivative (df/dt) of frequency provides the Rate-of-Change-of-Frequency (RoCoF), and it conveys how severe the imbalance is. Certain types of electrical protection devices and grid controls are sensitised to RoCoF.

In practice, frequency and RoCoF values are not measured directly but are estimated from sampled voltage waveforms using signal processing techniques such as zero-crossing detection or Fourier-based methods. The accuracy of these estimates is primarily influenced by factors such as sampling rate, noise, and waveform distortion.

In the United Kingdom (UK), the standard grid frequency is 50 Hz3, with an operational range of ±0.2 Hz and a statutory limit of ±0.5 Hz above or below. There is also a deadband notch of ±0.015 Hz around the nominal frequency of 50 Hz within which frequency regulation is not required.

Several methods exist for estimating power system frequency from voltage waveforms4. One of the most widely used approaches in practical measurement systems is zero-crossing detection, which is computationally efficient and straightforward to implement5. However, zero-crossing methods are sensitive to waveform distortion and noise, which can introduce estimation errors6. More advanced techniques, such as Fourier transform-based methods, are computationally more intensive but provide improved accuracy and robustness under non-ideal waveform conditions7.

Frequency stability is essential to avoiding operational problems, including brownouts and blackouts. Monitoring and managing frequency is increasingly challenging for grid operators as electrical systems undergo the energy transition to a climate friendly, low-carbon emissions future8. Renewable energy generation and load variability, along with network operations and equipment failure, complicate frequency regulation and stability9. The increasing integration of inverter-based resources (e.g., wind, solar, EV charging, air-conditioning, and battery storage) reduces system inertia. As a result, maintaining the nominal grid frequency within established limits becomes more challenging

The UK’s new publicly owned National Energy System Operator (NESO) provides frequency data at a temporal resolution of one sample-per-second (SPS) or 1 Hz. Which offers a comparatively coarse view of system stability10. Under IEEE performance standard C37.118, Phasor Measurement Units (PMU) can provide finer grained, time-synchronised frequency data.

The Power Grid Frequency Data (PGFD) empirical dataset presented in this paper offers microPMU (microsynchrophasor) derived frequency measurements at 2 samples per AC cycle (i.e. every 10 ms or 100 SPS on a 50 Hz system). Which is 100x higher temporal resolution than NESO’s publicly available data. The time-synchronised data is collected from five solar sites across England. The resulting high-quality dataset contains 8.64 million data records per site per day or a total of 1.3 billion data records across the five sites for the month of April 2023.

Capturing finer-grained, high-precision frequency measurements should enable the detection of subtle network fluctuations and transient events not visible from lower-resolution data. PGFD may help researchers better understand frequency instability and other electrical behaviour. It may also support the development of advanced monitoring, control, and mitigation strategies. By providing high-resolution empirical data, PGFD enables a more granular investigation into the challenges of frequency regulation and contributes to improving grid reliability in an era of low-carbon technology adoption. With the PGFD dataset, stakeholders can investigate the interplay of grid dynamics, develop anomaly detection methods, and design improved control strategies for a decarbonised grid. By capturing the complexities of real operational environments, this dataset can serves as an open-access resource for advancing power system research in low-inertia, renewable energy-dominated grids11.

Previous studies of power system behaviour have often relied on synthetic modelling approaches12,13. While such simulations enable controlled experimentation, including the injection of faults and stress-testing scenarios, they may not fully capture the complexity and variability of real-world grid dynamics14.

Open-access, high-resolution PMU-derived empirical frequency measurements remain limited15. This lack of accessible, high-quality datasets constrains the development and validation of advanced grid analytics and diagnostic tools, particularly given that real-world disturbances are infrequent and often poorly documented16,17. In addition, sensor malfunctions or communication failures during critical events can introduce gaps and inconsistencies in recorded measurements.

Several existing initiatives provide access to power grid frequency measurements. GridRadar18 provides real-time frequency monitoring services, while the Frequency Monitoring Network (FNET)19 enables synchronised frequency measurements for research applications. Public platforms such as power-grid-frequency.org20 also offer openly accessible frequency data streams, primarily at second-level resolution. However, these resources typically provide lower temporal resolution or limited spatial coverage compared to the high-resolution, site-distributed dataset presented in this work.

Recent research has turned to data-driven machine learning (ML) and other artificial intelligence (AI) techniques21. Deep learning and hybrid approaches have been employed to detect and classify frequency anomalies, with a focus on precise feature extraction and temporal modelling. However, the performance and generalization of AI/ML models depend heavily on the quality, realism, and resolution of the training data22,23. The availability of empirical datasets like PGFD should therefore prove helpful to developing data-driven AI/ML toolsets.

April 2023 represents a relevant and representative period for analysing UK grid frequency dynamics. According to the National Energy System Operator (NESO), approximately 46% of Britain’s electricity generation during this period was derived from zero-carbon sources. On April 20th, UK solar generation reached a then record high of 10.971 GW, while total electricity demand for the month was approximately 21 TWh, with peak load occurring on April 5th at around 18:00 h24. These conditions reflect a modern, renewable-rich power system, providing an appropriate context for interpreting the high-resolution frequency measurements presented in this dataset.

In summary, PGFD delivers a unique, high-resolution view of UK grid frequency dynamics under modern renewable-dominated operating conditions. By providing access to frequency measurements across five geographically dispersed sites, this dataset offers a valuable resource for understanding grid behaviour, analysing frequency stability, and developing advanced data-driven monitoring and control strategies.

Methods

Neuville Grid Data has been operating advanced electrical condition monitoring equipment at UK solar farms to improve the facilities’ reliability and performance since late 2019. The equipment is physically attached to the sites’ 11/33 kV distribution grid electrical point of connection. This enables it to simultaneously monitor the solar farm and the wider grid. Thus, providing the basis for collecting PGFD over a wide area.

Apparatus

Neuville engineers devised a patented apparatus, the Grid Data Unit (GDU)25, to collect electrical condition monitoring data. GDUs incorporate a microPMU (also referred to as a μPMU or microsynchrophasor), a power quality monitor (PQM), a Global Positioning System (GPS) receiver for time-synchronisation between sites, and secure bidirectional 3G/4G cellular data telemetry equipment. GDUs are typically installed in a substation. The GDU signal chain starts with analogue voltage and current signals from all three phases. The end result is a comprehensive set of time-synchronised power quality and phasor measurements. Onboard GDU edge-compute capabilities plus reference and buffering data storage support further local analytical processing.

The key GDU component for gathering the PGFD is a PQube3 microPMU from Powerside. The microPMU is compliant with the IEEE C37.118 standard but features much greater measurement precision to meet the needs of distribution network monitoring. With an underlying analogue-to-digital conversion (ADC) at 4 MHz, the microPMU’s digital signal processing (DSP) produces RMS voltage and current phasor values for each phase every half-cycle with a typical Total Vector Error (TVE) of ±0.01% (which is a 100x improvement on the ±1.0% TVE of conventional PMUs). On a 3-phase 50/60 Hz installation, the microPMU streams six channels (3 voltage +3 current) at 100/120 data frames per second per channel. These IEEE C37.118 PMU data frames include the GPS time-stamped frequency measurements extracted for the PGFD offering.

As shown in Fig. 3 workflow diagram, the frequency data was captured every 10 ms (100/120 Hz) and timestamped accordingly. This comparatively high temporal resolution allows for the detection and tracking of dynamic events. All timestamps are geosynchronised via GPS among sites and recorded in Greenwich Mean Time (GMT).

Overview of the data collection and processing workflow.

The two-field (tuple) data records (data-time, frequency) were first collected in a raw binary format with a separate 6,000 row file for each minute of data collection. Then 1,440 one-minute files were combined into a single file per day, containing a total of 8.64 million rows. Each row corresponds to a single frequency measurement captured precisely every 10 milliseconds. From the full set of recorded parameters, only the frequency estimate was extracted and saved into a new, merged PGFD file for further analysis.

Given the large volume of data and its big-data characteristics, the final dataset was stored in the more efficient, lossless compressed Parquet columnar format. Each site’s directory contains 30 daily Parquet files, making it straightforward for researchers to access, manipulate, and analyse the data.

This data processing workflow was applied consistently across all five solar sites and repeated for each day of the target month.

Ancillary Data

Ancillary NESO system frequency data files are also provided as an adjunct to the PGFD to facilitate comparative research and further cross-validation. They are relatively low-resolution with a one-second sampling rate and provide a single UK system-wide number. Twelve monthly NESO datasets are provided for the entire month of April for the years 2014 through 2025 (https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.30745268). April 2023 being the direct companion to PGFD. Alternately, live UK grid frequency data feeds are publicly available online from NESO and Elexon (Elexon manages Great Britain’s electricity markets). NESO’s website updates its system data every minute with a 4 minute delay – (internal NESO network control systems rely on more granular, near real-time information)29. Technical Validation

Pairwise Correlation

To cross-validate the raw frequency measurements, Fig. 4 provides a classic “pairwise” correlation matrix (pairplot). It shows a consistent relationships among the five locations: Site 1 (Kenninghall, Norfolk), Site 2 (Fen Road, Lincolnshire), Site 3 (Pond Farm, Norfolk), Site 4 (Lodge Farm, Milton Keynes), and Site 5 (Wroxton, Oxfordshire).

Statistical summary and inter-site correlation analysis of one-day frequency measurements obtained from Grid Data Units (GDUs) across the five solar farm locations.

The five sites are plotted in an identical format. On the diagonal are each site’s marginal distribution (orange histogram) with a bimodal spread near 49.7 and 50.3 Hz. Off the diagonal are pairwise scatter plots (blue dots) with the identity line y = x in magenta. In each panel, the bold number is the Pearson r. Every off-diagonal cell reads r = 1.00, and the blue scatter clouds collapse tightly onto the y = x line. The diagonal histograms likewise generally align in shape and location, centring on the same twin peaks near 49.7 and 50.3 Hz.

As expected, these sites move almost in lock-step with regard to their grid connection power frequency. All five exhibit generally the same distribution and inter-site correlation to within machine precision. Thus validating both the measurement accuracy of the GDU and the homogeneity of the UK distribution network around its nominal 50 Hz for the particular day in question. Overall, this demonstrates reproducibility and consistency.

Although the five sites follow an almost identical behavioural pattern, Site 2 (Fen Road) exhibits a noticeably narrower and more tightly centred frequency distribution compared with the other four locations. While the remaining sites show the characteristic bimodal structure around 49.7 Hz and 50.3 Hz, Site 2’s histogram is more compact, with reduced spread and fewer excursions toward the distribution tails. This indicates that, for the particular day analysed, the Fen Road site operated with exceptionally stable frequency measurements, remaining well within the operational target range for a larger proportion of the day than the other sites. This subtle distinction reinforces the sensitivity of the GDU sensors in capturing site-specific behaviour, even when overall inter-site correlations remain effectively perfect.

Comparison with NESO data

To further validate the consistency of the PGFD dataset, a comparison was conducted with the NESO system frequency measurements for the same sample period. The NESO data provides a lower-resolution (1-second) system-wide frequency reference, which enables external validation of the high-resolution PGFD measurements.

Figure 5 presents a representative comparison between the NESO and PGFD datasets over a 5-minute interval in 1st April 2023. Both datasets exhibit consistent overall frequency trends and excursions, indicating strong agreement at the system level. However, the higher temporal resolution of PGFD (10 ms) reveals additional fine-scale fluctuations and short-duration variations that are not observable in the NESO data.

Usage Notes

The PGFD dataset is provided in structured CSV format, with separate files for each measurement site and day. Each record contains timestamped frequency measurements at a resolution of 10 ms, enabling high-resolution time series analysis.

The data can be readily accessed and processed using common programming environments such as Python (e.g., pandas) and MATLAB. Due to the high temporal resolution, users may consider downsampling or windowing techniques for specific analytical tasks.

Potential applications of the dataset include frequency stability assessment, anomaly detection, event classification, and the development of data-driven models for low-inertia power systems. The accompanying ancillary NESO dataset may be used for system-level comparison and validation purposes.

Users should be aware of the high sampling rate and corresponding data volume when performing large-scale analysis.
