Frailty Among Patients With ADPKD
Authors: Jui Wang, Szu-Ying Lee, Chia-Ter Chao, Kuo-Liong Chien, Chih-Kang Chiang, Kuan-Yu Hung, Jenq-Wen Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAutosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) is a major inherited cause of end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), associated with various systemic complications. Frailty, a degenerative condition linked to adverse health outcomes, may be influenced by ADPKD and its complications; however, data on this association is limited. This study investigated whether ADPKD is associated with increased risk of incident or worsening frailty.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study, using the National Taiwan University Hospital Integrate Medical Database (2006–2021). Adults with ADPKD or simple kidney cysts (SKCs) were identified. After applying exclusion criteria and 1:4 propensity score (PS) matching, 775 patients with ADPKD and 3100 matched SKC controls were included. Frailty status was assessed using the FRAIL scale. Kaplan-Meier curves and Cox proportional hazards were used to evaluate associations with incident and worsening frailty, adjusting for clinical variables. Competing risk analysis accounting for mortality or ESKD was performed.ResultsOver 4.8 years of follow-up, 88 participants (2.3%) developed incident frailty and 810 (20.9%) experienced worsening frailty. ADPKD was not significantly associated with higher risk of incident frailty (hazard ratio [HR]: 1.20, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.69–2.08) or worsening frailty (HR: 0.98, 95% CI: 0.82–1.17). Results remained consistent across subgroups stratified by age, kidney function, and baseline frailty, and after accounting for competing mortality or ESKD.ConclusionIn this matched cohort study, ADPKD was not associated with an increased risk of incident or worsening frailty. These findings suggest that ADPKD may not independently drive functional decline beyond its impact on kidney function.Keywords: aging, autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease, chronic kidney disease, frailty, longitudinal cohort study, propensity score matching

ADPKD is the most common inherited kidney disorder, caused predominantly by mutations in the PKD1 or PKD2 genes, with rarer variants involving other loci.1 Globally, the prevalence of ADPKD is estimated at 2.48 to 9.3 per 10,000 individuals, affecting nearly 12 million people worldwide, although these figures may vary because of the disease’s indolent progression and evolving screening practices.2 ADPKD accounts for approximately 2% to 10% of ESKD cases and represents 12% to 14% of renal transplant recipients in the United States.3 Health economic analyses have demonstrated that, despite being younger and having fewer comorbidities, patients with ADPKD experience more kidney-related complications and undergo more surgical interventions for symptom control than compared kidney function-matched comparators.4 Their health care utilization, including hospitalizations and outpatient visits, can be 3 to 5 times greater than that of controls over a 6- to 12-month period.5

This substantial burden may be attributed to the multisystemic nature of ADPKD. In addition to kidney manifestations such as flank or abdominal pain from space occupying effect, hypertension, and recurrent urinary tract infections or nephrolithiasis, ADPKD may be further complicated by extrarenal abnormalities involving the cardiovascular system (e.g., intracranial and aortic aneurysms), liver, pancreatic, and seminal vesicle cysts.6 These complex presentations and complications raise concerns for accelerated functional decline among affected individuals.

Frailty is an age-related degenerative phenotype, characterized by an increased vulnerability to physiological stressors, resulting in disability and a greater risk of adverse outcomes, including hospitalization and mortality. Patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) are particularly susceptible to frailty, because of cumulative effects from uremic toxins, multimorbidity, and metabolic-endocrine dysregulation, some of which recapitulate the biological aging process.7 The prevalence of frailty in individuals with ESKD ranges from 36% to 67%, and the presence of frailty confers a 1.5- to 2-fold increase in the risk of hospitalization and death.8,9 As a progressive form of CKD, ADPKD may predispose individuals to frailty through inducing kidney function deterioration and complications such as hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, and their respective treatments.10

Despite this biologically plausible link, evidence on the relationship between ADPKD and frailty remains scarce. Whether ADPKD increases the risk of incident frailty or contributes to its progression over time has not been adequately investigated. We hypothesized that ADPKD, as a chronic progressive kidney disorder, might be associated with higher risks of incident and worsening frailty. To explore this hypothesis, we assembled a retrospective cohort of patients with cystic kidney disease, including individuals with ADPKD and a comparator group, and followed them longitudinally to assess frailty-related outcomes.

Methods

Ethical Considerations

This study is a subanalysis of a larger project aimed at characterizing the epidemiologic features and prognosis of adults with kidney cysts. The main study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of National Taiwan University Hospital (NO. 202205024RINA). Informed consent was deemed unnecessary because participant identification data were anonymized prior to data analyses and the study’s retrospective nature.

Study Cohort Assembly

We retrospectively identified adult patients (aged > 18 years) diagnosed with cystic kidney disease, including ADPKD and simple kidney cysts (SKCs), from the National Taiwan University Hospital Integrated Medical Database between 2006 and 2021. ADPKD diagnoses were identified using International Classification of Disease-9 Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) codes 753.12 or 753.13, and ICD-10 codes Q61.2 or Q61.3. SKCs were identified using ICD-9-CM code 593.2 and ICD-10 code N28.1. These diagnostic codes have demonstrated good specificity (∼90%) for identifying ADPKD and SKC cases.11,12

The exclusion criteria included the following: (i) absence of clinical data before the index date (defined as the date of diagnosis), (ii) presence of frailty at baseline (defined later), (iii) missing baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and (iv) lack of follow-up data. Patients with SKCs served as the comparison group because both SKCs and ADPKD belong to cystic kidney disease, and the former is generally considered outcome neutral. In addition, those with SKCs were selected as the control group with the aim of obviating the potential outcome influences of kidney cysts–related space-occupying effect. Follow-up continued until the development of study outcomes, death, or the end of 2022, whichever came first.

Variable Collection

Baseline clinical data were extracted from medical records before the index date. Body mass index was calculated as weight in kg divided by height in m squared. Comorbidities, including diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, CKD, cardiovascular disease (including aneurysms), cerebrovascular disease, cancer, and rheumatologic disorders, were identified based on ≥ 2-outpatient or 1-inpatient diagnostic records, using previously validated coding schemes.13,14 Previous records of kidney transplantation were obtained. CKD stages were categorized from 1 to 5 based on eGFR, in accordance with Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes guidelines.15

Medication use before the index date was recorded, including antihypertensives, antidiabetics, lipid-lowering agents, antiplatelets, anticoagulants, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory agents because these may influence frailty risk.10,16 Laboratory parameters included complete blood counts, metabolic parameters (glucose and lipid profiles), kidney function (uric acid, creatinine, and eGFR), liver function, and inflammation markers (C-reactive protein). eGFR was calculated using the 4-variable Modification of Diet in Renal Disease formula. Frailty status at baseline was assessed as described in the next section.

Outcome Ascertainment

The primary outcome of was incident frailty, and the co–primary outcome was frailty worsening during follow-up. Frailty was assessed using the FRAIL scale, which included 5 components as: fatigue, resistance, ambulation, illness, and weight loss. This scale has been validated in older adults and patients with various morbidities, including CKD.13,17 FRAIL-identified frailty is associated with increased mortality and adverse outcomes.18 The FRAIL components were operationalized using a combination of diagnostic codes, clinical presentations, and procedures, following previously published methods in populations with diabetes and CKD.13,19,20 The FRAIL score has shown good concordance with the frail index.10 Frailty was defined as ≥ 3 positive components, per the original criteria.21 Frailty worsening was defined as the development of ≥ 1 additional FRAIL component during follow-up compared with baseline, regardless of initial frailty status.10,16

The secondary outcome was all-cause mortality, identified via linkage to the National health Insurance database death registry, updated periodically in the National Taiwan University Hospital Integrated Medical Database.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviations (SDs) and compared using t tests. Categorical variables are presented as counts with percentages and compared using Chi-square tests. Standardized mean differences are calculated to describe group differences.

Patients were initially classified by cyst type (ADPKD vs. SKCs). Propensity score (PS) were calculated based on age, eGFR, comorbidities, medications, laboratory data, and the number of FRAIL components. Patients with ADPKD were then matched to SKC controls in a 1:4 ratio using a caliper of ± 0.2 on the calculated PS, with exact matching based on age group, number of FRAIL components, and CKD stages (according to eGFR categories). Baseline demographics, clinical characteristics, comorbidities, medications, laboratory findings, and FRAIL scores were compared between the matched groups.

Cumulative incidence and incidence density of frailty outcomes were calculated, and Kaplan-Meier analyses were used to estimate event-free survival, with comparisons made using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazard regression models were used to evaluate associations between ADPKD and outcomes, adjusting for different sets of covariates, including demographic profiles, comorbidities, baseline frailty results, medications, and laboratory data. Mortality or the development of dialysis-dependent ESKD was each treated as a competing risk in sensitivity analyses. For secondary outcome analyses, Kaplan-Meier curves and Cox models were used. Subgroup analyses were performed based on baseline frailty status, CKD stage, and age strata. A separate analysis was done to compare the incidence of outcomes according to tolvaptan, the only approved ADPKD medication, use or not among the ADPKD group. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Between 2006 and 2021, a total of 15,748 patients with cystic kidney disease were identified from the National Taiwan University Hospital Integrated Medical Database, including 1836 with ADPKD (11.7%) and 13,912 with SKCs (88.3%) (Figure 1). After applying exclusion criteria, 1281 patients with ADPKD (70.0%) and 11,042 with SKCs (79.4%) were eligible for PS matching. Ultimately, 775 patients with ADPKD were successfully matched to 3100 with SKCs at a 1:4 ratio for subsequent analyses (Figure 1).

A flow chart for study participant selection in this study. ADPKD, autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease; CKD, chronic kidney disease; eGFR, estimated glomerular filtration rate; SKC, simple kidney cyst.

Following PS-matching, there were no significant differences between the ADPKD and SKC groups in terms of demographics; comorbidities (hyperlipidemia, CKD, coronary artery disease, heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, ischemic stroke, cancer, rheumatologic disorders, and aneurysms); most medications use; or laboratory results including liver and kidney function, lipid profiles, and uric acid (Table 1). Compared with the SKC group, patients with ADPKD had a higher prevalence of hypertension, were more frequently prescribed antihypertensives, and exhibited elevated C-reactive protein levels, but had lower rates of diabetes and lower levels of hemoglobin, fasting glucose, and glycated hemoglobin (Table 1). Baseline frailty status did not differ significantly between groups.

Over a mean follow-up of 4.8 years, 88 patients (2.3%) developed incident frailty and 810 (20.9%) experienced worsening frailty. The cumulative incidence of frailty was 2.45% in the ADPKD and 2.23% in the SKC group, whereas the incidence of worsening frailty was 19.74% and 21.19%, respectively (Table 2). Kaplan-Meier analyses revealed no significant differences in the probability of incident frailty (P = 0.59; Figure 2a) or worsening frailty (P = 0.69; Figure 2b) between groups. Univariate Cox regression analysis showed no increased risk of incident (hazard rario (HR): 1.15, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.69–1.91) or worsening frailty (HR: 0.97, 95% CI: 0.81–1.15) in patients with ADPKD compared with those with SKCs (Table 2). After adjustment for demographic profiles, comorbidities, medication use, and laboratory parameters, ADPKD remained unassociated with either outcome (model 1; Table 2). The results remained unchanged when other potential confounders, including kidney transplantation, rheumatologic disorders, cancer, or certain ADPKD complication (aneurysms) were adjusted for (model 2; Table 2). Our findings were consistent when mortality (model 3; Table 2) or ESKD development (model 4; Table 2) was treated as a competing risk event.

Univariate Cox regression revealed a significantly increased risk of death in the ADPKD group (HR: 1.25, 95% CI: 1.03–1.51, P = 0.022), which remained significant after different combinations of variable adjustment (model 1, HR: 1.38, 95% CI: 1.14–1.68; model 2, HR: 1.41, 95% CI: 1.16–1.72; Table 2).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study involving patients with cystic kidney disease, we evaluated whether ADPKD was associated with an increased risk of incident frailty or worsening frailty over time. Despite the progressive nature of ADPKD and its multisystem complications, our findings did not support the existence of an association between ADPKD and either the development or progression of frailty. These results remained robust after adjusting for a variety of variables, including demographic characteristics, comorbidities, laboratory data, and medication use, and were consistent across sensitivity and subgroup analyses. Notably, despite the absence of frailty risk, patients with ADPKD experienced a significantly higher risk of mortality over the follow-up period after multivariable adjustment, constituting the discrepant consideration on ADPKD-associated outcome influence.

To our knowledge, this study is among the very few reports which specifically examine frailty outcomes in patients with ADPKD. Although frailty is increasingly recognized as an instrumental outcome modifier in the CKD and ESKD populations, available reports mostly focus on those of advanced age or individuals with advanced nongenetic kidney disease.22,23 Our study substantially expands the understanding of frailty risk in the context of a genetic and multisystemic disorder such as ADPKD. Despite the anticipated elevated risk based on pathophysiological mechanisms including kidney function decline, cardiovascular burden, and associated complications, ADPKD was not found to independently correlate with frailty development or progression. Several reasons may be responsible for our findings. First, our patients may be modestly younger (mean age: 58 years), have more preserved kidney function (mean eGFR: 73–74 ml/min per 1.73 m2) and functional status (mean FRAIL score, < 1 out of 5 components) (Table 1) at baseline compared with other studies addressing CKD and risk factors for frailty.24,25 This is partly supported by the relatively lower annual incidence of frailty in our cohort (Table 2). However, for the outcome of worsening frailty with literature-comparable event numbers, there remained no difference in the observed risk between groups. Second, the disease course of ADPKD tends to be protracted, and longer follow-up period (perhaps in decades) may be needed to detect meaningful differences in pathologic phenotypes and their changes, such as frailty. Third, there is a possibility that the main contributors to frailty development or worsening in patients with ADPKD are comorbidities (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, and others) instead of ADPKD or its complications. Finally, imbalanced in other unmeasured confounders, such as dietary features or exercise habits, may neutralize the effect on frailty brought by ADPKD. Further studies would be needed to answer these questions.

The most appropriate instrument for frailty assessment in patients with CKD or other kidney disorders remains controversial. Indeed, clinical practitioners frequently perceive that multidimensional frailty-screening instruments (such as the Kihon Checklist) may exhibit greater utility compared with unidimensional ones such as FRAIL scale.26 In addition, there are studies suggesting that the FRAIL scale may demonstrate differential sensitivity, specificity, and outcome predictive ability compared with index-based or physical measurement approaches.27,28 Nonetheless, a recent systematic review concludes that in patients with CKD, FRAIL scale possesses consistent discriminative validity and result agreement with other instruments.29 It would be therefore prudent to harness multiple frailty-assessing instruments to ascertain frailty status in this population.

Interestingly, though frailty risk did not differ, the mortality risk was significantly higher in the ADPKD group. This may reflect the impact of life-threatening ADPKD complications, such as aneurysmal rupture, cardiovascular events, and infections associated with urinary tract or cyst related compressions. This finding underscores the potential for ADPKD to contribute to adverse outcomes through mechanisms not fully captured by traditional frailty indices. In these genetic kidney disease patients, their life course may be influenced more by distinct complications instead of the genetic disease per se or the development of frailty. In contrast, the FRAIL scale, though validated in many populations including those with CKD, may exhibit limited sensitivity in detecting subtle or disease-specific manifestations of functional decline unique to ADPKD.

Our findings reinforce the importance of nuanced clinical assessment in patients with ADPKD. These results do not suggest that we do not need to assess frailty in this population; instead, we can be confident that frailty assessment can be performed in a similar fashion like for individuals with CKD of nongenetic origin. Although ADPKD does not appear to confer excess frailty risk relative to SKCs, the observed increase in mortality highlights the need for vigilant monitoring and management of systemic complications. Early identification of high-risk individuals, including those with uncontrolled hypertension, rapid kidney function decline, or significant extrarenal involvement, remains crucial.

Our study has its strengths and limitations. The link between ADPKD and frailty outcomes, either incident frailty or worsening frailty, is rarely examined before in the literature. Our findings fill this research gap through collecting a comprehensive set of variables and analyzing accordingly, with valuable insights obtained. However, several issues still warrant consideration. First, the retrospective design precludes causal inference, and residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded despite rigorous PS matching and multivariable adjustments, such as the effects of dietary variations and exercise habits mentioned earlier. Genetic features may pose influences on the relationship we investigated, but we did not have such information. Second, different frailty-assessment tools have been shown to exhibit discrepancies in the features of target population identified and outcome correlations.27 Nonetheless, a recent systematic review incorporating 105 studies disclosed that the FRAIL scale showed consistent discriminative validity for mortality in CKD population.29 Third, the generalizability of our findings may be limited to tertiary care settings or populations with comparable access to health care services. Fourth, it is plausible that classifications of ADPKD, such as atypical or typical, may influence our study findings, although we did not have such details. Fifth, the selection of patients with SKCs as the controls may affect the interpretability of the results, although we have explained the advantage of this approach. In fact, in our dataset, those with SKCs are younger and have lower prevalence of comorbidities relative to randomly selected out-patient individuals without cystic kidney disease. This suggests that our control group may be relatively fit at baseline, a feature that potentially increases the probability of detecting outcome differences. The absence of association in this setting may indirectly support the consistency of our results. Finally, influences on more extended trajectories of frailty beyond the study period remain uncertain. This issue may merit further investigation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, ADPKD was not associated with a higher risk of developing or worsening frailty compared with SKCs, despite its progressive kidney and systemic manifestations. Nevertheless, ADPKD was independently linked to an increased mortality, underscoring the need for continued efforts in risk stratification and multidisciplinary management. Future prospective studies incorporating more frailty assessment tools and longer follow-up durations are warranted to elucidate the functional implications of ADPKD across the disease continuum.
