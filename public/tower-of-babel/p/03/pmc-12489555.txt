Terminal ileum in screening colonoscopy: routine practice or risk-stratified approach?
Authors: Seong-Jung Kim
Published: 2025

The terminal ileum (TI) is an anatomical endpoint of colonoscopy and is well recognized as a key site involved in inflammatory bowel disease, particularly Crohn’s disease (CD), thereby carrying significant diagnostic value.1,2 Various pathologies, including intestinal tuberculosis, Behçet’s disease, drug-induced enteritis, and lymphoma, can also be detected in this region, necessitating its examination in specific clinical contexts.3,4 However, in average-risk asymptomatic populations undergoing screening colonoscopy, the clinical benefits of routine TI intubation remain debatable.

Advocates argue that diseases such as CD, intestinal tuberculosis, and lymphoma may initially be localized to the TI, and failure to detect them could delay diagnosis.5 Notably, TI intubation in patients with relevant clinical indicators yields diagnostic findings in approximately 30% of cases.4 Moreover, TI intubation adds only 2 to 3 minutes to the procedure and is generally safe, causing no significant increase in patient discomfort or complications.6 Given this favorable safety profile and potential for detecting clinically significant findings, proponents argue that routine TI intubation should remain a standard part of screening colonoscopy, especially when considering that even rare findings can substantially impact patient outcomes.

However, the skepticism persists. A meta-analysis by Agrawal et al. showed that TI intubation exhibits diagnostic yields under 2% in asymptomatic, average-risk individuals, with most findings being nonspecific.3 Additionally, factors such as increased procedure time, resource use, patient discomfort, and endoscopist fatigue raise concerns about cost-effectiveness.7

In this issue of Clinical Endoscopy, Dąbkowski et al.8 examined the utility of TI intubation using real-world data from more than 2,400 colonoscopies at a single center. They found that although the procedure time slightly increased, no differences were observed in pain or complication rates. The diagnostic yield was 1.7%, and most of the results were clinically insignificant. These findings support a selective rather than routine approach to TI intubation in asymptomatic patients.

However, most related studies were retrospective, with TI intubation decisions left to the discretion of the endoscopist, which introduces a potential selection bias. The lack of standardized protocols and clearly defined indications further limits the generalizability of this method. Moreover, little is known about the cumulative burden that TI intubation has on procedure time, operator fatigue, and healthcare costs. Despite these limitations, TI intubation remains essential in certain clinical scenarios, including suspected CD, right lower quadrant pain, unexplained anemia, abnormal imaging findings, suspected infectious ileitis or Behçet’s disease, and obscure gastrointestinal bleeding.5-7,9 In these contexts, selective TI intubation enhances diagnostic accuracy and guides appropriate management.

Even if clinically significant findings are rare (estimated at approximately 1%), they may have critical prognostic implications.3 Hence, these cases should not be dismissed based on probability alone. Clinical decision-making must be supported by clearly defined evidence-based indications. Routine TI intubation may serve as a safeguard against missing early or subtle lesions in high-risk or borderline cases.

Several priorities have emerged to better define the role of TI intubation. First, prospective multicenter studies are needed to objectively evaluate its utility in average-risk populations. Second, the indications for TI intubation must be standardized to ensure consistency and minimize unnecessary procedures. Third, long-term outcome studies integrating pathology and clinical follow-up could clarify the true impact of TI findings. Lastly, broader cost-effectiveness analyses that include metrics such as patient satisfaction, endoscopist workload, and procedural efficiency are essential. Further research on adjunctive tools, such as cap-assisted ileoscopy9 and compression devices,10 may also enhance procedural success and minimize burden. A nuanced, evidence-based approach would help clarify who benefits the most from TI intubation and under what conditions, ultimately refining the value of screening colonoscopy.
