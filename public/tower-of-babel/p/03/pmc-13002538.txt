Mapping System–Assisted Bipolar Radiofrequency Ablation for the Treatment of Ventricular Tachycardia Associated With Ventricular Aneurysm
Authors: Xiangzhi Kong, Mingxin Gao, Chengxiong Gu, Haiyang Li, Yang Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe management of malignant ventricular tachycardia (VT) in the aftermath of a myocardial infarction is a complex undertaking, particularly in cases accompanied by left ventricular aneurysm (LVA). It is frequently the case that conventional unipolar radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA) is inadequate.Case SummaryA 50-year-old male patient with a medical history of myocardial infarction presented with recurrent VT due to a LVA. Initial unipolar RFCA proved to be an ineffective treatment. Bipolar RFCA guided by CARTO mapping during coronary artery bypass grafting has been demonstrated to successfully ablate the LVA. Subsequent postoperative follow-up revealed no recurrence of VT and sustained cardiac function over a 2-year period.DiscussionRefractory VT due to LVA poses significant treatment challenges. Bipolar RFCA, guided by CARTO mapping, has been shown to offer an effective solution, overcoming the limitations of unipolar RFCA by targeting both epicardial and endocardial arrhythmogenic substrates. This case demonstrates a surgical approach with the potential to yield favorable outcomes.

Malignant ventricular arrhythmia accounts for 44% of the total death from left ventricular aneurysm (LVA),1 and it accounts for 29.6% and 36.8% of 30-day and 5-year deaths after coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) combined with LVA formation, respectively.2 Unipolar radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA) is not sufficient to resolve ventricular arrhythmia if electrophysiological abnormality presented in the epicardium or myocardial tissue. There is no suitable RFCA technique for ventricular arrhythmia resolution in surgery. We report a patient with refractory ventricular tachycardia (VT) secondary to LVA, who underwent surgery combined bipolar RFCA assisted by CARTO mapping with good outcomes after ineffective treatment with unipolar RFCA.Take-Home Messages•This case highlights the importance of multimodality imaging for procedural planning in complex ventricular tachycardia treatments.•Bipolar radiofrequency catheter ablation assisted by CARTO mapping is an effective treatment for refractory ventricular tachycardia caused by left ventricular aneurysm.

Case Report

A 50-year-old man with a history of myocardial infarction (MI) experienced over 10 episodes of intermittent VT in the previous month. Antiarrhythmic drugs were ineffective. Coronary angiography revealed approximately 70% to 80% proximal stenosis in the left anterior descending branch, with an ejection fraction of 31%; left ventricular end-diastolic diameter of 62 mm; left atrium of 50 mm; and an LVA measuring 61 × 25 mm. Electrocardiogram showed that VT originated from the left ventricle. The left and right ventricles were reconstructed using the CARTO system. VT activation and voltage mapping were conducted under CartoSound guidance. Two VTs were both LVA-related reentry. There were significant delayed potentials at the junction between the edge of the LVA and the normal myocardium and in the center of the LVA. The above 2 reentries and all delayed potentials were ablated and disappeared by unipolar RFCA (Figure 1). VT was no longer induced by stimulations. CABG was performed via the great saphenous vein to the left anterior descending branch under beating-heart cardiopulmonary bypass. The LVA (approximately 80 × 40 mm) was opened for removing a mural thrombus. According to the anatomical position indicated by CARTO, LVA was ablated from its center to the surrounding myocardium by 8 bipolar ablation lines (AtriCure), the shape as “米”. One ablation line was passed through the isthmus of the reentrant circuit of refractory VT. Holter monitoring showed 1 ventricular premature beat (VPB), 0 VT, and 594 supraventricular tachycardias (SVTs) after surgery. A beta-blocker was prescribed as an antiarrhythmic drug. One year later, Holter monitoring showed 2,443 VPBs, 1 VT (ventricular triple rhythm), and 0 SVT. Two years later, Holter monitoring showed 151 VPBs, 0 VT, and 0 SVT. The ejection fraction was 42%; left ventricular end-diastolic diameter was 60 mm; and left atrium was 36 mm. Cardiac function was grade I.

Discussion and Conclusions

RFCA is the main treatment for refractory VT caused by an MI scar. However, it is difficult to achieve the transmural effect of RFCA in the endocardium alone because the electrophysiological matrix of MI complicated with VT can be located in the sub-epicardium and endocardium, as well as in the epicardium.1 This patient underwent endocardial unipolar RFCA assisted by the CARTO mapping system, but this did not effectively curb the refractory VT. We considered that this could be due to 3 main reasons: (1) the reentrant circuit was located in the endocardium, epicardium, or sub-epicardium; (2) unipolar RFCA did not achieve true wall penetration; and (3) ischemia of the left ventricular was not resolved.

Guidelines for CABG highlighted that CABG could only effectively reduce VT caused by myocardial ischemia compared to VT caused by scarring after MI.3 Nearly 50% of patients with malignant VT relapsed after coronary revascularization.4 This indicated that scar reentry is another main reason for VT caused by LVA.5 In this study, scar reentry was detected at the junction between the normal left ventricular anterior myocardium and the edge of the LVA. Therefore, simple surgery could not effectively curb the refractory VT. Furthermore, implantable cardioverter defibrillator implantation within 2 years of coronary revascularization does not effectively reduce the incidence of sudden death.3,6

Ectopic foci can be located in the endocardium, a few in the epicardium and sub-epicardium, or even in the interventricular septum.7 However, irrespective of the location and number of ectopic foci, the abnormal electrical impulse usually pass through the reentrant circuit formed by LVA.8 Eliminating reentrant circuit plays a key role in the treatment of VT.

The ventricular arrhythmia caused by LVA was not completely suppressed from the cardiac electrophysiology9 indicating that due to technical limitations, it is impossible to achieve complete transmural ablation between endocardium and epicardium. Therefore, bipolar RFCA cater to the requirements, including stable power, easy operation, and the ability of automatically cut off when the ablation reaches the wall penetration. It can also be used under beating-heart conditions.10 In our case, full ventricular reconstruction and anatomical localization of the key isthmus assisted by the CARTO mapping system guided the surgeon to perform bipolar RFCA. The procedure effectively decreased refractory VT caused by LVA, and the patient had a good long-term outcome.

We described the approach for diagnosis and surgical treatment of LVA with refractory VT by CARTO mapping-guided bipolar RFCA, combined with CABG. This approach was able to minimize the incidence of refractory VT and could be regarded as a new treatment for LVA complicated by refractory VT.
