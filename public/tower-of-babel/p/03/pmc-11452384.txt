Investigating in vitro anticholinergic potential (anti-AChE and anti-BuChE) of Chaenomeles leaves extracts and its phytochemicals including chlorophylls, carotenoids and minerals
Authors: Igor Piotr Turkiewicz, Karolina Tkacz, Paulina Nowicka, Aneta Wojdyło
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The goal of this work was to evaluate the chemical constitution and health-promoting potential of 12 varieties of Chaenomeles × superba, speciosa and japonica leaves. Carotenoids, chlorophylls, triterpenes, sugars, polyols and acids were analyzed quantitatively and qualitatively using high pressure liquid chromatography (LC) coupled with mass spectrometry (MS), while the mineral profile was determined using atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS). Moreover, the in vitro anticholinergic potential (inhibition of acetyl-cholinesterase (AChE) and butyryl-cholinesterase (BuChE)) and antioxidant (ABTS, FRAP, ORAC) capacity were evaluated. For the first time in Chaenomeles genotypes 26 carotenoid derivatives and 22 chlorophyll derivatives were identified. Some varieties contained high amounts of carotenoids and chlorophylls (Ch. × superba ‘Colour Trail’, ‘Nicoline’, ‘Pink Lady’, ‘Texas Scarlet’), and triterpenes (Ch. speciosa ‘Simonii’, ‘Rubra’, and Ch. × superba ‘Colour Trail’, ‘Nicoline’) and showed high ORAC antioxidant (Ch. × superba ‘Pink Lady’ and Ch. speciosa ‘Simonii’) and anticholinergic (Ch. speciosa species) activity. The studied leaves also contained sugars (3.1 to 16.5 mg/100 g), organic acids (3.9–8.1 g/100 g), and minerals (Ca, Cu, Fe, K, Mg, Mn, Na, and Zn). In conclusion, Chaenomeles leaves show potential as a new source for the production of nutraceuticals, as well as for medical and/or cosmetic purposes.

Introduction

Chaenomeles Lindley is a shrub that grows up to 1–2 m in height, belonging to the rose family (Rosaceae). Chaenomeles japonica (Thunb.) Lindl. ex Spach, Chaenomeles speciosa (Sweet) Nakai and Chaenomeles × superba (Frahm) Rehder are grown not only for their fruit, but also for their ornamental qualities1. The greatest decorative attribute of Chaenomeles is the showy flowers (various shades of red or orange, less often pale coral, yellow or white) that bloom in the second half of April and May. The plant produces lemon-yellow fruit resembling small apples (usually 2–5 cm in diameter). They are very hard, very sour, and fragrant. The shoots have sharp thorns about 1 cm long. Chaenomeles leaves are dark green, stiff and slightly leathery. They do not change color in autumn, and they fall in winter2,3.

Most of the reported health benefits of Chaenomeles are related to their fruits and are attributed to the presence of phytochemicals with various (both in vitro and in vivo) biological activities4. Fruits are considered to be a good source of phenolic compounds and organic acids, while seeds are rich in triterpenes, tocopherols and tocotrienols5,6. There are only a few studies7–9 on the chemical composition of Chaenomeles leaves, and our recent publication revealed that they contain significant amounts of phenolic compounds and amino acids, and have hypoglycemic and anti-obesity potential10. Furthermore, these data are not comprehensive and do not relate to a wide variety of species and varieties of Chaenomeles leaves. Moreover, it is the first study to include the identification and quantification of chlorophylls, carotenoids, sugars and organic acids. Also, the anticholinergic potential as the ability to inhibit acetylcholinesterase (AChE) and butyrylcholinesterase (BuChE) has not been analyzed before.

The size of the global nutraceutical products market was valued at at USD 454.55 billion in 2021. Asia Pacific dominates the global market, North America holds the second largest share, while Europe is in third place. The growing demand for dietary supplements (containing, among other things, phenolic compounds, vitamins, minerals, etc.) and functional food (showing a positive effect in the prevention or treatment of selected diseases) is expected to be a primary driving factor for this market segment11. Nutraceuticals are biologically active ingredients that can enhance, weaken or modulate the physiological and metabolic functions of the body, and thus have a positive effect on its condition. They are obtained by crushing the raw material (e.g. whole fruit, rhizomes or flowers) and shaping the appropriate form and characteristics of a medicine, e.g. tablets, lozenges or capsules12. The growing demand for this type of product requires the search for new sources of nutraceuticals, which is associated with detailed analyses of the chemical composition and pro-health potential.

A research hypothesis was proposed that Chaenomeles leaves are a valuable source of phytochemicals showing anticholinergic and antioxidant potential. Detailed research on the leaves of chosen species and varieties of Chaenomeles shrubs was undertaken in order to determine the profile and content of (i) carotenoids and chlorophylls (by UPLC–PDA combined with LC–PDA–ESI–QTOF–MS); (ii) triterpenes (UPLC–PDA); (iii) micro- and macroelements (AAS); (iv) sugars and organic acids (UPLC–PDA and HPLC–ELSD, respectively). Moreover, (v) in vitro biological activities (anticholinergic potential and antioxidant capacity) in the 12 varieties of Chaenomeles × superba, speciosa, and japonica leaves have been assessed. Most of the chosen varieties were analyzed for the first time.

A total of 26 carotenoid derivatives and 22 chlorophyll derivatives were identified. This is the first time that a detailed analysis of plant pigments in Chaenomeles leaves has been made; therefore, most of these compounds were identified for the first time in the analyzed samples.

At Rt = 1.83 min peak 1 showed a molecular ion [M + H]+ at m/z 614.5, and a fragmentation ion at m/z 554.4 evidencing loss of all COOCH3 ester groups [M + H-60]+. In line with previous findings Milenković et al.13, this compound was identified as a chlorophyllide a. Peak 2 (Rt = 2.22 min) showed an identical molecular and fragmentation ion and was tentatively identified as a chlorophyllide a derivative. Peak 3 was identified as neoxanthin due to the characteristic absorption bands at λmax = 413.0, 438.4 and 465.1 nm, the molecular ion [M + H]+ at m/z 601.5 and the presence of fragmentation ions at m/z 583.1 [M + H-18]+, m/z 565.3 [M + H-18-18]+ and m/z 509.3, indicating loss of toluene [M + H-92]+14. Peak 4 (Rt = 2.51 min) showed the parent ion at m/z 585.4 and the following ion fragments at m/z 567.1 and 549.1 indicative of the loss of water molecules. The presence of a fragmentation ion [M + H-C7H8]+ at m/z 493.1 evidenced detachment of a toluene molecule (-92 Da). Therefore, this compound was assigned to antheraxanthin as determined by Delgado-Pelayo et al.15. Peaks 5 and 6 gave a protonated molecule at m/z 601.5 and were proposed as violaxanthin (Rt = 2.93 min) and violaxanthin derivative (Rt = 3.09 min), respectively. Both compounds presented a mass spectrum (fragments at m/z 565.3, 529.3 and 491.2) and showed UV-vis maxima similar to those reported in the literature16. Peak 7 was tentatively assigned to luteoxanthin. Indeed, this compound has already been described in Moringa oleifera leaves17. The mass spectral data showed a fragment at m/z 521.5 corresponding to the loss of methyl-cyclopentadiene (-80 Da). Moreover, this compound has characteristic UV-vis maxima at 400.7, 423.2 and 449.3 nm18. At Rt = 3.44 min peak 8 with a molecular ion [M + H]+ at m/z 601.5 has been suggested as neochrome. The assignment was substantiated by the fragment ions at m/z 583.1 [M + H-H2O]+, 565.3 [M + H-2H2O]+, and 221.3 [M + H-380]+, which confirmed the presence of an epoxide and/or furanoid group in the 3-hydroxy-β-ring19. Peak 9 at m/z 607.4 was tentatively identified as pheophorbide b, based on the MS data and literature14. Furthermore, peak 10 (Rt = 3.72 min) was identified as pheophorbide a, showing a molecular ion [M + H]+ at m/z 593.4, and fragment ions at m/z 533.4 [M + H-COOCH3]+ and 505.4, which is consistent with the analysis of the reference standard. Peak 11 at Rt = 3.95 min had λmax = 4231.1 and 456.6 nm and the parent ion at m/z 585.5. The protonated molecule was generated from the loss of a water molecule [M + H-18]+ at m/z 567.6 This compound was tentatively identified as lutein-5,6-epoxide, in line with previous findings of Crupi et al.20 and Mendes-Pinto et al.21. Compounds at Rt = 4.10 (peak 12) and 4.67 min (peak 14) present the same molecular mass m/z 569.4, and according to the literature data were proposed as zeaxanthin derivatives I and II. Peak 13 showed an ion at m/z 569.4 and MS/MS fragments at m/z 551.1 [M + H-H2O]+, 533.1 [M + H-2H2O]+, 477.3 [M + H-C7H8]+ and 459.3, and was identified as (all-E)-lutein, consistent with the authentic standard. This compound has very characteristic wavelengths of absorbance maxima (λmax = 425.0, 446.9 and 476.0 nm), as previously described by Wojdyło et al.22. Another nine peaks (15–19, 21–22, 24–25) having an identical molecular ion [M + H]+ at m/z 569.4 and fragment ions at m/z 551.1 and 533.1 were identified as lutein isomers I-IX, based on UV-vis absorption, MS data and previous literature findings23. Peaks 20 and 23 (Rt = 5.27 and 5.99 min, and λmax = 478.8 nm), with [M + H]+ at m/z 565.5 were identified as canthaxanthin derivatives I and II24. Peak 26 gave a protonated parent ion [M + H]+ at m/z 659.4 and two daughter ions at m/z 641.6 (loss of water, [M + H-18]+) and at m/z 581.3 (loss of acetic acid and water [M + H-18-60]+. These findings are in full agreement with reported mass fragmentation data for fucoxanthin25. Two consecutive peaks at Rt = 6.63 and 6.67 min showed an identical molecular ion at m/z 923.5 and a fragment ion [M + H-16]+ at m/z 907.5. According to the fragmentation pattern provided by Wojdyło et al.22, these compounds were assigned to hydroxy-chlorophyll b derivatives I and II, peaks 27 and 28, respectively. Peaks 29 and 30 were tentatively identified as hydroxy-lactone-chlorophyll b derivatives I and II. Both compounds were characterized by the parent ion at m/z 939.526, with hydroxy-lactone-chlorophyll b derivative I also showing a fragment ion of at m/z 596.3 [M + H-343]+ which has not been reported so far. Peak 31 (Rt = 7.04 min, and λmax = 462.7 and 647.1 nm) was identified as chlorophyll b, which showed an ion [M + H]+ at m/z 907.5, and fragmentation MS/MS at m/z 629.3 [M + H-278]+ (loss of phytyl chain as a phytadiene), and at m/z 597.3 [M + H-310]+ (loss of C20H38-CH3OH), which is consistent with data obtained for the reference compound. Based on the parent ion at m/z 893.4 five successive peaks (32, 34, 36, 39 and 44) at Rt = 7.26, 7.58, 7.96, 8.68 and 9.81 min were tentatively identified as chlorophyll a derivatives I–V. To accurately determine the structure of these compounds, it would be necessary to perform a more detailed analysis, such as nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy. Peak 33 having the same UV-vis and MS characteristics as peak 31 (chlorophyll b) was assigned to chlorophyll b’. As is known, some of the chlorophyll compounds are present as pairs, and the only difference between these compounds (apart from the retention time) may be the intensity of the fragmentation ions. At Rt = 7.70 min peak 35 with molecular ion [M + H]+ at m/z 909.5 has been suggested as a hydroxy-chlorophyll a derivative. The presence of this compound and its MS characteristics have previously been confirmed in green algae26. By comparing with the standard, peak 37 (λmax = 428.7 and 658.1) was identified as chlorophyll a. This compound showed an intense ion molecule [M + H]+ at m/z 893.4, and fragment ions at m/z 615.1 (loss of toluene, -278 Da) and at m/z 555.3 (loss of CH3COOC20H39, -338 Da). Likewise, showing identical mass profile characteristics, and a slight shift in the absorption spectra, peak 38 was identified as chlorophyll α. Peak 40 was assigned as (all-E)-α-cryptoxanthin; the protonated molecule was recorded at m/z 553.5 along with less intense response at m/z 535.1 [M + H-18]+ and 461.3 [M + H-92]+, derived from the loss of a -OH group and methyl benzene, respectively16. (All-E)-β-carotene (peak 41) was confirmed by coeluting with the reference compound, as well as comparison of the UV-vis and mass spectral features with those of the analytical standard. Furthermore, peak 42 (Rt = 9.29 min) was proposed to be a β-carotene isomer, showing a molecular ion [M + H]+ at m/z 537.4, and a fragment ion at m/z 444.1 [M + H-92]+, but also λmax similar to that of the β-carotene molecule. At Rt = 9.47 min peak 43 with a molecular ion [M + H]+ at m/z 537.5 and UV-vis maximum at 440.8 nm has been suggested as ε-carotene. This identification was confirmed by comparing the data for the standard. As already confirmed in the studies by Petrović et al.27 and Wojdyło et al.22, pheophytin b is eluted before pheophytin a, with the forms a’ and b’ occurring immediately after a and b. This applies to C18 columns worked in the reversed phase system. Moreover, of all chlorophyll derivatives, the pheophytins are eluted last, and they are preceded by chlorophyllides, pheophorbides and chlorophylls, respectively13. Peak 45 (Rt = 10.22 min, and λmax = 406.9 and 664.2 nm), with [M + H]+ at m/z 885.4 and fragmentation ions MS/MS at m/z 857.3 [M + H-28]+ and 607.3 [M + H-278]+, was tentatively identified as pheophytin b. Analogous to our explanation above, peak 46 has been assigned to pheophytin β. Peak 47 was identified as pheophytin a; the protonated molecule was detected at m/z 871.5 along with ion fragments [M + H]+ at m/z 593.3 (-278 Da; loss of C20H38) and 533.1 (-338 Da; loss of CH3CO2C20H38). Moreover, this peak was co-eluted with a reference standard. Finally, peak 48 (Rt = 11.30 min) was suggested as pheophytin a’, in line with UV-vis spectra, MS data and previous reports26.

Quantification by UPLC–PDA method

Table 2 presents the quantitative content of each identified carotenoid and chlorophyll compound in Chaenomeles leaves, showing high variability between studied genotypes (p ≤ 0.05). Ch. × superba ‘Colour Trail’ leaves had the highest content of carotenoids (2027.4 mg/100 g dw), while Ch. japonica ‘Cameo’ had the lowest content (1067.5 mg/100 g dw). Compounds (all-E)-β-carotene and (all-E)-lutein were found in the greatest amounts, accounting for 38.9% and 38.6% of the total carotenoid concentration. The remaining identified compounds were at levels not exceeding 10% of the average carotenoid content (below 162.8 mg/100 g dw). Comparing the obtained content of carotenoid compounds to their concentrations in popular green vegetables, such as broccoli, kale or spinach (4.6, 13.2 and 238.6 mg/100 g, respectively), it can be clearly seen that the analyzed leaves contain almost 7 times more in the case of spinach leaves28. In a study of the leaves of popular Polish fruit, Wojdyło et al.22 determined lower contents of carotenoids for apples, pears and quinces at 200–600 mg/100 g dw. Also, Górnaś et al.29 in a study of the impact of harvest time and sex on the content of lipophilic compounds in sea buckthorn leaves determined a much lower (about 25 times) content of β-carotene, in the range of 20.3–27.4 mg/100 g dw.

Translating the carotenoid content into retinol activity equivalents (RAE), the leaves of analyzed Chaenomeles species and varieties were characterized by their concentration ranging from 25.9 (Ch. japonica ‘Cido’) to 81.3 mg (Ch. japonica ‘Red Joy’). Thus, 100 g of analyzed leaves contain over 50 times more RAE than one of its richest sources – fish oil (30 mg of RAE)30.

From among all analyzed varieties, Ch. × superba ‘Colour Trail’ leaves had the highest concentration of chlorophylls (667.9 mg/100 g), while ‘Crimson and Gold’, belonging to the same species, had the lowest (236.9 mg/100 g). On average, 100 g of dry Chaenomeles leaves contained 398.3 mg of chlorophylls, and compared to carotenoids there were 4 times less, which is consistent with previous literature reports14. In the analyzed group of green plant pigments, chlorophyll a was the dominant compound, followed by pheophytin a and chlorophyll b (on average 37.7, 19.0 and 16.5% of total chlorophylls). The other derivatives were present in amounts not exceeding 5% of the sum of the discussed compounds, i.e. below 20 mg/100 g dw. The obtained results are consistent with the literature data stating that chlorophyll a, pheophytin a and chlorophyll b are the main photosynthetic pigments present in the greatest amounts in the green parts of plants, and indicating that the ratio of chlorophyll a to b is 3:131. It is worth mentioning that in the discussed studies the values of this ratio were obtained at the level of 2.3:1. Values at a similar level, in the range from 50 to 450 mg/100 g dw, were obtained by Wojdyło et al.14 analyzing chlorophyll compounds in apple, pear and quince leaves. For comparison, nettle, spinach and green basil contain 207.0, 150.3 and 142.1 mg in 100 g of leaves, respectively32.

Triterpenes

The content of triterpenes (Table 3) in Chaenomeles leaves ranged from 1763.6 (Ch. japonica ‘Red Joy’) to 3029.5 mg/100 g dw (Ch. speciosa ‘Simonii’). Pomolic acid accounted for almost 1/3 of all identified compounds (about 695.2 mg/100 g). Ursolic, oleanolic and corosolic acids were present in the analyzed varieties in the amounts of 23.1, 18.0 and 10.6% of the total triterpenes content (p ≤ 0.05). The remaining compounds can be ranked according to their decreasing concentration: maslinic acid > betulin > erythrodiol > alphitolic acid > betulinic acid > tormentic acid, and did not exceed 10% of the sum of these compounds. This is the first published report on the content of triterpenes in Chaenomeles leaves of the species and varieties discussed. According to the literature data, Chaenomeles fruits are a rich source of these compounds, and so Du et al.33, depending on the species analyzed, determined oleanolic and ursolic acid in the range of 1.5–33.9 and 4.7–27.3 mg/100 g of fruit. Moreover, these two compounds are characteristic chemical markers of Chaenomeles, which can be used to evaluate and classify the quality of this plant4.

Micro- and macroelements

Chaenomeles leaves of selected species and varieties showed significant variation in mineral contents (p ≤ 0.05). The identified elements can be ranked in order of decreasing quantity: calcium > potassium > magnesium (Fig. 2A), and further iron > sodium > manganese > zinc > copper (Fig. 2B).

The analyzed leaf samples were the most abundant in calcium, the content of which ranged from 725.6 (Ch. speciosa ‘Simonii’) to 2744.0 mg/100 g dw. The obtained results are consistent with those of a previous report22 where for the leaves of selected quince varieties, calcium was also the dominant mineral. Chaenomeles leaves contained an average of 1674.6 mg K, 489.5 mg Fe, and 5.4 mg Na per 100 g dw. Comparing the Na/K ratio, it can be stated that the analyzed leaves contain on average 310 times more potassium, which makes them a very good source of this element. Compared to Chaenomeles seeds, the leaves contain almost 5 times more potassium and almost 3 times more sodium5. Furthermore, Ch. japonica ‘Red Joy’ leaves had the most manganese (4.7 mg/100 g dw), Ch. speciosa ‘Rubra’ had the most zinc (3.3 mg/100 g dw), and Ch. × superba ‘Colour Trail’ turned out to be the richest in copper (0.45 mg/100 g dw). In a study by Grygorieva8 on Chinese quince leaves, they obtained similar results, where Zn, Mn and Cu contents were on average 3.2, 1.0 and 0.6 mg/100 g dw, respectively.

Sugars, polyols and organic acids

Figure 3 presents the results of the sugar and organic acid analysis in Chaenomeles leaves of chosen species and varieties. There was significant variation (p ≤ 0.05) in the content of identified fructose, glucose, sorbitol and sucrose. Ch. speciosa ‘Simonii’ leaves were characterized by the highest concentration of total sugars (16.5 g/100 g dw), and the lowest concentration was observed for Ch. × superba ‘Nicoline’ leaves (3.1 g/100 g dw). Sorbitol was the dominant compound and constituted over 3/4 of the total sugar content. Glucose, fructose and sucrose accounted for 12.2, 5.5 and 4.9% of the mean total sugar content, respectively. Moreover, glucose was not detected in leaves of Ch. × superba ‘Crimson and Gold’. These results are consistent with the research on apple leaves, for which sorbitol and glucose are the main sugars34,35. For comparison, Uysal et al.36 analyzed the leaves of nine fruit trees and obtained 4–7 times higher total sugar concentration from 28.1 g/100 g for grapes to 48.7 g/100 g dw for lemon. Comparing the sugar profile in Chaenomeles leaves and their fruits, it can be seen that the dominant sugar in the fruit was fructose37. For the analyzed leaves, the fructose to glucose ratio varied depending on the variety from 0.2 to 0.8. At the same time, these values were similar to those obtained previously38 for Japanese quince fruit.

Ch. × superba ‘Nicoline’ leaves have the highest organic acid content, 8.1 g/100 g dw (p ≤ 0.05), while Ch. japonica ‘Cido’ ranked at the other end of the scale (3.2 g/100 g dw). The main identified acid was galacturonic acid (over 1/3 of the mean total acid content), followed by citric, adipic and glucuronic acid. They accounted for approximately 17.7, 12.2, and 12.2% of the total identified organic acids, respectively. The others were in the minority and can be ordered in descending order of content as follows: quinic > malic > succinic > acetic > oxalic > shikimic. Moreover, the latter was not detected in the leaves of Chaenomeles japonica ‘Cido’ and Ch. × superba ‘Crimson and Gold’ and ‘Texas Scarlet’. The leaves are considered to be a poor source of pectin (approximately 10% in tea leaves) with 50% being homogalacturonan pectin consisting only of galacturonic acid, and the obtained results confirm it39.

For comparison, Oliveira et al.40 determined organic acid profiles for 36 Cydonia oblonga Miller leaves from Portugal and identified the following acids: quinic, citric, malic, oxalic, shikimic and fumaric, with quinic being the dominant one (4.9–9.1 g/100 g dw). In turn, Andrade et al.41 examined some traditional fruit tree leaves used in Brazilian folk medicine, and within organic acids, oxalic acid was also present in the highest concentration. Comparing the content of sugar to the content of organic acids, it was found that the ratio, depending on the analyzed leaf sample, ranges from 0.4 to 4.2. Moreover, looking at the average value of this coefficient (equal to 1.0), it was found that Chaenomeles leaves contain the same amount of sugars as acids, and thus they are similar to the fruits of Berberis L. or Hippophaë rhamnoides L42.

Anticholinergic potential shown by inhibition of acetylcholinesterase (AChE) and butyrylcholinesterase (BuChE)

Currently, oxidative stress and micro- and macro-inflammation in the nervous system are considered the main cause of brain damage in Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Progressive degradation in the functionality of cholinergic pathways leads to cognitive decline. The ability to keep acetylcholine levels in check by inhibiting AChE is now the mainstay of treatment for mild to moderate AD and is the primary mechanism of action of the FDA-approved drugs: donepezil, rivastigmine and galantamine. New scientific evidence emerging every now and then supports the potential use of dual inhibitors (AChE and BuChE) in the prevention and treatment of AD43.

That is why there is a constant search for natural sources of cholinesterase inhibitors, which will be characterized by the same or higher effectiveness than their synthetic counterparts, and at the same time will be safer to use, e.g. through lower allergic reactions. Additionally, plant nutraceuticals may have other effects, such as antioxidant, antidiabetic, anti-inflammatory, or anti-carcinogenic.

The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of acetylcholinesterase and butyrylcholinesterase ranged from 20.2 to 39.8 and from 26.4 to 39.9 mg of sample per mL of enzyme (p ≤ 0.05). The varieties showing the highest ability to inhibit AChE and BuChE were found to be Ch. × superba ‘Texas Scarlet’ and ‘Andenken an Karl Ramcke’, while the least effective in this analysis were Ch. japonica ‘Cameo’ and Ch. speciosa ‘Rubra’, respectively (Table 4). The tested Chaenomeles leaves samples showed similar mean AChE and BuChE inhibition values with IC50 of 29.5 and 30.0 mg/mL, respectively, as was the case with the Chaenomeles fruit6. Regarding the analyzed species, in the case of inhibiting the activity of both enzymes, Ch. speciosa leaves turned out to be a more effective inhibitor.

The calculated Pearson correlation coefficient revealed that carotenoids and triterpenes are more strongly correlated with the anti-AChE potential (R2 = 0.421 and 0.268, respectively), while chlorophyll compounds show a higher affinity for anti-BuChE activity (R2 = 0.407). Antioxidant capacity

In this study, ABTS, FRAP and ORAC protocols were used to assess the antioxidant capacity of the selected leaves samples (Table 4). The analyzed leaves of selected Chaenomeles species and varieties showed significant variation (p ≤ 0.05) among the variants. The strongest antioxidant potential verified by the ABTS method was shown by Ch. × superba ˈAndenken an Karl Ramckeˈ (30.8 mmol Trolox/100 g dw) and the lowest by Ch. japonica ˈRed Joyˈ (7.5 mmol Trolox/100 g dw, respectively). On the other hand, when considering the values of antioxidant activity within the analyzed species, they can be arranged according to increasing activity: Ch. × superba > Ch. speciosa > Ch. japonica, with mean values of 21.9, 17.1, 15.2 mmol Trolox/100 g dw, respectively. Much higher values of ABTS antioxidant activity for the leaves of three varieties (Rasa, Darius and Rondo) of Chaenomeles japonica were obtained by Urbanaviciute et al.9 in the range from 60.64 to 106.24 mmol Trolox/100 g dw. A similarly high value was also obtained by Chojnacka et al.7 for crude phenolic extract from Chaenomeles japonica L. leaves – 165 mmol Trolox/100 g dw. The highest antioxidant capacity in the FRAP assay was shown by Ch. speciosa ˈSimoniiˈ (46.7 mmol Trolox/100 g dw) while the lowest was shown by a variety of the same species – ˈNivalisˈ (9.9 mmol Trolox/100 g dw). Among species, the antioxidant activity showed the same dependence as in the case of the method with the ABTS radical. In the studies by Teleszko and Wojdyło44, the antioxidant activity measured by the same method for Chaenomeles japonica leaves was slightly higher at 40.09 mmol Trolox/100 g dw. In turn, in research of Urbanavičiūtė et al.9 the values were almost two times lower than the average FRAP activity in the analyzed samples – from 12.49 to 18.97 mmol Trolox/100 g dw. By analyzing the results of the antioxidant activity measured as the oxygen radical absorbance capacity (ORAC), significant variation was found between the analyzed varieties (p ≤ 0.05). Values ranging from 19.0 (Ch. japonica ‘Cameo’) to 96.5 mmol Trolox/100 g (Ch. × superba ‘Pink Lady’) were obtained. Comparing the obtained results with the values in the ABTS test, it was noted that the average ORAC antioxidant activity was over 2.5 times higher than that with ABTS reagent. A similar relationship between these two assays was noted by Tkacz et al.45 for sea buckthorn fruit, and Wojdyło et al.22 in research on selected Polish fruit and their leaves. In the same study, they obtained over 10 times higher activity of quince leaves (464.0–588.9 mmol Trolox/100 g dw).

Analyzing the calculated Pearson correlation coefficient for the methods of assessing antioxidant activity, a high positive correlation was found between the ABTS and FRAP tests (R2 = 0.649), a moderate positive correlation between the FRAP and ORAC assays (R2 = 0.468) and a low positive correlation between the ABTS and ORAC methods (R2 = 0.221). Moreover, only for the ORAC method is there a high positive correlation with anticholinergic activity, both in the acetylcholinesterase (AChE) and butyrylcholinesterase (BuChE) tests – R2 = 0.707 and 0.523, respectively. Among all phytochemicals determined in this study, only the content of triterpenes was moderately positively correlated with the ABTS and FRAP tests – R2 = 0.392 and 0.414, respectively.

Principal components analysis (PCA)

The first three principal components have eigenvalues (variances of the principal components) greater than 1, while PC1 and PC2 explain 66.07% of the variation in the data. Therefore, the rest of the components were considered to be not important enough to include. PCA (Fig. 4) examined the relationships between antioxidant and anticholinergic activity and chemical composition (carotenoid and chlorophyll compounds, triterpenes, minerals, sugars and organic acids) of leaves of 12 Chaenomeles varieties.

PC1, which explained 37.03% of the total variance, comprised mainly analyzed phytochemicals. As confirmed by positive values of PC1 (right half of the graph) some Ch. × superba varieties (‘Nicoline’, ‘Texas Scarlet’ and ‘Colour Trail’) were rich in chlorophylls and exhibited high anticholinergic potential, while others (Ch. speciosa ‘Rubra’ and Ch. japonica ‘Red Joy’) were rich in organic acids, iron and two main triterpenes – oleanolic and ursolic acids.

Conclusions

In this paper, a comprehensive analysis of pigments (carotenoids and chlorophylls), minerals, triterpenes, sugars and organic acids was undertaken for the first time in the leaves of 12 varieties of Ch. japonica, Ch. speciosa and Ch. × superba. The study confirmed biochemical and functional differences among studied samples. Ch. × superba ‘Colour Trail’, ‘Nicoline’, ‘Pink Lady’, ‘Texas Scarlet’ had a high content of carotenoids and chlorophylls compared to the remaining leaves, while Ch. speciosa ‘Simonii’, ‘Rubra’, and Ch. × superba ‘Colour Trail’, ‘Nicoline’ contained a high concentration of triterpenoids. Moreover, all Ch. speciosa varieties showed significant anticholinergic potential, measured as the ability to inhibit AChE and BuChE. In conclusion, Chaenomeles leaves may be used as components in medical and/or cosmetic industry or to become an important ingredient in the production of functional food. Nevertheless, more research is needed on an in vivo scale (animal, clinical and epidemiological studies).

Materials and methods

Chemicals and standards

Methyl cyanide, methanoic acid, and methyl alcohol for high pressure liquid chromatography and mass spectrometry were from Merck (Germany). Hexane, ethyl acetate, chloroform, dichloromethane, propanone, nitric acid, hydrochloric acid and magnesium carbonate hydroxide pentahydrate were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Germany). Reference standards came from Extrasynthese (France) and AccuStandard (USA), while all chemicals used for determination of biological activities were from MilliporeSigma (USA).

The purity of all reagents used for extraction and those used to determine in vitro biological activities was ≥ 98% (regent grade). All reagents used for mineral determination were ≥ 99% pure (spectroscopy grade). Reagents used for chromatographic analyses were ≥ 99% pure (LC-MS grade).

Material and procedure of sample preparation

Leaves of Ch. speciosa (‘Nivalis’, ‘SImonii’, ‘Rubra’), Ch. japonica (‘Cameo’, ‘Cido’, ‘Red Joy’) and Ch. × superba (‘Andenken an Karl Ramcke’, ‘Colour Trail’, ‘Crimson and Gold’, ‘Nicoline’, ‘Pink Lady’, Texas Scarlet’) were collected in October 2018 in Wrocław, Poland (Experimental Orchard) and Skierniewice, Poland (Research Institute of Horticulture).

Leaves were gently washed with water and frozen at -82 °C. Then they were dried using a FreeZone 2.5 freeze dryer (LABCONCO; USA) and crushed to obtain fine and dry powder for all further chemical analysis.

Qualitative and quantitative determination of carotenoids and chlorophylls

Extraction of carotenoids and chlorophylls was performed as follows: approximately 300 mg of the lyophilized sample was weighed into a 10 mL tube, 0.5 g of MgCO3 × Mg(OH)2 × 5H2O and 5 mL of 2-propanone : n-hexane : methyl alcohol (50% : 25% : 25%) were added, and extraction was performed for 30 min using an RS-60 rotator (bioSan; Latvia). Then, it was centrifuged at 14 875 rpm (10 min; 20 °C) using an MPW 352R laboratory centrifuge (MPW Med. Instruments; Poland), the extract was decanted from the sediment and the extraction was repeated three times. The extracts were finally combined and evaporated to dryness using an XcelVap system (Horizon Technology, Inc.; USA). The dry residue was dissolved in 100 µL of pure methyl alcohol and filtered using 0.20 μm syringe filter (Waters Corp.; USA).

Instrumental analysis was conducted using the ACQUITY LC system and coupled with the Xevo G2 QTof/ESI mass spectrometer (Waters; USA) in negative ionization mode. The samples (0.01 mL) were separated at 32 °C on an RP C18 column (dimensions: 0.21 × 10 cm, 1.7 μm, Waters; USA). A gradient separation over 16.60 min was performed with a mixture of methyl cyanide : methyl alcohol (70% : 30%) and 0.1% methanolic acid (aqueous solution) at a flow of 500 µL/min. The separation parameters and mass spectrometer settings have been previously described by Wojdyło et al.14. Retinol activity equivalent (RAE) was calculated assuming that 12 mg of β-carotene corresponds to 1 mg of RAE. Each sample was analyzed in three independent replicates and the result (which is the average) was expressed in mg per 100 g dry weight (dw).

Qualitative determination of triterpenes

Extraction of triterpenes was performed as previously described Turkiewicz et al.5.

Instrumental analysis was conducted using the ACQUITY LC system (Waters; USA). The samples (0.003 mL) were separated at 30 °C on a C18 PAH column (dimensions: 0.21 × 15 cm, 3.5 μm, Agilent Technologies Inc.; USA). A gradient elution over 30 min was performed with a mixture water and methyl cyanide at a flow of 250 µL/min. The chromatograms of triterpenes were measured at λ = 210 nm. Each sample was analyzed in three independent replicates and the result (which is the average) was expressed in mg per 100 g of dw.

Determination of micro- and macroelements

For the analysis of minerals, samples subjected to wet ashing were used, according to Turkiewicz et al.5.

Instrumental analysis was conducted using atomic absorption spectrophotometer AA-7000 (SHIMADZU; Japan). Calcium (Ca; λ = 422.7 nm), potassium (K; λ = 766.5 nm), magnesium (Mg; λ = 285.2 nm), iron (Fe; λ = 248.3 nm), sodium (Na; λ = 589.0 nm), manganese (Mn; λ = 279.5 nm), zinc (Zn; λ = 213.9 nm), and copper (Cu; λ = 324.8 nm) contents were analyzed using a flame continuous mode with an ASC-7000 autosampler. The oxidizing gas was air at a pressure of 350 kPa at a flow of 900 L/h and acetylene was the combustible gas (pressure: 90 kPa, flow: 180 L/h). Each sample was analyzed in three independent replicates and the result (which is the average) was expressed in mg per 100 g of dw.

Qualitative determination sugars, polyols and organic acids

The samples for sugars and organic acids analysis were prepared according to method previously published by Turkiewicz et al.46.

Instrumental analysis of sugars and polyols was conducted using a Nexera LC-40 system with an ELSD-LT III detector (Shimadzu Corp.; Japan). The analysis (injection volume 0.002 mL) was performed on a ULTRON AF-HILIC-CD (HT) (0.3 × 10 cm, 2 μm, Shinwa Chemical Industries Ltd., Japan) at 40 °C. A gradient elution over 6.5 min was achieved with a mixture methyl cyanide and water (85% : 15%) at a flow of 750 µL/min.

Instrumental analysis of organic acids was conducted using the ACQUITY LC system (Waters; USA). The samples (0.01 mL) were separated at 30 °C on an IEX H column (0.008 × 0.25 cm, 8 μm, Watrex; Czech Republic). A isocratic separation over 27 min was achieved with a 0.0841% sulfuric acid (aqueous solution) at a flow of 800 µL/min. The chromatograms were recorded at λ = 210 nm.

Each sample was analyzed in three independent replicates and the result (which is the average) was expressed in g of sugar or g of organic acid per 100 g of dw.

Determination of in vitro biological activities

The extraction process for spectrophotometric and fluorometric analyses was conducted as previously described Turkiewicz et al.6.

Anticholinergic activity was monitored as the ability to inhibit acetylcholinesterase and butyrylcholinesterase, as stated previously Tkacz et al.47. Briefly, in a 96-well clear plate, the assay medium in each well consisted of 0.04 mL of a 0.05 M Tris-HCl buffer at pH 7.5 containing 100 mg% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA), 0.1 mL of 0.001 M Ellman’s reagent, 0.02 mL of 0.0025 M substrate (acetylcholine iodide or butyrylcholine iodide) and 0.02 mL of 1 mU/µL enzyme (AChE or BuChE). The assay mixture containing 0.03 mL of diluted sample, buffer, DTNB, and substrate was preincubated for 15 min at 37 °C, before the enzyme was added to begin the reaction. Positive control (100% of enzyme activity) contained buffer instead of sample, negative control (no enzyme activity) contained buffer instead of sample and enzyme. Blank (background) contained buffer instead of enzyme. The absorbance was monitored at λ = 412 nm. The relative inhibition rate (%) was calculated for at least three extract concentrations. Each sample was analyzed in three independent replicates and the result (which is the average) was expressed in IC50 (mg per mL).

Antioxidant capacity was determined using ABTS, FRAP and ORAC assays following a method described previously Tkacz et al.45. Each sample was analyzed in three independent replicates and the result (which is the average) was expressed in mmol of Trolox per 100 g of dw.

All measurements were conducted using a Synergy H1 multi plate reader (BioTek Instruments, Inc., USA).

Statistical analysis and data visualization

Significant differences at the accepted level of significance equal to: p ≤ 0.05 between the results of individual replicates were evaluated using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test. Principal component analysis (PCA) (correlation biplot, Pearson’s type (n-1)) was performed to highlight correlations between samples.
