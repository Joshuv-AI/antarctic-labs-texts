Leveraging ChatGPT and explainable AI for enhancing clinical decision support
Authors: Radwa El Shawi, Leila Jamel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Large language models (LLMs) excel in many natural language processing tasks. However, their direct application to tabular, domain-specific clinical data remains challenging, as they lack innate mechanisms for reasoning over structured numerical features. This paper presents HealthAI-Prompt, a novel framework that systematically adapts LLMs for tabular clinical decision-making-specifically, predicting diabetes risk-through contextual prompts that combine detailed task descriptions with domain knowledge. Our domain knowledge integration leverages insights from high-performing machine learning models optimized via automated machine learning (AutoML) technique, together with local explanations for representative examples. These explanations, generated by multiple methods, are rigorously evaluated using fidelity, stability, and monotonicity metrics, ensuring reliability and clinical validity. The most accurate explanations are then embedded into the prompts, enabling the LLM to interpret structured features in a clinically meaningful way without fine-tuning. This approach uniquely bridges AutoML-driven predictive modeling with LLM-based reasoning over tabular inputs, improving predictive accuracy, and transparency in healthcare AI. Through comparative analysis of HealthAI-Prompt and various AutoML techniques under diverse data conditions, we offer insights into the impact of different prompt engineering strategies on model performance. As part of our evaluation, we also include Chain-of-Thought prompting as a baseline to contextualize the gains of our proposed method.

Introduction

In recent years, the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) and natural language processing (NLP) technologies into healthcare systems has significantly advanced medical diagnostics, treatment recommendation systems, and overall patient care1. One critical challenge in this field involves effectively interpreting and utilizing extensive medical data to make informed decisions2. To tackle this challenge, prompt engineering has emerged as a promising approach to enhance the performance and interpretability of AI models in healthcare applications3,4. Prompt engineering entails crafting precise and contextually relevant prompts that guide AI models to generate accurate predictions or analyses5.

A prompt can be understood as an additional contextual information provided to an AI model, which helps activate the model and provoke specific responses tailored to the task6. In healthcare, prompts have the potential to play a pivotal role by extracting contextual guidance and explicit instructions for AI models, thereby facilitating the accurate extraction of medical information and improving prediction performance. In medical image analysis, prompts are crucial in guiding AI models to focus on specific regions of interest or pathologies within images. For instance, a prompt highlighting the presence of pulmonary nodules in chest X-rays can enhance AI models’ accuracy in detecting and classifying these abnormalities. Additionally, prompts support clinical decision support systems by contextualizing patient data and delivering pertinent information to healthcare providers7. Integrating prompts that extract essential clinical details from electronic health records enables AI systems to formulate personalized treatment recommendations that cater to individual patient needs.

Prompt design strategies for large language models (LLMs) vary based on task complexity and the amount of contextual information provided. These strategies include zero-shot, and few-shot prompting techniques. Zero-shot prompting is suited for straightforward tasks where a single prompt leverages the model’s pre-existing knowledge to generate responses. For example, in healthcare, zero-shot prompting might provide an overview of common diseases. Conversely, few-shot prompting involves using a few examples or queries to guide the model towards generating desired outputs. An instance could be guiding the model with patient scenarios to predict the presence or absence of heart disease based on provided medical conditions. Historically, GPT-18 demonstrated initial capabilities in zero-shot tasks, while subsequent models like GPT-29 and GPT-310 expanded towards few-shot prompting11. However, the format and sequence of prompts significantly influence task performance, making prompt optimization a critical area of ongoing research. While fine-tuning for specialized tasks is beneficial, our research focuses on leveraging guided prompt design to enhance decision-making processes within the medical field.

While prior work has demonstrated the potential of LLMs in healthcare for tasks such as summarization12, question answering13, and dialogue generation14, these efforts have predominantly focused on unstructured text. In contrast, structured tabular data—including lab values, demographic information, and vital signs—underpins many real-world clinical decision support systems, yet remains significantly underexplored in the context of LLM prompt engineering. Some recent studies have attempted to apply LLMs to clinical prediction with simplified inputs or synthetic EHR formats15,16, but these typically use static templates and do not incorporate learning-based signals for prompt construction. Furthermore, prompting strategies such as Chain-of-Thought17, though successful in reasoning tasks, are not specifically tailored to clinical prediction tasks involving structured numerical and categorical data. Our work addresses this gap by introducing HealthAI-Prompt, a unified framework that transforms structured clinical data into effective LLM prompts by combining AutoML-driven case selection with explanation-guided enrichment. An AutoML-trained model identifies representative examples using predictive confidence, augments them with local explanations, and converts them into concise natural-language narratives for few-shot prompting with ChatGPT. This integration aims to enhance predictive performance in clinical decision support. Literature review

Prompt engineering

Prompt engineering has become a crucial technique for adapting large language models (LLMs) to healthcare applications. Early work demonstrated promising performance in clinical tasks such as chronic disease detection18, medical report generation19, and EHR question-answering20. Systematic studies of clinical LLM use highlight the importance of prompt design but also note that many efforts rely on manually crafted templates and lack optimization strategies21,22. A second strand of research investigates instruction tuning and prompt tuning approaches. For example, large biomedical LLMs-such as BioInstruct-are optimized via instruction tuning on medical prompts, improving performance in QA, information extraction, and generation over zero-shot baselines23. Similarly, methods like Biomed-DPT apply prompt tuning to adapt models for biomedical image classification24. While these methods excel in their domains, they require model updating and are not applied to structured tabular data. Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting, including automatic variants such as Auto-CoT, has been shown to improve reasoning in multi-step tasks and medical question answering25,26. However, CoT primarily targets interpretability and reasoning rather than classification accuracy; its effectiveness in structured clinical prediction remains underexplored. Emerging work also examines the trade-offs in prompt engineering: OpenMedLM shows that few-shot prompting and CoT can rival fully fine-tuned open-source LLMs on medical QA benchmarks27, and empirical studies on clinical NLP highlight that prompt strategy choice (e.g., simple vs. heuristic vs. ensemble) substantially affects performance across tasks22.

Most existing prompt engineering studies for healthcare-whether few-shot, CoT, or instruction-tuned-focus on unstructured text and rely on static templates or manual exemplar selection, which limits adaptability to structured clinical data18–20. Approaches that incorporate automated case selection and explanation-based enrichment remain largely absent from the literature, leaving an open space for methods that dynamically tailor prompts to the nuances of tabular prediction tasks. Pre-trained language models in healthcare

LLMs and generative AI have significant potential across diverse domains, including finance28,29, healthcare30,31, agriculture32, and recommender systems33. LLMs are fundamentally designed to predict the next token in a sequence based on a given set of preceding tokens34. These probabilistic models estimate the likelihood of various sequences, facilitating the prediction of subsequent tokens. Token prediction remains a foundational application in NLP, leading to the development of numerous models based on this principle. Prompt engineering leverages these models, significantly enhancing their effectiveness across various tasks. Yang et al.35 conduct a comprehensive survey on the potential applications of LLMs in healthcare, emphasizing their utility in developing domain-specific models to enhance clinical outcomes. These models are designed to minimize errors and maximize efficiency in global healthcare delivery, either through building new models from the ground up or refining existing larger models. This approach promotes decentralized research, allowing researchers worldwide to tailor LLM applications to specific clinical tasks. Medical interventions are systematically classified into three primary stages: (1) pre-consultation, which involves initial screening and consultations for symptomatic and asymptomatic patients; (2) diagnosis, comprising the collection of patient history, physical examinations, and diagnostic procedures such as CT scans; and (3) management, which includes the prescription of medications, patient education, counseling, and handling of insurance claims35. Nazi and Peng36 categorize LLM applications in healthcare into three main areas: (1) patients use LLMs for health education, lifestyle advice, and virtual medical assistants; (2) doctors use them for treatment planning, diagnosis, and clinical support; and (3) healthcare systems apply LLMs in drug discovery, medical report synthesis, and imaging.

HealthAI-prompt: few-shot generative classification

Figure 1 presents HealthAI-Prompt framework, comprising two primary phases: the prompt construction phase and the LLM interaction and prediction phase. The primary objective of the prompt construction phase is to convert numerical data from the test dataset into a format interpretable by LLM, ensuring effective contextualization of patient data. This phase focuses on using carefully designed prompts to facilitate efficient interaction between the LLM and the clinical decision-making process. The prompt construction phase includes two key components. The first component is patient profiling and few-shot learning (Section “HealthAI-prompt: few-shot generative classification”), which involves creating a detailed task description within the prompt and employing few-shot learning. The second component is domain knowledge augmentation (Section “HealthAI-prompt: few-shot generative classification”), which comprises integrating knowledge from a high-performing machine learning model discovered through the Tree-based Pipeline Optimization Tool (TPOT), an AutoML framework40. This component also involves generating local explanations for a set of representative, high-confidence predictions using multiple model-agnostic explanation techniques, including SHAP, LIME, and ILIME. These explanations are evaluated using quantitative measures to assess their reliability and consistency, and the most effective method is selected and integrated into the final prompt. The interaction and prediction phase aims to leverage the natural language processing capabilities of the LLM to understand and analyze patient data, leading to informed and accurate predictions.

Patient profiling and few-shot learning

Patient Profiling: In the context of classical machine learning problems within the health domain, practitioners often encounter tabular data predominantly composed of numeric values (e.g., laboratory test results and vital signs). Given that LLMs are intrinsically designed to process and comprehend textual input, a systematic approach is required to convert numerical patient data into a format that LLMs can effectively interpret. The patient profiling phase focuses on transforming patient medical information and laboratory results into a coherent textual format. This transformation is crucial for enabling LLMs to accurately comprehend and reason over structured medical data. Prior studies have shown that providing context-rich descriptions of tabular features improves model reasoning and predictive performance41–43. This stage includes task instruction and attribute description (See Fig. 2). Before constructing the prompts, patient features undergo a systematic preprocessing pipeline. Numeric values are standardized and discretized using clinically meaningful thresholds, then paired with descriptive textual labels. This ensures that the model interprets inputs in a semantically meaningful clinical context rather than as raw numbers, enhancing both predictive performance and interpretability. This preprocessing occurs prior to incorporating few-shot examples into the prompt, ensuring that all input data is presented in a consistent and semantically meaningful format for the LLM. Task instruction involves a user-generated dataset description that provides contextual information about the dataset and the task of assessing diabetes risk. Risk levels are categorized as low risk (’0’) and high risk (’1’). Attribute description involves listing feature names with contextual information for each attribute involved in the risk assessment. This includes descriptions of attributes such as number of pregnancies, plasma glucose concentration, diastolic blood pressure, triceps skinfold thickness, 2-hour serum insulin, body mass index (BMI), diabetes pedigree function, and age, along with their possible ranges, which help the model understand the relevance and variation of each attribute in relation to the task.Fig. 2Patient profiling overview.Fig. 3Few-shot examples based on Pima Indians diabetes dataset.Fig. 4Domain knowledge integration with TPOT-optimized model and SHAP explanations for diabetes risk prediction.

Few-Shot Learning: The prompt introduces representative examples from the training dataset that depict various patient evaluations. These examples, randomly selected from each diabetes risk category, demonstrate different risk factors and their influence on diabetes risk assessment outcomes. By providing concrete instances of patient data, these examples enhance the model’s learning capabilities and illustrate diverse interpretations for clinical decision-making. To further enrich the model’s contextual understanding, a number of few-shot examples, denoted as N, are incorporated into the prompt template (See Fig. 3).

Domain knowledge augmentation

Domain knowledge enrichment is a crucial component of the HealthAI-Prompt framework. This enrichment is achieved by integrating two key elements: insights identified from a high-performing machine learning model using an AutoML approach (e.g., TPOT), and local explanations generated for selected instances (see Fig. 4). AutoML streamlines the processes of model selection, feature engineering, and hyperparameter tuning, reducing the need for extensive manual intervention44,45. TPOT further enhances this by employing genetic programming to automatically optimize machine learning pipelines, thus improving model performance and efficiency. To complement the optimized model, we select N random instances from the training dataset that were correctly predicted with high confidence, including both low and high-risk cases. Local explanations for these instances are then generated using multiple model-agnostic local explanation techniques including SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)46, Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME)47, and ILIME48. Experimental setup

Task and model optimization

Task: This study focuses on binary classification tasks in the domain of health risk assessments. The primary objective is to develop a mapping function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f: X \rightarrow \{0, 1\}$$\end{document} to predict a binary outcome \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y \in \{0, 1\}$$\end{document} for each input feature instance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x \in X$$\end{document}.

Dataset: We utilize the Pima Indians Diabetes Database from the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases53. This dataset includes 768 patient records, both with and without diabetes, and contains variables such as the number of pregnancies, blood glucose concentration, diastolic blood pressure, skinfold thickness, insulin levels, body mass index (BMI), diabetes pedigree function (a measure of genetic predisposition), age, and the corresponding health outcome (presence or absence of diabetes).

Model Selection and Hyperparameter tuning: For domain knowledge enrichment, we employed TPOT, which conducted the search for optimal machine learning pipelines over 100 generations, with each generation maintaining a population of 100 individuals within the genetic programming framework.

Evaluation: We employed 5-fold cross-validation with an 80/20 training/test split. All frameworks, including AutoML models, were optimized for the F1 score. Each AutoML framework was allocated a 10-minute time budget. To mitigate the influence of non-deterministic factors, the reported performance in each experiment is averaged over 10 trials. To assess the reliability of predicted probabilities, we also evaluated calibration using the Brier Score and Expected Calibration Error (ECE). Calibration was performed using isotonic regression on the predicted probabilities of the test set54.

API Model of OpenAI: For assessing diabetes risk from patient health records, we utilized the OpenAI API with the gpt-3.5-turbo-instruct model. The completions endpoint enabled prompt-based interactions with token-level log-probabilities, facilitating probability estimation and calibration analysis. The temperature parameter was set to 0 for deterministic outputs. Patient feature data were batched and processed via API calls to generate predictions. The integration used the OpenAI API, Tenacity library for retry management, and standard data processing tools.

Baseline AutoML frameworks: optimization methods and search spaces.

TPOT: TPOT employs genetic programming for ML pipeline optimization55, representing pipelines as tree structures with branches for distinct preprocessing pathways. Evolutionary optimization refines these pipelines. To mitigate overfitting from the expansive search space, TPOT uses multi-objective optimization to balance complexity and performance56. The search space can be constrained by specifying a pipeline template, defining high-level steps (e.g., “Selector-Transformer-Classifier”)57. While TPOT supports neural networks58, TPOT’s default search space primarily uses SCIKIT-LEARN59 and XGBOOST components.

AUTO-SKLEARN: AUTO-SKLEARN60 is based on the design of AUTO-WEKA, uses Bayesian optimization and is implemented in Python and optimizes pipelines built with SCIKIT-LEARN59. It warm-starts optimization through meta-learning44, beginning pipeline search with the best pipelines for the most similar datasets61. After pipeline search concludes, an ensemble is created from pipelines trained during the search using the procedure described by Caruana et al.62,63.

FLAML: FLAML64 is a fast and lightweight AutoML Library optimizes boosting frameworks (XGBOOST65 and LIGHTGBM66) and a small selection of SCIKIT-LEARN algorithms through a multi-fidelity randomized directed search called Cost-Frugal Optimization (CFO)67. This search is based on an expected cost for improvement, which tracks the expected computational cost of improving over the best model found so far for each learner. Only after choosing which learner to tune, hyperparameter optimization proceeds by a randomized directed search, sampling a new configuration from a unit sphere around the previous sample point. After evaluating its validation performance, the next sample point is moved in that direction (if better) or the opposite direction (if worse). FLAML positions itself as a fast AutoML framework designed to work within small time budgets.

GAMA: GAMA is designed as a modular AutoML tool for researchers, GAMA’s search method and post-processing are easily configurable and extensible68. By default, GAMA uses genetic programming to optimize linear ML pipelines with an arbitrary amount of preprocessing algorithms. Similar to TPOT, GAMA’s evolutionary algorithm uses NSGA-II to perform multi-objective optimization69, maximizing performance while minimizing the number of components in the pipeline. In contrast, GAMA’s evolutionary algorithm is asynchronous and does not work with distinct generations, allowing for higher resource utilization. The final model is constructed through ensemble selection62,63, similar to AUTO-SKLEARN.

AutoGluon-Tabular: AutoGluon automates machine learning across various tasks, including image, text, and tabular data70. The subsystem dedicated to tabular data is called AutoGluon-Tabular, referred to here as AutoGluon. Unlike other AutoML systems discussed, AutoGluon does not perform pipeline search or hyperparameter tuning. Instead, it uses a predefined set of models that are combined through multi-layer stacking and ensembling. AutoGluon’s ensemble methodology consists of three layers. In the first layer, models from various model families are trained directly on the data. In the second layer, the same types of models from the first layer are used as stacking learners, trained with both the input data and the predictions from the first layer. In the third and final layer, the predictions from the second-layer models are combined into an ensemble using an ensemble method62, initially used in AutoML by AUTO-SKLEARN60. To comply with time constraints, AutoGluon may prematurely stop iterative algorithms or skip training certain models. Given more time, AutoGluon will train additional models using the same algorithms and hyperparameter configurations on different data splits, further enhancing the generalization of the stacking layer.

H2O AutoML: H2O AutoML71 is built on the H2O distributed machine learning platform72. H2O AutoML evaluates a portfolio of bespoke algorithm configurations and performs random searches across the majority of the supervised learning algorithms offered in H2O. Early stopping is applied to the searches for efficiency. The amount of time H2O AutoML spends searching each algorithm is pre-specified by the authors, though this can be customized. This allocates the optimization work done at each step of the algorithm, favoring stronger algorithms (e.g., XGBoost) or giving them more time over others (e.g., GLM). To further enhance model performance, H2O AutoML also trains two types of stacked ensemble models at various stages: an ensemble using all available models at a given step, and an ensemble with only the best models of each algorithm type at that step. H2O AutoML aims to cover a large search space quickly and relies on stacking to boost model performance. Additionally, H2O AutoML uses a predefined strategy for imputation, normalization, and categorical encoding for each algorithm and does not currently optimize over preprocessing pipelines by default, but preprocessing can be enabled as an option. The H2O AutoML algorithm is designed to generate models that are very fast at inference time, rather than strictly focusing on maximizing model accuracy, with the goal of balancing these two competing factors to produce practical models suited for production environments.

CoT Baseline: The CoT baseline adopts the Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting strategy by including intermediate natural language reasoning steps within few-shot examples, inspired by the methodology of Wei et al.17. While Wei et al. employed a different LLM (PaLM), our implementation uses the same GPT-3.5-turbo-instruct model and API configuration as our main method to ensure a controlled comparison. Both the CoT baseline and HealthAI-Prompt share identical input structure, including task instructions, clinical attribute descriptions, and patient examples. The key difference lies in the design of the reasoning component and the use of domain knowledge.

Conclusion and future work

This work introduces HealthAI-Prompt, a prompting-based framework that effectively leverages large language models (LLMs) for clinical risk prediction tasks, demonstrated here on diabetes risk assessment. By converting structured clinical data into detailed natural language prompts enriched with domain knowledge derived from high-performing AutoML models and explainability techniques, our approach achieves improved predictive accuracy and calibration compared to both traditional supervised learning and CoT-based prompting baselines. This highlights the potential of LLMs to serve as flexible, interpretable, and data-efficient tools in healthcare applications. We acknowledge that our evaluation is currently limited to a single dataset-the Pima Indians Diabetes Dataset-with 786 samples. While this dataset allows for controlled and reproducible benchmarking, it limits the generalizability of our findings. Our primary aim is not to build a deployable clinical tool, but to propose and validate a novel methodology for LLM-based structured prediction in healthcare. To address this limitation, future work will involve evaluating HealthAI-Prompt on larger, more diverse clinical datasets, including multi-center and longitudinal cohorts. This will enable robust assessment of generalization, fairness, and scalability in real-world settings. Despite strong performance, several practical limitations warrant consideration. Each prediction requires submitting a full prompt-including task instructions, few-shot examples, and domain-grounded explanations-to the LLM at inference time. In our experiments, prompts with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_s=16$$\end{document} examples and explanation components typically consumed 2500–3000 tokens per query. This introduces a constant per-query computational and financial cost that may limit scalability in real-time or high-throughput clinical environments. Moreover, dependence on proprietary API-based models such as GPT-3.5-turbo-instruct presents challenges around inference latency, limited control over model behavior, and potential data privacy risks-especially important in regulated healthcare settings. To mitigate these barriers, future directions include exploring prompt compression, adaptive context selection, and leveraging open-source LLMs deployable in local or hybrid infrastructures. We also plan to expand HealthAI-Prompt to multi-class and temporal prediction tasks. Collaborations with clinicians will inform enhancements to prompt design and interpretability, supporting practical deployment and clinical trust.
