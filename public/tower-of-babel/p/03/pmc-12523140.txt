Longitudinal multi-omics analyses of chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy in response to taxanes
Authors: Anukriti Sharma, Ken B Johnson, Alper Sen, Bihua Bie, Emily E Rhoades, Jacob Steenblik, Jennifer DeVecchio, Courtney Hershberger, Mei Wei, N Lynn Henry, Carla Bou Dargham, G Thomas Budd, Joseph Foss, Daniel M Rotroff
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTaxanes are an effective class of chemotherapeutics, but they frequently cause chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN). CIPN complicates treatment, often leading to alterations or discontinuation. This study aims to elucidate mechanisms underlying CIPN associated with taxane administration in breast cancer patients, utilizing a multi-omic, longitudinal approach to characterize molecular changes over time that can help gain insight into the pathophysiology of CIPN.MethodsWe assessed 358 patients receiving taxanes in (neo)adjuvant chemotherapy regimens over 12 months, evaluating CIPN at seven time-points, including pre- and post-treatment. CIPN was defined as an increase of eight or more points from baseline linearized CIPN20 scores. Changes in 194 mRNAs, 798 miRNAs, and 85 metabolites were analyzed at each of the seven time points, representing changes across six time-intervals. Molecular marker changes were analyzed using the semi-parametric OmicsLonDA package. Features identified through this analysis were then employed in Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) to evaluate the activation (Z score > 0) and inhibition (Z score < 0) of pathways related to CIPN.ResultsOur analysis revealed significant differences (FDR-P < 0.05) in the expression levels of 99 mRNAs, 55 miRNAs, and ten metabolites between CIPN-positive and negative patients at various time points. Notably, Opioid-receptor-mu-1 (OPRM1) mRNA levels were elevated in CIPN-negative patients while CAMK1D mRNA was higher in CIPN-positive patients from two to 12 months post-infusion. Tyrosine and capric acid metabolites were elevated in the CIPN-positive group from one to nine months post-infusion. Specific neuropathy-associated miRNAs, hsa-miR-31-5p and hsa-miR-184, showed differential expression between groups. Differentially expressed mRNAs were linked to 120 pathways, particularly in the Cyclic AMP Response Element-Binding Protein (CREB) signaling, opioid signaling, and endocannabinoid signaling pathways. Longitudinal analyses revealed activation-inhibition patterns in CREB and opioid signaling pathways over time.ConclusionsOur study offers valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms of CIPN in breast cancer patients treated with taxanes. By identifying key molecular markers and pathways, such as CREB and opioid signaling, we provide initial evidence for hypothesis generation and future research. While these findings are correlative and require further validation, they highlight potential biological mechanisms and serve as a basis for validating candidates as biomarkers or therapeutic targets.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14901-7.Keywords: Chemotherapy induced neuropathic pain, Breast cancer, Taxane, Paclitaxel, MRNA, MiRNA, Metabolites

Background

Many breast cancer patients treated with taxanes experience chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN) [1]. While some patients experience a resolution of CIPN symptoms after completing chemotherapy, around 30% of patients may suffer from CIPN lasting months or years [2–7]. Severe CIPN can lead to dose reductions or discontinuation of therapy, affecting overall treatment efficacy [8]. Therefore, addressing this adverse treatment effect is crucial for improving patient care and ensuring optimal therapeutic outcomes.

CIPN is influenced by multiple factors including genetic, lifestyle and other personal factors that are reflected in the complex interplay between genomics, proteomics, and metabolomics [9, 10]. This multifactorial nature complicates the identification of specific biomarkers or pathways involved in its pathogenesis. However, recent research has shed light on several key molecular pathways that play a role in CIPN development.

For example, the transcription factor CREB cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB) has been shown to participate in paclitaxel-induced neuropathic pain by activating the Dnmt3a gene in dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons, leading to epigenetic modifications that promote pain hypersensitivity [11]. Similarly, the opioid signaling pathway, especially involving mu-opioid receptors (MOR), has been implicated in the persistence and amplification of CIPN pain. Evidence suggests that MOR interacts with toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) pathways to sustain neuropathic pain and contribute to opioid hyperalgesia [12]. Additionally, the endocannabinoid system is gaining recognition for its potential to modulate neuroinflammation and pain in CIPN, with preclinical studies indicating that targeting endocannabinoid system (ECS) enzymes can alleviate neuropathic pain by reducing glial activation and neuroinflammation [13].

Beyond these pathways, studies investigating single nucleotide variants (SNVs) have identified genes involved in regulating microtubule function (e.g., TUBB2A, MAPT, GSK3B) and drug metabolism (e.g., CYP3A4, CYP2C8, CYP1B1) as associated with paclitaxel induced neuropathy [14–18]. Paclitaxel exposure also has been found to increase expression of inflammatory markers like C–C Motif Chemokine Ligand 2 (CCL2), Tumor Necrosis Factor Alpha (TNFα), and interleukin 6 (IL-6). It also been found to activate the NLR Family Pyrin Domain Containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, a multi-protein complex involved in inflammatory response [19, 20]. Evidence of micro ribonucleic acids (miRNA) role in CIPN is also limited. Studies have shown that miR-155 and miR-124 play a role in inflammation and neurodegeneration. miR-155 impacts a signaling pathway that involves Tumor Necrosis Factor Receptor 1 (TNFR1) and Transient Receptor Potential Ankyrin 1 (TRPA1), affecting the pain response in patients receiving bortezomib treatment. miR-451a influences paclitaxel resistance by regulating P-glycoprotein [21, 22]. There are few studies describing metabolic changes from taxanes and their role in the pathogenesis of neuropathic pain, and to our knowledge, only a single study specific to paclitaxel [23]. A cell-based metabolomics survey performed in neuroblastoma cells suggested a potential role of altered fatty acid oxidation in different neurotoxicity patterns observed such as neuropathic pain induced by paclitaxel treatment [23]. However, the role of metabolites deriving from other drugs, such as oxaliplatin, have been implicated to be involved in the etiology of CIPN. We previously published a review of various -omics and their reported associations with CIPN in the literature [9, 10]. Here we investigated whether molecular changes in mRNA, miRNA and inflammatory proteins, which are under both genetic and environmental influence, are associated with CIPN. Other studies have investigated independent associations with mRNA [19, 20, 24], miRNA [21, 22], metabolites [23, 25] and inflammatory proteins [19]. Each omics layer produces large-scale data that can be integrated effectively to provide a comprehensive view of the biological processes and interactions involved in CIPN; thus far, each omics platform has been assessed independently. Additionally, the aforementioned studies have specifically investigated the highest grade/severity CIPN instead of longitudinal monitoring, which could otherwise track the progression and resolution of CIPN over time. Since, CIPN may manifest at different times during and after the treatment, longitudinal assessment could provide insights into the temporal dynamics of molecular changes associated with neuropathy.

The aim of this study was to (i) explore possible relationships between changes in mRNA, miRNA, and metabolites over time in patients that did or did not develop CIPN when treated with taxanes for breast cancer and (ii) to explore the role of changes in mRNA expression in curated metabolic and cell signaling pathways throughout the 12-month observation period.

Methods

Study design

The study was reviewed and approved by the Cleveland Clinic (IRB# 20–908) and University of Utah Institutional Review Boards. Females presenting with breast cancer at the Cleveland Clinic and the University of Utah were screened for eligibility. Patient eligibility included being > 18 years of age, no history of pre-existing neuropathy or chronic pain, no prior treatment with taxanes, planned treatment with a taxane (paclitaxel, docetaxel) as a treatment for breast cancer in the adjuvant or neo-adjuvant setting, and being able to provide informed consent. For additional details on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, please refer to Supplementary Table S1.

The Genetics and Inflammatory Markers for CIPN (GENIE) study has been previously described [9]. This study followed and assessed 358 eligible patients at seven time points: pre-treatment (visit 1), during treatment (visits 2–4), and post-treatment (visits 5–7, Fig. 1A). Out of 358 patients, 202 were enrolled at Cleveland Clinic and 156 were enrolled at University of Utah. Panels of 194 pain/inflammation-related mRNAs, 798 untargeted miRNAs, and 85 identified metabolites were assessed at each time point. mRNA data was available for 358 patients, miRNA data for 333 patients, and metabolite data for 239 patients.

Longitudinal CIPN scores

CIPN was measured using the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire Chemotherapy Induced Peripheral Neuropathy 20 (CIPN20) [26]. Details of this survey questionnaire are described in Supplemental File 1. All scale scores were linearly converted to a 0–100 scale, following the methodology used in previous studies [27]. Linearized CIPN20 scores over time were divided into two groups by CIPN severity: CIPN positive [CIPN(+)] and CIPN negative [CIPN(-)]; CIPN(+) was defined as an increase in CIPN20 score of 8 or more from baseline at any time during the 12-month observation period [28]. Our decision to use an 8-point increase on the entire CIPN20 instrument as the threshold for stratifying CIPN(+) and CIPN(-) patients is supported by prior studies [27, 29, 30], which demonstrate the clinical relevance of this criterion. Additionally, our preliminary analyses showed no significant differences in the trajectories of the CIPN8 sensory subscale and the entire CIPN20 instrument (P > 0.05), supporting the use of the full CIPN20 as a conservative approach for comprehensiveness. CIPN20 scores were graphically presented over time using violin plots using the package geom_violin from the ggplot2 package in R v4.2 [31].

Blood sample processing

Whole blood samples were processed at each site to extract DNA, RNA, and Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cells (PBMCs). DNA was stored at 4 °C, RNA was stored in Qiagen RNA Lysis (RLT) Plus buffer at −20 °C, and PBMCs were kept at −80 °C until further processing. For detailed information about each extraction, please refer to Supplementary File 1.

mRNA and miRNA processing

The mRNA and miRNA were extracted from blood samples as described above and expression data were generated using the Nanostring nCounter platform (Seattle, WA). A custom code set of 194 mRNAs were developed using 106 mRNAs that were part of Sensory Perception of Pain Pathway (Gene Ontology ID: 0043991) and 88 mRNAs that were associated with chronic pain and neurotoxicity as per previous literature [16, 32, 33]. Individual mRNAs are listed in Supplementary Table S2. mRNA expression detection was confirmed using the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) portal https://gtexportal.org/ and the Human Protein Atlas https://www.proteinatlas.org/. miRNA expression panel consisted of 827 miRNAs across a broad range of biological targets including six positive controls, eight negative controls, two spike-in controls, six ligation controls, and five miRNA reference controls, namely Beta-2-microglobulin (B2M), Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), Ribosomal protein L19 (RPL19), Actin beta (ACTB), and Ribosomal protein lateral stalk subunit P0 (RPLP0). The mRNA data was normalized using the following housekeeping genes— Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), TATA-box binding protein (TBP), Hypoxanthine–guanine phosphoribosyltransferase 1 (HPRT1), Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD), Glucuronidase beta (GUSB), and Phosphoglycerate kinase 1 (PGK1). From these data the background noise was calculated using the geometric mean of all negative controls plus two standard deviations, and subsequently subtracted from the data, then a positive control normalization was performed. See Supplementary File 1 for additional details on quality control of mRNA and miRNA data generated from Nanostring.

Metabolite processing

Plasma samples were assayed for selected metabolites using gas chromatography mass spectrometry (GC/MS) by the University of California Davis West Coast Metabolomics Center (Davis, CA). Details of the assay are provided in the supplemental text under Methods (Supplemental File1). Additionally, based on the information provided in the manual by the core facility that generated the data, vector normalization was utilized to mitigate the effects of instrument sensitivity drifts between series. This process involved calculating the total peak heights for each identified metabolite in each sample. The peak sums derived from the total ion chromatogram (mTIC) were used to eliminate potential non-biological artifacts—such as column bleed, plasticizers, or other contaminants—from the biological normalization procedure. Furthermore, pooled controls (using samples) and run-specific internal controls were incorporated into each run to maintain quality control and facilitate quantification corrections.

RNA and metabolite changes over time between CIPN(+) and CIPN(-) groups

To explore our study hypothesis, mRNA, miRNA, and metabolites were compared across time between CIPN(+) and CIPN(-) groups. Analyses were performed using the statistical software, R v4.2 [31]. Significant changes (FDR P < 0.05) in mRNA, miRNA, and metabolites were referred to as features. Three datasets were analyzed: mRNA, miRNA, and metabolites. Differences between groups in features over time were analyzed using the OmicsLonDA package in R [34]. This is a semi-parametric approach utilizing smoothing splines to model longitudinal data and identify significant time intervals of features based on an empirical distribution constructed via a permutation procedure. Features were identified over six-time intervals between the seven patient visits. The intervals were labelled as: int1-2 (interval between Visit1 and Visit2), int2-3 (Visit2 and Visit3), int3-4 (Visit3 and Visit4), int4-5 (Visit4 and Visit5), int5-6 (Visit5 and Visit6), and int6-7 (Visit6 and Visit7). To address the challenge of multiple comparisons and control the false discovery rate (FDR), the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure was applied separately for each omics dataset (mRNA, miRNA, metabolites) across all time intervals. This approach ensures that the probability of false positives is controlled at the desired FDR threshold (P < 0.05).

By dividing the data into specific intervals, this interval-based analysis facilitates the examination of how changes in omic markers progress over time between specific time points, enhancing the sensitivity of the temporal tends. This method also addresses the challenge of varying interpersonal -omics values by implementing two strategies: (1) adjusting each sample relative to its baseline and (2) normalizing each feature’s measurements using min–max scaling [34]. Following the adjustment of measurements based on each subject's profile, the Gaussian smoothing spline regression model was applied, after which a permutation test was conducted to generate an empirical distribution of the test statistic for each time interval. The P values were then corrected for multiple testing using the BH procedure to manage the false discovery rate (FDR) [35]. The results from OmicsLonDA for mRNA and miRNA were visualized in a volcano plot using the EnhancedVolcano package version 1.16 in R (see Supplementary Figures S1 and S2). The key statistical steps used in the analysis of CIPN data, including the application of the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR), are summarized in Supplementary Table S3.

Pathway analyses

Significant features (FDR P < 0.05) identified using the interval-based OmicsLonDA approach (described above) were subsequently analyzed using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) software (QIAGEN, Redwood City, CA) [35]. mRNA, miRNAs, and metabolites, along with their corresponding fold change (FC) values between CIPN(+) and CIPN(-) groups, were mapped to the corresponding database objects in the IPA knowledgebase to identify significantly enriched canonical pathways. In IPA, canonical pathways refer to well-established biological pathways that have been defined and curated based on the current scientific literature. A comparison analysis was done using P value overlap to calculate a right-tailed Fisher’s exact test that identified significant pathways [35]. To control for multiple comparisons, P values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure, ensuring an FDR threshold of P < 0.05. Significant canonical pathways were identified in the mRNA data across the six -time intervals; however, we did not find any significant pathways using the miRNA and metabolite data, so we only proceeded with the mRNA analysis. Pathway Activity Analysis was employed to determine whether canonical pathways were activated or inhibited, with the overall activation/inhibition states predicted based on IPAs Z-score algorithm [35]. mRNA data were also mapped to neurological conditions in the disease and function knowledgebase in IPA. P values were adjusted for multiple testing using BH-FDR. The significant canonical pathways were visually organized using heatmap across six time-intervals, with Z-scores for inhibition and activation represented by a color gradient that ranges from grey (indicating positive Z-scores or activation) to dark red (indicating negative Z-scores or inhibition). The individual genes associated with the selected four canonical pathways—namely, the Cyclic AMP Response Element-Binding Protein (CREB) signaling pathway, opioid signaling pathway, endocannabinoid neuronal synapse pathway, and neuropathic pain signaling pathway—were presented in heatmap visuals. These heatmaps show fold-change expression: positive fold-change (in red) indicates higher expression in the CIPN(-) group, while negative fold-change (in blue) indicates higher expression in the CIPN(+) group. The four chosen canonical pathways were also illustrated through schematic mechanistic networks provided by IPA. Additionally, the neurologic pathways that were significantly enriched in the data were represented in a heatmap, with activation Z-scores denoted in red and inhibition Z-scores in blue. Individual genes related to the neurologic pathways were illustrated in radial networks, where upregulation in the CIPN(-) group was represented by red node colors and downregulation in the CIPN(+) group was shown in green, with arrows indicating individual mRNA’s predicted activation or inhibitory relationships within the overall pathway. In our results section, we have clearly distinguished between observed data and predictions generated by pathway analysis tools such as Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA). For each pathway, we created two sub-sections:"Observed Data"to report raw gene expression findings and"IPA Predictions"to describe inferred network-level interactions and pathway activation/inhibition patterns.

Longitudinal multi-omics analyses

Multi-omics longitudinal data were analyzed between groups using the R package, timeOmics [36]. Temporal features with significant differences (FDR P < 0.05) between CIPN(+) and CIPN(-) groups were identified using a univariate analysis [36]. To control for multiple comparisons, the BH procedure was applied to adjust P values, ensuring an FDR threshold of P < 0.05. Significant temporal features were then evaluated in a multivariate analysis to find a common temporal patterns suggestive of complex interactions between different omic platforms [36] and explore relationships between selected features using Pearson correlation analysis. Each feature was assigned a contribution value in the timeOmics analyses which was either positive or negative. A positive contribution indicated that the levels of those features increase over time, while a negative contribution signified that their levels decrease over time.

Results

Cohort characteristics

Study enrollment was performed from January 2021 to February 2024. The study cohort comprised participants with a median age of 53 years (Table 1). The racial distribution was predominantly White (83.7%), followed by Black or African American (8.2%) and Asian (3.3%) (Table 1). Chemotherapeutic regimens varied among participants; 40.7% received docetaxel every three weeks, and 35.5% were treated with weekly paclitaxel (Table 1). The detailed patient demographic and clinical data are presented in Table 1. mRNA data was available for 358 patients, miRNA data for 333 patients, and metabolite data for 239 patients.

Longitudinal CIPN scores

The study design and timeline of visits are summarized in the schematic diagram in Fig. 1A. CIPN20 scores for all patients in both groups combined are presented in Fig. 1B. CIPN20 scores were the lowest at baseline (V1) and peaked three months after taxane initiation (V4) (Fig. 1B). The average CIPN20 scores for patients in the CIPN(+) group were significantly higher and exhibited a wider distribution of scores at each of the six visits following baseline visit compared to the CIPN(-) group (FDR P < 0.05) (Fig. 1C).

mRNA and metabolite changes over time

Ninety nine out of 194 mRNAs, 147 out of 798 miRNAs and 10 out of 85 metabolites were significantly different between patients in the CIPN(+) group compared to those who were CIPN(-) across different time intervals. Figure 2A presents a summary of the number and percentage of features that are significantly different between the CIPN(+) and CIPN(-) groups across all three omics platforms at six different time intervals. The results of the mRNA differential expression analysis using OmicsLonDA were also illustrated in a volcano plot (Supplementary Figure S1), with a threshold of |log2FC|> 1 and p < 0.05. Red points in the plot denote genes significantly upregulated in the CIPN(-) group compared to the CIPN(+) group, including GRIN2D, MAPK1, and CAMK1D. Conversely, blue points represent genes significantly upregulated in the CIPN(+) group, such as ULK1 (Supplementary Figure S1). A similar volcano plot was generated for miRNA analysis (Supplementary Figure S2), highlighting miRNAs with significant differential expression between the two groups. Red points indicate miRNAs significantly upregulated in the CIPN(-) group, including has-miR-329-3p, has-miR-31.5p, and has-miR-140-3p, while blue points represent miRNAs upregulated in the CIPN(+) group.

Out of the 194 mRNAs, fifty-one percent of mRNAs (n = 99) were significantly different between the two groups (FDR P < 0.05) (Fig. 2A). 79 of the significantly differential genes were downregulated and 31 were upregulated in the CIPN(+) group at least at one time interval, noting that some genes exhibited both upregulation and downregulation across different time points. Most of the gene expression differences were observed in the last month of treatment and then persisted throughout post-treatment 12-month observation period (Fig. 2A). Genes with the 10 highest fold change in differential mRNA expression between groups are presented in Table 2. In seven genes—OPRM1, GRIN2D, CXCL12, CACNB2, NTRK1, SLIT2, and NPFF—the CIPN(-) group showed substantial increases in gene expression compared to the CIPN(+) group (FDR P < 0.05, Table 2). Six of these occurred in the post-treatment period, while CACNB2 was elevated early, between visit 1 and visit 2. Figure 2B presents samples of five selected genes with significant differential mRNA expression between groups across all visit time intervals. OPRM1 and GRIN2D were expressed more in the CIPN(-) group during and after treatment. CAMK1D, NDRGF1, and NDEL1 were expressed more in the CIPN(+) group during and after treatment (FDR P < 0.05, Table 2, Fig. 2B).

Pathway analyses

Using the significant features from the mRNA OmicsLonDA analyses, IPA reconstructed 120 canonical pathways that were significantly enriched (FDR P < 0.05) (Supplemental Figure S3). Hierarchical clustering of z-scores of these 120 pathways revealed two distinct clusters (FDR P = 0.001) (Supplementary Figure S3). Cluster #1 included signaling pathways such as Rho Family GTPase (FDR P = 0.02), Peroxisome Proliferator-Activated Receptor Alpha (PPARa) (FDR P = 0.02), GABAergic receptor (FDR P = 3.67 × 10–05), and Phosphoinositide 3-Kinase/Protein Kinase B pathway (PI3K/AKT) (FDR P = 7.27 × 10–03), all of which showed consistent activation across the six intervals (Supplementary Figure S3). In contrast, pathways in Cluster #2 exhibited highly differential Z-scores, indicating both activation and inhibition across the intervals (Supplementary Figure S3). Within Cluster #2, we identified four pathways of particular interest due to their substantial variation in Z-scores over time and their relevance to CIPN mechanisms in the literature (Fig. 3A). These pathways include CREB signaling (FDR P = 1.39 × 10–11), opioid signaling (FDR P = 1.79 × 10–9), the endocannabinoid neuronal synapse pathway (FDR P = 4.89 × 10–8), and neuropathic pain signaling in dorsal horn neurons (FDR P = 6.62 × 10–5) (Fig. 3A). Notably, all four pathways also exhibited similar sets of genes mapped to them.

CREB signaling pathway

IPA predicted the CREB signaling pathway to show activation between visits 1 and 2 (Z1-2 = 1.79, FDR P = 1.39 × 10–11) in the CIPN(+) group compared to patients who were CIPN(-) (Fig. 3B). Following visit 2, i.e. 1 month after the first infusion, the pathway was inhibited in the with CIPN(+) with downregulation persistent until visit 6 (Z2-3 = −2.68, Z3-4 = −2.68, Z4-5 = −3.13, Z5-6 = −4.03), before re-activating at visit 7 (Z6-7 = 0.89).

Observed data

In this pathway, the differential expression of 18 genes in our data were mapped across the six study intervals (Fig. 3B). CACNB2 was among the highest upregulated genes at the initial time point (int1-2) in CIPN(+) group, with a fold change expression (FC) of 86 (FDR P < 0.05, Fig. 3B, Table 2). However, post-treatment initiation, CACNB2 declined in expression, becoming downregulated at int2-3 (FC = −5.16) and continuing to decrease through int6-7 (FC = −26.12) (Fig. 3B, Table 2). ADRA2C followed a similar pattern, at int1-2, it showed a positive FC of 16.28 but then experienced substantial downregulation starting at int2-3 (FC = −14.30), which continued through the subsequent time points, ultimately reaching −20.68 by int5-6, and then was upregulated at int6-7 (FC = 33.20) (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Table S3). F2R demonstrates a relatively stable expression profile, starting at 0.79 and gradually increasing to 9.23 by int6-7. (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Table S4). GRIN2A initially displays a marked downregulation (−25.07) but experiences a remarkable increase to 76.94 by int6-7 (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Table S4).

IPA predictions

In the IPA pathway network for CREB signaling that is presented in Supplementary Figure S4, some genes mapped in our data are represented as gene classes rather than individual gene names. For example, ADORA1, ADRA2C, EDNRB, F2R, HTR2A, MC1R, NPY1R, OPRD1, OPRK1, P2RY1, and PTAFR are categorized under the G-protein coupled receptor (GPCR) class and are collectively represented as GPCR. Similarly, CACNB2, GRIN1, and GRIN2A fall under the ion channels class, while MAPK1, MAPK3, and NTRK3 are classified as kinases, and EP300 is identified as a transcription factor (Supplementary Figure S4). IPA predicted that downregulation of voltage-gated calcium ion channels, GPCRs, and glutamate receptors following chemotherapy initiation was associated with further downregulation of genes such as calmodulin, protein kinase C (PKC), RAF-1 protooncogene (serine/threonine kinase), MAP2K1/2, phosphatidylinositol 3'-kinase (PI3K), CAMKII, and CAMK4 (Supplementary Figure S4).

Opioid signaling pathway

IPA predicted activation of the opioid signaling pathway between pre-treatment baseline (Z1-2 = 0.83) and week 4, followed by inhibition (Z2-3 = −1.94, Z3-4 = −1.94, Z4-5 = −2.49, Z5-6 = −3.05) (Fig. 3A, C).

Observed data

In this pathway, 14 genes were mapped (Fig. 3C). OPRD1 was initially upregulated with FC of 12 in the int1-2 interval and exhibited a significant reduction in expression following week 4, decreasing to FC of −12 at int2-3 and continuing to decline through int5-6 (FC = −15) before increasing to 26 by int6-7 in CIPN(+) group compared to CIPN(-) patients (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Table S4). OPRK1 displayed a similar pattern, with an initial FC expression of 21 at int1-2; however, it was subsequently downregulated to −81 by int5-6, then modestly increasing to 12 at int6-7 (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Table S4). Conversely, OPRM1 reflected consistent downregulation throughout the initial intervals, starting at −6.63 and remaining negative until int5-6 (FC = −8.10); however, it displayed a substantial increase to 52.42 by int6-7, suggesting a notable positive modulation of expression (Fig. 3C, Table 2). Other genes linked to this pathway included CACNB2 and GRIN2A, which are also associated with the CREB signaling pathway and therefore discussed above (Fig. 3B, C).

IPA predicted activation of the endocannabinoid neuronal synapse pathway between visits 1 and 2 (Z1-2 = 0.33, FDR P = 4.89 × 10–8), followed by inhibition with negative Z-scores (Z2-3 = −2.33, Z3-4 = −2.33, Z4-5 = −2.33, Z5-6 = −3.00) (Fig. 3A, D). Neuropathic pain signaling pathway

IPA predicted inactivation of the neuropathic pain signaling pathway throughout all visits, with consistent negative Z-scores of −0.82 for int1-2, int2-3, int3-4 and maximum inactivation between visit 5 and 6 (int6-7; Z = −2.45) (Fig. 3E).

Observed data

Six genes were mapped to neuropathic pain signaling pathway from our dataset namely: GRIN1, GRIN2A, GRIN2D, CAMK1D, MAPK1, and MAPK3. CAMK1D starts with positive expression (FC = 6.98) but experiences a dramatic decline to −58.42 by int4-5 before stabilizing at −1.69 at int6-7 (Fig. 3E, Table 2).

IPA predicted 30 pathways associated with neurological conditions to display significant differential activation and inhibition profiles between patients with moderate-to-severe CIPN and those that did not (FDR P < 0.05) (Fig. 4A). IPA predicted that downregulation of ARRB2 and upregulation of CHRNA4, IAPP, IL10, OPRD1, OPRK1, OPRM1, ADORA1, ADRA2C led to inhibition of function of analgesia (Fig. 4B, C).

Out of these pathways, motor dysfunction demonstrated the highest positive Z-score of 2.40 (FDR P = 4.6 × 10–9) and 12 genes from our data were mapped to this pathway (Fig. 4D, E).

Observed data

Compared to baseline, genes such as CCL5 and CDKN1A were significantly downregulated and genes such as CHRNA4, GRIN1, GRIN2A, GRIN2D, HTR2A, MAPT, MECP2, PTGS2, UCHL1, and ADRA2C were upregulated (FDR P < 0.05) (Fig. 4D, E).

IPA further predicted that downregulation of genes CCL5, CDKN1A and upregulation of genes GRIN1, MAPT, MECP2, and UCHL1 led to overall activation of motor dysfunction post-taxane initiation (Fig. 4D, E).

Discussion

This study offers a thorough examination of multiple -omic platforms, clinical data, and demographic information collected over a 12-month period from a cohort of 358 patients receiving taxane therapy for breast cancer. The longitudinal data provided valuable insights into the relationships between the onset of chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN) and alterations in gene expression and metabolite profiles within cellular signaling pathways of interest. We documented changes in mRNA, miRNA, and metabolites among patients who developed CIPN compared to those who did not, both during and after treatment. Notably, the analyses indicated that the opioid and CREB signaling pathways may elucidate the differences observed between patients with and without CIPN.

Our study cohort had similar characteristics to prior CIPN observational studies in patients treated with taxanes for breast cancer [37, 38]. This included taxane exposure, treatment regimens, age, adjuvant therapy, and tumor staging. The CIPN symptoms in our cohort increased throughout the treatment period, peaking near the last infusion at three months. Patients that had moderate-to-severe symptoms were also more likely to have persistent CIPN (Fig. 1C).

We identified significantly modified expression of 99 mRNAs, 147 miRNAs, and 10 metabolites associated with CIPN, which suggested that they may play a role in CIPN pathogenesis. We identified the highest proportion (51%) of significant results for mRNA data, likely due to the pre-established panel of pain-related mRNA genes designed specifically for this study; however, this high percentage may not necessarily indicate biological relevance, as the other assays were untargeted (Fig. 2A). By comparison, the percentage of patients with a significant change in mRNA expression and metabolite concentrations peaked over the 3- to 6-month period after the treatment completion (i.e. int5-6 as shown in Fig. 2A). Significant changes in miRNA expression begin to appear during the three-month period after starting treatment. It is also notable that later time intervals i.e. after three months of treatment initiation overall displayed a high number of significant features (Fig. 2A) that may indicate critical periods for CIPN pathology, possibly linked to cumulative chemotherapy exposure. The early peak in CIPN scores observed after the second visit (approximately 1 month) suggests that initial molecular changes may contribute to the onset of neuropathy symptoms. The differential features identified during the initial time intervals (e.g., int1-2 and int2-3), which include genes such as GRIN2D and CAMK1D, as well as miRNAs hsa-miR-155-5p and hsa-miR-196a-3p, may be involved in the early stages of CIPN development. These findings align with recent literature emphasizing the role of pathways like CREB signaling, opioid signaling, and the endocannabinoid neuronal synapse pathway in CIPN pathogenesis [11–13].

Pathway analyses revealed key pathways involved in CIPN, including CREB signaling, opioid signaling, endocannabinoid neuronal synapse pathway, and neuropathic pain signaling. The inhibition of these pathways post-treatment initiation and their subsequent reactivation post-treatment aligns with the clinical timeline of CIPN symptoms. The downregulation of CREB signaling, driven by changes in GPCR and ion channel genes, highlights a potential target for therapeutic intervention. In the literature, it has been observed that paclitaxel injections increase CREB expression on day 7 following the initial injection, with levels remaining elevated for a week before returning to baseline by week 3 [11]. In our data, we demonstrate an increase in CREB following the start of treatment; however, at int2-3 i.e. after visit 2 (one month), we noted a significant inhibition that persisted for up to 9 months after treatment began. These findings highlight the importance of conducting longitudinal studies with extended follow-up periods, as CIPN can have long-term effects on patients. This suggests that the transformation in activation and inhibition states—particularly of neuroinflammatory and pain modulation pathways—is critical and may underpin both the development and resolution of CIPN. Similarly, the inhibition of opioid signaling and endocannabinoid pathways suggests altered pain modulation mechanisms in CIPN. There are several models showing involvement of endocannabinoids in CIPN, with potential for analgesia through agents with activity at CB1 and/or CB2 receptors, which may be peripheral or central [39]. Notably, all four pathways also exhibited similar genes associated with them, highlighting shared molecular mechanisms potentially contributing to the variations observed among CIPN groups. These pathways, with shared molecular mechanisms, could underpin the variations observed among CIPN-positive and -negative groups, especially given their complex and overlapping roles in neuroinflammation and pain regulation.

The mapping of mRNA data to neurological conditions further validated the relevance of our findings. The activation of pathways related to motor dysfunction and sensory disorders post-chemotherapy aligns with clinical observations of CIPN symptoms [40]. Conversely, the inhibition of analgesia pathways post-treatment initiation highlights novel analgesic approaches as potential targets for CIPN symptom management. The integration of longitudinal multi-omics data identified molecular features with strong associations within CIPN-positive and CIPN-negative groups.

As with any study, it is important to consider study limitations when interpreting these findings. First, the reliance on the QLQ-CIPN20 questionnaire, a patient-reported outcome measure, may introduce subjective bias due to its nature as a subjective assessment tool. This could affect consistency in diagnosing CIPN across different studies and clinical practices, as there is no universally accepted standard. In this study, we employed an 8-point increase on the QLQ-CIPN20 instrument to stratify patients into CIPN(+) and CIPN(-) groups. Li et al. in 2023 [41] have emphasized the absence of established thresholds for identifying clinically relevant CIPN using PROMs, and in light of this, we propose the 8-point increase as a threshold to capture clinical relevance, informed by prior studies [27, 29, 30] and our preliminary analyses. Although Li et al. introduced a method to define minimally clinically important changes (MCIC) using a clinical anchor, their work also highlights the broader challenges associated with CIPN PROMs, particularly the need for more robust thresholds. Looking ahead, we aim to validate our findings in additional cohorts and explore methodologies, such as those suggested by Li et al., to further refine our criteria for CIPN stratification. Additionally, the candidate features identified in our study lack external validation, which is essential to confirm their relevance and applicability in different contexts. This gap highlights the need for further research to substantiate these findings and ensure their robustness by validating in other clinical cohorts such as SWOG S1714 (https://www.swog.org/clinical-trials/s1714) and SWOG S0221 (https://www.swog.org/clinical-trials/s0221). Another significant limitation is the potential bias introduced by using targeted vs. untargeted panels, which may restrict the discovery scope to predefined pathways. Specifically, the mRNA panel used in our study was designed to target pain pathways, limiting the exploration of other biological processes. In contrast, our metabolomics approach was untargeted, allowing for the discovery of a broader range of metabolites. Multiple test corrections, such as the FDR correction utilized here, assume all tests are independent. Due to the interrelatedness between -omics assays, we adjusted each assay platform for multiple testing separately, to avoid an overly conservative adjustment that would increase false negative findings. However, this approach may also increase the risk of false positive findings and therefore these findings remain exploratory and warrant validation. Finally, the generalizability of our results is limited due to a lack of complete representation of all patient groups that may experience taxane-induced CIPN, highlighting the importance of including more diverse patient cohorts in future studies. Expanding molecular assays and investigating targeted pathways could provide higher-resolution datasets, and exploring combination therapies that address multiple pathways might further enhance therapeutic outcomes.

This study advances our understanding of CIPN by identifying key clinical and molecular features associated with this condition. The integration of clinical data with multi-omics analyses provides a comprehensive view of CIPN pathogenesis and highlights potential targets for therapeutic intervention. Future validation studies will be essential to confirm these findings and translate them into clinical practice, ultimately improving patient outcomes in breast cancer care.

Conclusions

This study provides an in-depth analysis of the complex relationships between chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN), gene expression, and metabolite profiles in a cohort of 358 breast cancer patients undergoing taxane therapy. The longitudinal data revealed significant temporal changes in mRNA, miRNA, and metabolite levels that differentiate patients with CIPN from those without. Notably, we identified critical pathways, such as CREB and opioid signaling, which play pivotal roles in the mechanisms underlying CIPN. While the study has limitations, including the reliance on patient-reported outcomes (CIPN20), the lack of diverse patient representation and lack of validation, these findings contribute to our understanding of the molecular changes associated with CIPN and identify candidate pathways that merit further investigation as potential targets for improving pain management. Future studies in more diverse populations with comprehensive molecular profiling will be essential to validate and extend these findings, ultimately informing therapeutic strategies for CIPN.
