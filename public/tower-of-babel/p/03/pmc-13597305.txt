Early parenting is associated with the development of neural reward responsiveness from childhood through adolescence
Authors: Kaylin E. Hill, Christian A. L. Bean, George Abitante, Connor Lawhead, Aline K. Szenczy, Ellen Kessel, Greg Hajcak, Brady D. Nelson, Daniel N. Klein, Autumn Kujawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reward responsiveness plays an important role in promoting health and well-being in youth. Understanding associations between early experiences and variation in the typical developmental trajectory of reward responsiveness is essential to identifying potential pathways to health. We examined developmental trajectories of the reward positivity, a neurophysiological indicator of reward responsiveness, across 4 assessments in late childhood through late adolescence in 465 youth. We estimated growth curves and assessed parenting, observed via parent–child interactions, and stress exposure at age 3, as early environmental predictors of reward positivity trajectories. The reward positivity to win and loss feedback showed a quadratic trajectory of change across childhood and adolescence. Lower positive parenting and higher negative parenting predicted a reduced increase in the reward positivity to win feedback over time; stress exposure associations were smaller and statistically non-significant. Together, results suggest a link between early parenting and reward development across childhood through adolescence.

Early parenting is associated with the development of neural reward responsiveness from childhood through adolescence

Rewards shape behaviors across development, and individual differences in reward responsiveness have emerged as key processes in mental1,2 and physical3,4 health. Reward responsiveness is a fundamental process nested within positive valence systems—that is, the functional domain responsible for anticipating, obtaining, and responding to positively valenced cues and rewards5–7. Growing evidence links altered functioning in the positive valence systems domain with psychopathology, particularly depression1,8–22 and substance use23–31. Early experiences may influence the degree to which individuals expect their external environment to be rewarding, such that those who experience more early adversity, especially interpersonal stress, tend to withdraw from their surroundings due to decreased expectation of reward32–34. Consequently, investigations focused on trajectories of reward response development, as well as early predictors of variation in this development, are needed to better understand mechanistic precursors of depression vulnerability.

Positive valence systems undergo significant developmental changes from infancy through adulthood. First, infancy and early childhood showcase rapid neural development in reward-related circuitry wherein caregiving and social exchanges are fundamental35–39. In infancy, reward learning is supported via interactions with caregivers, most often a child’s biological parents. For example, 7-month-old infants show increased reward learning cues (e.g., anticipatory arousal) and responses (e.g., pupil dilation) during learning opportunities with their mother versus a stranger40. Neurophysiological reward responses can be measured as early as 4 to 7 years of age41 and continue to be observed throughout childhood and adolescence42. Adolescence is marked by increased reward responsiveness, supported by increased neural activation in key subcortical and cortical regions43–45. This increased reward responsiveness is theorized to support growing independence, and seems to then decrease as adolescents transition to adulthood43,45,46. In summary, the literature to date suggests trajectories of reward responses begin developing early in the lifespan and may be characterized by a curvilinear pattern wherein reward responses increase from early childhood through adolescence and then decrease from adolescence into adulthood43,44,47,48.

Understanding variation in this typical developmental trajectory is essential to identifying potential pathways to increased vulnerability to psychopathology and other adverse outcomes. Contributors to reduced reward responsiveness likely span genetics, temperament, and early experiences (for a review, see Kujawa et al., 202048). Importantly, these contributors interact49 such that early experiences can be biologically embedded and impact subsequent functioning and outcomes50,51. Parenting styles and stress exposure are types of early experiences that may be the most associated with individual differences in developmental trajectories of reward responsiveness. Parenting is fundamental to early experiences37,38, and parental relationships are key processes by which emotions are socialized52. Importantly, parent–child relationships are built on the summation of many day-to-day interactions37. As such, interactions with parents, shaped by the parent’s parenting style, are important contextual features that may affect both baseline levels and trajectories of reward development. In addition to evidence that infants demonstrate increased reward learning with their parent versus a stranger40, preschoolers display more dispositional positive affect when parents report higher use of positive emotion socialization approaches in daily interactions53, and parental encouragement is associated with enhanced reward responses in real-time54. Further, low positive parenting in early childhood is prospectively associated with reduced reward responsiveness in later childhood55 and parent responsiveness moderates the impact of familial risk for depression on decreased reward responses56. Prior studies suggest parenting, including parent physical presence, parenting style, as well as affect and behaviors are associated with reward responsiveness at single time points; however, examining the association between parenting behaviors and the developmental trajectories of reward responsiveness is important for supporting long-term health and well-being.

Associations between parenting and reward processes are not limited to childhood. Positive parenting practices—that is, demonstration of warmth, respectful communication, and consistency in caregiving57—are also associated with higher self-esteem and lower depressive symptoms in adolescents58. Further, parent–adolescent interactions are associated with adolescent reward responsiveness, such that increased maternal negative affect in these interactions was associated with decreased adolescent reward response to social acceptance59. Notably, parent–adolescent conflict may shape associations between reward responsiveness and adolescent depression60. Across child and adolescent samples, parent emotion socialization practices, parenting styles, and parent behaviors seem to play a key role in positive valence systems development52,61,62. Given the substantial evidence of concurrent associations between parenting styles and parent–child interactions and the significant influence early experiences have on developmental trajectories38,63–65, the evidence to date suggests early observed parenting behaviors in parent–child interactions may shape baseline and longitudinal trajectories of reward responsiveness.

In addition to parenting behaviors, stress exposure is a key early environmental experience that may directly alter developmental trajectories of reward responsiveness. Adversity and stress exposure are often implicated in positive valence systems development34,66,67, though specific associations and directions of effects provide mixed evidence. For example, acute stress exposure via laboratory paradigms is associated with blunted reward responsiveness68. Several studies provide evidence that early life stress exposure is associated with reduced reward responsiveness in childhood69 as well as adolescence and adulthood70–74. However, other studies suggest that early stress exposure may be associated with increased reward responses75,76. Cumulatively, the evidence indicates that early stress exposure likely alters reward responses, but these associations are complex and may depend on the timing and nature of the exposure. Across studies, a recent meta-analysis of the literature found a statistically non-significant negative association between stress exposure and reward response77. The evidence to date suggests low reward responses are often reported in experimental studies of stress exposure but findings from naturalistic studies are more mixed77.

In the present study, we used the reward positivity (RewP), a neurophysiological indicator of reward responsiveness78–80, to examine developmental trajectories of reward responsiveness from late childhood to late adolescence and early environmental predictors of these trajectories. The RewP is particularly suited for the challenge of longitudinal reward response investigations because it is observed across the lifespan [e.g., from ages 4 or 5 years to adulthood41,81]. Evidence suggests it is a reliable measure, at least from late childhood through adulthood82–85. We examined early parenting behaviors and stress exposure as predictors of reward responsiveness measured by the RewP from late childhood to late adolescence. Parenting behaviors were observed during mother–child interactions in the lab and stress exposure was measured using structured clinical interviews at the first study wave at child age 3 years. We focused on parenting behaviors observed at child age 3 as observational methods are considered gold standard86 and conceptual frameworks propose that early experiences may shape trajectories over time38,39. We controlled for maternal depressive history given observed associations with parenting behaviors and offspring reward responsiveness55. The RewP was measured during a monetary reward guessing task every 3 years at approximate ages 9, 12, 15, and 18 years. We expected that reward responses would demonstrate a curvilinear pattern across development wherein responses would increase from childhood to adolescence and begin to decrease from early adolescence to late adolescence. Specific to the current study, we hypothesized that greater observed positive parenting would be associated with greater baseline levels of reward responses and a stronger curvilinear trajectory (that is, a steeper increase from childhood to early adolescence and then decrease to late adolescence). Similarly, we hypothesized that greater negative parenting would be associated with lower baseline levels of reward responses and a weaker curvilinear trajectory (that is, a shallower increase from childhood to early adolescence and then decrease to late adolescence). Though there is mixed literature regarding early stress exposure, we hypothesized a similar pattern to negative parenting such that greater naturalistic stress exposure would be associated with lower baseline levels of reward responses and a weaker curvilinear trajectory.

Methods

Participants and procedure

Participants were from the Stony Brook Temperament Study, an ongoing prospective community cohort study focused on childhood temperament and risk for internalizing disorders in which parents and children completed assessments approximately every 3 years starting at child age 3 years through 18 years87. Initial eligibility criteria for study participation included the child being 3 years of age, without a diagnosed developmental disability or serious medical condition, with at least one English-speaking biological parent, and living within twenty miles of Stony Brook, New York. The Stony Brook University Institutional Review Board approved this study.

At the first study wave, initiated in 2004, 559 families with a 3-year-old child were recruited. Most families were white, middle class, with approximately half of parents having obtained a four-year college degree or higher (see Olino et al., 201088 for more details). Parents gave written informed consent at the first wave and each subsequent wave of data collection until the child was 18, at which time the child then gave written informed consent. In addition, children provided assent at the age 12 and 15 waves. Of the families recruited to participate in the study, 541 families completed parental structured clinical interviews to assess child stress exposure since birth, and 525 families completed dyadic behavioral tasks coded to assess parenting behaviors. Excluding participants with missing or unusable EEG data at every wave led to a final sample of 465 children with data on stress exposure since birth and 458 who completed dyadic parenting tasks with their mothers.

Participants completed the monetary reward task as part of a battery of tasks while continuous EEG was recorded for the first time at the third study wave when children were approximately 9 years of age (Meanage = 9.19 years, range = 8.75-10.92). In each of the subsequent waves, occurring every 3 years [approximate ages 12 (Meanage = 12.68, range = 11.83-14.17), 15 (Meanage = 15.24, range = 14.97–17.65), and 18 (Meanage = 18.35, range = 18.00-20.83) years], participants completed the same monetary reward task while continuous EEG was recorded. Demographic information, including child sex, was obtained via parent-report. The sample composition of those included in analyses (N = 465) were approximately 50% male versus female and predominately white and non-Hispanic, per parent report. Compensation was provided at each assessment wave. Compensation amounts varied across waves and was prorated to reflect which assessments were completed, with total amounts adjusted across time to account for inflation and promote retention. The maximum total compensation amount was $400 USD per wave if all assessments were completed including separate diagnostic and life stress assessments with the child and one or both parents; diagnostic interviews with each parent about the parents’ history of psychopathology; extensive questionnaire batteries for both parents and the child; in the early waves separate laboratory temperament and parent-child interaction observational assessments; an EEG assessment of the child, and in one wave, a parent; home-based saliva collections for hormonal assays (on up to nine occasions); and in several waves intensive 2-week (5x per day) ecological momentary assessments. Participants were generally reimbursed at the rate of $25–30 USD/hour.

Measures

Parenting behavior

Parenting behaviors were observed at the initial study wave (child age 3 years). Parents and their children completed a modified version of the Teaching Tasks battery89 while being videorecorded. The battery consisted of 6 standardized tasks designed to elicit parent and child behaviors (e.g., book reading, block building). As reported in Kujawa and colleagues (2015)55, trained coders rated videotapes of each task for parent support, confidence, and instruction as well as intrusiveness and hostility. All ratings were on a 5-point scale, except for confidence, which was rated on a 3-point scale. To reduce skewness and kurtosis, hostility and confidence were dichotomized into a 2-point scale for each task. All ratings were averaged across tasks. Interrater reliability was acceptable for all variables55. Following Kujawa and colleagues (2015)55, ratings of parental support, confidence, and instruction during these tasks were standardized and then summed to form the positive parenting composite. Ratings of parental intrusiveness and hostility during these tasks were standardized and then summed to form the negative parenting composite. Further, because parents participating in the Teaching Tasks overwhelmingly identified as mothers (>95%), the present analyses included only data obtained from mothers.

Early stress exposure

Exposure to stress was assessed using the Preschool Age Psychiatric Assessment (PAPA90). Trained interviewers completed the PAPA with parents during assessment at child age 3, probing child exposure to stressors in the preceding 3 years (i.e., in total, from birth to 3 years). The PAPA captures stressful and traumatic life events experienced by the child and their immediate family. For example, physical and sexual abuse, death of a parent, vehicular accidents, falls, near drownings, hospitalization, natural disasters, separation from a significant attachment figure for over a week, and “lives/attends daycare or school in a chronically unsafe environment” are all included. Cumulative stress exposure from birth to the first study wave (child age 3 years) was estimated by summing the number of stressful life events reported during the PAPA. Participants were reported to have been exposed to an average of 2.72 (SD = 1.75) stressful life events through this period (range = 0–11).

Maternal lifetime depression diagnosis

Lifetime depression history in mothers was assessed using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV-Non-Patient Version (SCID91) at child age 3 and age 9 waves. The SCID was administered to mothers by trained interviewers. Scores at both waves were combined to reflect lifetime diagnoses. Interrater reliability, scored as Kappas for diagnoses of depressive disorders, was excellent (age 3 Kappa = 0.93, age 9 Kappa = 0.9155). For the present study, we focused on a diagnosis of maternal present and lifetime major depressive disorder, which was used as a covariate in the main analyses. One participant was missing maternal lifetime diagnosis data and so was excluded from the conditional growth models.

EEG data collection and processing

Monetary reward task

Participants completed a guessing reward task while continuous EEG data were recorded to measure neural responses to rewards (i.e., monetary wins) and non-rewards (i.e., monetary losses)92. Two doors were presented on the screen at the start of each trial and participants were instructed to select which door to open using the computer mouse. After the participants selected a door, feedback was presented denoting whether 50 cents USD was won (monetary win) or 25 cents USD was lost (monetary loss). A fixation cross was presented before the onset of each stimulus. The task consisted of 60 trials with 50/50 consisting of monetary win and loss presented in a pseudorandom order. For each trial, stimuli were presented as (1) two doors presented until participant response, (2) fixation cross for 1000 ms, (3) feedback stimulus for 2000 ms, (4) fixation cross for 1500 ms, (5) instructions to “Click for next round” until participant response. The task was administered using Presentation software (Neurobehavioral Systems, Inc., Albany, CA).

EEG data collection and processing

Continuous EEG data were recorded using a 34-electrode cap, which included 32 electrode channels with the addition of FCz and Iz, with the BioSemi system (BioSemi, Amsterdam, Netherlands). Two additional electrodes were placed on mastoids. Facial electrodes were placed approximately 1 cm above and below the eye and 1 cm from the outer corners of the eyes to measure eye movements. Recordings were digitized at a 24-bit resolution with a sampling rate of 1024 Hz. Offline processing was completed using BrainVision Analyzer software (Brain Products, Munich, Germany). Data were first mastoid referenced, then band-pass filtered with a low threshold of 0.01 Hz and upper threshold of 30 Hz. Data were then segmented from 500 ms before stimulus onset (e.g., feedback cues) to 1000 ms following stimulus onset. Data for single electrodes were interpolated as needed from surrounding electrode signals. The EEG was corrected for eye movements that were recorded using facial electrooculogram (EOG) electrodes93. Artifacts were identified and removed using a semi-automatic procedure—first using automatic detection criteria of a voltage step of 50 μV between sample points, maximum voltage difference of 300 μV within a 200 ms interval, or minimum activity of 0.5 μV within a 100 ms interval; then, additional artifacts were identified and removed via visual inspection. Data were averaged across trials separately for win and loss feedback stimuli, then baseline corrected to 500 ms before stimulus onset. The RewP to win was scored as the mean amplitude 250–350 ms after win feedback at electrode Cz. The RewP to loss was scored as the mean amplitude 250–350 ms after loss feedback at electrode Cz. Data analytic plan and preliminary analyses

In the current study, we sought to examine early environmental predictors of the developmental trajectory of reward responsiveness assessed at 4 time points from approximately age 9–18 years. Reward responsiveness was operationalized as the RewP to win feedback. Traditional ERP analyses include a contrast of experimental conditions to isolate processes of interest94; however, there is a tension between the necessity of isolated processes and the lower internal consistency of difference-based scoring methods (see95). For example, Kujawa (2018) and colleagues demonstrated that the RewP to win and loss feedback each demonstrated greater test-retest reliability than difference-based scores, especially subtraction-based scores80. The RewP to win and RewP to loss shared large associations across timepoints (see Table 3), further reducing the reliability of difference score approaches. We address this tension by presenting models focused on the primary process of interest (e.g., the RewP to win) in the main text and isolated process of interest (i.e., (1) the RewP to win covarying for the RewP to loss and (2) RewP subtraction-based difference scores) in the supplement. The predictors of interest were maternal parenting behaviors assessed at age 3 and cumulative stress exposure from birth to age 3. We used a multilevel modeling framework. All statistical tests were two-sided. For growth curve models estimating environmental predictors of reward response developmental trajectories, we included participants in analyses if they had observed scores for the predictor variables (i.e., positive parenting, negative parenting, stress exposure). These criteria led to a different analytic sample size for each set of models. All analyses were conducted in Stata version 1796 with time centered at time 1 (approximately child age 9). Preliminary analyses estimated the shape of RewP to win trajectories in three potential models estimated with maximum likelihood estimation: a model containing only an intercept; a model including the intercept and a linear effect of time; and a model including the intercept, linear effect of time, and a quadratic effect of time. Improvements in model fit as complexity increased were assessed by comparing nested -2 log-likelihood functions97. The model containing only the RewP to win intercept showed an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.52 (95% CI [0.47, 0.58]), indicating that slightly more than half of the variance in RewP to win intercepts was due to between-person differences. All models including time as a predictor featured a random slope for the linear effect of time, a fixed quadratic slope, and an unstructured random effects covariance matrix. The model featuring a linear time trend significantly improved model fit over the model containing only the RewP to win intercept (Δχ2(3) = 42.15, p < 0.001). However, the model including both linear and quadratic trends further improved model fit over the linear-trend model (Δχ2(1) = 63.43, p < 0.001) and the intercept-only model (Δχ2(4) = 105.58, p < 0.001). Thus, the model including both a linear and quadratic trend for the RewP to win was chosen as the preferred model.

In the unconditional model without any predictors of trajectories, the intercept of RewP to win was significantly greater than zero (π0j = 16.17, 95% CI [15.19, 17.16], z = 32.24, p < 0.001). The linear trend of time was positive and statistically significant (π1j = 4.63, 95% CI [3.62, 5.65], z = 8.93, p < 0.001), while the quadratic effect of time was also statistically significant but negative (π2j = –1.37, 95% CI [–1.70, –1.04], z = –8.11, p <0.001). There was substantial variability in intercepts (variance = 71.11, 95% CI [58.51, 86.43]) and linear slopes (variance = 3.99, 95% CI [2.38, 6.70]). Results

Main analyses

Bivariate associations

Bivariate correlations across study variables demonstrated several associations of interest. First, the RewP to win shared small, positive associations with positive parenting across all assessments, but these correlations were only statistically significant at child age 15 years. Second, the RewP to win shared small, not statistically significant associations with negative parenting over time. Finally, the RewP to win did not demonstrate any reliable associations with early life stress exposure. At child age 9, the RewP to win was negatively associated with child sex such that boys demonstrated a larger RewP to win; at age 18, the RewP to win was positively associated with child sex such that girls demonstrated a larger RewP to win. Summary

Overall, positive parenting, but not negative parenting or stress exposure, was statistically significantly associated with the RewP to win intercept. Both positive and negative parenting were related to trajectories of reward responsivity, such that participants with higher levels of positive parenting showed stronger increases in RewP over development and adolescence while participants with higher levels of negative parenting showed weaker increases in RewP. In contrast, stress exposure did not demonstrate a statistically significant association with RewP trajectories. An exploratory model with all three predictors together (positive parenting, negative parenting, and early stress exposure) is presented in the supplement.

Discussion

We investigated early environmental predictors of the developmental trajectory of reward responsiveness across ages 9, 12, 15, and 18 years. The overall sample was characterized by a curvilinear trajectory such that neural reward responsiveness as measured by the RewP to monetary win feedback, increased from age 9 through 15 years and decreased from age 15 to 18 years. Higher levels of positive parenting, observed via parent–child interactions when children were 3 years old, was associated with a higher average RewP as well as a stronger increase in the RewP over time. In contrast, higher levels of negative parenting was associated with a weaker increase in the RewP over time. Unlike parenting behavior, early stress exposure, measured via parent-report from participant birth to age 3 years, was not statistically significantly associated with the RewP average or the RewP trajectory across time.

This study builds on previous evidence that early environmental factors relate to reward responsiveness at single points in time by demonstrating that early environmental factors also relate to trajectories across time. Previous work emphasizes the importance of parenting in early development broadly37,38 and in positive valence system development specifically40, and has consistently demonstrated associations between positive parenting and reward responsiveness in childhood54,55 and adolescence59,99. Further, previous studies have demonstrated associations between both positive and negative parenting behaviors and childhood depressive symptoms100 and the importance of positive parenting in association with adolescent and young adult mental health101–103.

In the current study, both positive parenting and negative parenting behaviors, when examined separately, were prospectively associated with reward response trajectories. Sensitivity analyses, in which positive and negative parenting behaviors were tested in the same model, did not demonstrate unique prospective associations. This is at odds with previous evidence suggesting positive and negative parenting are independent spectra100. This may be due to the measurement approach used in the present study wherein parenting behaviors were coded from the same teaching tasks. The bivariate correlation between positive and negative parenting behaviors suggests that while they are not fully overlapping, they may be considered reflective of a broader parenting quality dimension rather than fully independent processes. In total, the present results provide evidence that parenting experienced in the preschool years are prospectively associated with reward responsiveness across the next 15 years—a period characterized by heightened depression and substance use risk104–106.

The parenting behaviors we observed at age 3 are only a glimpse into the child’s environmental context for the development of positive affect, reward learning, and reward trajectories across childhood and adolescence. Parent–child relationships are built on the summation of many day-to-day interactions37, and the one-time lab-based observation of parenting we used does not fully reflect the cumulative nature of everyday parent–child interactions, especially those caried out in naturalistic contexts. The importance of daily interactions between parents and children has been observed from infancy to young adulthood107–109, with changing needs across developmental transitions110. It is likely many additional factors also contribute to the child’s early environment, from the macro to micro layers of context111,112. These contextual factors, including parenting health, cultural norms, and resource availability to name a few, may impact the child through parent cognitions and emotions related to the child, parenting beliefs and behaviors, physical proximity to the child37,38,113–115 and through complex interactions amongst child, family, and community levels wherein the child always plays an active role in their development108,116. It will be important to investigate how single observations of parenting at age 3 years, as in the present study, in contrast to more ecologically valid observations—for example, home based daily assessments at multiple timepoints—relate to reward response trajectories.

We also examined stress exposure from birth to age 3 years in relation to developmental trajectories of reward responsiveness. Although evidence to date suggests that stress exposure is likely implicated in reward development34,66,67, there is mixed evidence for the specific context and nature of these associations. Notably, there is evidence supporting positive75,76 and negative69–73 associations between stress exposure and reward response magnitude across the lifespan. We did not find statistically significant associations between early stress exposure (measured from birth to three years and from birth to nine years of age) and either the overall magnitude of reward responses or the trajectory of reward responsiveness across 9 to 18 years. These observations may be due to the observed associations with parenting, as previous work suggests that parenting buffers the effects of stress exposure117. The findings may also be specific to the present sample, which reported a relatively limited range and severity of early stress exposure. As such, the relatively low risk nature of the present sample could lead to reduced observed associations in contrast to samples selected for greater variability or known stress exposure. The present findings suggest that to the degree stress exposure and reward responsiveness are associated, these associations are likely complex and may be more salient when examined in samples that include a greater proportion of participants exposed to severe life events and traumas.

It is important to note that results varied depending on ERP scoring approach. Although it is common to use subtraction-based ERP difference scores to isolate the process of interest across experimental conditions94, these are prone to low reliability and may not be the most appropriate for examining change across time. Here, we used the RewP to win as the primary outcome changing across time, and we conducted a series of sensitivity analyses using other scoring approaches. The RewP to loss demonstrated a similar growth trajectory over time to the RewP to win, though this was not statistically significant when including the RewP to win in the model. When examining the RewP to win while covarying the RewP to loss as a time-varying covariate, we observed a similar growth trajectory over time as in the primary models and higher negative parenting scores predicted weaker linear and quadratic trends over time. However, positive parenting interactions with linear and quadratic time were no longer statistically significant. When we examined subtraction-based difference scores (e.g., RewP to win—RewP to loss) as the outcome, the RewP difference score demonstrated quadratic growth over time, but neither positive parenting nor negative parenting interacted with time effects in predicting the RewP difference. As such, the quadratic effect of time appeared robust across RewP scoring approaches (e.g., RewP to win scores, RewP to win covarying for RewP to loss, and RewP win—loss models). However, many of the interactions of interest only held when modeling the RewP to win or RewP to win covarying for RewP to loss. The reduced reliability of subtraction-based difference scores likely contributed to changes in results, and additional research is needed on best practices for longitudinal analyses of ERP components.

Limitations

Future work would benefit from testing alternative approaches to measuring parenting constructs and stress exposure. A strength of the present study was that parenting was measured via direct observation of parent–child interactions rather than parent reports; however, this measure is nonetheless limited to the task and context at assessment. Emerging evidence suggests that directing parents to teach their young children increases parent intrusiveness part of the negative parenting composite in the present study118. This may have impacted observed parenting behaviors in the present study. In addition, alternative approaches to measuring parenting behaviors—notably ecological momentary assessments, home visits, multi-informant reports collected across development—may better capture naturalistic parenting behaviors and how they unfold across development. In the present study, positive and negative parenting behaviors shared a large correlation, limiting our interpretation of which may be most related to reward trajectory. Relatedly, when comparing models with and without the RewP to loss as a time varying covariate, only negative parenting remained a statistically significant predictor of reward trajectory across models. Lastly, regarding the Teaching Tasks, the present study included parenting behaviors exclusively of mothers. While either biological parent was invited to participate in the study, parents who participated in the Teaching Tasks were overwhelmingly mothers (>95%) and more work, especially studies focused on recruitment and retention of father participants, is needed to examine the generalizability of the associations observed between mothers and their children in the present study.

Stress exposure was measured via gold-standard clinical interviews90 with parents at regularly scheduled intervals across early development. It is unclear from the present study, however, the degree to which parent-reported stress exposure across adolescence and early adulthood (e.g., participant ages 10–18) or self-report clinical interviews (e.g., with the participants rather than their parents) would reveal associations disparate from those found in the present study. In addition, parenting approaches and behaviors are in large part culturally constructed119. While the importance of some parenting dimensions, such as warmth120,121 are observed across cultures from an evolutionary perspective, evidence suggests that family circumstances, cultural context, and parent past experiences influence parenting beliefs and behaviors113,119,122,123. The current sample was predominately white and the degree to which the observed results generalize is unknown. Future work should directly test whether cultural context and family circumstances moderate associations between parenting dimensions and reward trajectory and how these associations may change over time.

Conclusion

These findings suggest that early environment, specifically parenting behaviors observed at only one point in time, predicted the development of reward responsiveness from late childhood through late adolescence. Notably, this multimethod study leveraged parent clinical interviews, behavioral observations of parent–child interactions, and neural measures of reward responsiveness at multiple stages of development. These results suggest there are prospective associations between early parenting observed in toddlerhood and reward response trajectories across critical periods of adolescent neurodevelopment. Importantly, these findings provide further empirical evidence as to how preventive interventions targeting parent–child interactions could support resilience across adolescence (e.g., Burkhouse et al., 2023124) by supporting developmental trajectories of reward responsiveness52,125.
