Universal Neural Network Potential-Driven Molecular Dynamics Study of CO2/O2 Evolution at the Ethylene Carbonate/Charged–Electrode Interface
Authors: Motoki Horibe, Naoto Tanibata, Hayami Takeda, Masanobu Nakayama
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Long-term durability and safety are required to develop Li-ion batteries that can operate at high voltages. However, side reactions, including the release of O2 from the electrode and CO2 from the organic electrolyte, occur at the positive-electrode/electrolyte interface during charging at high voltages. In this study, universal neural network potential (UNNP)-driven molecular dynamics (MD) calculations are used to investigate the mechanism of the reaction between LixCoO2 (0 ≤ x ≤ 1) or LixNiO2 (0 ≤ x ≤ 1), as the positive-electrode material, and an ethylene-carbonate-based electrolyte, with a solid–liquid interface composed of ∼1700 atoms. Molecular CO2 and O2 evolve from the partially or fully Li-deintercalated LixNiO2, while no gas–evolution reactions are observed for LixCoO2. Hence, compared LixNiO2, the LiCoO2 electrode is more stable toward the decomposition of ethylene carbonate in the charged state. The decomposition reactions at the solid–liquid interface during charging are also analyzed using a NN force field. This study provides a robust approach involving MD simulations using UNNP to better understand the side reactions in electrochemical devices, which can guide manufacturers in selecting appropriate materials.

Introduction

Li-ion batteries (LIBs) are widely used in portable devices and as power sources for applications, such as in electric vehicles. Consequently, their safety and durability are of utmost importance. LIBs undergo electrochemical side reactions at the electrode/electrolyte interface owing to strong oxidation and reduction effects on the positive and negative electrodes, respectively, during charging and discharging,1−4 producing organics5 and gases.2,6−10 For example, an electrolyte composed of ethylene carbonate (EC), C3O3H4, is thought to undergo reductive decomposition by electrons from the anode. These reactions trigger irreversible capacity losses and issues related to durability and safety.11 In particular, CO2 and O2 generation by oxidative decomposition can cause serious problems, such as a high internal pressure and LIB ignition or even explosion. Consequently, electrochemical side reactions that occur at the interface during charging must be precisely understood.

Many researchers have investigated the electrochemical side reactions between the cathode and electrolyte that generate CO2 in LIBs.5,12−20 Some studies have attributed the electrochemical oxidation of the electrolyte to the coexistence of active materials and conductive agents, such as carbon, which results in the release of CO2.2,12 Conversely, the active oxygen in the electrode contributes to electrolyte decomposition.12−20 The release of oxygen from the cathode has also been reported. Dahn et al. compared the oxygen release from charged LixCoO2 and LixNiO2 and showed that delithiated LixNiO2 was more thermally unstable and prone to releasing oxygen.11 The evolution of oxygen gas was subsequently extensively studied using various techniques, such as time-resolved X-ray diffractometry,21 Raman spectroscopy,22−25 and X-ray absorption spectroscopy.26−29 This evolution significantly deteriorates the cathodic performance, can induce battery ignition, and threatens the battery safety.30 Experimentally, the release of oxygen is related to cation migration.26 By contrast, computational techniques, primarily the density functional theory (DFT) calculations, have been used to analyze reactions at the cathode–electrolyte interface by considering electron–transfer reactions.31−33 For example, Kumar et al. proposed a model in which the EC electrolyte decomposed in two steps:33 the first step involved the dissociation of hydrogen from the EC, which was followed by ring opening in the second step. However, in many of these studies, only reactions involving a single electrolyte molecule and the cathode have been examined, whereas the interactions between the electrolyte molecules have been neglected. Notably, Okuno et al.34 recently mentioned the existence of several adsorption patterns for EC on the electrode by considering interactions between the electrolyte molecules using DFT-driven molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. They also proposed a model in which the ethylene group was adsorbed onto surface oxygen atoms on the electrode, which initiated the ring-opening reaction. This adsorption mode was observed experimentally via sum frequency generation.35,36 Therefore, considering the interactions between the electrolyte molecules in the analysis of the reactions at the cathode–electrolyte interface is important.

Although DFT-MD calculations can provide accurate data for the chemical reactions, including the electron–transfer reactions, they are computationally demanding, which limits the number of atoms included in the model and simulation time.37 Classical force-field methods are generally used in long-time MD calculations involving large systems.38 However, the typical classical force-field calculations are unable to simulate chemical reactions that involve bond breakage and formation; consequently, they are difficult to use when studying interfacial cathode–electrolyte reactions.37 Hence, in this study, we addressed the issues of computational cost and the inability to handle chemical reactions by performing MD simulations using the universal neural network potential (UNNP), which has recently attracted significant attention.39 The UNNP method can be used to compute the energies and forces acting on atoms for an arbitrary input structural model. This is achieved by the deep learning NN architecture pertained by large amounts of compositional/structural (input) and energy/force (output) data obtained via first-principles calculations. We used the PreFerred Potential (PFP) as the UNNP implementation in MATLANTIS software; the PFP was built based on approximately 107 DFT calculation results including various slab and adsorption structure models.40 Accordingly, we reproduced the computational accuracy of the first-principles calculations, while enabling MD simulations for arbitrary structural inputs with large numbers of particles for long times. Moreover, we focused on LixCoO2,41 which is widely used in current LIBs owing to its cycling stability, and LixNiO2,42 which has a higher energy density but lacks thermal stability, as the cathode materials. We investigated the mechanisms associated with the interfacial reactions involving the generation of CO2 and O2 and reaction-difference functions of the state of charge for the above-mentioned two cathode materials by simulating the cathode/EC interface using UNNP-MD.

UNNP-MD Simulations

Figures 2 and S4 show the interfacial structures of after 500 ps of MD simulations at 373 K, while those acquired at 473 K are shown in Figure S5. Formation of significant chemical bonds between EC and LixMO2 (M = Co, Ni) was not observed during the entire compositional regions in LixCoO2 (0 ≤ x ≤ 1) and the first half of charging (x > 0.5) in LixNiO2, whereas well-maintained layered structures were observed. Hydrogen was adsorbed onto the surface to form hydroxide ions during the second half of charging in LixNiO2 (x ≤ 0.25). The minimum bond lengths (dmin) between H and O at the electrode surface (Osurface) are approximately 0.95 Å for LixNiO2 (x ≤ 0.25), which clearly indicates the formation of hydroxide ions, whereas LixCoO2 (0 ≤ x ≤ 1) and LixNiO2 (0.5 ≤ x ≤ 1) show longer minimum bond lengths of O–H (dmin > 1.7 Å), as shown in Figure S4. Some Ni atoms at the surface moved to the Li layer in LixNiO2 (x = 0, 0.25) to occupy the tetrahedral sites, with chemical bonds forming between EC and LixNiO2, which corresponds to the ring opening of EC. CO2 and O2 gas formation was also confirmed, as shown in Figure S4. The oxygen atoms of the CO2 gas originated from the oxygen atoms on the outermost surface of the electrode material and those of the EC molecules. By contrast, the oxygen atoms of the O2 gas originated from the surface oxygen of the LixNiO2 electrode. Other than the outermost oxygen, neither LixNiO2 nor LixCoO2 (0 ≤ x ≤ 1) exhibited oxygen diffusion behavior, such as site hopping, in the lattice.

A detailed analysis of the UNNP-MD results for the EC/LixNiO2 interfacial model confirmed the presence of three types of reactions [annotated as (I)–(III)]. Notably, under the simulation conditions (duration: 500 ps, temperature: 373 and 473 K), decomposition reactions do not statistically occur in the LixCoO2 electrode. Reaction (I) involved a hydrogen atom dissociating from the molecular EC and being adsorbed by the surface oxygen of LixNiO2 (0 ≤ x ≤ 1); this process is hereafter referred to as the “hydrogen dissociation adsorption”. In reaction (II), the LixNiO2 (0 ≤ x ≤ 1) surface adsorbed an EC molecule, which subsequently decomposed through ring opening accompanied by the evolution of gaseous CO2 or CO. Notably, EC can decompose after the hydrogen dissociation adsorption step (I) or after the formation of a CC–Osurface bond. In reaction (III), O2 was generated from the LixNiO2 (0 ≤ x ≤ 1) surface oxygens, accompanied by the movement of Ni.

Hydrogen Dissociation

The hydrogen dissociated from EC was adsorbed by LixNiO2 (Figure 3). This reaction was observed to be accelerated at higher temperatures and in highly delithiated states (x < 0.5 for LixNiO2). Details of the MD-simulated hydrogen dissociation absorption process are shown in Figure 3a–c. Hydrogen dissociation absorption often proceeds through one of the two hydrogen bonds involving the CC atom in EC, which dissociates and reacts with oxygen on the electrode surface to form hydroxyl groups. These hydrogen dissociation results are consistent with the calculation results reported by Kumar et al.31 Furthermore, we observed the dissociation of up to two hydrogen atoms from a single EC molecule, as shown in Figure S6. Two hydrogen atoms were observed to dissociate in four out of nine EC molecules in which hydrogen had dissociated for the EC/LixNiO2 (x = 0) interface after 100 ps at 373 K.

CO2 Evolution by EC Decomposition

All CO2 molecules were generated by the opening of the EC ring adsorbed onto the electrode surface in the present MD simulations. The following two types of EC ring-opening reactions were identified by observing the various reactions: (1) ring opening after hydrogen dissociation and simultaneous CE–Osurface bonding or (2) ring opening after the formation of the CC–Osurface bond.

The former reaction was observed at x = 0 and 0.25, whereas the latter was observed at x = 0.5 (with CO2 production) and 0.75. The details of the former reaction are described in Figure 4, which shows snapshots of the processes wherein EC underwent ring opening to generate CO2 following the dissociation of hydrogen. This dissociation enabled the CE atom of EC to bond with the surface oxygen of LixNiO2 (0.5 ≤ x ≤ 0.75), after which ring opening occurred (Figure 4b,c). CO2, which was generated from the carbonate moiety in the EC molecule, was dissociated subsequently. Additionally, the acyclic molecule remaining after CO2 production reacted with an oxygen atom on the LixNiO2 surface to generate CO2 and/or CO molecules, as shown in Figures 4e–h and S7. This mechanism of the formation of CO2 from the carbonate moiety during the decomposition of EC is in accordance with the DFT-MD results reported by Okuno et al.34 In our UNNP-MD simulation, ring opening was observed in eight instances at x = 0 and 0.25, and always resulted in the formation of CO2.

As previously mentioned, hydrogen dissociation reactions on the surface of LixNiO2 (x < 0.5) triggered the ring opening of EC and the generation of CO2. For a quantitative analysis of the progress of the reaction, the number of bonds between the dissociated hydrogen and surface oxygen of LixNiO2 (0 ≤ x ≤ 1) that are shorter than 1.2 Å are shown in Figure 5a,b. The hydrogen atom became increasingly monotonically bound to the electrode surface oxygen as Li deintercalation progressed, thus indicating that the reaction proceeded continuously and was irreversible. In addition, the number of bonds between CE and the surface oxygen of LixNiO2 (0 ≤ x ≤ 1) was determined (Figure 5c,d), with separations less than 2.0 Å defined as “bonds”. The number of bonds between CE and the surface oxygen increased as hydrogen dissociation adsorption increased, albeit with a slight delay. Hence, we confirmed that CO2 was generated in a stepwise manner, in which hydrogen dissociation occurred first, followed by CE/surface-oxygen bonding, and finally led to EC ring opening.

Okuno et al.34 investigated the reaction at the EC/LiNi0.5Mn1.5O4 interface. Although the ring opening of EC molecules after the formation of the Osurface–CC bond was reported using the nudged elastic band (NEB) and first-principles DFT calculations, CO2 generation from the surface reaction was not observed. We inferred that the different CO2-evolution behavior stems from differences in the electrode composition or higher reaction-event probabilities due to the larger- and longer-scale MD simulations. Only one example of CO2 formation was observed at x = 0.5, indicating that from a statistical-frequency perspective, longer calculations are necessary to examine this surface reaction in detail.

O2 Evolution from the Surface of the LixNiO2 Electrode

The formed O2 gas (Table 1) was found to originate from the O atoms at the outermost surface of LixNiO2 (0 ≤ x ≤ 1), with the EC molecule atoms not being involved. Figure 8 depicts the atomic reactions associated with the O2 generation from LixNiO2 (x = 0.25) at 473 K, which involves the following sequence: (1) Ni ions at the octahedral sites on the electrode surface migrate to neighboring tetrahedral sites and (2) two neighboring oxygen atoms, one located at a NiO4 tetrahedron and the other at a NiO5 polyhedron (truncated NiO6 octahedron at the outermost surface), approach each other and form a bond that generates an oxygen dimer. (3) The oxygen dimer is subsequently released.

(d,e) Oxygen dimer formation involving the oxygen atoms at the NiO4 tetrahedron and NiO5 truncated octahedron. (f) The release of the surface-oxygen dimer as molecular oxygen. The EC molecule has been omitted for clarity.

Interestingly, neither Ni migration nor oxygen release was observed when the EC molecules were removed from the model (i.e., at the vacuum/LixNiO2 interface; Figure S8). Therefore, the introduction of EC molecules enhanced the migration of Ni ions to the surface, where they formed NiO4 tetrahedra. An examination of the area where Ni movement was observed revealed that OH formed through hydrogen adsorption at the surface oxygens of the octahedral sites prior to Ni migration, thus indicating that hydrogen dissociation adsorption from EC to the LixNiO2 surface triggered the release of oxygen gas. According to a previous first-principles calculation study using the NEB method, Ni migration is unlikely to occur in bulk LixNiO2 when no oxygen vacancy exists.43 We observed no Ni migration events in the bulk Li0.25NiO2 and NiO2 structure during the 500 ps UNNP-MD simulation even at 1073 K (not shown). Hence, Ni migration may relate to the formation of defects in NiO2 framework. To confirm the role played by the surface OH in the migration of Ni, we performed MD calculations at 473 K for 500 ps in the absence of the EC molecules using LixCoO2 (x = 0.25) and LixNiO2 (x = 0.25) electrodes with hydrogen-capped surface oxygen (Figure 9a). The results indicated the migration of Ni and Co at the outermost surface to the Li layer (Figure 9b,c). Therefore, the adsorption of hydrogen atoms on the surface promoted the movement of Ni and Co. Oxygen was not released in these reactions, which is attributed to the introduction of hydrogen atoms on the surface oxygen that prevented the formation of the oxygen dimer. Note that the hydrogenation of surface oxygen in LiCoO2 and LiNiO2 occurred not only via reactions involving EC molecules but also through reactions with atmospheric H2O molecules. Hence, water contamination during battery assembly may affect the O2 gas release in the charged state.

Additionally, the LixNiO2 (x = 0)/EC interface (wherein all outermost surface oxygen atoms of NiO2 were capped with hydrogen) was subjected to MD calculations at 473 K. Neither hydrogen desorption nor CO2 generation was observed during the 500 ps MD simulation. Hence, capping the surface oxygen atoms with hydrogen atoms inhibited hydrogen dissociation from the molecular EC. This finding further confirmed that the dissociation of hydrogen from EC is necessary for EC ring opening and CO2 generation. Therefore, capping the surface oxygen atoms is an effective approach for preventing side reactions between EC and the charged electrode. However, the OH ions on the surface promoted Ni migration, leading to a disrupted surface structure during the MD calculation (Figure S10a). Furthermore, water molecules were formed by an additional hydrogen atom reacting with a hydrogen-capped surface oxygen (Figure S10b).

Gas Evolution and Energy Changes

Thus far, we have discussed the individual elemental reactions occurring during the MD simulation. Next, we chronologically analyzed the reaction events and energy changes that occurred during the MD simulation of the EC/LixNiO2 interfacial model at 473 K, with the potential energy changes shown in Figure S11. No significant change in the potential energy was observed for x ≥ 0.5, whereas the highly delithiated LixNiO2 (x < 0.5) exhibited a monotonic decrease in the potential energy, consistent with that of an exothermic reaction. The calculated energy change corresponded well with the four types of elemental reactions observed in the MD simulation, viz, EC dehydrogenation and adsorption, EC ring opening, CO2 (or CO) formation, and O2 release, since these reactions were observed at highly delithiated compositions (x ≥ 0.5). Figure 10a,b show the potential energy of the system, hydrogen dissociation adsorption, cumulative values for CE–OE ring opening, and cumulative values of CO2 generation as a function of the simulation time; the changes in the hydrogen dissociation adsorption with time are the same as those presented in Figure 6. The cumulative values for CE–OE ring opening were determined from the number of instances where the CE–OE distance exceeded 2.0 Å, whereas those for CO2 generation were determined from the number of carbon atoms with only two oxygen atoms within a distance of 1.97 Å. Figure S12 shows the radial distribution function profiles of oxygen on the electrode surface (Osurface) with hydrogen (Osurface–H) and with the carbon atom of EC (Osurface–CE) over the MD simulation time. At t = 0, no peaks are observed within 1.8 Å. However, after 100 ps, a peak near 0.95 Å, which is thought to correspond to the hydroxylation of the electrode surface oxygen owing to hydrogen dissociation, and a peak near 1.4 Å, which corresponds to Osurface–CE from the ring-opening reaction of EC and its adsorption to the electrode, are observed. These observations confirm the progression of the aforementioned reactions. Figure 10a shows that hydrogen dissociation adsorption is associated with a significant decrease (stabilization) in energy. For example, an energy decrease of 1.42 × 10–18 J was observed between 470 and 475 ps (indicated by the green arrow in Figure 10a and the magnified image in Figure S13), during which one hydrogen atom dissociated from both EC molecules. This energy decrease is comparable to the heat associated with the complete combustion of an EC molecule (1.85 × 10–18 J).44 The energy decrease at this point was ∼0.8 times the heat associated with the complete combustion of one EC molecule. This decrease in energy can be attributed to the stabilization of the electrode surface oxygens through capping with hydrogen. These results are consistent with the exothermic nature of the hydrogen-extraction process from an EC molecule on the cathode surface, as determined by Okuno et al., who employed NEB calculations in the absence of CO2 evolution.34 However, no significant decrease in energy was observed for the EC-ring-opening and CO2-evolution reactions at 150 ps (Figure 10a). By contrast, Figure 10b shows a clear decrease in energy for the EC-ring-opening and CO2-evolution reactions, as highlighted by the green bands in the figure. Thus, the lack of energy change observed at the time of the EC-ring-opening and CO2-evolution processes in Figure 10a is attributed to an exothermic delay, as the energy continuously decreased between 150 and 170 ps. We analyzed the chronological variation of Bader charge for Ni and Co ions in LixNiO2 and LixCoO2 (x = 0.25 and 0) electrodes, respectively, as shown in Figure S14. While the valence of Co ions showed almost no change over time due to no progress in the decomposition reaction as shown in Figure 2, the oxidation state of Ni ions in the first interfacial layer of NiO significantly decreased (i.e., reduction of Ni) within the initial 100 ps. In this region, oxidative decomposition reactions involving H, EC ring-opening, and CO2 evolution occur in a complex manner, leading to the reduction of the surface Ni ions as they receive electrons. Overall, all three reactions are exothermic, with energy changes comparable to the energy associated with the combustion of the EC molecule. Although the discussion above has focused on the changes in the energy profile related to the three mentioned reactions, other elementary processes may be involved. Therefore, a quantitative discussion of the overall changes in the energy profile is not straightforward.

Conclusion

We performed UNNP-MD calculations to elucidate the side-reaction mechanism at the positive-electrode/electrolyte interface, where a layered rocksalt-type oxide LixCoO2 or LixNiO2 (0 ≤ x ≤ 1) was selected as the electrode material and EC was used as the electrolyte solvent.

We found that EC decomposed through two reaction routes: (1) EC ring opening following hydrogen dissociation adsorption and (2) EC ring opening following Osurface–CC bond formation. The release of CO2 gas followed these reactions, and the MD simulations revealed that reaction (1) occurred more frequently than reaction (2). Furthermore, hydrogen dissociation adsorption in reaction (1) was exothermic because the reactive Osurface atoms were capped by H atoms. Therefore, suppressing the dissociation of hydrogen from EC or the reduction of reactive Osurface atoms may reduce undesirable EC-decomposition reactions.

The MD calculations indicated that EC molecules may enhance oxygen release from the electrode surface. The dissociation of hydrogen from the EC molecule [first half of reaction (1)] led to the formation of surface OH and reduction of the transition metal at the outermost surface, with lower–valent transition metal ions migrating to tetrahedral sites, thereby promoting the release of O2 gas.

This UNNP-MD study revealed that the dissociation of hydrogen from EC and the resulting formation of hydroxyl groups are responsible for the generation of CO2 and O2. Therefore, hydrogen dissociation adsorption onto active Osurface atoms is primarily responsible for the side reactions that decompose EC. We believe that reducing active-oxygen exposure, possibly by using surface coatings, may mitigate gas evolution from the positive-electrode/electrolyte interface.

Methods

The reported crystal structures of the layered-type LiCoO2 and LiNiO2 (i.e., R3m) were used as structural inputs.45,46Figure 1 shows a representative computational model of the EC solvent molecule (liquid) and the LiMO2 (M = Co, Ni; solid) slab. The (104) facet was selected as the LixCoO2 or LixNiO2 electrode surface, as it has been reported to be among the most stable surfaces.47,48

The UNNP calculations in this study used the MATLANTIS software and PFP version 2.0.0.40 The UNNP was validated by comparing the energies of various interfacial models calculated using both first-principles DFT and UNNP calculations. The following models were used to compare accuracies: (1) a vacuum layer and a slab with the (104) facet of Li48Ni48O96 (Li48Co48O96) and Ni48O96(Co48O96) and (2) EC and each of the above electrode slab models, in which 18 EC molecules were introduced. DFT was used to preliminarily relax the structures, and models with introduced artificial strains were created by randomly displacing the constituent atoms. Energies were calculated using both DFT and UNNP. First-principles DFT calculations were conducted using the Vienna ab initio Simulation Package49−51 applying the projector-augmented-wave method.52,53 We used the revised Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof exchange–correlation functional for solids to describe exchange and correlation interactions under the general gradient approximation.54,55 The cutoff energy was set to 500 eV, and only the Γ-point was used for the k-point mesh. On-site Coulombic correction (DFT + U) was applied to the localized electronic states of the Co and Ni ions, and the U values for the Ni and Co 3d orbitals were set to 6.2 and 3.32 eV, respectively.56,57 DFT-MD simulations were performed for 18 EC/(Li48Ni48O96, Li48Co48O96, Ni48O96, or Co48O96) interfacial models over 8 ps at 1000 K with an energy cutoff of 350 eV. The time-evolution outputs of the structure, energy, and forces obtained from the DFT-MD simulation were compared with the UNNP-derived energy and forces.58,59

MD simulations using UNNP were performed for the supercell models (Figure 1). A slab with the (104) facet of Li180Ni180O360 (Li180Co180O360) and a cell with 100 EC molecules were attached. The lattice size of the EC liquid electrolyte was adjusted to match the experimental density value of 1.32 g cm–3, and 100 EC molecules were randomly placed inside. Each LixM180O360 (M = Co or Ni) electrode contained 0, 45, 90, 135, and 180 lithium ions, representing the 100, 75, 50, 25, and 0% charged states, respectively. We prelabeled whether the O atoms were in the electrode phase or contained in EC before MD simulation. This allowed us to determine whether the oxygen involved in the CO2 or O2 generation reactions originated from the electrode or the EC molecules. MD simulations were conducted for the aforementioned interfacial models in a canonical (NVT) ensemble for 500 ps with a time step of 1 fs, after performing structural-relaxation calculations. Thus, the UNNP-MD simulations in this study were performed on a scale of ∼1700 atoms and 500 ps. Compared with conventional DFT-MD simulations, this simulation is significantly larger in terms of the number of atoms and simulation time. However, considering that real systems involve particle counts on the scale of Avogadro’s number and observed on time scales of seconds to hours, the MD size in this study is still very small. Therefore, to accelerate the decomposition reaction, the MD simulations were performed at 373 and 473 K, which are higher than the standard battery operating temperatures but lower than the boiling point of EC (517 K).
