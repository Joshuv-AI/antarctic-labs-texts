Rapid response and impact assessment of water quality incidents in urban water distribution systems using forensic techniques
Authors: YooJin Oh, HaeKeum Park, Taehyeon Kim, Jayong Koo, Do Guen Yoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Water quality incidents in drinking water distribution networks require rapid operational decision-making to limit contaminant propagation and service disruption. This study proposes a hydraulically informed operational shutdown framework that integrates incident occurrence time and utility response time to support real-time decision-making during water quality emergencies. Network-based forensic analysis is applied to estimate contaminant travel times under variable demand conditions and to identify valve isolation actions that are feasible under realistic field constraints. To translate analytical results into actionable operations, the concept of Possible Operated Segments (POS) is introduced, defining the smallest isolation units that can be practically actuated during emergency response. In addition, a Cause–Impact–Duration (CID) analysis is employed to quantify location-specific impacts and the time-dependent escalation of service disruption. By jointly considering hydraulic propagation dynamics and operational feasibility, the proposed framework enables utilities to evaluate when and where intervention is most effective. Application to representative turbidity scenarios demonstrates that the framework provides clear guidance for prioritizing valve operations, containing incident impacts, and improving response timeliness, highlighting its potential as a practical decision-support tool for enhancing emergency response capability and operational resilience in drinking water distribution systems.

Introduction

Ensuring safe water quality is a critical priority for urban water systems, which are essential components of sustainable and resilient cities. Despite significant advances in water treatment and distribution technologies, water quality degradation incidents—such as red and black water events—continue to occur, posing risks to public health and the integrity of water infrastructure supplies1–3. Red water events are commonly caused by the release of iron-related corrosion products or sediments, whereas black water events are typically associated with manganese-related discoloration within distribution pipes4,5. Recent discoveries of larvae in tap water further highlight the vulnerabilities in water system maintenance and the potential threats to critical infrastructure6.

Water quality incidents arise from a broad range of causes across both the production and supply processes7. In the treatment stage, insufficient residual chlorine, inadequate removal of turbidity or color, and excessive chemical dosing may result from aging facilities or poor operational management8. In the supply stage, rapid fluctuations in flow and pressure can resuspend sediments, causing chromaticity issues or discoloration events. Construction activities—such as pipe replacement or emergency repairs—may introduce contaminants due to pressure disturbances or physical damage. Improper valve operations can also disturb accumulated deposits, whereas inadequate management of drainage or ancillary systems may allow small animals or foreign materials to enter the system9,10.

Contaminants associated with these incidents include microorganisms (e.g., general bacteria and coliforms), metals (iron, zinc, manganese), turbidity, chromaticity, and chemical substances leached from pipe materials or coatings. These contaminants are influenced by the environmental conditions of buried pipelines, reservoirs, and the quality of the supplied water. Exposure to trace toxic substances may lead to long-term health effects, while acute contamination—such as pathogenic microorganisms or high chemical loads—can trigger outbreaks and infrastructure service interruptions11–13.

Such water quality incidents not only cause physical and economic damage but also reduce consumer trust in tap water, leading to long-term declines in drinking-water usage and system reliability14–17. In Korea, the severity of water quality incidents is formally assessed based on the number of affected customers, in accordance with the Water Supply and Waterworks Installation Act (Republic of Korea). The designation of “priority management zones”—areas vulnerable to water-quality degradation due to aging pipelines—further institutionalizes population-based impact assessment. Accordingly, this study quantifies incident impact using the number of exposed or disconnected customers, ensuring consistency with actual operational decision-making. Although preventive measures must be implemented, the complete elimination of water quality incidents is infeasible; therefore, immediate and well-designed response strategies are essential for minimizing damage18.

In recent years, scientific and technological advances—particularly the emergence of smart water distribution systems—have significantly enhanced real-time monitoring and anomaly detection. In water supply systems, response strategies for water quality incidents are generally divided into three phases: contamination detection and investigation, operational response, and remediation and recovery19. In the detection and investigation phase, research has primarily focused on optimizing sensor placement, improving detection accuracy, and identifying contamination sources within limited budgets20–23. While these efforts have advanced early warning capabilities, conventional response methods still suffer from delays in the operational response stage, as utilities often rely on dispatching field personnel to manually identify and operate valves. These delays increase the likelihood of contaminant propagation and broaden the spatial extent of impact18,20.

During the operational response stage, adjustments to system operations—such as rapid valve control and network reconfiguration—are necessary to minimize the spread of contamination and restore normal conditions24–27. Several studies have explored response-oriented strategies for contamination or failure events in water distribution systems, including optimization-based response planning, case-based decision support, and impact-reduction methodologies28–30. However, despite the importance of this time-critical stage, these efforts often rely on offline analyses, simplified operational assumptions, or limited consideration of response-time constraints, and relatively few studies provide immediate, actionable, and time-sensitive response frameworks that can be directly implemented under realistic field conditions. Most existing studies remain focused on detection and source identification, resulting in a notable research gap between early warning and operational intervention31–34.

To address this gap, this study introduces a novel operational response framework that expands the role of forensic techniques beyond traditional post-event source tracing. In the proposed framework, forensic methods are repurposed to reconstruct contamination propagation pathways and infer the sequence of valve operations required under realistic field constraints. Building on this approach, we define the concept of Possible Operated Segments (POS)—the smallest verifiable and operable isolation units during emergency response. POS serves as a practical counterpart to theoretical isolation segments, representing only those pipeline units that can be feasibly actuated within the constrained time window associated with contamination response.

Additionally, this study enhances the existing Cause–Impact–Duration (CID) framework by incorporating hydraulically derived contaminant travel times and empirically validated operational parameters. Through this integration, CID transitions from a descriptive conceptual tool to a quantitative, time-sensitive decision-support mechanism capable of capturing the spatiotemporal dynamics of contamination. This enriched CID analysis provides an intuitive visualization of incident progression, the potential scale of customer impact, and the time window available for effective intervention.

By integrating forensic reconstruction, POS-based segmentation, and a hydraulically informed CID analysis, the proposed methodology offers a comprehensive operational framework for real-time contamination control. This unified structure enables water utility managers to anticipate damage escalation, prioritize feasible isolation actions, and enhance rapid-response capabilities in smart urban water distribution systems.

Beyond immediate emergency response, the framework also generates insights that are directly relevant to long-term asset management. Because CID quantifies both the spatial extent of impacts and the time feasibility of operational isolation, it reveals pipeline segments that consistently produce large service disruptions or cannot be isolated within acceptable response windows. These segments can be prioritized for rehabilitation planning, targeted valve replacement, or installation of remote-operated devices. In this way, the framework extends its value from short-term operational mitigation to long-term reliability improvement and maintenance decision-making.

Materials and methods

Select a representative scenario and location for a water incident

A review of water quality incidents recorded in Korea’s KRM (K-water Risk Management) system from April 2018 to June 2021 indicates that turbidity and red-water events constitute the majority of reported cases, with turbidity being the most frequent cause. Turbidity events generally arise from two mechanisms: (i) sediment resuspension caused by flow-rate fluctuations during system operations such as network switching, valve adjustments, or pipe breakage, and (ii) changes in flow direction during similar operations, which transport degraded water toward consumers.

To further understand how these events manifest within the system, water quality incidents in drinking water systems can be broadly classified into (i) events originating at water treatment plants or service reservoirs where the effluent quality deteriorates before entering the distribution system, and (ii) incidents occurring within the distribution network due to operational disturbances. In treatment or storage facilities, elevated raw-water turbidity during flooding, excessive chemical dosing, or inadequate process control during maintenance activities can result in water quality deterioration. Within the distribution network, rapid changes in flow rate or pressure—often induced by valve operations or construction activities—can mobilize accumulated sediments and cause discoloration or turbidity. Insufficient management of ancillary systems may also allow foreign materials or small organisms to enter the supply system.

Based on these patterns, four representative turbidity-related scenarios were defined for analysis:High turbidity originating from water treatment plants (or reservoirs) and propagating into the service area;Case 2High turbidity occurring due to aging infrastructure in large-diameter mains;Case 3Turbidity triggered by sudden velocity changes in low-flow sections with substantial sediment deposition;Case 4High turbidity caused by flow-direction changes due to system switching, valve operations, or pipe damage.

To simulate turbidity generation associated with flow fluctuations, numerical analysis was performed using EPANET 2.2 to evaluate the velocity and flow-direction variability of individual pipes. Because demand uncertainty significantly affects the propagation of water-quality disturbances, Monte Carlo simulation was employed to assess sensitivity to demand fluctuations. Following prior studies35–38, nodal demands were generated using a normal distribution, and 100 demand realizations were produced for each node.

The parameters of the demand distribution were determined using observed demand statistics during the peak summer season, when water consumption is highest, with the mean and variance estimated from actual billing-based demand data collected over a representative one-month period. To maintain consistency with observed system-wide water use, each demand realization was adjusted so that the aggregate nodal demand remained within the range of total consumption derived from billing records.

Demand realizations were generated at an hourly temporal resolution and used to represent plausible system demand conditions for hydraulic analysis. Hydraulic simulations were conducted for each demand realization using EPANET 2.2 to evaluate the resulting flow conditions. A pipe was classified as “unidirectional” if its flow direction remained consistent in more than 80% of simulations; otherwise, it was classified as “bidirectional” These classifications were subsequently used in calculating shortest paths and identifying sensitive locations for scenario initiation.

Estimating spread and residence time for water incidents

Water quality modeling in water supply networks has been explored using various tools such as PODDS and EPANET-MSX39,40. However, the current limitations in water quality monitoring infrastructure and available data can reduce calibration accuracy, and the use of highly complex models may hinder rapid simulation and real-time response during emergency incidents.

To ensure operational practicality, this study adopts a simplified yet robust analytical approach—specifically, a residence-time analysis combined with a non-reactive tracer (Tracer) model. This approach enables fast computation of contaminant spread while maintaining a level of realism suitable for field application. In this study, the term forensic techniques refers to a retrospective, network-based analytical approach that reconstructs the likely propagation pathways, elapsed times, and valve-isolation sequences of a water quality incident from a hypothesized source, using hydraulic simulations and shortest-path–based residence-time estimation.

To evaluate the propagation of water quality disturbances, multiple EPANET 2.2 hydraulic simulations were performed under varying demand conditions. The resulting pipe-level flow patterns were summarized in a matrix, and representative residence times were derived using the shortest path between nodes, based on average pipe flow velocities and pipe lengths.

Elapsed time since the incident occurred

To minimize the spread of a water quality incident, it is essential to estimate the time required for utilities to recognize the incident and initiate an on-site response. This elapsed time includes (i) the detection time through water quality sensor alerts or resident reports, (ii) the mobilization time required to dispatch field personnel, and (iii) the travel time to the site followed by the valve-operation time needed to isolate the affected area.

The mobilization and travel time can be estimated based on the distance between the dispatch location and the incident site as well as the average vehicle speed. For example, if the distance to the incident site is 20 km and the average travel speed is assumed to be 40 km/h, the estimated travel time is approximately 30 min.

In this study, field observations were conducted to determine realistic valve-operation times, including necessary preparation tasks such as cleaning valve boxes. These observations were conducted by operating field valves under routine maintenance conditions, and the number of revolutions and the time required for each operation were directly measured by trained utility staff. The results indicated that valve operation requires approximately 5 s per revolution under good valve conditions and up to 15 s per revolution under poor conditions. Based on these measurements, the opening or closing time of a 150–200 mm valve (requiring approximately 20–25 revolutions) was estimated to be around 12 min, including preparation time. This estimated valve-operation time includes the time required for valve location verification, on-site setup, and mechanical operation of the valve after arrival, but excludes additional delays associated with severe obstructions or abnormal site conditions. Because this study focuses on operational response feasibility, the proposed framework primarily aims to reduce response delays by improving valve location verification and decision-making rather than by shortening physical clearance tasks.

Deriving shortest paths and residence times between cut points based on network analyzer techniques over time

Network analysis is a widely used method for quantifying the structure, propagation, and temporal evolution of events within water distribution systems. Because the objective of this study is to evaluate how water quality disturbances spread from one location to another, a network-based approach provides a suitable analytical foundation. However, accurately simulating repair or response actions across all time steps is challenging because real-time calibration is difficult during abnormal situations such as water quality incidents. Under such conditions, additional uncertainties—such as fluctuating demands, variable operational states, and unsteady hydraulic conditions—must be considered.

To address these uncertainties, this study probabilistically generated a range of possible system conditions by incorporating variations in demand, supply, and operational status. Based on these probabilistic scenarios, we applied a shortest-path–based residence time estimation framework, using network analysis techniques previously developed for water distribution systems. The derivation of shortest paths and corresponding residence times between nodes followed the method proposed by38.

Using the final adjacency matrix derived through the procedure shown in Fig. 1, the shortest path between any two points in the observation system was computed, and all intermediate nodes along each path were identified. The residence time for each pipe segment was then calculated based on its length and the average flow velocity obtained from the numerical analysis. This estimation assumes that turbidity propagates at the bulk flow velocity of water, without accounting for significant diffusion, adsorption to pipe walls, or reactive processes, thereby providing a conservative, first-order approximation suitable for rapid operational response. By summing the residence times of all pipe segments along a given shortest path, the total spread time of a water quality incident was estimated for each scenario.

Area shutdowns to prevent the spread of a water incident

Minimum isolation zone (segment) by water valve

A segment in a water supply network, first introduced by41, refers to a group of water mains and cutoff points that become isolated together when a specific valve is closed. Korean water supply design standards require shut-off or control valves to be installed at critical locations, including the starting and end points of water mains, branching points, connecting pipes, major drainage facilities, and crossings such as bridges or railways. Operating these valves makes it possible to isolate the region affected by a water quality incident, and this isolated region is defined as a segment. However, valve operation cannot occur instantaneously. The number of valves that can actually be operated during an emergency depends on the spread time of the water quality incident and the dispatch and travel time of field personnel. Consequently, theoretical segments may not fully reflect the zones that can be isolated in practice under strict time constraints.

To address this limitation, this study introduces the concept of Possible Operated Segments (POS)—the smallest isolation units that can be feasibly actuated in real emergency situations. The criteria used to define POS were established through technical discussions with the local water utility, reflecting current operational practices and field constraints. A POS is defined as a segment that meets practical criteria: it contains valves whose physical locations can be reliably verified in GIS datasets, valves with a diameter of at least 100 mm, valves located such that the travel time between them is at least one hour, and valves installed at network branching points. These criteria ensure that POS represents isolation units that are not only theoretically defined but also operationally achievable within the available response window.

Incident impact is quantified by the number of customers exposed to contaminated water, the portion of demand affected, and the spatial extent of contamination. In this study, the impact is evaluated by simultaneously considering the number of customers exposed before the contamination is detected and the number of customers who experience temporary service interruption due to valve shutdowns required for isolation and flushing operations. Analyzing CIDs

CID (Cause–Impact–Duration) is a framework used to characterize the cause of an incident, its resulting impact, and the time scale over which the impact evolves42. CID analysis enables quantitative assessment of incident risk at specific locations and supports the development of operational response strategies. In this study, CID was applied to express how the impact of a water quality incident changes according to its point of origin and the elapsed time required for detection and response.

In the CID structure, the Incident Cause was defined as turbidity events triggered at flow-variation–sensitive pipelines previously identified through hydraulic analysis. The Incident Impact was quantified as the number of customers affected by the incident, which increases as the elapsed time extends. The Incident Duration incorporates the time required for incident recognition, dispatch of field personnel, and valve operation, reflecting real operational conditions.

The resulting CID plots illustrate when and where intervention is most effective by linking contamination propagation dynamics with operational feasibility. For example, if the CID curve shows a rapid escalation in affected customers when valve isolation is delayed beyond a threshold (e.g., 30 min), utilities can set response targets to initiate valve actions within the feasible window. Considering that on-site valve closure may require up to 12 min depending on valve conditions, the CID framework enables water utilities to plan timely interventions and minimize the overall impact of water quality incidents.

Study area

The study area consists of two water-supply service blocks, M1 and M2, which correspond to the official middle blocks used by the local municipality for operational management of the water distribution system. In Korea, middle blocks are the primary spatial units utilized for hydraulic operation, maintenance planning, pressure management, and emergency response. Thus, the selection of M1 and M2 ensures consistency with real-world management practices and provides a practical basis for evaluating the applicability of the proposed methodology.

As shown in Fig. 2, M1 is supplied by the GB basin, whereas M2 receives water from the MJ basin. The two blocks possess distinct network structures—M1 is predominantly dendritic, while M2 is reticulated—allowing for comparative assessment of scenario behavior under different hydraulic configurations. Table 2 provides detailed characteristics of each block, including the number of customers, pipeline length, total number of valves, and the count of valves that are available for operational use.

To minimize hydraulic modeling error, the pipe-network data for both blocks were validated and calibrated using field-measured flow information. Flow-based calibration using a harmony-search method was applied to improve the accuracy of residence-time estimation, and GIS-based valve inventories were used to ensure that only realistic, operable valves were incorporated in the shutdown analysis. To further enhance practical relevance, operational parameters essential for emergency response—such as detection time, dispatch duration, average vehicle travel speed, and realistic valve-operation times—were verified through interviews with on-site utility personnel.

Incident duration analysis results

To estimate the time required to recognize and respond to a water-quality incident, this study considered the combined duration of incident detection, dispatch of field personnel, and on-site valve operation. The minimum recognition time corresponds to situations where the incident is immediately identified through real-time monitoring, while the maximum recognition time reflects the hydraulic residence time required for the contaminant to reach the farthest downstream location before detection. Dispatch time was calculated based on the travel distance from the dispatch point to the valve that must be operated first, and when multiple valves were required to isolate a POS, the farthest valve among them was used as the representative distance. Because the valve locations operated during response vary across scenarios, both dispatch time and valve-operation time change as the recognition time increases.

Table 3 presents the minimum and maximum elapsed-time ranges for each scenario in M1. In Case 1, the elapsed time can be minimized when the incident is detected immediately and the outlet valve at the reservoir is closed without delay, although care is required to avoid releasing high-turbidity water into receiving streams during the closure period. In Case 2, more time is needed because additional valves must be operated due to multiple branching points along the large-diameter main; in M1, two valves must be manipulated sequentially, beginning with the downstream valve to prevent further propagation and then closing the upstream valve to prevent movement toward adjacent POSs. In Case 3, the incident occurs in a low-flow terminal section, and the dendritic structure of M1 prevents spread to the next POS, so only a single valve operation is necessary. In Case 4, demand-driven fluctuations in flow direction require closing upstream and downstream valves around the affected section.

Incident impact analysis results

The impact of each incident scenario was quantified as the number of customers within the affected segment. As time progresses, contaminated water propagates into adjacent segments; once contamination reaches a segment, all customers within that segment are considered exposed.

Figure 4 presents the minimum and maximum impact ranges for the four incident scenarios in Middle Block M1. In Case 1 (high turbidity at the reservoir outfall), even with immediate recognition and rapid isolation, at least three customers are affected; in the worst case, all customers supplied downstream of the outfall may be exposed. In Case 2, the impact is initially confined to the small area supplied by the large-diameter main, but in the worst case the contamination can propagate to all customers downstream of that main. In Case 3, where the incident occurs in a low-flow section, the contamination does not spread to the next POS, and thus the impact remains localized within the affected POS. In Case 4, characterized by flow-direction variability, the entire POS may be affected, and in the worst case, contamination may spread to the adjacent POS.

A comparison between M1 and M2 reveals clear structural differences in system vulnerability. M1, with its dendritic configuration, exhibits unidirectional flows and therefore enables faster contaminant propagation, resulting in a shorter response window and a steeper increase in incident impact. In contrast, M2’s reticulated (looped) structure provides hydraulic redundancy, slowing contaminant movement and allowing operators more time to intervene. These differences underscore the need for network-specific response strategies and demonstrate the adaptability of the CID framework across varying hydraulic configurations.

In addition, repeated interpretation of CID plots across multiple incidents can reveal network segments that consistently experience high impacts under delayed response conditions, indicating areas that may be considered chronically vulnerable and informative for future rehabilitation prior itization.

Network-aware decision protocol for rapid response to water quality incidents

Building on the CID-based impact analysis presented in Sect. Creating a CID plot, this study formalizes the proposed response logic into a network-aware decision protocol for rapid response to water quality incidents. The objective of this protocol is to translate hydraulic analysis results and CID outcomes into structured, actionable response decisions that can be implemented under time-critical emergency conditions in urban water distribution systems.

In this protocol, Possible Operated Segments (POS) are identified and validated during normal operating conditions, prior to any incident. POS represent the smallest isolation units that can be realistically actuated based on valve locations, network topology, travel time between valves, and operational feasibility. This advance preparation allows incident response to focus on selecting and prioritizing feasible isolation actions rather than redefining segmentation under emergency conditions.

When a water quality incident is detected through real-time monitoring systems or customer reports, the protocol initiates incident localization and contaminant propagation assessment using a calibrated hydraulic model. Dominant propagation paths are identified under the prevailing hydraulic conditions, enabling operators to determine whether contamination is expected to spread primarily upstream or downstream.

Network topology directly influences response strategies. In dendritic systems, where flow paths are largely unidirectional and hydraulic redundancy is limited, response actions prioritize downstream valve operations to intercept contaminant propagation along the primary flow direction. In looped systems, multiple isolation options may exist, allowing operators to compare alternative valve combinations and select configurations that minimize both contamination spread and service disruption. Despite these structural differences, the same POS-based analytical framework is consistently applied to both network types.

For each candidate POS intersecting the dominant propagation paths, response feasibility is evaluated by explicitly quantifying incident recognition time, field crew dispatch and travel time, and valve operation time. These temporal components are integrated into the Cause–Impact–Duration (CID) framework to evaluate how incident impact escalates as response is delayed.

Final response decisions are made by ranking candidate valve-operation strategies according to contaminant interception time, expected reduction in affected customers and demand, and operational feasibility under current field conditions. Through this structured decision process, the proposed protocol provides a transparent and reproducible framework for rapid and effective response to water quality incidents across diverse water distribution system configurations (Table 5).

Conclusion

This study emphasizes the importance of establishing real-time monitoring systems and an integrated operational framework for effectively managing water quality incidents in urban water distribution networks. By incorporating forensic analysis concepts, we assessed both the occurrence and response timelines of water quality incidents and developed strategies to minimize their impact. Four representative turbidity-related scenarios were selected based on conditions commonly observed in Korea, and hydraulic simulations using EPANET 2.2 were conducted to analyze incident occurrence points and their potential spread within the study area. Additionally, a Cause–Impact–Duration (CID) analysis was performed to quantify the severity of incidents in relation to elapsed time.

The CID plot proved to be an intuitive and valuable visualization tool for rapid decision-making. By illustrating the relationship between the cause of the incident, the magnitude of the impact, and the time available for intervention, CID plots enable operators to quickly evaluate incident severity and identify the optimal timing for response actions. This visual approach supports faster, more informed decisions during emergencies, which is critical for minimizing damage and optimizing resource allocation.

Our findings highlight the need for tailored incident response strategies that account for hydraulic characteristics of each network. When response actions are initiated before contaminated water travels to adjacent segments, the impact can be largely contained within the initially affected area. This underscores the importance of rapid detection through real-time monitoring and timely intervention through valve operations. Incidents stemming from reservoir turbidity or sections sensitive to flow-direction changes particularly require enhanced sensing capabilities and the application of remotely controlled or automated valves to ensure prompt isolation and protection of downstream consumers.

Within this context, advances in smart sensing technologies—such as remote metering, automated valve control, and digital-twin–based decision support—provide a practical pathway for operationalizing the proposed framework. In particular, the POS and CID framework can function as a core decision engine within a digital twin environment, where real-time sensor data continuously update elapsed time and contamination spread, and dynamically highlight optimal valve-isolation sequences on an operator’s dashboard. This integration directly links real-time system states with actionable response decisions, thereby strengthening operational resilience during emergencies.

Based on the findings, water utility managers can adopt several actionable strategies to enhance their preparedness and response capabilities. Strengthening real-time water quality monitoring and installing remotely controlled valves can significantly reduce the time required for incident detection and containment. Incorporating CID plots into emergency response protocols can support rapid assessment of incident severity and improve decision-making during critical periods. Additionally, pre-assessment of hydraulic impacts associated with valve closures can prevent unintended side effects and improve the reliability of emergency operations.

Nonetheless, this study has limitations. While the framework incorporates field-validated flow calibration and operational parameters, practical validation remains constrained because real-world water quality incidents cannot be deliberately induced for testing. As a result, residence-time estimation, detection timing, travel speed, and valve-operation parameters rely on simulation supplemented by interviews and measured data, which may not fully capture real-time variability during actual emergencies. In addition, this study primarily focused on optimizing valve operations to reduce contaminant spread, without explicitly evaluating potential hydraulic side effects such as pressure drops, backflow risks, or temporary service interruptions. Future research should verify the proposed framework using actual incident records, real-time sensor data, and pilot-scale field demonstrations, while also assessing its scalability across larger, more complex distribution networks. In particular, future research should investigate the sensitivity of valve operation time assumptions, as variations in this parameter directly influence POS feasibility, response time windows, and the effectiveness of operational isolation strategies.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the importance of real-time monitoring, advanced simulation tools, and strategic response frameworks in improving the resilience and sustainability of urban water supply systems. The combined use of smart sensing technologies, digital twins, and context-specific response strategies enhances the ability to manage emergencies effectively, protect public health, and maintain the stability of critical water infrastructure. These improvements are essential not only for ensuring safe drinking water but also for strengthening the resilience of cities under growing environmental and social pressures.
