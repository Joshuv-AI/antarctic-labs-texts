Outcomes of Kidney Transplants From Donors With Stage 3 Acute Kidney Injury Requiring Dialysis: A National Cohort Study
Authors: Amber B Paulus, Gaurav Gupta, Ahmet Gungor, Alice Toll, Alexandra Lewis, Carlos Martinez, Cassandra McCharen, Wida Cherikh, Bekir Tanriover
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundWhether severe donor acute kidney injury (AKI) requiring dialysis affects posttransplant outcomes remains debated and may influence center‐level acceptance decisions.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study of United States deceased‐donor kidney transplants (2015–2020) used OPTN data with follow‐up through September 2024. Donor AKI was staged by KDIGO 2012 criteria, and dialysis was identified using a validated rule‐based natural language processing (NLP) algorithm applied to DonorNet records. The primary outcome was all‐cause graft failure (ACGF). Secondary outcomes included delayed graft function (DGF), 1‐year estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and a composite tolerability outcome within 6 months defined as DGF, eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2, graft failure, or death. Models used inverse probability weighting (IPW), clustering by transplant center, and exploratory interpretable machine learning.ResultsOf 74 939 transplants, 24.6% were from AKI donors (Stage 2 7.8%, Stage 3 without dialysis 5.8%, Stage 3 with dialysis 2.1%). DGF increased with AKI severity (Stage 3 with dialysis 54.5% vs Stage 2 38.1%), but adjusted ACGF risk was similar across groups. Donor KDPI and recipient EPTS were stronger predictors of graft failure than AKI stage. Exploratory tolerability analyses showed that early adverse outcomes were driven primarily by recipient age, BMI, dialysis duration, and organ quality, not by donor AKI severity or dialysis.ConclusionsAmong transplanted kidneys, Stage 3 AKI requiring donor dialysis was associated with higher early DGF but similar adjusted graft survival compared with Stage 2 AKI after accounting for organ quality and recipient risk. These findings reflect outcomes among AKI‐affected kidneys accepted for transplantation and should not be generalized to declined or discarded kidneys.Keywords: acute kidney injury, deceased donor, delayed graft function, graft survival, inverse probability weighting, kidney donor profile index, kidney transplantation

Introduction

Kidney transplantation is the most effective treatment for end‐stage kidney disease, providing superior survival and quality of life compared with dialysis [1, 2, 3, 4]. However, the continued shortage of donor organs remains a major challenge [5, 6], prompting efforts to expand the deceased donor pool through the use of higher‐risk organs, including those from expanded criteria donors and donation after circulatory death (DCD) [7]. Among these, kidneys from donors with acute kidney injury (AKI) represent a controversial but potentially valuable source. AKI affects up to 30% of deceased donors and nearly half of DCD donors, often resulting from pre‐recovery factors such as hemodynamic instability or cardiac arrest [8, 9].

While donor AKI has been linked to higher rates of delayed graft function (DGF) and other early complications, growing evidence indicates that long‐term graft survival is often comparable to transplants from non‐AKI donors [10, 11]. Despite this, kidneys from donors with severe AKI, particularly those meeting KDIGO Stage 3 criteria, are frequently discarded due to concerns about primary non‐function and early graft loss [10, 11, 12, 13]. DGF, defined as the need for dialysis within the first week after transplant, is influenced by donor, perioperative, and recipient factors. Severe donor AKI, prolonged cold or warm ischemia time, and recipient factors such as older age, obesity, and long dialysis duration increase this risk [14, 15, 16, 17]. Understanding how these factors interact is essential to identifying which recipients can best tolerate kidneys from donors with significant injury.

This study evaluates the impact of moderate‐to‐severe donor AKI on short‐ and long‐term posttransplant outcomes in a large national cohort of deceased‐donor kidney transplants. Because OPTN recipient data capture transplanted organs, these analyses reflect outcomes among AKI‐affected kidneys that proceeded to transplantation and do not include kidneys that were declined or discarded. We also examine donor and recipient factors, including the need for dialysis at AKI Stage 3, that may influence graft survival to inform evidence‐based strategies for allocation and matching of AKI‐affected donor kidneys.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This retrospective cohort study included adult recipients (≥18 years) of solitary deceased donor kidney transplants performed between January 1, 2015 and December 31, 2020. Data were obtained from the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network (OPTN) database. The OPTN data system captures all U.S. organ donors, wait‐listed candidates, and transplant recipients, as submitted by OPTN member institutions. Oversight of the OPTN is provided by the Health Resources and Services Administration (HRSA), U.S. Department of Health and Human Services. Follow‐up data were available through September 30, 2024. The study was reviewed and determined to be exempt from institutional review board oversight as it involved secondary analysis of de‐identified data. The requirement for informed consent was waived.

Cohort and Exclusions

The analytic cohort was limited to adult recipients of solitary kidney transplants from deceased donors with moderate‐to‐severe AKI. This study was designed as a posttransplant outcomes analysis and therefore includes only kidneys that were transplanted; it does not evaluate center‐level offer acceptance behavior or outcomes among kidneys that were recovered but declined or discarded. Donors were excluded if they were younger than 16 years, weighed less than 40 kg, had only one serum creatinine measurement, or were part of a multi‐organ transplant.

Donor AKI Classification

Donor AKI was defined using the 2012 Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria based on changes in serum creatinine. Consistent with prior registry‐based studies, donors with a terminal creatinine >1.5 mg/dL were also classified as having AKI to capture clinically significant injury when baseline trajectories were uncertain. Donors with fewer than two recorded creatinine values were excluded. Urine output was not used for primary staging due to inconsistent reporting but was evaluated descriptively and in sensitivity analyses using the RIFLE classification (Risk, Injury, Failure, Loss, End‐stage kidney disease) to assess robustness of findings. Creatinine values used for staging were restricted to within seven days of organ recovery. Final donor categories were:

Stage 2 AKI: increase in serum creatinine ≥2.0 to 2.9 times baseline (reference group).

Stage 2 AKI: increase in serum creatinine ≥2.0 to 2.9 times baseline (reference group).

Stage 3 AKI without dialysis: increase ≥3.0 times baseline or terminal creatinine ≥4.0 mg/dL without dialysis.

Stage 3 AKI without dialysis: increase ≥3.0 times baseline or terminal creatinine ≥4.0 mg/dL without dialysis.

Stage 3 AKI with dialysis: Stage 3 AKI in donors who received dialysis before procurement, identified through natural language processing (NLP) as described below.

Stage 3 AKI with dialysis: Stage 3 AKI in donors who received dialysis before procurement, identified through natural language processing (NLP) as described below.

Ascertainment of Donor Dialysis Using NLP

Because donor dialysis is not a structured field in OPTN data, we developed a deterministic rule‐based NLP algorithm to extract dialysis information from free‐text DonorNet records. The algorithm required the co‐occurrence of a dialysis‐related term (e.g., “hemodialysis,” “CRRT,” “RRT,” “SLED,” “SLEDD”) with an active verb (“initiated,” “continued,” “performed”) within a 6‐word context window. Negations (e.g., “no dialysis,” “not on CRRT”) were excluded. Mentions clearly outside the donation timeline were filtered using temporal anchors referencing the admission‐to‐procurement window (±72 h). Validation was performed in a stratified random sample of 200 donors (100 NLP‐positive and 100 NLP‐negative) reviewed independently by two clinical adjudicators blinded to NLP results. Agreement between NLP classification and manual adjudication was assessed by percent agreement and Cohen's κ. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus, and overall accuracy exceeded 90%.

Urine Output Data Extraction

When reported, urine output was extracted from DonorNet in cubic centimeters per hour and converted to milliliters per kilogram per hour using donor admission weight. Three windows were analyzed: 6‐, 12‐, and 24‐h periods ending at the time of cross‐clamp or the closest documented interval within 6 h prior. When proximal data were unavailable, the most complete contiguous 6‐, 12‐, or 24‐h intervals within the preceding 48 h were used and flagged for sensitivity analysis.

Study Outcomes

The primary outcome was all‐cause graft failure (ACGF), defined as return to dialysis, re‐transplantation, or recipient death during follow‐up.

Secondary outcomes included:

DGF: need for dialysis within 7 days posttransplant.

DGF: need for dialysis within 7 days posttransplant.

Kidney function at 1 year: estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR, mL/min/1.73 m2).

Kidney function at 1 year: estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR, mL/min/1.73 m2).

Composite tolerability outcome: occurrence within 6 months of any of the following: DGF, eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2, graft failure, or death.

Composite tolerability outcome: occurrence within 6 months of any of the following: DGF, eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2, graft failure, or death.

Recipient “Tolerability” Analysis

To identify recipient characteristics that modify the effect of donor AKI on posttransplant outcomes, we conducted an exploratory logistic regression evaluating the composite tolerability outcome. Prespecified interactions between donor AKI stage and recipient factors were tested, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), preemptive listing, dialysis duration, functional status, and travel distance between the recipient residence and transplant center (ZIP‐to‐ZIP straight‐line distance). Additionally, interpretable machine learning models were tested to validate the logistic findings and assess complex interactions. These included:

Explainable Boosting Machines (EBM) to capture nonlinear effects and global importance measures.

Explainable Boosting Machines (EBM) to capture nonlinear effects and global importance measures.

Filter‐based variable selection using mutual information gain to retain variables with the strongest relationship to the outcome before modeling.

Filter‐based variable selection using mutual information gain to retain variables with the strongest relationship to the outcome before modeling.

Global and patient‐level interpretability metrics, including variable importance and partial dependence plots, were examined to visualize protective or adverse recipient profiles.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) and compared using Student's t‐test, one‐way ANOVA, or Kruskal–Wallis tests. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies (percentages) and compared using chi‐square tests. Survival probabilities were estimated using Kaplan–Meier methods and compared using the log‐rank test. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for ACGF across donor AKI groups. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using log‐log plots and Schoenfeld residuals.

To reduce confounding, inverse probability weighting (IPW) was applied to create a weighted pseudo‐population balanced across AKI exposure groups. Covariates in the weighting model included donor factors (age, KDPI, biopsy findings of glomerulosclerosis, arteriosclerosis, tubular atrophy, and interstitial fibrosis; DCD status; use of machine perfusion; donor hospital‐to‐transplant center distance; and cold ischemia time category) and recipient factors (age, diabetes, dialysis duration, re‐transplantation, and BMI). Weights were stabilized and truncated at the first and 99th percentiles to avoid extreme values. Covariate balance was confirmed by standardized mean differences (<0.1) and variance ratios (∼1.0). Three modeling approaches were compared:

Univariate (unadjusted) analysis,

Univariate (unadjusted) analysis,

IPW‐adjusted model, and

IPW‐adjusted model, and

Fully adjusted multivariable model incorporating additional immunologic variables (cPRA, HLA mismatch, CMV serostatus).

Fully adjusted multivariable model incorporating additional immunologic variables (cPRA, HLA mismatch, CMV serostatus).

All models were clustered by transplant center to account for center‐level variation. Interaction terms between AKI stage and key donor or recipient variables (KDPI category, cold ischemia time, DCD status) were included to test for effect modification. Missing covariate data were handled using indicator categories. Statistical significance was defined as two‐tailed p < 0.05. Analyses were performed using R version 4.1.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and Stata version 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

Results

Cohort Selection

Between 2015 and 2020, 74 939 deceased donor kidney‐only transplants were performed in the United States. Of these, 18.6% involved donors with KDIGO Stage 1 AKI, 7.8% Stage 2, and 7.9% Stage 3. The analytic cohort comprised 11 756 recipients whose donors had moderate to severe AKI (Stage 2 or Stage 3) as defined in Methods (Table S1). Because OPTN recipient registry data capture transplanted organs, these analyses reflect outcomes among AKI‐affected kidneys that proceeded to transplantation and do not include declined or discarded kidneys.

Kidney Donor Profile Index differed across groups. Stage 3 on dialysis had the lowest median KDPI at 33% (IQR 20–50) compared with 49% (IQR 30–69) for Stage 2 and 46% (IQR 29–66) for Stage 3 without dialysis (p < 0.001). High‐quality organs were more common in Stage 3 on dialysis (0%–0% KDPI in 24.2%) than Stage 2 (12.6%) and Stage 3 without dialysis (10.7%). Oliguria less than 400 mL per day was more frequent in Stage 3 on dialysis (25.7%) than Stage 2 (7.6%) and Stage 3 without dialysis (13.5%). Distribution of DCD and machine perfusion use was similar across AKI categories.

Donors with Stage 3 AKI requiring dialysis were younger (median 34 years [IQR 26–42]) and had lower KDPI (median 33% [IQR 20–50]) compared with Stage 2 and Stage 3 without dialysis (p < 0.001). Oliguria was more frequent among Stage 3 dialysis donors (25.7%), but donation type and machine perfusion use were similar across groups. Within this subgroup (Table 2), donors with functioning grafts were younger (33 vs. 36.5 years, p < 0.001), more often male (64.7% vs. 54.0%, p < 0.001), and had lower KDPI (32 vs. 40, p < 0.001). Recipients with functioning grafts were also younger (53 vs. 60 years, p < 0.001), had lower EPTS (44 vs. 66, p < 0.001), and were less likely to have diabetes (35.5% vs. 48.4%, p < 0.001). Other factors, including cold ischemia time, HLA mismatch, and biopsy findings, did not differ significantly between groups. These results suggest that younger age, lower KDPI, and favorable metabolic profiles in donors and recipients were associated with better graft outcomes among Stage 3 dialysis kidneys.

Recipient Tolerability Analysis

Exploratory analyses were performed to evaluate whether recipient characteristics modified the relationship between donor AKI severity and early posttransplant outcomes. Among 11 708 recipients with complete data, the multivariable logistic regression model for the composite tolerability outcome (DGF, eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2 at 6 months, graft failure, or death within 6 months) demonstrated that recipient factors, rather than donor AKI stage, were the primary drivers of early adverse events.

Older recipient age, higher BMI, and longer pre‐transplant dialysis duration were each associated with higher odds of the composite outcome (all p < 0.01). In contrast, donor AKI stage, including Stage 3 AKI with dialysis, was not independently associated with the composite outcome after adjustment for donor, recipient, and transplant factors. Donor urine output and dialysis status similarly demonstrated no significant association. Among continuous variables, KDPI, recipient BMI, cold ischemia time, and dialysis duration were the four strongest contributors to model fit in the ANOVA decomposition. The model discriminative performance was modest (c‐statistic 0.66).

Pre‐specified interaction terms (AKI × age, AKI × BMI, AKI × dialysis duration, AKI × preemptive listing, AKI × travel distance, and AKI × functional status) did not reveal significant effect modification. These findings indicate that recipient risk factors exert larger and more consistent influence on tolerability than donor AKI stage itself.

To validate these results and evaluate nonlinear patterns, multiple interpretable machine learning models were tested. After hyperparameter tuning, an XGBoost model performed best (cross‐validated c‐statistic 0.66). SHAP feature importance rankings closely mirrored logistic regression results, with KDPI, donor age, and recipient age emerging as the most influential variables. Donor AKI stage, including dialysis exposure, ranked low in importance across all models. No clinically meaningful donor AKI × recipient interactions emerged from SHAP dependence analyses. Taken together, these results suggest that early vulnerability to AKI‐affected kidneys is driven primarily by recipient frailty indicators (age, BMI, dialysis vintage) and overall organ quality (KDPI) rather than by AKI severity or donor dialysis itself.

Kaplan–Meier Survival

Unadjusted survival curves are shown in Figures S1–S3. For ACGF, the log rank p value was 0.01 with the best trajectory in Stage 3 on dialysis. Death censored graft failure curves overlapped (log rank p = 0.07). Patient survival favored Stage 3 on dialysis (log rank p = 0.003).

Discussion

This study shows that kidneys from donors with severe AKI, including KDIGO Stage 3 with dialysis, can achieve outcomes comparable to kidneys from donors with moderate AKI when organ quality and recipient risk are accounted for. After IPW and multivariable adjustment, donor AKI stage was not an independent predictor of ACGF, whereas Kidney Donor Profile Index and recipient estimated posttransplant survival carried greater prognostic weight. These findings support careful use of selected severely injured donor kidneys within evidence‐based matching strategies rather than categorical decline based solely on AKI stage.

Interpretation of these findings must be framed within the context of organ selection. Because this cohort includes only kidneys that proceeded to transplantation, our results reflect outcomes among AKI‐affected kidneys deemed acceptable for use by transplant centers rather than the broader population of donors with severe AKI. Donor age, KDPI, biopsy interpretation, hemodynamic trajectory, and center‐specific risk tolerance likely influenced which Stage 3 AKI kidneys were ultimately used. Accordingly, these findings should not be generalized to all AKI‐affected donor kidneys, including those declined or discarded. Rather, they demonstrate that among the selected subset of AKI kidneys deemed acceptable for transplantation, AKI severity and donor dialysis were not independent predictors of graft failure after accounting for organ quality and recipient risk. This reflects center‐level clinical judgment and survivorship of organs that successfully progressed to transplant.

In addition to donor‐level risk, our study incorporated a comprehensive assessment of recipient tolerability, informed by both logistic regression and interpretable machine learning methods. Across approaches, donor AKI stage, including Stage 3 with dialysis, did not significantly interact with key recipient factors such as age, BMI, dialysis duration, or preemptive listing status. Instead, early vulnerability was most strongly associated with recipient age, metabolic profile, and dialysis vintage, along with kidney quality as captured by KDPI. Machine learning models confirmed these findings, identifying KDPI, donor age, and recipient age as the primary drivers of risk, with donor AKI stage contributing minimally to outcome prediction. These results emphasize that the decision to transplant kidneys affected by severe AKI should be guided more by recipient fitness and organ quality than by AKI severity alone.

Our results align with contemporary reports that dialysis in the donor increases DGF without worsening adjusted long‐term outcomes [19, 20, 21, 22]. Although 1‐year mortality was numerically higher in the Stage 2 group, this rate aligns with expected national benchmarks for similar recipient risk profiles and appears driven by underlying recipient characteristics rather than donor AKI severity. Donors in the Stage 3 dialysis group were younger and had lower KDPI than those in other AKI categories, indicating that these organs were selectively accepted on the basis of favorable overall quality despite severe AKI. The favorable outcomes observed in this subgroup likely reflect this careful donor selection rather than an absence of biological risk associated with severe injury. These findings reinforce that organ quality metrics, particularly KDPI and donor age, remain central to posttransplant prognosis. We extend this literature by using a large, recent national cohort and by explicitly testing interactions that identify recipient profiles more likely to tolerate challenging kidneys. Younger recipients, preemptive or with shorter dialysis vintage, and those with lower BMI demonstrated attenuated early risk in exploratory analyses. This provides a practical allocation signal that can be operationalized alongside KDPI and EPTS.

Although this analysis focused on comparisons within moderate‐to‐severe AKI categories, prior national registry studies have demonstrated that transplanted kidneys from donors with AKI, including severe AKI, have long‐term graft survival comparable to kidneys without AKI after adjustment for KDPI and recipient factors [10, 11, 12, 13]. Our findings are consistent with this broader literature and further refine the evidence by suggesting that even within severe AKI, dialysis exposure does not independently worsen adjusted graft survival when organ quality and recipient risk are considered.

Methodologically, this study improves ascertainment of donor dialysis exposure by applying a transparent, rule‐based NLP approach with blinded manual validation. While this enhances exposure classification in registry data where dialysis is not a structured field, it does not eliminate uncertainty regarding the clinical heterogeneity of severe AKI. Differences in donor physiology, timing of injury, and response to dialysis remain incompletely captured. The higher proportion of DCD among dialysis donors in our cohort likely reflects increased DCD utilization during 2015–2020 and differences in ascertainment relative to earlier studies such as Wen et al.20 Together, these observations suggest that center experience and era effects influence which severely injured kidneys progress to transplant.

Several implications follow. First, allocation and acceptance should prioritize organ quality and recipient fitness over AKI stage alone. In practice, low‐KDPI Stage 3 kidneys, even with donor dialysis, appear suitable for lower‐risk recipients, particularly those with low EPTS. Second, programs should consider integrating structured dialysis and urine output fields into donor records to reduce information bias and facilitate consistent staging. Third, policy efforts aimed at improving consistency in acceptance practices could incorporate decision support that highlights favorable donor‐recipient pairings when kidneys present with severe AKI. Whether such strategies reduce discard requires analyses that include declined and discarded kidneys.

Limitations include the observational design, potential selection of transplanted organs, and incomplete granularity on donor clinical course. Despite targeted validation, dialysis exposure may be misclassified in some cases. Duration, modality (e.g., CRRT vs. intermittent hemodialysis), and cumulative intensity of donor dialysis were inconsistently documented in free‐text records and could not be reliably quantified. We therefore could not evaluate whether duration or modality of dialysis influenced posttransplant outcomes. Urine output was heterogeneous in timing and completeness, which is why it was used descriptively and in sensitivity analyses rather than for primary KDIGO staging. These factors introduce residual confounding and information bias. This study was intentionally designed as a posttransplant outcomes analysis and therefore includes only transplanted kidneys, introducing survivorship and selection bias. We could not evaluate outcomes among AKI‐affected kidneys that were declined or discarded. As such, the findings apply to the subset of AKI kidneys accepted for transplantation rather than to all severe AKI donors.

Future work should prospectively capture standardized donor dialysis and urine output data, adjudicate AKI etiology and trajectory, and test allocation tools that use KDPI, EPTS, and simple recipient features to recommend match quality. A testable hypothesis is that a strategy directing low‐KDPI Stage 3 kidneys to recipients with low EPTS, younger age, lower BMI, and shorter dialysis vintage will reduce discard without increasing early complications. Pragmatic trials or policy pilots could evaluate this approach. Finally, external validation in systems with older donor populations would clarify generalizability and help define whether similar tolerability profiles hold across different donor ages and case mixes [18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23].

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that among transplanted kidneys, those from donors with severe AKI, including those who required dialysis, can achieve favorable long‐term outcomes when organ quality is preserved and recipients are carefully selected. These findings inform ongoing debate regarding the use of kidneys affected by severe AKI and highlight their potential to expand the donor pool without evidence of increased adjusted graft failure among transplanted kidneys.

By refining donor and recipient matching, such as allocating low‐KDPI kidneys with severe AKI to younger or lower‐EPTS recipients, transplant programs may support more consistent acceptance of appropriately selected AKI kidneys. Whether such approaches reduce discard will require analyses that include declined and discarded kidneys. This supports a more individualized and evidence‐based approach to organ acceptance rather than a categorical exclusion of AKI donors.

Unanswered questions remain about the biological mechanisms that enable recovery of severely injured kidneys and whether targeted perioperative strategies, such as ischemia mitigation or tailored immunosuppression, could further improve results. Addressing these through future prospective studies, along with standardized reporting of donor AKI severity and dialysis exposure, will be essential. In the meantime, these findings support a thoughtful shift toward greater acceptance of kidneys from donors with severe AKI, guided by structured risk assessment and deliberate recipient pairing. Such an approach can expand the donor pool, reduce discard of viable kidneys, and ultimately improve access and equity in kidney transplantation.
