Antibacterial and wound healing activities of AgNPs, ZnONPs, and Fe3O4NPs against Acinetobacter baumannii and Escherichia coli
Authors: Mohammad Hossein Soleimani, Pegah Shakib, Mohammad Reza Zolfaghari, Ashkan Dirbaziyan, Reza Heidari, Mohsen Chamanara, Saeed Khoshnood, Faeze Fallahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate the individual and combined effects of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs), zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO-NPs), and iron oxide nanoparticles (Fe₃O₄-NPs) on wound healing in a rat model of infection with Acinetobacter baumannii and Escherichia coli, and to compare their efficacy with a standard antibiotic treatment.ResultsAgNPs demonstrated the strongest antibacterial growth inhibition in vitro, while ZnO-NPs showed limited antibacterial activity under the tested conditions. Combination assays indicated enhanced bacterial growth inhibition for AgNP + Fe₃O₄-NPs, whereas ZnO-NPs did not increase antibacterial inhibition in vitro. Nanoparticle-treated wounds showed faster healing than controls in rat models infected with E. coli and A. baumannii. Complete wound closure by day nine was observed in the Ag + ZnO and Fe₃O₄ groups for E. coli infection and in the Ag, ZnO, and Ag + ZnO groups for A. baumannii. Wound-healing rates reached 100% in the Fe₃O₄ and Ag + ZnO groups, which was significantly higher than in untreated controls. Histological evaluation revealed no necrosis in any group, while treated wounds exhibited increased granulation tissue formation and reduced inflammation. Overall, the Ag + ZnO nanoparticle combination showed a synergistic effect, leading to superior antibacterial activity and accelerated wound repair.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

It is imperative that wound healing be carried out rapidly and without side effects as infections have the potential to compromise patient quality of life and even increase mortality [1]. Multi-drug-resistant pathogens like A. Baumannii and E. Coli pose an increasingly challenging issue in modern medicine due to the complications they cause and resulting delay in treatment and healing [2–6]. While antimicrobial therapy, which can be in the form of agents such as chlorhexidine and the inclusion of metals (iron, zinc, silver) within the topical dressing, is the most common treatment strategy for infected wounds [7–10], nanoparticles have recently been examined for their broad antimicrobial activity and healing benefits [11, 12].

Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) are among the most extensively studied nanomaterials and are well known for their potent AgNPs are among the most explored nanomaterials and are well-known for their potent activity against MDR bacteria. In recent years ZnO NPs (ZnO NPs) and FeO NPs (FeO NPs) have also garnered significant attention because of their described antimicrobial activity and impact on cellular processes like oxidative stress response [13], fibroblast proliferation and angiogenesis. However, the use of nanoparticles in therapy has limitations such as variability in the therapeutic efficiency of nanoparticles and the concern over dose optimization and cytotoxicity [14–16]. It has been shown that AgNPs are effective against Gram negative bacteria like A. Baumannii and E. Coli in an in-vitro context. FeONPs, ZnO NPs and Ag NPs are of particular interest due to their broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity and their demonstrated low toxicity at relatively low doses [17].

Some research groups have reported a combined synergistic effect of various NPs; however, this is based mostly on in vitro studies, often looking at minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)-based effects and concluding efficacy based on improved bacterial growth inhibition. These results may not be translatable in an infected wound as healing involves inflammation, regeneration of tissues and interaction between the host and microbes [18]. The link between in vitro antibacterial activity of nanoparticles and in vivo wound healing is not clearly defined. The aim of this study was to examine the in vitro and in vivo (rat wound model using MDR E. Coli and A. Baumannii) effects of individual NPs and a combined formulation of AgNPs, ZnO NPs and FeO NPs, to correlate the antibacterial effect to the wound healing efficacy in a physiological condition [19, 20].

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

All procedures involving human-derived samples and animals were conducted in accordance with relevant ethical guidelines and regulations. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Lorestan University of Medical Sciences (Approval No. IR.LUMS.REC.1403.303). Written informed consent was obtained where applicable. Animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Clinical sample collection and processing

Sixty non-duplicate clinical samples (blood, respiratory secretions, and urine) from patients with suspected infections were collected and cultured on MacConkey and blood agar at 37 °C for 18–24 h. Presumptive E. coli and A. baumannii were identified by morphology, Gram staining, and biochemical tests, and confirmed by species-specific PCR.

Bacterial isolation and identification

Bacterial isolates were identified by colony morphology, Gram staining, and biochemical tests following the ASM Manual of Clinical Microbiology. E. coli was confirmed using IMViC tests, while A. baumannii was identified by oxidase test, growth at 44 °C, glucose oxidation, and non-motility. Species confirmation was performed by PCR targeting 16 S rRNA (E. coli) and blaOXA-51-like (A. baumannii), using ATCC reference strains as controls [21].

Antibiotic susceptibility testing

To select multi-resistant clinical strains the antibiotics susceptibility was performed. Strains were tested against 7 antibiotic classes including beta-lactams, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines, sulfonamides, macrolides and polymyxins. Antibiotics susceptibility were performed using the disk diffusion method (Kirby-Bauer) according to CLSI guidelines. Inhibition zone was read and interpreted following CLSI/EUCAST criteria. All multi-drug-resistant (MDR) strains selected for nanoparticle testing were resistant against all 7 antibiotic classes. ASB-2 and ESC-12 were internal lab codes assigned for the multi-resistant strains [18, 22]. Standard reference strains (E. coli ATCC 25922 and A. baumannii ATCC 19606) were used for PCR, culture validation and antibiotics susceptibility test. They were handled together with clinical isolates in this part.

Nanoparticles and preparation

Commercially silver, zinc oxide and iron oxide nanoparticles (AgNPs, ZnO-NPs and FeO-NPs, respectively) were purchased from MSE PRO (MSE Supplies LLC, USA). ZnO-NPs have an average size of 30, 50–90 nm and > 99.9% purity according to the specification provided by supplier. FeO-NPs have a size range of 50–100 nm and purity ~ 99.5%. The specifications of the morphology and the zeta potential of the nanoparticles were not sufficiently given from the datasheets of the suppliers, so we didn’t characterize them experimentally in this work. Stock suspensions were prepared in sterile distilled water. These stock solutions were sonicated for 15 min (40 kHz) prior to use, to enhance their dispersions. No dispersing agents were used to prevent possible interference with biological systems and the experiments were performed with fresh suspensions at each time.

Antibacterial growth inhibition assay

The antibacterial activity was determined using a liquid growth inhibition assay in MHB. The concentration of the bacterial cultures was adjusted to 0.5McFarland standard and 1:100 diluted to approximate 5 10 CFU/mL, as described in CLSI recommendations. Nanoparticles were evaluated at several concentrations for initial antibacterial screening. Optical density was measured at 600 nm to determine bacteria growth and visually observed at 24 h following incubation at 37 C. Positive control (bacteria in MHB) and negative control (sterile MHB) were included in each experiment [23, 24]. The MICs were studied at three concentrations of 0.064, 10 and 20 mg/ml.

The interaction between nanoparticle formulations was investigated using checkerboard microdilution. Fractional inhibitory concentration index (FICI) was calculated as follows:

FICI = FICAAA + FICBBB, where FIC is the MIC of an agent in combination/MIC of an agent alone. Interaction is considered as synergistic if FICI 0.5, indifferent if 0.5 < FICI 4.0 and antagonism if FICI > 4.0.

Cytotoxicity assay (MTT)

Cytotoxicity effects of the nanoparticles were evaluated using MTT assay (Sigma; CT 02) against mouse fibroblast cell lines L929 (ATCC # CCL-1). Cells were cultured in DMEM (Sigma; cat# D6434) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco; cat# A5670401) at 37 C with 5% CO2 and humified condition. Cells were seeded at density of 5 10 cells/well in a 96-well plate for 24 h to form a confluent monolayer and nanoparticles were then added and incubated for 24 h. MTT solutions (0.5 mg/mL, 10 L/well) were added and incubated for 4 h. The formazan crystals formed were dissolved in DMSO and absorbance measured at 570 nm [25]. The cell viability (%) was calculated as:

Animal model of infected wound healing

Animals and housing

Fifty-one male Wistar rats weighing between 200 and 250 g were purchased from Lorestan University, Animal husbandry. All animals were housed under standard conditions with a 12 h light/dark cycle and 22 ± 2 °C and had ad libitum access to food and water. Three rats were excluded because of stress-related conditions after housing and therefore 48 rats remained for further analysis [26].

Wound creation and infection

All procedures were performed under deep anesthesia induced by ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) administrations. The standard full-thickness excisional wound model was employed. Briefly, animals were anesthetized and the dorsal area was shaved and sterilized. A circular 8 mm full-thickness skin punch biopsy was applied in such way that it penetrated through the skin down to the panniculus carnosus. Right after wounding, the defect site was inoculated with 10 CFU of the clinical strains E. coli or A. baumannii suspended in 50 L PBS.

Experimental groups and treatment

Animals were randomly allocated into sixteen experimental groups (n = 3 per group), including eight groups infected with E. coli and eight groups infected with A. baumannii. For each bacterial strain, the treatment groups included ZnO-NPs, Fe₃O₄-NPs, AgNPs, ZnO-NPs + Fe₃O₄-NPs, ZnO-NPs + AgNPs, Fe₃O₄-NPs + AgNPs, ZnO-NPs + Fe₃O₄-NPs + AgNPs, and an infected untreated control receiving vehicle alone. Nanoparticle formulations were applied topically once daily directly onto the wound surface, freshly dispersed in a sterile aqueous vehicle (PBS and/or gel base), with ZnO-NPs at 3.125 µg/mL, Fe₃O₄-NPs at 20 µg/mL, AgNPs at 0.781 µg/mL, and combination treatments containing the same concentrations of each constituent nanoparticle. Treatments were continued for nine consecutive days, and wounds were left uncovered to heal by secondary intention.

Wound healing evaluation

Wound healing was assessed using a combined quantitative and qualitative approach. Quantitatively, macroscopic observations were made daily by tracing wound edges on measured images and calculating the wound area. Histologically, stains were analyzed to assess levels of inflammation, granulation tissue formation, re-epithelization, and necrosis. Finally, bacterial activity was measured by counting colony forming units in wound tissue homogenates. Data were utilized to quantify the rate of wound healing, wound quality, and antibacterial impact. The area of wounds was determined by tracing the borders on daily images from a digital camera and using the ImageJ software for analysis. The rate of wound healing in percentage was calculated as:

Histopathological analysis

On day 7 post-wounding, rats were humanely euthanized using a CO₂/O₂ inhalation chamber in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines. Wound tissues were carefully excised from the lesion site, immediately fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin (HT501128; Sigma-Aldrich) processed routinely, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. Tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and examined under a light microscope (Olympus BX51, Japan). Histopathological evaluation focused on inflammatory cell infiltration, granulation tissue formation, and re-epithelialization [27].

Statistical analysis

All values are represented as mean ± SD. Normal distribution was checked by the Shapiro- Wilk test. Statistical significance between three groups was determined by one-way ANOVA post hoc Tukey’s test, or by Kruskal-Wallis test post hoc Dunn’s test when data were not normally distributed. Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for non-normal data were used when comparing two groups. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v10, and significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Molecular confirmation of bacterial isolates

Presumptive E. coli and A. baumannii isolates obtained by culture and biochemical testing were subjected to molecular confirmation. PCR amplification of the 16 S rRNA gene for E. coli and the blaOXA-51-like gene for A. baumannii produced the expected amplicons in all isolates included in subsequent analyses. Isolates that failed to yield PCR products during initial amplification were excluded from further experiments due to insufficient DNA quality or amplification failure. Only PCR-confirmed isolates were used in antimicrobial and in vivo wound-healing assays.

Bacteria isolation and antimicrobial resistance pattern

The PCR results confirmed the identification with 16 S rRNA in > 90% and OXA-51 in > 80% of the isolated E. coli and A. Baumannii, respectively (Fig. 1). Bacteria strains showed resistance to different ranges of antibiotics. Bacteria strains revealed extremely resistant to the antibiotics as follows: A. baumannii was 100% resistant to streptomycin and nalidixic acid, 90% resistant to tetracycline, 85% resistant to ampicillin whereas E. coli had lesser resistant as follows: 30% resistant to cefotaxime/ceftazidime, 28% resistant to ciprofloxacin/ceftriaxone and 23% resistant to imipenem. The presence of MDR strain was evident (resistant to 7 antibiotics), which includes strain ASB-2 and ESC-12 (see Tables 1, 2).

In vitro antibacterial activity and combination effect

The antibacterial effects of each nanoparticle alone and in combination were tested in E. coli and A. baumannii using standard broth microdilution method for MIC testing and checkerboard assay for combination effects testing. The FICI was calculated to represent synergistic (FICI < = 0.5), indifferent (0.5 < FICI < = 4) or antagonistic (FICI > 4) effects. All combinations involving ZnO were classed as indifferent. Combinations involving ZnO such as Ag + ZnO revealed an indifferent effect while Ag + FeO shows synergistic effect according to FICI.

Therapeutic effect of NP on bacteria wound infection

The results showed enhanced wound closure (Figs. 3, 4 and 5) with all NP treated groups. On day 9 of the treatment the E. coli experiment showed full closure on both FeO and Ag + ZnO treatments (P = 0.03), whereas on A. baumannii the healing was seen on Ag, ZnO and Ag + ZnO group (P = 0.014). The histological examination of wounds indicated lower level of inflammation and the increase of granulation tissue and decrease of necrotic tissue (Fig. 4). The bacteria count was significantly low in treated groups compared to the control group (P < 0.05) and among treated groups, Ag + FeO group revealed minimum bacteria counts (Fig. 5).

Discussion

It was noted in this investigation that the antibacterial activities of AgNPs were significantly high against E. coli and A. baumannii, whereas those of ZnO-NPs and Fe3O4 NPs alone were lower. Nonetheless, a reduction in MIC and a high performance in co-formulation with ZnO and Fe3O4 NPs resulted from synergism between the combined NPs. In accordance with the work of Luengo et al. And Cruzet al., it was confirmed that bimetallic nanocomposites boost the antibacterial activity [28, 29].The synergism observed for the NP combination at sub-MIC levels may be caused by many different synergistic mechanisms such as high production of ROS, high penetration ability of the NPs to bacteria membrane, the release of multiple ions (Ag, Zn, Fe/Fe) from individual NPs, and higher bacterial surface interaction, but is a synergistic activity not an individual activity [30, 31].

Mechanistically, ion releasing from Ag Nps kills bacteria by induction of oxidative stress and damage to bacterial DNA, Fe3O4 NPs disrupt the bacterial membrane structure and ZnONPs enhance ROS generation. As a result, combined use of Ag and Fe3O4 Nps or ZnO Nps activates multi-targets for the antibacterial performance [32]. This outcome correlates with the finding of Jafari et al. In which they found a synergism between Ag/ZnO nanocomposite caused by combining the antimicrobial property of AgNPs with the photocatalytic ability of ZnO [33]. Regarding toxicological results, it was observed that among all of NPs AgNps, which shows superior antibacterial effect, exhibited most potent time- and dose-dependent cytotoxicity; like the report by Shelembe et al. [34]. In contrast, ZnO-NPs showed medium to high cytotoxicity depending on synthesis conditions and Fe3O4 NPs showed low toxicity or induced cell proliferation. This relationship between potent antibacterial activity and biocompatibility demonstrates the necessity of proper selection of NPs type and component combination for use in clinical settings. Microscopic examination assessed four important indices: inflammation, granulation, necrosis, and scar tissue formation. It was seen that there was no necrosis in any groups; indicating safety and good integration of treatments with natural tissue repair. Most of the groups had significantly higher granulation tissue formation than the control group and it was evident that this effect was particularly high in the case of the Fe3O4 NPs and Ag + ZnO NPs, proving the activation of the fibroplasia phase and stimulation of ECM synthesis necessary for tissue repair. The inflammation levels decreased in the majority of the treatment groups which might be due to the anti-inflammatory characteristics of NPs; similar to previous studies which reported anti-inflammatory and regenerative features of NPs compounds [33, 35].

Overall, the treatment using NP-based formulation had beneficial impacts on the infected wound healing in the E. coli and A. baumannii wound models. Particularly, the group treated with Ag + ZnO formulation achieved best performance in terms of wound closure, granulation and inflammation. Meanwhile, Fe3O4 formulation also promoted healing, especially when combined with Ag, and ZnO formulation performed fairly at single state, whereas significantly enhanced by combining with Ag or Fe3O4. As a result, combining two different types of NPs gave better antibacterial effect and wound healing performance against MDR infections. In vitro synergy of Ag + ZnO was not identified with the FICI, however, in vivo wound healing was significantly improved; showing that MIC based study is a limit for determining in-vivo therapeutic effects. Better wound healing may attribute to the synergism combined antibacterial efficacy of Ag NPs and anti-inflammatory, pro-angiogenic activity of ZnO Nps, demonstrating in vivo functional synergy at wound site. The Ag/ZnO nanocomposite exhibits stronger activity against Gram negative bacteria, thus, overall antimicrobial activity is higher than other nanocomposites or single NPs. In general, Ag, ZnO Nps, particularly the combined Ag + ZnO Nps exhibited an outstanding therapeutic effect against wound infection healing by dual mechanism of antibacterial activity and reduction in inflammation as well as enhance tissue formation.

Limitations

This study used a single-species wound model (E. coli or A. baumannii), which does not reflect the complexity of clinical multi-microbial infections and limits their transmissibility. Although single-species cytotoxicity was assessed in L929 cells and no adverse effects were observed in vivo, combined toxicity was not assessed. Future studies could address these limitations by using multi-microbial models.

Conclusion

The results showed that Ag/ZnO and Ag/Fe₃O₄ nanocomposites accelerated the healing of infected wounds by reducing inflammation and increasing tissue formation without causing necrosis. These nanocomposites also showed strong antibacterial activity against E. coli and A. baumannii with reduced MIC and were safe at the tested doses. Overall, these nanocomposites are promising candidates for the treatment of antibiotic-resistant wound infections.
