Scalable and stretchable 1D multifunctional fibers for multimodal sensing and stimulation
Authors: Junyi Yin, Jinjin Zhu, Shaolei Wang, Jiangnan Yuan, Chenyang Li, Samuel Margolis, Hong Bao, Yanan Wang, Wei Zhao, Enbo Zhu, Longlong Mu, Shuai He, Kaidong Wang, Jie Zhao, YongAn Huang, Yunlei Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
One-dimensional (1D) multifunctional fibers have garnered significant attention due to their advantageous geometry properties, which allows conformal interfacing with soft biological tissues and efficient charge transport. Here, we developed a solution-deposition strategy for the scalable and cost-effective fabrication of stretchable liquid metal fibers integrated with electrochemically stable, tissue-interfacing electrodes, thereby enabling the realization of stretchable multifunctional fibers. This fiber seamlessly combines electrodes and conductive pathways into a single structure, enabling versatile applications such as electrophysiological signal sensing, in vivo nerve stimulation, and wireless energy transmission. The multifunctional fiber demonstrates significantly improved electrical performance under strain, maintaining conductivity during stretching and bending, and exhibits lower impedance and higher signal stability, particularly during physiological monitoring and electrical stimulation. The fiber’s excellent biocompatibility and mechanical compliance makes it well suited for wearable systems and long-term biomedical applications, offering a robust platform for next generation 1D bioelectronics.

Introduction

Stretchable one-dimensional (1D) multifunctional fibers exhibit superior flexibility, isotropic deformability, breathability, and lightweight characteristics, which are promising for emerging areas such as wearable systems, smart sutures, and minimally invasive medical devices1–6. Although textile electronics have achieved considerable success in wearable applications, single fibers offer superior characteristics for particular applications7–16. Their inherent flexibility, stable conductivity, sensing capabilities, and reliable signal transmission in dynamic environments make them exceptionally well-suited to integrate biological tissues as functional platforms17–19. Electrical conductivity is a fundamental requirement for multifunctional fibers, and a widely adopted strategy for developing stretchable 1D conductors involves hybridizing or coating methodologies that synergistically integrate elastic polymeric substrates with conductive nanomaterials20–24. The electrical contact between conductive fillers is pivotal in determining conductivity, regardless of the material selected. However, during stretching, the mechanical property mismatch between the conductive material and the matrix, coupled with the separation of fillers, leads to a marked deterioration in conductivity25,26. Moreover, stretchable 1D conductors must exhibit dynamic conductivity and ensure stable electrochemical performance to facilitate prolonged and reliable dynamic monitoring within the in vivo environment27. It remains challenging to fabricate stretchable fibers that meet the demands of conductivity, scalability, and stable electrochemical performance.

In recent years, liquid metals have been recognized as a promising partial solution to this challenge. They have distinctive properties, including a low gauge factor, exceptional deformability with zero modulus, and high electrical conductivity28–31. However, liquid metals exhibit inherent limitations that must be addressed to fully harness their potential for developing stretchable 1D multifunctional fibers. Firstly, liquid metals possess high surface tension, which impedes their direct adhesion to elastic polymers32,33. To address this issue, a commonly employed method involves incorporating a significant amount of gallium oxide into the liquid metal to reduce surface tension34,35. Nevertheless, such approach provides limited control the microscopic interfacial contact between the polymer and the liquid metal, making it difficult to achieve a uniform distribution. Another approach utilizes the direct fabrication of liquid metal fibers through injection and thermal drawing techniques, which are intrinsically constrained by the size of the fiber dimensions, typically yielding a minimum diameter of 250 µm36–38. Furthermore, these liquid metal fibers cannot directly interface with biological tissues or fluids; instead, they can only indirectly sense biomechanical or thermal signals through deformation or temperature changes transmitted via surrounding elastomer layers. The surrounding biofluid contains water and anions that rapidly react with gallium, thereby inhibiting stable and reliable operation for long-term in vivo applications29,39. Although various solution-based methods for preparing liquid metals have been reported, the large-scale fabrication of a single liquid metal fiber remains unexplored40–43. The main challenge arises from the structural differences between films and fibers: large-area films possess abundant reactive interfaces that readily interact with liquid metals, whereas single fibers have a much smaller surface area and thus require higher-quality electrochemical coatings to achieve uniform and stable encapsulation. This constraint significantly limits their performance in implantable electrophysiological signal detection and electrical stimulation of biological tissues. Collectively, These practical challenges pose major barriers to liquid metal-based 1D bioelectronics.

In this study, we developed a scalable fabrication strategy for stretchable, multifunctional 1D bioelectronics fibers. We first established single liquid-metal fibers to enable efficient signal transmission and subsequently constructed PEDOT:PSS inert electrodes on selected regions of the fibers, thereby enabling both sensing and electrical stimulation capabilities (Fig. 1). Furthermore, by twisting and assembling multiple encapsulated liquid-metal electrodes, a multichannel sensing system can be realized (Fig. 1b). Specifically, a polydopamine (PDA) layer was formed via the self-polymerization of dopamine solution, providing a universal surface functionalization that facilitates electroless deposition of a copper (Cu) layer onto the fibers (Fig. 1c). The Cu layer subsequently serves as a stabilizing interface through reactive wetting with the liquid metal (Fig. 1d). Since this method relies on liquid-phase chemical reactions to stabilize the adhesion of liquid metal on the fiber surface, it overcomes the size constraints of conventional fabrication approaches (e.g., infusion and thermal extrusion), achieving a fiber diameter as small as 50–100 µm. By segmentally encapsulating the liquid-metal fibers with materials of distinct functionalities, such as insulating SEBS elastomers or stretchable, chemically stable conductive carbon nanocomposites, this process yields mechanically robust and electrochemically stable microelectrodessuitable for long-term applications (Fig. 1e–g). The engineered stretchable 1D multifunctional fibers exhibit excellent electrical conductivity and maintain stable performance under mechanical deformation, as demonstrated by their ability to continuously power commercial light emitting diodes (LEDs) during stretching (Fig. 1h, i). We further introduced an inert CNT/PEDOT:PSS electrode layer, allowing the exposed regions to directly interface with biofluids and perform stable sensing functionality. This bilayer electrode aechitecture, composed of liquid metal and inert functional layer, integrates high conductivity with reliable sensing performance under complex biological conditions (Fig. 1j). Applied to multimodal electrophysiological monitoring, these stretchable bioelectronic fibers effectively suppress motion artifacts during dynamic activities. When combined with machine learning algorithms, the platform achieved an action-recognition of 99.5%. Additionally, leveraging the fibers’ high electrochemical stability and biocompatibility, we performed precise electrical stimulation of the sciatic nerve in animal models, successfully modulating muscle movements. Results

Fabrication and characterization of stretchable multifunctional fiber

The fabrication of the stretchable multifunctional fiber begins with the formation of a stretchable conductive layer. To produce continuous one-dimensional liquid metal layer, we employed a chemical solution deposition process based on the alloying reaction between gallium-based liquid metal and copper, as shown in Fig. 1c. In contrast to conventional methods (Fig. 1a), which typically require the repetitive application of oxidized liquid metal to ensure adhesion to the fiber, this solution-based manufacturing process enables continuous, scalable production while ensuring a more uniform distribution of liquid metal across the fiber surface. The fabrication begins with an ultrathin polyurethane (PU) fiber core, which provides intrinsic mechanical stretchability to the electronic system (Fig. S9). A PDA adhesive layer is applied to the fiber surface, creating abundant and uniformly distributed active sites for silver nanoparticle (AgNP) deposition. Successive copper layers are subsequently deposited onto the AgNP-coated surface, and all these steps are performed using low-cost, scalable solution-based methods. In the final step, liquid metal is introduced into dilute hydrochloric acid, selectively alloying with the copper to form a continuous, high-conductivity pathway along the fiber. As schematically illustrated in Fig. 2a, the stretchable, high-integration, multifunctional conductive fiber fabrication relies on a rapid alloying reaction between the Cu coating and the liquid metal. This alloying process ensures robust adhesion and efficient electronic transport pathways across the fiber structure (Fig. 2b). To evaluate the wetting behavior critical for the alloying process, the contact angle of a liquid metal droplet was compared on two substrates: the untreated PU substrate and the chemically deposited Cu substrate (Fig. 2c). The contact angle on the PU surface is relatively high at 119.7°, indicating poor wettability, whereas the Cu-coated surface significantly reduces the contact angle to 36.5°, facilitating improved wetting and interaction with liquid metal. The uniformity and compositional integrity of the electronic fiber at various stages of the layer-by-layer fabrication process were further validated using SEM imaging and elemental mapping (Fig. 2d). The elemental distribution images clearly illustrate the growth of AgNPs on the PDA-coated fiber, the subsequent Cu surface modification on the Ag fiber, and the uniform spatial distribution of In after the final alloying reaction between Cu and liquid metal. The multilayer fabrication process resulted in only a minimal increase in the overall fiber diameter, essentially adding a thin surface layer of approximately 2–3 µm (Fig. S2). Moreover, the corresponding stress-strain behavior was not significantly affected (Fig. S6). In contrast, the electrical conductivity changed markedly at each fabrication stage, as shown in Fig. S7. The initial Ag layer failed to form a stable conductive network, resulting in negligible conductivity. The subsequent Cu coating achieved a low conductivity of 0.1133 S cm−¹. Finally, after reaction with the liquid metal, the conductivity dramatically increased to 39,623 S cm−¹. The thickness and deposition amount of the internal layers (PDA, AgNW, and Cu) were found to be highly dependent on the deposition time. As summarized in Fig. S8, the optimized parameters are as follows: a 14-h PDA deposition formed a uniform and continuous adhesive layer, providing an ideal foundation for subsequent metallization. A 3-h AgNP deposition achieved sufficient surface coverage to act as an effective conductive activation layer, while a 30-min copper electroplating produced a continuous and dense metallic film that served as an excellent conductive substrate for the subsequent Galinstan coating. Collectively, these optimized parameters ensure ideal, full-surface coverage of the final liquid metal layer. To uncover the chemical composition and surface morphology, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was performed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of the solution-processed liquid metal patterning method (Fig. 2e). After treatment in silver nitrate solution, the XPS spectrum (TPU/PDA/Ag) exhibits peaks at 374 eV (Ag 3d3/2) and 368 eV (Ag 3d5/2), verifying the formation of Ag seed nanoparticles27. These nanoparticles, which are sparsely distributed on the PDA film, have sizes in the tens of nanometers. The AgNP seeds catalyze the subsequent chemical deposition of the Cu film, as evidenced by the appearance of Cu 2p 1/2 (952.6 eV) and Cu 2p 3/2 (932.8 eV) peaks in the XPS spectrum (TPU/PDA/Ag/Cu)44. The structural evolution of the fiber was further analyzed through X-ray diffraction (XRD), as shown in Fig. 2f. The XRD spectrum of the chemically deposited Cu layer exhibits two broad peaks at 43.3° and 50.5°, corresponding to fine-grained crystalline Cu. Following the deposition of the liquid metal, the XRD spectrum reveals a series of sharp crystalline peaks, attributed to the formation of CuGa and CuGa2 intermetallic compounds45,46. The alloying reaction occurring at the Cu-liquid metal interface is a key driving force that significantly enhances the wettability of the non-oxidized liquid metal47. This improved wettability is crucial for achieving a uniform and continuous conductive pathway. The Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) spectrum acquired from the selected area clearly shows the characteristic peaks of Ag, Ga, In, and Sn. Ga is identified as the dominant phase, with the significant presence of Cu being consistent with the formation of a Cu-Ga intermetallic alloy (Fig. S4).

The uniformity and quality of the liquid metal coating are crucial for ensuring the reliable performance of fiber electrodes. This deposition process is influenced by several key processing parameters. To establish a robust and reproducible process window, we systematically investigated the effects of drawing speed, fiber modulus, ambient temperature, and the volume of Galinstan droplets on the final coating morphology. In Fig. S3, at speeds approaching 20 cm/s, the reaction between the liquid metal and copper becomes kinetically limited, resulting in non-uniform coverage and a defective interface. This finding allows us to define a precise operating window for high-speed coating. In contrast, the Young’s modulus of the elastic fiber substrate, ambient temperature, and the volume of liquid metal exhibited unexpectedly minimal influence within the tested ranges, indicating broad material compatibility of our approach. Furthermore, cross-sectional SEM imaging together with elemental analysis demonstrates the robust adhesion of liquid metal to the TPU fibers (Fig. S5). A complementary video (Supplementary Video 1) was recorded to validate the superior durability and adhesion of our conductive fiber under dynamic mechanical stress. The footage shows the sensor undergoing repeated stretching and bending cycles. Notably, the metallic coating retained its structural integrity throughout, with no evidence of cracking, delamination, or interfacial detachment. The high throughput of the continuous melt-spinning and all-solution processes enabled the successful production of 50-meter-long TPU fibers and corresponding liquid metal-coated fibers, demonstrating their potential for scalable manufacturing (Fig. S10).

The encapsulation layer is critical in the multifunctional fiber’s water resistance and stable electrical performance. As shown in Fig. 2g, the elastic polymer encapsulation can protect the liquid metal fiber while maintaining efficient electron transport through the conductive pathway. We use the feasible drop-casting process to encapsulate the liquid metal fiber. Figure 2h illustrates the encapsulation process, where polymer droplets are uniformly coated onto the fiber surface to seal the liquid metal core. This encapsulation enhances the durability of the fiber by protecting it from oxidation, corrosion, or current leakage when contacting bio-tissues. Stress-strain measurements were conducted to evaluate the mechanical performance of the encapsulated liquid metal fiber, as shown in Fig. 2i. The fiber demonstrates an impressive stretchability of up to 250%, withstanding significant tensile strain without mechanical failure. Electrical performance under mechanical deformation was further assessed by monitoring the normalized resistance as a function of uniaxial tensile strain (Fig. 2j). The resistance increases by two at 100% strain and by a factor of 9 at 200% strain. These results indicate that the liquid metal fiber maintains low resistance and stable conductivity under high tensile strain, demonstrating its potential for dynamic applications requiring flexibility and durability. The chemical stability of Gallium-based liquid metal is known to be unsatisfactory, especially under physiological conditions. As shown in the inset of Fig. 2k, significant chemical corrosion of the liquid metal appeared after a few hours of immersion in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution. Correspondingly, the resistance increases dramatically by over 100 times within 4.5 h.

In contrast, when coated with a 25 μm thick SEBS (styrene-ethylene-butylene-styrene) elastomer layer, the resistance of the liquid metal fiber remains nearly unchanged after prolonged storage in PBS solution, further confirming its ability to maintain stable electrical performance. The thin elastomer encapsulation layer is therefore indispensable for ensuring the long-term stability of liquid metal features in practical applications. By controlling the movement of the polymer solution droplet along the liquid metal fiber, the liquid metal fiber can be selectively encapsulated with a specific section exposed (Supplementary Video 2). As shown in Fig. 2l, we conducted the continuous resistance measurements along the fiber to validate the selectively encapsulated liquid metal fiber. The encapsulated fiber exhibits a consistent voltage distribution throughout its length, indicating uniform conductivity and reliable performance. This programmable encapsulation further enhances the multifunctionality of the conductive fiber, making it suitable for a wide range of applications requiring precise electronic control.

Building upon the successful fabrication of the liquid metal layer, the stretchable multifunctional fiber is further functionalized with an electrochemically inert electrode layer that is fully compatible with physiological environments (Fig. 3a). Stretchable nanocomposite materials represent an attractive platform for designing electrode with tailored properties. Multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) were selected as conductive fillers due to their high electrical conductivity and excellent electrochemical stability. By dispersing MWCNTs into a SEBS (styrene-ethylene-butylene-styrene) solution to form a viscous ink, patterned conductive nanocomposites could be feasibly deposited via a mask-spray coating process. To further reduce impedance and enable high-fidelity interfacing between the electrode and biological tissues, a layer of poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene)-poly(styrenesulfonate) (PEDOT: PSS) was electrochemically deposited onto the MWCNT electrodes (Fig. 3b). Surface morphology and elemental composition of the PEDOT: PSS-coated fibers were analyzed using SEM and elemental mapping (Fig. 3c). The SEM images revealed a uniform PEDOT: PSS deposition on the nanocomposite electrode, and the inset confirms the presence and homogeneous distribution of sulfur, indicating the formation of the PEDOT: PSS layer. Volumetric capacitance and cathodic charge storage capacity, as key indicators of the PEDOT:PSS coating, were evaluated for electrodes subjected to varying numbers of deposition cycles (Figs. S11 and S12). Their electrochemical behavior was assessed in PBS solution, revealing a clear saturation effect: the electrochemical capacity plateaued once the number of PEDOT:PSS deposition cycles reached 30. Considering this saturation behavior alongside a comprehensive performance evaluation, 30 electrochemical deposition cycles were selected as the optimal condition. This uniform coating enhances high-fidelity signal recording while maintaining mechanical flexibility. The electrochemical stability of the conductive nanocomposites was further evaluated through linear sweep voltammetry (Fig. 3d). The results demonstrate a broad electrochemical stability window spanning from −1.85 to 2.15 V, confirming the reliability of the CNT/PEDOT: PSS electrode under physiological conditions. Conversely, when subjected to applied positive voltage, the liquid metal fiber exhibits performance degradation. This degradation process can also release high concentrations of gallium and indium ions, which pose significant cytotoxicity risks. The cyclic voltammetry (CV) performance of the fiber electrodes is shown in Fig. 3e. The pristine nanocomposite electrodes exhibit minimal current response over the entire voltage range during the 1st and 40th cycles, indicating poor electrochemical activity and limited charge storage capacity. In contrast, the PEDOT: PSS-coated electrodes show significantly larger and more stable current responses across multiple cycles, highlighting the enhanced charge transport properties and electrochemical activity introduced by the PEDOT: PSS layer. Furthermore, the consistent CV curves over repeated cycles demonstrate excellent cycling stability, making the modified electrodes ideal for applications requiring reliable and efficient charge transfer, such as bioelectronic sensing and stimulation. The mechanical performance of the fiber electrodes under uniaxial tensile strain was evaluated by monitoring the normalized resistance as a function of strain (Fig. 3f). The normalized resistance increases to 7.2 at 50% strain and 16.9 at 100% strain. This excellent stretchability is attributed to the electrodes forming a structurally continuous and intrinsically deformable conductive network within the SEBS elastomer. Such mechanical stability ensures reliable electrical performance in dynamic applications requiring repeated deformations.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was used to evaluate the electrical performance of the fabricated fiber electrodes. As shown in Fig. 3g, the bare nanocomposite electrode impedance magnitude is relatively high, which can be attributed to the limited active surface area of the exposed MWCNTs. This high impedance poses a significant challenge for high-fidelity recordings due to signal attenuation and distortion. Electrochemically depositing a PEDOT: PSS layer onto the fiber electrodes reduced the impedance by two orders of magnitude. The phase angle analysis further reveals a phase angle approaching 0°, indicating a dominant resistive component suitable for electrophysiological recording and stimulation. The impedance stability of the fiber electrodes was assessed under mechanical strain (Fig. 3h). The PEDOT: PSS layer exhibited relatively stable interfacial impedance during stretching, which can be attributed to the volumetric charge storage mechanism36. The fiber electrodes underwent 1000 stretching cycles at 30% strain to evaluate durability. The comparison of impedance at 1 kHz before and after cycling (Fig. 3i) shows negligible changes, confirming the outstanding mechanical and electrical stability of the PEDOT: PSS-coated fibers. To ensure the reliability of the fiber electrodes in physiological environments, we evaluated the long-term stability of the PEDOT:PSS functional layer in simulated body fluid (phosphate-buffered saline, PBS, pH 7.4). Throughout the immersion period, no significant increase or fluctuation in impedance was observed, indicating that the interfacial properties between the coating and the electrolyte remained intact (Fig. S13).

Integration of stretchable multifunctional fiber into textiles for wireless energy transmission

The fiber-like structural characteristics and scalable manufacturing capabilities of stretchable multifunctional 1D fibers offer high potential for integration with textiles through embroidery techniques. In this study, we embroidered these fibers onto fabrics to fabricate radiofrequency (RF) antennas for wireless power transmission. As shown in Fig. 4a and Supplementary Video 3, conductive liquid metal fibers are stitched into flexible electronic textiles using a programmable embroidery process, creating textile-based inductors with complex, stable conductive pathways. The inset highlights the uniform structure of the encapsulated conductive fibers, which ensures stable and reliable performance under mechanical deformation. Figure 4b demonstrates the flexibility and adaptability of the electronic textile under various mechanical deformations, including bending, twisting, and stretching. Despite these dynamic deformations, the textile maintains its structural integrity. The electrical stability of the textile inductors under mechanical stress was evaluated by monitoring resistance changes during bending, twisting, and hammer impacts (Fig. 4c). The results show that the resistance remains constant under these conditions, verifying the excellent mechanical stability of the embroidered conductive fibers. In practical applications, electronic textiles integrated with conductive fibers conform well to curved biological surfaces. As shown in Fig. 4d, the textile closely conforms to the palm, demonstrating its potential for integration into wearable bioelectronic systems. Moreover, due to the excellent waterproof properties of the encapsulated conductive fibers, the textile successfully transmits energy. It maintains stable illumination of multiple light-emitting diodes (LEDs) even in the presence of water droplets (Fig. 4e). This highlights the waterproof characteristics of the textile inductors and their reliable wireless energy transmission performance under complex environmental conditions. To compare the wireless energy transmission efficiency of the liquid metal fiber inductors with conventional copper wire inductors, the quality factor (Q) was measured across a range of frequencies (Fig. 4f). The results show that the Q-factor of the liquid metal fiber inductors is comparable to that of copper wire inductors and performs exceptionally well at higher frequencies, demonstrating their capability for efficient energy transmission. The mechanical flexibility and durability of the electronic textiles were further assessed through cyclic bending tests. Figure 4g presents the resistance as a function of bending cycles, where the copper wire fractures after around 170 cycles. In contrast, the liquid metal conductive fibers exhibit superior yield strain, maintaining minimal resistance variations even after 400 bending cycles, significantly outperforming commercial copper wires’ durability.

Additionally, Fig. 4h compares the resistance during the first and final bending cycles, showing negligible changes, further confirming the mechanical and electrical stability of the embroidered conductive fibers over extended use. The textile resistance remains constant during each bending cycle, underscoring its consistent performance. These characteristics make the embroidered textile conductive traces well-suited for next-generation wearable electronic systems, enabling stable operation under mechanical deformation and complex environmental conditions while facilitating efficient wireless energy transmission. Application of stretchable multifunctional fiber for electrophysiological monitoring

The multifunctional fiber was further utilized for electrophysiological monitoring, enabling high-fidelity and stable recordings of electrocardiogram (ECG) and electromyogram (EMG) signals. It exhibited excellent performance and robustness under various physiological and environmental conditions. As shown in Fig. 5a, the multifunctional fibers were wrapped around volunteer’s wrists, with the electrodes maintaining tight contact with the skin for ECG signal acquisition. By integrating the fibers with the skin using prestress, the device forms a stable electrical interface while preserving breathability and water resistance (Fig. 5b). The breathability of the fiber electrodes was evaluated by applying them to the forearm, as shown in Fig. 5c. The photos showed the air permeability of the bioelectronic fiber, which significantly reduces moisture accumulation and enhances comfort during prolonged wear. In contrast, traditional gel electrodes lack breathability, leading to discomfort, skin redness, and irritation, particularly after extended use. These characteristics are critical for long-term wear, effectively reducing discomfort caused by moisture accumulation and ensuring reliable signal transmission. Figure 5d compares ECG signals recorded using fiber electrodes and traditional electrodes. Due to the decreased electrode adhesion caused by motion and sweating, the traditional electrodes (black curve) exhibit noticeable baseline drift and noise interference. In contrast, the fiber electrodes (red curve) provide clear and stable ECG signals, capturing main physiological features such as the P wave, QRS complex, and T wave. Additionally, during motion, the fiber electrodes show excellent resistance to motion artifacts (Fig. 5e), maintaining signal quality even during repetitive movements37. This highlights their superior mechanical stability and noise suppression capabilities. As illustrated in Figs. 5g and S17, electrical signals travel through neural pathways during muscle contraction to stimulate muscle fibers, causing potential changes inside and outside the muscle cells. These bioelectrical signals propagate to the skin surface and are detected by the electrodes of multifunctional fibers. Compared with traditional Ag/AgCl gel electrodes (Fig. 5h), the fiber electrodes demonstrated superior comfort and electrical contact stability, enhancing their reliability for long-term monitoring. Under muscle contraction, the fiber electrodes recorded clear and stable EMG signals (Fig. 5i) with consistent waveforms and amplitudes. The signals maintained high quality under both dry and wet conditions. Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) analysis results (Fig. 5j) showed that the fiber electrodes retain a high SNR even in humid environments, significantly outperforming traditional electrodes. This validates their stability and anti-interference capabilities under complex conditions.

Additionally, the multifunctional fiber effectively captured EMG signals corresponding to muscle contractions of varying forces (Fig. 5k), with signal amplitude increasing proportionally to the contraction force. A hand gesture recognition experiment was conducted further to validate the multifunctional fiber’s multi-channel monitoring capabilities. As shown in Fig. 5l, the fiber electrodes successfully recorded four-channel EMG signals corresponding to six different hand gestures hand opening, clenching, and bending in the forward, backward, leftward, and rightward directions. The signals from each channel were clear, independent, and non-interfering, accurately reflecting the muscle activity characteristics of each gesture. The collected data were further processed using classification algorithms, generating a confusion matrix (Fig. 5m). The results showed high classification accuracy, with several gestures achieving 99.6% average recognition accuracy, confirming the potential of fiber electronic devices for high-precision gesture recognition and motion classification.

Application of stretchable multifunctional fiber for in vivo nerve stimulation

The multifunctional fibers were applied in vivo nerve stimulation experiments to validate their stable interfacing with biological tissues and precise control of muscle activity. As shown in Fig. 6a, the schematic illustrates the placement of fiber electrodes on the rat’s sciatic nerve. Due to its stretchability and softness, the thin multifunctional fiber can be wrapped around the sciatic nerve. The conductive pathways within the fibers ensure efficient charge transport, while the PEDOT: PSS-coated electrode surface further reduces impedance and optimizes the electrical contact interface with the nerve tissue. The fiber electrodes transmit electrical stimulation to the nerve by effectively converting voltage pulses into current, inducing muscle contraction. The nerve stimulation was visualized by monitoring the rat’s hindlimb movement (Fig. 6c). In the initial state (left image), the hindlimb remains relaxed without movement (Supplementary Video 4). The hindlimb undergoes a significant inward contraction upon applying electrical stimulation through the fiber electrodes (middle image). To evaluate the dynamic response of hindlimb movement, continuous tracking was conducted under different stimulation frequencies (Fig. 6d). At low frequencies, such as 1 Hz and 5 Hz, the hindlimb exhibits stable and periodic flexion and extension with consistent amplitude and clear waveforms, indicating the high reliability and control precision of the fiber electrodes. Figure 6e further quantifies the maximum movement angle under varying stimulation frequencies (200 mV and 500 mV voltage pulses). At low frequencies (e.g., 1 Hz), the hindlimb initially extends outward (extension), but as the frequency increases, the movement gradually transitions to flexion (inward bending). At higher frequencies (above 50 Hz), the movement amplitude significantly decreases, reflecting the physiological limit of the nerve-muscle system in response to high-frequency stimulation. Compared to 200 mV, the 500 mV condition produced greater movement amplitudes, validating the enhanced nerve activation efficacy at higher voltages. Figure 6f further quantifies the stimulation success rate using a 500 mV voltage pulse across a 1–100 Hz frequency range. The results show that at low frequencies (1–10 Hz), the success rate approaches 100%. However, as the frequency increases, the success rate gradually decreases, with a notable drop observed at 30 Hz, highlighting muscle fatigue and the diminishing response of the system under rapid stimulation. Additionally, the relationship between stimulation voltage and hindlimb movement response was explored (Fig. 6g). As the voltage increased from 50 mV to 1000 mV, the movement angle significantly increased, and the success rate improved accordingly, reaching nearly 100% at voltages above 200 mV. This result confirms that the fiber electrodes can trigger effective neural responses at relatively low voltages, with enhanced stimulation performance at higher voltages.

To evaluate the stability of the fiber electronic devices in physiological conditions, they were soaked in PBS solution for 2 days, and their stimulation performance was compared with the initial state (Fig. 6h). The results show no significant change in either maximum movement angle or success rate at 200 mV and 500 mV after soaking, demonstrating the excellent durability and stability of the fiber electrodes in simulated biological environments. The biocompatibility of the fiber electronic devices was further validated through cell culture assays using HEK293T cells. Figure 6i shows fluorescence images of cells cultured in the PBS control group, the conductive fiber group, and PEDOT-coated electrodes. Cells exhibited good viability in all groups, with intact morphology and uniform distribution, particularly in the conductive fiber and PBS groups (Fig. S18). Quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity (Fig. 6j) revealed a slight decrease in the MWCNT electrode group, which was significantly improved after PEDOT:PSS modification. Ultimately, the fluorescence intensity in both the conductive fiber and electrode groups showed no significant difference compared with the PBS control group. These results demonstrate the excellent biocompatibility of the fiber electronic devices, with minimal impact on cell viability, making them well-suited for long-term biomedical applications.

Discussion

This study presents a scalable solution-based chemical deposition strategy for constructing stretchable one-dimensional (1D) multifunctional fibers with high electrical conductivity and mechanical durability. Systematic material characterization confirms the effective deposition and alloying process of the liquid metal, while the introduction of an encapsulation layer further enhances fiber robustness by preventing liquid metal leakage, chemical corrosion, and mechanical degradation under physiological conditions. To ensure compatibility with diverse and demanding environments, the electrode of the fiber is engineered as an inert composite material composed of conductive carbon nanomaterials coated with PEDOT:PSS. While inert electrodes exhibit a more significant increase in resistance under tensile strain compared to liquid metal electrodes, electrochemical impedance under practical measurement conditions is predominantly governed by interfacial contact effects, making the difference between the stretched and pristine states negligible. Further development in realizing the intrinsic electrochemical stability of liquid metal electrodes is expected to enhance the overall electrode performance. In both in vitro and on-skin applications, the fiber electrodes reliably record electrophysiological signals such as ECG and EMG, exhibiting reduced motion artifacts, excellent breathability, and strong water resistance. Further multichannel experiments demonstrate precise real-time motion and gesture classification with an average accuracy of 99.6%, underscoring the potential of the fiber for advanced physiological monitoring and motion tracking. In vivo nerve stimulation studies confirm that the fiber electrode forms a stable interface with the sciatic nerve, enabling precise control of hindlimb muscle contraction with high stimulation efficiency. Long-term stability assessments show that the electrodes retain their performance even after prolonged immersion in simulated physiological fluids, and biocompatibility assays reveal minimal cytotoxicity. Collectively, these findings demonstrate the safety and viability of the multifunctional fibers for both implantable and wearable biomedical applications.

Methods

Materials

The chemical reagents and elastomers used in this study were commercially sourced. These included thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU, Tecothane Soft AR-62A) form Lubrizol Inc. hydrogenated styrene-ethylene-butylene-styrene (SEBS, Tuftec H1221) from Asahi Kasei, and Ecoflex 00-30 from Smooth-On Inc. The chemical reagents comprised 3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene (EDOT), poly(4-styrenesulfonate sodium) (NaPSS), phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 0.01 M, pH 7.4), copper sulfate pentahydrate, disodium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), triethanolamine, sulfuric acid, Triton X-100, and dimethylamine borane, all obtained from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs, XFQ041) with a diameter of 10–20 nm were purchased from Nanjing XFNANO Materials Tech Co., Ltd. Galinstan was prepared by melting a metal mixture of Ga, In, and Sn in a weight ratio of 68.5:21.5:10 in a glove box at 80 °C for 2 h.

Fabrication of the conductive fiber electronics

The TPU fibers were fabricated using a desktop melt spinning system (model: HZ-RF-01) from Huizhi Electrospinning Co., Ltd. TPU pellets were melted in an extruder at 220 °C and then stretched and cooled at a speed of 20 m/min using a draw mechanism. Finally, the fibers were wound and collected using a winder. To deposit PDA on the fiber surface, the TPU fibers were immersed in a dopamine hydrochloride (2 mg/mL) solution in 50 mM Tris buffer (pH 8.5) and shaken continuously for 14 h. After deposition, the fibers were rinsed with deionized water to remove excess PDA and subsequently immersed in an aqueous silver nitrate solution (0.2 M) for 3 h, facilitating the growth of AgNPs as a seed layer. The fibers were then immersed in a chemical bath for copper deposition. The bath solution contained 0.0258 M copper sulfate pentahydrate, 0.0258 M disodium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 0.268 M triethanolamine, 0.09 M sulfuric acid, 0.1 w/w% Triton X-100, and 0.074 M dimethylamine borane. After 30 min of reaction, ~1 µm thick copper film was deposited in regions containing the AgNP seed layer. To create a liquid metal coating, bulk Galinstan was roll-coated over the copper film on the fiber while the substrate was immersed in a dilute hydrochloric acid solution (4 w/v%). During the alloying reaction, the copper film gradually dissolved into the liquid metal, forming a compliant, liquid-phase conductive coating on the fiber.

Fabrication of the fiber electrodes

The preparation process of the MWCNT/SEBS nanocomposite involved dispersing 50 mg of MWCNT into 10 mL of toluene using ultrasonication for 20 min, followed by thorough mixing with 1.5 g of SEBS solution [20% (w/w) in toluene]. The uncured MWCNT/SEBS nanodispersion was then fed into the liquid nozzle of a spraying device using a syringe pump, while 0.2 MPa compressed air was supplied to the gas nozzle to atomize the liquid into fine droplets. This process enabled the selective deposition of a smooth and uniform MWCNT/SEBS composite coating on the unmasked fibers. The coated nanocomposite was placed in an oven and heated at 70 °C to ensure complete drying. Subsequently, PEDOT:PSS was electrochemically deposited onto the MWCNT/SEBS nanocomposite electrodes to form an interfacial layer. The electrodeposition was performed using a three-electrode setup with an electrochemical workstation (Interface 1010E). The individual MWCNT/SEBS nanocomposite electrode served as the working electrode, a graphite rod as the counter electrode, and an Ag/AgCl electrode as the reference. An aqueous electrolyte solution containing 0.01 M EDOT and 0.1 M PSS was used for cyclic voltammetry electrodeposition. The deposition process involved scanning the potential from −0.5 V to 1.2 V at a rate of 50 mV/s for 30 cycles.

Characterization of fiber electronics

The microstructure of the fibers was examined using an optical microscope and a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Thermo Fisher Apreo 2S SEM). Mechanical properties were evaluated using an Instron universal testing machine (Model 5944). All tensile measurements were conducted at a pulling rate of 10 mm/min using fiber samples with a length of 5 cm. Electrochemical characterization was performed with an Interface 1010E potentiostat in a three-electrode configuration. The electrochemical stability window was measured in PBS solution using linear sweep voltammetry at a scan rate of 5 mV/s. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted at open circuit potential with an amplitude of 5 mV over a frequency range of 0.1 Hz to 1 MHz.

Epidermal electrophysiological signal recording

Informed and written consent was obtained prior to participation in the experiments by all subjects. The research process was harmless to humans. The phenomena measured represent natural physiological responses and are not influenced by sex. Because sex was not included as a variable in the study design, the results are not specific to any particular sex. Fiber electronics were connected to an Intan RHD2132 amplifier (Intan Technologies LLC) to record ECG and EMG signals. All channels were band-pass filtered, pre-amplified, and recorded at a sampling rate of 10 kHz. Before recording, the skin was cleaned with alcohol. For long-term and comparative tests, Red Dot 2223 electrodes (3 M, USA) with a surface contact area of 2.4 cm² were used as the control group. Single-channel electrodes employed bipolar recording to capture electrophysiological signals. Conductive gel was applied to all electrode-skin interfaces to enhance signal quality. The wearable-related experiments were conducted under the guidance of Peking University People’s Hospital and were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University People’s Hospital (approval no. 20240118).

In vivo animal experiments

All animal protocols were approved by the Tab of Animal Experimental Ethical Inspecrion of the Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine and conformed to the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the National Institutes of Health. The Number of Animal Use Permit was SRRSH2025-0018. A total of two adult male C57BL/6 J mice (8 weeks old, 20 g) were used in this study. Since the experiment was solely a proof-of-concept demonstration of the fiber electrode for neural electrical stimulation, sex was not a relevant factor and therefore was not considered. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and a 37 °C heating pad was placed beneath them. A 2 cm incision was made on the skin, and the muscles near the femur were carefully separated to expose the sciatic nerve. Fiber electrodes or platinum wires were bound to the exposed sciatic nerve for electrical stimulation. For hindlimb movement experiments, biphasic charge-balanced rectangular voltage pulses were applied to the sciatic nerve using a function generator (UTG900E, Uni-Trend Technology Co., Ltd.). Various voltage amplitudes (50 mV to 500 mV) were used to stimulate the nerve, and toe movement trajectories were recorded simultaneously. The success rate of toe movements was calculated as the percentage of successful toe responses relative to the total number of stimulation pulses. Success rates were measured across different stimulation frequencies. After the experiments, the mice were euthanized immediately.

Biocompatibility test

The biocompatibility of the fiber electronics was evaluated using 293 T cells through cell viability (CCK-8) and live/dead staining assays, with material extract solutions prepared at a concentration of 20 mg/mL. The extract solution was prepared by incubating the material in a culture medium at 37 °C for 24 h, followed by filtration through a 0.22 μm membrane to remove particulates. Cells were seeded into a 96-well plate in standard culture medium and incubated for 24 h to allow attachment. The culture medium was then replaced with the prepared extract solution, and the cells were further incubated for 24 h. For the CCK-8 assay, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well after incubation, and the plate was incubated for an additional 3 h at 37 °C. Cell viability was quantified by measuring the optical density (OD) at 450 nm using a microplate reader. For live/dead staining, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed with PBS before adding a live/dead dye solution. The cells were incubated with the dye for 30 min at 37 °C, and fluorescence signals were observed using a confocal microscope, where live cells stained green and dead cells stained red. These methods provided a comprehensive assessment of the material’s biocompatibility over the 24-h exposure period.
