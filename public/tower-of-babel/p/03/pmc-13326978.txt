Crimean‐Congo Haemorrhagic Fever Virus Exposure in Horses and Donkeys in Border Livestock Markets of Nigeria

Serum samples from 808 equids (305 horses and 503 donkeys) were tested for CCHFV‐specific IgG antibodies using a commercial multi‐species ELISA. These findings indicate widespread CCHFV exposure among equids and suggest that horses may serve as useful sentinel animals for monitoring CCHFV circulation in livestock trade corridors.

Introduction

Crimean‐Congo haemorrhagic fever virus (CCHFV) is a segmented, negative‐sense RNA virus in the genus Orthonairovirus, family Nairoviridae and order Bunyavirales (Lombe et al. 2021; Sana et al. 2022). The virus was first identified in Crimea in 1944 and subsequently in the Congo in 1969, hence its name (Bente et al. 2013). CCHFV is primarily transmitted by ticks, particularly species of the genus Hyalomma, and is the causative agent of Crimean‐Congo haemorrhagic fever (CCHF), a severe zoonotic disease in humans. CCHF is endemic across large parts of Africa, Asia, Eastern Europe and the Middle East, with reported case fatality rates ranging from 5% to 40% (Bente et al. 2013; Mertens et al. 2013; Shayan et al. 2015). Ticks play a vital role in the maintenance of CCHFV in nature due to the virus' ability to persist through transstadial and transovarial transmission (Spengler and Estrada‐Peña 2018). Among vertebrate hosts, domestic ruminants such as cattle, sheep and goats develop transient but sufficiently high viremia lasting approximately 3–7 days, which is sufficiently high to infect feeding ticks and are therefore regarded as important amplifying hosts within the transmission cycle (Hoogstraal 1979; Whitehouse 2004; Ergönül 2006). Humans typically become infected through tick bites or through contact with blood or tissues of infected vertebrates, particularly during slaughter, animal handling or veterinary procedures (Kadanali et al. 2009; Mustafa et al. 2011; Sargianou et al. 2013).

Detection of antibodies in animals through serological surveys is essential for tracking CCHFV exposure and these surveys help define ecological risk zones and inform public health responses (Seyma et al. 2025). Equids, including horses ( Equus caballus ) and donkeys ( Equus asinus ), are also infested by ticks and may be exposed to CCHFV. Experimental studies have shown that equids can develop short‐lived viremia following infection, lasting only a few days (2–7 days). The magnitude of viremia in equids is generally lower than that observed in other livestock species (Smirnova 1979). However, available evidence suggests that viremia in equids may be insufficient to consistently infect feeding ticks, indicating that, unlike ruminants, equids are unlikely to serve as efficient amplifying hosts for CCHFV. Their epidemiological importance may therefore lie primarily in acting as sentinel species reflecting environmental exposure and in supporting tick populations as blood meal sources, rather than directly sustaining viral circulation. Seroprevalence studies have nonetheless confirmed exposure of equids to CCHFV across several regions of the Middle East and western Asia (Vasilenko et al. 1971; Shanmugam et al. 1976; Tantawi et al. 1981; Badalov et al. 1969; Berezin et al. 1969; Smirnova et al. 1971; Saltık 2022). On the African continent, CCHFV antibodies in equids have been recorded in Benin, Kenya, Senegal and Uganda (Yessinou et al. 2025; Omoga et al. 2023; Msimang et al. 2020; Mangombi et al. 2019), indicating widespread exposure of these animals to infected ticks in diverse ecological settings. In Nigeria, serological evidence of CCHFV circulation has been documented in several domestic ungulates, including cattle, camels, sheep and goats, highlighting widespread viral exposure within livestock populations (Oluwayelu et al. 2020; Dzikwi‐Emennaa et al. 2022; Adamu et al. 2024; Igah et al. 2024; Chinonyerem et al. 2025; Ehizibolo et al. 2025). However, data on CCHFV exposure in equids remain scarce, despite their extensive involvement in livestock movement, transport and market activities, particularly in northern Nigeria. This study therefore aimed to investigate the seroprevalence of CCHFV in horses and donkeys in major livestock markets in northern Nigeria with the objective of assessing their potential value as sentinel indicators of CCHFV circulation within livestock trade corridors.

Materials and Methods

Study Area, Animal Data and Sample Collection

Between June 2023 and July 2024, through a longitudinal study, active surveillance was conducted in three international livestock markets situated in Nigeria's North West and North East border states to investigate the presence of CCHFV antibodies in horses and donkeys. These markets are known for their high volume of weekly equidae trade, including animals originating from neighbouring countries, specifically Niger and Cameroon (Figure 1). In the North West, sampling was performed at Ilella Livestock Market (LM) in Sokoto State and Maigatari LM (Jigawa State). In the North East, sampling occurred at Ganye LM (Adamawa State). Horses were sampled exclusively at Maigatari LM (n = 305), while donkeys were sampled across all three markets: Maigatari (n = 225), Ilella (n = 229) and Ganye (n = 49). The markets were visited weekly, and during each visit, five animals per species were sampled using convenient sampling. In Ganye LM, however, depending on the number of donkeys presented for sale on a given day, between zero and five animals were sampled. All animals were sampled with the consent of their owners or handlers. Animal data were systematically collected using a structured questionnaire administered to the owners or handlers. Information collected included estimated age, sex and origin of the animals. To categorise animals by age group, we used a combination of dentition (tooth eruption and wear patterns) and owner‐reported age. Animals were classified into young (≤ 4 years) and adult (> 4 years) based on this assessment. The cut‐off at 4 years reflects the age at which most animals reach full dental maturity and reproductive capability, marking a biologically meaningful transition from immature to fully mature adults (Richardson et al. 1995; Bello et al. 2013). This categorization was used solely for descriptive purposes, while age was treated as a continuous variable in regression analyses, acknowledging that risk of tick exposure or CCHFV infection does not change abruptly at this cut‐off. The location or geographic origin of the animal was determined through direct questioning of the owner or handler, who provided the point of origin and travel history of the animal prior to arrival at the market. This information was used to assess potential cross‐border movement and assign animals to domestic or foreign origin categories. Whole blood was collected from the jugular vein into serum separation tubes. Samples were allowed to clot at ambient temperature (20°C–25°C) for 30 min to 2 h. Clotted blood was stored in cool boxes with ice packs and transported to the laboratory for processing the same day. The resulting sera were aliquoted into well‐labelled sterile cryovials and stored at −20°C until tested.

Serological Testing

Samples were tested for CCHFV‐specific IgG antibodies using a commercial enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (ID Screen Double Antigen for multi‐species, ID Vet, Grabels, France) based on the recombinant nucleoprotein (NP) antigen, with 98.9% sensitivity (95% CI: 96.8–99.8) and 100% specificity (95% CI: 99.8–100) (Sas et al. 2018). Assays were performed on 96‐well microplates following the manufacturer's instructions, with duplicate positive and negative controls on each plate. OD values were read at 450 nm using an automated spectrophotometer Multiskan ELISA reader (Thermo Scientific Multiskan Go), and test validity was confirmed per manufacturer criteria (positive control OD > 0.35; ratio of mean negative to positive OD > 3). Quality control samples, including both positive and negative controls, were included in each run to validate assay performance. Sample‐to‐positive percentages (S/P%) were calculated as the ratio of the sample OD to the mean OD of positive controls, with S/P% > 30% considered positive. Individual S/P% values were recorded per plate and combined into a final database for analysis.

Data Analysis

Data were analysed using R statistical software (version 4.3.0; R Core Team 2023). Descriptive statistics were computed for all variables, with categorical data summarised as frequencies and percentages. Seroprevalence estimates were calculated with 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the exact binomial method implemented in the binom package (Dorai‐Raj 2022). All statistical tests were two‐sided, with a significance level of 0.05. To identify factors associated with CCHFV seropositivity, odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were derived from univariable logistic regression models fitted for each explanatory variable (age, sex, tick infestation status and animal origin). To further account for potential confounding among explanatory variables and clustering of observations within markets and states, a multilevel binomial logistic regression model was fitted using the lme4 package (Bates et al. 2015). Fixed effects included age (continuous), species, sex, tick infestation status and animal origin, while random intercepts were specified for market nested within state. The selected predictors were chosen based on biological plausibility and previous literature. Model fit and assumptions were evaluated using residual diagnostics, assessment of linearity for continuous variables on the logit scale and likelihood ratio tests comparing nested models. Model outputs were processed and tidied using the broom.mixed package (Bolker et al. 2024).

Results

A total of 808 serum samples were collected from equids during a longitudinal study conducted across three international livestock markets. The descriptive characteristics of equids sampled across the study markets showed that donkeys were mostly young (80.1%), whereas horses were predominantly adults (60.2%). Sex distribution was balanced in both species, with a slight male predominance. Most donkeys were of foreign origin (69.6%), while the majority of horses were domestic (57.2%). Tick infestation was relatively low overall but slightly higher in horses (18.1%) than in donkeys (12.3%) (Table 1).

The overall seroprevalence of CCHFV in equids was 23.1% (95% CI: 20.3%–26.2%). Horses exhibited a significantly higher seropositivity rate (52.1%, 95% CI: 46.3%–58.0%) compared to donkeys (5.7%, 95% CI: 3.7%–8.1%) (p < 0.001). Comparison of seropositivity across the three market locations was restricted to donkeys, as horses were not sampled in all markets. Donkey seroprevalence differed significantly by market (p = 0.001), indicating geographic variation in CCHFV exposure. Ganye livestock market (Adamawa state) recorded a significantly higher seroprevalence of 20% (95% CI: 9.1%–31.7%), whereas Illela (Sokoto state) and Maigatari (Jigawa state) livestock markets, had markedly lower rates of 4.4% (95% CI: 2.2%–8.1%) and 4.0% (95% CI: 1.9%–7.6%), respectively (Table 2). Notably, in the market where both horses and donkeys were sampled, donkeys showed a much lower seroprevalence of 4.0%. Table 3 shows significant differences in horses based on age and sex, with adult horses exhibiting a higher seropositivity rate (59%, 95% CI: 51.7%–66.0%) compared to younger horses (42%, 95% CI: 33.3%–51.4%). An odds ratio of 1.98 (95% CI: 1.23–3.18) suggest that adult horses are nearly twice as likely to be seropositive as their younger counterparts. Similarly, female horses had higher seropositivity rate (60%, 95% CI: 51.7%–68.4%) compared to the males (46%, 95% CI: 38.3%–53.6%). An odds ratio of 0.57 (95% CI: 0.37–0.89) indicates that male horses had 43% lower odds of seropositivity relative to females. Additionally, no significant association were found between CCHFV seropositivity and either tick infestation status and origin of animal (whether from neighbouring countries or within Nigeria) in horses. Likewise, in donkeys, none of the assessed variables (age, sex, tick infestation status and origin of animals) showed a significant association with CCHFV seropositivity (Table 4).

Discussion

Serological surveys are essential for monitoring the spread of CCHFV in animal populations and assessing spillover risk to humans (Seyma et al. 2025). In this study, the observed overall seroprevalence of 23.1% in equids suggest widespread prior exposure to the virus consistent with ongoing transmission pressure within livestock trade corridors in West Africa. The detection of CCHFV antibodies in equids at transboundary livestock markets indicates prior exposure but does not confirm active infection or viremia; seropositive animals are therefore unlikely to directly transmit the virus to humans. However, the convergence of humans, animals and potential vectors (e.g., ticks) in these markets may create conditions conducive to zoonotic transmission if viremic animals are present. The frequent exchange of animals across borders further increases the likelihood of introducing or exporting infected ticks or viremic animals into new areas, particularly in regions lacking robust veterinary and human surveillance infrastructure (Ali et al. 2021). Animal handlers, livestock traders, veterinarians and butchers who come into frequent contact with animals, blood, tissues or tick‐infested hides remain at occupational risk. These findings underscore the need for ongoing surveillance and strengthened biosecurity measures in livestock markets to mitigate potential public health risks.

Horses had a higher rate of prevalence (52.1%), compared to donkeys (5.7%). Notably, in the market where both species were sampled, horses demonstrated a substantially higher seroprevalence than donkeys. This disparity may reflect species‐specific differences in behaviour, grazing range and tick exposure frequency, with horses being considerably more mobile and used more extensively in trade activities, compared to donkeys. However, it is important to note that horses were sampled predominantly from a single market location, whereas donkeys were sampled across all three study zones. This unbalanced sampling limits the ability to attribute the observed differences in seroprevalence solely to species effect, as geographic variation in exposure risk may also have contributed to the patterns observed. Consequently, comparisons between horses and donkeys should be interpreted with caution. Previous studies in more localised settings in Africa have reported lower seroprevalence in horses but higher seroprevalence in donkeys compared with the present study, including 16.7% (4/24) in horses from Benin (Yessinou et al. 2025) and 31.4% (16/51) in donkeys from Kenya (Omoga et al. 2023). However, these reports were based on relatively small sample sizes, which may limit the precision and generalizability of their seroprevalence estimates compared with the larger, multi‐site sampling framework employed in the present study. The age‐related increase in seropositivity among horses observed here mirrors patterns seen in other CCHFV‐endemic regions, where older animals accumulate more exposure over time (Ergönül 2006; Shayan et al. 2015). However, The association between age and CCHFV seropositivity observed in horses should be interpreted conservatively. While consistent with cumulative exposure in endemic settings, this relationship does not imply causality. Within the limits of the available data, multilevel modelling indicated that age remained independently associated with seropositivity after adjustment for sex and market‐level clustering, supporting age as a plausible correlate rather than an artefact of population structure. The higher seroprevalence initially observed in female horses did not persist after adjustment, suggesting confounding by age distribution rather than a true sex‐specific effect. In the absence of detailed population and management data, no independent conclusions regarding sex‐related risk can be drawn. No age‐ or sex‐related associations were detected in donkeys. This likely reflects the low overall seroprevalence in this species, limiting statistical power to identify demographic patterns, rather than evidence for the absence of such effects. Overall, age is the only demographic variable with consistent statistical support in horses, and these findings should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating, underscoring the need for more detailed, longitudinal investigations. Interestingly, tick infestation status did not significantly correlate with CCHFV seropositivity in either species, suggesting that visible tick presence at the time of sampling may not reliably reflect past exposure risk. Similar observations were made in sero‐surveillance studies in Uganda and Senegal, where seropositivity did not always align with tick burden (Msimang et al. 2020; Mangombi et al. 2019). The lack of a significant association between CCHFV seropositivity and animal origin suggests that viral exposure is not restricted to a specific geographic location but reflects widespread endemic circulation within Nigeria and neighbouring regions. Animals traded through international livestock markets are frequently sourced from multiple states and across national borders, resulting in extensive mixing of livestock from diverse endemic settings. These markets therefore act as convergence points where animals with prior exposure from different regions are aggregated, facilitating pathogen maintenance and increasing the potential for cross‐border dissemination through continued animal movement and trade. The results from this study have some policy and surveillance implications which includes the need to broaden the surveillance of CCHF beyond traditional livestock species. Horses, due to their high seropositivity and mobility, may serve as effective sentinel species for early detection of CCHFV exposure in livestock populations. It is also important to undertake studies to elucidate the dynamics of viremia in these species and potential risk of zoonotic transmission to humans. National and regional surveillance programs may consider expanding their scope to include horses and donkeys, especially in international trade zones. Livestock market biosecurity, tick control strategies, awareness campaigns targeting animal handlers and traders, and cross‐border collaborations within the ECOWAS region are vital to contain and mitigate the spread of CCHFV. Furthermore, future studies should incorporate molecular diagnostics and genomic surveillance to identify circulating CCHFV strains, vector mapping to assess tick species distribution and sero‐epidemiological studies in high‐risk human populations. Together, these efforts will build a more comprehensive understanding of the epidemiology of CCHF in West Africa and enhance preparedness for future outbreaks.
