Unravelling homozygous PLA2G6 variants in Pakistani individuals with diverse clinical manifestations via whole exome sequencing
Authors: Hamid Khan, Muhammad Zahid, Muhammad Imran Shabbir, Adeel Ahmed, Bakhtawar Khan, Mubin Mustafa Kiyani, Turki Abualait, Shahid Bashir, Hong-Tao Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhole Exome Sequencing (WES) was performed on 265 Pakistani individuals with suspected neurodevelopmental disorders, revealing that seven of them carried homozygous mutations in the PLA2G6 gene, which is linked to neurodegenerative diseases such as Infantile Neuroaxonal Dystrophy (INAD) and Parkinson’s disease 14 (PD14). These patients presented with a typical cluster of symptoms, including ataxia, gait instability, cognitive decline, motor regression, and psychiatric manifestations like autistic features, impulsive behaviour, and emotional lability. However, the clinical presentation varied across patients, underscoring the phenotypic diversity associated with PLA2G6 mutations. We identified six distinct variants in the PLA2G6 gene (NM_003560.4) across seven affected individuals. These comprised two pathogenic variants (c.2370T > G and c.1427 + 1G > T), three likely pathogenic variants c.929T > A (observed in two patients), c.1591 + 5G > C, and c.2276G > T, and one variant of uncertain significance c.905T > G. All these mutations were predicted to be disease-related by in silico tools, confirming their likely role in disease causation. This study elucidates the variety of clinical features due to PLA2G6 mutations, with manifestations ranging from motor-cognitive to psychiatric features. Our results emphasize the importance of new homozygous variants for the genetic heterogeneity of these diseases. We want to underscore the value of genetic testing in patients with progressive neurodevelopmental signs, like in the case of our patient, which may result in early diagnosis and help in deciding treatment later. Furthermore, this study provides additional insight into genotype-phenotype correlations of PLA2G6-associated diseases and offers directions for future studies to develop tailored therapeutics.Keywords: PLA2G6, Homozygous variants, Neurodegenerative disorders, Whole exome sequencing, Infantile neuroaxonal dystrophy, Parkinson's disease 14, Genotype-Phenotype correlation, Consanguineous marriages, Clinical manifestations

Introduction

Phospholipase A2 group VI (PLA2G6), a calcium-independent phospholipase A2 (iPLA2β), is critically involved in phospholipid remodelling, membrane homeostasis and neurodevelopment. The gene is situated on chromosome 22 and encodes an enzyme that participates in phospholipid hydrolysis, which releases arachidonic acid. This enzyme is important in the CNS, as it contributes to neuronal signalling, membrane dynamics, and inflammation [1]. Homozygous mutations in PLA2G6 are associated with a range of neurodegenerative disorders such as infantile neuroaxonal dystrophy (INAD), neurodegeneration with brain iron accumulation (NBIA) and Parkinsonism with early-onset dementia [2]. These clinical manifestations include progressive motor and cognitive symptoms, frequently showing heterogeneous clinical phenotypes, making diagnosis difficult [3].

PLA2G6 gene mutations have been associated with several neurodegenerative disorders, such as infantile neuroaxonal dystrophy (INAD), neurodegeneration with brain iron accumulation (NBIA), or early-onset Parkinson’s with dementia [4]. It should be kept in mind that some patients may show atypical presentations such as severe seizures and cortical visual impairment, resulting in a wide phenotypic variability for PLA2G6-related disorders [5]. Clinically, the disorders present with progressive motor and cognitive deterioration, ataxia, gait disturbances, seizures, psychiatric features-and in some cases-atypical neurological features. The clinical classification and diagnosis of PLA2G6-associated diseases can be challenging because of the phenotypic heterogeneity [6].

Global genetic prevalence of PLA2G6-related neurodegeneration is more and more recognized, particularly in populations with high rates of consanguinity, where recessive forms of inheritance are more common [7]. However, the full range of mutations and phenotypes occurring with this gene has not yet been appreciated, and the association of genotype with phenotype requires further study [2].

Adding to the already-large number of known pathogenic mutations in this gene will not only refine diagnostic accuracy but will also provide further insight into the biological pathways of PLA2G6-related diseases [8]. This information will provide valuable insight into next-generation treatment options. PLA2G6 pathogenic variants have been associated with a broad spectrum of various neurodegenerative diseases, such as infantile neuroaxonal dystrophy (INAD), neurodegeneration with brain iron accumulation (NBIA), and early-onset Parkinsonism with dementia [4]. Furthermore, some patients showed atypical disease symptoms of the group, such as severe seizures and cortical visual impairment, emphasizing a variable expressivity of PLA2G6-related diseases. Patients typically present with slowly progressive motor and cognitive decline, ataxia, gait disturbance, seizures, psychiatric symptoms, and occasionally unusual neurological symptoms. The clinical diagnosis of PLA2G6-associated diseases can be difficult, due to phenotypic heterogeneity [9].

Materials and methods

This observational genetic study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant institutional guidelines. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the International Islamic University Islamabad (IIUI; approval No. [No.IIUI/D(BS)/FBAS/2021], dated [30/03/2021]. Written informed consent to participate was obtained from all adult participants; for minors, consent was obtained from parents/legal guardians and age-appropriate assent was secured when applicable. Data were de-identified before analysis, and confidentiality was maintained throughout. This study was not a clinical trial; clinical trial registration is not applicable. Full ethical declarations (including consent to participate and consent for publication, where relevant) are provided in the Declarations section.

Blood samples were obtained from affected individuals and available family members after informed consent. Genomic DNA was extracted from the sample using the Qiagen DNA Extraction Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Patients were included according to young age at onset of neurological symptoms, progressive character of the disease, and the presence of an evident positive family history, to increase the possibilities of finding pathogenic variants. Because patients were enrolled at different times, sequencing was performed on different WES platforms. Patients 2 and 6 were sequenced on the MGI T7 platform (TWIST Human Comprehensive Exome Kit, GRCh38/hg38). Patients 1, 3, 4, 5, and 7 were sequenced on Illumina platforms (NovaSeq/HiSeq, GRCh37/hg19) at ~ 100× mean coverage. Reads were mapped to the human reference genome GRCh38/hg38 (MGI T7) and GRCh37/hg19 (Illumina- based data) according to the specific testing laboratory. The variants were called using GATK HaplotypeCaller and UnifiedGenotyper (MGI) or the DRAGEN variant caller (Illumina). Variants were annotated using Variant Effect Predictor (VEP, Ensembl) and filtered to remove common polymorphisms as per the population frequency observed in gnomAD, 1000 Genomes and ExAC data. To infer pathogenicity, variants were compared with ClinVar, OMIM, and SwissVar. Moreover, in silico prediction programs such as PolyPhen-2, SIFT, MutationTaster, DANN, MutationAssessor, and SpliceAI were used to evaluate the possible functional implications of missense and splicing variants. Variants were prioritized if status had MAF < 0.01, were deleterious predictors, and correlated with the patient’s phenotype. All were classified in accordance with ACMG/AMP and ClinGen standards. The predicted splicing variants and those that were consistent with the family segregation were selected for further analysis. Variant-disease or phenotype relationships were established for all the identified variants by comparing them with the phenotype. Segregation analysis in the available family members was performed (as illustrated in Fig. 2.) by targeted next-generation sequencing on an Illumina MiSeq platform, which verified heterozygous carrier status in both parents. Stringent quality control (QC) was performed by applying minimum read depth, high mapping percentage and removal of duplicate reads. All families received genetic counselling with follow-up suggested where feasible. Pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants were submitted to ClinVar (accession SUB15317870 and SUB15318203).

Results

Here we examined a cohort of 265 patients with variable neurological phenotypes, ranging from neurodevelopmental delay, motor regression, to movement disorder. Subjects were recruited through our research program in collaboration with clinicians who referred patients with suspected genetic neurological disorders. Inclusion criteria implied (i) neurological symptoms of unknown origin, including developmental delay, seizures, movement disorder, and/or regression, (ii) early onset in childhood, and (iii) presence of a DNA sample for molecular investigation. These classification criteria are consistent with a diagnostic enrichment approach rather than targeting patients with monogenic disorders specifically, accounting for the broad spectrum of genetic results identified in this group.

We found homozygous likely pathogenic or pathogenic variants in PLA2G6 in seven patients from five unrelated, but apparently consanguineous, families among the 265 recruited individuals. These results underline the role of consanguinity in the detection of recessive disorders and also emphasize the wide phenotypic variability of PLA2G6-related disease.

Clinical spectrum

Seven families were recruited between 2019 and 2024 through our research program, in collaboration with pediatric neurology clinics across Pakistan. All patients had been initially referred for evaluation of idiopathic neurodevelopmental and motor delay. Initial clinical diagnoses were cerebral palsy (n = 3), idiopathic neurodevelopmental/ID (n = 2), and presumed leukodystrophy (n = 1). Mean age at onset of symptoms was 14.3 ± 5.8 months (range 6–24 months). Motor delay and global developmental delay were seen from an early age, and in all cases (7/7, 100%). Motor regression, usually appearing after the second year of age, was observed in three patients (50%). Cerebellar characteristics evolved, and 3/7 (43%) had ataxia, gait instability, and regular falls. Neuroimaging was available for a limited group of patients pursued by referring physicians and showed cerebellar atrophy in 3/7 (43%), and leukodystrophy and global brain atrophy in individual patients. Pyramidal tract involvement was observed to occur later during the course of the disease, with spasticity and hyperreflexia in 2/7 patients (28%). Cognitive deterioration and regression of speech were reported in one child with the p.Arg759Ile variant. Neuropsychiatric symptoms were observed during preschool years in 2/7 patients (28%) as autistic traits and impulsive behaviour. Trained professionals following DSM-5 criteria after the age of 3 years made diagnosis of ASD Diagnosis of ASD. Systemic features were added in follow-ups later. One patient with the c.1591 + 5G > C variant also had severe constipation associated with head-banging behaviour that could indicate autonomic and peripheral nerve system involvement. Genomic testing was completed in the context of this research study after referral of the patients, who were referred at 2–6 years of age on average. Genetic findings

We found six homozygous variants in PLA2G6 among our seven patients. These were nonsense, splice site, and missense changes. The nonsense mutation NM_003560.4:c.2370T > G (p.Tyr790*) was related to an infantile, rapidly progressive course with early motor regression and profound cognitive deterioration. Within this spectrum of severity, NM_003560.4:c.1427 + 1G > T was associated with severe early-onset, NM_003560.4:c.1591 + 5G > C yielded an intermediate phenotype with a mixture of central and peripheral neurological manifestations, including autonomic involvement and constipation. Among these, the missense variants NM_003560.4:c.929T > A (p.Val310Glu) in two unrelated individuals and NM_003560.4:c.905T > G (p.Met302Arg) had later onset and slow disease progression. However, three out of seven patients eventually showed substantial neurological disability, characterised by gait disturbance and cognitive deficits. The VUS NM_003560.4:c.2276G > T (p.Arg759Ile) was also identified in a patient with a less severe degree of cognitive impairment. Its possible variant-specific or modifying effect is being raised (Fig. 1). Taken together, the results demonstrated a clear genotype–phenotype correlation, where loss-of-function variants (including nonsense and canonical splice-site changes) were associated with severe neonatal-onset disease followed by rapid progression to severe disability; missense changes allowed for a milder but ultimately disabling course; and non-canonical splice-site variants caused intermediate severity of disease with additional systemic features. In available families, co-segregation of the identified PLA2G6 variants with the disease is demonstrated by pedigrees (Fig. 2). Confirmation of these findings demonstrates that PLA2G6-related NPD encompasses a broad phenotypic range from the classic phenotypes of infantile neuroaxonal dystrophy/neurodegeneration with brain iron accumulation to atypical forms with seizures or psychiatric involvement, as previously reported. This study expands the mutational spectrum of PLA2G6 and demonstrates the importance of considering comprehensive genetic analysis in unexplained neurodevelopmental regression, particularly in consanguineous cohorts. Discussion

The neurodevelopmental disease field has been a source of clinical and research challenge as it is a highly heterogeneous phenotype disease and having complex genetic underpinning [10]. Within the spectrum of neurodegenerative diseases, mutations in the PLA2G6 gene have emerged as a key event in both infantile neuroaxonal dystrophy (INAD) and Parkinson’s disease 14 (PD14) [11]. Among this group of 265 Pakistani probands with NDD, we found 7 such individuals with homozygous mutations in the PLA2G6 gene. This finding highlights the involvement of PLA2G6 in Pakistani patients with NDD and strongly underscores the importance of genetic diagnostics in patients with progressive neurological conditions.

The phenotypic presentation in the seven subjects was relatively uniform but with a broad range, including ataxia, general unsteadiness when walking, cognitive impairment, loss of motor skills, as well as psychiatric manifestations, including autistic features, impulsive behaviour, and emotional lability. These overlapping phenotypic features may represent a common disease mechanism related to the disruption of PLA2G6, as the centrality of this gene in neuronal homeostasis and development seems to be the general association in these various disorders [12]. However, the significant phenotypic heterogeneity observed among patients with PLA2G6 defects, as in the case of our own patient, emphasizes the multifaceted relationship between modulators and nongenetic and environmental factors on disease expression. Our findings are consistent with findings from other studies, suggesting the causal genes may explain varying severities of disease resulting in a large clinical spectrum of phenotypes.

We identified six distinct variants in the PLA2G6 gene (NM_003560.4) across seven affected individuals. These comprised two pathogenic variants (c.2370T > G and c.1427 + 1G > T), three likely pathogenic variants c.929T > A (observed in two patients), c.1591 + 5G > C, and c.2276G > T, and one variant of uncertain significance c.905T > G. These results broaden the mutation spectrum of PLA2G6 and help gain insight into these disorders’ genetic background. Pathogenicity prediction programs, such as SIFT, PolyPhen and MutationTaster, predicted the possible impact of these variants on the pathogenesis of disease, therefore supporting their clinical significance.

The identification of these variants could reveal important information about functional effects of PLA2G6 mutations. Notably, mutations, including NM_003560.4:c.2370T > G(p.Tyr790*), NM_003560.4:c.1427 + 1G > T are listed as pathogenic and NM_003560.4:c.929T > A(p.Val310Glu), NM_003560.4:c.1591 + 5G > C are classified as likely pathogenic and NM_003560.4:c.905T > G(p.Met302Arg) and NM_003560.4:c.2276G > T(p.Arg759Ile) as variant of uncertain significance. These mutations are presumed to impact the activity and stability of the PLA2G6 protein and set the stage for further research on the underlying molecular mechanisms of disease development. The wide range of clinical manifestations from motor and intellectual disabilities to psychiatric forms is likely to mirror the pleiotropic functions of PLA2G6 in brain development, neuronal physiology, lipid turnover, and cell homeostasis (Table 1.). These are critical processes required for the nervous system’s structure and to maintain its functional ability.

Additionally, our study highlights the clinical relevance of novel homozygous variants identified in PLA2G6. These data contribute to the genetic heterogeneity in neurodevelopmental disorders and clarify genotype-phenotype correlations. They therefore reinforce the importance of genetic testing in patients with a progressive neurodevelopmental phenotype, which allows for early diagnosis and more specific management. Besides its diagnostic value, genetic testing allows genetic counseling and the application of early therapeutic measures to attenuate the effects of the symptoms, including motor and cognitive impairments [13].

Our report adds to the expanding literature demonstrating the overall usefulness of advanced molecular techniques, like WES and whole-genome sequencing, in the diagnostic approach of neurodevelopmental phenotypes. These have revolutionised how we are able to identify the genetic variant(s) causing the condition, resulting in improved diagnostic accuracy and speed. In addition, the combination of genetic data with clinical and neuroimaging data will allow a deeper insight into genotype-phenotype correlations, and consequently, the application of even more personalised therapeutic strategies [14].

Identifying new PLA2G6 mutations in our cohort further illustrates the genetic heterogeneity underlying NDD and the opportunity to identify additional genetic risk factors for these disorders [15]. Although the phenotypic heterogeneity associated with PLA2G6-related disease is daunting, it provides a potential roadmap for future studies to identify the molecular and genetic factors influencing disease penetrance [4]. Future studies are needed to determine the contribution of modifier genes and establish targets for treatment and personalisation of treatments.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The small number of PLA2G6-positive cases limits the ability to establish strong genotype–phenotype correlations. Functional validation experiments, such as transcript or protein assays, were not performed, which restricts direct confirmation of variant pathogenicity. Additionally, the study cohort was limited to Pakistani individuals, which may not fully represent the genetic diversity of PLA2G6-associated disorders across populations. Lastly, limited longitudinal clinical and neuroimaging data prevented detailed assessment of disease progression. Future studies involving larger, multi-ethnic cohorts with functional analyses are needed to better clarify the molecular and phenotypic spectrum of PLA2G6-related neurodevelopmental disorders.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights the importance of genetic tests in diagnosing neurodevelopmental disorders, PLA2G6 mutations in particular. Predictive genetic testing at an early age can facilitate early diagnosis, genetic counselling, and personalised interventions, thereby improving the quality of life for patients. Moreover, further understanding of the genetic and molecular regulators of PLA2G6-related diseases will also be critical to tailoring treatments capable of inhibiting disease progression and easing the burden for patients.
