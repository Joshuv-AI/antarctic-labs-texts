Understanding context in the implementation of school health promotion: a scoping review and conceptual framework
Authors: Madhura Joshi, Anu Linnansaari, Jackline Ngowi, Elizabeth Mwaniki, Kevin Kahshamba, Salla Atkins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSchools offer an influential setting for delivering health promotion interventions. However, the success of these efforts largely depends on how well the implementation context is understood and addressed. While “context” is recognized as a key concept in implementation science, it remains inconsistently defined and applied. Therefore, the aim of this scoping review was to critically examine theoretical approaches used in implementation of school health intervention programs, and to explore how these approaches conceptualize and address context. Ultimately, the goal is to develop a conceptual framework that can support researchers, practitioners, and decision-makers in better understanding and addressing context in school health promotion.MethodThis scoping review was conducted following the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guidelines. A search was conducted in Medline and ASSIA on June 18th, 2024. Articles were screened independently by two authors. After data extraction, a reflexive thematic analysis approach was used to analyze data.ResultsOur findings reveal that various theoretical approaches including implementation, adaptation, and system frameworks, as well as organizational and actor-network theory, have been utilized to conceptualize and address context in school health promotion. These approaches were applied across different stages of implementation: planning, delivery, and evaluation. However, our results confirm considerable variation in how these theoretical approaches address and define context. These different ways of defining context not only highlight a lack of consensus around its definition but also have significant implications leading to the prioritization of certain aspects of context over others, resulting in a fragmented, incomplete understanding of the social, cultural, economic, and political setting within which interventions are implemented. Using reflexive thematic analysis, we identified two overarching themes. The first theme, dimensions of context, comprises four contextual dimensions: organizational, socio-cultural, economic, and political. The second theme, features of context, captures the dynamic, interconnected, non-linear nature of context, and its spatial and temporal aspects. Together, these dimensions and features are integrated into a visual framework, the Contextual Framework for School Implementation (CFSI).The framework is further supported by practical guiding questions spanning all implementation phases: planning, delivery, and evaluation, emphasizing the importance of considering implementation context from the outset of program development.ConclusionsThis review examined how theoretical approaches have been used to understand and address context in school health promotion. It shows that while context is often recognized as important, current ways of describing and applying it are fragmented and incomplete. By integrating the key dimensions and features of context, the proposed framework provides phase-specific questions to guide planning, delivery, and evaluation for understanding how context shapes implementation. The framework may also help explain certain implementation challenges, supporting both researchers and health practitioners in navigating contextual complexity throughout the implementation process, thereby informing context-sensitive health policy and practice.Systematic review registrationThis study was based on a registered protocol (https://osf.io/azdv4/). It is part of the Changemaker project funded by the European Union (GA No 101137359).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13643-026-03232-8.

The framework underscores the imperative of integrating both contextual dimensions and core features to achieve a holistic and nuanced understanding of context. Background

Schools serve as a critical arena for health promotion and prevention efforts [1] and are potential settings for impacting the prevalence of non-communicable diseases (NCDs). They offer a structured environment and have a wide reach, allowing health promotion activities to be integrated into daily routines. WHO Health Promoting Schools, the EU’s Healthier Together, are some examples of the health promotion programs in schools to prevent NCDs. However, the success of any health intervention largely depends on how the program is implemented—a process that is often complex and multifaceted. Among the many factors that shape the implementation, context is considered as the most critical element of this process.

Social scientists from various disciplinary backgrounds agree that human actions are meaningful only when placed within their contexts [2]. However, Paul Bate (2014) argues that context is a massively misunderstood and understudied concept, with not a single study offering explicit and well-articulated theory of context. In healthcare research, context is generally used in an informal way to refer to the explanation of some situation or environment in which certain phenomena take place [3]. The term “context” often does not have a universally accepted definition, and implementation science is no exception. A significant body of research within implementation science emphasizes the role of context but lacks a uniform definition of the concept [4–6]. To effectively integrate context into implementation processes, we first need to clearly understand what context means.

“What is Context”? The Oxford Dictionary defines it as “the situation in which something happens and that helps you to understand it.” [7]. From this definition, it is clear that understanding and explanation lie at the heart of the concept. Therefore, context inherently involves a qualitative dimension, as it requires interpretation and meaning. Moreover, context not only helps us grasp the nature of certain phenomena but also reveals the reasons behind them.

Studies reflect that school health promotion offers a good example of the role of context in implementation. Schools are embedded in a complex system involving multiple levels—organizational, social, cultural, political—and multiple stakeholders, including teachers, students, parents, and school administrations. The inter-relationships and interactions among these multiple contextual factors influence and shape the implementation processes [8, 9]. Although there is consensus on the importance of contextual factors in school-based implementation, the current literature lacks a holistic approach to studying context within school settings.

Theoretical approaches, such as implementation science theories, models, and frameworks (TMFs), are often employed to navigate implementation complexities and to enhance understanding of context. Despite their utility, TMFs often have deficiencies in accounting for context. Not all TMFs clearly consider context, and those that do may define it in distinct ways, emphasizing certain contextual factors over others thereby lacking a holistic approach to understand how context shapes health intervention and their implementation [10, 11]. It is well acknowledged that while some TMFs account for the impact of context, they do so in many cases superficially, treating context as merely one of the determinants within the model or framework [10]. This reductionist approach fails to capture the dynamic and interconnected nature of context, thus lacking the potential to support implementation.

According to the literature, addressing complex implementation challenges often requires drawing on multiple theoretical frameworks. Nilsen [10], in his seminal paper, discusses how implementation science has evolved to incorporate various theoretical approaches to better understand and explain the success or failure of implementation efforts. He categorizes these into process models, determinant frameworks, classic theories, implementation theories, and evaluation frameworks each serving distinct purposes in the implementation process. Similarly, a scoping review by Wang et al. [12] identified 143 TMFs used in implementation science, many of which are derived from multiple grand theories across disciplines. This convergence highlights the field’s complexity and underscores the value of integrating insights from various theoretical perspectives to enhance both the explanation and development of effective implementation. Supporting this view, the NCBI Bookshelf describes the dynamic and multifaceted nature of the phenomena studied in implementation science, suggesting that combining specific theories to address different aspects of a problem can be a productive and necessary approach [13]. Collectively, these sources affirm that complex implementation challenges such as those encountered in school health promotion are best approached through the integration of multiple theoretical frameworks.

Bringing together insights from multiple theoretical approaches to understanding context helps address a key gap in current school implementation literature, namely, the fragmented and inconsistent conceptualizations of context. This integrative approach can lead to more effective and sustainable implementation, specifically in complex and diverse educational settings. Therefore, this review aims to examine how context is defined and considered within the theoretical approaches used in school health promotion studies. Building on these insights, we propose a conceptual framework designed to support researchers and practitioners in understanding and addressing contextual factors in the planning and implementation of school health promotion. A theory-based, holistic understanding of context including its various features and dimensions can help to plan, deliver, and evaluate the implementation efforts to the unique needs of each school. This, in turn, can foster more supportive, inclusive, and health-promoting school environments, benefiting both the scientific community and those working in practice. Ultimately, the study seeks to strengthen implementation efforts in schools and contribute to health promotion initiatives.

Data and methods

Study design

This scoping review, along with the development of the conceptual framework, was conducted following the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guidelines [14] to ensure methodological rigor and transparency. The method includes (1) identifying the research question; (2) inclusion criteria; (3) concept; (4) context; (5) types of evidence sources; (6) search strategy; (7) evidence screening and selection; (8) data extraction; (9) data analysis; and (10) presentation of results.

Scoping reviews are useful for providing a comprehensive understanding of complex topics and examining how specific concepts have been studied within the existing body of literature, especially when literature is heterogeneous in terms of methods or disciplines. They are also helpful to identify inconsistencies or gaps in terminology and conceptualization [15]. This review was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) reporting guidelines [16]. The completed PRISMA Checklist is available in Supplementary File 3. This study is based on a registered protocol (https://osf.io/azdv4/).

Search strategy

A pilot search in MEDLINE was conducted to identify key terms and refine the search strategy. The development of search terms was guided by the Joanna Briggs Institute’s Population, Concept, and Context (PCC) framework [14] and refined through an iterative process to ensure relevance and comprehensiveness. The final strategy included three components:Population: Adolescents, young people, and students, as well as school staff including teachers, senior managers, principals, and headmasters within school settings.Concept: Implementation science theories, models, and frameworks (TMFs), along with other theoretical or conceptual approaches that support the implementation of health interventions in schools.Context: Factors within the school setting and broader social, cultural, and environmental influences. Interactions between and within these contextual layers were also considered.

Study selection and screening

Following the search, all identified studies were downloaded as CSV files and uploaded to Zotero, where duplicates were removed [17]. The articles were then uploaded to Rayyan.ai [18] for the title and abstract screening. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria, along with screening instructions, were shared among the research team. Prior to independent screening, all reviewers screened 10 papers together in a joint meeting to ensure consistent understanding of the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Following this calibration exercise, titles and abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers per record, with workload distributed among four researchers (MJ, EM, KK, and JN). Any conflicts were discussed with a third researcher (AL, SA) to make the final decision.

This was followed by the retrieval of full texts for all potentially eligible studies. Two researchers (MJ and AL) independently reviewed the full text. Any conflicts during this process were resolved by a third researcher (SA). Predefined exclusion criteria, agreed upon by the entire research team, were applied during full-text screening. For any additional exclusions, the articles and reasons for exclusion were discussed and agreed upon collectively by all researchers (See Supplementary File 5).

The search process identified 1028 studies after removing 299 duplicates. We excluded 840 papers based on irrelevant titles and abstracts, leaving us with 188 studies for which we retrieved the full text. Of these, 37 studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final review.

Data extraction

The data extraction process followed a structured and systematic approach. First, a pilot extraction was done with two researchers (MJ, AL) simultaneously extracting data from 2 to 3 articles to determine if the extraction template should be adjusted. MJ conducted the extraction from all the remaining articles with support from AL and SA when needed. The extracted data included authors, publication year, country, paper aim, study design, implementers, implementation phase, purpose of the use of theory, definition of context, main findings, and conclusion.

Data analysis

We employed the reflexive thematic analysis approach by Braun and Clarke [19] to guide the interpretive analysis of extracted data to conceptualize the context. Reflexive thematic analysis (RTA) is a qualitative research method used to identify and analyze specific patterns, i.e., themes, within data. Coding is open and organic. Themes are constructed through the researcher’s interpretive engagement with the data and are considered as the final outcome of a dynamic and iterative process [19].

Our reflexive thematic analysis process was situated along an inductive–deductive continuum.

Inductive phase

We began by extracting data that captured key information on the theoretical approaches used in included studies, including their purpose and how they conceptualized context. Through iterative engagement with this extracted data reading, comparing, reflecting, and synthesizing, we identified recurring patterns. Two researchers (MJ, AL) were involved in coding, and differences in interpretation were discussed in regular meetings with a third researcher (SA) to reach consensus. From this process, we identified two main themes with various subthemes.

Deductive phase

These inductively derived themes were subsequently interpreted through a deductive lens informed by few key theoretical contributions in implementation science to understand how the context operates and how its features influence implementation. Drawing on Pfadenhauer and colleagues’ [5] conceptualization of context as multi-dimensional and dynamic, Bate’s [3] argument that context is constitutive of implementation, and Kislov et al.’s [20] emphasis on theorizing context as emergent and relational, this interpretive process applied a set of overarching analytic lenses rather than generating additional themes.

Reflexivity

Our team comprised researchers from multidisciplinary backgrounds, including experts in implementation science, epidemiology, health sciences, and sociology. Team discussions were central to challenging disciplinary assumptions and diverse ways of conceptualizing context. Arriving at the dimensions and features of the proposed framework was an iterative process involving multiple rounds of discussion and several drafts. These reflexive engagements ensured that the framework integrated empirical insights with theoretical rigor and reflected a nuanced understanding of context.

Characteristics of included studies

A total of 37 articles were included in the review, focusing on theoretical approaches used to understand context in the implementation of health interventions in schools. The theoretical approaches were utilized at various stages of implementation, predominantly for evaluation (n = 24), planning (n = 6), and adaptation (n = 6), and, in a few cases, they were also applied for delivery (n = 3), scaling up (n = 1), and sustainability (n = 1). Out of the 37 articles included, 25 papers used qualitative methods, while others utilized mixed methods to study implementation. All studies were conducted in school settings; only one intervention was delivered online via a computer-based platform, whereas the others were delivered in person or through school-based activities. Of these 37 included papers, 36 studies were conducted in high-income countries—including the USA, Sweden, the UK, Norway, and Australia with only one study conducted in Pakistan. Implementers across studies included teachers, peers, parents, school staff, school nurses, community health professionals, family therapists, social workers, subject teachers, school leaders, principals, and special tutors. Detailed information on individual studies is provided in Supplementary File 4.

Across the theoretical approaches used in the reviewed papers, a consistent pattern emerged in how context was conceptualized. Context was most often framed in terms of conditions within schools (e.g., school size, school culture, and school administration), conditions beyond schools (e.g., broader social and political contexts, policies, and resource availability), and relationships among actors and networks involved in implementation (e.g., teachers, students, parents, and their interactions).The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) emerged as the most frequently applied framework, used in 15 of the included studies to identify contextual barriers and facilitators for implementation. Other commonly used frameworks included the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) applied in 3 studies, and the Exploration, Preparation, Implementation, Sustainment (EPIS) framework, utilized in 2 studies. Notably, in these frameworks, context was often embedded within certain determinants or domains rather than conceptualized as an independent category with a clear and consistent definition. Each framework operationalized context through list of elements, often categorized as internal and external factors. For instance, in CFIR, it was captured under “inner” and “outer settings; in the TDF, it was represented as “environment”; and EPIS addressed it through “implementation climate.” Given the breadth and complexity of factors encompassed within these domains, the included studies often focused on selected contextual aspects, which may result in an incomplete representation of context and contribute to its treatment as a largely static construct.

Beyond these widely used frameworks, several studies employed a range of other theoretical approaches, such as organizational theory, systems theory, and actor-network theory, that emphasized the dynamic and multifaceted nature of context. Additional approaches, including active implementation framework, super-setting approach framework, and personal network analysis, drew attention to other dimensions of the context by highlighting interrelationships among schools, children, and their families embedded in social, environmental, and cultural contexts. Furthermore, although only a few studies applied a theoretical lens to guide cultural adaptations of interventions, those that did demonstrated how context played a central role in shaping implementation processes and outcomes.

Reflexive thematic analysis to develop a conceptual framework

Through inductive and deductive reflexive thematic analysis of the theoretical approaches outlined above as well as other relevant literature, we identified two overarching themes with various subthemes that allowed for conceptualization of context and the exploration of how it operates within the school setting. The first theme illustrated the contextual dimensions while the second theme captured the features of context. Building on this understanding, we developed a conceptual framework that synthesized the findings in a visual format.

Theme 1: Dimensions of Context

The first theme, dimensions of context, emerged through the inductive RTA. These dimensions described the different layers of context within which key factors influencing implementation were situated. Together, they captured what context comprised by outlining the organizational, socio-cultural, economic, and political layers that shaped implementation. Context in the reviewed studies was often understood through these dimensions.

Organizational

The organizational dimension pertained to the internal structures and functioning of the school. It included factors such as school size, administrative hierarchy, institutional practices, and patterns of internal communication and relationships. It reflected the structural and relational features that shaped implementation within the school organization.

Many included studies focused on the organizational context of the schools, with fifteen studies applying CFIR [21–36], which conceptualized context primarily through its “inner setting.” This included structural and organizational elements such as school size, stability, leadership, school culture, and implementation climate defined as receptivity of the organization to an intervention, including readiness for change, compatibility, and priority for implementation. Other frameworks also addressed organizational and individual factors within schools. EPIS [40, 41] and TDF [38] emphasized planning, policy, partnerships, and readiness, while the Active Implementation Framework [43] highlighted organizational and leadership drivers. The Socio-Ecological Model [49] considered curriculum and school environment, and approaches such as the Interactive Systems Framework, Implementation Fidelity Measurement Framework [53], and R = MC2 heuristic framework [56], Innovation Framework [42], addressed organizational structures, staff characteristics, communication patterns, and relationships among staff and stakeholders.

This perspective emphasized both the formal structures like decision-making and resource allocation and the relational dynamics among school staff, district administrators, community partners, and families. Organizational Change Theory further broadened this view by highlighting how environmental factors, individual motivation, and systemic relationships interacted to shape a school’s capacity to adapt, improve, and sustain change [44].

Approaches such as Actor-Network Theory (ANT) and Personal NetworkAnalysis brought individual networks to the forefront of contextual understanding [46, 47]. ANT posited that both human and non-human entities were interconnected in networks that shaped social phenomena. In the context of school-based health interventions, ANT helped map the complex relationships between stakeholders (teachers, students, parents, policymakers) and the tools and resources they engaged with [46].

Socio-cultural

This dimension encompassed the cultural values, beliefs, norms, traditions, and social relationships that existed both within the school environment and in broader society. It reflected how societal expectations and cultural practices influenced individual interactions and behaviors.

Understanding context was crucial when implementing an intervention in a new setting. Theoretical approaches and frameworks such as the Framework for Cultural Adaptations and FRAME (Framework to Report Adaptation and Modifications-Expanded) emphasized the importance of tailoring interventions to align with local cultural values, beliefs, and practices. These frameworks guided how interventions could be modified to enhance relevance, acceptability, and sustainability in varied school settings [57].

The broader socio-cultural structures not only shaped public and participant perceptions of health promotion programs but also directly affected their practical execution and long-term sustainability of the intervention [37, 39, 45, 52].

Individual actors, their cultural beliefs, values, behaviors, and the social networks they were part of were crucial contextual factors that influenced the implementation and effectiveness of interventions. Theoretical approaches from behavioral sciences emphasized that individual behavior was shaped by environmental factors and social interactions, positioning social and cultural environment of the actors and their networks central to implementation processes [49, 56]. Bronfenbrenner’s Ecological Systems Theory [37] considered interrelationships among actors and structures across multiple levels with a focus on relationships between the individual and their immediate settings such as their family, peers, school, religious community, and neighborhood. The super setting approach framework [45] highlighted families’ embeddedness in social and cultural contexts.

These social attributes were not static; they evolved through learning processes among the involved actors, which were closely linked to changes in program design and activities over time. Additionally, the values, interests, and power dynamics of these actors played a direct role in the adoption, adaptation, and sustainability of health programs. Systems theory was grounded in the understanding that different components of a system were in constant interaction, collectively forming a complex and dynamic whole [48]. Health promotion programs operated within this complexity, where individual factors and larger social structures continuously influenced one another [52, 55].

Political

This dimension of context captured the broader political environment in which the school operated. It included national and local governance systems, legislation, public policy, power dynamics, and political ideologies that shaped educational priorities, resource allocation, and institutional autonomy, and expectations placed on schools. These political structures influenced how interventions were authorized, implemented, and sustained within educational settings.

Several theoretical approaches emphasized the importance of this outer political context. The EPIS framework [40, 41] and the TDF [38] addressed system-level factors such as state policies, government priorities, and the broader political environment. Government legislation played a critical role in determining how health programs were implemented and maintained within schools [39]. Similarly, the Professional Development Model [51] highlighted the influence of ministries’ directives, policies, and administrative support on school-based health promotion initiatives. The Innovation Framework also emphasized the role of school health policy in shaping implementation processes. However, this dimension was often implicitly addressed rather than explicitly articulated as a separate contextual dimension.

Economic

The economic context referred to the financial and material resources available to the school. It included funding mechanisms, budgeting practices, and the broader economic conditions at national or regional levels that shaped the school’s capacity to function and implement interventions. Macro level economic structures influenced the budget allocations, resources availability, and time-related resources within schools. This dimension was reflected in several implementation frameworks, including the TDF [53], which accounted for economic context; EPIS [39], which considered funding sources; Innovation Framework [45], which highlighted available time and budget; and Conceptual Framework for School Based Physical Activity Promotion [51], which emphasized the influence of macro- and meso-level economic structures.

Theme 2: Features of Context

The second theme, features of context, was derived partly from the inductive reflexive thematic analysis but primarily through deductive interpretation. These features captured the ways in which context actively shaped implementation processes, influencing what activities took place, how they were carried out, and the interactions between different actors and resources. Context was not static; it shifted and evolved over time, responding to changes in the environment, the needs and actions of participants, and the broader social, cultural, and political landscape. While these features shaped implementation processes, they were often not explicitly examined or articulated in studies’ conceptualizations of context, remaining largely implicit in how context was understood.

Interconnected

This feature of context, interconnected, reflected the idea that context does not operate in isolation but was always in relation to the other dimensions of context. It highlighted the importance of understanding context as a network of interrelated factors rather than separate, standalone elements. This perspective was reflected in Systems Theory, which was grounded in the understanding that different components of a system were in constant interaction, collectively forming a complex and dynamic whole [50]. Similarly, Bronfenbrenners Ecological Systems Theory [37] conceptualized context as a set of interrelated actors and structures operating across multiple levels, highlighting relationships between individuals and their immediate settings, such as family, peers, schools, religious communities, and neighborhoods.

Non-linear

This subtheme, non-linear, captured the reality that implementation was not a straightforward or predictable process [54]. It involved iterative learning, adaptation, and the potential for unexpected developments across all phases. Theoretical approaches and frameworks, such as the Framework for Cultural Adaptations and FRAME, reflected the dynamic and evolving nature of school contexts, highlighting the need for continuous adaptation throughout the implementation process [57].

Dynamic

The dynamic feature of context highlighted that context evolved over time, requiring continuous assessment, reflection, and responsiveness throughout the implementation journey. According to systems theory, the various components of a system continuously influenced one another, resulting in a complex and dynamic whole [48].

Spatial

The spatial aspect of context referred to the geographical settings within which educational institutions, such as schools, were situated. This included local, regional, and national contexts, each of which shaped implementation through distinct policy environments, governance structures, and resource distributions. Considering spatial aspects underscored how context varied across settings and how interventions were influenced by the locations in which they were enacted.

Temporal

The temporal aspect of context related to the timing, sequencing, and duration of interventions and implementation processes. Educational initiatives were shaped by when they were introduced and how they unfolded over time. Attention to temporal aspects highlighted the evolving nature of context and the need for ongoing adaptation across different phases of implementation.

Synthesis—Contextual Framework for School Implementation

Building on both inductive and deductive RTA, we propose a Contextual Framework for School Implementation (CFSI), which is described in Fig. 2. In the framework, the context refers to multilevel factors that may influence the implementation of an intervention in a school setting. There we further conceptualize context as a dynamic, complex, non-linear, and multifaceted process encompassing socio-cultural, political, economic, organizational dimensions embedded in spatial, and temporal aspects. The framework is visually represented as a circular model to emphasize the evolving and interconnected nature of context. Unlike many existing approaches that primarily focus on internal factors, CFSI gives equal attention to internal contextual features that shape implementation complexity. It offers a comprehensive and integrative perspective by organizing context into four interrelated dimensions: socio-cultural, organizational, political, economic, and highlights three defining features: interconnectedness, non-linearity, and dynamism and overarching elements such as spatial and temporal.Fig. 2Contextual Framework for School Implementation (CFSI)

Practical guidance on the use of the framework

The key features of the context described in the framework must be considered in relation to their interactions with the outer layer of the various dimensions in order to effectively address the context in school health implementation. We present spatial and temporal aspects of the context as overarching elements that intersect with all other contextual factors. The temporal aspect relates to the timing of interventions and implementation processes, recognizing that educational initiatives are influenced by the time in which they are executed. The spatial dimension considers the geographical context local, regional, and national within which educational practices and policies are enacted.

This framework bridges a knowledge gap in the existing literature by bringing together contextual dimensions (what context includes) and features (how context operates) within a single conceptual structure. It is operationalized through a set of practical guiding questions (Table 2) that span the planning, design, and implementation phases. These questions help users systematically consider context from the outset, reinforcing the premise that implementation is not a discrete or subsequent phase, but an integral component of program development. Discussion

Our findings reveal that while the theoretical approaches utilized in understanding the context in school implementation offer valuable insights into its various dimensions, their application remains fragmented, isolated, and methodologically heterogeneous. As a result, there is a lack of a cohesive and integrative understanding of context within the existing theoretical landscape. This fragmentation underscores the need for a comprehensive yet clear conceptualization of context that can support more consistent and effective application in school health promotion research and practice.

Although school health interventions often consider multiple levels of context, these are frequently treated as static and in isolation from the core features of the concept, such as complexity, dynamism, and interconnectedness. While identifying what constitutes context is important, it is equally critical to study what its characteristics are, how it is constructed, evolve, and influences intervention and implementation processes. A deeper understanding of these characteristics is necessary to explore the impact of context.

The approach of schools as social complex adaptive systems provide important insights into context as a complex process in school settings. According to this approach, schools are complex, dynamic systems shaped by numerous and heterogeneous components. These components can be divided into formally structured elements like school policies, curriculum, and environment, and abstract elements like school ethos and social environment [58]. Our findings indicate that schools’ collective behavior depends on interactions between various components such as rules, interactions, information, context, time, other systems’ actions, and the availability of resources. School health is shaped by interplay of these factors. In non-linear systems like schools, small changes can lead to large effects and vice versa. There is an element of unpredictability, making it challenging to predict exact outcomes because the relationships and interactions between various factors are not straightforward. The dynamic nature of schools means that systems and processes are constantly evolving, and health promotion programs must evolve accordingly. This evolving nature demands that health promotion programs adapt continuously, with implementation and evaluation processes accounting for these dynamics. In this view, context encompasses all aspects of the school system that may influence implementation.

The use of theoretical approaches across different phases of implementation presents an opportunity to move beyond their current, often limited application typically confined to evaluation. A more integrated use of theory across all stages could enhance understanding and practice [20].

Emerging perspectives on context increasingly challenge the conventional notion of context as a fixed entity, which results in a predictable and overly simplistic understanding of context as lists of key factors or influences with little academic benefit. Instead, these perspectives define context as a dynamic, fluid process embedded in a system’s intrinsic “situatedness,” shifting from “context” to “contextualizing.” This approach emphasizes the ongoing interactions between actors and their environment and their constant need to adjust and adapt to these changes [3].

In advancing a more nuanced and holistic understanding of context, the conceptual framework proposed in this review builds on the insight that context is dynamic and constantly evolving. Several context-sensitive frameworks in implementation science such as the Context and Implementation of Complex Interventions (CICI) framework [59], Integrated Promoting Action on Research Implementation in Health Services (i-PARIHS) framework [60], and Normalization Process Theory (NPT) [61] offer valuable ways of examining contextual factors. CICI foregrounds contextual domains; i-PARIHS highlights facilitation, recipients, and context; NPT examines the social mechanisms through which implementation work is accomplished. Yet in practice, these frameworks are often operationalize context as a set of domains assessed at baseline or at discrete points, which can unintentionally position context as a background condition rather than a dynamic factor to be revisited across planning, design, delivery, and evaluation.

A further challenge with domain-based conceptualizations of context is that domains themselves are often broad categories encompassing multiple elements. In practice, this can lead users to prioritize certain domains over others. For example, in school-based implementation research, our review found that CFIR is the most frequently used framework, with disproportionate emphasis placed on the “inner setting” (i.e., organizational context), often at the expense of other contextual dimensions. While organizational factors are undoubtedly important, schools are embedded within wider social, political, economic structures and operate as complex adaptive systems where contextual conditions are dynamic, interconnected, and continuously evolving. Focusing narrowly on a single domain risk obscuring these broader contextual influences.

In contrast, our framework conceptualizes context as a dynamic and relational process that evolves throughout implementation. Context is neither a strategy nor an outcome; rather, it both shapes and is shaped by implementation processes. The CFSI framework does not engage with implementation strategies, mechanisms of change, or implementation outcomes; its focus is solely on context. It emphasizes the importance of examining how contextual conditions interact with these processes over time without conflating context with these related but distinct concepts. Reflecting this conceptualization, the guiding questions within the framework are intentionally reflexive rather than checklist-based, recognizing the qualitative, emergent, and evolving nature of context.

Therefore, understanding and addressing context must occur throughout all phases of intervention and implementation. For school health promotion programs, it is not sufficient for interventions and implementation strategies to be merely evidence-based; they must also be context-sensitive. Embracing the dimensions and features of context is essential for achieving more meaningful, sustainable, and impactful school health promotion.

Strengths and limitations

As a scoping review, this study provides a broad overview of the available evidence without assessing the quality of individual studies or comparing the strength of different theoretical approaches. While this broad mapping enabled the identification of key patterns, conceptual gaps, and areas where further research is needed across diverse contexts and frameworks, it also introduces certain limitations.

First, we limited the search to theories that had been applied in school implementation research. This may have excluded theoretical perspectives important to understanding context, such as the Normalization Process Theory. It would be important to consider including these theories in future research. Notably, the review excluded grey literature such as policy documents and reports. Although this decision was consistent with the review’s focus on developing theoretical understanding of context in school implementation across diverse global settings, future research could draw on grey literature to support the operationalization of the proposed framework within specific school settings. The review also included only studies published in English, which may have resulted in missing important theoretical insights from non-English-speaking regions on how context is defined and addressed in school health implementation.

Although some relevant studies may have been missed, the iterative refinement of the search strategy helped ensure a thorough and representative overview of the literature. The process of organizing and interpreting the findings inevitably involves a degree of subjectivity; however, this allowed for a nuanced synthesis that reflects the complexity of implementation in school health promotion.

Of the 37 studies reviewed, 36 were conducted in high-income countries. While this limits the transferability of findings, it clearly highlights the need for more evidence from low- and middle-income settings. Although the framework is intended as a conceptual lens rather than a prescriptive checklist, its dimensions may manifest differently across contexts. For example, the relationship between school and society, governance structures, and resource constraints can vary substantially between high- and low- and middle-income countries. This could be explored in future research to examine how the framework translates to diverse settings and whether adaptation is needed to extend its applicability globally.

Despite these limitations, the study has several strengths. A key strength of this study lies in its dual contribution: it not only maps how context is understood across various theoretical approaches in school health promotion implementation, but also interprets these perspectives propose a new, integrated conceptual framework. This framework deepens understanding of context by offering a comprehensive structure, accompanied by practical guiding questions for researchers, practitioners, and policymakers. Additional strengths include a comprehensive and systematically developed search strategy, as well as the involvement of a multidisciplinary global team. Each member brought diverse perspectives that enriched the analysis and interpretation of findings.

Conclusion

Context plays a critical role in implementation. While various theoretical approaches have been used to address context in school health promotion implementation, inconsistencies remain in how it is defined and applied. By examining a wide range of theoretical approaches from implementation science and other disciplines, this review underscores the centrality of context and its interconnectedness with intervention planning, delivery, and evaluation. While numerous implementation frameworks address aspects of context, they often present fragmented or partial understandings. This review addresses that gap by proposing a dedicated framework focused specifically on the role of context in school health promotion.

The findings highlight the limitations of fragmented or level-based interpretations and advocate for a more comprehensive and nuanced view. In response, we propose a conceptual framework that synthesizes key contextual dimensions such as socio-cultural, organizational, political, and economic alongside core features including dynamism, interconnectedness, and non-linearity. Rather than reiterating existing models, the framework encourages critical reflection and supports a more holistic and actionable understanding of context. It offers a valuable tool to guide future research, practice, and policy in implementation of school health promotion, with potential applications in both planning and evaluation phases.

The review opens important avenues for future research. With a broadened and more comprehensive understanding of context, it becomes increasingly valuable to explore how context influences actual implementation outcomes. Applying and refining the proposed framework will be an important next step and may yield meaningful insights for improving health promotion efforts in diverse educational environments.

Overall, the review offers meaningful implications. For researchers, it emphasizes the importance of critically evaluating theoretical approaches and paying closer attention to the role of context in implementation processes. For practitioners and policymakers, it reinforces the need to tailor school health promotion programs to the specific realities of the schools and communities they aim to serve.
