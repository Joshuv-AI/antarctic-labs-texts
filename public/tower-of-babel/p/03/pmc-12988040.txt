Microplastics exposures in cataract surgery and potential clinical concerns
Authors: Yun-Hee Choi, Myung-Sun Song, Nayoon Park, Seul-Ah Park, Jaehak Jung, Yoon-Hyeong Choi, Dong Hyun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicroplastic (MP) exposures have been a significant global public health concern. Cataract surgery is the most frequently performed ocular surgery worldwide, and various plastic tools are used in cataract surgery. This study aimed to evaluate the characteristics of MPs in surgical devices, intraocular fluids, and cataractous crystalline lenses in cataract surgery. We collected surgical solutions/devices (irrigation saline, balanced salt solution [BSS], and ophthalmic viscosurgical device [OVD]), ocular inflow/outflow fluids from six phacoemulsification cases, cataractous crystalline lenses from two extracapsular cataract extraction patients, and operating room (OR) air samples. Numbers and characteristics of MPs were measured using raman spectroscopy and surface morphologies of MPs were measured using scanning electron microscopy with energy dispersive spectroscopy. There were no MPs in saline or BSS in syringes nor in OVD. A small number of MPs were detected in ocular inflow fluids (mean ± standard deviation [SD] = 0.1 ± 0.1/mL) and OR air (mean ± SD = 0.5 ± 0.3/mL). Higher concentrations were observed in intraocular outflow fluid, with one plastic bag-collected sample showing 50.4 ± 0.3/mL, compared with 1.5(± 1.4)–7.2(± 3.5)/mL in other patients whose samples were collected in glass containers. MPs were also detected in cataractous crystalline lens (mean ± SD = 12.3 ± 5.8/100 mg). Ocular inflow fluids contained only 10–19 μm polyethylene (PE) and polypropylene (PP) fragments. However, intraocular outflow fluids and cataractous crystalline lenses contained smaller 5–9 μm fragments and fibers of six polymer types: PE, PP, polystyrene (PS), polycarbonate (PC), polyethylene terephthalate (PET), and polyamide (PA). Patients who had other ocular diseases within past three years or who had diabetes at the time of surgery showed higher concentrations of total MPs and PE (P < 0.05). MPs can be exposed into eyeball during cataract surgery, potentially from surgical instruments or indoor air. Introduction

Plastic pollution as a major public health issue is also becoming an important concern in healthcare facilities1,2. Cataract is a leading cause of blindness and severe visual impairment worldwide3, and cataract surgery is the most frequently performed operation in ophthalmology. In 2019, 29 million cataract surgeries were performed worldwide, and the number is expected to increase to 50 million by 20504. Numerous plastic surgical supplies such as disposable infusion bags, syringes, and phaco machines are used in cataract surgery5. Therefore, plastic contamination is also possible during cataract surgery.

Microplastics (MPs), plastic particles with size < 5 mm, are ubiquitously distributed in atmospheric and aquatic environments and the human body6,7. MPs with sizes of 10–500 μm easily penetrate the human body, spread throughout the whole body within 1 h, and accumulate in organs at 5 times the original amount after 48 hours8,9. MP accumulation in the human body can induce inflammation, oxidative stress, tissue damage, and genotoxicity10,11. Previous epidemiological studies have detected MPs in cardiovascular and respiratory organs, the liver, spleen, and the brain, and suggested that MPs may cause disorders in these organs8.

Current studies detecting MPs in human samples primarily consider inhalation, dermal contact, and dietary intake as exposure routes for MPs12. The eye, being an organ directly exposed to the external environment, can also be exposed to MPs from the atmosphere or via the blood stream13. However, little research has investigated the potential ocular exposure route for MPs. Only one study has revealed that the use of contact lenses may lead to MP exposure in the eyes14. Many plastic medical devices are used in cataract surgery; therefore MPs may be introduced directly into the eyeball. In this study, we investigated the quantity and characteristics of MPs in surgical devices and media and explored the possibility of MP pollution in cataract surgery.

Results

Quantity of microplastics (MPs) detected in cataract surgery

No MPs were detected in saline, BSS in syringes, or OVD (Table 1). MPs were found in ocular inflow fluids, intraocular outflow fluids, cataractous crystalline lenses, and indoor OR air. After sampling blank subtraction adjustments, the mean(± SD) MP number concentration was 0.1(± 0.1)/mL in ocular inflow fluids, compared with 50.4(± 0.3)/mL in the outflow fluid from patient 1 and 1.5(± 1.4)–7.2(± 3.5)/mL in those from patients 2–6. In cataractous crystalline lenses, MP concentrations ranged 0.07 to 0.18/mg across two patients. Compared to inflow fluid, mean MP number concentrations of outflow fluid were significantly higher across all six patients (p < 0.05). Indoor OR air had a mean(± SD) number concentration of 0.5(± 0.3)/m2/h.

Morphological and polymeric characteristics of MPs

Comparing shapes, only fragments were found in ocular inflow fluids, while fragments and fibers appeared in some intraocular outflow fluids (detection frequency [DF]: 2.2–6.5% in patients 1–4 and 6), cataractous crystalline lenses (DF: 22.2% in patient 7), and indoor OR air (DF: 14.6%) (Fig. 1A). For sizes, only 10–19 μm MPs were found in ocular inflow fluids, while smaller 5–9 μm MPs were additionally found in all intraocular outflow fluids (DF: 8.9–33.7% in patients 1–6), cataractous crystalline lenses (DF: 11.1% and 53.8% in patients 7 and 8), and indoor OR air (DF: 14.3%) (Fig. 1B). For polymer types, only PP (DF: 50%) and PE (DF: 50%) were found in ocular inflow fluids. However, PET (DF: 0.1–2.2% in patients 1 and 6), PS (DF: 0.3–30.4% in patients 1, 2, 4–6), and PC (DF: 11.3–26.1% in patients 4–6) were additionally found in intraocular outflow fluids. In cataractous crystalline lenses, PP (DF: 69.2–88.9% in patients 7 and 8), PS (DF: 7.7% in patient 8), and PA (DF: 11.1–23.1% in patients 7 and 8) were found. In indoor OR air, PP (DF: 85.7%) and PET (DF: 14.4%) were found (Fig. 1C). Overall trends in fragment/fiber distribution, size range, and polymer composition remained consistent after excluding the plastic bag–collected outflow sample (patient 1).

Patients’ clinical information and MPs observed in their intraocular outflow fluid

Among eight cataract patients, seven had other ocular diseases and all had systemic diseases (Supplementary Table S1). None had a history of intravitreal injections or glaucoma-related procedures, although one patient had acute glaucoma managed medically. All patients reported preoperative use of artificial tears, with no prior history of contact lens use.

Only one phaco-patient had diabetes (HbA1c ≥ 6.5%) at surgery, while all had normal blood pressure (Fig. 4A). Among the six phaco-patients, those with other ocular diseases showed significantly higher PE levels compared to those without (p = 0.027) (Fig. 4B). Among patients with other ocular diseases, those diagnosed within the past three years showed significantly higher total MPs and PE concentrations than those without recent diseases (all p < 0.05) (Fig. 4C). The diabetic patient showed significantly higher total MPs and PE concentrations (all p < 0.05) (Fig. 4D).

Potential sources of identified MPs

To identify potential MP sources, we compared polymer types of MPs in ocular inflow, outflow, and cataractous crystalline lenses with those of surgical devices used during cataract procedures (Table S2). In ocular inflow samples, PP was likely introduced from BSS bags connected to the phaco machine, while PE may have originated from the phaco machine and infusion lines. In ocular outflow samples, PE was similarly attributable to the phaco machine and infusion tubing, whereas PP have been introduced from saline or BSS bags, syringes, and intraocular lens injectors. PET was not identified in surgical devices and may have originated from indoor air. PS, also unmatched to device polymers, was considered likely pre-existing within the patients’ ocular environment. In cataractous crystalline lenses, PP may have been introduced via saline or BSS bags and syringes used during surgery, whereas PS and PA were not linked to surgical device polymers and were therefore likely present prior to surgery. Additionally, as all patients had used artificial tears prior to surgery (Table S1), pre-existing exposure to MPs such as PS and PA via this route cannot be excluded.

Discussion

This study examined MP contamination in surgical solutions, patient samples, and OR air in cataract surgery. Higher MP concentrations were found in intraocular outflow fluid and cataractous crystalline lenses versus inflow fluid and OR air. Ocular inflow fluids contained only fragments, 10–19 μm particles, and PE or PP polymers. In contrast, intraocular outflow fluids and cataractous crystalline lenses exhibited a more diverse profile, including fibers, 5–9 μm particles, and a broader composition including PE, PP, PS, PET, PC, and PA. While MPs may come from surgical instruments or OR air, significant amounts likely already exist in the eye and are expelled during surgery.

Previous studies have primarily reported that MPs are exposed through inhalation, dermal contact, and oral ingestion15–17, and ocular exposure is documented only in cases involving contact lens use and artificial tear applications14,18. Our findings suggest that the eyes can be exposed to MPs in cataract surgery. When comparing the polymer types of detected MPs with those of surgical equipment or indoor OR air, our results indicate that potential ocular MP exposure pathways during cataract surgery involve the phaco machine, plastic infusion bag, line, and indoor air (Supplementary Table S2). Similarly, one study has identified MPs in indoor OR air, with a concentration of 1,924 ± 3,105 particles m−² day− 1 during a 12-hour surgical period19. Moreover, previous studies have reported that intravenous administration through plastic infusion bag, tube, and bottle can directly expose the bloodstream to MPs20,21. One study on 23 coronary artery patients revealed that the use of plastic surgical instruments during percutaneous coronary interventions significantly increased intravascular MP levels from 4.96 ± 3.40 particles/10 mL before the procedure to 93.57 ± 35.95 particles/10 mL after the procedure22. Cataract surgery also uses plastic infusion sets for intraocular administration; therefore, direct MP exposure to the eye from surgical equipment can occur. Furthermore, in this study, MPs entering the eye through the plastic infusion tube were 1 particle/10 mL. Considering that approximately 50–180 mL of BSS flows into the eye through a phaco machine during cataract surgery, this indicates an exposure of 5–18 particles per 50–180 mL23. This quantity is higher than those reported in infusion bags (1–2 particles per 500 mL) used for intravenous administration20.

Substantial MP concentrations were observed in the intraocular fluid and cataractous lenses. These MPs may originate from pre-existing MPs in the patients’ ocular lens or intraocular fluid. One study of 49 patients with ocular diseases found 1,745 MP particles in their vitreous humor, demonstrating correlations between MP presence and intraocular pressure, aqueous humor opacities, and retinopathy23,24. This study observed MP concentrations ranging from 1.5 ± 1.4/mL to 50.4 ± 0.3/mL in the intraocular fluid and 0.07 to 0.18/mg in the cataractous crystalline lenses. Notably, significantly higher concentrations of total MP and PE were detected in patients who had been diagnosed with ocular diseases within the past three years or who had diabetes at the time of surgery. This may be attributed to an impaired blood aqueous or retinal barrier, potentially resulting from previous presence of ocular diseases25. Moreover, diabetes, which weakens both systemic and ocular vasculature, may impair the clearance of MPs from the blood vessels by increasing blood viscosity26. Although statistical significance was marginally limited due to the small sample size, the observed high correlation between MPs and DBP (r = 0.77, p = 0.077) suggests that MPs (Supplementary Table S3), in conjunction with inadequate blood supply, may adversely affect ocular health. Although none of the enrolled patients reported prior contact lens use, all had a history of using topical ophthalmic medications, including single-use and multi-use eye drops, which may represent an additional potential source of ocular MP exposure (Supplementary Table S1).

MPs detected in intraocular fluids or cataractous lenses may potentially impact ocular health through several biological mechanisms. Primary MP pathological mechanisms include inflammatory response and oxidative stress27. This may influence numerous ocular diseases associated with these mechanisms, such as uveitis, dry eye diseases, cataracts, glaucoma, and age-related macular degeneration28 and could exacerbate adverse effects with vascular and systemic disorders. Cataracts develop from damaged lens proteins, with risk increasing as oxidative damage accumulates during aging29. Inflammatory processes can damage the lens by increasing oxidative stress through cytokines and prostaglandins30. MPs may influence these mechanisms, potentially damaging lens proteins and cell membranes, thereby affecting cataract pathogenesis. Previous studies showed MPs are associated with aqueous humor opacities, which cause protein strand entanglement24. Since aqueous humor opacities are early cataract risk factors31, MPs may contribute to cataract development. Furthermore, previous study documented that MPs in anterior chamber aqueous humor can flow into vitreous humor and diffuse to retinal tissue32. Therefore, MPs may penetrate the blood-retinal barrier into the posterior segment, elevating inflammatory markers and reactive oxygen species levels within the retina, potentially causing retinal cellular damage.

Prevalent MPs in ocular fluids of cataract patients may significantly affect post-surgical complications. First, MPs can contribute to toxic anterior segment syndrome (TASS), an acute, aseptic anterior segment inflammation following surgery. Cases of TASS characterized by localized corneal endothelial damage are specifically termed toxic endothelial cell destruction syndrome (TECDS). TASS typically results from residual toxic substances on surgical instruments (e.g., medical gauze fibers or organic toxins), as well as impurities in ophthalmic medications or eye drops used during the procedure33. MPs in intraocular fluid may contribute to TASS and TECDS pathogenesis by inducing inflammatory responses. Second, residual MPs in the anterior chamber after cataract surgery can induce inflammation that may traverse the vitreous to the macula, potentially resulting in postsurgical cystoid macular edema (CME). CME results from cystic intraretinal fluid accumulation in the outer plexiform and inner nuclear retinal layers due to blood-retinal barrier breakdown34. Since MPs can cross the blood-retinal barrier32, intraocular MPs may contribute to CME development. Further investigation into MPs’ role in surgical outcomes is warranted.

Based on our study results, we propose the following measures to reduce plastic usage during ocular surgery. First, medical device manufacturers should develop alternative medical packaging materials utilizing bioplastics. According to a study conducted at a German hospital, plastic packaging waste generated per patient amounts to 16 g daily35. Therefore, there is a need to develop and implement medical packaging materials incorporating bioplastic alternatives such as cellulose, starch, and chitin36. Second, surgeons should use silicone instruments when possible and rinse plastic products with purified water before use. Finally, to reduce airborne MPs in ORs, this study suggests ensuring adequate ventilation through proper air handling units and ventilation systems in operation.

This study has several limitations. First, we couldn’t collect ocular fluids before and after surgery from the same patient due to sterility requirements and standard surgical workflow constraints. Inflow fluids were therefore collected as representative samples, whereas outflow fluids were collected on a per-patient basis. Nevertheless, triplicate experiments consistently showed higher MP concentrations in outflow than inflow fluids. Second, one outflow sample was collected using a plastic bag due to surgical constraints. Although polymer types beyond plastic bag-related materials (PP and PE) were detected in the sample and sensitivity analysis did not alter the overall results, potential procedural bias cannot be fully excluded; therefore, the findings should be interpreted cautiously. Third, sampling during intraocular lens implantation was not included, although the acrylic intraocular lens is designed as a stable solid implant and is unlikely to generate microplastics under routine surgical conditions. Fourth, Raman spectroscopy’s technical limits prevented detection of MPs smaller than 5 μm. Finally, we used devices from one manufacturer to avoid material variability. While different manufacturers use varying materials and processes that might affect MP production, similar plastics suggest no significant differences in MP generation are expected across manufacturers.

Conclusions

This is the first study to investigate MPs in cataract surgery. Although the number is small, MPs can enter eyes during cataract surgery from surgical instruments or operating room air. Significant MPs may already exist in cataractous crystalline lenses or intraocular fluid. We recommend creating sustainable surgical environments to minimize MP exposure and identifying MP-related disease mechanisms in ocular diseases. Our findings suggest potential MP contamination concerns across all surgical departments.

Methods

Cataract surgical procedures and sample collection

We investigated MP contamination in two cataract surgeries: (1) phacoemulsification (standard procedure) and (2) extracapsular cataract extraction (ECCE) for hypermature cataracts. We examined all surgical steps involving plastic devices (Fig. 5). First, we irrigated the ocular surface with sterile saline from a plastic syringe for better visualization. Second, we applied viscoelastic materials from an ophthalmic viscosurgical device (OVD) into the anterior chamber to facilitate surgery. Third, we performed intraocular manipulation and irrigation with balanced salt solution (BSS)-filled syringes. For phacoemulsification, we examined phaco machine use for BSS infusion and fluid drainage to maintain intraocular pressure. All phacoemulsification procedures were performed using the same phacoemulsification system (CENTURION® Vision System, Alcon), with standard disposable tubing sets and irrigation–aspiration (I&A) handpieces supplied by the manufacturer used consistently across all cases. I&A was performed using a coaxial technique. Both phacoemulsification and I&A shared the same infusion line for inflow fluid delivery; therefore, inflow fluid exposure was identical across these stages. Following lens removal, a foldable acrylic intraocular lens (Tecnis Eyhance™, model ICB00) was implanted using an injector system (TECNIS Simplicity® System). For ECCE, we examined whole cataractous crystalline lenses manually extracted using stainless steel instruments (e.g., vectis and lens loop). Finally, we considered indoor operating room (OR) air as a potential MP exposure source.

Human and indoor air samples were collected during cataract surgeries performed by one ophthalmologist (DH Kim). During phacoemulsification, we collected: (1) 10 mL ocular inflow fluid from BSS, collected directly from the plastic infusion line exiting a phaco machine prior to surgery from three patients; and (2) 10 mL intraocular outflow fluid, collected after phacoemulsification and I&A through plastic infusion lines and prior to entry into the phaco machine collection bag, from six patients. To further clarify the sampling points and the surgical fluid circuit, a schematic diagram of the setup has been added as Supplementary Fig. S1. All outflow fluid samples were stored in glass containers, except one initially collected in a plastic bag attached to a phaco machine and immediately transferred to a glass container. Additionally, two whole cataractous crystalline lenses nuclei were extracted during ECCE. The extracted lenses were collected intraoperatively, stored in glass containers, and processed for MP analysis using the same protocol applied to ocular fluid samples. Since outflow fluid and lens samples contained cataract debris, they were digested for seven days in 30% hydrogen peroxide. Indoor OR air was collected using lidded glass Petri dishes (50 × 15 mm) placed 1–1.5 m above floor level at three locations during surgery. The lids were removed only during the predefined sampling period and were immediately replaced thereafter to minimize external contamination during transport and handling. The glass Petri dishes were then rinsed with purified water and the suspension was collected for analysis. Surgical device details are in Supplementary Table S4.

Raman spectroscopy measurements

Quantity, morphology, and polymeric types of MPs and surgical devices were identified using an XploRA Plus confocal Raman microscope (Horiba, France). For morphological characteristics, we identified particle size (5–9, 10–19, 20–49, 50–99, or ≥ 100 μm), shape (fragment or fiber), and color. The Raman microscopy was equipped with a 532 nm laser and 1,024 × 256-pixel cooled charge-coupled device detector. Mosaic dark field images of the filter surface were obtained using an M Plan Semi Apochromat BD objective lens 20x/N.A. 0.45 (Olympus). Mosaic images were processed using Particlefinder™ module in Labspec 6 software, and all bright particles were selected against the dark background. Raman spectra were recorded with 1 × 2 s exposure time over 1,020–3,400 cm− 1. Sample spectra were searched using Labspec6 software, and polymer types were manually identified to avoid false positives or missing identifications by know-it-all spectrum matching software.

Scanning electron microscopy with energy dispersive spectroscopy (SEM/EDS) analyses

Surface morphologies of MPs were identified using scanning electron microscopy with energy dispersive spectroscopy (SEM/EDS) (JSM 7001 F, JEOL, Japan). Before analyses, samples were dried at 60℃ and platinum-coated for 100 s using a sputter coater. Double-sided carbon tape and metal tweezers were then used to ensure clear imaging under the microscope. Images were taken at 15 kV with 100×–8,000× magnification. EDS analyses were performed on selected particles to verify their composition.

Quality control

To prevent MP contamination, we wore nitrile gloves and cotton coats throughout all processing steps. We excluded all plastic tools and used glass materials that had been cleaned with filtered ultrapure water. Cataract tissues were stored in ultrapure water–rinsed glass containers that had been previously confirmed to be free of MPs. Prior to digestion, tissues were transferred into separately ultrapure water–rinsed glass beakers, and hydrogen peroxide digestion was performed exclusively in glassware. Hydrogen peroxide used for digestion was filtered through a 0.5 μm metal filter prior to use and stored in glass containers. Method blanks processed with hydrogen peroxide were included to assess potential reagent-derived contamination, and no MPs were detected in these blanks. Only one outflow fluid sample (patient 1) was collected with a plastic bag and corrected using a sampling blank. For the sampling blank, an empty plastic bag containing 10 mL of 30% hydrogen peroxide was analyzed. For all other samples and analytical processes, procedural blank tests were conducted to verify MP contamination during sampling or analytical processes. As a control for indoor air sampling, pre-cleaned glass Petri dishes were rinsed with ultrapure water and analyzed prior to use, and no MPs were detected in these blanks, confirming the absence of vessel-derived contamination. After sampling, sample pretreatment and filtration were conducted within a laminar flow box (SINAN science industry, HSCV-1300), preventing indoor MP contamination in the laboratory. All solutions (i.e., ultrapure water and chemical reagents) were filtered using a GF/F filter followed by a metal filter (0.5 μm).

For accuracy, all samples were measured in triplicate. Samples for irrigation saline, BSS, viscoelastics, and sampling blank (e.g., empty plastic bag) were measured using three products each. During phacoemulsification, one ocular inflow fluid samples was collected from BSS exiting a phaco machine in each of three surgeries, whereas three separate intraocular outflow fluid samples were collected from each of six surgeries. Indoor OR air sample was collected from three locations within the OR during three surgeries (patients 1–3). Due to the limited number of ECCE surgeries performed, only two lens nuclei samples were obtained from hypermature cataract patients.

Data analysis

The MP concentrations of samples were corrected using the sampling blank using the following methods: total number of MPs in the sample/the volume of the corresponding sample − mean number of MPs in the sampling blank regardless of polymer type/the volume of the sampling blank37. Then, they were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The shape, size, and polymer type of MPs in each scenario were presented as percentages of MPs corresponding to each category divided by total MPs. Mann-Whitney tests were used to compare the mean difference in MP concentrations between ocular inflow and outflow fluids and the mean concentration of MPs in outflow intraocular fluid of six patients according to their clinical histories. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1, R Foundation, Vienna, Austria), with significance level established at a two-sided p-value < 0.05.
