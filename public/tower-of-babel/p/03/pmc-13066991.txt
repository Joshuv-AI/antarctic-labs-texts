Corylin inhibits liver cancer cell growth through the endogenous apoptosis and mitophagy
Authors: Renshuang Zhao, Xin Wang, Mingming Cao, Yaru Li, Shanzhi Li, Zhiru Xiu, Yilong Zhu, Yiquan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCorylin is a bioactive extract of Psoralea corylifolia L. with reported antioxidant, anti-tumor, and anti-inflammatory effects. Its anti-tumor effects have been investigated, but mechanistic explanations are lacking. This study mainly focuses on the role and mechanism of Corylin in inducing liver cancer cell death.MethodsThe effects of Corylin on liver cancer cell proliferation were analyzed using crystal violet staining and cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assays. Nuclear changes were detected via Hoechst staining, while apoptosis levels were assessed using Annexin V- fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)/propidium iodide (PI) staining. Changes in mitochondrial membrane potential and reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were analyzed using JC-1 and tetramethylrhodamine methyl ester (TMRM) staining combined with flow cytometry. Immunofluorescence staining and Western blot (WB) experiments assessed alterations in apoptosis- and autophagy-related proteins. Finally, the effects of Corylin on liver cancer cells in vivo were validated by establishing a subcutaneous tumor model.ResultsIn this study, we found that Corylin had a significant anti-liver cancer effect and significantly increased the apoptosis level of liver cancer cells. Additionally, Corylin was found to significantly reduce mitochondrial membrane potential and significantly increase ROS release. It was also observed that Corylin significantly influenced autophagy levels in liver cancer cells. Further analysis revealed that Corylin induced mitophagy, with the increase in autophagy levels being directly proportional to the rise in apoptosis levels. Finally, the establishment of a subcutaneous tumor-bearing model showed that Corylin also had a significant anti-liver cancer effect in vivo.ConclusionsThe above results demonstrated that Corylin mainly caused the death of liver cancer cells through the endogenous apoptosis and mitophagy pathway and had great potential as an anti-liver cancer drug.Keywords: Psoralea corylifolia, apoptosis, autophagy, liver cancer, mitochondria

Highlight box

Key findings

• Corylin markedly suppressed liver cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion both in vitro and in vivo.

• Corylin induced endogenous apoptosis via mitochondrial dysfunction, including loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, increased reactive oxygen species production, cytochrome C release, and caspase activation.

• Corylin concurrently activated PINK/PARKIN-mediated mitophagy, which functioned as a protective mechanism that partially attenuated apoptosis.

What is known and what is new?

• Corylin, a bioactive compound extracted from Psoralea corylifolia L., has demonstrated antioxidant and anticancer properties; however, its antitumor mechanisms in liver cancer have not been fully elucidated.

• This study reveals that Corylin exerts its antitumor effects through coordinated regulation of mitochondrial apoptosis and mitophagy. Importantly, inhibition of autophagy significantly enhanced Corylin-induced apoptosis, uncovering a functional crosstalk between these two processes.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These findings suggest that protective mitophagy limits the cytotoxic efficacy of Corylin in liver cancer cells.

• Combining Corylin with autophagy inhibition may enhance therapeutic efficacy and reduce tumor cell survival.

• Corylin represents a promising candidate for further translational development as an anticancer agent derived from traditional Chinese medicine.

Introduction

According to World Health Organization (WHO) estimates, there were 870,000 new cases of liver cancer and 760,000 deaths globally in 2022, which is the cancer with the third highest incidence rate (1). Recent decades have seen decreases in global liver cancer incidence and mortality, but the 5-year survival rate is still only 12.1% (2). Liver cancer is characterized by higher morbidity and mortality and susceptibility to recurrence and metastasis than other tumor types, presenting a challenge for treatment (3). The need to improve current methods of diagnosis and treatment, which include both surgical and non-surgical options (4), is urgent. After surgery, there is still a high risk of metastasis and recurrence (5-7) and drug therapy of varying efficacy is often used for early-stage cancer (8). Fluorouracil (9) and sorafenib (10) work well for early-stage liver cancer but perform poorly for advanced disease. The search for novel liver cancer drugs with fewer side effects is a priority.

Apoptosis, or type 1 programmed cell death, consists of genetically controlled events maintaining intracellular stability (11,12). Three signaling pathways mediate it: mitochondrial, death receptor, and endoplasmic reticulum (13,14). Mitochondrial and death receptor pathways induce apoptosis via caspase-3 activation. Endogenous apoptosis is activated by internal stress (15-18). Pro-apoptotic proteins, such as cytochrome C (CytC) and HtrA2/Omi, are released into the cytoplasm, where apoptosis-activating factor-1 (APAF-1) protease forms an apoptosis complex with CytC, leading to pro-caspase-9 recruitment and activation, which activates caspase-3 and caspase-7. The resulting caspase cascade causes cleavage of cell substrates and cell death (19). Altered mitochondrial membrane potential is reflected by reactive oxygen species (ROS) release and membrane permeability changes (20). These apoptotic mechanisms can regulate tumor cells.

Autophagy is also a programmed cell death. Macroautophagy (the autophagy that we specifically refer to in this study) is a high-capacity process, which arises from the target membrane rotation protein, forms autophagy corpuscles, and then forms autophagy lysosomes, as well as degradingunnecessary internal substances and other constituents. More than 30 types of proteins and regulatory factors are involved in this process (21,22). p62 (sequestosome 1), a specific substrate of autophagy, is capable of mediating selective autophagy. The degradation of microtubule-associated proteins light chain 3 (LC3)-II and p62 is associated with the degradation of other autophagy substrate proteins, which can reflect the flux of autophagy (23).

Psoralea (Psoralea corylifolia L.) is the dried, mature fruit of the Fabaceae family, considered by traditional Chinese medicine to improve kidney health, strengthen yang, warm the spleen, stop diarrhea, and relieve asthma (24). Many pharmacological effects have been reported for the Psoralea corylifolia extract, Neobavaisoflavone, including anti-inflammatory, anti-cancer, antioxidant, inhibition of platelet aggregation, and moderate to high levels of DNA polymerase stimulation (25-27). Corylin may be isolated from the whole plant, fruit, and seeds of Psoralea corylifolia L. (28), and antioxidant and stimulation of osteoblast proliferation activities have been attributed to it (29). Corylin has also been shown to inhibit interleukin-6 (IL-6) induced signal transduction and transcriptional activator 3 (STAT3) activity in Hep3B liver cancer cells (30). These anticancer effects have been demonstrated but mechanisms by which it induces tumor cell death remain unclear.

In this study, the growth inhibition and cytotoxicity of Corylin on liver cancer cells and the involved apoptosis and autophagy pathways were studied through various experiments in vivo and in vitro. The aim was to lay a theoretical foundation for the potential development of a new Corylin-based anti-cancer treatment. We present this article in accordance with the ARRIVE and MDAR reporting checklists (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-aw-2517/rc).

Methods

Cells, chemicals, and animals

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cell lines, HCCLM3 and HepG-2, were purchased from Beina Biology Science and Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), and the organization that issued the STR report for the HCC cells is the Henan Engineering Research Center of Industrial Microbiology (Kaifeng, China): HCCLM3, BNCC342335; and HepG-2, BNCC339266. Corylin (CAS No. 53947-92-5), 3-MA (CAS No. 5142-23-4), Z-VAD-FMK (CAS No. 161401-82-7), and 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) (CAS No. 51-21-8) were purchased from MedChemExpress (MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA).

Antibodies: anti-glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA, USA; 5174), anti-beta-actin (Cell Signaling; 8457), anti-E-cadherin (Cell Signaling; 3195), anti-N-cadherin (Cell Signaling; 13116), anti-vimentin (Cell Signaling; 5741), anti-matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-2 (Cell Signaling; 40994), anti-MMP-9 (Cell Signaling; 13667), anti-cleaved caspase-3 (Cell Signaling; 9661), anti-poly(ADP)-ribose polymerase (PARP) (Cell Signaling; 5942), anti-CytC (Cell Signaling; 11940), anti-LC3 (Cell Signaling; 3868), anti-p62 (Cell Signaling; 39749), anti-Beclin-1 (Cell Signaling; 3495), anti-PINK1 (Cell Signaling; 6946), anti-Parkin (Cell Signaling; 4211, anti-cleaved caspase-9 (Cell Signaling; 9509), anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG) (H + L), biogenic antibody (Cell Signaling; 14708), anti-mouse IgG (H + L), biogenic antibody (Cell Signaling; 14709). The antibodies are used at a dilution of 1:1,000.

The BALB/c female nude mice were 4–5 weeks old and were obtained from Beijing Viton Lihua Experimental Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Six mice were randomly selected from each group after treatment and were sacrificed by cervical dislocation, while the remaining mice were euthanized with three times the standard dose of sodium pentobarbital (150 mg/kg) intraperitoneally, following the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) guidelines for animal euthanasia. The animal experiments were divided into two batches, totaling 30 mice. The first batch of BALB/c-nu: grade: SPF grade No. 11032424110256241; the second batch of BALB/c-nu: grade: SPF grade No. 11032424110662821; Laboratory Animal License No. SYXK-2018-0012. Ethical approval was granted by the Changchun University of Chinese Medicine Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) (No. 2024015). All animal experiments in this study were conducted in accordance with the Regulations for the Administration of Laboratory Animals and Guidelines for the Ethical Review of Laboratory Animal Welfare in China for the care and use of animals. A protocol was prepared before the study without registration.

Measurements of cell growth

Growth of HepG-2 and HCCLM3 cells was measured by the cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. Cells were cultured at a density of 5,000 cells per well in 96-well plates, Corylin was added at the indicated concentrations, and the CCK-8 assay was performed after 48 h. The medium was removed, and 100 µL of CCK-8 working solution (LJ621; Dojindo, Japan) was added [serum-free Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM): CCK-8 solution =9:1]. The plates were then incubated at 37 ℃ for 120 min in the dark (CO2 incubator 900EX, Wakenyaku Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) Optical density was measured at 450 nm (PerkinElmer ArvoX2; PerkinElmer Japan Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), and the effective concentration of Corylin was identified.

HepG-2 and HCCLM3 cells were cultured at a density of 2×105 cells per well in 12-well plates and treated with Corylin for 48 h. The medium was removed, the wells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and 500 µL of 0.1% crystal violet solution (C0121; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was added. The plates were incubated for 30 min, the medium was discarded, the wells were washed three times with PBS, air-dried, and cell viability was observed.

Network pharmacological analysis

The target prediction of Corylin was performed by SwissTargetPrediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) database and GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/) database; The target genes of liver cancer were obtained by GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) and OMIM database (https://www.omim.org/); After searching for the intersecting targets of drugs and diseases, the common target genes of “Corylin-liver cancer” were analyzed by using the DAVID database (https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov/), the PPI interaction network analysis was conducted and processed through the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) and Cytoscape software (version 3.10.1). The Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis were performed. The top 10 results were selected for data visualization using WeiShengXin online mapping platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Cell scratch assay

HepG-2 and HCCLM3 cells were cultured at 5×105 cells per well in six-well plates with Corylin for 24 h. Cell monolayers were evenly scored with a sterile spear, rinsed with PBS, and photographed under a microscope [ECLIPSE Ts2R; Nikon Precision Machinery (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China]. Scratch width was measured from images taken 24 and 48 h after treatment. The following equation was used to calculate the cell migration rate: cell migration rate = (0 h scratch width − 24/48 h scratch width)/0 h scratch width.

Cell invasion assay

HepG-2 and HCCLM3 cells were cultured at 1×105 cells per well in 24-well plates with Corylin at 37 ℃ and 5% CO2 for 48 h, resuspended in DMEM with 0.5% fetal bovine serum and aliquots of 2×104 cells inoculated into the upper Transwell chamber (3422; Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA) previously coated with a 1:7 mixture of Matrigel to DMEM for 1 h. The lower Transwell chamber contained 500 µL DMEM with 10% FBS. Chambers were incubated at 37 ℃, and 5% CO2 for 24 h, and cells were fixed for 5 min in a solution of 0.1% crystal violet and 4% neutral formaldehyde. Cells were washed and counted under a microscope. Metastatic capacity was reflected by the migration of cells from the upper to the lower Transwell chamber.

Western blotting

HepG-2 and HCCLM3 cells were cultured at 5×105 cells per well in six-well plates with 30 or 60 µM Corylin at 37 ℃ and 5% CO2 for 48 h. Cells were trypsinized, extracted total protein using Protein Extraction Kit (Cat# DE101-01; TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China), quantified, and separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) (30 µg per lane) before transfer to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. The blocking solution was applied to the membranes for 2 h, followed by an overnight incubation with the primary antibody at 4 ℃. After three washes with TBST, the membranes were incubated with goat anti-rabbit or goat anti-mouse secondary antibodies for 40 min. The results were detected by enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) (ChemiDoc MP Imaging System, Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA, USA; and GE Amersham Imager600; GE Healthcare Inc., Shanghai, China). This experimental method was performed in accordance with the standard procedures outlined in the referenced literature (31).

Quantitative reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using SparkZol Reagent (AC0101-A; Sparkjade, Jinan, China) and reverse transcribed to complementary DNA (cDNA) using SPARKscript II RT Plus Kit (AG0304-A; Sparkjade). Messenger RNA (mRNA) was analyzed using 2× SYBR Green qPCR Mix (AH0101-A; Sparkjade). Applied Biosystems 7300 Real-Time PCR System (7300; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used to analyze mRNA expression levels. All quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) primer sequences used in this study are listed in Table 1. The results of qRT-PCR were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method (32).

Immunofluorescence

In 12-well dishes, the cells were plated and cultured at 2×105 cells per well for 24 h before being fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (P0099; Beyotime) for 30 min after 48 h of Corylin treatment. A PBS solution containing 0.5% Triton and 1% bovine serum albumin (P0260; Beyotime) was used to block the samples for 2 h. After incubation with the corresponding primary antibodies at 4 ℃ for 8–12 h, the samples were washed three times with PBS for 5 min each. Incubation of the samples in the dark for 1 h was followed by adding fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) or Cy3-labeled secondary antibodies (P0186/P0193; Beyotime). Finally, the samples were examined under a Confocal fluorescence microscope (LSM 980; Zeiss Group, Carl Zeiss AG, optical and opto-electronic industries, Oberkochen, Germany). This experimental method was performed in accordance with the standard procedures outlined in the referenced literature (33).

LC3 detection assay

HepG-2 and HCCLM3 cells were cultured at a density of 2×105 cells per well in 12-well plates containing coverslips for 12 h and then transfected with 1 µg pEGFP-LC3 plasmid (GenePharma, Shanghai, China). The cells were treated with Corylin for 48 h, fixed, and 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) nuclear staining was examined under a fluorescence microscope.

Apoptosis

HepG-2 and HCCLM3 cells were cultured at 2×105 cells per well in 12-well plates with Corylin for 48 h, harvested, resuspended in 1,000 µL buffer containing 10 µL Annexin V-FITC/PI dye (556547; BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA), and incubated for 20 min at room temperature before flow cytometric detection.

HepG-2 and HCCLM3 cells were cultured at 2×105 cells per well in 12-well plates containing cover slips with Corylin for 48 h. The medium was discarded, and 500 µL fixative was added per well for incubation overnight at 4 ℃. A fluorescent microscope was used to observe the wells after three times of PBS rinse, Hoechst 33258 (C1011; Beyotime) staining for 5 min, and three times of PBS rinse.

JC-1 staining

HepG-2 and HCCLM3 cells were cultured at 2×105 cells per well in 12-well plates with Corylin for 48 h. The cells were washed with serum-free DMEM, and 1 mL of JC-1 solution (C2003S; Beyotime) was added for incubation at 37 ℃ for 30 min in the dark. Cells were imaged under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX71; Olympus Life Science Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

HepG-2 and HCCLM3 cells were cultured at 5,000 cells per well in 96-well plates with Corylin for 48 h. The medium was discarded, and 100 µL of JC-1 solution was added to fresh medium for a 20 min incubation at 37 ℃ in the dark. The absorbance was measured at 435 and 585 nm.

Tetramethylrhodamine methyl ester (TMRM) staining

HepG-2 and HCCLM3 cells were cultured at 2×105 cells per well in 12-well plates with Corylin for 48 h. The cells were washed with serum-free DMEM, and 1 mL of TMRM solution (C2001S; Beyotime) was added for incubation at 37 ℃ for 15 min in the dark. Cells were washed twice with PBS and imaged under a fluorescent microscope.

ROS detection

HepG-2 and HCCLM3 cells were cultured at 2×105 cells per well in 12-well plates with Corylin for 48 h. The cells were rinsed with serum-free DMEM and supplemented with 1 mL of DHR dye solution (HY-101894; MCE) for incubation at 37 ℃ for 15 min. The cells were washed twice with PBS, digested with ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-free trypsin, and resuspended in PBS for flow cytometric detection.

In vivo analysis of antitumor effects

SPF-grade 5-week-old BALB/c nude mice were acclimatized for 5–7 days, and a subcutaneous injection of 100 µL of a 5×107 cells/mL suspension of HCCLM3 cells was administered into the right thigh. Mice were randomly divided into four groups (n=3, 7 days post-inoculation): 5 mg/kg Corylin, 10 mg/kg Corylin, 5 mg/kg 5-FU, or PBS (control), and were given by an intra-tumoral route every 2 days. Tumor size and body weight were measured weekly. The mice were sacrificed at week 6, and tumor protein expression and immunohistochemistry were analyzed.

SPF-grade 5-week-old BALB/c nude mice 12 were acclimatized and fed for 1 week, and grouped in sets of four, 100 µL of HepG-2 cell suspension was injected into the tail vein of the mice at 1×107 cells/mL using a 1 mL syringe, and it was ensured that there was no extravasation of the cell suspension. Intragastric administration of Corylin was initiated 1 week after cell injection, and mice were euthanized at 14 days after modeling, and the lungs were removed for observation of lung tumor formation. The effect of Corylin on the invasive metastatic ability of tumors was evaluated by simulating the process of tumor cells detaching from the primary lesion and entering the blood circulation, and observing the formation of metastatic foci in the lungs.

Statistical analysis

The data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of at least three independent replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPadPrism 8.0 by unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test or analysis of variance (ANOVA). A value of P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Corylin inhibited liver cancer cell proliferation

In order to detect the inhibitory effect of Corylin on liver cancer cells, we treated HepG-2 and HCCLM3 cells with different concentrations of Corylin and analyzed the inhibitory effect on HepG-2 and HCCLM3 cells for 48 h. The structure of Corylin is shown in (Figure 1A). And the results showed that Corylin had the ability to inhibit the proliferation of liver cancer cells, and there was a dose-effect relationship (Figure 1B). Corylin had an inhibitory rate of approximately 50% on HCCLM3 (47.2%) and HepG-2 (53.9%) cells at a dose of 60 µM. Therefore, we chose to use 60 and 30 µM for the follow-up experiments. Similar results to CCK-8 experiment were also obtained in the crystal violet staining experiment, which showed that Corylin had a significant inhibitory effect on liver cancer cells (Figure 1C).

Results of Corylin network pharmacology analysis

We extracted liver cancer target data from multiple databases. After deduplication, we obtained 2,124 unique targets. Meanwhile, through retrieval from the TCMSP database, we identified 138 targets corresponding to the active components of Corylin. Cross-referencing these targets with liver cancer targets identified 67 core genes (Figure 2A). These core genes were uploaded to the STRING platform (confidence score >0.90), which constructed a protein interaction network (Figure 2B). This network comprised 67 nodes and 1,864 connections, with darker node colors and denser connections indicating higher importance. Data were processed using Perl and imported into Cytoscape 3.10.1 to construct the “active component-key target” regulatory network for Corylin’s treatment of liver cancer. Ten critical core targets were identified, predominantly associated with apoptosis and mitochondrial autophagy regulation. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed these targets predominantly clustered in cancer signaling pathways, apoptosis, cell cycle, and mitophagy pathways (Figure 2C). GO enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of core targets in apoptosis pathways, redox level activation, and mitochondrial-related activities (Figure 2D). These findings suggested that further investigation into Corylin-induced apoptosis and mitophagy in liver cancer cells could explore the potential of Corylin for cancer therapy.

Corylin inhibited migration and invasion of liver cancer cells

Cell migration during scratch assays decreased with increasing Corylin concentration (Figure 3A), being reduced to 7.97% for HepG-2 and to 5.98% for HCCLM3 by 48 h treatment with 10 µM Corylin (Figure 3B). Cell invasion was also decreased by Corylin treatment in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 3C,3D). Corylin thus appeared to inhibit migration and invasion of liver cancer cells.

The expression levels of the metastasis-related proteins, MMPs (MMP-2 and MMP-9), were reduced by Corylin treatment compared with controls (Figure 3E,3F), which may have indicated that Corylin reduced the capacity of cancer cells to degrade the extracellular matrix (ECM). E-cadherin expression was increased, and that of vimentin was decreased. We also detected the mRNA expression levels of the above-mentioned metastasis-related genes by qPCR (Figure 3G), and the results were consistent with the detection results at the protein level. The above results indicated that Corylin significantly reduced the migration and invasion abilities of liver cancer cells.

Similar results were obtained in nude mice in vivo experiments. By injecting HepG-2 cells into the tail vein of mice, the Corylin-treated group (10 and 5 mg/kg) inhibited the generation of lung metastases in mice to different degrees compared with the control group (Figure 3H), effectively suppressing the metastatic ability of liver cancer cells in vivo.

Corylin increased liver cancer cell apoptosis

First, we observed the nucleus by Hoechst staining, and according to the results, the Corylin group showed significantly more nuclear fragmentation and dense nuclear staining than the control group (Figure 4A). Subsequently, Corylin’s apoptosis effects on liver cancer cells were quantified using Annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometry. It was found that Corylin significantly increased the apoptosis level of liver cancer cells, and the apoptosis rate reached 58.7% (HCCLM3) and 52.1% (HepG-2) at a dose of 60 µM (Figure 4B,4C). In addition, it was also found that Corylin enhanced the expression of cleaved-PARP, cleaved-caspase-3, and CytC in liver cancer cells (Figure 4D,4E). We also detected the mRNA expression levels of the above-mentioned apoptosis-related genes by qPCR (Figure 4F). The expression levels of CASP3 and CYCS were upregulated, while the mRNA expression of PARP1 decreased after cleavage. According to the above results, Corylin was capable of significantly promoting apoptosis in liver cancer cells.

ROS levels were shown to increase with increasing Corylin concentration by DHR staining (Figure 5D,5E). Levels of CytC were enhanced in the Corylin group, as shown by Western blotting (Figure 4D). These results suggested that Corylin induced apoptosis through activation of the endogenous apoptotic pathway.

Changes in autophagy activity

After treating HCCLM3 cells with Corylin, we examined whether autophagy levels were affected. As shown in Figure 6A, cells in the Corylin group showed obvious LC3 green fluorescence from 24 h, which also increased significantly with time. Afterward, we examined the levels of autophagy-related proteins (Figure 6B,6C). A significant increase in LC3-II, Beclin-1, PINK, and PARKIN levels was observed after Corylin treatment, while p62 levels showed a marked decline. In addition, we also detected the mRNA expression levels of the above-mentioned autophagy-related genes by qPCR (Figure 6D). The results indicated that autophagy levels in liver cancer cells increased in a dose-dependent manner. Based on the above results, Corylin affected the autophagy levels of HCCLM3 cells.

A further study examined the co-localization of mitochondria and LC3, and found significant co-localization between mitochondria and LC3 in Corylin group cells (Figure 6E). The results showed that autophagy induced by Corylin strongly engulfed mitochondria of HCCLM3 cells.

The interaction between apoptosis and autophagy

In order to examine the effect of Corylin-induced apoptosis on autophagy, GFP-LC3 was first stained on HCCLM3 cells (Figure 7A). QVD, an apoptosis inhibitor, significantly reduced the number of LC3-positive fluorescent puncta, indicating that autophagy was significantly suppressed by apoptosis inhibition. The detection of autophagy-related proteins followed (Figure 7B). Inhibiting apoptosis significantly reduced the expression levels of autophagy-related proteins LC3-II, Beclin-1, PINK, and PARKIN, while there was a significant increase in the expression level of p62 (P<0.05). Results from the above study suggested that reducing apoptosis attenuated Corylin-induced autophagy. Then, the co-localization experiment of LC3 and mitochondria was performed, and inhibiting apoptosis led to a reduction in autophagy levels and mitochondrial colocalization with LC3 (Figure 7C). Combined with the expression of PINK/PARKIN, these results indicated that Corylin not only induced apoptosis through the mitochondrial pathway in HCCLM3 cells, but also triggered mitophagy.

In order to determine whether autophagy affected Corylin-induced apoptosis, we examined apoptosis levels (Figure 7D). When autophagy was inhibited, Corylin-induced apoptosis was significantly increased, exceeding the level of apoptosis caused by Corylin alone (P<0.05). Detection of apoptosis-related proteins followed. The addition of 3-methyladenine (3-MA) resulted in significant increases in the expression levels of cleaved-caspase-3 and cleaved-caspase-9 (Figure 7E), as well as CytC released (Figure 7F). As shown above, Corylin-mediated apoptosis was significantly increased by autophagy inhibition.

Further, we examined whether Corylin kills liver cancer cells more efficiently when autophagy is increased. As a result, Corylin’s killing effect on HCCLM3 cells was greatly improved by inhibiting autophagy, with an inhibition rate reaching 66.82% after 48 h (Figure 7G).

Corylin inhibited tumor growth in vivo

During the 6-week monitoring of tumor size changes, it was found that the tumor size of the control group increased rapidly throughout the experimental period, while the tumor size of other groups decreased gradually. The tumor size of the 10 mg/kg Corylin treatment group was significantly lower than that of the 5 mg/kg Corylin treatment group (Figure 8A,8B).

A continuous weight change was measured in each group of nude mice after successful tumor bearing. It was not observed that the weight of nude mice in the control group increased more than those in other treatment groups (Figure 8C). However, the body weight of mice treated with 10 and 5 mg/kg Corylin increased gradually during the experimental period. The above results showed that Corylin not only inhibited tumor growth in vivo but also exhibited no apparent toxicity in mice.

Subsequent immunohistochemical results showed that Corylin not only inhibited the proliferation of tumor cells, but also induced apoptosis and mitophagy-related proteins, as expected based on the in vitro results (Figure 8D,8E).

Discussion

As a second leading cause of death in developing countries, cancer has been increasing in incidence and mortality. The latest statistics show 19.29 million new cases and 9.96 million deaths globally (34-37). A common malignant tumor is liver cancer, which can be treated by surgery and chemotherapy. With the increase of chemotherapy cycles, the dose-limiting toxicity of chemotherapy drugs increases, which eventually leads to the decrease of patients’ sensitivity to drugs. Diagnosis of early liver cancer is difficult. In recent years, with the development of imaging screening, although the diagnosis rate has improved, the detection of cancer is often late after recurrence (38). This is why finding new anti-liver cancer drugs has always been a priority for treating liver cancer.

The antioxidant, antibacterial, and anti-cancer effects of the Psoralea extract, Corylin, have attracted a wide range of research interests (39-41), but the mechanism of its anti-tumor effect is still unclear (42). The results of this study indicated that Corylin exerted a dose-dependent inhibitory effect on liver cancer cells both in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, our findings showed that Corylin suppressed liver cancer cell migration and invasion, accompanied by reduced MMP-2/MMP-9 expression and attenuated ECM degradation. In parallel, Corylin increased E-cadherin and decreased vimentin, indicating inhibition of the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) program. Recent studies increasingly view EMT in HCC as a dynamic and partially reversible spectrum of cellular states that contributes to metastatic competence and therapeutic resistance, rather than a binary switch (43). In addition, EMT is tightly intertwined with tumor-microenvironmental cues [e.g., tumor-associated macrophage (TAM)/cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF)-driven inflammatory and fibrotic signaling], which collectively shape an invasion-permissive niche (44). Within this context, MMP-9 is not only a key mediator of matrix remodeling but also a functional hub linked to EMT-associated invasion and metastasis; emerging translational efforts even leverage MMP-9 as a metastasis-related imaging/engineering target (45). Therefore, the simultaneous suppression of MMP-2/MMP-9 and EMT markers by Corylin suggests that disruption of ECM remodeling and EMT-related plasticity may represent an important mechanism underlying its anti-metastatic effects in liver cancer.

Programmed cell death may take the form of pyroptosis, autophagy, ferroptosis, or apoptosis. As a result of treating liver cancer cells with Corylin, we found that the level of apoptosis increased significantly. As a result, Corylin was shown to significantly reduce the mitochondrial membrane potential, while increasing CytC release and the expression of apoptosis-related proteins. These results indicated that Corylin induced apoptosis in liver cancer cells through activation of the endogenous apoptotic pathway. In liver cancer, autophagy-induced drug resistance affects the therapeutic effect of the chemotherapeutic agent pentafluorouracil, whereas appropriate nutritional deprivation can increase cell death under the action of 5-FU through mitochondrial membrane damage and excessive ROS production without inducing starvation autophagy, thus triggering apoptosis, which is in line with Corylin-induced decrease of mitochondrial membrane potential in liver cancer cells ultimately leading to cell apoptosis (46). Therefore, in addition to drug therapy alone, nutrient deprivation combined with drugs can also be considered as a new effective strategy to control liver cancer cell.

Autophagy refers to the cellular autodigestion process marked by autophagosomes in the cytoplasm, when damaged, denatured, or aged, degradation and digestion of cells and organelles take place in the lysosomes of cells. The autophagosomes with bilayer membrane structure covering part of the cytoplasm and organelles were used as the judgment index. There is a strong correlation between autophagy and tumors. Several drugs that regulate autophagy are used in the treatment of tumors in clinics. As revealed by staining analyses of autophagy-related proteins and mitochondria, Corylin inhibited liver cancer cells and modulated autophagy, which appeared to function as a protective mechanism. Mitophagy has been increasingly recognized as a context-dependent process in cancer, potentially supporting either survival and therapy resistance or contributing to tumor suppression depending on cellular stress and metabolic state (47). In our study, mitophagy appeared to serve as a mechanism by which cancer cells attempted to counteract drug-induced stress and promote survival. Although protective autophagy generated by Corylin attenuated the cell-killing effect of drugs to a certain extent and may confer drug resistance to tumor cells, the nature of autophagy is not unregulated, and the switch that regulates autophagy is dependent on the status of p53 in tumor cells (48), so further investigation of the role played by p53 in the regulation of autophagy within tumor cells will help to explore the potential switching relationship between the protective and non-protective forms of autophagy (49).

The effect of Corylin on autophagy and apoptosis was also examined here. Corylin-mediated autophagy was reduced when apoptosis was inhibited. In contrast, autophagy inhibition increased Corylin-mediated apoptosis. Similar to the role of Corylin, it was found that down-regulation of insulin-like growth factor (IGF)-binding protein 7 enhanced mitochondrial autophagy and attenuated lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced inflammation and apoptosis in WI-38 cells (50), suggesting that autophagy can maintain cellular homeostasis by removing damaged organelles, or attenuate apoptosis by phagocytosis of damaged mitochondria by eliminating the various death signals released by damaged mitochondria. A study has shown that autophagy can reduce apoptosis by selectively decreasing the abundance of pro-apoptotic proteins in cells; for example, autophagy can selectively remove the active caspase-8. When the autophagy gene Atg7 was knocked down, the activity of caspase-8 was increased, suggesting that autophagy defects can promote cell apoptosis (51). Moreover, we examined the role of autophagy in mitochondrial function. After analyzing the mitotic-related protein PINK-PARKIN and observing the co-localization of LC3 and heat shock protein 60 (HSP60), the Corylin treatment led to significant increases in the two proteins mentioned above with strong colocalization. Based on these preliminary results, we hypothesized that Corylin might induce mitophagy and endogenous apoptosis.

Conclusions

In conclusion, Corylin significantly inhibited the proliferation of liver cancer cells and induced endogenous apoptosis and mitophagy (Figure 9). These findings provided a theoretical basis for the development of anticancer drugs derived from Psoralea extracts.
