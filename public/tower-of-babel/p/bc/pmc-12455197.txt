Training future antimicrobial stewards in the ESCMID AMS certificate training programme: from knowledge to action
Authors: Suzanne M E Kuijpers, Marlies E J L Hulscher, Jan M Prins, Jaap ten Oever, Teske Schoffelen, Neda Milevska-Kostova, Kim C E Sigaloff, Bojana Beovic, Jeroen A Schouten
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesESCMID has established the Antimicrobial Stewardship (AMS) Certificate programme to provide healthcare professionals with AMS expertise. This study evaluated the effectiveness of this 2-year training programme, measuring knowledge, skills and tangible results.Participants and methodsThis observational cohort study included participants in the first AMS certificate programme, which started in June 2022. The programme included online and live courses covering relevant AMS learning goals. Participants also carried out a local AMS project using a determinant analysis with pre- and post-intervention measurements. Data were collected through surveys, exams, and an evaluation of mandatory reports to assess progress across Kirkpatrick’s four levels of learning: reaction, learning, behaviour and results.ResultsForty participants from 22 countries took part in the programme. Individual courses were positively evaluated (reaction, level 1). All participants passed the final exam (learning, level 2). After 2 years, 35/40 (88%) submitted their AMS project reports (behaviour, level 3). In 31/35 (89%) of projects a post-intervention measurement was performed, of which 26/31 (84%) showed a positive effect on either process outcomes (e.g. increase in guideline adherence), quantity outcomes (e.g. reduced broad-spectrum antibiotic use), or patient outcomes (e.g. length of stay) (results, level 4).ConclusionsThe ESCMID AMS certificate programme effectively transfers knowledge and skills to participants who intend to develop ASPs in different clinical settings. Moreover, the course prompted the development and execution of local AMS projects with measurable impact on appropriate antibiotic use practices.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance is recognized as one of the top 10 global threats to public health and development.1 Excessive and inappropriate use of antibiotics has accelerated the emergence of resistant pathogens, leading to higher mortality rates, prolonged hospital stays, and increased healthcare costs.2,3 Antimicrobial stewardship programmes (ASPs) are effective in improving the quality of antibiotic use in healthcare institutions.4,5 These programmes involve coordinated interventions designed to promote the optimal use of antimicrobials.6 The knowledge of and approach to ASPs varies widely between different countries and sometimes even within the same country. These differences highlight the importance of standardized training programmes to share experiences and learn how to develop effective ASPs.

ESCMID has therefore set up an antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) certificate programme consisting of interactive courses, workshops, and the carrying out of an AMS project. It is aimed at early-career healthcare professionals with a clinical background who are either already involved in or plan to contribute to AMS activities within their institution. Target participants include but are not limited to infectious disease physicians, internal medicine physicians, paediatricians, clinical microbiologists, clinical pharmac(olog)ists, and intensive care physicians. This programme aims to shape participants into active contributors in the field of AMS with an important objective of creating a cohort of well-trained future local AMS leaders.

Evaluating the effectiveness of a training programme facilitates the improvement of future courses and examines whether the learning objectives are being met. This is, however, often restricted to measuring participant’s satisfaction with the course. The objective of the current study was to evaluate the first ESCMID certificate programme that ran between 2022 and 2024, and to assess whether this 2-year training programme was effective in enhancing not only participants’ knowledge, but also their skills related to AMS.

Participants and methods

Study design and participants

This was an observational cohort study including international participants of the first ESCMID AMS certificate programme that started in June 2022 and ended in April 2024 at the annual ESCMID global conference in Barcelona. Data of participants were gathered during the 2-year programme. The programme was evaluated according to the four dimensions of learning as defined by Kirkpatrick.7 This framework was originally developed to evaluate training in business, but has been increasingly used in the evaluation of education in healthcare.8,9 The framework evaluates the effectiveness of training programmes across four levels.7 It aims to measure the trainees’ satisfaction with the training (reaction, level 1), the knowledge gained during the training (learning, level 2), the behavioural changes resulting from the training (behaviour, level 3) and the tangible outcomes resulting from the training (results, level 4).7,10 All participants of the programme were eligible for inclusion. Participation in this evaluation was voluntary. Permission was asked from all participants both during the mandatory course and in the follow-up surveys.

Programme overview

The AMS certificate programme starts with a 5-day course covering the basics of AMS. This basic course is followed by nine courses covering all core competencies for (potential) AMS team members regardless of one’s position and profession (Table 1).11 These nine courses include six mandatory courses complemented by three elective courses, which participants select from a range of available options allowing them to focus on their specific professional interest (Figure 1, Table 2). The courses are delivered either online or face to face, with the exception of the mandatory course on AMS and infection prevention and control, which was offered in a hybrid format, and are held at various locations across Europe. These courses are also open to non-ESCMID certificate participants. Exams have to be completed after the basic course and each advanced course to assess engagement with the course material. A final exam comprising questions from all mandatory courses was taken online at the end of March 2024; only two attempts were allowed for this final exam.

In addition, as part of the AMS certificate programme, participants have to develop, carry out and evaluate an AMS project in the participant’s own healthcare setting. This active learning approach encourages their learning about how to systematically and effectively improve antibiotic use through a goal-driven and activity-based experience.12

During the basic course, participants identify a problem in the quality of antibiotic use in their own setting, and choose methods to measure the quantity and/or quality of the identified problem; identify targets for improvement from the measurements; assess local determinants of appropriate use, including both barriers and facilitators; and develop an action plan including improvement strategies to overcome these barriers and to use the facilitators to their advantage.13 To support this process, specific implementation frameworks are introduced, of which the Flottorp framework is discussed in greater depth due to its relevance for identifying determinants of antibiotic (mis)use.14 Each participant is assigned a tutor from a pool of experienced professionals with a background in AMS. This tutor supports the participants throughout the programme in developing and carrying out their project. At the end of the programme participants have to write a report summarizing their project, and have to submit an abstract with the results of their project to the annual congress, ESCMID Global.

Data collection

Various types of data were collected throughout the programme to evaluate the four different levels of learning by Kirkpatrick (Figure 2). At baseline, pseudonymized surveys were administered to collect the characteristics of participants. Throughout the programme, surveys were sent out after each individual advanced course to collect information on participants satisfaction with the lectures and workshops [see File S1 (available as Supplementary data at JAC-AMR Online)] (reaction, level 1). In total, eight exams had to be taken that tested the acquired knowledge: seven after individual courses and one final exam (learning, level 2). Exam results were aggregated.

Kirkpatrick model.

Next, after specific mandatory courses at 6 months, 10 months, and 18 months, a survey on the progress of the individual project was conducted (behaviour, level 3). This progress survey contained five questions asking participants to rate the current stage of project development on a scale from zero (have not started) to five (have finished). The stages included the selection of topic/problem, pre-intervention measurement, analysis of determinants, implementation of project, and writing of abstract and manuscript (see File S2). At the end of the programme (24 months) additional data on level 3 were gathered from the project reports by five researchers (S.K., J.P., J.S., K.S., T.S.) using a pre-defined scoring sheet (see File S3). The specific data collected included: whether a pre-measurement was performed, along with the type of measurement; whether a determinant analysis was performed, including the type of barrier analysis; whether an intervention was developed and, if so, the type of intervention; and whether a post-intervention measurement was performed. Finally, tangible results resulting from the programme (results, level 4) were retrieved from the project reports and abstracts. These project reports had to report, among other things, whether an improvement was found in either process (e.g. increase in guideline adherence), quantity (e.g. decrease in broad-spectrum antibiotic use) or patient outcomes (e.g. length of stay) (see File S3).

In addition to items of the Kirkpatrick model, if mentioned, information on facilitators and challenges encountered during participation in the programme and execution of the project, and collateral benefits resulting from participation in the programme were collected from the project reports.

Outcomes

The main outcome was the effectiveness of this programme in enhancing participants’ knowledge and skills related to AMS. Acquired knowledge was defined as the participants’ understanding of the core competencies of AMS in different clinical settings (learning, level 2).11 Acquired AMS skills were defined as the participants’ ability to design, carry out and measure the effect of an AMS intervention tailored to the needs of their own hospital setting (behaviour, level 3), and the impact of the intervention on patient care (results, level 4).

Data analysis

Baseline characteristics of participants and satisfaction with the courses (reaction, level 1) were presented using descriptive data. Responses to open-ended questions were analysed qualitatively through coding and thematic analysis. Each response could be assigned to more than one theme. Exam results (learning, level 2) were presented as percentage of participants achieving a passing grade (minimum of 55% correct answers). The progress of participants on their projects was analysed by assessing the number of participants that had completed the different stages of project development at different time points (behaviour, level 3). Level 4 (results) was analysed, using the result from the scoring sheet by calculating percentages of participants that measured tangible results after project implementation (see File S3).

Results

From 2022 to 2024, a total of 40 participants attended the first ESCMID certificate programme. Of these, 36 responded to the first questionnaire, representing a total of 22 countries, primarily from Europe. Most participants were internal medicine/infectious diseases specialists (22/36, 61%) or clinical microbiologists (8/36, 22%). Other participants comprised clinical pharmac(olog)ists, paediatricians, and infection prevention and public health professionals. Most participants were younger than 45 years (32/36, 89%).

Level 1 and 2: satisfaction and acquired knowledge

Table 2 shows the satisfaction with each course (level 1). Overall, the courses were positively evaluated. The lectures were often mentioned as being of high quality. The most important area for improvement was reduction of overlap of information between different courses. All participants successfully completed the final exam with a passing grade.

Level 3: behaviour

Level 3 assessed whether participants could translate acquired knowledge into practice. The progress surveys evaluated participants’ progress on the different steps of carrying out the AMS project, where necessary with the help of their tutor (see Files S3 and S4). Halfway through the programme 8 out of 18 (44%) participants (who completed the survey) had performed a successful measurement. At that moment, only 2 out of 18 (11%) participants had fully performed a barrier analysis. Although the programme was structured with the expectation that most participants would conduct their pre-intervention measurement early on, a substantial number completed this step during the second half of the programme. The main problem mentioned by the participants was not having sufficient dedicated time to work on the project.

At the end of the 2-year programme, 35 of 40 (88%) participants had completed their project and handed in a project report and an abstract. All project reports are available online (see File S5). The other five participants postponed their graduation by 1 year, primarily due to job transitions, after which four of them successfully handed in a project report. The most chosen stewardship project was prescribing appropriate empirical antibiotic treatment, followed by improving the use of peri-operative antibiotic prophylaxis. All participants performed a pre-intervention measurement (35 out of 35, 100%). The most common method chosen was a (retrospective) audit. A determinant analysis was carried out by 33 out of 35 participants (94%). The determinant analysis was most often performed using the Flottorp framework for identifying determinants of practice.14 All participants developed an improvement strategy of which 34 out of 35 (97%) were executed. Most improvement strategies were multi-faceted, frequently consisting of the education of health care providers and the development or distribution of guidelines or protocols, in many instances combined with audit and feedback, multidisciplinary meetings or bedside consultations.

Level 4: results

We finally evaluated whether the ESCMID certificate training programme had a measurable impact on patient care in one’s own hospital setting (level 4). A post-intervention measurement was conducted by 31 out of 35 participants (89%). Of these, 26 projects (84%) demonstrated positive effects on either process outcomes (e.g. increase in guideline adherence), quantity outcomes (e.g. decrease in broad-spectrum antibiotic use), or patient outcomes (e.g. length of hospital stay). Overall, 26 out of 40 participants (65%) of the 2022–2024 cohort performed a project that resulted in beneficial effects on patient care (see File S5).

In addition to these improvement projects, several collateral benefits were reported, such as the establishment of AMS teams within the participants’ institutions. Furthermore, new projects where participants applied the acquired knowledge and skills were initiated as a direct result of the certificate programme.

Discussion

The ESCMID AMS certificate is a 2-year postgraduate programme that covers all core competencies for AMS team members.11 Throughout the first cohort of this programme, participants were evaluated at different levels of the Kirkpatrick model, assessing programme satisfaction, acquired knowledge, shown behaviour and the resulting benefits to patient care in their own organization. The ESCMID AMS certificate demonstrated its effectiveness not only in imparting theoretical knowledge about AMS, but also in equipping participants with practical skills needed to apply this knowledge in real-world settings. Most participants were positively evaluated on all levels of the Kirkpatrick model, with measurable effects on patient care, and were therefore trained to be competent antimicrobial stewards.

Postgraduate programmes require a considerable amount of time, money, and effort from both participants and organizers, making it essential to ensure their effectiveness. For the AMS certificate programme, the total fee (excluding travel expenses) is 5000 euros. Several funding mechanisms are available to reduce financial barriers. The Kirkpatrick model for evaluating training programmes is a method based on four different levels. A previous review showed that many AMS training programmes fall short in evaluating the highest levels of this model and often limit evaluation to only measuring participants satisfaction (reaction, level 1).15 Of the 48 articles included in this review, only 22.9% reported Kirkpatrick’s third level results and none evaluated the impact of the training on the actual prescribing behaviour (level 4). The authors of this review questioned whether current educational initiatives are sufficient to achieve long-term impact, and called for more rigorous evaluation of programmes to assess whether levels 3 and 4 of the Kirkpatrick model are attained.

The evaluation of a programme should be a continuous process to ensure that learning activities align with and support the achievement of pre-defined learning goals.16 Several adjustments for the next cohorts of the AMS certificate programme have been made on the basis of the survey results of this first edition, such as streamlining of the programme structure to ensure that the right information is delivered at the right time to prepare for the next steps in project development, and reducing overlap of information between courses. In addition, tutors who support the participants throughout the programme are now specifically trained to guide the stepwise process of project implementation. The extensive evaluation of this first cohort of the programme also gave insight into challenges encountered by the participants that can limit successful engagement in this programme. Foremost, the requirements for participation in this programme should be adequate. For example, sufficient dedicated time should be available to work on the project, and sufficient support from hospital management and staff is crucial for achieving impact. To ensure these requirements, participants in following editions were equipped with a letter explaining the importance of dedicated time, which could be shown when needed.

Education and development of guidelines were the most frequently chosen interventions, which may have reflected the results of the determinant analysis (e.g. lack of existing guidelines) but also the relative ease and familiarity of implementing educational strategies rather than more complex or unconventional strategies. We are aiming to further align the different courses targeting behaviour change throughout the 2-year period for a more consistent approach to determinant analysis and choosing interventions. In addition, tutors will continue to gain expertise in this area, enabling them to guide participants more effectively in selecting and implementing a broader range of interventions.

Finally, the analysis of all project reports showed that, in addition to the executed improvement strategies, several collateral benefits emerged, such as the initiation of an official AMS team, AMS activities at national level, or newly initiated local, regional, or even national AMS projects. This shows that participation in this programme not only enhances individual knowledge, but also results in antimicrobial stewards who can take the lead in new AMS initiatives and education.

The AMS certificate programme aims to evolve into a formal, accredited European training programme for AMS stewards supporting the broad professionalization of AMS across healthcare systems, by creating a cohort of well-trained clinicians who will be able to expand AMS activities in their own setting, including training of a new generation of stewards. The structure and learning objectives of the programme are closely aligned with the AMS core competencies developed through international consensus by ESGAP, ensuring the curriculum meets current expectations for AMS practice across disciplines (Table 1).11 In response to growing global demand, we are also developing tailored programmes for long-term care and primary-care settings. In addition, in August 2025 the first AMS certificate designed for Latin America started.17 A key added value of this programme is that participants remain connected beyond the programme itself, frequently collaborating and supporting each other in their AMS initiatives. Many have since taken on mentoring roles as tutors in subsequent cohorts, further strengthening the programme’s sustainability and shaping a new generation of AMS leaders.

The main strength of this study is that we performed an innovative in-depth analysis of the impact of an AMS postgraduate programme up to level 4. By comprehensively evaluating this programme we aimed to determine its overall effectiveness in enhancing both participants’ knowledge and skills in AMS practices. In addition, this evaluation provided the organization of the ESCMID certificate programme with insight into areas for improvement. Limitations are that not all participants completed the surveys and progress surveys, so mid-term data and evaluations are not fully complete. This could have caused selection bias due to surveys only being completed by more invested participants. Mid-term evaluations could thus suggest an overestimation of the progress. In addition, as the courses were open to non-ESCMID certificate participants, some level 1 survey responses included data from other participants. Furthermore, it is unknown whether the achieved results are sustained after the programme is finished and dedicated time might no longer be available. We are, however, confident that competent future antimicrobial stewards have been educated, as 65% of participants have shown to be able to apply the knowledge that we provided (level 3), resulting in tangible results (level 4). Finally, focus groups could have been helpful to discuss in depth what could be changed in this course to achieve maximum long-term impact.

We can conclude that the ESCMID AMS certificate is an example of an effective training programme to transfer knowledge and skills to antimicrobial stewards who intend to develop ASPs in different clinical settings. The programme was not only well accepted, but also resulted in measurable effects on appropriate antibiotic use practices. This study also shows that it is feasible to comprehensively evaluate a course designed to provide participants with knowledge and practical skills alongside the study activities.
