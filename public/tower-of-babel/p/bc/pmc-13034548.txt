Differential life expectancies and life years lost associated with multiple myeloma in the United States: a simulation modeling study
Authors: Tzeyu L Michaud, Yi-Hsuan Shih, Mengmeng Ji, John Huber, Wei Zhang, Mei Wang, Martin W Schoen, Theodore S Thomas, Graham A Colditz, Jr-Shin Li, Su-Hsin Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultiple myeloma (MM) health disparities are well-documented, with non–Hispanic Black (NHB) and male individuals experiencing higher disease burdens than their non–Hispanic White (NHW) or female counterparts. However, no studies have shown that how these disparities translate into differences in life expectancy, particularly for monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS), a precursor to MM. This study quantified the remaining life years and life years lost associated with MGUS and MM to inform MM prevention and control priorities.MethodsWe developed a discrete-event simulation (DES) model of the natural history of MM, calibrated using nationally representative data on serologic-detected MGUS and registry-based MM. The model was stratified by race and gender (NHB/NHW men and women). Life years lost was computed as the difference in life years between populations without and with MGUS/MM.ResultsRemaining life years for MGUS/MM were 17.8/6.3 (95% prediction interval [PI]: 17.4-18.2/5.9-6.9) for NHB men, 20.1/7.7 (95% PI: 19.7-20.5/7.1-8.3) for NHB women, 20.9/7.3 (95% PI: 20.3-21.4/6.7-8.0) for NHW men, and 23.0/8.5 (95% PI: 22.5-23.6/7.7-9.4) for NHW women. Corresponding life years lost associated with MGUS/MM was 7.6/14.2 (95% PI: 7.3-8.0/13.3-15.1), 8.0/16.0 (95% PI: 7.7-8.4/15.0-17.0), 8.4/16.0 (95% PI: 8.0-8.9/14.7-17.3), and 8.8/18.1 (95% PI: 8.4-9.2/16.9-19.4), respectively.ConclusionSubstantial racial and gender differences were identified and quantified in disease burden associated with MGUS and MM, which provides concrete targets for MM prevention and control efforts. Our findings underscore the need for tailored strategies to reduce MM disparities, for example, enhancing disease monitoring among NHB populations and improving treatment adherence among men with MM.

This study quantifies remaining life years and life years lost associated with monoclonal multiple myeloma (MM) and its pre-malignant condition, gammopathy of undetermined significance by race/ethnicity and gender, providing actionable target for MM prevention and control. The findings provide the basis for intervention strategies tailored to target populations with different degrees of MM burden with the goal to reduce MM burden and MM health disparity.

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a lethal neoplasm and a common hematologic malignancy. Despite recent therapeutic advances, the overall 5-year survival rate was 62%.1 Moreover, MM management is exceedingly costly, representing the highest among the cost of care of all cancer types.2

Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) is an asymptomatic precursor condition that may progress to MM, other lymphoproliferative diseases, or plasma cell disorders3–5 with an annual risk of progression of ∼1%.6 MGUS is present in 3-5% of the US population aged ≥50 years,7 compared to 0.34% in the population aged <50 years.8,9 Over time, ∼20% of those diagnosed with MGUS progress to MM.10

Marked health disparities in MGUS and MM are well-documented.11–15 Epidemiological studies have reported a higher prevalence of MGUS among non–Hispanic Black (NHB) individuals, compared to non–Hispanic White (NHW) or Hispanic individuals.8,16 Additionally, a modeling study conducted by our team found that the risk of developing MGUS was 6.1% for the NHB population aged 50-75, compared to 2.8% for their NHW counterparts, with a shorter preclinical duration from MGUS to MM for the NHB population (14.2 years), relative to the NHW population (20.5 years).10 In MM, studies have shown that Black individuals had a 2- to 3-fold greater risk of developing MM compared to White individuals.17 MM incidence is consistently higher among NHB populations than NHW, American Indian/Alaskan Native, and Asian American and Pacific Islander populations.18 Furthermore, male sex is a known risk19,20 with estimated MM incidence 1.4 times higher in men than in women,21 which is likely to be attributable to the higher incidence of MGUS in men compared to women.22

Despite the well-established health disparities, there is limited understanding of how each disease stage contributes to the differential disease burden across demographic groups. Therefore, quantifying this burden using remaining life years and life years lost across gender and racial/ethnic groups is crucially important, because these measures estimate premature death due to MGUS or MM. Particularly, the population burden of MGUS is largely unknown due to its asymptomatic nature leading to delayed or under-diagnosis in clinical data. Comparing these measures across demographic groups quantifies MGUS/MM health disparities, identifies vulnerable populations that necessitate tailored interventions, and establishes target goals to address the existing issues via MM prevention and control policies.

To quantify the burden, we constructed an evidence-based, calibrated, discrete-event simulation (DES) model to simulate the natural history of MM. A simulation-based approach was chosen to integrate evidence from multiple sources, offering a robust framework to understand population-based burden by disease stage. Moreover, it allows for projecting long-term outcomes, extending beyond the limited follow-up of clinical trials or incomplete clinical data while using evidence from various published work. Our findings provide evidence of MM health disparities beyond epidemiological and clinical trial studies to guide MM prevention and control strategies.

Methods

The model

We constructed a DES model with a lifetime horizon to model the natural history of MM, from disease-free, to a premalignant state, MGUS, and malignant MM, followed by death (Figure 1). Our model did not include smoldering MM (SMM), a more advanced premalignant stage than MGUS, due to limited, inconsistent data on SMM in the United States, which largely stems from its asymptomatic nature and the absence of specific diagnostic codes for SMM.23 DES models the operation of a system as a discrete sequence of well-defined events occurring in continuous time.24 Given the prolonged duration of disease progression, DES was selected over Markov models because DES does not require a fixed cycle length and can handle multiple concurrent events, making it well-suited for modeling complex disease trajectories.25–28

Discrete event simulation model scheme for the natural history of multiple myeloma. MGUS, monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance; MM, multiple myeloma.

The population was compartmentalized by gender and race/ethnicity (ie, NHW women, NHW men, NHB women, and NHB men) based on existing evidence on MM health disparities.1,7,12,16,17,22,29 Hispanic populations were not included because current evidence does not indicate a differing risk of MGUS or MM compared to non–Hispanic populations and available data remains limited.8,16,30 From a disease-free population (Figure 1), over time, some individuals developed MGUS, while others remained disease-free. Individuals with MGUS may or may not progress to MM. Regardless of disease states, individuals could die from all causes. Disease progression, death, and timing varied based on an individual’s gender, race/ethnicity, and age.

Microsimulation

We assumed that all individuals entered the model at age 20, disease-free, as the prevalence of MGUS before age 20 is negligible,8 and exited the model at death or age 100, whichever arrives first. For each demographic group, we conducted Monte Carlo simulations on a hypothetical cohort of 100 000 individuals. For each individual, an event was first determined based on the associated probabilities. For individuals experiencing an event, the time-to-event was then determined by the corresponding CDFs.

Calibration

We calibrated each demographic group by sequentially minimizing the squared distance between model outputs and nationally representative data. We first calibrated MGUS-related parameters (ie, PMGUS,50, PMGUS,65, PMGUS,75, PMGUS,85) to closely approximate the observed MGUS prevalence. Next, we calibrated MM-related parameters.

Age-specific MGUS prevalence was estimated from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) as the calibration target. We estimated age-specific prevalence using five-year age groups due to the relatively small sample size of MGUS in the NHANES and assigned the group-level prevalence to that of the midpoint of the corresponding age window.

To account for differences in data characteristics, we used empirical coverage rate (ie, the proportion of model outputs that fell within the observed 95% confidence intervals [CIs]) for MGUS prevalence; we used the 95% prediction interval (PI) coverage rate (ie, the proportion of observed values that fell within the model’s 95% PIs) for MM incidence.

Microsimulation using the calibrated model and model outputs

For each demographic group, we conducted 1000 microsimulations of 100 000 hypothetical individuals using the calibrated model to generate the disease history. Per individual, we recorded age at MGUS and MM onset. We computed (1) remaining life years at MGUS/MM; (2) life years lost associated with MGUS/MM, defined as the difference between the life expectancy without and with MGUS/MM; and (3) preclinical duration of MM (ie, time from MGUS to MM). At the population level, we computed MGUS prevalence and MM incidence and summarized the individual-level outputs at a given age using means and 95% PIs of the model outputs across 1,000 iterations.

All simulations were programmed in MATLAB (R2024a Release).

Results

MGUS prevalence and MM incidence

The calibrated DES model reproduced the age-specific trend in MGUS prevalence and MM incidence, closely aligning with observed data (Figure 2). Based on 1000 simulations, the mean RMSE for MGUS prevalence and MM incidence were 1.9 (standard deviation [SD]: 2.0) and 23.8 (SD: 45.9) per 100 000 individuals for NHB men; 1.4 (SD: 1.5) and 5.7 (SD: 7.6) for NHW men; 1.1 (SD: 1.5) and 11.4 (SD: 19.8) for NHB women; and 1.2 (SD: 1.3) and 3.4 (SD: 4.0) for NHW women.

For MGUS, the empirical coverage of the model’s 95% PIs with the data’s CIs was 86.4% for NHB men, 68.1% for NHW men, 82.9% for NHB women, and 93.9% for NHW women. For MM, the coverage was 65.6% for NHB men, 92.1% for NHW men, 68.8% for NHB women, and 91.7% for NHW women.

Preclinical duration of MM

The preclinical duration of MM was 9.5 (95% PI: 9.3-9.6) years for NHB men; 8.6 (95% PI: 8.2-8.8) years for NHW men; 7.9 (95% PI: 7.7-8.1) years for NHB women; and 12.4 (95% PI: 11.8-13.0) years for NHW women (Figure 5, Table S2). Age was inversely associated with preclinical duration, with NHB individuals experiencing shorter preclinical duration (10.7-7.5 years for men and 9.8-3.8 years for women) than their NHW counterparts (12.4-4.7 years for men and 18.7-7.2 years for women), except for NHB men aged 65-85 (Table S2).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to quantify MM burden and health disparities in remaining life years and life years lost associated with MGUS and MM, using an evidence-based, well-calibrated simulation modeling approach. Our results indicate that the remaining life years at MGUS or MM vary significantly by age, gender, and race/ethnicity. The model is reasonably calibrated across all demographic groups. The group-specific variation in the deviations between model output and the data likely reflect data limitation, model fit, and heterogeneity in the underlying disease dynamics. The model has the potential to evaluate hypothetical screening strategies for MGUS and treatment strategies for MM. This modelling approach may inform clinical guidelines for the prevention and management of MGUS and MM by simulating the impact of intervention strategies across the continuum of cancer care.

Across all age groups, NHB men had the shortest remaining life years at MGUS and MM among all four gender and racial/ethnic groups, likely driven by MM-specific and all-cause mortalities in this population.29 Interestingly, NHW individuals exhibited the greatest life years lost associated with MGUS, while women experienced the greatest life years lost associated with MM. These findings may seem counterintuitive given the higher incidence of MGUS and MM among NHB individuals and men.8,16,19,20 These results highlight that disease burden, as measured by life years lost, had the greatest impact on populations with higher life expectancy.

Potential explanations underlying the quantified disparities, including shorter remaining life years and preclinical duration among NHB individuals compared with NHW individuals may be of many folds. Studies have shown a higher genetic susceptibility to MM and greater prevalence of genomic abnormalities as the fraction of African ancestry increases.34,35 Moreover, these differences may reflect a complex interplay of social determinants of health, for example, differential health insurance status and socioeconomic status that have been shown to link to access to care and quality of care, residential neighborhoods associated with MM-specific survival and overall survival.36–38

Although MGUS is a precancerous and asymptomatic condition, the associated life years lost is not negligible. Our prior work demonstrated a 20% higher risk of all-cause mortality among individuals with MGUS compared to the general US population.32 This is reinforced by prior studies showing that individuals with MGUS who are classified as low/borderline risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease have a 2.53-fold increased risk of cardiovascular events compared with their counterparts in the same group.39 This elevated risk may be attributed to a variety of causes including chronic inflammation, genetic abnormalities, vascular calcification, cryoproteins, autoantibodies, and the direct or indirect effects of monoclonal proteins on the vascular endothelium.4

Despite growing evidence of MM disparities, it remains unclear which populations should be prioritized for tailored, equity-focused interventions. Our findings raise an important and nuanced question regarding whether intervention strategies should target increasing remaining life years for the populations with the lowest remaining life years at the disease onset or reducing life years lost for the populations that had the greatest life years lost to effectively reduce MM health disparities. Nonetheless, our results reveal differential remaining life years and MGUS/MM-associated life years lost for all four demographic groups that may serve as a target for potential interventions.

Although routine MGUS screening is not currently recommended,40,41 several ongoing studies, for example, iStopMM and UK SECURE study, has been evaluating its potential benefits (eg, early detection).42 Until evidence from these studies is established, increasing awareness of MGUS and promoting early detection through closely monitoring disease progression remains crucial to reduce life years lost associated with MGUS and MM.40

We observed a generally negative association between age and preclinical duration of MM, with NHB individuals experiencing shorter pre-malignant phases than their NHW counterparts,10,22 except for NHB men aged 65-85. This exception may be due to higher MM mortality and overall shorter life expectancy in NHB populations.43 While previous studies suggested that shorter pre-malignant/preclinical duration among NHB individuals may be linked to differences in immunoglobulin isotype distribution in MGUS (eg, 81% IgG in NHB individuals vs. 70% in NHW individuals),10,44 it is also possible that NHB individuals surviving past age 65 represent a healthier subset, potentially less likely to progress to MM. Further research is needed to clarify the mechanisms of driving gender and racial/ethnic disparities in MGUS and MM. In particular, studies should explore the roles of biological sex, race, and their interaction in MM pathogenesis, including genetic, molecular, and immunologic factors that may be contributed to differential risks and progression patterns.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. First, our model did not differentiate disease onset and diagnosis. Compared to MM with its symptomatic nature, which typically leads to prompt diagnosis, MGUS is asymptomatic and often diagnosed incidentally,45 which may result in delayed or missed diagnosis and thus deviates our assumption with clinical data. However, this bias may be minimized by our model calibration using serum-detected MGUS cases from nationally representative data mimicking nationwide population-based screening (ie, serologic data as opposed to clinical data). Second, the simulation used a hypothetical stationary population without accounting for the cohort effect, which may not reflect the temporal trend of MM epidemiology. Nonetheless, the goal of this study was to provide estimates of differential MM burden across demographic groups using population health metrics. While MM incidence has increased and mortality has decreased over the past two decades, NHB individuals continued to experience the highest incidence and mortality rates compared with other racial/ethnic groups.18 Therefore, our findings should remain robust to set policy prioritization and targets in estimating the benchmark differences across demographic groups despite these temporal trends. Third, SMM, a more advanced premalignant condition than MGUS, was not considered in our model due to the lack of reliable data. As oftentimes for reimbursement purposes, treated patients with SMM were coded for MM and untreated SMM were not coded, it may be clinically acceptable to assume that disease progression to MM includes patients who receive MM treatment, encompassing both active MM and treated SMM. Finally, although we quantified the differential burden associated with MGUS and MM, our study was not able to provide insights on the reasons or factors contributing to it.

Conclusion

The study results reveal clear differential remaining life years and life years lost associated with MGUS and MM across demographic groups in the United States. These findings underscore the need for tailored strategies to reduce MM health disparities, such as reinforcing routine screening among high-risk NHB populations or enhancing treatment adherence for men with MM. More importantly, this study also quantified these differences, providing insights for setting measurable goals in efforts to reduce MM disparities.
