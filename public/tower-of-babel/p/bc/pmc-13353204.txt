Weight‐Related Self‐Esteem, Group Behavioral Therapy, and Weight Loss
Authors: Joshua Bernal, Jo A Wick, Byron Gajewski, K Allen Greiner, Jianzheng Wu, Christie Befort
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivePatients with obesity report experiencing weight stigma resulting in poor self‐esteem. Based on baseline self‐esteem, this paper explores recommendations for group versus individual visits for lifestyle components of comprehensive obesity treatment in primary care.MethodsParticipants (n = 1220) were rural primary care patients in a 24‐month cluster randomized controlled trial that demonstrated superior weight loss with group versus individual treatment. Baseline weight‐related self‐esteem scores (0–100), as measured by the Impact of Weight on Quality of Life‐Lite (IWQOL‐L), were used. A Bayesian normal dynamic linear model with a changepoint parameter estimates the relationship between self‐esteem and weight loss across group versus individual treatment at the 24‐month primary endpoint.ResultsThe posterior probability that patients receiving group lost more weight than those receiving individual visits remained > 0.999 for patients with self‐esteem scores greater than 60. Those with the highest self‐esteem (scores 80–100) receiving group visits lost 6.7 kg (95% CI, −8.15 to −5.20) at 24 months compared to 3.9 kg (95% CI, −5.45 to −2.39) for those receiving individual visits.ConclusionsPeople may benefit more from group than individual intervention if their self‐esteem is above an IWQOL‐L score of 60 when starting an intensive behavioral intervention for weight loss. Trial Registration: ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02456636.

Introduction

Nearly half of adults with obesity report experiencing weight stigma or weight‐related discrimination [1]. Moreover, 40%–50% have internalized weight bias wherein they apply the negative weight‐based stereotypes to themselves and engage in self‐blame [2], leading to poor self‐esteem, maladaptive eating behaviors, and ultimately worsening metabolic health [3, 4]. Weight stigma in healthcare settings impacts provision of care for patients, often resulting in patients losing agency in seeking care [5, 6].

Little is known about how weight‐related self‐esteem impacts response to obesity treatment in healthcare settings. Poor weight‐related self‐esteem includes feeling self‐conscious, embarrassed, and rejected because of one's weight. Individuals with varying degrees of self‐esteem may respond more or less favorably to a variety of delivery modalities for comprehensive obesity care. As an alternative to the traditional individual office visit model, group visits have emerged as a more effective and cost‐effective treatment modality [7]. However, with a higher degree of interpersonal interaction in group visits, patients with lower self‐esteem may not experience the benefits of this treatment modality. Understanding how weight‐related self‐esteem at the outset of treatment influences response to group versus individual visits could guide clinical decision‐making.

A cluster randomized controlled trial (CRCT) in 36 rural primary care clinics demonstrated that group visits, either in clinic or by telehealth, achieved greater weight loss (−4.4 and −3.9 kg) than individual visits (−2.6 kg) 24 months after randomization (p = 0.01 and p = 0.06 respectively) [8]. This secondary analysis examined whether baseline weight‐related self‐esteem predicted weight loss outcomes overall and by group versus individual treatment modalities.

Methods

The parent study for this secondary analysis was a multisite primary care‐based pragmatic CRCT among individuals in rural communities in the midwestern United States. Clinics were randomized to have their patients with obesity receive lifestyle intervention from a healthcare provider (practice‐employed clinicians or trained counselors) either through individual in‐clinic visits, group in‐clinic visits, or group telehealth visits. All three arms provided the same diet (1200–1500 kcal/d if weight was < 114 kg; 1500–1800 kcal/day if weight was ≥ 114 kg with a balanced diet with 5 or more fruit and vegetable servings per day), exercise (increase planned physical activity up to 225 min per week), and behavioral recommendations (goal setting and monitoring). Adjunctive medical management was provided by each patient's treating primary care provider according to their standard of care. For this analysis, the two group interventions were pooled together due to very similar weight losses with only 0.5 kg difference at 24 months. Despite the higher weight loss with the group visits arms, the individual visits arm had higher attendance overall likely due to individualized scheduling compared to a set day and time for group [8] visits. All participants completed four follow‐up visits, with the final visit being at 24 months. The study was approved by the University of Kansas Medical Center's Institutional Review Board [8].

Participants were given an Impact of Weight on Quality of Life‐Lite (IWQOL‐L) self‐reported assessment at baseline. The self‐esteem domain includes seven items (e.g., “Because of my weight, I am…self‐conscious, unsure of myself, embarrassed, afraid of being rejected”). It generates values of 0–100, where higher values correspond to higher self‐esteem. Based on exploratory analysis and convenient range values for clinical use, the scores were aggregated to create five ordinal categories (Figure 1).

To assess the association of baseline self‐esteem and its interaction with weight loss at 24 months, multiple models were explored with various complexities, including a first‐order Bayesian normal dynamic linear model (NDLM) with changepoint (CP) and a separate model (online Supporting Information). We selected the best model using deviance information criteria (DIC) for final inference. Analyses were conducted using OpenBugs software version 3.2.3 (MRC Biostatistics Unit). For each level of self‐esteem score, we calculated a 1‐sided posterior probability that group intervention was better than individual intervention.

Results

Among the 1220 participants (mean [SD] age, 55.4 [11.5] years; 941 females [77.1%]; 415 in individual visits arm [34%]), mean (SD) self‐esteem score was 51.2 (26.8). The percentage of patients within each ordinal category is shown in Figure 1. As baseline self‐esteem scores increased, the average weight loss increased. However, the superior treatment effect of group versus individual intervention occurs for patients with higher self‐esteem. The posterior mean for the CP occurred at self‐esteem fourth category which corresponds to around 60 on the IWQOL‐L self‐esteem score. The posterior probability that patients receiving group visits lost more weight than those receiving individual visits remained at > 0.999 for self‐esteem scores greater than 60. For scores 60–80, patients receiving group visits lost 5.4 kg (95% CI, −6.66 to −4.22 kg) compared to 3.6 kg (95% CI, −4.86 to −2.39 kg) among those receiving individual visits. For scores 80–100, group intervention resulted in 6.7 kg (95% CI, −8.15 to −5.20 kg) compared to 3.9 kg (95% CI, −5.45 to −2.39 kg) with individual intervention. Figure 2 shows the NDLM with CP and observed mean estimates per self‐esteem category. The posterior probability for the difference between group vs. individual weight loss drops below 0.99 as self‐esteem scores fall below the 60–80 category.

Discussion

In this secondary analysis of a CRCT, individual visits for intensive behavioral therapy yield about a 3 kg weight loss independent of self‐esteem score. However, for group visits, weight loss improves with increasing self‐esteem scores, yielding almost 7 kg of weight loss among those with the highest self‐esteem. People benefit, with high probability, more from group visits vs. individual visits if their baseline weight‐related self‐esteem is above an IWQOL‐L score of 60 when starting treatment for weight loss in primary care. This represents approximately 40% of the treatment‐seeking adults in this trial. Below this threshold of self‐esteem, weight losses observed from group vs. individual visits are similar.

This study was limited to people who lived in the rural midwestern United States, were high percentage female, and enrolled in a primary care‐based weight loss trial. Findings should be replicated in other settings. This study also did not formally assess potential weight bias among the participating clinicians. Strengths of the study include the real‐world applicability of the findings given the pragmatic design elements, such as minimal patient exclusions, high participation rates, and clinicians trained to deliver the intervention locally in their clinics.

These findings may inform clinical decision‐making in light of the prevalent experience of weight stigma and variable levels of weight‐related self‐esteem among treatment‐seeking adults. As greater numbers of patients seek care for obesity, primary care and the broader health system are tasked with developing models of care to integrate multimodal treatment including comprehensive lifestyle programs or behavioral therapy [9]. Cost‐effective models incorporating group visits should be considered, especially for patients with higher weight‐related self‐esteem.
