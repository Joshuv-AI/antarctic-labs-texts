Efficacy of a video refresher surgical technical assistance tool on surgeon performance of fasciotomies and proximal vascular control
Authors: Kristina Fuller, Samuel Tisherman, Lorreen Agandi, Kristin A Madenci, Adam C Puche
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExsanguinating hemorrhage is the leading cause of death in combat casualty care. With improvement in body armor, risk for extremity injuries is increasingly making surgical control of vessels and extremity fasciotomies critically important for saving life and/or limb. Yet, surgeon hands-on experience with major trauma has decreased. In military settings, with limited surgical resources, surgeons could be required to work independently and outside their scope of expertise. We explored whether just-in-time skill refreshment with video review improves performance of selected procedures.MethodsSurgeons (n=44) performed upper and lower extremity fasciotomies and axillary and femoral artery exposures on anatomic donors. They were divided into two groups: one group received Surgical Technical Assistance Tool (STAT) video procedure assistance and the other received no assistance (control group). Performance was evaluated using a five-component (anatomy, pathophysiology, patient management, technical skills, and procedural) Individual Procedure Score (IPS). The total number of compartment decompressions/constriction releases (maximum 10, combined fasciotomy procedures) and number of vessels controlled (maximum of four, combined vascular procedures) were measured. Each compartment decompression/constriction release and each vessel controlled are also single data points within the IPS.ResultsIn combined fasciotomies, STAT significantly improved the number of successful compartment decompressions/constriction releases as well as procedural, anatomy, and technical IPS components. In combined vascular procedures, STAT improved anatomy IPS, but not the number of vessels successfully controlled. For successful compartment decompression/constriction release, there was a significant linear correlation with procedural, anatomy, and technical IPS components. For successful vascular control, there was a significant linear correlation with procedural and technical IPS components. There was no correlation between success and pathophysiology or patient management scores.ConclusionA video-based, just-in-time refresher tool can dramatically improve procedural success and performance scores in procedural, anatomy, and technical components during trauma-related extremity procedures.Level of evidenceLevel II, Original Research, Therapeutic/Care Management.Keywords: general surgery, hemorrhage, Compartment Syndromes, Multiple Trauma

Background

Exsanguinating hemorrhage is a leading cause of death in combat casualty care. During the past decades improvements in body armor have reduced rates of penetrating head/neck, thorax, or abdominal injury, resulting in a shift in the relative balance of injury to the less protected extremities in recent conflicts. Indeed, a 2021 analysis indicated that head/torso injury now accounted for only 20% of combat primary injury sites.1 Thus, the surgical control of proximal vessels and extremity fasciotomies is of increasing importance for saving life and/or limb.

A combination of factors has led to a decrease in surgical trainee experience with these critical procedures including decreases in surgical training hours, increases in non-operative management of injuries, and decreases in the number of civilian or military trauma patients overall. Vascular operative case experience for general surgery trainees has declined from an average of 60.4 cases in 1999, to 33.5 cases in 2012, and further to 20.8 cases in 2020, a decline of over 65% across the past several decades.2 This is exemplified by several specific limb-saving procedures, for example, the average number of exposures of the brachial artery reported by chief residents throughout their training in 2020 was 0.1 cases. Thus, only 1 in 10 newly certified general surgeons has performed a brachial artery exposure, a procedure very likely to be needed with combat upper extremity gunshot or shrapnel injury. This experience gap is similarly pronounced in the management of compartment syndrome, which is a common complication of major vascular, crush, burn or blast injury. Graduating chief residents in 2023 reported the average number of fasciotomies performed was 0.2 cases.2 Thus, only one in five newly certified general surgeons has performed a single fasciotomy, which is most likely a lower extremity fasciotomy and not an upper extremity fasciotomy.

Modern surgical training programs emphasize the importance of working in teams with multiple medical professionals contributing to achieve successful surgical outcomes. In military settings, time-sensitive situations can arise where there is a shortage of medical professionals in theater to attend to the volume of combat injuries. In situations of limited surgical resources, surgeons would be required to work independently without an assisting surgical colleague and with only basic assistance from medic/nursing staff. Additionally, surgeons could be responsible for treating patients with traumatic injuries that are outside of their specific scope of surgical expertise. In situations of high casualties, all surgeons essentially become trauma surgeons and patients may require procedures for which the surgeon has little to no previous training or experience. We hypothesized that, in circumstances of unassisted performance of uncommon procedures, it would be beneficial to introduce a “just-in-time” surgical assistance refresher tool that could assist surgeons operating independently with these procedures.

A rapid refresher video tool is a potentially dependable option for just-in-time assistance. This refresher tool would include a library of video clips highlighting important steps and common pitfalls to avoid in various procedures. Surgeons could review the clips and they are scrubbing or during the procedure, potentially improving their preparation for, and performance of, critical procedures. The collection of annotated video clips could be stored on a computer, tablet, smartphone, or flash drive making it suitable for austere situations where high-fidelity external telecommunication is limited or contraindicated by combat conditions.

Mental rehearsal and mental imagery, which can be prompted by video review, are a common practice used in many high-performance fields. Before execution of the skill itself, a mental run-through to review the critical steps leads to improved skill execution in fields of sporting performance as well as medicine.3 4 This type of mental rehearsal for a critical undertaking is low cost, requiring no additional equipment beyond a display, and can be performed simultaneously with patient preparation to save time before execution of the task. One assessment of the practice of mental imagery in operation suggested that this tactic has been employed by surgeons for many years by mentally reviewing either general issues or specific steps in a surgical procedure. However, this mental review is typically performed without an external reference to a “gold standard” performance of the procedure. If the surgeon does not know the procedure, this runs the risk of a surgeon mentally visualizing a suboptimal approach when they are unaware of the best approach.

This study aims to test the hypothesis that a just-in-time video refresher tool, which we termed “Surgical Technical Assistance Tool (STAT)”, can improve surgeon performance of fasciotomies and proximal vascular control of extremity trauma in a cadaveric simulation. The primary outcome variable was successful completion of the critical aspects of the procedures. Secondary variables included Individual Procedural Scores (IPS) by evaluators on a validated measurement scale.

Methods

A prospective study was performed from May 2017 to November 2020.

Using data reported from a previous study5 of procedures taught in the Advanced Surgical Skills for Exposure in Trauma (ASSET) course of the American College of Surgeons, four open surgical procedures that can feasibly be performed without assistance were chosen for this study. The procedures included two fasciotomies, lower extremity fasciotomy (LEF) and upper extremity fasciotomy (UEF), and two vascular exposures, axillary artery exposure (AAE) and femoral artery exposure (FAE). The fasciotomy procedures included a total of 10 compartments or constrictions to release. The vascular exposure procedures included a total of four vessels to isolate and control.

Three senior attending trauma surgeons performed the four chosen procedures on freshly embalmed anatomic donors, annotating important steps and pitfalls. These sessions, in addition to templated scripts developed in previous work,5 were used to create updated procedure scripts to be used when evaluating surgeon performance in this study. The scripts begin with a case scenario followed by questions regarding pathophysiology, patient management, and anatomy followed by evaluator assessments of anatomic knowledge, technical skills, and procedural performance during the procedure. A combined total of 223 questions or observation points is in the two fasciotomy procedures and 184 in the two vascular procedures. Co-located evaluators scored participants on their answers and performance related to these five categories: anatomy, pathophysiology, patient management, technical skills, and procedural steps.6 Data was combined for the two fasciotomy procedures and for the two vascular procedures.

Raw video footage of experts performing these procedures was refined, spliced, and edited using Cyberlink PowerDirector V.19 software to make video clips ranging from 10 s to 1 min in length for each procedural step deemed to be most important by advising surgeons. Using Adobe After Effects software, brief text and labels were overlayed on the video pointing out key anatomic landmarks, important notes, and pitfalls to avoid. The composite videos were incorporated into a hypertext markup language-based STAT interface (figure 1A,B). Distractor procedures with dead links were included in the interface front page so that the subjects could not predict the procedures on which they would be evaluated as the session progressed. An example of the FAE video and STAT interface is available as onlinesupplemental figure 1 video 1. Three surgeons pilot tested the STAT interface before subject recruitment. One surgeon voiced a concern that the STAT tool could be considered distracting if they were already confident in the procedures. Nonetheless, these surgeons noted that having the landmarks and incision positions readily available to visualize before or during the procedure was helpful and that their confidence was increased because of the real-time help that the interface provided with the sequencing of procedural steps.

(A) Home page of the STAT with study and distractor procedures menu selection links. (B) Example screenshot from STAT with the lower extremity fasciotomy showing the start of the tangential “H” incision spanning crural fascia over the anterior and lateral compartments.

Subjects were recruited using a database of surgeons who had taken the ASSET course at least 6 months before their participation in this study. Subjects were cohort matched based on experience (eg, resident, fellow, attending, senior attending) and assigned to one of two study groups such that each group included subjects with similar experience. One group received assistance using the STAT interface (STAT group) and one group received no assistance (Control group).

Study subjects performed the selected procedures on an anatomic donor during one session in a clinical training laboratory. The setup in the laboratory, including lighting, instruments, and recording equipment, was standardized. Co-located, trained evaluators were either anatomists or surgeons who had been trained on the evaluation tools and had observed more experienced evaluators before they participated as evaluators.

The scripts were read aloud to the participants during the session. The answers to the questions and their performance of the procedures were evaluated by the evaluators with each answer/observation entered using an electronic scoring tool created and built into touch display tablets. Raters also provided a subjective assessment of a global rating scale (GRS) using a Likert 5 level score in categories of (1) patient management, (2) anatomy, (3) technical skills, and (4) readiness to perform. Evaluators also provided an overall gestalt of the participants’ performance in an evaluator rating (ER) of readiness to perform the procedure using a score between 50 and 100. Inter-rater reliability has been demonstrated previously with these scripts and approach.6 Participants in both the control and STAT study groups performed the procedures with only assistance in holding or retracting under the direction of the surgeon by a study member serving as an assistant who provided no guidance regarding what the surgeon should do and did not ask or answer any questions. The STAT group had the same conditions, only this group had access to the STAT video refresher. Each step of the STAT was shown once to the participant before the procedure was performed, as a simulation of the mental warm-up and a surgeon is preparing for the procedure. During the performance of the procedure the participant could verbally request to view any step or steps of the procedure on a display within the line of sight of the surgeon.

All subjects wore a head camera that recorded both audio and video footage of their performance. The room had two additional overhead cameras recording the procedure.

Subjects were asked to fill out surveys regarding their confidence in performing the procedures used in the study, as well as the distractor procedures, both pre-evaluation and postevaluation. In addition, after completion of the procedures, subjects in the STAT group were asked if they had past experience with such an intervention. They were asked to estimate how their performance using STAT compared with their expected performance if they did not have access on a Likert scale with options of 1 (much worse), 2 (worse), 3 (neither better nor worse), 4 (better), 5 (much better). Participants were also asked how distracting the STAT intervention was to performance with Likert scale options of 1 (very distracting), 2 (moderately distracting), 3 (mildly distracting), 4 (neutral), and 5 (not at all distracting).

Data was analyzed using Microsoft Excel and the NCSS V.8.0 statistics software package (NCSS). All data are deidentified and presented in aggregate for display and analysis. The Student’s t-test was used for continuous variables and χ² tests or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables when data had a single degree of freedom. In cases where analysis had two or more df, analysis of variance (ANOVA) or multifactorial ANOVA was used respectively. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

Sample size considerations were performed using prior studies with IPS, outcome, and performance of critical errors,5 6 using an estimate of the possible effect size of a STAT intervention of 0.36 absolute difference in IPS. Power analysis with these assumptions indicated a prospective sample size of 35 surgeons per group would be adequate for 95% power to detect a difference between the STAT and control groups.

Results

Recruitment was disrupted in 2020 when the Institutional Review Board suspended all human studies due to the COVID-19 pandemic, resulting in less than the targeted n=35/group sample size in the study. A total of 44 subjects from 24 states and two countries were recruited (22 per group). Given the recruitment disruption by the pandemic, challenges finding surgeons who had completed ASSET and were willing to travel to Baltimore, that the end of the period of performance of the grant was approaching, and restarting recruitment would have created disparate timing between early and later participants, the decision was made not to try to recruit more subjects after the pandemic restrictions were lifted. In addition, there was no additional funding to perform any long-term follow-up. There was no statistically significant difference between groups with regard to gender, experience, time since ASSET training, and time since residency (table 1).

The qualitative measures of GRS and ER were compared between groups. For fasciotomy procedures, ER in the STAT group was 18% higher than control (p<0.001), GRS anatomy 74% higher (p<0.001), and GRS overall readiness 39% higher (p<0.001). GRS for patient management and technique did not differ statistically between STAT and control. For vascular control procedures, GRS and ER scores were not significantly different between the control and STAT groups.

All 22 participants in the STAT group reported that the tool helped them perform better or much better than they would have without using it (mean Likert rating 4.4±0.5). Regarding distraction, 21 participants responded reporting that the STAT tool was neutral or not distracting at all (mean Likert rating 4.82±0.5) with only 1 participant indicating it was mildly distracting.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that emergency refreshing of combat surgical skills that are part of the ASSET course via a simple video-based procedural support tool (STAT) was effective at improving performance of uncommon procedures, reflected both in terms of successful completion of select surgical procedures and higher IPS. Surgeons performed these procedures on anatomic donors without assistance from colleagues, simulating a high-intensity combat zone with a busy surgical environment where surgeon availability is stretched resulting in single surgeons using an unskilled assistant. These data validate the concept that a video assistance tool is effective at allowing a surgeon to perform an uncommon or unfamiliar procedure more accurately under these conditions.

Overall, we found a significant linear correlation between success and IPS procedural, anatomy, and technical component scores. This makes sense as these are intuitively the key attributes that a surgeon needs to successfully complete a procedure. In addition, surgeons who received the STAT video assistance were all clustered at the upper end of the correlation spectrum indicating higher success and IPS across those components. In contrast, we found no significant correlation between control and STAT groups in IPS component scores of participant knowledge related to the pathophysiology injury mechanisms or general patient management. This was anticipated, as the STAT tool was designed for assisting with performance of the uncommon surgical procedure, but not to assist with knowledge of general pathophysiology or the specifics of resuscitation and longer-term patient management. We do not think that the tool needs to provide those components as conceptually, patient management postoperatively is not as time critical as the trauma surgery itself. Additionally, it is likely that postoperative care will be provided by intensive care teams, not the original surgeons, either in theater or en route to a higher level of care and the primary surgeon moves to the next patient. Thus, in designing the STAT tool we did not incorporate management or pathophysiology. This also serves as an internal control indicating the overall demographic distribution of cohort-matched participant experience, and thus probable pathophysiology/management knowledge or experience base, did not differ materially between the groups.

Although the use of video review tools to rehearse and assist with open surgery, particularly with trauma procedures, has not been well studied, the results of this study align with the literature published by other groups assessing the utility of general mental imagery. Immenroth et al showed that one-on-one mental training with an experienced surgeon prior to simulated laparoscopic cholecystectomy improves outcome.7 The authors suggest mental imagery is a more cost-effective means of optimizing the outcomes of operation than practical skills training.7 A randomized clinical trial by Arora et al,8 demonstrated that mental rehearsal prior to a laparoscopic cholecystectomy improved performance of surgeons who had previously trained with virtual reality. In open surgery, Souiki et al reported that mental imagery rehearsal exercises improved the outcome of hand-sewn anastomoses on bovine intestine.9 They concluded that mental imagery is an important approach for quickening the learning phase and improving the acquisition of new technical skills for experienced individuals but cautioned it might not be a suitable substitute for hands-on learning for novice surgeons.8 For open/endovascular aortic procedures, Patel et al, demonstrated that mental rehearsal decreased errors during the procedures.10 What makes our study unique is the utility of prompted just-in-time mental rehearsal in operation.

The STAT study reported here expands on previous literature to analyze the capabilities of a video review tool intended to be referenced immediately before undertaking the procedure or in real time whereas performing the intervention. Although not strictly rehearsal, in that the surgeon did not watch the videos multiple times temporally separate to the performance, there is intrinsically a cognitive training rehearsal component.11 The surgeons in this study viewed the full procedure immediately prior to performance, serving as a type of just-in-time rehearsal, and had access to the tool at any point during the procedure for a novel within-performance rehearsal/refresher. The outcome improvements here suggest not only advance rehearsal but also imagery assistance in close temporal proximity to, or within, performance can improve surgical outcome. The study also segregated performance into categories to identify IPS components most applicable to success that are amenable to just-in-time assistance, highlighting strong correlations with anatomy, procedural, and technical components of performance as key to outcome as well as readily targetable by the STAT intervention.

This study has several limitations. First, the study only included surgeons who had participated in the ASSET course. This was intentional for homogeneity in the previous training for the participants and for military significance since military surgeons participate in this course prior to deployment. Future studies should also study surgeons who have not taken the course. Long-term follow-up to explore decay in performance would also be important. Second, enrollment was curtailed because of the COVID-19 pandemic closing human subjects research prior to the conclusion of the recruitment phase. Despite this limitation of reaching approximately two-thirds of the planned enrollment, the study had a larger than expected effect size resulting in ample statistical power to detect improvement in performance with the STAT intervention. This also provided the statistical power to subdivide and analyze individual IPS components. Additional subjects may have allowed demonstration of relationships in areas of higher variability, such as between surgeons’ ongoing clinical experience or time from ASSET training with performance of these procedures, although this was not a primary outcome goal. Third, the study was limited to four procedures that can be performed by a surgeon without assistance. The findings cannot a priori be generalized to other procedures, particularly procedures that require coordination with a skilled assistant, such as intrathoracic or intra-abdominal procedures. Fourth, although the use of fresh cadavers provides realistic tissues for performing procedures and is the gold standard for surgeon training outside the operating room, this model does not include bleeding, which would typically make these procedures more difficult to perform in a timely manner. Future studies could use perfused cadavers that might add this additional layer of realism to the procedures, although that model has its own set of challenges and limitations. Fifth, although the fasciotomy procedures combined had 10 possible compartment decompressions/constriction releases, the vascular control procedures only had four vessels to be controlled. This produces a ceiling effect where it is difficult to demonstrate improvement in the number of vessels controlled as many surgeons controlled all four vessels. A much larger participant cohort may be necessary to overcome that ceiling effect in outcome. However, we were able to see improvements in anatomy IPS scores and in correlations between procedure/technique and outcome with the STAT intervention.

Conclusion

In conclusion, a video-based tool for just-in-time assistance with surgical procedures can improve surgeon performance. Additional research is needed to analyze if such a tool could provide similar results for more complex procedures that require an assistant, for non-general surgeons performing trauma surgical procedures, or for situations with active, simulated hemorrhage.
