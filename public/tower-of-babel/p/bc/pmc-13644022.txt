Discovery of a dimeric isopimarane derivative by reexploring a labdane-related diterpenoid biosynthetic gene cluster in Aspergillus nidulans
Authors: Shu-Yi Lin, Cory B Jenkinson, Quoc-Dung Tran Huynh, C Elizabeth Oakley, Ching-Kuo Lee, Haiyao Ma, Berl R Oakley, Clay C C Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiterpenes derived from geranylgeranyl diphosphate include the pimarane family, a class of tricyclic diterpenoids widely distributed in plants and fungi. Previous studies on Aspergillus nidulans identified a biosynthetic gene cluster containing a diterpene cyclase that produces ent-pimara-8(14),15-diene, whereas subsequent biochemical analysis suggested the enzyme instead generates an isopimarane scaffold.ResultsIn this study, we have reexplored this diterpene biosynthetic pathway by using a multi-cluster deletion strain to reduce the metabolite background and a modified version of the highly inducible alcA promoter system to drive expression of a transcription factor in the cluster that includes the diterpene cyclase gene. The transcription factor, in turn, drives expression of all the genes of the cluster. Induction of expression resulted in production of compounds not seen with prior methods. Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) and Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analysis has enabled us to identify dipleosmarane (3), a new dimeric isopimarane derivative, together with six known isopimarane diterpenoids (1, 2, 4a, 4b, 5, 6). Gene deletion experiments targeting the terpene cyclase (pbcA) and geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate synthase (pbcB) confirmed that these metabolites originate from this cluster.ConclusionsOur findings support the conclusion that the A. nidulans diterpene cyclase primarily synthesizes an isopimarane scaffold rather than an ent-pimarane derivative. Furthermore, the combination of transcription factor activation and a multi-cluster deletion background proved effective for revealing previously undetected metabolites from this pathway. Background

Pimarane diterpenes, a type of tricyclic diterpene, are most commonly found in plants and fungi, and in limited instances in other phyla. These compounds are divided into pimarane, isopimarane, ent-pimarane, and ent-isopimarane groups, distinguished by their stereochemical differences, with “ent” indicating the enantiomer form (Fig. 1) [1–3]. Previously Bromann et al. identified a biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC) in the filamentous fungus Aspergillus nidulans that contains a gene encoding a diterpene cyclase [4]. The BGC was designated the pbc cluster (pimaradiene biosynthetic cluster), and it consists of eight genes involved in the biosynthesis of pimarane-derived diterpenoids. It contains a transcription factor designated pbcR and when the promoter of pbcR was replaced with the strong constitutive gpdA promoter, the expression of seven contiguous genes in additon to pbcR was dramatically increased relative to the parental control strain as determined by quantitative PCR and DNA array analysis [4]. These results establish the boundaries of the BGC and reveal that the expression of the genes of the BGC are driven by the pbcR transcription factor. Bromann et al. determined that pbcA (= AN1594 using the FungiDB designation (FungiDB.org)) is a diterpene cyclase. Gas chromatography-mass spectroscopy (GC-MS) indicated that the BGC produces ent-pimara-8(14),15-diene [4, 5]. Bromann et al. later found, however, that overexpression of pbcA alone was sufficient to produce this compound which strongly suggests that this compound was an early intermediate and not the final product of the BGC [5]. In addition, Xu et al. characterized four terpene cyclases from Aspergillus species by heterologous expression and purification from Escherichia coli [6]. The metabolites were also analyzed by GC-MS. They determined that the diterpene cyclase from Aspergillus fumigatus Af293 (AfCPS-PS) and the diterpene cyclase from Aspergillus oryzae (AoCPS-PS) produce isopimara-7-15-diene. The diterpene synthases from Aspergillus niger (AnCPS-PS) and Neosartorya fischeri (NfCPS-PS) produce isopimara-8(14),15-diene. Xu et al. argued that because the A. nidulans CPS-PS shares 84% amino acid sequence identity with NfCPS-PS and greater than the 74% identity with the two functional analogs AfCPS-PS and AoCPS-PS, it is likely that the A. nidulans cyclase also produces isopimara-8(14),15-diene. The presence in the BGC of neighboring P450 and other genes with putative functions in secondary metabolism suggests that one or more elaborated isopimaranes is produced by A. nidulans as well.

The objective of the present study is to reexplore the pbc cluster with the goals of resolving the discrepancy between the two previous studies and determining whether more elaborated terpenes are produced by the biosynthetic pathway in A. nidulans. To strongly induce expression and facilitate compound purification, we engineered a strain optimized for expression from the inducible alcA promoter alcA(p) and in which eight secondary metabolite gene clusters were deleted to reduce the secondary metabolite background [7–10]. We replaced the pbcR promoter with alcA(p) (Fig. 2) and, upon induction, LC-MS revealed new peaks compared to our control strain. Large-scale fermentation followed by flash chromatography and reverse-phase preparative HPLC enabled us to isolate compounds 1–6 that were not previously reported by Bromann et al. (Fig. 3). NMR structural determination identified compounds 1, 2, 4a, 4b, 5 and 6 as sphaeropsidin C [11], pleosmarane S [12], pleosmarane A [12], pleosmarane B [12], aspewentin A [13–15], and sphaeropsidin G [16], respectively. Compound 3 is new to science, and we named it dipleosmarane. Sphaeropsidin C is a tricyclic isopimarane produced by various fungi, including Diplodia cupressi, the causal agent of a severe canker disease of Italian cypress in the Mediterranean basin [17, 18]. Sphaeropsidins were previously isolated from Aspergillus chevalieri at the Lederle laboratories and were named LL-S491 β and γ, respectively. These compounds exhibited antibacterial, antifungal and antiviral activities [18–20]. To our knowledge, this is the first time that Sphaeropsidins have been linked to the cluster that produces them. Our data shows that the cyclase PbcA most likely produces isopimarane, not ent-pimarane, as the initial Bromann et al. paper proposed. Our results also open the possibility of genetically dissecting the sphaeropsidin biosynthesis pathway and the generation of sphaeropsidin compounds in A. nidulans, a fungus with powerful genetic and molecular genetic systems [21–23].

Results and discussion

Bromann et al. [3] have assigned putative functions to the pbc cluster genes, and these are consistent with BlastP and protein domain homology searches we have carried out (Table 1).

A strategy that has been successful in turning up expression of secondary metabolites in A. nidulans is to replace the promoter of pathway-specific transcription factor genes located within a secondary metabolite gene cluster with a strong promoter [24–27]. Bromann et al., previously characterized this cluster by replacing the native promoter of pbcR with the strong constitutive gpdA promoter. They analyzed the metabolite profile using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) and reported the detection of ent-pimara-8 (14),15-dienes [5]. To increase yields of compounds produced by the cluster and, thereby, gain further insights as to the compounds produced by this gene cluster, we employed a similar approach but with several modifications. To drive expression of pbcR, we used alcA(p) which is highly inducible with methyl-ethyl-ketone (MEK). To maximize expression from alcA(p), we created a strain (LO11945) in which the promoter of the alcR gene is replaced with the gpdA promoter. AlcR is an activating transcription factor for alcA and overexpression of AlcR is predicted to increase expression from alcA(p) [10]. LO11945 also carries deletions of eight of the most highly expressed secondary metabolite BGCs in A. nidulans, which lowers the secondary metabolite background facilitating detection of novel compounds [8]. The replacement of the pbcR promoter in LO11945 was accomplished using our previously reported strategy involving an nkuA∆ strain and fusion PCR (Fig. 2) [28–30]. The resulting strain was LO12177.

Induction of pbcR with MEK in this strain resulted in the production of a series of compounds detected by LC-MS (Fig. 3A). To verify that these compounds are produced by the pbc BGC, we deleted pbcA and, separately, pbcB, creating strains CW13001 and CW13007, respectively. These genes encode a putative terpene cyclase (pbcA) and a geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate synthase (pbcB) and deletion of either should stop production of compounds by the BGC. This proved to be the case, confirming that the peaks are produced by the BGC (Fig. 3A). To produce larger amounts of the compounds for structural determination, LO12177 was cultivated in lactose minimal medium (LMM) at a large scale (10 L) at 37 °C and induced with MEK. After harvesting, the culture media and mycelia were extracted with ethyl acetate (EtOAc) and purified by flash chromatography and reverse-phase preparative HPLC. We were able to isolate seven compounds that were not detected in the control strain LO11945 in which pbcR is under control of its native promoter (Fig. 3A). The structures of these seven compounds were determined by HRESIMS, 1D, and 2D NMR spectra and by comparison to literature data. Compounds 1, 2, 4a, 4b, 5 and 6 were identified as sphaeropsidin C, pleosmarane S, pleosmaraneA, pleosmarane B, aspewentin, and sphaeropsidin G, respectively by comparison with literature data, while compound 3 is new to science (Fig. 3B). The compounds we isolated validate the Xu group’s hypothesis that the A. nidulans cyclase produces isopimara-8(14),15-diene, and the pathway does produce additional elaborated isopimaranes.

Compound 3 was isolated as white crystals. Its molecular formula was determined to be C38H50O3 based on MS analysis. In the LRESIMS ion trap system, an ion peak was observed at m/z 577 [M + Na]+. However, in the HRESIMS system, this compound appeared unstable and did not generate typical molecular ions such as [M + H]+, [M + Na]+, [M-H]-, or [M+HCOO]-. Instead, two stable fragment ions corresponding to m/z 269.18974 ([C19H25O]+) and m/z 285.18565 ([C19H25O2]-) were detected in positive mode and negative mode, respectively. These fragments suggested that compound 3 consists of two related monomeric units. In the ¹H-NMR spectrum, six methyl signals were observed at δH 0.83, 1.19, 1.22, 1.23, 1.25, and 1.26; eight olefinic protons at δH 6.72, 6.71, 6.17, 5.64, 5.10, 5.06, 4.93, and 4.85; and two oxygenated methine protons at δH 4.23 and 4.34. The ¹³C-NMR spectrum also revealed 16 olefinic carbons at δC 109.6, 109.8, 110.5, 111.7, 120.3, 120.9, 124.5, 124.5, 134.4, 135.1, 143.0, 145.4, 145.6, 146.8, 153.6, and 153.9. These 1D NMR data, combined with MS analysis, supported the presence of two diterpenoid units. (Table 2). Detailed analysis of COSY and HMBC correlations confirmed that the molecule consists of two closely related substructures resembling pleosmarane derivatives (Fig. 4).

Key COSY and HMBC correlations of compound 3

Although direct HMBC correlations between H-14 and C-14’ or H-14’ and C-14 were not observed in the standard HMBC experiment, this is likely due to a small ³JCH coupling constant across the ether oxygen resulting from an unfavorable dihedral angle. ³JCH couplings along C–O–C–H pathways are known to follow a Karplus-type angular dependence and can approach zero when the dihedral angle is near 90°, rendering such correlations undetectable under standard HMBC experiments [31–33]. Despite this limitation, the structure of compound 3 is strongly supported by multiple converging lines of spectroscopic evidence. Most notably, comparison of the ¹³C-NMR chemical shifts with those of pleosmarane S and pleosmarane T (in DMSO-d6) revealed dramatic downfield shifts at C-14 (Δδ = +8.6 ppm, from 67.5 to 76.1 ppm) and C-14’ (Δδ = +8.0 ppm, from 68.7 to 76.7 ppm), which are diagnostic for ether bond formation (Table S4) [34–38]. This chemical shift evidence is further corroborated by the MS fragmentation pattern, which reveals two stable fragment ions with asymmetric oxygen distribution: m/z 269.18974 ([C19H25O]+, containing one oxygen atom) and m/z 285.18565 ([C19H25O2]-, containing two oxygen atoms). This differential oxygen content is diagnostic for cleavage of a C-O-C ether linkage, where the single ether oxygen becomes associated with one fragment upon ionization. The molecular formula C38H50O3, the presence of exactly two oxygenated methine protons in the ¹H-NMR spectrum, and the 16 olefinic carbons in the ¹³C-NMR spectrum are all consistent with a dimeric isopimarane connected by a single ether linkage. On the basis of this convergent spectroscopic evidence, compound 3 is assigned as a dimeric isopimarane derivative consisting of two pleosmarane-like units connected through a C-14–O–C-14′ ether linkage.

In the current study, we did not detect ent-pimara-8(14),15-diene, the compound previously reported by Bromann et al. as the product of this cluster [4]. Bromann et al. based their structural assignment primarily on GC-MS spectral library matching, a technique that often struggles to differentiate between closely related diterpene stereoisomers without authentic standards. Later biochemical studies by Xu et al. demonstrated that a highly homologous cyclase from N. fischeri produces isopimara-8(14),15-diene, leading them to hypothesize that the A. nidulans PbcA cyclase also generates an isopimarane scaffold [6]. Our isolation and rigorous NMR characterization of compounds 1–6 definitively establish that the downstream products of this pathway possess an isopimarane stereochemistry. Consequently, the initial hydrocarbon intermediate produced by PbcA should be isopimara-8(14),15-diene, explaining the absence of the ent-pimarane enantiomer in our extracts. Furthermore, the elaborated metabolites reported here (compounds 1–6) were not observed in previous studies due to several key methodological differences. First, earlier studies relied heavily on GC-MS, which is well-suited for detecting volatile, unfunctionalized dienes but is generally incompatible with large, highly oxygenated, or dimeric diterpenoids without derivatization. Second, our use of a multi-cluster deletion strain significantly reduced the background noise from other abundant secondary metabolites, which likely masked these compounds in wild-type backgrounds [8]. Finally, our modified alcA promoter system, coupled with alcR overexpression, likely provided a stronger and more coordinated induction of the entire cluster, driving the metabolic flux toward the accumulation of these fully decorated final products [10, 39].

The differential distribution of the isolated metabolites—with compounds 1 (sphaeropsidin C) and 3 (dipleosmarane) detected exclusively in the growth medium, while compounds 2, 4a, 4b, 5, and 6 were found in both the medium and mycelium—prompts consideration of their secretion mechanisms. The exclusive extracellular accumulation of compound 1 is consistent with its known biological role as a secreted phytotoxin agent, a function that requires export to the extracellular environment [17, 40]. For the newly characterized compound 3, its dimeric nature may confer higher lipophilicity and molecular bulk compared to the monomeric pleosmaranes. Highly lipophilic, high-molecular-weight secondary metabolites are often actively exported to prevent intracellular membrane disruption and self-toxicity. Once secreted, their physicochemical properties likely hinder passive re-entry into the mycelium, leading to their accumulation in the broth [41, 42]. In Aspergillus species, the export of such bulky, hydrophobic metabolites is typically facilitated by specialized cellular machinery, including vesicle-mediated exocytosis (e.g., aflatoxisomes) and active efflux via transporters [43–45]. In contrast, the smaller monomeric isopimaranes may partition more freely between intracellular compartments and the extracellular medium.

Conclusions

In summary, we have demonstrated that activation of the pbc biosynthetic gene cluster in A. nidulans results in the production of several isopimarane-derived diterpenoids, including a previously undescribed dimeric compound, dipleosmarane. The replacement of the promoter of the pbcR transcription factor with the alcA promoter along with up-regulation of alcR allowed us to turn on the biosynthetic pathway, and the use of a multicluster deletion strain facilitated the identification and purification of the compounds produced by the cluster, leading to the isolation of metabolites that were not detected in earlier studies. The metabolites isolated in this study demonstrate that the terpene cyclase in this pathway produces decorated isopimarane, as proposed by the Xu group. Genetic deletion of the terpene cyclase (pbcA) and the GGPS (pbcB) genes establish their roles in the biosynthesis of these compounds. The distinct structural modifications observed across compounds 1–6 highlight the complex tailoring steps involved in this pathway. The pbc cluster encodes several putative tailoring enzymes (Table 1) whose specific functions remain to be experimentally validated. For instance, the cytochrome P450 monooxygenase (PbcG) is highly likely to be responsible for the initial regio- and stereospecific hydroxylations of the inert isopimara-8(14),15-diene scaffold, a hallmark of fungal diterpene biosynthesis [46, 47]. The short-chain dehydrogenase (PbcE) is presumably involved in the subsequent oxidation of these hydroxyl groups to the ketones observed in the isolated metabolites [48–50]. A complete functional characterization of this pathway will require the systematic generation of additional gene deletion strains targeting these tailoring enzymes. Finally, although additional compounds (and potential final products) produced by this cluster may remain unidentified due to stability and titer issues, our work opens the opportunity to genetically dissect this terpene cyclase BGC and can open the possibility of generating additional isopimaranes in this Aspergillus species.

Materials and methods

General experimental procedures

The UV spectra were recorded on an Agilent BioTek Synergy HTX Multi-mode Microplate Reader with UV-Vis detector. The NMR spectra were recorded using a Varian VNMRS-400 spectrometer (1H NMR, 400 MHz; 13C NMR, 100 MHz). Chemical shifts are expressed in parts per million (δ) with solvents as internal standards. Mass spectra ESI-MS was obtained with a Thermo Finnigan LCQ Advantage ion trap mass spectrometer, while HRESI-MS was obtained with a Thermo Fisher Q-Exactive orbitrap mass spectrometer. Silica gel C-18 (Sigma-Aldrich) was used for column chromatography. Reversed-phase HPLC purification was performed on a preparative HPLC (Cosmosil 5 μm C18 (2), 250 × 20 mm), with a flow rate of 8.0 mL/min and monitored using a DAD detector.

Molecular genetic techniques and strain construction

The transformation procedures largely followed previously reported methods [28–30], with a modification in which 100 or 200 mg/mL of VinoTaste Pro (Novozymes) was utilized for protoplasting. Transforming fragments were generated through fusion PCR, as described previously, with a few adjustments [28–30]. The DNA polymerase employed was Q5 Hot Start High-Fidelity 2X Master Mix from New England Biolabs. The annealing temperature was set at Tm (calculated for the primer with the lowest melting temperature) plus 3–5 °C, where Tm was computed as previously described [29] based on the primer with the lowest melting temperature. The extension time was 30 s per kilobase. The flanking regions were approximately 1 kilobase with minor variations tailored to primer design. Miniprep DNA was prepared for diagnostic PCR, following the protocol described by Edgerton and Oakley [30, 51]. The DNA polymerase employed was OneTaq Hot Start Quick Load 2X Master Mix from New England Biolabs. The annealing temperatures ranged from 55 to 62 °C, and the extension time was one minute per kilobase. Multiple diagnostic PCR reactions were conducted for each gene.

pbcR gene sequences and primers information

In placing the pbcR gene under the control of alcA(p) we used the the start site annotation following Bromann et al. The complete pbcR coding sequence and the primers for the construction of the alcA(p)-pbcR fusion PCR fragment are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Fermentation and LC-MS analysis

Liquid LMM medium (15 g/L lactose, 6 g/L NaNO3, 0.52 g/L KCl, 0.52 g/L MgSO4·7H2O, 1.52 g/L KH2PO4, 0.72 g/L fructose and 1 mL/L trace elements solution) [52] was inoculated with 3 × 107 spores in a 30 mL volume and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 150 rpm in 125 mL flasks. When required, supplements were added to the medium, including riboflavin (2.5 mg/L), pyridoxine (0.5 mg/L), uracil (1 g/L), uridine (10 mM), p-aminobenzoic acid (1 mg/L), biotin (0.02 mg/L), l-lysine (200 mg/L) or choline HCl (20 mg/L). To induce alcA(p), methyl ethyl ketone (MEK), was added into the medium to a final concentration of 50 mM at 42 h after inoculation. Following 72 h of MEK induction, the culture broth was separated from the mycelia by filtration and extracted with an equal volume of EtOAc. The mycelial biomass was extracted with 30 mL 1:1 DCM/MeOH with 1 h of sonication. The resulting extract was dried in vacuo to obtain an aqueous residue, which was then suspended in 30 ml H2O and partitioned with 30 ml EtOAc. The EtOAc extract from culture medium or mycelium was evaporated in vacuo, re-dissolved in 1 ml of 20% DMSO in MeOH, and a portion (10 µL) was subjected to high performance liquid chromatography-photodiode array detection-mass spectroscopy (HPLC-DAD-MS) analysis.

HPLC–MS analysis was performed using a reverse-phase C18 column (Phenomenex Luna Omega Polar C18, 3 μm, 100 × 2.1 mm) on a ThermoFinnigan LCQ Advantage ion trap mass spectrometer. The flow rate was set at 125 µL/min. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (5% MeCN–H₂O with 0.05% formic acid) and solvent B (95% MeCN–H₂O with 0.05% formic acid). The gradient program was as follows: 0% B from 0 to 5 min, 0–100% B from 5 to 35 min, 100–0% B from 40 to 45 min, followed by re-equilibration at 0% B from 45 to 50 min.

Isolation and characterization of metabolites

To purify metabolites, EtOAc was used to extract the broth from a 10 L culture. Mycelia were also extracted with 800 mL 1:1 DCM-MeOH at ambient temperature overnight, and the resulting extract was concentrated and re-extracted with EtOAc. Both of the extracts were subjected to silica-gel column chromatography followed by further separation through semi-preparative HPLC.
