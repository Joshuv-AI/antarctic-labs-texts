Pygenic acid A, a small-molecule PD-1/SHP-2 inhibitor, enhances efficacy of therapeutic melanoma vaccines and prophylactic influenza vaccines
Authors: Yan Yan, Yitong Li, Wenyi Mei, Yixin Li, Qian Wang, Honglin Li, Lu Lu, Shibo Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOvercoming immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments remains a critical challenge in advanced vaccine development. Here, we evaluated Pygenic acid A (PA), an intracellular small-molecule inhibitor targeting the PD-1/SHP-2 axis, as a novel vaccine adjuvant. The adjuvant efficacy of PA was systematically assessed in two murine models: a therapeutic B16-F10 melanoma lung metastasis model and a prophylactic lethal H1N1 influenza virus challenge model. In the melanoma metastasis model, PA potentiated the anti-tumor effect of the mTRP2 vaccine, markedly inhibiting pulmonary metastatic lesions and prolonging the survival of tumor-bearing mice. Mechanistically, PA robustly boosted the intratumoral infiltration of functional T cells, thereby reversing local tumor immunosuppression. In the influenza vaccination model, consistent immunostimulatory effects were observed: the PA-adjuvanted hemagglutinin (HA) vaccine effectively elicited broad-spectrum cross-neutralizing antibody responses and provided complete protection against lethal heterologous influenza virus challenge. Further mechanistic investigations demonstrated that PA specifically promoted the differentiation of T follicular helper (Tfh) cells and the expansion of germinal center (GC) B cells in draining lymph nodes, while triggering a robust Th1-type cellular immune response dominated by IFN-γ secretion. Furthermore, in vivo safety assessments verified that PA intervention induced no obvious systemic inflammation, hematological abnormalities, or visceral organ injury, indicating a favorable safety profile. Collectively, these results demonstrate that PA serves as a potent and safe intracellular checkpoint-targeting adjuvant capable of potentiating both cellular immunity and cross-protective humoral immunity, holding great translational promise for the development of advanced cancer vaccines and broad-spectrum influenza vaccines.Keywords: Vaccine adjuvant, Pygenic acid A (PA), PD-1/SHP-2 axis, Influenza virus, Melanoma, Germinal center

Introduction

Innovation and optimization of adjuvant formulations are pivotal to boosting the protective efficacy of modern vaccines. Clinically approved adjuvants represented by aluminum salts, together with emerging investigational adjuvants such as Toll-like receptor (TLR) agonists, exert their immunostimulatory effects predominantly by triggering innate immune activation. This process elicits local inflammatory responses and further facilitates antigen presentation and immune cell recruitment (Marrack et al., 2009; Awate et al., 2013; Pulendran et al., 2021). Nevertheless, such innate immunity-dependent adjuvant strategies have inherent limitations in the fine-tuned modulation of adaptive immune responses, particularly in overcoming immunosuppressive cues in the immune microenvironment (Yi et al., 2023; Hanahan et al., 2025). Both the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and physiological pathogen-specific immune responses are constrained by activated immune checkpoint pathways, which act as core intrinsic barriers that restrict the magnitude and durability of T- and B-cell-mediated immune responses (Good-Jacobson et al., 2010; Pardoll, 2012). Accordingly, the development of novel adjuvants capable of reversing adaptive immune suppression has become a cutting-edge research direction in the field of vaccine adjuvants (Ha et al., 2008).

Among diverse immune inhibitory signaling cascades, the programmed cell death protein-1/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) axis serves as a central regulator of adaptive immune networks (Keir et al., 2008; Pardoll, 2012; Sharpe and Pauken, 2018; Pan et al., 2023). In chronic infections and malignant tumors, persistent PD-1 signaling activation directly drives the functional exhaustion of effector CD8+ T cells (Barber et al., 2006; Wherry, 2011). Accumulating evidence demonstrates that the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway is not only implicated in immune suppression during tumor progression and chronic infection, but also participates in physiological immune regulation and immune homeostasis maintenance following routine prophylactic vaccination (Good-Jacobson et al., 2010; Crotty, 2014; Sharpe and Pauken, 2018). During germinal center reactions, PD-L1 expressed on antigen-presenting cells (APCs) and B cells binds to PD-1 on T follicular helper (Tfh) cells. This interaction restricts Tfh cell expansion and functional stability, and impairs Tfh cell-mediated helper effects on B cells, and ultimately modulates the intensity of germinal center responses and humoral immune homeostasis (Good-Jacobson et al., 2010; Crotty, 2014; Shi et al., 2018). Multiple preclinical studies have verified that PD-1/PD-L1 pathway blockade can alleviate Tfh cell suppression, potentiate humoral immune responses, and enhance the immunogenicity of prophylactic vaccines in various animal models (Finnefrock et al., 2009; Song et al., 2011; Okagawa et al., 2023). Based on these findings, we hypothesized that blockade of the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway represents an effective strategy for enhancing both antigen-specific cellular and humoral immune responses.

Despite the theoretical potential of PD-1/PD-L1 blockade in augmenting vaccine-elicited immune responses, the clinical translation of current PD-1/PD-L1 monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) as vaccine adjuvants faces multiple bottlenecks. First, due to their large molecular weight and unique physicochemical properties, mAbs exhibit poor tissue penetration and cannot be efficiently co-delivered with vaccine antigens to draining lymph nodes and target APCs (Jain and Baxter, 1988; Tzeng et al., 2015). Second, the high binding affinity of therapeutic mAbs induces sustained, systemic PD-1 signaling inhibition, which disrupts the physiological selective pressure required for B cells to compete for Tfh cell help within germinal centers (Good-Jacobson et al., 2010; Victora and Mesin, 2014). This disruption compromises antibody affinity maturation and ultimately leads to the production of low-quality antibodies (Good-Jacobson et al., 2010). Furthermore, excessive systemic immune activation triggered by systemic checkpoint blockade frequently causes severe autoimmune adverse events, which fail to meet the strict safety criteria for prophylactic vaccine application (Postow et al., 2018). Therefore, an ideal checkpoint-targeted adjuvant should transiently relieve immune suppression at the early stage of immune activation while preserving physiological immune homeostasis in the late immune response phase.

Small-molecule inhibitors targeting intracellular signaling nodes may provide a viable solution to address the aforementioned drawbacks. A recent study identified Pygenic acid A (PA), a natural pentacyclic triterpenoid with a unique mechanism of action (Li et al., 2025). Distinct from mAbs that block extracellular receptor binding via steric hindrance, PA possesses superior tissue diffusion and cell membrane permeability. PA specifically occupies the PD-1-binding site in the C-SH2 domain of SHP-2, which is the recognition locus for the phosphorylated immunoreceptor tyrosine-based switch motif (ITSM) of PD-1, thereby efficiently blocking intracellular downstream inhibitory signaling (Li et al., 2025). Based on this unique mechanism, we propose that PA can be developed as a novel small-molecule adjuvant for precise remodeling of the local immune microenvironment. Different from conventional extracellular checkpoint blockade strategies, PA-mediated intracellular inhibitory signal modulation avoids interference from complex extracellular ligand networks. This modality is expected to create a favorable immune microenvironment for the robust early activation of antigen-specific T cells and efficient antibody affinity maturation of B cells, without perturbing systemic immune homeostasis and physiological immune selection pressure.

To systematically validate the above hypothesis, the adjuvant efficacy of PA was comprehensively evaluated in two immunologically complementary and translationally valuable vaccine models. For therapeutic vaccine assessment, the B16 melanoma model was adopted, which features defined melanoma-associated antigens, low inherent immunogenicity, and a highly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (Overwijk and Restifo, 2001). Given these characteristics, the B16 model is widely applied in tumor vaccine and immune checkpoint research, serving as an ideal platform to verify the capacity of adjuvants to reverse local immune suppression and provoke antitumor T-cell immunity. In this model, PA was co-administered with tumor antigens to evaluate its adjuvant effects on enhancing antitumor immune responses and suppressing tumor growth. For prophylactic vaccine evaluation, PA was combined with influenza A (H1N1) virus hemagglutinin (HA) antigen. We further systematically investigated the regulatory effects of PA on Tfh cell function, germinal center responses, and the induction of high-affinity neutralizing antibodies against lethal viral challenge. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to integrate an intracellular small-molecule inhibitor targeting the PD-1/SHP-2 axis into a vaccine adjuvant system. Our findings aim to provide a theoretical and experimental basis for the development of next-generation targeted vaccine adjuvants with high safety and broad immunopotentiation capability.

Results

PA potentiates the antitumor efficacy of the mTRP2 vaccine in a murine melanoma lung metastasis model

To characterize the adjuvant activity of PA in therapeutic tumor vaccines, a B16-F10 melanoma lung metastasis model was established in C57BL/6 mice. Following the experimental workflow illustrated in Fig. 1A (Mai et al., 2021), mice were intravenously injected with luciferase-expressing B16-F10 cells (B16-F10-Luc). On days 3 and 10 post-tumor inoculation, mice received immunization with either the mTRP2 vaccine alone, mTRP2 vaccine combined with PA, or an equal volume of vehicle control. On day 17, in vivo tumor burden was quantified via bioluminescence imaging (Fig. 1B). Quantitative analysis revealed that the total pulmonary bioluminescence intensity in the mTRP2+PA group was significantly lower than that in both the vehicle and single mTRP2 vaccine groups (Fig. 1C). These results indicate that PA effectively augments the therapeutic efficacy of the mTRP2 vaccine, thereby robustly suppressing in vivo tumor progression.

Lung tissues were subsequently harvested to directly assess pulmonary melanoma metastasis and proliferation. Consistent results were obtained from morphological observation (Fig. 1D) and quantitative analysis of tumor nodule coverage (Fig. 1E): mice in the mTRP2+PA group displayed markedly fewer pulmonary melanoma nodules and reduced tumor coverage compared with the vehicle and monotherapy groups.

Notably, we observed a strong positive correlation between bioluminescence signals and quantified tumor nodules (R = 0.84, P = 0.00068; Supplementary Fig. S1). These data indicate that in vivo bioluminescence imaging provides a reliable and accurate representation of the actual pulmonary metastatic burden. To further dissect the immunological mechanism underlying the enhanced antitumor effect, immunohistochemical staining was performed to detect T-cell infiltration in lung tumor tissues (Fig. 1F). Abundant CD3+ T-cell recruitment was observed within tumor lesions of the mTRP2+PA group. Quantitative validation confirmed a significant increase in intratumoral CD3+ T-cell infiltration in the combination group relative to both control groups (Fig. 1G), demonstrating that PA facilitates T-cell infiltration into the tumor microenvironment and consequently amplifies the antitumor immune response elicited by the mTRP2 vaccine.

The survival benefits of different treatments were further evaluated (Fig. 1H). Mice in the vehicle group exhibited a median survival time of approximately 24 days, while single mTRP2 vaccination only conferred a marginal survival advantage, with a median survival of 28 days. Notably, combination treatment with mTRP2 and PA substantially prolonged mouse median survival to 38 days. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that PA effectively promotes T-cell infiltration into the tumor microenvironment and exhibits potent immune-potentiating effects when combined with a therapeutic vaccine, thereby significantly improving the efficacy of the mTRP2 vaccine against melanoma lung metastasis and prolonging mouse survival.

PA acts as a potent adjuvant to augment humoral immune responses and protective efficacy of recombinant HA influenza vaccine

To explore the potential of PA as an adjuvant for prophylactic viral vaccines, we utilized recombinant hemagglutinin (HA) antigen derived from the H1N1 influenza strain A/Wisconsin/67/2022 to evaluate the immunogenicity and protective capacity of PA-adjuvanted HA vaccines. As outlined in Fig. 2A, BALB/c mice were immunized via a prime-boost regimen with a 14-day interval (day 0 and day 14). Sera were collected on day 21 (7 days post-boost immunization) for the detection of HA-specific binding antibodies. The results showed that the HA + PA group generated significantly higher levels of HA-specific IgG titers than the PBS and single HA groups (Fig. 2B), indicating that PA effectively potentiates antigen-specific humoral immune responses.

To further assess the neutralizing potency of vaccine-induced antibodies, in vitro neutralization assays were performed against two classic H1N1 influenza strains, A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 (PR8) and A/WSN/1933 (WSN). Sera from PA-adjuvanted vaccine-immunized mice exhibited significantly higher neutralizing antibody NT50 titers against both viral strains than those from the HA-only group (Fig. 2C). These findings suggest that PA not only enhances antigen-specific humoral immunity but also facilitates the production of cross-reactive neutralizing antibodies.

A lethal PR8 viral challenge model was subsequently employed to evaluate the in vivo protective efficacy of the HA + PA vaccine. On day 21, mice from each group were intranasally challenged with a lethal dose (10 × LD50) of PR8 virus and monitored daily for 14 consecutive days. Mice in the PBS and HA-alone groups suffered severe body weight loss and eventually succumbed to viral infection by day 9 and day 10, respectively (Fig. 2D and E). In contrast, mice immunized with HA + PA experienced only transient mild weight loss and achieved 100% survival after viral challenge (Fig. 2D and E).

To further compare the severity of pulmonary viral infection across groups, additional immunized BALB/c mice were challenged with PR8 virus and sacrificed on day 5 post-infection for the detection of pulmonary viral load and histopathological lesions (Fig. 2F). Quantitative PCR analysis demonstrated that pulmonary viral HA mRNA levels in the HA + PA group were extremely low or undetectable compared with control groups, indicating efficient viral clearance mediated by PA-adjuvanted vaccination (Fig. 2G). H&E histological staining revealed extensive pulmonary consolidation, severe inflammatory infiltration, and massive alveolar structural destruction in the PBS and HA-alone groups. In contrast, lung tissues from the HA + PA group showed only mild pathological damage with well-preserved alveolar architecture (Fig. 2H).

In summary, PA serves as an effective vaccine adjuvant that enhances cross-protective humoral immune responses induced by influenza vaccination, inhibits pulmonary viral replication, and confers robust protection against lethal influenza challenge. This highlights the great potential of PA as a promising adjuvant for the development of broad-spectrum protective influenza vaccines.

PA enhances local germinal center responses and drives Th1-biased cellular immunity

To further elucidate the immunological mechanism underlying PA-mediated adjuvanticity and validate the core hypothesis of this study, we systematically investigated the regulatory effects of PA on germinal center (GC) responses. Mice were subjected to a prime-boost immunization regimen (day 0 and day 14), and draining lymph nodes and spleens were harvested on day 21 to prepare single-cell suspensions for subsequent analysis (Fig. 3A).

G Enumeration of antigen-specific IFN-γ and IL-4 spot-forming cells (SFCs) per 5 × 105 input splenocytes. Quantitative results are shown as mean ± SEM, and exact P values are marked on all graphs.

Flow cytometry analysis showed that the PA adjuvant significantly promoted the expansion of CXCR5+PD-1+ follicular helper T (Tfh) cells in draining lymph nodes compared with the HA-alone group (Fig. 3B and C; Supplementary Fig. S2A). Tfh cells are essential for GC formation and high-affinity antibody maturation. Consistent with Tfh cell expansion, the proportion of Fas+GL-7+ GC B cells in draining lymph nodes was also markedly elevated in the HA+PA group (Fig. 3D and E; Supplementary Fig. S2B). Notably, PA treatment only slightly increased the frequencies of splenic Tfh and GC B cells, with no significant differences observed between the HA and HA + PA groups. These results indicate that PA-mediated promotion of Tfh cell differentiation and GC responses is predominantly localized in regional draining lymph nodes rather than systemic lymphoid tissues.

Antigen-specific T-cell immune responses were further evaluated via ELISpot assays (Fig. 3F). Splenocytes isolated from immunized mice were restimulated with specific antigens in vitro. The results revealed that the number of IFN-γ-secreting T cells was significantly higher in the HA + PA group than in the HA-alone group. Although the HA + PA group showed a slight increase in IL-4-secreting T cells, no statistically significant difference was detected between the two groups (Fig. 3G). These findings indicate that PA strongly and preferentially induces IFN-γ-dominant Th1-type cellular immune responses.

Taken together, PA enhances vaccine-induced humoral immunity by boosting local Tfh cell differentiation and GC reactions in draining lymph nodes, while simultaneously eliciting robust Th1-biased cellular immune responses. These findings solidify the potential of PA as a broad-spectrum, high-efficiency adjuvant for viral vaccines.

PA exhibits an excellent in vivo safety profile in mice

To comprehensively evaluate the in vivo biosafety of PA as a novel vaccine adjuvant, mice were immunized with PA or vehicle control following the standard prime-boost regimen. Specifically, mice received two injections of PA adjuvant or vehicle control on days 0 and 14; then the whole blood and major organ tissues were collected for analysis on day 21. Body weight was continuously monitored starting from the first immunization. Mice in the PA-treated group exhibited steady body weight gain comparable to that of the vehicle group, with no abrupt weight loss or abnormal behavioral changes throughout the observation period (Fig. 4A), preliminarily confirming the favorable systemic tolerability of PA.

To further assess the effects of PA on systemic physiological function, whole blood samples were collected 7 days post-boost immunization for hematological and serum biochemical detection. Serum biochemical indicators were analyzed to evaluate the function of major organs: alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and AST/ALT ratio for liver function; blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine (CREA) for renal function; and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) for myocardial injury. All serum biochemical parameters in the PA group were consistent with those in the vehicle group and remained within the normal physiological range, indicating that PA causes no obvious functional damage to the liver, kidney, heart, and other major organs (Fig. 4B).

Complete blood count analysis further showed no abnormal fluctuations in key hematological indices, including white blood cells (WBC), red blood cells (RBC), hemoglobin (HGB), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), platelets (PLT), packed cell volume (PCV) and other hematological indices (Fig. 4C). These results suggest that PA adjuvant does not disrupt hematopoietic function or peripheral immune cell homeostasis.

Moreover, histopathological examination of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) was performed via H&E staining. Microscopic observation showed intact tissue structure and regular cellular arrangement in all major organs of PA-treated mice (Fig. 4D). No obvious parenchymal cell degeneration, necrosis, tissue fibrosis, or excessive inflammatory cell infiltration was observed in the PA group compared with the vehicle group.

To complement the in vivo safety profile, we assessed whether PA induces an inflammatory response in THP-1 cells. RT-qPCR analysis revealed that PA treatment for 6 h did not lead to a significant increase in the relative mRNA expression (fold change) of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, IFN-β1) or chemokines (CCL-3, CCL-4) compared with the vehicle group (Fig. 4E). These data demonstrate that PA possesses no significant immunostimulatory activity in vitro, aligning with its observed lack of systemic toxicity.

Collectively, systematic in vivo toxicological assessments confirm that PA possesses excellent biocompatibility and biosafety. This study provides a solid preclinical foundation for the translational application of PA as a safe and efficient novel vaccine adjuvant.

Discussion

The development of vaccine adjuvants that can elicit robust cellular immunity, broad-spectrum humoral immune responses and desirable safety profiles remains a core challenge in contemporary vaccinology (Reed et al., 2013; Pulendran et al., 2021). In the present study, we systematically evaluated for the first time the adjuvant potential of Pygenic acid A (PA), a small-molecule intracellular inhibitor targeting the PD-1/SHP-2 signaling axis. Functional assays demonstrated that PA substantially enhances the anti-tumor efficacy of the mTRP2 vaccine against B16-F10 melanoma, thereby prolonging the survival of tumor-bearing mice. When combined with seasonal influenza HA antigens, PA also conferred broad cross-protection against lethal infection with the heterologous influenza PR8 strain. Collectively, these findings validate that small-molecule-mediated blockade of immune checkpoint signaling can safely amplify antigen-specific cellular and humoral immune responses, highlighting a viable strategy for novel adjuvant development.

A key mechanistic insight from this study lies in the molecular basis of PA-induced cross-protective immunity. For highly mutable pathogens such as influenza A virus, conventional strain-matched vaccination is often undermined by ongoing viral antigenic drift, resulting in insufficient cross-protection against emerging variants (Erbelding et al., 2018; Krammer et al., 2018). Our in vivo data revealed that PA treatment markedly elevates the frequencies of germinal center (GC) B cells and T follicular helper (Tfh) cells in draining lymph nodes (dLNs). Given that germinal centers are the primary anatomical site for antibody affinity maturation and memory B cell generation (MacLennan, 1994; Victora and Nussenzweig, 2012), we propose that PA alleviates PD-1-dependent suppression of Tfh cells. This relief of immune inhibition strengthens Tfh cell-mediated helper signaling, sustains prolonged GC reactions, and ultimately drives the production of high-affinity, broadly cross-neutralizing antibodies. In line with our preclinical observations, emerging clinical evidence has demonstrated that cancer patients receiving anti-PD-1 immunotherapy exhibit augmented circulating Tfh (cTfh) responses and improved seroconversion following seasonal influenza vaccination (Läubli et al., 2018; Kedzierska and Nguyen, 2022). These clinical correlations provide robust translational validation for our murine experimental findings and support the immunopotentiating role of PD-1 blockade in vaccine-elicited humoral immunity.

In tumor vaccination settings, the therapeutic superiority of PA can be largely attributed to its capacity to potentiate T cell activation and selectively induce Th1-biased cellular immunity. Our results confirmed that PA strongly and preferentially promotes IFN-γ-centered Th1 immune responses, which effectively remodels the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) (Gocher et al., 2022). This phenotypic alteration is consistent with the robust infiltration of CD3+ T cells observed in metastatic melanoma lesions. Given that B16-F10 melanoma is generally considered a poorly immunogenic (‘cold’) tumor model with low baseline immune infiltration (Overwijk and Restifo, 2001; Kamensek et al., 2021), PA’s ability to promote T-cell infiltration and suppress metastasis in this challenging model underscores its significant potential for clinical translation. Notably, the immunostimulatory activity of PA is predominantly restricted to draining lymph nodes, with only marginal immune activation detected in the spleen, a key systemic immune organ. This localized immunomodulatory pattern minimizes excessive systemic immune activation and immune cell exhaustion, thereby reducing the risk of immune-related adverse events (Irvine et al., 2013).

Safety is a critical criterion for clinical adjuvant application. Traditional aluminum-based adjuvants frequently trigger local inflammatory responses at injection sites while failing to induce potent Th1-type cellular immunity, limiting their utility in therapeutic and broad-spectrum vaccines (Exley et al., 2010; Kuroda et al., 2013). By contrast, pattern recognition receptor (PRR) agonist-based adjuvants can elicit robust Th1 immune responses but often induce excessive innate immune activation, driving the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines including IL-6 and TNF-α. This uncontrolled inflammatory cascade leads to common adverse reactions such as local erythema, swelling, pain and systemic fever (Pulendran et al., 2021). To further characterize the safety profile of PA, we performed in vitro assays using the human monocytic THP-1 cell line. The results showed that PA stimulation did not induce the secretion of canonical proinflammatory cytokines (Fig. 4E), indicating that PA acts not as a conventional innate immune activator but as a modulator to reverse adaptive immune suppression. Specifically, PA exerts its adjuvant function by relieving intracellular immune inhibitory signaling rather than triggering nonspecific inflammatory responses. This unique mechanism accounts for the favorable in vivo safety profile of PA, with no observable body weight loss, hematological or biochemical abnormalities, or pathological damage to major vital organs in treated mice. Furthermore, as a small-molecule compound, PA possesses distinct translational advantages, including mature synthetic procedures, low manufacturing costs and independence from cold-chain transportation. These superior physicochemical and production properties overcome the inherent limitations of current antibody-based immune checkpoint adjuvant strategies (Joffe et al., 2024).

Despite these promising results, several limitations of the present study remain to be addressed. First, the broad applicability of PA to diverse immune microenvironments warrants further validation. Subsequent investigations ought to assess its adjuvant efficacy in an expanded panel of tumor and infectious disease models. Second, while THP-1 cells were used for safety profiling, immortalized cell lines have limitations compared to primary human immune cells, warranting further validation in primary cells. Third, considering the inherent immunological disparities between mice and humans, further evaluation in non-human primate (NHP) models is essential to confirm the translational efficacy and safety of PA for clinical transformation (Thompson and Loré, 2017). Fourth, combinatorial strategies integrating PA with low-dose, safe immunostimulatory agents (e.g., TLR agonists) could be explored in subsequent research. At the same time, given that PA significantly prolonged survival but did not achieve complete tumor clearance, combining PA with other immune checkpoint (e.g., CTLA-4, LAG-3, TIGIT) inhibitors might further improve the survival benefit observed in our B16-F10 melanoma model by addressing multiple immunosuppressive pathways (Buchbinder and Desai, 2016; Andrews et al., 2017; Cui et al., 2025). Based on this, the strategy’s dual mechanism of proinflammatory innate activation and immune checkpoint disinhibition may further optimize vaccine efficacy (Mullins et al., 2019).

In summary, the present study identifies and preliminarily characterizes the small-molecule PD-1/SHP-2 inhibitor PA as a novel and versatile vaccine adjuvant. PA exhibits a unique immunomodulatory mechanism, effectively facilitating melanoma eradication and eliciting broad cross-protection against heterologous influenza viruses without inducing systemic inflammatory toxicity. These findings provide novel insights for the development of therapeutic cancer vaccines and broad-spectrum influenza vaccines, and offer a robust theoretical basis for the design of next-generation immune checkpoint-based adjuvants.

Conclusions

This study comprehensively validates Pygenic acid A (PA), an intracellular small-molecule inhibitor targeting the PD-1/SHP-2 immune checkpoint pathway, as a novel high-efficiency and safe vaccine adjuvant with broad immunopotentiating capacity. In preclinical models, PA effectively augmented anti-tumor cellular immunity in a therapeutic melanoma vaccine paradigm and simultaneously potentiated robust humoral immune responses and heterologous cross-protection in a prophylactic influenza vaccination setting. Mechanistically, PA enhances Tfh cell expansion and local germinal center reactions, selectively drives Th1-biased cellular immune polarization, and avoids overt systemic inflammation and tissue toxicity. Collectively, these results provide solid proof-of-concept evidence that small-molecule-mediated intracellular immune checkpoint blockade represents a promising strategy for the development of next-generation vaccine adjuvants with optimized efficacy and safety profiles.

Materials and methods

Animals

Six-to eight-week-old male C57BL/6 and female BALB/c mice were purchased from Zhejiang Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd (Zhejiang, China). All mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) animal facility under standard laboratory conditions, including a 12 h light/dark cycle, a constant temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, and ad libitum access to standard rodent chow and sterile water. All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Laboratory Animal Care and Use Committee of Fudan University (Shanghai, China) and performed in strict accordance with the institutional guidelines for animal care and use.

Cells, viruses, and recombinant proteins

All cell lines used in this study were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). The luciferase-stably expressing mouse melanoma cell line B16-F10-Luc and human monocytic THP-1 cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 (RPMI 1640) medium (Meilunbio, Dalian, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (PS; Meilunbio) mixture. Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cells were maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Meilunbio) containing 10% FBS and 1% PS. Influenza A virus strains A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 (H1N1, PR8) and A/WSN/1933 (H1N1, WSN) were obtained from BEI (Biodefense and Emerging Infections) Resources. The recombinant hemagglutinin (HA) protein of the A/Wisconsin/67/2022 (H1N1) influenza strain was prepared with minor modifications based on a previously described protocol (Liu et al., 2022). Briefly, HA-encoding plasmids were transiently transfected into Expi293F cells using the EZ-Trans transfection reagent (Life-lab Biotech, Cat# AC04L092, Shanghai, China). Cell culture supernatants were collected at 7 days post-transfection, and the recombinant HA protein was subsequently purified using Ni Smart Beads 6FF (Smart-Lifesciences, Cat# SA036025, Changzhou, China) following the manufacturer’s standard protocols. The melanoma antigen mTRP2 was synthesized and purified by GenScript (Cat# RP20250, Nanjing, China).

Establishment of B16-F10 melanoma lung metastasis model and in vivo bioluminescence imaging

To construct the pulmonary metastasis model of melanoma, C57BL/6 mice were intravenously (i.v.) injected with 2.5 × 105 B16-F10-Luc cells on day 0. The tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into three groups and immunized with vehicle control, free mTRP2, or mTRP2 formulated with PA adjuvant on days 3 and 10 post-tumor inoculation. On day 17, in vivo tumor burden was assessed via bioluminescence imaging using a VISQUE InVivo ART 100 imaging system (Vieworks Co., Ltd., Anyang, South Korea). Prior to imaging, mice were intraperitoneally injected with D-Luciferin potassium salt (Absin, Cat# abs42075819-1g, Shanghai, China) and anesthetized for 10 min. The total bioluminescence intensity within the predefined region of interest (ROI) was quantitatively analyzed. After imaging, all mice were euthanized, and lung tissues were dissected and photographed. The area fraction of metastatic tumor nodules on the lung surface was quantified using ImageJ software (v1.53K, NIH, USA).

Influenza virus immunization and lethal viral challenge

BALB/c mice were subcutaneously immunized on days 0 and 14. The immunization groups included PBS, HA antigen alone, and HA combined with the PA adjuvant (HA + PA). For the HA + PA group, PA solution was mixed with the antigen solution immediately before each injection to form a co-injection mixture. Serum samples were collected on day 21 post-initial immunization for subsequent antibody detection. For viral challenge assays, mice were lightly anesthetized and intranasally infected with a lethal dose (10 × LD50) of the PR8 influenza virus strain. Mouse body weight and survival status were monitored daily for 14 consecutive days after viral infection. A humane endpoint was defined as a body weight loss exceeding 25% of the initial baseline weight. To evaluate viral clearance efficiency and lung pathological damage, a separate cohort of immunized mice received the same viral challenge and were euthanized on day 5 post-infection for lung tissue collection.

ELISA and microneutralization assay

HA-specific serum IgG endpoint titers were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). In brief, half-area 96-well microplates were coated with purified recombinant HA protein, blocked with blocking buffer, and incubated with serially diluted mouse serum samples (starting at 1:100). After thorough washing, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) antibody (Proteintech, Cat# SA00001-1, Rosemont, IL, USA) was added, and the color reaction was developed using tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate. The absorbance value at 450 nm was detected using an EnSight Multimode Plate Reader (PerkinElmer, Shelton, CT, USA). For the microneutralization assay, heat-inactivated serum samples were serially diluted (starting at 1:10) and co-incubated with 100 TCID50 of live PR8 or WSN influenza viruses. The mixture was then added to MDCK cell monolayers. The neutralizing antibody titer (NT50) was defined as the reciprocal of the highest serum dilution that protected 50% of MDCK cells from virus-induced cytopathic effects.

Flow cytometry analysis

On day 21 after immunization, draining lymph nodes (dLNs) and spleens were isolated from immunized mice and mechanically homogenized to prepare single-cell suspensions. After red blood cell lysis, cell suspensions were blocked with anti-CD16/32 antibody to eliminate non-specific binding. Subsequently, cells were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated specific antibodies for phenotypic identification: anti-CD4, anti-CXCR5, and anti-PD-1 antibodies for follicular helper T (Tfh) cell detection; anti-B220/CD19, anti-GL-7, and anti-Fas antibodies for germinal center (GC) B cell detection (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA). All flow cytometry data were acquired using a BD LSRFortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) and further analyzed with FlowJo software (v10.8.1, BD Biosciences, USA).

ELISpot assay

Antigen-specific T cell immune responses were evaluated using commercial IFN-γ and IL-4 ELISpot kits (Mabtech, Stockholm, Sweden). Briefly, 5 × 105 isolated splenocytes were seeded into pre-coated ELISpot plates per well and restimulated with HA antigen in vitro for 48 h. After five washes with PBS buffer, biotinylated detection antibodies were added to the plates and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. Following another round of washing, streptavidin-alkaline phosphatase (streptavidin-ALP) was added and incubated for 1 h. The immune spots were developed using filtered 0.45 μm BCIP/NBT-plus substrate solution, and the chromogenic reaction was terminated by thorough rinsing with tap water. Finally, clear and distinct spots were counted and analyzed using an ELISpot reader (Cellular Technology Limited, Shaker Heights, OH, USA).

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from homogenized mouse lung tissues (collected on day 5 post-infection) and THP-1 cells using RNAiso Plus reagent (Takara Bio, Cat# 9109, Beijing, China). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was reverse-transcribed from total RNA using a commercial reverse transcription kit (Abclonal, Cat# RK20429, Wuhan, China). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed with SYBR Green Master Mix (Abclonal, Cat# RK21203, Wuhan, China) on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). The relative mRNA expression levels of target genes were calculated via the 2−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH used as the internal reference gene. All primer sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Histopathology and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

For histopathological examination, major mouse organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 4–5 μm continuous sections. The tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and observed under a bright-field optical microscope. For IHC staining of lung tumor tissues, tissue sections were subjected to antigen retrieval and non-specific blocking, followed by incubation with primary anti-CD3 antibody (BioLegend, Cat# 100201, San Diego, CA, USA). After washing, HRP-conjugated secondary antibody was applied, and positive staining was visualized via DAB color development. The area fraction of CD3+ T cell infiltration was quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

In vivo safety and systemic toxicity evaluation

To assess the in vivo safety and systemic toxicity of the PA adjuvant, BALB/c mice received prime-boost injections of PA adjuvant or vehicle control on days 0 and 14. Mouse body weights were dynamically recorded throughout the experimental period. On day 21 post-initial administration, whole blood samples were collected for routine hematological analysis using an automated hematology analyzer. Serum was separated by centrifugation, and serum biochemical indicators including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (CREA), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) were detected using a fully automatic biochemical analyzer. All biochemical and hematological tests were completed by Shanghai Labway Clinical Laboratory Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (v10.1.2, GraphPad Software, USA). All experimental data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test was used for statistical comparison between two groups, while one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post-hoc multiple comparison test was applied for multi-group statistical analysis. Survival curves were analyzed using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
