Introduction

Implantable loop recorders (ILRs) have become an important diagnostic tool in the evaluation of patients with suspected arrhythmias by enabling prolonged ambulatory cardiac rhythm monitoring. The integration of remote monitoring platforms for cardiac implantable electronic devices has further enhanced their clinical utility by allowing timely review of ILR-detected arrhythmias and prompt clinical intervention.1

Despite these advantages, ILRs generate a disproportionately high number of remote transmissions compared with other cardiac implantable electronic devices, creating a significant workload for clinicians and device clinics.2 Several factors contribute to this alert burden. Because ILRs are subcutaneous devices, they are particularly susceptible to oversensing, undersensing, noise, and other sensing artifacts that can result in false arrhythmia detections.3,4 In addition, patient-triggered symptom transmissions frequently do not correlate with clinically meaningful arrhythmias, further increasing the number of alerts requiring review.

To address this challenge, remote monitoring platforms have increasingly incorporated automated filtering and artificial intelligence-based algorithms designed to reduce false-positive transmissions and improve workflow efficiency. However, such systems may occasionally misclassify true arrhythmic events. Herein, we report a case of prolonged complete heart block with asystole in which clinically significant ILR-detected episodes were erroneously excluded by the remote monitoring platform’s artificial intelligence filtering algorithm.

Case report

An 88-year-old woman with a medical history of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and severe calcific aortic valve stenosis with remote transcatheter aortic valve replacement presented with transient expressive aphasia and was diagnosed with ischemic stroke. Brain magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated bilateral scattered ischemic foci suggestive of a cardioembolic etiology. Computed tomography angiography of the carotid arteries showed moderate calcific atherosclerotic changes without significant stenosis. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a normal left ventricular ejection fraction of 65%, a well-functioning bioprosthetic aortic valve, and moderate mitral annular calcification. Given the high suspicion for cardioembolic stroke, transesophageal echocardiography was performed and demonstrated no intracardiac thrombus or patent foramen ovale. All electrocardiograms showed normal sinus rhythm with normal intervals, and no atrial fibrillation was observed on telemetry during her hospitalization.

In the absence of documented atrial fibrillation, an ILR (Reveal LINQ II, Medtronic) was implanted for long-term monitoring for occult atrial fibrillation. At the time of implantation, the sensed R-wave amplitude was 0.22 mV. Sensitivity was set to the nominal value of 0.035 mV. The device was programmed to detect bradycardia at heart rates <30 beats per minute, tachycardia at heart rates >143 beats per minute, pauses of >5 seconds, and atrial fibrillation episodes lasting >6 minutes. The remote monitoring was established using a home communicator device (MyCarelink Relay, Medtronic). The patient was discharged on aspirin 81 mg daily, atorvastatin 80 mg daily, and amlodipine for hypertension.

Continuous ILR monitoring did not reveal any episodes of atrial fibrillation or other clinically significant arrhythmias. During the 11 months of monitoring before her final hospitalization, 7 “pause” episodes more than 5 seconds in duration were detected on the CareLink network. However, review of the transmitted electrograms demonstrated that these events were due to R-wave under-sensing rather than true pauses. The patient presented with recurrent syncope, experiencing 4 episodes within 2 hours before hospital arrival, 11 months after ILR implantation. In the emergency department, she continued to have recurrent syncopal episodes coinciding with documented episodes of complete heart block and asystole on telemetry (Figure 2). The patient was emergently transcutaneously paced and underwent placement of a temporary transvenous pacing wire via the right internal jugular vein. A 12-lead electrocardiogram confirmed the presence of complete heart block with a ventricular escape rhythm (Figure 3). The ILR was interrogated in the emergency department using CareLink Express; however, surprisingly, no new events were found on the remote monitoring interface (Figure 4). Manual direct interrogation of the ILR subsequently revealed multiple pause episodes corresponding to the timing of her syncopal events (Figure 5). Review of the stored electrograms demonstrated complete heart block with ventricular asystole lasting up to 30 seconds (Figure 6). We reviewed the tracings at the time of true heart blocks with Medtronic engineers. There were a few patient-specific characteristics on the tracings that could cause the AI algorithm to misclassify the tracings: presence of myopotential noise, and noise spikes at the time of asystole followed by exponential decay, triphasic morphology of P wave and potential miss-categorization as a QRS complex, and presence of a long 30-second pause which was very rare in database that AI training was performed on are among the hypothetical etiologies that could have led to this missing. Fortunately, the patient did not sustain serious injury. Her workup did not identify any reversible causes for developing complete heart block. Her transthoracic echocardiogram continued showing a normally functioning bioprosthetic aortic valve with normal left ventricular systolic function. Therefore, the following day she underwent implantation of a permanent dual-chamber pacemaker, with removal of the ILR and temporary pacing wire.

Discussion

ILRs have become an essential diagnostic tool for long-term rhythm surveillance in patients with suspected arrhythmias, cryptogenic stroke, or unexplained syncope. Their integration with remote monitoring platforms has substantially improved the timeliness of arrhythmia detection and clinical response. However, the high volume of transmitted alerts generated by ILRs—many of which are false positives related to oversensing, under-sensing, noise, or artifact—creates a significant burden for remote monitoring clinics. The most common causes of false-positive ILR transmissions are signal dropout, R-wave under-sensing, and atrial or ventricular ectopies. Contemporary ILR detection algorithms employ multiple analytical parameters—including R–R interval variability, beat-to-beat variation, ventricular scatter analysis, heart rate density metrics, P-wave morphology, noise discrimination, pattern detection, and, more recently, artificial intelligence-based processing—to improve false-positive rejection.

AI-based filtering algorithms can significantly reduce false-positive ILR transmissions.5,6 This can potentially improve remote monitoring efficiency by decreasing nonactionable alerts. In July 2021, the United States Food and Drug Administration granted clearance for 2 AccuRhythm AI algorithms integrated into the Medtronic Reveal LINQ II system, with nationwide clinical rollout beginning in February 2022. The current AccuRhythm AI algorithm evaluates selected pause events, including reported pauses occurring more than once monthly and those not temporally associated with patient-triggered symptomatic events (defined as no symptom event or >20 minutes from symptom transmission).7 In patients with syncope as the monitoring indication, pause events may bypass AI adjudication when more than 1 pause has occurred since the prior transmission. This appears to be an intentional design feature to reduce the risk of inadvertent suppression of clinically significant events in a high-risk population prone to true pauses. The pause adjudication algorithm was validated using a dataset comprising 2766 ILR-detected pause episodes from 382 Reveal LINQ II patients spanning 1713 pause alerts. Reported diagnostic performance included a sensitivity of 99.7%, specificity of 98.3%, and positive predictive value of 95%.7

Although the algorithm lacks patient-specific inputs or adaptive learning at the individual level, the occurrence of 7 false-positive pause events before the true asystole episode in our patient likely contributed to the triggering of AI-based pause adjudication. Because the indication for ILR implantation was cryptogenic stroke rather than syncope, syncope-specific algorithm bypass safeguards were not applicable. Furthermore, the patient was unable to activate a symptom transmission because of abrupt loss of consciousness during the event. Together, these factors, along with possible patient-specific electrocardiogram tracing characteristics, may have increased the likelihood of erroneous filtering of the true asystole episode.

Despite the substantial benefits of AI-based filtering in reducing false-positive transmissions, this case highlights the inherent tradeoff of relying on automated adjudication systems. Although minimizing false-positive alerts improves workflow efficiency, overly aggressive filtering may inadvertently suppress clinically significant arrhythmias. In high-acuity scenarios, such as prolonged asystole, complete heart block, or ventricular tachyarrhythmias, even rare false-negative filtering may have catastrophic consequences. Clinicians should recognize that the absence of events on a remote monitoring platform—particularly when symptom-triggered transmissions are not activated immediately after an event—does not definitively exclude an arrhythmia or pause event. Familiarity with the basic principles of AI-based algorithms used in remote monitoring systems is important, with algorithm information often available through device manufacturers’ websites or upon request. We also recommend that clinicians report cases of AI-based misclassification to device manufacturers, as real-world examples are essential for identifying algorithm limitations and guiding future improvements. In response to this case, Medtronic has indicated that future versions of the AccuRhythm AI algorithm will incorporate modifications informed by these findings. ILR vendors should continue refining their algorithms to identify pitfalls such as that demonstrated in this case report and implement iterative updates to improve performance. If feasible, providing clinicians access to unfiltered data, or at minimum an indication that AI filtering has occurred on specific dates and times, may help identify clinically important events that would otherwise remain concealed.
