Effect of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum Y279 and Weissella cibaria Y113 inoculation on lipid metabolism and flavor formation in Yu Jiaosuan
Authors: Wenkang Hu, Hongyan Chen, Qin Cen, Shan Yu, Fuyi Hui, Xuefeng Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lipids are key precursors of aroma-active compounds and essential nutrients in Yu Jiaosuan (YJS), a traditional Chinese fermented fish product. This study evaluated the effects of co-inoculating Lactiplantibacillus plantarum Y279 and Weissella cibaria Y113 on microbial succession, lipid metabolism, and flavor formation during loach YJS fermentation. The inoculated group (HY) showed rapid acidification and effective suppression of spoilage bacteria, with Lactiplantibacillus and Weissella emerging as dominant functional taxa. Untargeted lipidomics identified 1224 lipid species, indicating accelerated triglyceride degradation and enhanced accumulation of lysophospholipids (LPC and LPE) in HY samples. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed intensified glycerophospholipid metabolism and unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis, providing key precursors for aroma development. Flavoromics detected 213 volatile compounds, with HY samples exhibiting increased esters and ketones and reduced fishy aldehydes. Multivariate and correlation analyses revealed a microbiota–lipid–flavor regulatory network linking lactic acid bacteria to lipid-derived aroma precursors. Sensory evaluation further confirmed enhanced umami intensity, aroma complexity, and reduced off-flavors. Overall, targeted co-inoculation effectively improved YJS quality through coordinated lipid remodeling and flavor maturation.

Introduction

Yu jiaosuan (YJS), a traditional Southwest Chinese loach-and-chili fermented food, is rich in fatty acids (FA), organic acids, and free amino acids (FAA), serving as a vital nutritional source (Jiang et al., 2022). However, limited industrial starter cultures often yield flavor quality inferior to traditional products. In traditional YJS, indigenous microbiota from raw materials and the processing environment are key quality determinants. Isolates from these sources exhibit superior environmental adaptability, flavor biogenesis, and pathogen antagonism. Consequently, mixed-culture fermentation with selected strains can accelerate the process and inhibit undesirable microbes, thereby enhancing both safety and overall product quality (Belleggia & Osimani, 2023).

Similar to other fermented foods, YJS quality is dictated by microbial metabolic activity. Dominant genera include Lactiplantibacillus, Weissella, Fructobacillus, and Pectobacterium (Jiang et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023), whose dynamic succession shapes the core microbiota and distinctive flavor profiles. Although influenced by environmental and processing factors (e.g., oxygen, temperature, and origin), the specific determinants of this succession and the microbial role in lipid transformation remain poorly understood. Indigenous L. plantarum Y279 enhances YJS acidity, amino nitrogen, and sensory attributes while reducing the thiobarbituric acid reactive substances and nitrite levels (Hui et al., 2024). Similarly, W. cibaria Y113 exhibits robust probiotic traits, antimicrobial activity, and γ-aminobutyric acid production (Liu et al., 2023).

Lipid metabolism drives fermented fish flavor via volatile carbonyls. Lipases hydrolyze triglycerides and glycerophospholipids into free fatty acids (FFAs) (Wang et al., 2022a), yielding ketones through β-oxidation or aldehydes via auto-oxidation (Feng et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2018). While general pathways exist, high-resolution dynamics of lipid subclasses like lysophospholipids remain poorly understood. This study maps over 1200 lipid species in YJS to decode precise metabolic flux, providing granular insights into the lipid-flavor nexus.

Autochthonous starter cultures promote lipid degradation and liberate free fatty acids (FFAs) (Casaburi et al., 2008), which are subsequently converted into volatiles that shape the flavor and sensory quality of fermented foods. Thus, autochthonous inoculation is a key strategy for the industrialization and flavor standardization of fermented YJS. Co-inoculation with W. cibaria Y113 and L. plantarum Y279 suppresses peroxide levels and lipoxygenase activity while elevating lipase activity in YJS (Chen et al., 2024), potentially promoting FAs release and aroma biogenesis. Thus, we hypothesized that this co-inoculation modulates microbial succession and lipid metabolism to optimize flavor development during fermentation. Untargeted lipidomic offers a holistic view of lipid composition and metabolic dynamics in food systems (Wu et al., 2015). This study utilized this approach to characterize lipid shifts in YJS during co-inoculated fermentation and elucidate how metabolic pathways drive flavor development. Our findings provide insights for optimizing fermentation processes, enhancing functional lipid accumulation, and improving the overall sensory quality of fermented YJS.

This study investigated the effects of natural and fortified fermentation (with L. plantarum Y279 and W. cibaria Y113) on YJS quality and identified drivers of microbial succession. Additionally, the correlations between differential lipid metabolites and core functional microorganisms involved in lipid transformation were characterized. These findings enhance the understanding of microbe–lipid interactions during YJS fermentation and provide a theoretical basis for standardized starter culture development for controlled industrial production.

Materials and methods

Preparation of YJS

Loach (Misgurnus anguillicaudatus), red chili, and seasonings were sourced from Huaxi Center Market (Guiyang, China). Loaches (30 ± 5 g, 12 ± 2 cm) were humanely euthanized following ethical guidelines. Red chilis were selected based on full ripeness and physical integrity, ensuring they were free of pests, diseases, or mechanical damage.

Loach and chili (1:10 w/w) were mixed with 4% ginger, 7% salt, and 3% Baijiu (52% ABV; Beijing Hongxing Co., Ltd.). Portions (200 g) were naturally fermented in 250 mL glass fermenters (Shubo Co., Ltd., China) at 25 ± 1 °C as the uninoculated control (ZY group) (Shi et al., 2025).

L. plantarum Y279 ensures product safety and stability via rapid acidification, while optimizing quality through intensified proteolysis and flavor biogenesis alongside suppressed lipid oxidation. Simultaneously, W. cibaria Y113 drives lipid remodeling by elevating lipase activity and FA accumulation, thus minimizing oxidative deterioration and supporting flavor development. L. plantarum Y279 and W. cibaria Y113 (Bo Microbiology, Shanghai, China) were activated in MRS broth for 18 h and subcultured twice. Cells were harvested by centrifugation (6000 ×g, 1 min, 4 °C) and resuspended in sterile saline to 1 × 107 CFU/g. 1% (v/w) L. plantarum Y279 and 1% (v/w) W. cibaria Y113 were placed in, homogenized, and designated as the inoculated group (HY group) (Chen et al., 2024).

YJS was fermented in sealed glass bottles at room temperature. Three biological replicates were collected at each sampling interval (0, 4, 8, 12, and 16 d) and stored at −80 °C.

Physiochemical analysis

5 g of sample was homogenized with 25 mL deionized water at 10,000 ×g for 2 min. The pH meter (PHS-3E, Shanghai, China) was employed to determine the pH in samples. Titratable acidity (TA) was analyzed using a titration method with 0.05 mol/L NaOH and described as lactic acid equivalents (g/Kg).

FAA were determined with reference to the method of (Yu et al., 2018) with minor modifications. The 1 g of sample was homogenized with 50 mL of 0.01 mol/L hydrochloric acid for 30 min, instead of 2 g of sample with 10 mL of 5% trichloroacetic acid. The mixture was then centrifuged at 10,000 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter membrane and injected into an automatic amino acid analyzer (A300, menbraPure GmbH) for determination. These modifications were made to accommodate differences in sample matrix and analyzer requirements.

DNA extraction, PCR amplification and Illumina sequencing

DNA was extracted by adopting the Rapid Bacterial Genomic DNA Isolation Kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) using the CTAB method. DNA concentration and purity were evaluated using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) by determining the absorbance ratios at 260/280 nm, with ratios of approximately 1.8 and, indicating acceptable DNA quality.

The bacterial 16S rRNA V3-V4 region was amplified with primers 338F (5′- ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′) and 806R (5′- GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). The bacterial 16S rRNA gene was sequenced by high-throughout sequencing (Illumina-Hiseq 2500, USA). The original sequences obtained were filtered, and chimeric sequences were removed with Trimmomatic software (version 0.33) and UCHIME software (version 8.1). Next, tags with less than 75% of the quality control length were filtered to yield high quality tag data. The high-quality sequences acquired were clustered and intercompartmentalised into operational units by USEARCH software (version 10.0). Prior to clustering, raw sequences were quality-filtered to remove low-quality reads, truncated sequences, and reads containing ambiguous bases. Chimeric sequences were identified and removed using both de novo and reference-based methods. Sequences were then clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at a 97% sequence identity threshold, and the most abundant sequence within each OTU was selected as the representative sequence. Low-abundance OTUs and non-target sequences were further filtered from the OTU table to reduce noise. The most abundant sequence within each OTU was selected as the representative sequence. To ensure the reliability of the sequencing data, the sequencing depth per sample was calculated, and all samples achieved a Good's coverage of >99%.

Lipidomics analysis

Samples (10 mg) were mixed with 50 μL methanol, vortexed, homogenized (30 Hz, 20 s), and centrifuged. Extraction solvent (1 mL MTBE:MeOH = 3:1, v/v) containing internal standards (12:0 Lyso PC, Cer(d18:1/4:0), PC(13:0/13:0); Avanti/Alabaster, USA) was added. The mixture was vortexed (15 min), followed by adding 200 μL water and vortexing (1 min). After centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 10 min), the upper organic phase (200 μL) was evaporated and reconstituted in 200 μL ACN:IPA (1:1, v/v) for LC-MS/MS.

Lipids were analyzed using an ExionLC AD UPLC coupled to a QTRAP® MS (Sciex) with a Thermo Accucore™ C30 column (2.6 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm). Mobile phase A was 6:4 ACN:H₂O and B was 1:9 ACN:IPA, both with 0.1% formic acid and 10 mM ammonium formate. The 20-min gradient (0.35 mL/min, 45 °C, 2 μL injection) was: 20% B (0 min), 30% (2 min), 60% (4 min), 85% (9 min), 90% (14 min), 95% (15.5–17.3 min), and re-equilibration to 20% B (20 min).

The ESI source (Analyst 1.6.3) parameters: 500 °C; spray voltage ±5500/4500 V; GS1/GS2/CUR at 45/55/35 psi; CAD at medium. MRM mode used nitrogen (5 psi) as collision gas. Mass calibration utilized polypropylene glycol. Pooled quality control (QC) samples were analyzed at regular intervals. PCA demonstrated tight QC clustering (Fig. S1A), confirming high analytical reproducibility and stability.

Flavor analysis

The samples were ground into a fine powder, and 0.2 g of the powder was transferred into a 20 mL headspace vial containing 0.2 g of NaCl and 10 μL of 50 μg/mL 2-octanol as an internal standard. For SPME analysis, the vials were equilibrated at 60 °C for 5 min, and a 120 μm DVB/CAR/PDMS fiber (Agilent, USA) was then exposed to the headspace for 15 min. After extraction, the volatile compounds were thermally desorbed in the GC injection port at 250 °C for 5 min in spitless mode.

Volatile organic compounds were analyzed using an Agilent 8890 GC-7000D MS system (Agilent, USA) with a DB-5MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). Helium (1.2 mL/min) served as the carrier gas. The oven program was: 40 °C (3.5 min), 10 °C/min to 100 °C, 7 °C/min to 180 °C, and 25 °C/min to 280 °C (5 min). MS was operated in EI mode (70 eV), using SIM for identification and quantification. Pooled QC samples were analyzed to ensure stability (Fig. S1B).

Sensory evaluation

Sensory evaluation was conducted to assess the aroma, taste, texture, and overall acceptability of YJS samples. The ethical approval was granted by Guizhou University Research Ethics Committee (Number: HMEE-GZU-2026-T005). Considering that samples with incomplete fermentation may have adverse effects on the health of the test participants, only the samples at the 16 d (fermentation endpoint) were selected. A semi-trained sensory panel consisting of 12 panelists (aged 22–30 years old) who had undergone training in sensory description and terminology standards were recruited. All participants provided informed consent before participating in the sensory evaluation. The evaluated attributes included overall aroma intensity, fermented aroma, fishy odor, off-odor, sourness, umami, bitterness, overall taste intensity, texture acceptability, and overall acceptability. The sensory attributes and scoring criteria for YJS was shown in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed in triplicate. Statistical significance was determined via Student's t-test using SPSS (v21.0). Multivariate analysis of lipidomics data was conducted in SIMCA (v16.0.2, Sartorius Stedim Data Analytics AB). K-means clustering was performed using the R package “k-cluster” (v3.1.1), and metabolic pathways were mapped against the KEGG database.

Results and discussion

Physicochemical properties and sensory

TA and pH are critical quality indicators in fermented foods, as they directly influence microbial growth and the formation of metabolic by-products (Yang et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 1A, the pH of YJS continuously decreased in both groups, reaching a stable level during the late fermentation stage. During the fermentation, the pH of the ZY group decreased from 5.90 to 3.93, while decreased from 5.72 to 3.67 in the HY group. On 16 d, the pH of the HY group was significantly lower than that of the ZY group (p < 0.05). This observation is consistent with previous studies (Chen et al., 2024), indicating good experimental stability and reproducibility. A rapid decline in pH effectively inhibits the proliferation of spoilage and pathogenic microorganisms, thereby enhancing the microbial safety of fermented foods (Zeng et al., 2013). Similarly, the TA content in both groups exhibited an increasing trend throughout fermentation. The TA increased from 0.84 g/kg to 2.90 g/kg in the ZY group, VTA = 0.129 g/kg/d, whereas it increased from 1.13 g/kg to 4.00 g/kg in the HY group, VTA = 0.179 g/kg/d, which was significantly higher than that of the ZY group (p < 0.05).Fig. 1(A) The pH and TA of YJS during fermentation. The elevated TA in the HY group is attributed to the dominance of inoculated LAB, which accelerated acid production compared to the slower, indigenous LAB-driven process in natural fermentation (Zeng et al., 2013). These results align with Hui et al. (2024), who reported similar pH (3.51) and TA (3.85 g/kg) levels in YJS inoculated with L. plantarum Y279 by 16 d. This suggests that W. cibaria Y113 does not antagonize or impede the acidifying capacity of L. plantarum Y279 during co-inoculation.

FAAs are pivotal taste-active compounds and aroma precursors (Wei et al., 2023) whose biogenesis is linked to microbial activity (Liang et al., 2020). 16 FAAs were quantified (Table 1) and categorized by their taste contributions: umami (ASP and GLU), sweetness (THR, SER, GLY, and ALA), and bitterness (TYR, LEU, ILE, VAL, PHE, LYS, HIS, ARG, and MET). Total amino acid content increased steadily during fermentation. By 16 d, umami-associated ASP (303.21 mg/100 g) and GLU (14.33 mg/100 g) were significantly higher in the HY group than in the ZY group, whereas bitter-associated TYR, ILE, and LYS were significantly lower (p < 0.05). This indicates that inoculation intensified umami while mitigating bitterness in YJS. The lower bitter-FAA levels in the HY group likely stem from: (1) the suppression of indigenous microbes with high endopeptidase activity (Guillot et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2023), or (2) the active conversion of bitter FAAs into acetylated derivatives or other metabolites by L. plantarum (Jiang et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2023). Bacterial richness (ACE and Chao1) increased temporally in both groups but was significantly lower in the HY group, likely because inoculated strains suppressed spoilage and pathogenic bacteria (Xiao et al., 2020). Conversely, the HY group exhibited higher microbial diversity (Shannon and Simpson indices), indicating a more balanced community distribution. LEfSe (LDA ≥ 4.0) identified 29 differentially abundant taxa between the HY and ZY groups across fermentation (Fig. S2), comprising 3 phyla, 4 orders, 5 families, 7 genera, and 7 species. Throughout all stages, Lactobacillaceae, Lactiplantibacillus, and Weissella emerged as consistent biomarkers in the HY group, underscoring the competitive dominance of the inoculated L. plantarum Y279 and W. cibaria Y113 starter cultures.

Lactiplantibacillus produces antimicrobial compounds and organic acids that rapidly lower pH to enhance food safety (Wu et al., 2015). Simultaneously, Weissella contributes to flavor biogenesis—specifically short-chain FAs, organic acids, and esters—while offering potential probiotic benefits (Yu et al., 2018). By 8 d, Pectobacterium emerged as an HY biomarker. Likely introduced via chili, this genus secretes cell wall-degrading enzymes (e.g., pectinases and cellulases) that facilitate tissue softening (Radke et al., 2024). This enzymatic activity liberates intracellular sugars and amino acids, providing essential precursors for volatile flavor biogenesis (Su et al., 2022).

Inoculated W. cibaria Y113 and L. plantarum Y279 effectively dominated YJS fermentation, distinguishing the HY group from the ZY control. This microbial dominance ensures fermentation stability and safety, underpinning the enhanced flavor and metabolic landscape of the final product.

Lipid molecular in YJS

Lipid species in YJS during fermentation were characterized using untargeted Lipidomics (Fig. S3). A total of 1224 lipids (6 classes, 32 subclasses) were detected in positive ion mode, and 718 lipids (4 classes, 17 subclasses) in negative mode. Major classes included glycerophospholipids (GPs, 46.55%), glycerolipids (GLs, 31.15%), sphingolipids (SPs, 15.24%), fatty acyls (FAs, 5.51%), sterolipids (STs, 1.49%), and isopentenolipids (PRs, 0.05%). Triglycerides (TGs) and phosphatidylcholine (PCs) were most abundant, accounting for 24.15% and 6.75%, followed by PEs (6.08%), consistent with previous reports (Chen et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2022b).

GPs and GLs predominated in both groups (Fig. 1C, D), where microbial hydrolysis and oxidation liberate linoleic and linolenic acids—critical precursors for volatile aldehydes and ketones. FAs (Fig. 1B) increased steadily, though a sharp decline in HY at 12 d suggests accelerated conversion into aroma compounds. These trends, mirrored by FFAs, underscore their pivotal role in flavor biogenesis (Flores, 2018).

SPs stabilized after an initial increase (Fig. 1F), while GPs and PRs (Fig. 1E) declined progressively. In HY, lower TG levels and DG fluctuations indicated accelerated TG degradation and delayed DG accumulation. Major phospholipids (PC, PE, PS) decreased as lysophospholipids (LPC, LPE, LPS) accumulated, signaling partial hydrolysis. Interestingly, HY maintained higher PC levels, potentially due to LPC-acyl-CoA-mediated resynthesis, which mitigated GP loss. Enhanced phospholipase activity and lipid oxidation likely drove these transformations (Guo et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2020).

Overall, inoculated LAB orchestrated lipid remodeling by promoting phospholipid hydrolysis and TG breakdown. Higher lysophospholipid levels in HY reflect intensified enzymatic and oxidative flux, facilitating precursor release. Combined with the selective enrichment of polyunsaturated FAs, these results highlight how targeted inoculation optimizes the YJS lipidome to enhance flavor complexity and functional lipid content.

Lipidomics analysis

Based on the Lipidomics results (Fig. 2), principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that the first principal component (PC1) explained 36.8% of the variance, while the second principal component accounted for 15.8%. Along PC1, the sample points were arranged according to fermentation time. ZY samples clustered at 12–16 d, indicating lipid stabilization after 12 d. Conversely, HY stabilization occurred as early as 8 d, suggesting that co-inoculation with L. plantarum Y279 and W. cibaria Y113 accelerated lipid accumulation and transformation during fermentation.Fig. 2The PCA and volcano plot of DELs in YJS during fermentation.Fig. 2

Differential expressed lipids (DELs, |Log₂FoldChange| > 1.5 and p-value <0.05) were identified to evaluate the impact of inoculation (Fig. 2). DEL counts were 82, 109, 89, 172, and 122 at 0, 4, 8, 12, and 16 d, respectively, with GLs and GPs being the most affected classes. Early shifts in GLs (0–4 d) suggest rapid transformation post-inoculation, while persistent GP variations indicate sustained metabolic involvement throughout fermentation. Collectively, LAB inoculation significantly modulates GL and GP dynamics, underpinning flavor biogenesis and fermentation progress in YJS.

Volcano plots (Fig. 2) summarized DEL dynamics, showing a predominant up-regulation of lipids in the HY group relative to the ZY control. Across fermentation, up-regulated DELs (20, 33, 61, 113, 81) consistently outnumbered down-regulated ones (19, 7, 76, 89, 24). Aligning with Jiang et al. (2022), inoculation promoted lipid metabolite accumulation in YJS, especially during late stages, driving the extensive transformation of the lipidomic profile.

Higher LPC and LPE levels in the HY group likely resulted from rapid PC and PE degradation during early fermentation. Furthermore, late-stage inoculation-induced antioxidant effects potentially slowed lysophospholipid degradation, causing production rates to exceed degradation (Zhang et al., 2018), consistent with findings in fermented Mandarin and Jinchang fish (Chen et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2022b). Notably, LPC and LPE are closely linked to volatile aldehyde and alcohol biogenesis (Chen et al., 2023). The significant increase in PC (17:1_20:4) in the HY group suggests enhanced resynthesis from LPC and acyl-CoA via lysophosphatidyltransferase (Wang et al., 2022b). Conversely, DG levels were markedly lower in the HY group, reflecting the robust glycerolipid catabolism of LAB in promoting TG and DG breakdown into FFAs (Navarro et al., 1997; Xiao et al., 2020). Overall, LAB inoculation remodeled the YJS lipidome by accelerating GL degradation and lysophospholipid accumulation, ultimately optimizing the flavor profile.

These findings demonstrate that LAB inoculation not only reshapes the lipidome but also governs the enzymatic and oxidative pathways driving flavor biogenesis. Overall, targeted inoculation accelerates lipidomic remodeling and lysophospholipid enrichment, ultimately enhancing the aromatic complexity of YJS.

Pathway analysis

Comparing ZY samples at 0 and 16 d revealed 525 DELs (228 up, 297 down). The simultaneous up-regulation of all ceramides (152) and acylcarnitines (51) alongside the down-regulation of all TGs (290) suggests microbial conversion of loach TGs into Cer and CAR. KEGG enrichment (Fig. 3A) confirmed these shifts involve sphingolipid metabolism, FA degradation, steroid biosynthesis, and primary bile acid/taurine metabolism. The enrichment of sphingolipid, steroid, and bile acid/taurine pathways reflects robust microbial homeostasis and active metabolic regulation during fermentation. As ceramides are essential sphingolipids, the enrichment of sphingolipid metabolism underscores the microbial conversion of TGs into ceramides. Specifically, 12-oxochenodeoxycholic and 6,7-diketolithocholic acids were significantly up-regulated, while taurochenodeoxycholic, taurocholic, and ursocholic acids, along with various sulfate-conjugated bile acids, were down-regulated. These shifts highlight robust bile acid turnover via the primary biosynthesis pathway during YJS fermentation.

To evaluate the impacts of co-inoculation, lipid dynamics in the HY group were analyzed between 0 and 16 d. Of the 530 identified DELs (231 up-regulated, 299 down-regulated), 36 ceramides were up-regulated and only one was down-regulated, mirroring the ceramide accumulation observed in natural fermentation. KEGG analysis (Fig. 3B) highlighted significant enrichment in the biosynthesis of unsaturated FAs, along with glycerophospholipid, linoleic acid, arachidonic acid, sphingolipid, primary bile acid, and ether lipid metabolism. Unsaturated FAs and linoleic acid derivatives serve as precursors for volatile compounds such as aldehydes, ketones, alcohols, and esters, enhancing aroma complexity. Notably, the enrichment of the biosynthesis of unsaturated FAs pathway explains the observed accumulation of unsaturated FAs in the inoculated group, indicating that L. plantarum Y279 and W. cibaria Y113 actively promote the formation of functional lipids during YJS fermentation (Liu et al., 2022; Widyastuti et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2024). Ether lipids and sphingolipids with antioxidant properties could further protect lipids from oxidative degradation, preserving both flavor and product stability.

To further elucidate the effects of L. plantarum Y279 and W. cibaria Y113 on lipid metabolism in YJS, a direct comparison was performed on the final products at 16 d (HY vs ZY). The inoculation process may affect the lipid metabolism of YJS by enhancing the fat breakdown of microorganisms. Exogenous L. plantarum Y279 or W. cibaria Y113, have strong abilities to produce lipases and phospholipases (Chen et al., 2024). The inoculation process may also inhibit the native microorganisms with lower lipolysis activity, further accelerating the conversion of complex lipids into simpler lipid types. Pathway enrichment analysis (Fig. 3C) underscores the significance of glycerophospholipid metabolism in shaping the flavor of YJS. The metabolic flux (Fig. 3D) initiates with the hydrolysis of GPs into FFAs or their oxidative conversion into phosphatidic acid. The subsequent generation of DG provides a pivotal substrate for the reciprocal transformation of PC and PE. Specifically, phospholipase C-mediated hydrolysis of PC/PE yields DG, which is concurrently utilized for phospholipid resynthesis. In the YJS fermentation system, the homeostasis of PE, PC, and PS is maintained by a suite of enzymes: phosphatidylserine decarboxylase (EC 4.1.1.65) converts PS to PE, while the reverse base-exchange is catalyzed by l-serine phosphatidylethanolamine phosphatidyltransferase (EC 2.7.8.29). Simultaneously, the Land's cycle—mediated by lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase—reincorporates acyl-CoA into LPC to regenerate PC, thereby sustaining the glycerophospholipid pool.

TGs and DGs represent the predominant GLs in YJS, with DG primarily arising from TG hydrolysis. This catabolic process is orchestrated by rate-limiting lipases and synergistic microbial enzymatic activity, liberating FAs (Feng et al., 2021). Consequently, co-inoculation accelerates the hydrolytic release of FAs. Furthermore, microbiota biotransform primary bile acids into more lipophilic and active secondary metabolites, which modulate lipid digestion and cholesterol homeostasis (Hu et al., 2022a). Microbial enzyme profiles dictate bile acid diversity (Collins et al., 2023), potentially underlying the reduced bile acid levels observed in inoculated YJS. Overall, co-inoculation drives extensive lipidomic remodeling across at least seven metabolic pathways; however, the precise enzymatic mechanisms involved warrant further investigation.

Flavor analysis

Volatile compounds define the sensory identity of fermented foods, directly shaping consumer perception and acceptability (Han et al., 2024). As essential flavor precursors, lipids generate characteristic aromas through enzymatic hydrolysis into FFAs, which subsequently undergo oxidative degradation to yield aldehydes, ketones, alcohols, and organic acids (Yao et al., 2025). To characterize flavor evolution in YJS, volatile profiles at 0 d and 16 d were compared. PCA (Fig. 4B) revealed a distinct separation between these time points, reflecting extensive modifications in the aroma composition. Notably, HY samples exhibited tighter clustering than the ZY group, suggesting that co-inoculation fosters greater flavor homogeneity and stability. At the fermentation endpoint (16 d), 213 volatiles were identified (Fig. 4A), primarily comprising esters (16.90%), terpenes (16.43%), ketones (15.02%), alcohols (11.73%), and aldehydes (10.32%). Consistent with previous reports linking LPC and LPE to flavor precursors (Kathuria et al., 2025), our findings suggest these lipids mediate the biogenesis of key aromatic compounds. The synergistic enrichment of glycerophospholipid and unsaturated fatty acid metabolic pathways in co-inoculated groups underscores a remodeled metabolic framework that favors the synthesis of lipid-derived volatiles (Li et al., 2024b). Collectively, co-inoculation orchestrates a profound reconfiguration of the lipidome, channeling glycerolipids and glycerophospholipids toward flavor-active pathways. However, the precise metabolic flux and enzymatic drivers underlying these transformations warrant further validation through targeted enzyme assays and isotope-labeling studies.

Beyond their sapid properties, FAAs function as pivotal precursors for volatile synthesis, with their metabolic trajectories tightly coupled with lipid degradation during YJS fermentation. The catabolism of branched-chain and sulfur-containing amino acids generates key intermediates whose biogenesis is frequently influenced by the oxidative state of lipids; conversely, FAA composition can modulate lipid breakdown by altering microbial redox conditions. In the co-inoculated (HY) group, the enrichment of sulfur-containing volatiles likely results from a synergistic interplay between intensified proteolysis and accelerated lipid oxidation. The concomitant activation of glycerophospholipid and linoleic acid metabolism provides a robust scaffold for this amino acid–lipid crosstalk, which collectively orchestrates the production of aroma-active molecules. This integrated metabolic interaction represents a decisive mechanism in shaping the signature flavor profile of YJS.

The accumulation of FFAs, LPE, LPI, and SPH significantly correlated with the biogenesis of key odorants such as benzyl acetate, ethyl butyrate, and pyrrole. These correlations underscore a metabolic axis where sustained TG lipolysis provides a steady flux of FFAs, which serve as foundational precursors for ester and aldehyde synthesis (McSweeney & Sousa, 2000; Smit et al., 2005). Beyond simple hydrolysis, the enrichment of LPE and LPI suggests a more complex role for lysophospholipids in shaping the YJS flavor landscape, potentially by regulating oxidative pathways or serving as signaling precursors in volatile biosynthesis.

Conversely, the systematic degradation of structural lipids (PC, PE, PS, and PA) suggests their recruitment as essential metabolic substrates, a premise supported by their negative correlation with flavor-active volatiles. This degradative flux likely underpins the observed lipidome remodeling and subsequent precursor release. Notably, the enrichment of SPH and its positive association with key odorants underscore its potential involvement in regulating microbial physiological homeostasis, thereby indirectly steering the enzymatic pathways of flavor development.

A holistic synthesis of lipidome-flavorsome correlations reveals that lipid-derived flavor formation is a complex, multi-pathway process. Enzymatic TG degradation significantly expands the FFA pool, providing essential precursors for the microbial synthesis of aromatic esters, ketones, and aldehydes. In the ZY group, the strong alignment of FFAs with esters and nitrogenous heterocycles underscores their function as key metabolic drivers that dictate the flavor landscape during fermentation.

Furthermore, LPE and LPI dynamics underscore an indirect yet pivotal modulation of the flavor matrix. Beyond signaling glycerophospholipid hydrolysis—which yields fatty acid precursors for flavor biogenesis—these lysophospholipids may function as endogenous surfactants. By regulating interfacial tension and oxidative stability within the fermentation system, LPE and LPI likely optimize the microenvironment for volatile synthesis and retention, thereby refining the signature sensory profile of the final product.

Flavor morphogenesis in fermented products is driven by the synergistic metabolic interplay between indigenous and introduced microbiota (Hu et al., 2022b). Through coordinated glycolysis, lipolysis, and proteolysis, these microbial communities transform substrates into flavor-active organic acids and alcohols. Principally, esters serve as cornerstone volatiles that define sensory identity and attenuate off-notes. This process is propelled by LAB-derived esterases, which catalyze the biogenesis of diverse ester species via transesterification and acidolysis (Pradal et al., 2025).

Beyond the production of alcohols, esters, and ketones, L. plantarum Y279 and W. cibaria Y113 possess an enzymatic repertoire that facilitates sophisticated flavor development. The synergistic action of citrate lyase, acetolactate synthase, and acetolactate decarboxylase enables the efficient conversion of citrate and pyruvate into diacetyl and acetoin. This specific metabolic pathway serves as a critical driver in enhancing the aromatic depth and sensory complexity of the fermentation system (Malekijahan et al., 2025).

Loach-derived lipids function as pivotal aromatic precursors within the YJS fermentation environment. The accumulation of lipid-derived volatiles (alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, and acids) is underpinned by the convergence of autoxidation, LOX-mediated pathways, and microbial β-oxidation (Hu et al., 2024; Que et al., 2023). Such mechanisms are consistent with those in Suanrou and Suanyu, where specific microbial consortia orchestrate ester synthesis and aromatic optimization (Wenkang et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2021). Furthermore, the role of halotolerant LAB in fish sauce—catalyzing the hydrolytic liberation of polyunsaturated FAs and their subsequent oxidative transformation into “fatty” volatile markers—highlights the universal importance of lipid-microbe interactions in shaping the sensory identity of fermented fish products (Han et al., 2023).

Subsequently, PLS-DA was employed to delineate the interrelationships between core microbiota, key lipids, and characteristic volatiles (Fig. 5B). Component 2 effectively captured the temporal succession across both groups, with variables co-clustering with late-phase samples (12–16 d) identified as primary drivers of the terminal flavor profile. Notably, the late-stage enrichment of Lactobacillus and Fructobacillus strongly correlated with SPH, ceramide species (Cer-NS, Cer-ADS), FFAs, and various lysophospholipids (LPC, LPE, LPI, and LPS). These findings underscore the role of core LAB in orchestrating lipidome remodeling to facilitate cumulative flavor biogenesis.

Altogether, our findings suggest that terminal flavor development is a systematic process driven by core LAB, facilitated by lipidome remodeling, and characterized by the synergistic accumulation of volatiles. The “microbiota-lipid-flavor” regulatory axis proposed in this study offers a comprehensive multi-omics framework for understanding flavor biogenesis, providing actionable insights for quality optimization via the modulation of microbial architecture and lipid metabolic flux. Although primarily based on correlative evidence, this study provides a crucial roadmap for deciphering the lipid-flavor nexus. Subsequent investigations incorporating enzyme activity assays and isotope-based metabolic flux analysis are essential to confirm the underlying biochemical pathways.

Sensory evaluation

Sensory profiling at 16 d revealed that HY exhibited a more intense and complex fermented aroma than ZY (Fig. 5C). This enhancement aligns with the significantly enriched profile of esters, ketones, and phenolic volatiles in the inoculated group. Notably, the suppression of fishy odors in HY was primarily attributed to the reduction of undesirable aldehydes, while the concomitant increase in alcohols suggests an active LAB-mediated aldehyde reduction that promotes aroma softening. Although sulfur-containing compounds were more prevalent in HY, their potential off-flavor contribution was likely masked by the robust aromatic background provided by the diverse ester and ketone matrix.

The HY group exhibited intensified sourness, corroborating its elevated titratable acidity (TA). As an inherent and desirable attribute of YJS, this increased acidity enhanced both taste intensity and overall acceptability. Heightened umami perception in HY aligned with the significant accumulation of aspartic and glutamic acids, while bitterness was attenuated by the reduction of bitter-tasting amino acids. Furthermore, TG hydrolysis facilitated the liberation of FFAs, providing essential precursors for the biogenesis of esters, ketones, and alcohols. This lipidome remodeling fostered the synthesis of desirable volatiles while concurrently suppressing off-flavors. Consequently, the HY group achieved superior aromatic intensity, enhanced umami, and higher acceptability, complemented by a significant reduction in fishy off-notes and bitterness.

These results indicate that the co-inoculation of L. plantarum Y279 and W. cibaria Y113 effectively translates lipid metabolic regulation into perceptible sensory improvements, thereby enhancing the sensory quality of YJS.

Conclusions

Inoculated fermentation with L. plantarum Y279 and W. cibaria Y113 significantly alters the physicochemical properties, microbial community, and lipid composition of YJS. The inoculation accelerated the fermentation process, resulting in lower pH, higher TA, and a richer amino acid profile that enhanced umami, reduced bitterness, and improved sensory quality. Lipidomics analysis revealed dynamic changes, particularly in GLs and GPs, which correlated with the formation of volatile flavor compounds. In addition, inoculated fermentation promoted the accumulation of desirable ketones and phenolic compounds while reducing aldehydes, highlighting its role in improving flavor complexity, sensory appeal, and overall product quality. However, this study was conducted under laboratory conditions, and the results may not fully reflect the industrial-scale. Future research should explore the synergistic mechanisms between L. plantarum Y279 and W. cibaria Y113, optimize inoculation ratios, and investigate the effects on shelf-life, sensory acceptability, and flavor stability in commercial YJS production. These efforts will provide a more comprehensive understanding of how targeted microbial inoculation can be leveraged to enhance the quality and sensory characteristics of fermented foods.
