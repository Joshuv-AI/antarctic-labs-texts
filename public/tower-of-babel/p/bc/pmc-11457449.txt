Assessing the causal relationship between non-small cell lung cancer and sepsis: a Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Huixin Cheng, Xuehan Wang, Juyi Yao, Na Guo, Jian Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA Two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) Analysis was used to assess the causal relationship between non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and sepsis.MethodSingle nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) closely associated with NSCLC were utilized as instrumental variables (IVs) in this study. The Inverse Variance Weighted (IVW) method was used as the primary method for MR analysis, supplemented by the Weighted median, Weighted model, and MR-Egger regression method. Sensitivity analysis was conducted to improve result robustness, and data from various sources were validated and integrated. Bonferroni tests were applied to adjust for multiple comparisons.ResultsAfter Bonferroni tests correcting the combined results, MR analysis revealed a significant association between genetically predicted NSCLC and an increased susceptibility to sepsis (odds ratios [OR]: 1.140, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.085–1.199, P = 2.61 × 10− 7). The combined results demonstrated that NSCLC is associated with a heightened risk of sepsis in patients under 75 years of age (OR: 1.085, 95%CI: 1.037–1.353, P = 3.84 × 10− 4). Furthermore, lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) was found to be potentially associated with an increased susceptibility to sepsis (OR: 1.040, 95% CI: 1.009–1.073, P = 1.16 × 10− 2). These results withstood multiple sensitivity analyses, demonstrating their robustness.ConclusionThis study confirms that NSCLC can significantly increase susceptibility to sepsis at the genetic level, providing valuable insights for the early identification of individuals at risk for sepsis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-024-13003-0.Keywords: Non-small cell lung cancer, Sepsis, Mendelian randomization, Causality, Genome-wide association study

Introduction

Sepsis is a clinical syndrome of life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated immune response to infection (sepsis 3.0) [1]. Despite advancements in anti-infective treatment, fluid resuscitation, organ support therapy, and other medical technologies, sepsis remains a leading cause of global mortality, accounting for 19.7%(95% confidence interval [CI]: 18.2-21.4%) [2]. Research indicates a steady increase in sepsis incidence over the past three decades, with an annual growth rate ranging between 8 and 16%. This upward trend is placing a considerable strain on global healthcare systems and presenting a substantial threat to human health [3, 4]. Moreover, individuals afflicted with sepsis may endure persistent physical, psychological, and cognitive impairments despite medical interventions [5]. Timely identification of sepsis in patients and prompt intervention have been shown to significantly improve the prognosis of patients [6]. Consequently, there is a critical need to actively investigate the risk factors associated with sepsis and develop early identification methods.

In a global context, lung cancer is one of the most common cancers worldwide, representing 11.6% of all cancer cases. It is also a leading cause of cancer-related mortality, with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) being the most prevalent subtype (accounting for 85%) [7–9]. The failure of the immune system to adequately regulate the aberrant proliferation and transformation of malignant cells serves as a significant factor in cancer development [10], while sepsis, which arises when the host’s immune system is unable to ward off pathogen invasion, shares analogous pathophysiological features with malignancies [11]. The correlation between cancer and sepsis has long been a longstanding concern in the medical field, with numerous studies yielding conflicting conclusions. At present, many studies support the idea that lung cancer causes sepsis. A prospective study indicated that certain cancer survivors, including lung cancer, face an elevated risk of sepsis [12]. Rosolem et al. revealed that sepsis is a prevalent complication among cancer patients, with solid tumors accounting for 77% of these cases [13]. Additionally, an epidemiological study revealed that cancer patients are almost 10 times more likely to develop sepsis compared to non-cancer patients [14], other observational studies have corroborated these findings [15, 16]. It is hypothesized that the heightened susceptibility to sepsis among cancer patients is linked to immunosuppression and specific anti-tumor treatments [17]. However, some research posits that sepsis may elevate the risk of subsequent cancer development. For instance, Liu Z et al. identified a correlation between sepsis and nine types of cancer (lung, thyroid, prostate, colon, rectum, and liver and follicular lymphoma, melanoma, and acute myeloid leukemia), noting an increased cancer risk within five years following sepsis diagnosis [18]. Furthermore, a recent epidemiological study echoed these findings, reporting a higher one-year incidence of cancer among sepsis survivors [19]. Regarding the underlying mechanisms, some researchers suggest that sepsis-induced immunosuppression may facilitate tumor cell evasion, thereby contributing to increased cancer susceptibility [17, 20]. It is worth noting that no consensus exists regarding the relationship between lung cancer and sepsis; thus, further research is essential to elucidate this connection.

Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis is a method that simulates randomized controlled trials by utilizing single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as instrument variables to evaluate the causal relationship between exposure and outcome [21]. One of the key advantages of MR compared to traditional observational studies is the utilization of a large sample size, which improves statistical power. Additionally, MR can effectively address issues related to reverse causality since genotypes are determined at birth and remain constant regardless of the outcome. The relationship between the NSCLC and sepsis is intricate and unclear. In this study, MR analysis was employed, utilizing genetic variation data derived from a large genome-wide association study (GWAS) mainly to explore the causal relationship between NSCLC and sepsis.

Methods

Study design

In this study, we systematically assessed the causal relationship between sepsis and NSCLC through a two-sample MR design. To ensure the validity of instrumental variables (IVs) in MR Studies, three critical assumptions should be satisfied: (1) SNPs are directly associated with exposure; (2) SNPs are independent of confounders; (3) SNPs must solely influence the outcomes through their impact on the exposure. GWAS data for NSCLC

We obtained GWAS datasets for NSCLC from the International Lung Cancer Consortium (ILCCO) [22] and the FinnGen cohort (https://www.finngen.fi/en). ILCCO comprised 11,348 NSCLC patients and 15,861 controls, while the FinnGen cohort included 4,901 cases of NSCLC and 287,137 controls. Additionally, genetic data for lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) and lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) were extracted from two databases for subgroup analysis (Supplementary file 1: Table S1).

GWAS data for sepsis

Publicly accessible summary statistics estimating the associations between genetic variants and sepsis, as well as its subgroups (defined by under 75, critical care, 28-day death, 28-day death in critical care), were acquired from the IEU Open GWAS. These summary-level data were derived from the UK Biobank, encompassing a cohort of 11,643 sepsis cases and 474,841 control subjects. The GWAS study was performed using regenie v2.2.4, adjusted for age, sex, chip, and the first 10 PCAs (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk). Further details on the sepsis subgroup can be found in Table S1 of the Supplementary file 1.

Selection of IVs

The IVs utilized in this MR analysis satisfied the three key assumptions. Initially, for NSCLC, we selected genetic variants with association thresholds at P < 5 × 10− 8. Subsequently, a clumping procedure based on R software was used to identify independent variants, setting the linkage disequilibrium (LD) threshold of r2 < 0.1 within a 1000 kilobase (kb) region. Independent and nominally associated SNPs (P < 5 × 10− 6, r2 < 0.1 within 1000 kb) were extracted for LUAD and LUSC. The F-statistic was employed to assess the validity of IVs. A threshold exceeding 10 was considered indicative of sufficient instrument strength. The F-statistic was calculated using the following formula: F = R² × (N - k − 1) / [(1 - R²) × k], where N denotes the sample size of the exposure, k represents the number of SNPs, and R² indicates the proportion of variance in the exposure variable explained by the SNP [23]. Finally, during the harmonization process of the exposure and outcome datasets, we excluded SNPs that were palindromic, had minor allele frequencies ≤ 0.01, or exhibited mismatched alleles [24]. All IVs selected for this MR study are detailed in Supplementary file 1: Table S2. For the reverse MR analysis, we extracted independent and nominally associated SNPs (P < 1 × 10− 5, r2 < 0.1 within 500 kb) for sepsis and its subgroup.

Statistical analysis

The Inverse Variance Weighted (IVW) method is widely regarded as one of the most robust methods in MR analysis [25]. In our study, we primarily utilized the IVW method to estimate the association between NSCLC and sepsis, complemented by the Weighted median, Weighted mode, and MR-Egger regression methods [26–28]. To enhance the robustness of our findings, we independently validated the cancer data from different sources and combined their results. Heterogeneity among individual IVs was assessed using Cochran’s Q test [29], and potential outliers were identified through MR Pleiotropy Residual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO) [30]. To address the potential influence of horizontal pleiotropy on the results, we utilized the intercept value of MR-Egger and the global test of MR-PRESSO for detection [26]. Visualization of heterogeneity and pleiotropy was achieved through funnel plots and scatter plots. To avoid false positives from multiple tests, we used the Bonferroni correction for significance (Bonferroni-corrected P < 0.05/6, 6 = 3exposure×2different sources) [31]. Significant causal relationships were considered when the P-value (Bonferroni) was less than 0.0083, while P-values (Bonferroni) between 0.05 and 0.0083 were viewed as indicative of potential causal relationships. Additionally, a leave-one-out analysis was conducted to evaluate the influence of individual SNPs on the MR analysis.

We utilized a phenome-wide association test to identify the phenotypes linked to each genetic instrument [32], excluding SNPS associated with potential confounders (PhenoScanner V2: http://www.phenoscanner.medschl.cam.ac.uk). Subsequently, we conducted MR analyses to reassess the findings without confounding factors, to confirm the significance of the association between exposure and outcome. All statistical analyses were performed using R4.3.1 software.

Results

Selection of IVs

A two-sample MR analysis was performed with GWAS data on NSCLC from two distinct sources to investigate the causal connection between NSCLC and sepsis. The number of selected IVs for the study ranged from 7 to 21. (Supplementary file 1: Table S2). The F-statistic for each IVs surpassed 10, signifying their adequate strength (Supplementary file 1: Table S3).

Causal effects of NSCLC on sepsis and sepsis subgroup

Using the IVW method, the adjusted multiple-test correction revealed a significant association between genetically predicted NSCLC and increased susceptibility to sepsis in both the ILCCO and FinnGen databases. The odds ratios (OR) were 1.150 (95% CI: 1.080–1.223, P = 1.08 × 10− 5) in the ILCCO database and 1.123 (95% CI: 1.033–1.221, P = 6.68 × 10− 3) in the FinnGen database. A combined result from these two data sources showed an OR of 1.140 with a P-value of 2.61 × 10− 7 (95CI%: 1.085–1.199). Notably, a strong positive relationship was observed between NSCLC and sepsis (under 75) in the ILCCO database (OR: 1.089, 95% CI: 1.025–1.157, P = 5.46 × 10− 3). Conversely, the FinnGen database only identified a potential association after adjusting for multiple comparisons (OR: 1.080, 95%CI: 1.009–1.156, P = 2.66 × 10− 2). However, the combined results revealed a significant correlation between NSCLC and sepsis (under 75) (OR: 1.085, 95%CI: 1.037–1.353, P = 3.84 × 10− 4). In addition, an association between LUAD and increased susceptibility to sepsis was identified. While the ILCCO database and combined results suggested a potential association between LUAD and sepsis (ILCCO: OR: 1.040, 95%CI: 1.006–1.076, P = 2.26 × 10− 2; combined results: OR: 1.040, 95%CI: 1.009–1.073, P = 1.16 × 10− 2), the results were not statistically significant in the FinnGen database (Fig. 2). In addition, a potential association between LUAD and sepsis (critical care) was only observed in the ILCCO databases (OR: 1.137, 95%CI: 1.007–1.285, P = 3.89 × 10− 2). A significant association was observed between LUSC and sepsis (OR: 1.105, 95%CI: 1.049–1.164, P = 1.58 × 10− 4), as well as its subgroup (under 75) (OR:1.069, 95%CI: 1.023–1.117, P = 2.78 × 10− 3), exclusively within the ILCCO database, no such association was identified in the FinnGen database or the combined result (Fig. 2). Detailed analysis results are presented in Table S3 of the Supplementary file 1. In the ILCCO and FinnGen databases, the sensitivity analyses of all results were present in the Supplementary file 1: Table S3. The funnel plot and scatter plot displaying significant results are depicted in Figs. 3 and 4. Additionally, we conducted a search in the PhenoScanner database for SNPs linked to sepsis. Following the exclusion of outliers and potentially confounding SNPs, our findings demonstrated similar statistical values and directional trends compared to the original analysis (Supplementary file 1: Table S5). Leave-one-out analyses are shown in Supplementary flie 2: Fig. 1.

Causal effects of sepsis on NSCLC and its subgroup

In reverse analysis, a potential association was revealed between sepsis (28-day death in critical care) and LUAD susceptibility in the ILCCO database (OR: 0.960, 95%CI: 0.924–0.998, P = 4.01 × 10− 2). However, the findings did not attain statistical significance after adjusting for multiple tests in the combined result. In addition, data from the ILCCO database suggested that sepsis (critical care) may increase susceptibility to LUAD (OR: 1.094, 95CI%: 1.006–1.190, P = 3.49 × 10− 2), but when integrated with the FinnGen database, the results did not demonstrate statistical significance (Fig. 2). Sensitivity analysis revealed no heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy, and the MR-PRESSO analysis did not identify any outliers. The detailed results of the reverse analysis are shown in Table S4 of the Supplementary file 1. Leave-one-out analyses are shown in Supplementary flie 2: Fig. 2.

Discussion

In the two-sample MR study, we integrated results from diverse databases, which revealed a causal relationship between gene-predicted NSCLC and sepsis. Our analysis indicates that NSCLC may heighten the risk of developing sepsis, especially in patients under 75 years of age. Moreover, a potential causal relationship may exist between LUAD and sepsis. In the reverse analysis, we identified a potential association between sepsis (28-day death in critical care) and susceptibility to LUAD exclusively within the ILCCO database. However, the combined results did not achieve statistical significance after adjustment for multiple tests.

Based on inconsistent findings in observational studies, the relationship between NSCLC and sepsis has remained a controversial topic. Our analysis of data from two independent databases revealed that NSCLC significantly increases the susceptibility to sepsis, confirming and expanding on previous research findings [12, 14]. Previous studies have demonstrated that cancer and sepsis exhibit common pathophysiological features. The immune dysregulation linked to these conditions encompasses changes in both the quantity and functionality of innate and adaptive immune cells [8]. The primary cause of cancer-induced immunosuppression arises from acquired immune dysfunction associated with cancer treatment, particularly chemotherapy and radiotherapy. These therapies can lead to a reduction in neutrophil counts and impair phagocytic function [33]. In addition, various chemotherapy regimens may reduce the number of T cells or B cells, alter the function of lymphocytes and natural killer cells, and thereby raise the risk of infection. A study conducted in mice illustrated that lung cancer causes T cell depletion, leading to post-infection immune insufficiency that heightens susceptibility to sepsis [34]. Furthermore, the evidence indicates that patients diagnosed with NSCLC are at a heightened risk of recurrent infection, which may be related to the tumor disease itself as well as factors such as advanced age, smoking history, presence of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and immunosuppression linked to lung cancer treatment [35, 36]. This finding has been validated at the genetic level in our study. Subgroup analysis revealed that patients with LUAD were more susceptible to developing sepsis; however, this conclusion was only considered to be potentially related after multiple corrections. The underlying mechanism behind this association remains unclear and necessitates further investigation. Future research is anticipated to explore the molecular mechanisms involved in its occurrence. Previous research revealed that patients with both sepsis and cancer experienced a higher in-hospital mortality rate compared to non-cancer patients, with a 43% increase in mortality among cancer patients combined with sepsis [15]. However, the extent of this association varied significantly among different types of cancer, with hematological malignancies exhibiting a higher mortality rate linked to sepsis compared to patients with other cancer types [15, 37]. Notably, our study did not find an association between NSCLC and sepsis mortality.

Our research has several strengths compared to previous studies. Firstly, we analyze the relationship between NSCLC and sepsis as well as its subgroups, utilizing a large-scale GWAS dataset with a substantial sample size. This approach effectively addresses the limitations of prior studies that relied on smaller sample sizes. In addition, we utilized NSCLC data from two distinct databases, which improved the statistical power of our analysis and avoided potential bias from relying on a single database. Finally, the MR analysis employed in our study effectively minimizes the impact of confounding factors and reverse causality. We further enhanced the reliability of our results by excluding SNPs associated with other sepsis risk factors using PhenoScanner.

However, the study has several limitations. Firstly, to mitigate bias stemming from demographic stratification, the collection of GWAS data is limited to European populations, thereby restricting the generalizability of our findings to non-European populations. Secondly, we were unable to perform stratified analyses based on age, sex, or disease severity. The absence of these stratifications hinders a nuanced understanding of potential variations in findings across different stratified populations. Finally, due to the predominance of epidemiological and observational studies in this specific field [12–16, 18, 19], there is a lack of foundational research that delves deeper into the mechanisms underlying this association. Consequently, our elucidation regarding the increased susceptibility to sepsis in NSCLC patients remains insufficient. Further studies are required to delve into the specific mechanisms connecting these conditions.

Conclusions

The MR analysis, based on the ILCCO and FinGenn databases, confirms that NSCLC can significantly increase sepsis susceptibility at the genetic level. The intricate mechanisms underlying the interaction between NSCLC and sepsis require further extensive investigation from multiple perspectives. Our study also provides valuable insights for the early identification of individuals at risk for sepsis.
