APE1/Ref-1: multifunctional biology, selective inhibition, and the path to clinical translation
Authors: Jessica A. Occhiuto, Silpa Gampala, Melissa L. Fishel, Mark R. Kelley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Apurinic/apyrimidinic endonuclease 1/redox factor-1 (APE1/Ref-1) is a multifunctional stress-response regulator that coordinates genome maintenance, redox signaling, RNA biology, and cellular metabolism. Its expression and subcellular localization further determine disease states and severity. The growing appreciation of its biological complexity and clinical relevance makes APE1/Ref-1 an increasingly attractive therapeutic target for redox-stress-related diseases.Areas covered:In this review, we aim to consolidate information on the structural and mechanistic basis of APE1/Ref-1 redox and repair functions, while recognizing emerging evidence in DNA/RNA-forming G-quadruplex (rG4) biology, RNA metabolism, protein homeostasis, and mitochondrial function. We discuss mechanisms regulating APE1/Ref-1 expression, activity, and trafficking, which dynamically influence function in physiological and disease contexts. Continued investigation into the context-dependent and multifunctional roles of APE1/Ref-1, together with the progression of mechanism-informed therapeutic design, is steadily strengthening the translational potential of APE1/Ref-1-directed therapies.

Introduction

Apurinic/apyrimidinic endonuclease 1/redox factor-1 (APE1/Ref-1) is a multifunctional protein that plays central roles in DNA repair, redox signaling, and cellular stress adaptation. Initially characterized as the major apurinic/apyrimidinic endonuclease (APE1) within the base excision repair (BER) pathway [1], APE1 preserves genomic integrity by processing abasic DNA lesions generated during oxidative and alkylation damage [2]. This repair activity is essential for maintaining cell survival under genotoxic stress and is highly conserved across evolution, from bacteria to mammals [3,4], underscoring its fundamental importance in normal cellular physiology. Elevated APE1 expression has been observed in multiple disease settings, including cancer and inflammatory disorders, where cells often exhibit increased dependence on DNA damage response pathways. Consequently, inhibition of APE1 endonuclease activity has emerged as an attractive therapeutic strategy for sensitizing diseased cells to chemotherapy, radiation, and oxidative stress. However, because BER is also indispensable in normal tissues, global suppression of APE1 repair activity presents significant concerns regarding systemic toxicity and therapeutic selectivity. These challenges have shifted interest toward context-dependent or combination-based targeting strategies designed to exploit differential stress thresholds between normal and diseased cells while minimizing collateral damage.

In parallel with its DNA repair function, APE1/Ref-1 possesses a mechanistically distinct redox regulatory activity (Ref-1) mediated primarily through its N-terminal domain [5–7]. Through this function, Ref-1 modulates the DNA-binding activity of several transcription factors, including NF-κB, STAT3, HIF-1α, AP-1, and p53, thereby coupling intracellular redox status to transcriptional programs governing proliferation, inflammation, angiogenesis, metabolism, hypoxic adaptation, and cell survival. Unlike the endonuclease function, the redox activity is not essential for basal cell viability, making it a more therapeutically tractable target for selective modulation in disease states characterized by aberrant stress signaling [8,9]. This distinction has driven substantial interest in redox-selective therapeutic strategies, particularly in cancer [10], inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [11–14], neuroinflammatory disorders [15,16], and ocular diseases such as diabetic retinopathy [17–19], where dysregulated inflammatory and oxidative signaling contribute to disease progression. Importantly, emerging evidence now suggests that APE1/Ref-1 functions far beyond a conventional dual-function repair/redox protein. Recent studies have implicated APE1/Ref-1 in RNA metabolism [20,21], DNA/RNA G-quadruplex (G4) biology [22–24], microRNA processing, protein homeostasis [25,26], mitochondrial regulation [27], and cellular bioenergetics [28], broadening its role as a central coordinator of stress-adaptive signaling networks. Interactions with oxidatively modified G-quadruplex structures further suggest that APE1/Ref-1 may integrate genome stability with transcriptional regulation in response to oxidative stress. Additionally, APE1/Ref-1 has been proposed to exhibit redox chaperone-like activity by maintaining transcription factors in a reduced and functionally competent state [29,30].

Consistent with this broad functional repertoire, APE1/Ref-1 is ubiquitously expressed and essential for embryonic development, with genetic ablation resulting in early lethality and profound defects in cellular homeostasis [31–33]. Further, its dynamic localization across nuclear, cytoplasmic, and mitochondrial compartments underscores its role as a central coordinator of cellular responses to oxidative [34], inflammatory, and metabolic stress [27,35]. Collectively, advances in understanding the canonical and noncanonical functions of APE1/Ref-1 have substantially expanded therapeutic interest in selectively modulating APE1/Ref-1 and laid the foundation for diverse mechanism-informed therapeutic strategies discussed throughout this review [36]. Herein, we summarize the most recent understanding of APE1/Ref-1 biology, beginning with the structural basis of activity and regulation, and then leading into the top disease states for the clinical application of these modulators, including cancer, ocular diseases, and gastrointestinal diseases. Finally, we conclude with discussion of current and near-future clinical translation of APE1/Ref-1 targeted therapies through the specific lens of redox activity modulation.

APE1/Ref-1 redox biology and mechanism of action

Structural basis of redox activity

The redox regulatory activity of APE1/Ref-1 is structurally and mechanistically distinct from its endonuclease function and is primarily mediated through its flexible N-terminal region [1,30] (Figure 1). Unlike the highly conserved catalytic residues responsible for DNA repair, the redox function depends on a network of cysteine residues that collectively regulate the protein’s ability to maintain transcription factors in a reduced, DNA-binding competent state [37,38]. Structural and biochemical studies have demonstrated that this redox-active architecture is dynamically influenced by intramolecular interactions, conformational flexibility, and local oxidative conditions, enabling Ref-1 to function as a context-dependent regulator of stress-responsive transcriptional signaling [39]. Ref-1 contains seven cysteine residues (C65, C93, C99, C138, C208, C296, and C310), among which C65 serves as the primary redox-active residue responsible for reducing transcription factors such as NF-κB and STAT3, thereby promoting their DNA-binding and transcriptional activity [40,41]. The remaining cysteines do not directly mediate electron transfer but instead play supportive and regulatory roles that influence redox competence. Specifically, C93 and C99 in the N-terminal region are proposed to maintain a conformation permissive for redox function and to participate in intramolecular thiol-exchange reactions, though their roles secondary to C65 in this function. C138, located within the structured core, contributes to protein stability and proper folding [42,43]. C208, C296, and C310 in the C-terminal region are thought to act as redox-sensitive residues that undergo reversible oxidative modifications or transient disulfide formation under conditions of oxidative stress. This cysteine-centered organization regulates the structural dynamics and local thiol environment, thereby governing C65 accessibility and reactivity and, collectively, defining the functional state of APE1/Ref-1 [44–46]. Together, this coordinated cysteine network establishes a structurally adaptable framework that enables APE1/Ref-1 to respond to cellular redox cues and effectively mediate the reduction of transcription factors such as NF-κB, STAT3, and HIF-1α [38,47].

Upstream regulation of APE1/Ref-1 activity

APE1/Ref-1 expression and activity are regulated by cellular stress, but the mechanisms vary by stimulus and cell type. Oxidative stress represents a major upstream stimulus for APE1/Ref-1 expression, as reactive oxygen species not only generate DNA lesions that require base excision repair but also activate signaling pathways that enhance APE1/Ref-1 transcription [25]. In mammalian cells, APE1/Ref-1 is induced after oxidant exposure, participates in clastogenic adaptation, and requires CREB, supporting a defined transcriptional mechanism rather than a nonspecific stress response [48–50]. Additionally, Hsieh et al. showed that APE1/Ref-1 redox activation is mediated by reactive oxygen species and PKC phosphorylation [51]. In inflammatory epithelial models, Helicobacter pylori and H2O2 increase APE1/Ref-1 expression in human gastric epithelial cells, directly linking infection-associated oxidative stress to elevated APE1/Ref-1 levels [52]. Follow-up investigation in related gastric epithelial cell studies by O’Hara and colleagues demonstrated that APE1/Ref-1 is required for H. pylori-induced IL-8 expression and later showed that TNF-α-induced IL-8 expression involves ROS and APE1/Ref-1, supporting a functional role for APE1/Ref-1 in cytokine-linked inflammatory signaling rather than proving that cytokines universally induce APE1 transcription [53,54]. Further, in macrophage models, asbestos exposure increased nuclear Ref-1 and AP-1 DNA-binding activity, further connecting oxidant stress to Ref-1-associated transcription factor regulation in inflammatory cell systems [55]. Therefore, oxidative and inflammatory stress increase APE1/Ref-1 expression, activity, or nuclear availability in specific epithelial, macrophage, and mammalian cell models, while APE1/Ref-1, in turn, regulates downstream inflammatory transcriptional outputs through redox-dependent effects on transcription factors such as NF-κB and AP-1.

Apart from oxidative stress, post-translational modifications (PTMs) also regulate APE1/Ref-1 expression and activity. Tell et al. summarized that Sp1, Egr-1, STAT3, CREB, and AP-1/ATF4 increase APE1/Ref-1 expression, while autoregulatory loops involving APE1/Ref-1 itself, Egr-1, and p53 further modulate its abundance [5]. Functional activation also occurs through rapid cytoplasmic-to-nuclear translocation, controlled by defined NLS/NES regions and protein interactions [56,57]. In addition, PTMs such as acetylation (of N-terminal lysines, including K6/K7) [58–61], phosphorylation (by protein kinase C, Casein kinases, Cyclin-dependent kinase 5 and Ataxia-Telangiectasia mutated proteins) [51,62,63], S-nitrosation (of C93 and C310) [39], and proteolytic cleavage dynamically regulate APE1/Ref-1’s transcriptional activity, subcellular localization, mitochondrial/nucleolar trafficking, and interactions with DNA repair and RNA-processing pathways [64,65]. Zhang et al. demonstrated that methylation of APE1/Ref-1 by PRMT1 at R301 promotes mitochondrial translocation of APE1/Ref-1 to protect cells from oxidative damage [66]. More recently, our published and ongoing work using RNA sequencing and metabolic functional assays demonstrated that APE1/Ref-1 exerts redox-mediated control over the tumor mitochondrial metabolic signature. Alongside PTMs regulating repair or redox functions of APE1/Ref-1, they also turn it into a DNA/RNA G-quadruplex reader. Under stress, guanine-rich regions within DNA or RNA fold into unusual four-stranded secondary structures called G-quadruplexes (G4S) and recruit APE1/Ref-1 [20,67,68]. The binding of APE1/Ref-1 to these G4S functions as a molecular switch that triggers an epigenetic-like signaling mechanism that alters gene expression or contributes to RNA quality control, ribosome biogenesis, and nucleolar stress responses [69–74]. Changes to the N-terminal domain, including acetylation, protein binding partners like NPM1 and PARP1, and high physiologic potassium ion levels, are some of the regulators of APE1/Ref-1 recruitment to G4S, especially in oxidative damage-relevant diseases like cancer and neuro-degenerative diseases [22,24,72]. Together, these studies highlight APE1/Ref-1 as a multifunctional stress-responsive protein whose activities are dynamically regulated through transcriptional control, subcellular localization, post-translational modifications, and interactions with DNA/RNA secondary structures to coordinate genome stability, transcription, and RNA metabolism.

APX3330 and next-generation analogs: mechanism and selectivity

APX3330, formerly referred to as E3330 ((2E)-3-[5-(2,3-dimethoxy-6-methyl-1,4-benzoquinoyl)]-2-nonyl-2- propenoic acid), is a first-in-class small molecule inhibitor of APE1/Ref-1 redox activity. Originally identified in the early 1990s through a compound screen for therapeutic management of endotoxin-mediated hepatitis [75], APX3330 was found to suppress TNFα production in LPS-stimulated myeloid cells [76]. This discovery informed the hypothesis that APX3330 could inhibit NF-κB transcriptional activity, and by the end of the decade, this had been demonstrated to occur through inhibition of NF-κB DNA binding, though the specific mechanism remained unknown [77]. At the turn of the 21st century, a novel method of receptor identification using high-performance affinity latex beads identified APE1/Ref-1 as the target protein for APX3330, rather than NF-κB [78]. Subsequently, heteronuclear single quantum coherence (HSQC) NMR studies demonstrated that APX3330 reversibly binds to APE1/Ref-1 and induces partial protein unfolding, a conformational change that renders it less capable of activating target transcription factors, with no impact on DNA repair functionality [79]. Following these preliminary findings and complementing mechanistic studies, APX3330 was tested preclinically in a wide range of indications, many of which are discussed in the sections below. Importantly, APX3330 is orally bioavailable, with a pharmacokinetic profile supporting once- or twice-daily dosing, and has advanced to clinical trials. A first-in-human dose-escalation study (NCT03375086) in patients with advanced solid tumors demonstrated a favorable safety profile and stable disease in approximately one-third of patients [36]. Additional early-stage trials have reinforced this initially reported safety profile [80], and APX3330 has progressed to stage II trials for non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (ZETA-1, NCT04692688). Completion of the ZETA-1 trial revealed a clinically significant ebbing of disease progression, leading to the approval of an SPA for a phase III trial, which is currently on hold. However, while APX3330 demonstrated efficacy in retinal tissues, a more potent retinal analog has led to the synthesis of second-generation analogs. Targeted structure-activity relationship studies led to the napthoquinone derivatives APX2009 and APX2014 (among others), which demonstrate improved potency and increased permeation across the blood-retinal barrier and the blood-brain barrier, respectively [81–84].

Structurally, these second-generation analogs diverge from APX3330 by replacing its 2,3-dimethoxy-1,4-benzoquinone core with a 1,4-naphthoquinone scaffold and by shortening and amidating the side chain, converting the 2-nonyl propenoic acid of APX3330 into a propyl-substituted amide. This yields APX2009, an N,N-diethyl amide, and APX2014, an N-methoxy amide, with the two compounds differing only in this amide substituent [28]. These modifications markedly increase redox potency: in electrophoretic mobility shift assays of Ref-1-dependent AP-1 DNA binding, the half-maximal inhibitory concentration decreases from approximately 25 μM for APX3330 to 0.45 μM for APX2009 and 0.2 μM for APX2014 [28]. Critically, this gain in potency does not compromise selectivity, as APX2009 and APX2014 continue to inhibit the redox function while leaving the endonuclease and base excision repair activity of APE1/Ref-1 intact, preserving the redox-selective mechanism of the parent compound [28,82]. Comparative preclinical profiling further supports improved drug-like properties for the naphthoquinone series, including greater metabolic stability in mouse and human S9 fractions, favorable plasma half-life and solubility, and the enhanced barrier permeability that underlies the ocular activity of APX2009 and the central nervous system exposure of APX2014 [82]. Formal comparative toxicology in humans is not yet available, because APX2009 and APX2014 remain at the preclinical stage, but their preserved redox selectivity, which spares the essential DNA repair function, is expected to maintain a tolerability profile comparable to that observed for APX3330

APE1/Ref-1 in cancer: preclinical rationale and translational evidence

While both the endonuclease and redox functions of APE1/Ref-1 are essential for maintaining healthy normal tissue, the signaling pathways and cell-survival advantages they confer can also promote the progression of malignant cells and tumor growth. These processes have been extensively studied in the context of many cancer subtypes, including pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, gastrointestinal malignancies, respiratory cancers, sarcomas, malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors, and others.

General oncology rationale

APE1/Ref-1 activation supports pro-tumorigenic processes that span the hallmarks of cancer, famously defined by Hanahan and Weinberg [86]. These include tumor-promoting features across early (e.g. genome instability/mutation, dysregulated cellular energetics, enabling replicative immortality), middle (e.g. tumor-promoting inflammation, sustained proliferative signaling), and late (e.g. dissemination into vasculature, invasion & metastasis, treatment resistance) stages of disease (Figure 2). Specifically, APE1/Ref-1-mediated reduction of cysteine residues on several cancer-related transcription factors, including p53, NF-κB, AP-1, HIF-1α, and STAT3, supports transcription of target genes, as this reduced state enhances their DNA-binding affinity [58]. Activation of these transcriptional programs promotes various pro-cancer cellular mechanisms, including increased growth factor signaling, activation of survival pathways, and inflammation. Although less studied in the context of cancer, the endonuclease function also supports tumor growth by reducing genotoxic stress, which is elevated in tumor cells, through base excision repair [87]. It follows that APE1/Ref-1 overexpression occurs in many types of cancer, including those of the pancreas, gastrointestinal tract, esophagus, and lung. This overexpression is linked to poor patient outcomes and resistance to anti-cancer treatments in clinical settings [88]. Interestingly, although APE1/Ref-1 is usually found in the nucleus, cytoplasmic expression is associated with poorer survival rates in several cancers, including hepatocellular carcinoma, lung, pancreatic, breast, thyroid, and ovarian cancers [36]. The abundance, dynamic nature, and multifunctional roles of APE1/Ref-1 in malignant progression make it a protein of significant interest in both preclinical and clinical cancer research. Clinical translation of APE1/Ref-1 inhibitors is more advanced in PDAC than in other clinical indications, with the previously described compound APX3330 having progressed through dose-escalation phase I trials (NCT03375086 [36]).

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC)

PDAC is one of the deadliest malignancies characterized by late detection, rapid progression and metastatic spread, and resistance to anti-cancer therapies [89]. Much of this aggression is primed by the unique desmoplastic microenvironment of PDAC, defined by a dense extracellular matrix that crushes vascular structures and produces a hypoxic, nutrient-starved environment that demands intense adaptability from tumor cells. This adaptation is supported by aberrant activation of NF-κB, HIF-1α, and STAT3 in tumor cells, which is promoted and sustained by the redox function of APE1/Ref-1 [90–92]. Notably, downregulation of STAT3 and NF-κB alleviates gemcitabine resistance in pancreatic tumor cells [93]. These unique disease characteristics indicate inhibition of APE1/Ref-1 as an effective therapeutic target for PDAC, and multiple compounds have shown promise in this malignancy. The parent compound APX3330 and its second-generation derivatives APX2009 and APX2014 suppress the survival and outgrowth of primary PDAC patient-derived tumor cells in vitro, in monolayer and 3D cultures, as well as in murine models in vivo [27,28,94,95]. These compounds primarily target the redox function of APE1/Ref-1, as evidenced by dose-dependent decreases in relevant transcription factor activity [28,95]. Importantly, the PDAC-promoting activity of APE1/Ref-1 is not limited to its redox function, as selectively ablating the endonuclease function of APE1/Ref-1 in pancreatic cancer cells reduces their ability to form primary tumors and metastasize to distant locations in a murine model of human pancreatic cancer [2]. APE1/Ref-1 has also been strongly implicated in tumor-promoting immunosuppression within the PDAC tumor microenvironment, particularly through NF-κB- and STAT3-mediated myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC) expansion and tumor-promoting macrophage polarization [96,97].

Cancers of the gastrointestinal tract

APE1/Ref-1 is basally expressed throughout the gastrointestinal tract under normal physiological conditions and becomes significantly upregulated in the event of inflammatory insult [96,98]. This response is necessary for maintaining cellular health in the highly oxidative, often hypoxic, inflammatory microenvironment by upregulating transcription factor activity and promoting base excision repair. This is critical for the maintenance of these tissues, as the highly vascular and dynamic functions of the gastrointestinal tract subject them to high levels of stress and a constantly changing environment. However, these cytoprotective processes facilitate malignant cell progression and tumor survival, especially in the context of chronic inflammation [88], and APE1/Ref-1 expression levels increase further upon malignant transformation and tumor establishment in the gastrointestinal tract [99]. Additionally, as tissues of the gastrointestinal tract are highly enriched with immune cells, these populations play a unique role in these diseases, with APE1/Ref-1 as a crucial mediator of many tumor-promoting characteristics. For example, APE1/Ref-1-mediated overexpression of PD-L1 through NF-κB activation [100] in gastric cancer is associated with tumor invasion, lymph node metastasis, and worse overall survival in patients [101]. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that APE1/Ref-1 knockdown blocks the metastatic potential of gastric cancers by decreasing MDSC infiltration and favorably modulating anti-tumor T cell ratios [102]. Another group has shown similar effects on T cells and MDSCs in colorectal carcinogenesis by inhibiting APE1/Ref-1 with the small molecule APX3330 [103], demonstrating the therapeutic potential of pharmacological APE1/Ref-1 inhibition in treating cancers of this anatomical compartment. In addition to tumor promotion through direct modulation of cell signaling mechanisms and modification of the immune landscape, APE1/Ref-1 is associated with drug resistance in gastrointestinal cancers, particularly to platinum-based and fluoropyrimidine chemotherapeutic drugs [104], as well as with disease recurrence after surgical resection [105]. As such, APE1/Ref-1 inhibitors have been proposed as combination treatments to enhance the anticancer effects of chemotherapy and immunotherapy, particularly in colorectal cancer [101,104,106]. Ongoing research into these precision medicine strategies continues, as these cancers remain highly prevalent in the population and notoriously difficult to manage clinically [89].

Respiratory cancers

Similar to the gastrointestinal tract, the respiratory tract is highly perfused and densely populated with immune cell populations, both resident and circulating. This, and the continuation with the external environment, potentiates the need for tissue homeostasis mechanisms such as APE1/Ref-1. And, like other cancers, APE1/Ref-1 dysregulation facilitates malignant progression in these tissues [107,108], and overexpression is associated with worse survival and poor clinical outcomes for patients with esophageal or lung cancers. The increased survivability of APE1/Ref-1-overexpressing tumor cells is compounded by APE1/Ref-1-dependent activation of additional cytoprotective mechanisms, such as NRF2, which detoxifies reactive oxygen species and promotes resistance to chemotherapy [109]. Veritably, a polymorphism variant of APE1/Ref-1e, which blunts transcription and reduces the binding affinity of the promoter region to target transcription factors, is associated with a significantly reduced risk of developing lung cancer [110]. The tumor-promoting effects of APE1/Ref-1 in respiratory cancers involve myriad signaling axes including metabolism, extracellular matrix remodeling, immunosuppression, and angiogenesis [111,112]. For example, one of the transcription factor targets of APE1/Ref-1, HIF-1α, has been directly linked to esophageal squamous cell carcinoma metastasis by targeting the transcriptional regulatory protein SP1 [113]. Additionally, the activation of Yes-associated protein 1 (YAP1), a transcriptional coactivator that drives tumor development by increasing cell proliferation, inhibiting apoptosis, and promoting stemness and metastatic spread, is dependent on the redox function of APE1/Ref-1 in esophageal adenocarcinoma [114]. APE1/Ref-1 also promotes resistance to anticancer therapies, including radiation and platinum-based chemotherapy, through mechanisms including mitophagy and immunomodulation, specifically by inhibiting the STING pathway and increasing infiltration of immunosuppressive regulatory T cells [115–117]. It follows that inhibition of APE1/Ref-1 reduces preclinical esophageal and lung tumor burden through induction of cell death, alleviation of chemoresistance, and immunomodulation [118–120]. This information, alongside the dynamic role of the immune system in respiratory cancers [121], provides strong rationale for the clinical implication of APE1/Ref-1 inhibition in combination with either chemotherapy or immunotherapy in these diseases.

Sarcomas (soft tissue and bone)

Functional studies in osteosarcoma suggest that APE1/Ref-1 contributes to maintenance of stress-tolerant tumor phenotypes through coordinated regulation of DNA damage responses, angiogenic signaling, and redox-dependent survival pathways [122,123]. In osteosarcoma models, APE1/Ref-1 upregulates fibroblast growth factor 2 and fibroblast growth factor receptor 3, and regulates angiogenesis through a TGF-β-dependent mechanism, indicating that APE1/Ref-1 contributes to vascular support and tumor adaptation [124,125]. Therapy resistance is further influenced by APE1/Ref-1 localization, as cytoplasmic APE1/Ref-1 has also been linked to cisplatin resistance in osteosarcoma patients [126]. Importantly, miR-765 enhances the anti-angiogenic effects of cisplatin by downregulating APE1/Ref-1, whereas pharmacologic redox inhibition with APX3330, combined with ATM inhibition, sensitizes osteosarcoma cells to ionizing radiation by inducing ferroptosis [123,127,128]. Together, these studies suggest that the most effective sarcoma strategy may not be broad APE1/Ref-1 inhibition, which risks toxicity given its essential role in repair, but rather context-specific targeting of its disease-driving functions. Redox-selective inhibitors, including newer APE1/Ref-1-targeted compounds with improved potency, may suppress pro-survival, angiogenic, and stress-adaptive signaling, while endonuclease-directed approaches may be best used transiently or through rational combination approaches with chemotherapy, radiation, or DNA damage response inhibitors [28,129] for osteosarcoma.

Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (MPNST)

There is limited information available regarding the direct role of APE1/Ref-1 in the progression of malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor (MPNST), in part due to the rarity of this cancer and the scarcity of well-annotated patient cohorts [130]. However, emerging evidence, including our own work, supports a role for APE1/Ref-1 in MPNST biology. In our published study using a cohort of patient samples, including relapsed cases, we demonstrated that APE1/Ref-1 expression is consistently elevated across MPNST disease states, with the highest expression observed in aggressive tumors, and that its expression correlates with disease progression. Functionally, we further showed that pharmacologic inhibition of APE1/Ref-1 redox activity with APX3330 suppresses STAT3 and HIF-1α signaling, identifying Ref-1 as a regulator of key survival pathways in MPNST [131,132]. This is particularly relevant given that STAT3 and HIF-1α have independently been shown to drive oncogenic phenotypes, angiogenesis, and poor prognosis in MPNST [133,134]. Additional studies have implicated STAT3 signaling in MPNST progression and survival, including associations with VEGF expression and clinical outcomes [135], as well as broader kinase-driven survival dependencies in NF1-associated MPNST [136]. In this context, APE1/Ref-1 functions as a redox regulator of transcription factor activity, placing it upstream of multiple signaling axes that are already established as critical drivers of MPNST biology. Early mechanistic and translational studies, including work using patient-derived xenolines, further demonstrated that targeting APE1/Ref-1 redox function disrupts survival signaling in MPNST models [132]. Ongoing work from our group using genotype-informed in vivo models continues to identify next-generation APE1/Ref-1 redox inhibitors capable of disrupting MPNST metabolism and stress-adaptive signaling that contribute to disease progression.

Other solid and liquid tumors

APE1/Ref-1 dysregulation goes beyond the tumor types mentioned earlier and continues to be linked to a wide range of solid and hematologic cancers, including hepatocellular, ovarian, breast, prostate, blood, and bladder cancers [137]. This aligns with its broader function as a stress-response regulator of redox-sensitive transcriptional programs that promote proliferation, survival, angiogenesis, and inflammatory signaling [84,137,138].

Evidence from hematologic malignancies further strengthens the case for APE1/Ref-1 as a clinically relevant target beyond traditional solid tumor settings. A recent study in Cell Stem Cell showed that inflammatory signaling in Tet2-mutant preleukemic hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells could be suppressed by APX3330, linking APE1/Ref-1-targeted therapy not only to overt leukemia but also to earlier clonal hematopoietic states that promote leukemogenesis under inflammatory stress [139]. In acute myeloid leukemia models, inhibiting APE1/Ref-1 redox function with APX3330 increased retinoic-acid-induced differentiation and apoptosis, supporting a role for APE1/Ref-1 in maintaining leukemic survival and preventing differentiation. In pediatric T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia, APE1/Ref-1 was identified as a potential therapeutic target, and redox inhibition decreased leukemia cell viability across multiple genotypes, including relapsed and therapy-resistant samples [140]. Overall, these findings support APE1/Ref-1 as a common vulnerability in both solid and liquid tumors, indicating that future development strategies may be guided as much by pathway dependence and inflammatory conditions as by tissue of origin [84,137,138].

APE1/Ref-1 in other diseases: clinical studies

Importantly, APE1/Ref-1 is implicated in many other nonmalignant chronic diseases. The common theme of APE1/Ref-1-mediated inflammation drives these disease states by initiating a positive feedback loop of elevated ROS and DNA damage, leading to increased APE1/Ref-1 expression, which, in turn, further drives aberrant inflammation. Notably, these functions are driven by the redox function of APE1/Ref-1, and therefore, the major focus of research into these disease states has been redox activity. Two main highlights that are becoming increasingly well understood in the context of APE1/Ref-1 redox function are ocular disease and gastrointestinal disease.

Ocular disease

The ocular field currently offers the most advanced disease-specific clinical evidence for targeting APE1/Ref-1. This is logically consistent because retinal vascular disease is driven by oxidative stress, hypoxia, inflammation, and VEGF-linked angiogenesis, all of which are influenced by transcription factors regulated by APE1/Ref-1 (Figure 3). Its upstream position makes APE1/Ref-1 appealing not only for suppressing neovascular signaling but also for broader modulation of stress-responsive pathways in retinal disease [19,141,142].

In the randomized Phase II ZETA-1 trial, APX3330 did not meet the primary endpoint of study-eye improvement in diabetic retinopathy, but it demonstrated a positive effect on disease progression: no APX3330-treated subjects experienced binocular three-step or greater worsening at 24 weeks, compared with 16% of placebo-treated subjects. Equally important, the study demonstrated a favorable safety and tolerability profile, which is crucial for a systemic therapy targeting earlier-stage disease. These results then supported FDA discussions regarding a worsening-based endpoint and a Special Protocol Assessment for Phase 3 development. This represents the clearest current clinical proof-of-concept for APE1/Ref-1 inhibition [84].

In wet age-related macular degeneration, the rationale for targeting APE1/Ref-1 remains primarily preclinical, yet remains compelling. APE1/Ref-1 contributes to angiogenic and inflammatory signaling pathways central to neovascular eye disease, and its expression is increased in human ocular neovascular tissues. Consistent with this biology, second-generation inhibitors such as APX2009 have shown activity in models of choroidal and subretinal neovascularization. Collectively, these findings support wet AMD as a credible future indication for APE1/Ref-1-targeted therapy, although its translational development remains less advanced than that of diabetic retinopathy [17,141].

Nonmalignant pathologies of the gastrointestinal tract

Inflammatory bowel disease is one of the clearest nononcology settings in which APE1/Ref-1 biology closely aligns with disease pathogenesis. The inflamed intestine is characterized by oxidative stress, epithelial barrier injury, hypoxia, and persistent immune activation, all of which increase reliance on redox-sensitive transcriptional programs such as NF-κB, AP-1, and HIF-1α. In this setting, APE1/Ref-1 functions as an upstream integrator rather than a singlepathway effector, making it an attractive target with the potential to modulate multiple disease-driving processes simultaneously [12,14,98,143]. Within this framework, Crohn’s disease is also a strong and biologically compelling indication, as its hallmark features of transmural inflammation, macrophage activation, and oxidative tissue injury are all consistent with APE1/Ref-1-dependent inflammatory signaling. In parallel, the current translational evidence base is better developed in chronic colitis and ulcerative-colitis-like settings, supporting ulcerative colitis as the most immediate path for clinical advancement. Thus, Crohn’s disease should be viewed not simply as a downstream extension, but as a closely aligned parallel opportunity, with ulcerative colitis presently serving as the most advanced entry point for development [14].

In the Winnie model of spontaneous chronic colitis, APX3330 improved disease severity [12–14,98], reduced immune cell infiltration, restored gastrointestinal function, and protected myenteric neurons, supporting the concept that inhibition of APE1/Ref-1 can suppress intestinal inflammation while simultaneously improving tissue function and neuroimmune injury [14]. More recent work has extended this biology beyond the gut itself and strengthened the case for a systemic hematopoietic role of APE1/Ref-1 in ulcerative colitis (UC). In a 2026 Communications Biology study, chronic UC drove APE1/Ref-1/HIF-1α/IL-1r1-dependent skewing of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells toward inflammatory myelopoiesis, while APX3330 suppressed HIF-1α, reduced pro-inflammatory Ly6Chi monocytes, and rescued abnormal hematopoietic dynamics under the UC inflammatory milieu [13]. In parallel, a 2026 Blood study linked IBD-associated inflammation to clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) and reported that APE1/Ref-1 inhibition reduced IBD severity in DNMT3A-mutant CHIP, raising the possibility that hematopoietic abnormalities such as CHIP status or related inflammatory myeloid signatures could serve as blood-based markers to identify biologically enriched patients for future APE1/Ref-1-directed trials [144]. Together, these findings position IBD and UC as compelling next translational opportunities, as APX3330 appears capable of interrupting both local intestinal inflammation and a broader systemic feed-forward inflammatory program [12–14,98,143,144].

Emerging indications for future clinical translation

Among additional indications, necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) is particularly noteworthy for its overlap with gastrointestinal inflammatory injury, hypoxia, epithelial damage, and oxidative stress. Early data show that APE1/Ref-1 expression is elevated in neonatal NEC tissue and that APX3330 improves sickness scores and reduces histologic intestinal injury in experimental NEC, supporting NEC as a particularly relevant GI-related future indication for APE1/Ref-1-directed therapy [145]. Other areas that may warrant future clinical investigation include chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy, for which APX3330-related APE1/Ref-1-targeted compounds have been developed to reduce platinum-associated sensory neurotoxicity in cases lacking approved preventive therapies, as well as neurovascular injury, for which APX3330 has shown neurorestorative effects following experimental stroke in diabetic rats [138,146]. Additional retinal vascular diseases, including retinopathy of prematurity and retinal vein occlusion, may also be amenable to APE1/Ref-1 inhibition, as it suppresses hypoxia-driven retinal endothelial signaling and pathological neovascularization in oxygen-induced retinopathy models [17]. Second-generation analogs with increased potency and barrier permeability will likely drive efforts to characterize small-molecule-mediated APE1/Ref-1 inhibition in these contexts (Table 1).

Conclusion

The importance of APE1/Ref-1 in cellular biology, as evidenced by its evolutionary conservation across species, is reflected in the extensive research aimed at understanding its role in both normal physiology and disease (Figure 4). These efforts have led to substantial programs focused on the therapeutic exploitation of this multifunctional protein. The preclinical and clinical status of the six most well-characterized small-molecule inhibitors of APE1/Ref-1 are summarized in Table 1. Most notable is the first-generation compound APX3330, which has demonstrated favorable safety profiles in humans and has advanced through stage II clinical trials, with stage III trials in active planning. Limitations in potency in some clinical indications prompted the design and synthesis of next-generation derivatives, including APX2009 and APX2014, which are currently being evaluated at the preclinical stage for several indications. Subsequent structure-activity relationship studies continue to yield novel compounds with increasing potency and selectivity (Table 1). As the inflammation-modulatory effects of APE1/Ref-1 are increasingly recognized and better characterized, therapeutic targeting of its activity for the treatment of inflammatory diseases is being clinically pursued. The next decade will bring continued expansion of next-generation analogs, as well as the advancement of lead compounds through the stages of clinical trials, with FDA approval visible on the horizon.

Expert opinion

APE1/Ref-1 represents a compelling therapeutic target whose multifunctional biology, including redox signaling, DNA repair, RNA metabolism, G-quadruplex regulation, and mitochondrial function, has posed both challenges and significant opportunities for its therapeutic development. The key finding driving current progress is that disease states involve context-specific rewiring of APE1/Ref-1-dependent signaling, creating a manageable therapeutic window absent in healthy tissues. However, a critical remaining challenge is the limited mechanistic understanding of how its non-canonical functions, especially in RNA biology and protein homeostasis, contribute to disease in ways that could be therapeutically targeted. The ultimate goal of the field is to achieve selective, indication-specific modulation of APE1/Ref-1 activity, and proof of concept has been demonstrated by APX3330, which showed favorable safety and disease stabilization in Phase I solid tumor trials. In the Phase II ZETA-1 diabetic retinopathy trial, it prevented binocular worsening in all treated subjects, compared with 16% in placebo-treated subjects, supporting a Special Protocol Assessment for Phase III development. The therapeutic landscape is expanding into inflammatory bowel disease, where recent translational studies indicate APE1/Ref-1 inhibition interrupts both local intestinal inflammation and a broader systemic feed-forward inflammatory response driven by hematopoietic reprogramming, making IBD one of the most exciting current research areas and blood-based hematopoietic signatures among the most promising emerging biomarkers for patient selection. The biggest challenge ahead in the cancer arena is achieving adequate tumor concentrations while maintaining homeostatic APE1/Ref-1 function in normal tissues, a problem being addressed by next-generation analogs, such as those with improved potency. This is complemented by rationally designed combinations with chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and DNA damage response inhibitors. APE1/Ref-1’s position as an upstream regulatory hub controlling multiple disease-driving transcription factors offers a strategic advantage in drug design, enabling the potential to disrupt entire pathological signaling networks rather than individual downstream targets. Looking forward, the immediate future is expected to yield the first Phase III trial results, followed by the registration of a Ref-1 targeting redox inhibitor compound, the expansion of redox-selective strategies into inflammatory and other indications, and the advancement of biomarker-guided trial designs, with regulatory approval of the first APE1/Ref-1-directed therapy becoming a realistic short-term goal.
