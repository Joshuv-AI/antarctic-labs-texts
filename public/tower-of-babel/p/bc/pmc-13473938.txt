The utility of machine learning for predicting donor non-use in lung transplantation
Authors: Tahir Hafeez Malik, Abiha Abdullah, Irene Tsai, Jose A Plascencia Jiménez, Etornam Amesimeku, Stephanie H Chang, Luis F Angel, Darren Stewart, Harish Seethamraju, Puneet Garcha, Abbas Rana, Jake G Natalini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEfforts to reduce waitlist mortality in lung transplantation have been hindered by a high donor lung non-use rate, with approximately one-fifth of deceased organ donors ultimately providing lungs for transplantation. Rationale for organ decline varies across individual providers and transplant centers. Greater donor lung utilization could significantly reduce waitlist mortality and improve clinical outcomes for adult lung transplant candidates.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate whether machine learning models can successfully predict donor lung non-use and characterize donor factors associated with current utilization patterns, with the long-term goal of informing more consistent and data-driven donor lung evaluation.MethodsUsing U.S. national registry data collected between 2012 and 2022, we generated five machine learning models – logistic regression, decision tree, random forest, XGBoost, and Naive Bayes – all of which were trained with 19 donor variables to predict donor lung non-use. Models were tested with both full donor feature sets and restricted donor feature sets comprised of the top 15, 10, and 5 predictors. Performance metrics, including area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score, were assessed using cross-validation.ResultsThe random forest model incorporating the full feature set achieved the highest performance with an AUROC of 0.960 (95% CI 0.958–0.961) and an F1 score of 0.9489. However, the logistic regression (AUROC 0.886, 95% CI 0.883–0.888) and XGBoost (AUROC 0.906, 95% CI 0.902–0.908) models also demonstrated robust performance. A restricted random forest model that included only the top 15 predictors demonstrated similar efficacy (AUROC 0.956, 95% CI 0.954–0.958). Key predictors included PaO2:FiO2 ratio, donor age, body mass index, and serum aspartate aminotransferase levels. More restricted random forest models (i.e., those that only included 5 or 10 predictors) and any of the restricted logistic regression and XGBoost models had inferior but still acceptable performance (AUROC 0.867–0.958).ConclusionMachine learning models, particularly random forest, XGBoost, and logistic regression, accurately predicted donor lung non-use. Restricted models using fewer predictors also maintained strong performance, demonstrating technical feasibility for future evaluation in time-sensitive workflows. Because the models were trained on historical utilization decisions rather than recipient outcomes or independent measures of donor suitability, prospective validation is needed to determine whether they can improve appropriate donor lung utilization or support acceptance decisions without reinforcing practice-pattern bias.Keywords: Donor lung non-use, Machine learning, Lung transplantation, Organ utilization, Extreme gradient boosting, Donor evaluation, Organ procurement and transplantation network

Introduction

Lung transplantation is a life-saving procedure for patients with end-stage pulmonary disease. Although the number of lung transplants performed in the United States has increased over the past decade, persistent scarcity of donor lungs continues to constrain access to transplantation.1 In 2024, 4775 patients were on the lung transplant waiting list, with 3822 new candidates added during the year, while 3404 lung transplants were performed.1, 2 Of 16,989 deceased donors nationally, 3697 (21.8%) had at least one lung recovered for transplantation.1, 2 These data underscore a persistent mismatch between the need for transplantation and donor-lung availability.

Appropriate non-use of damaged or medically unsuitable organs is necessary to avoid poor post-transplant outcomes. However, evidence from kidney transplantation suggests that donor characteristics such as diabetes and hypertension are associated with organ discard, despite substantial overlap in measured donor quality between discarded and transplanted kidneys.3 Risk and loss aversion may also influence acceptance decisions, potentially contributing to repeated declines of organs that might otherwise warrant careful review.4 Following implementation of continuous distribution, overall lung utilization remained low, increasing only from 17.0% in the pre-policy era to 17.5% in the first post-policy year.5 In addition, among United States single-lung transplant recipients, the second lung from the same donor was used in fewer than half of cases, although registry data cannot determine how often the unused lung was medically unsuitable for transplantation.6 Clinical and system-level factors that vary across transplant programs further contribute to heterogeneity in donor lung utilization decisions.7 Together, these challenges highlight the need for more transparent and standardized approaches to donor lung evaluation. Predictive approaches, including donor-risk indices, regression models, and machine-learning models, have been developed to estimate allograft discard or utilization in abdominal transplantation.8, 9, 10, 11 In lung transplantation, the Allograft Discard Risk Index and LUNDON score were developed to estimate observed donor-lung discard and acceptance, respectively.12, 13 To bridge this gap, our study aimed to evaluate the ability of several machine learning algorithms to predict donor lung non-use and to identify which donor characteristics most strongly contribute to current utilization patterns. We hypothesized that machine learning models would outperform traditional statistical methods in predicting donor lung non-use and could provide a foundation for future tools designed to support more standardized and data-informed donor lung evaluation.

Methods

Study design, data source, and population

We performed a retrospective study of potential lung donors between 2012 and 2022 using data from the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network (OPTN) United States transplant registry. Donors were included if they were ≥18 years old at the time of potential donation and had one or both lungs evaluated for transplantation. For this study, lung “non-use” was defined as donor lungs that were evaluated but ultimately not transplanted. Donors with missing data for any predictor variables of interest were excluded from the analysis, and analyses were conducted using complete cases. This approach avoided the additional assumptions required for imputation. Due to the deidentified nature of the database, this study was exempt from Institutional Review Board approval.

Predictor variables

The analysis focused on the following 19 donor predictor variables, all selected a priori based on established clinical relevance in donor lung evaluation and prior literature on lung allograft utilization.12, 13, 14 These variables included age, body mass index (BMI), serum creatinine, serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST), serum sodium, serum total bilirubin, the ratio of partial pressure of oxygen in arterial blood (PaO₂) to the fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO₂) (hereafter, referred to as the P:F ratio), height, cause of death, sex, seropositive hepatitis B virus (HBV) core antibody status, seropositive hepatitis C virus (HCV) antibody status, history of diabetes, history of hypertension, history of cigarette use, abnormal chest radiograph findings, Public Health Service (PHS) increased-risk donor designation (i.e., meeting established risk-based criteria for transmission of blood-borne pathogens, including HIV, HBV, and HCV), and ethnicity.

Data preparation, model evaluation metrics, and statistical analysis

Because the unit of analysis was the individual lung, donor characteristics were compared between transplanted and non-used lungs using an independent-samples t-test or χ2 test, as appropriate.

To predict donor lung use, five machine learning techniques were employed: logistic regression, decision tree, random forest, XGBoost, and Naive Bayes. Logistic regression was chosen for its effectiveness and interpretability in binary classification problems and was fit using a binomial family. Decision trees, selected for their ability to handle both numerical and categorical data, were optimized by adjusting complexity parameters to prevent overfitting. To enhance prediction accuracy and reduce variance, the random forest model was implemented by averaging several decision trees. Specifically, it utilized 100 trees and avoided splitting nodes into groups smaller than five.15, 16 Naive Bayes, known for its simplicity and effectiveness in high-dimensional data, was included for comparison. Finally, XGBoost was configured with 100 trees, a learning rate of 0.3, a lambda regularization term of 1, a maximum tree depth of six, and a feature fraction of 0.6, reflecting its strong performance and reliability with structured data.17, 18

To evaluate the performance and generalizability of the machine learning models, the dataset was split into 70% for training and 30% for testing on unseen data. Initially, five-fold cross-validation was conducted to assess model performance and guide initial hyperparameter selection. Subsequently, the models underwent refinement through ten-fold cross-validation combined with Bayesian optimization for hyperparameter tuning, ensuring robust model performance and preventing overfitting. During each cross-validation iteration, the training data was divided into equal-sized folds, with models trained on subsets and validated on the remaining data to optimize performance. This two-step approach ensured that every data point contributed to both training and validation, providing reliable estimates of model performance during tuning.

After completing cross-validation, the optimized models were tested on the 30% hold-out test set to evaluate their performance on unseen data. Model performance was assessed using five metrics: (1) area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), which measures the probability that the model assigns a higher predicted probability to a randomly selected positive instance than to a randomly selected negative instance; (2) accuracy, the proportion of correctly classified outcomes among all observations; (3) precision, the proportion of correctly predicted positive cases among all predicted positive cases; (4) recall, the proportion of true positive cases identified among all actual positive cases; and (5) F1 score, the harmonic mean of precision and recall, which balances the trade-off between these metrics.19, 20 Receiver operating characteristic curves were generated to illustrate model discrimination across classification thresholds.

AUROC was prioritized as the primary evaluation metric due to its robustness in assessing overall performance across varying classification thresholds. Unlike accuracy, AUROC measures the model's ability to distinguish between classes independently of threshold selection, making it particularly valuable for imbalanced datasets. Precision, recall, and F1 score complemented the evaluation by examining trade-offs between false positives and false negatives, providing additional insight into the models’ predictive reliability.

To further assess model interpretability and determine whether simpler models could achieve comparable performance to their full-feature counterparts, restricted models were developed using the top 15, 10, and 5 most important predictors. Feature importance scores were derived from the XGBoost, random forest, and logistic regression models. Following validation of the full models, which utilized all 19 predictor variables, the number of features was systematically reduced based on these importance scores. Each restricted model underwent the same 10-fold cross-validation process as the full models to ensure consistency in performance comparison.

Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using R statistical software (version 4.4.2).

Results

Dataset

The unit of analysis was the individual lung (one observation per lung), such that donors who contributed two lungs were counted twice. The original cohort included 116,681 donors and was reduced to 107,576 donors (214,719 lungs: 45,334 allocated and 169,385 non-used lungs) after excluding cases with missing values in one or more predictor variables. Variable-specific missingness rates were as follows: age, 3%; height, 8%; BMI, 4%; ethnicity, 0.1%; history of hypertension, 13%; serum creatinine, 13%; and history of cigarette use, 12%.

Donor characteristics

Key donor characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Allocated donors were significantly younger than non-use donors (36.9±13.1 vs. 45.4±14.5 years, p<0.001). In addition, mean BMI was significantly lower in allocated donors compared to non-use donors (26.8±5.7 vs. 29.4±7.4 kg/m², p<0.001). Donor causes of death also differed, with allocated donors more frequently experiencing head trauma (37.3% vs. 23.2%), whereas non-use donors were more likely to have died from anoxia (43.5% vs. 30.2%).

Biochemical data showed that transplanted donors had lower mean creatinine levels compared to non-use donors (1.5±1.6 vs. 1.7±1.8 mg/dL, p<0.001) and better oxygenation as indicated by a higher mean P:F ratio (390.9±138.3 vs. 183.4±124.8, p<0.001). Full model performance

Five machine learning models – logistic regression, decision tree, random forest, XGBoost, and Naive Bayes – were evaluated to predict lung allograft non-use, as shown in Table 2. Random forest demonstrated the highest performance across most metrics, achieving an AUROC of 0.960, an F1 score of 0.949, an accuracy of 91.79%, a precision of 93.29%, and a recall of 96.54%. XGBoost closely followed with an AUROC of 0.906, an F1 score of 0.914, an accuracy of 86.31%, a precision of 90.46%, and a recall of 92.39%. Logistic regression performed comparably, with an AUROC of 0.886, an F1 score of 0.905, an accuracy of 84.56%, a precision of 88.17%, and a recall of 92.90%.

Restricted model performance

To evaluate the impact of feature reduction, restricted models were developed using the top 15, 10, and 5 predictors, as determined by variable importance scores from random forest, XGBoost, and logistic regression (Figure 2B, C, and D, respectively). A summary of restricted model performance is shown in Table 3. Random forest maintained robust performance with reduced features; with the top 15 predictors, it achieved an AUROC of 0.956 and an F1 score of 0.945, while the top 5 predictors yielded an AUROC of 0.951 and an F1 score of 0.944. XGBoost also retained strong performance, with the top 15 predictors resulting in an AUROC of 0.882 and the highest precision of 0.890 in the 5-predictor model. Logistic regression exhibited consistent performance across all feature sets, achieving an AUROC of 0.869 with 15 predictors and 0.867 with 5 predictors, highlighting its robustness and adaptability.

Relative variable importance, confusion matrices and classification accuracy

We further analyzed relative variable importance across all models to identify the key predictors contributing to lung allograft non-use, as shown in Figure 2. Confusion matrices provided additional insights into the classification accuracy of the full models (Figure 1B). Logistic regression correctly identified 72.8% of non-use lungs but exhibited the highest misclassification rate for transplanted lungs (5.6%). Random forest demonstrated the lowest misclassification rate for non-use lungs (5.5%) but misclassified 2.7% of transplanted lungs. The decision tree model showed moderate performance, with misclassification rates of 7.7% for non-use lungs and 13.4% for transplanted lungs. These findings were derived from the independent 30% hold-out test set, underscoring the generalizability of the models. While logistic regression excelled in identifying non-use lungs, random forest offered a more balanced classification performance across categories.

Discussion

In this national OPTN registry analysis, we applied multiple machine learning models to predict donor lung non-use. Random forest emerged as the best-performing model overall, consistently demonstrating the highest AUROC and F1 score across full and restricted feature sets. XGBoost and logistic regression also demonstrated robust performance. To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate that several machine learning algorithms can accurately predict donor lung non-use using routinely collected donor characteristics. These findings suggest that donor lung utilization decisions are highly patterned and that machine learning may provide a useful foundation for future data-driven tools to support donor evaluation. Importantly, because the outcome reflects historical use and non-use decisions, these models should be viewed as predictors of utilization patterns rather than independent measures of donor lung suitability.

Currently, there are no universally accepted standards for determining donor lung quality, as shown in a recent qualitative study by Koons et al., in which transplant pulmonologists and surgeons described multiple clinical and system-level factors influencing lung offer decision-making7. In this context, the ability to accurately predict donor lung non-use using machine learning could eventually enhance donor evaluation by providing a more standardized, quantitative estimate of how donor characteristics relate to utilization decisions. Such a tool should not function as an automatic acceptance or decline mechanism. Rather, after prospective validation, it could serve as an adjunct to clinical judgment by improving transparency, identifying offers that warrant closer review, and supporting more consistent evaluation across programs and organ procurement organizations.

In prior literature, the LUNDON score has been used to evaluate donor lung acceptability based on criteria such as age, smoking history, chest radiograph findings, and the P:F ratio.13 However, it relies on predefined thresholds and rule-based logic. In contrast, our machine learning models capture complex, non-linear interactions among multiple donor characteristics and provide predictions of historical donor lung utilization patterns. While the LUNDON score is directed towards acceptability assessment, our study addresses non-use prediction, a decision outcome that may be influenced by variability in practice. These approaches should therefore be considered complementary rather than interchangeable. Whether a non-use prediction model can identify lungs that are being declined inappropriately or improve appropriate donor lung utilization will require prospective validation incorporating recipient outcomes.

The most important predictors in our models, including P:F ratio, donor age, BMI, smoking history, and chest radiograph findings, are consistent with established donor evaluation practices and prior literature.12, 14 Therefore, the value of this study is not that it identifies entirely novel clinical risk factors. Rather, the novelty lies in demonstrating that national donor lung non-use can be predicted with high discrimination using several machine learning approaches, including restricted models with relatively few routinely available variables. This finding provides quantitative evidence that current utilization decisions are strongly patterned and supports further development of machine learning models as potential adjuncts to donor evaluation.

Our results also align with previous studies that have shown the utility of machine learning in predicting allograft outcomes in other transplant settings. Bishara et al. developed a machine learning model to predict liver allograft utilization, achieving an AUROC of 0.84.10 Meanwhile, Rana et al. developed the Discard Risk Index, which predicted liver allograft discard with a C-statistic of 0.80.11 Similarly, Pettit et al. employed machine learning techniques to predict liver and kidney utilization, with their models achieving an AUROC of 0.925 for liver organ use and 0.952 for kidney use.8 Our study extends this approach to lung transplantation, where machine learning has been less frequently applied despite the critical need to reduce non-use rates. The higher AUROC values observed in our study for the random forest and XGBoost models reflect the complexity of lung transplant data and the ability of these algorithms to capture non-linear interactions between multiple donor characteristics, such as P:F ratios, BMI, and chest radiograph findings.

Donor-risk and discard-prediction indices have been developed across multiple areas of transplantation. The Allograft Discard Risk Index and Pediatric Discard Risk Index were developed to predict donor lung discard and pediatric liver allograft discard, respectively.9, 12 In the lung-transplant validation cohort, the Allograft Discard Risk Index achieved a C-statistic of 0.871 for donor lung discard but did not reliably predict post-transplant survival.12 More broadly, the Kidney Donor Risk Index and Liver Donor Risk Index provide donor-risk frameworks designed to estimate kidney and liver graft-failure risk, respectively.21, 22 These indices and the present models address related but distinct questions. Direct comparisons of predictive performance or clinical utility would require evaluation in a common cohort using prespecified endpoints, including recipient outcomes. Accordingly, our models should be viewed as complementary to existing donor-assessment frameworks, with prospective study needed to define their clinical role. In this study, model performance remained strong after restricting the number of predictors. This finding has practical relevance for future tool development because real-time donor evaluation requires approaches that are accurate, interpretable, and feasible to apply in time-sensitive workflows. Restricted models using as few as five variables continued to perform well, suggesting that a limited set of routinely available donor characteristics captures much of the signal associated with non-use. Although this does not establish clinical utility, it supports the feasibility of developing streamlined models for prospective evaluation as adjuncts that could highlight donor offers warranting closer, more standardized review.

Despite its relevance, our study has several limitations. While we tested multiple machine learning models, the analysis was not exhaustive. We included logistic regression and decision tree models for their interpretability but did not explore more complex architectures, such as elastic net regression or advanced deep learning methods. Additionally, the study did not manually engineer features, which could have enhanced predictive performance. The retrospective nature of the data may have introduced inherent limitations, as historical registry data may not fully capture center-specific practices, logistical factors, recipient considerations, or changes in clinical practice over time. Because the outcome was donor lung non-use, the models may also reflect existing practice patterns and unmeasured sources of variability in acceptance decisions. Cross-validation and independent hold-out testing helped reduce overfitting and evaluate model performance, but they cannot determine whether predicted non-use reflects appropriate donor selection. The study was also limited to deceased donor data and lacked a suitable external validation dataset. Finally, recipient outcomes were not assessed, and prospective validation will be necessary before these models can be used to support clinical decision-making.

Despite these limitations, this study demonstrates that donor lung non-use can be accurately predicted using routinely available donor characteristics. These models may provide a foundation for future efforts to characterize and standardize donor evaluation, but they should not be interpreted as independent measures of donor suitability or used to direct acceptance decisions in their current form. Future studies should incorporate external validation, recipient and center-level variables, reasons for decline, and post-transplant outcomes among recipients of lungs predicted to be at high risk for non-use. Such studies are needed to determine whether machine learning can identify potentially underutilized lungs and improve appropriate donor lung utilization without reinforcing existing practice-pattern bias.

Conclusion

Machine learning algorithms, particularly random forest and XGBoost, accurately predicted donor lung non-use in a national OPTN registry cohort. Restricted models maintained strong performance with fewer routinely available predictors, supporting feasibility for prospective evaluation in time-sensitive workflows. These findings characterize current utilization patterns rather than donor lung suitability. Prospective studies incorporating recipient outcomes and assessment of practice-pattern bias are needed to determine whether these models can support donor evaluation or improve appropriate donor lung utilization.
