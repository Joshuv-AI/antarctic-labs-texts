Comparative Analysis of Aromatic Sulfonated Polyamide and Perfluorinated Sulfonic Acid Ionomers: Implications for Polymer Electrolyte Fuel Cell Performance
Authors: Xiao Gao, Buyi Bian, Noboru Ohta, Yu Chen, Wenbin Yi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ionomers critically impact catalyst layer performance in polymer electrolyte fuel cells (PEFCs). While sulfonated aromatic polyether–ketone (SPEEK) and Perfluorinated sulfonic acid-Nafion ionomers are widely used, their structure–property relationships under operating conditions remain insufficiently understood. In this study, we systematically analyzed Pt-supported SPEEK and Nafion films (13 nm) using in situ ellipsometry, grazing incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS), and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) to investigate their morphology, water uptake, and proton transport properties. Compared to Nafion, the ultrathin SPEEK films exhibit dense, well-defined crystalline domains at the buried interface, attributed to a relatively stronger affinity for the Pt substrate. Analysis revealed that at 90% RH, the buried interface layer of SPEEK maintained a stable thickness of ∼13.8 Å, while Nafion exhibited a more pronounced swelling, increasing to ∼18.7 Å. Consequently, SPEEK thin films show reduced water uptake rates and conductivity, particularly under dry conditions. The proton conductivity measurements further highlighted a significant gap between in-plane and out-of-plane directions for SPEEK, with out-of-plane conductivity being 1–2 orders of magnitude lower, whereas Nafion displayed a smaller anisotropy. We propose that the intramolecular forces between ether groups, along with the interactions between Pt and oxygen atoms in the repeating units of SPEEK, contribute to the differences observed in the buried interface structure. This study enhances our understanding of ionomer-containing films in relation to molecular structure, confinement effects, and substrate interactions, thereby facilitating further fundamental investigations of the catalyst-ionomer interface.

Introduction

PEFCs offer many advantages in electronics and mobile transport. , Membrane Electrodes Assemblies (MEAs) are essentially composed of a membrane, a catalytic layer and a gas diffusion layer. Nanoscale ionomer thin films coat the electron-conducting surface of the catalysts, forming a solid–liquid–gas three-phase interface involving catalyst, electrolyte, and reactive gas. At this interface, O2 molecules diffuse through the membrane from the CL holes after traveling a long path, while protons (H+) transfer along the membrane, and electrons first pass through the carbon carriers and then the Pt particles. These three components arrive simultaneously at the interface to facilitate the completion of the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR). , Thus, the ionomer film controls both local ion conduction and reactant transport through the film and to/from the electrochemical interface.

SPEEK, a typical sulfonated aromatic polyether–ketone, is synthesized via the chemical modification of PEEK to incorporate sulfonic acid groups. It demonstrates excellent thermal stability (T g approximately 206 °C) and compressive strength, making it a promising alternative material for various polymer electrolytes and a potential replacement for the widely used Nafion in PEFCs. − Several nonfluorinated polymeric materials have been explored as alternatives to Nafion, due to their cost-effectiveness and straightforward synthesis processes. − The two materials demonstrate substantial differences in their chemical structure and performance characteristics, leading to notable variations in mass transport, swelling behavior, and the morphology of hydrophilic and hydrophobic domains. Kreuer modeled the proton conduction mechanisms of Nafion and SPEEK, discovering that the transport properties and swelling behaviors of Nafion and various sulfonated SPEEK can be attributed to clear differences in microstructures and the pK a values of the acidic functional groups. He points out that the less pronounced hydrophobic/hydrophilic separation in SPEEK compared to Nafion results in narrower, less connected hydrophilic channels and larger separations between the less acidic sulfonic acid functional groups. Wu investigated the differences in the percolation threshold for proton conduction and the excess mixing volume for water adsorption between SPEEK and Nafion, and found that SPEEK exhibits spherical hydrophilic domains, whereas Nafion features rod-like or plate-like hydrophilic domains The reduced conductivity and related microstructural characteristics still necessitate further exploration in terms of thin films, taking into account substrate interactions. It is essential to compare the structures of both materials in thin films and leverage SPEEK’s thermal stability advantage to provide new insights for the design of catalyst layers.

Reducing polymer film thickness to the molecular scale intensifies local surface and interface effects, which manifest as structural nonuniformity, unique stability characteristics, and dynamics that differ from those observed in bulk materials. − To date, several studies have focused on PFSA thin films supported by Pt or other substrates, with thicknesses ranging from 4 to 200 nm, in order to replicate the interfacial structure of catalyst layers under near-real practical conditions. − The primary challenge in characterizing thin films lies in their nonhomogeneous structure, as revealed by neutron/X-ray reflectivity. , This morphology is influenced by the interplay between confinement and interfacial interaction with the substrate. Our previous studies have investigated the heterogeneous crystallization structure of aromatic ionomer thin films. Building upon these findings, we further examine whether PFSA thin films similarly exhibit heterogeneous crystallization and how such structural characteristics may influence mass transport properties. As far as we know, comparative studies of PFSA ionomers have examined the effects of side-chain variations and equivalent weights, aromatic ionomers featuring fundamentally different main-chain architectures have received far less attention, especially in the context of ultrathin film.

Despite its potential advantages, comparative studies on the aromatic ionomer sulfonated SPEEKparticularly regarding its fundamental confinement effects and substrate interactionsremain limited. SPEEK exhibits relatively high thermal stability and a distinct main-chain structure compared to Nafion, suggesting promising opportunities for further research. Specifically, investigations into how differences in interfacial interactions and internal structure influence phase separation and mass transport behavior are warranted. Therefore, our investigation primarily aims to elucidate the underlying physicochemical properties of SPEEK.

In this study, we investigated typical 13 nm Pt-supported ultrathin films of SPEEK and Nafion, which mimick the ionomer–electrode interface in PEFCs. We systematically examined the differences in thermal transitions, morphology, water uptake, and proton transport behavior between the two materials. The effects of confinement and substrate interactions were found to significantly influence their morphological characteristics, resulting in notable variations in proton conductivity and water uptake behavior, particularly in light of their heterogeneous structure. We provided an in-depth analysis of these differences, focusing on aspects such as phase separation, confinement effects, and, crucially, the characteristics of the buried interface. These findings underscore the necessity of optimizing the design of ionomer materials, emphasizing that a deeper understanding of the interplay between molecular structure, confinement effects, and substrate interactions can contribute to enhanced efficiency in fuel cell applications.

Experiment

Preparation of SPEEK Dispersion and Thin Films

SPEEK was prepared by a sulfonation reaction of polyether ether ketone (PEEK, VICTREX450P) with sulfuric acid (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., AR, 98.0 wt %) at 45 °C for 6 h. 1H NMR (AVANCE III 500 MHz) showed a degree of sulfonation of 65.0 ± 2.2% (Figure S1). The polymer strips were washed with deionized water until the pH of the water was neutral. The neutral SPEEK polymers were dried in an oven at 60 °C. 13 nm films were prepared on a Cr, Pt-sputtered Si substrate. The thickness of SPEEK films on Pt substrates was measured using ellipsometry (EP3SW, Accurion, Germany) at a fixed angle of incidence (45°) and wavelength (633 nm). The thickness results are shown in Figure S2.

Preparation of Nafion Dispersion and Thin Films

A Nafion dispersion solution was prepared by diluting a 5 wt % Nafion stock dispersion (Shanghai Diba Biotechnology Co. with an equivalent weight (EW) of 1100) using 99.5% 1,1,1,3,3,3-hexafluoro-2-propanol (Bidepharm, 98%). Nafion thin films of various thicknesses were subsequently fabricated via the spin-casting technique, depositing the films onto Pt substrates by the same method as we reported previously. ,

Proton Conductivity Measurement

To measure the conductivity along the in-plane direction against the Pt substrate, the SPEEK thin films were prepared using the self-assembly method on the interdigitated array electrodes. For out-of-plane measurement, a conventional sandwich setup with a digital pressure indicator was designed as we previously reported. , Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was used to determine the proton conductivity of membranes. The relative humidity for the proton conductivity measurements was changed from 20 to 90% RH at 25 °C. The measurements were performed at 25 °C with 20–90% relative humidity (RH) by a four-point probe along in-plane and out-of-plane direction, similar to our previous work. Alternative amplitude potential and frequency range was set as 100 mV and 0.01 Hz–7 MHz, –0.01 Hz, respectively (SP300, Biologic). The analysis software EC-lab was used to construct an equivalent circuit for extracting film resistance.

Water Uptake by In Situ Ellipsometry Measurements

The swelling behavior of the ionomer films was assessed by monitoring the thickness variation at different relative humidities (RH) using a self-built environmental chamber via autonulling an ellipsometer (EP3SW, Accurion Co., Germany). The incident angle was set at 60° with a wavelength of 550 nm, equipped with an INSTEC heating stage to ensure sample temperature at 25 °C. Prior to humidification, films underwent a pretreatment step at 80 °C for 1 h under high vacuum (<10–6 mbar) as we previous reported. Water uptake in thin films was quantified by estimating the water content, λ, which represents the number of water molecules per acid group in the ionomer. The water content was calculated using the following equation: 1 λ=mol(H2O)mol(SO3)=hRH−h0h0{EWMH2O}{ρH2Oρionomer}

EW refers to the equivalent weight of the ionomer (Nafion: 1100 g/mol, SPEEK: 417.39 g/mol), MH2O denotes the molecular weight of water, which is 18.01 g/mol. ρionomer refers to the density of the dry ionomer (Nafion: 2.0 g/cm3, SPEEK: 1.53 g/cm3). The variable h 0 corresponds to the thickness of the ionomer thin film under dry conditions, and h RH indicates the thickness of the film equilibrated at a specific relative humidity (RH). These parameters are essential for calculating the swelling and water uptake behavior of the ionomer films under varying humidity conditions.

The experimental procedure involved initially conditioning the thin films in a dry environment (0% RH) for over 1–2 h until thickness remained almost unchanged, allowing the determination of the dry film thickness (h 0). Subsequently, the humidity was gradually increased in steps, starting from 20% up to 90% RH. At each RH level, the film was allowed to equilibrate for at least 30 min, ensuring the thickness change rate was less than 0.05%/min.

In Situ Characterization of Thermal Properties

The nonisothermal behavior of as-prepared SPEEK and Nafion thin films was characterized using variable-temperature in situ spectroscopic ellipsometry (EP3SW, Accurion Co., Germany) equipped with an INSTEC heating stage. A fixed incidence angle of 60° and a wavelength of 550 nm were employed. Prior to thermal analysis, trapped water removal pretreatment at 80 °C were conducted under identical conditions to those described in our published protocol, ensuring reproducibility. Following pretreatment, the films were heated to 160 °C (Nafion), 350 °C (SPEEK) at a constant rate of 2 °C min– 1 while the ellipsometric angles (Ψ and Δ) were continuously recorded.

GIWAXS Measurements

The in situ GIWAXS experiments were performed under at SPring-8, BL40B2, Japan. The detector used was a Pilatus 2D plate with a pixel size of 0.172 mm. The wavelength of the X-ray and the sample-to-detector distance were set to 0.1 nm and 548 mm, respectively. During GIWAXS experiments, the humidity level was precisely controlled by a digital humidity control system (JY-WWCS, WUWANG Co., Ltd.) with precision of ± 2% RH. CeO2 was used as the reference. The humidity level of all the experiments was set as 90% RH at an ambient temperature to avoid condensation. Based on the preliminary test, the incident angle was set 0.09° and 0.14° to separate the surface and buried interface components. The Nika analysis package was used for data reduction.

X-ray Reflectivity Measurements

Reflectivity measurements were conducted under grazing incidence angles ranging from 0.1 to 1° with a step size of 0.0005° using a diffractometer (Rigaku SmartLab). The X-ray generator settings were precisely maintained at 40 kV and 40 mA. A custom-designed chamber, sealed with a beryllium window, was employed. Humidified argon gas was introduced into the chamber, with humidity precisely controlled using the same digital humidifier as employed in the GIWAXS measurements. All samples were analyzed at ambient temperatures of 25 ± 2 °C.

To ensure measurement accuracy, each sample underwent a pretreatment under pure argon for 6 h to ensure complete drying of the film. This was followed by a systematic increase in relative humidity up to 90% RH. Equilibrium was determined by monitoring adjacent interference fringes and the critical edge from the reflectivity pattern at 20 min intervals. The film was considered to have achieved equilibrium when there was no significant change in either the interference fringes or the critical edge. Each sample was maintained for approximately 80 min at each relative humidity to achieve equilibrium. Additionally, thickness and density were analyzed and fitted by modeling the reflectivity data using the Parratt formalism. The data fitting was performed using GenX 3 with advanced model, with error bars representing combined statistical and systematic uncertainties as determined by a 5% FOM variation threshold. The chemical structures of Nafion (EW 1100) and SPEEK (sulfonation degree = 69% RH) were assumed to be C20HF40O5S and C19H12O5S0.7, respectively. The densities of the bulk and buried interface layers in the fitting model were constrained to be higher than that of pure water and lower than that of the dry ionomer.

Results

When the thin film is subjected to high confinement or interacts strongly with a substrate, the mobility of molecules in and near the interfacial region may be constrained. It is widely acknowledged that organic, nonionic thin films confined to a thickness less than twice their unperturbed radius of gyration experience distortion in their coiled structure, leading to observable alterations in thin film mobility. Previous studies attempted to determine the Tg from the thermal expansion coefficient by monitoring thickness variation, but obtaining reliable data for Pt-supported films has remained challenging. When a substance undergoes a phase transition, its thermodynamic parameters experience sudden changes, resulting in discontinuities in the first-order derivatives of these parameters. In probing Delta, this implies that near the phase transition point, the properties of the substance undergo sudden changes, which result in a sudden jump in the rate of change of the thermodynamic parameter (first-order derivative), rather than a smooth and continuous alteration. , Consequently, such deviations could lead to significant differences in the thermal expansion coefficients of thin films when compared to their bulk counterparts.

Thin film thermal transitions can be conveniently examined through variable-temperature ellipsometry by observing the changes in ellipsometric angles (Ψ and Δ) with temperature (T), as shown in Figure a. Previous research , has demonstrated that a pronounced rise in Δ correlates with a sharp reduction in sample volume, and the peak in dΔ/dT signifies the minimum value of the coefficient of thermal expansion. This approach is considered to be more sensitive to thickness changes and thermal expansion than using the intersections of low and high regimes during the heating process. The temperature at which the expansion coefficient appears to be minimal represents the cold crystallization temperature of the film. Figure b presents the transition temperature as a function of film thickness. Generally, Nafion’s lower T g compared to SPEEK can be attributed to its intrinsically flexible PTFE backbone, which requires less energy for molecular motion. The T g of Nafion thin films almost unchanged from around 128 to 136 °C across the examined thickness range (13 to 109 nm). On the other hand, SPEEK films show a slightly increase in T g with increasing thickness. For thinner films (∼20 nm), the T g is approximately 175 °C, while at greater thicknesses (∼100 nm), it approaches 201 °C. The greater magnitude of the T g in SPEEK suggests that the reduced mobility at the buried interface may propagate deep into the interior (bulk phase), influencing the overall dynamics of the films over tens of nanometers or more. Previous in situ thermal ellipsometry studies on ionomer thin films with varying substrates and thicknesses suggest that relatively weak substrate/ionomer interactions (e.g., with SiO2 or octadecyltrichlorosilane-modified surfaces) can result in a more pronounced thermal expansion coefficient, consequently inducing a thickness-dependent T g relationship. Inspired by these findings, our results support that the observed slight and gradual increase in T g with thickness for SPEEK films may suggest relatively weaker interfacial interactions, while Nafion exhibits significantly stronger interfacial bonding at the buried interface. However, these findings cannot account for SPEEK’s superior thermal stability. In fact, the thickness-dependent T g behavior in SPEEK originates from its heterogeneous microstructure. As demonstrated in our previous study, the increasing proportion of free surface to bulk phasecharacterized by enhanced molecular mobilitydirectly governs the thickness-dependent T g variation. Therefore, these observations collectively demonstrate that while interfacial interactions (between sulfonic acid groups and the Pt surface) influence the thermal behavior of both ionomers, the distinct T g-thickness relationships originate from their inherent heterogeneous structures. Detailed characterization and discussion of the buried interface structure are presented in subsequent GIWAXS results. In the GIWAXS spectra of 13 nm Nafion and SPEEK films, a broad peak located at q = 1.2–1.3 Å–1 (Figure a–d) is typically associated with the hydrophobic backbone of SPEEK. The formation of this scattering halo results from the contributions from both the crystalline and amorphous regions. Subsequent detailed data analysis and fitting allowed for the differentiation of these scattering contributions, facilitating further investigation into their impact on the material properties. Based on the results in Figure a–d, the clarity of the scattering halo increases gradually as the incident angle changed from 0.09° to 0.14°. This distinct scattering halo suggests that the ordering of the main chain increase, resulting in increased crystallinity. In contrast, Nafion thin films remain almost unchanged as the incident angle varies. As we previously calculated based on the position of Yoneda peak, the scattering pattern obtained at 0.09° (below the critical angle of the film) reflects the free surface, while that obtained at 0.14° (above the critical angle of the film) reflects the entire structure. This suggests that the comparatively ordered buried interface structure contributes to a well-defined structure in SPEEK.

To better analyze the morphology of SPEEK and Nafion thin films, intensity profiles were extracted from the 2D scattering patterns in Figure e,f by integrating along both vertical and horizontal directions. Since scattering images represent reciprocal space, the correlation distance in real space can be calculated as d = 2π/q. In the in-plane scattering (Figure e), similar trends are observed for both SPEEK and Nafion thin films, with both exhibiting a broad crystalline/amorphous peak around 1.2–1.4 Å–1. The GIWAXS peak deconvolution methodology was demonstrated in our previous work in detail. The sharpness or full width at half-maximum (fwhm) did not show significant changes, except that the peak center of SPEEK slightly shifted to lower d-spacing compared to Nafion. In the out-of-plane results, SPEEK exhibited comparatively higher scattering intensity as the incident angle increased from 0.09° to 0.14°, reflecting the oriented structure of the buried interface. In contrast, Nafion thin films remained almost unchanged even as the incident angle varied, indicating a dispersion-like or amorphous structure throughout the film.

XRR Fitting Results for Nafion and SPEEK Thin Films

As expected, the observed reduction in density can be attributed to water absorption. Moreover, the more pronounced change in Nafion suggests the presence of a more substantial hydrophilic domain within the buried interface region. This disparity in water adsorption is thought to arise from factors such as chain conformation and ionomer/substrate interactions. Additionally, the angle-resolved GIWAXS results further corroborate the existence of a dense layer formed by the packing of the hydrophobic matrix. Unlike neutron reflectometry, which primarily reflects the water components of the structure, XRR can provide valuable insights into the non- water components or matrix density, roughness based on the different scattering length densities of the matrix between Nafion/SPEEK. Notably, the density obtained from XRR fitting is not able to accurately reflect the water content and distribution, but it can offer reliable density information regarding the film’s overall matrix density at a certain humidified level.

To address the insensitivity of XRR data regarding the characterization of the hydrophilic domain within thin films, we measured the thickness as a function of time and initial film thickness using in situ ellipsometry. Swelling is intricately connected to the structural dynamics within Nafion or SPEEK thin films, significantly influencing their water absorption behavior. This methodology provides deeper insights into how the differential solubility and absorption behavior of water in Nafion and SPEEK are affected by confinement within thin films and interactions with the substrate. Such interplay can markedly impact their water uptake dynamics and/or proton transport behavior. The results presented in Figure a illustrate the temporal evolution of film thickness for Nafion and SPEEK thin films, measured under conditions of 95% relative humidity (RH) at a constant temperature of 25 °C. Both materials exhibit an increase in thickness over time, indicative of their swelling behaviors. Notably, Nafion demonstrates a more pronounced and rapid increase in thickness compared to SPEEK during the initial stages of the measurement. Ultimately, Nafion reaches an equilibrium state with a thickness of approximately 14.2 nm. In contrast, SPEEK exhibits a more gradual increase in thickness before achieving its equilibrium film thickness. Based on eq , the average water content (λ) for Nafion is estimated to be 6.4, which aligns with previous studies reporting values between 5.9 and 7.3 for EW 725–1100 perfluorinated sulfonic acid (PFSA) ionomers. In contrast, SPEEK exhibits a lower λ value of 4.8. This discrepancy can be attributed to their differing affinities for water absorption, which arise from both the interfacial interactions and the bulk properties of the materials. The substantial difference in water uptake between the two materials highlights the influence of their chemical structures on their hydration behavior.

The distinct slopes of the thickness curves further highlight the differences in swelling dynamics between the two materials. Based on our fitting model in XRR, the swelling behavior can be attributed to two main components: the buried interface and the bulk phase. We propose that the total thickness during swelling can be expressed as follows: 2 L=A⁡exp(−τA/t)+B⁡exp(−τB/t)

L represents the total film thickness. The left term on the right side indicates the contribution of the buried interface to the overall film thickness, reflecting the strength of the interfacial interactions. Similarly, the right term denotes the initial influence of the bulk phase on the film thickness. τ A and τ B are time constants for diffusional transport in the interfacial transport and bulk phase swelling processes, respectively. The estimated τ A for Nafion and SPEEK are estimated to be 0.01 s and 0.018 s, respectively. Conversely, τ B of Nafion (0.93 s) is comparatively close to SPEEK (3.53 s). Referring to the depth analysis from the GIWAXS results, the intensification of the scattering halo in SPEEK confirms the existence of an ordered layer formed by the hydrophobic matrix, which is associated with the observed reduction in water uptake and subsequently influences the proton transport behavior.

Such a multilayer arrangement enhances ion transport in the in-plane direction while inhibiting the expansion of hydrophilic ion domains, resulting in reduced ion transport in the normal direction. In contrast, SPEEK’s main chain packing is more densely arranged along the in-plane orientation, resulting in shorter ion-leap distances and more uninterrupted transport pathways. Consequently, with fewer available water channels under low humidity conditions, ion mobility within SPEEK is limited, leading to its relatively inferior performance compared to Nafion. However, as humidity increases, the conductivity of both materials significantly improves due to the expansion of water channels within the bulk phase, ultimately achieving levels comparable to Nafion.

The phase separation pattern in films, delineating the division between water-enriched regions and the hydrophobic polymer matrix, profoundly influences the pathway and efficiency of proton transport. Discrepancies in the variance of proton conductivity between Nafion and SPEEK in thin films and bulk could stem from variations in their chemical compositions. ,− Nafion has a flexible backbone and long side chains, whereas SPEEK has no side chain and its backbone is a rigid aromatic structure. The sulfonic acid group is attached directly to a phenyl ring along the backbone of the polymer. This structural difference results in distinct microstructures for the hydrophilic domains of Nafion and SPEEK. In Nafion, sulfonic acid groups link through flexible side chains, promoting the creation of swelling lamellar structures upon water absorption, leading to interconnected channels. ,, Conversely, SPEEK features sulfonic acid groups directly bonded to the polymer backbone, constraining the hydrophilic region’s morphology and yielding smaller spherical clusters that demand a greater number of water molecules for establishing a continuous conduction pathway. Even though SPEEK can accommodate higher operating temperatures, its lower ionic conductivity may limit the overall operational efficiency of the proton exchange fuel cell.

In thin-film structure, particularly at the nanoscale, the confinement effect and interfacial interactions are significant physical phenomena that collectively influence the properties and behavior of the material. , Confinement effect typically induces disorder within the internal structure by disrupting traditional long-range ordering due to size limitations. Conversely, interfacial interactions tend to promote local order by influencing atoms or molecules near the interface, potentially leading to specific arrangements or orientations. , As a result, the behavior of the ionomer thin film is affected by these two key interactions, both of which propagate through the film in opposing directions and potentially with varying strengths. In summary, from the current state of understanding of SPEEK and Nafion thin films, ,,− the following thickness regimes can be identified in ultrathin film:(i) Bulk-like regime: where the majority of ionomer volume behaves like the inner regions of the bulk membrane. (ii) Buried interface regime (less than 3–5 nm): Fundamentally, both SPEEK and PFSA ionomers can be regarded as composites comprising a nonionic matrix (backbone) interspersed with localized ionic domains (hydrophilic domain), which are linked through a network of complex, winding pathways. This irregular connectivity within the matrix provides a unique structural framework for ion conduction as shown in Figure . For Nafion, when the film thickness is confined to approximately 13 nm, dispersion-like behavior with weak phase-separation is presented because the chemical dissolution energy overcomes the mechanical support energy. Even MD simulation revealed that a 4 nm layer exists around Pt (111), but experimental evidence is still lacking. This may be attributed to the challenge faced by Nafion films in sustaining adequate mechanical strength, resulting in morphological instability that impacts the film’s orderliness. For SPEEK, interfacial interactions are considered to overcome the energy barrier, and the molecules tend to be more ordered in their arrangement, especially at the buried interface. SPEEK is derived from the direct sulfonation of PEEK, featuring hydrophobic PEEK as the main chain and hydrophilic sulfonic acid groups as the side chain. PEEK molecular structure includes ether bonds (−O−) and ketone groups (>CO), endowing it with high molecular chain rigidity. Additionally, intermolecular interactions via van der Waals forces occur between the ether bonds and ketone groups of adjacent PEEK chains. Although relatively weak, these interactions are notably enhanced by the aggregation of numerous molecular chains, enabling SPEEK to exhibit considerable stability in ultrathin films. As a result, GIWAXS results indicate a robust interaction between the substrate and sulfonic acid groups at 13 nm, potentially prompting the emergence of a locally ordered structure. In Kodama’s study of PESA, it was discovered that the ether groups strongly interacted with the platinum surface through the lone pairs of oxygen atoms. Therefore, the ordering of the SPEEK thin films may be attributed to a combination of robust interactions between the ether and sulfonic acid groups of the main chain and the platinum substrate, as well as intermolecular van der Waals forces between the ether and ketone bonds on neighboring PEEK chains.

Scheme for comparison of the typical PFSA ionomer (Nafion) and aromatic ionomer (SPEEK) with a thickness of 13 nm. In the interfacial layer, Nafion exhibits a more interconnected hydrophilic channel structure that promotes higher ionic conductivity, whereas SPEEK displays a denser and more ordered interfacial structure that restricts the formation of proton conduction pathways, thereby influencing its overall electrochemical performance.

Considering the molecule design, unlike PFSA comparative long and flexible side-chain, GIWAXS reveals SPEEK’s dense interfacial layer stems from multipoint Pt coordination (sulfonate/main-chain ether–ketone), restricting proton pathways. To optimize performance, future ionomers should minimize backbone oxygen content to reduce Pt–O induced densification while incorporating fluorinated side chains to enhance phase separation.

Conclusions

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of thin films composed of the ionomers sulfonated poly­(ether ether ketone) (SPEEK) and perfluorinated sulfonic acid (Nafion), designed to mimic the catalyst layer environment. Our findings reveal that SPEEK films, particularly at a thickness of 13 nm, exhibit enhanced thermal stability and crystallinity. This enhancement stems from comparatively strong interfacial interactions with the Pt substrate, resulting in an ordered buried interface and a more pronounced heterogeneous morphology. While Nafion exhibits a dispersion-like structure, interfacial interactions dominate the morphology of SPEEK. These distinct morphological characteristics between SPEEK and Nafion arise from their fundamental chemical differences: SPEEK possesses a rigid aromatic backbone with sulfonic acid groups attached directly to the phenyl rings, forming restricted hydrophilic clusters. In contrast, Nafion’s flexible backbone and longer side chains facilitate significant swelling upon hydration, leading to the formation of interconnected hydrophilic channels that promote higher ionic conductivity. Although SPEEK exhibits superior thermal stability, its denser interfacial structurecharacterized by higher local ordering and reduced d-spacing, as revealed by GIWAXSmay simultaneously constrain proton transport pathways and limit oxygen permeation. This microstructural feature likely stems from the rigid aromatic backbone and restricted hydrophilic domains of SPEEK, in contrast to Nafion’s flexible, interconnected channels. Consequently, the suppressed gas transport properties could further compromise the overall fuel cell performance.

Although oxygen permeation was not explicitly tested in this work, existing studies on aromatic sulfonated polymers like SPEEK, or materials with short side chains, have experimentally demonstrated that such structures impede well-defined hydrophilic/hydrophobic phase separation. This restricted phase separation negatively impacts proton mass transport. These insights inform the future design of alternative ionomer conducting materials. Specifically, minimizing oxygen content within the polymer backbone while incorporating extended side chains of deliberately engineered length could serve as an effective strategy to mitigate excessive adsorption-induced dense interfacial structures at the ionomer/substrate interface. Optimizing the design and development of advanced ionomer materials based on these principles is crucial for enhancing fuel cell performance.
