Scabies outbreak in Bangladesh: a growing public health crisis
Authors: Md Nasir Ahmed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Scabies is spreading quickly in crowded, low-income communities.•Poor hygiene and tight living spaces drive rapid transmission.•Reinfection is common, even after standard treatment.•Mass drug campaigns help, but fragile systems limit long-term success.•Policy action needed: treat scabies as a climate-sensitive disease.Keywords: Climate outbreak, Public health, Reinfection, Scabies, Tropical infectious disease, Bangladesh

Bangladesh has witnessed a sharp increase in scabies infections, locally known as khosh-pachra, with outbreaks recorded in Cumilla, Chattogram, and Rajshahi in early 2025. Meanwhile, Cox’s Bazar saw a surge in cases among refugee populations in early 2023. The highly contagious nature of scabies, caused by the human itch mite, results in intense itching, rashes, sores, and, in severe cases, crusted lesions. Vulnerable populations, including children, the elderly, and immunocompromised individuals, face the highest risk of infection and complications.

The scabies outbreak at Rajshahi University has escalated rapidly, affecting students in residential halls and adjacent messes, raising concerns across campus [1]. Meanwhile, the Cumilla outbreak has resulted in 80% of dermatology patients being infected, with high transmission rates in Muradnagar and Debidwar Upazilas. Delayed treatment has led to kidney complications and drug-resistant infections, increasing pressure on local healthcare services [2]. Similarly, Chattogram has seen a dramatic rise in cases, jumping from 200 daily in March to more than 500 in May. Previously more common in slum areas, scabies now affects all socioeconomic groups, with hostels, dormitories, and schools identified as high-risk environments. Women and children remain the most vulnerable, with doctors emphasizing early diagnosis, hygiene awareness, and simultaneous treatment of all family members to prevent reinfection [3].

Although intervention efforts have been implemented in urban outbreaks, the challenges are even more pronounced in refugee settings. The scabies outbreak within the Rohingya refugee camps in Cox’s Bazar has reached critical levels, with 66.42% of the population affected, surpassing previous estimates by Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF) (40%) and aligning with WHO reports of prevalence as high as 70% [4]. Over 18 months, MSF recorded 179,000 consultations, with one-fifth of cases presenting complications, highlighting the overcrowded living conditions, poor sanitation, and limited access to medical care as key drivers of transmission [5]. Environmental factors such as dust exposure, soil flooring, and seasonal variations accelerate the spread of disease, reinforcing the necessity for better healthcare infrastructure and awareness programs [4].

Scabies remains a major public health concern in Bangladesh, particularly among children under 12 years old, with overcrowding and poor hygiene driving transmission. The disease is especially prevalent in madrasahs (Islamic educational institutions), where physical contact and shared personal items increase infection risks, leading to a 34% prevalence rate. Despite its significant impact on quality of life, scabies remains underrepresented in national health strategies, unlike other tropical diseases such as tuberculosis and malaria [6]. The hyperendemicity in Dhaka’s madrasahs is especially alarming, with 98% infection rates and 71% reinfection, predominantly in winter months, exacerbated by poor ventilation, overcrowded sleeping arrangements, and inadequate hygiene practices. Socioeconomic factors further compound transmission, as low parental education and income levels contribute to unsanitary conditions, where many children share towels, undergarments, and bed linen [7]. Similarly, Dhaka’s residential institutions reflect equally high infection rates (98%), with 71% reinfection, particularly in boys living in congested, unsanitary spaces. Although anti-scabies treatments show an 85.5% cure rate, the persistent reinfection cycle highlights the urgent need for sustainable hygiene interventions and improved living conditions [8]. To effectively curb the transmission of disease and protect vulnerable groups, the review emphasizes the need for coordinated public health action, comprehensive awareness campaigns, and prevention strategies in schools [9].

Complementing these findings, a study involving 1131 resident school and madrasah children revealed a 21% prevalence rate, with the highest infection rates among children aged 7-12 years and those from rural areas. Socioeconomic factors played a critical role, as most affected children came from families with low parental education and income, contributing to poor hygiene awareness. Overcrowding was a major concern, with 79% of infected children sharing sleeping spaces and more than half residing in non-cemented homes. Many children experienced prolonged symptoms and had frequent contact with domestic animals. Clinically, almost all cases presented with severe itching at night, and many children had symptoms lasting more than 2 weeks. These findings emphasize the urgent need for public health initiatives, mass awareness campaigns, and targeted interventions to enhance hygiene education and improve environmental conditions in both institutional and rural areas [10].

The high prevalence of scabies in rural communities of north-central Bangladesh underlines the widespread nature of the disease, with itching and sleep disturbances emerging as the most reported symptoms. Common skin lesions, including excoriations, vesicles, and papules, were observed, predominantly affecting the abdomen, inguinal area, and interdigital spaces. Poverty-related factors, such as illiteracy, low household income, absence of solid flooring, and overcrowding, were strongly associated with infestation, while behavioral patterns such as sharing beds, pillows, and clothing contributed to further transmission. Regular bathing and soap use were identified as protective factors, highlighting the role of hygiene practices in disease prevention [11]. Beyond rural areas, flood-affected regions in Bangladesh have faced an alarming surge in scabies cases, exacerbated by poor sanitation, overcrowding, and lack of healthcare access. Reports from volunteer medical camps revealed high infection rates, with scabies symptoms observed in more than 75 patients out of 110 consultations, and approximately 2800 cases among 3500 patients over 2 days. These conditions have severely impacted displaced individuals and impoverished households, further reinforcing the link between hygiene challenges, socioeconomic hardship, and disease persistence. The outbreak signals a looming epidemic, demanding urgent intervention through improved public health education, enhanced sanitation measures, and sustainable healthcare access to prevent further escalation [12]. The high prevalence of scabies in Bangladesh is evident across diverse settings, from educational institutions and urban communities to flood-affected areas, reflecting widespread transmission risks. In industrial areas, studies at Monno Medical College, Manikganj, reported 28.66% prevalence among children aged 1-10 years, with low socioeconomic groups (58.66%) disproportionately affected, highlighting the link between poverty and infestation rates [13]. Beyond industrial settings, the social and psychological burden of scabies is also evident, as research at Enam Medical College, Dhaka, shows that 92% of adults face workplace difficulties, while 78.79% experience embarrassment, reinforcing the stigmatization of the disease [14]. In urban healthcare facilities, the significant burden of scabies is further highlighted by findings from Ashiyan Medical College Hospital, Dhaka, where overall prevalence stands at 34.2%, primarily affecting individuals aged 19–36 years, with males impacted more than females. Environmental and demographic factors, such as crowded living conditions and exposure to dust, exacerbate transmission risks. Additionally, seasonal influences play a role, with winter months accounting for 72.5% of cases, while family history (71.7%) suggests a genetic or environmental predisposition [15]. A common thread across these settings is the role of hygiene and behavioral practices, as shared sleeping spaces and bedding/clothing are strongly associated with scabies occurrence. Improving hygiene, offering targeted help, and making healthcare more accessible are key to reducing scabies spread and impact in Bangladesh [15]. The first study, conducted at BIRDEM General Hospital in Dhaka, focuses on clinical diagnosis and treatment, identifying topical 5% permethrin as the primary therapy. Although oral ivermectin is not widely licensed, it may be beneficial for institutional outbreaks or patients unable to comply with topical treatment. Strict hygiene and systematic treatment of close contacts are essential to prevent reinfection, with itching resolution taking up to 4 weeks. The study underlines scabies prevalence in overcrowded conditions, affecting individuals across all social groups, with children (1–12 years) experiencing the highest incidence [16].

Building on these epidemiological insights, public awareness remains a crucial factor in managing scabies transmission. The second study, conducted in Chattogram City Corporation, highlights widespread misconceptions regarding symptoms, prevention, and hygiene practices. Among 200 surveyed participants, 68% correctly identified itching as the primary symptom, while 27% expressed uncertainty. Although 89% acknowledged the role of personal hygiene in prevention, only 14% recognized the importance of environmental disinfection. These findings reveal persistent misunderstandings about scabies’ contagious nature and associated stigma, emphasizing the urgent need for targeted public health campaigns. Strengthening community education, healthcare provider training, and preventive strategies is crucial to reducing transmission and improving overall health outcomes [17].

Although public education remains critical, structured intervention programs in institutional settings have also demonstrated high efficacy in reducing scabies prevalence. The third study, a controlled trial in urban Bangladesh madrasahs, assessed the effectiveness of a scabies control program in high-risk environments. The study involved four intervention madrasahs (2359 students) and four control madrasahs (2465 students) in Dhaka, where scabies prevalence was initially high (61% and 62%, respectively). Following mass treatment with 5% permethrin cream, daily hygiene monitoring, weekly health education, and preventive measures, prevalence dropped significantly to 5% in intervention madrasahs, compared to 50% in control madrasahs after 4 months (P <0.001). Alongside improved personal hygiene practices, scabies knowledge scores increased from 40-99% among intervention students. The program, costing US $1.60 per student, demonstrated high efficacy, emphasizing the importance of hygiene education, preventive strategies, and affordable intervention models [18].

Although institutional interventions have succeeded, controlling scabies outbreaks in refugee settings presents additional challenges due to overcrowding, poor sanitation, and limited medical access, requiring urgent, coordinated action from policymakers, non-governmental organizations, and healthcare providers. While oral ivermectin is effective, permethrin poses adherence issues in water-scarce settings, and bacterial superinfections demand antibiotic intervention, further straining resources. Although mass drug administration has reduced cases elsewhere, its long-term sustainability in Cox’s Bazar remains uncertain, necessitating community engagement, improved WASH (Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene) interventions, and health education. Screening, treatment, and family-centered hygiene programs must be expanded, alongside camp decongestion and sanitation upgrades to prevent reinfections. Securing international funding, empowering health workers, and fostering research innovation are critical, while public awareness campaigns should reinforce personal hygiene and transmission prevention. Strengthening primary health care and advocacy for sustainable intervention funding are essential to long-term disease control [4,5].

All in all, scabies, a neglected tropical and climate-sensitive infectious disease, affects more than 455 million people annually, with children and institutional populations bearing the highest burden [19]. To control scabies, new strategies are needed that address reinfection and the effects of climate change, as rising temperatures, changes in humidity, and extreme weather conditions increase its spread [20,21]. In Bangladesh, recurrent outbreaks emphasize the urgent need for integrated public health responses. High reinfection rates are driven by inadequate sanitation, overcrowded living conditions, and limited access to healthcare, particularly in madrasahs, refugee camps, and low-income communities. Although permethrin remains the primary treatment, oral ivermectin offers a practical alternative in institutional settings and areas with limited water resources. However, without sustained improvements in hygiene and environmental interventions, transmission cycles are likely to persist. The emergence of permethrin-resistant scabies further complicates control efforts [22], demanding new management strategies, including alternative pharmacological options such as moxidectin [23], plant-derived remedies [24], and enhanced surveillance systems [25].
