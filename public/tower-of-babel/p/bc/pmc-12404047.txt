Regarding: ‘comparison between butorphanol and nalbuphine for alleviation of catheter-related bladder discomfort and emergence agitation in patients undergoing open spinal surgery: a randomized clinical trial’
Authors: Po-Ni Hsiao, Hsing-Hao Huang, Ming-Hui Hung
Published: 2025

To the Editor

We read with great interest the randomized clinical trial by Wang et al. which compared butorphanol and nalbuphine for alleviating catheter-related bladder discomfort (CRBD) and emergence agitation (EA) after spinal surgery [1]. The authors are to be commended for highlighting this important but often under-recognized complication and the potential role of κ-opioid receptor agonists in its prevention.

While the study concluded that butorphanol is superior to nalbuphine, we believe the chosen dose ratio may represent an important methodological consideration. The study protocol adopted a 1:5 ratio (butorphanol 0.02 mg/kg vs. nalbuphine 0.1 mg/kg) based on their relative analgesic potencies to morphine [2,3]. This rationale is appropriate for somatic pain but may not be applicable to CRBD, which is predominantly a visceral discomfort mediated through κ-receptor activation. Using equianalgesic doses derived from general analgesia may therefore not ensure equipotency at the relevant receptor target.

Independent pharmacological data by Peng et al. demonstrated a marked disparity in κ-receptor binding: the Ki value for butorphanol was 0.079 compared with 2.20 for nalbuphine, with κ/μ selectivity ratios of 3.0 versus 0.4, respectively [4]. These differences suggest that the nalbuphine dose selected may have been subtherapeutic for CRBD prevention, thereby underestimating its efficacy. Thus, the apparent superiority of butorphanol may partly reflect non-equipotent dosing rather than intrinsic pharmacological advantage.

In addition, it should be emphasized that postoperative pain itself is a strong confounder for both CRBD and EA. Even in the context of a randomized clinical trial, inadequate control of wound pain may overlap with catheter-related discomfort and agitation, complicating interpretation of the results. Both agents may differentially influence somatic pain as well as visceral discomfort, further challenging attribution of effects solely to κ-receptor pharmacology.

Future trials incorporating dose-ranging designs or receptor occupancy-based modeling, alongside more rigorous pain control stratification, would help establish equipotent regimens for this specific indication. Until such data are available, clinicians should interpret the superiority of butorphanol with caution, recognizing that both dose selection and postoperative pain may underlie the observed differences.
