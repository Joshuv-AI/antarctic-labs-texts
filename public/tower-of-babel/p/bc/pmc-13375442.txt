Functional Vestibular Abilities in Children and Adolescents With and Without Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus: Preliminary Results
Authors: Anwar B Almutairi, Jennifer B Christy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeThe aim of this study was to explore functional use of the vestibular system in children and adolescents with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) aged between 10 and 17 years compared to age and gender‐matched control group using a comprehensive battery of standardized tests.Materials and MethodsParticipants with T1D were recruited from hospitals in Kuwait. The control group was recruited through social media. All participants went through functional vestibular system testing that included: (1) clinical dynamic visual acuity (cDVA), (2) the head impulse test (HIT), (3) the modified clinical test of sensory interaction on balance (mCTSIB), (4) the bucket test of subjective visual vertical (SVV), and (5) functional gait assessment (FGA).ResultsA group of 11 children and adolescents were (female n = 5 [45.45%], mean age = 13.64 years, SD = 2.69) compared with 11 age and gender‐matched peers without T1DM. All participants tolerated vestibular testing. Significant differences between groups were seen in the overall scores of the mCTSIB (T1DM = 111.54, SD = 13.92 vs. control = 118.73, SD = 2.43, p = 0.001) and specifically, condition 4 (T1DM = 23.33, SD = 10.45 vs. control = 29.09, SD = 2.28, p < 0.001) compared to their peers. None of the other tests were significantly different between the groups. Only 18% of the T1DM group (n = 2) had a positive HIT. SVV variance was positively correlated with HbA1C (r(9) = 0.772, p = 0.005).ConclusionsChildren and adolescents with T1DM had more difficulty using vestibular function to resolve sensory conflict for postural control compared to their peers without T1DM. The finding suggests that dynamic visual acuity and functional gait were comparable between groups. Although not significantly different between groups, the variance of perception of verticality was correlated with glycemic control.Level of EvidenceLevel 4Keywords: adolescents, balance, children, gaze stability, type 1 diabetes mellitus, vestibular function

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a complex metabolic disorder which is known to be heterogeneous and characterized by elevated concentration of blood glucose due to deficient insulin secretion or resistance to the action of the insulin, or both [1, 2]. Type 1 DM (T1DM) and type 2 DM are the most common prevalent types of diabetes in both children and adolescents. T1DM is caused by autoimmune damage of the β cells of the pancreas [3, 4]. The incidence of T1DM in Kuwait has been reported as 40.9 per 100,000 individuals [5] compared to a much lower incidence report of 3.96 per 100,000 individuals in the early 1980s [6]. The well documented complications of T1DM are peripheral neuropathy, retinopathy, and nephropathy [3, 4]. These complications are caused by chronic hyperglycemia leading to micro‐ and macro‐vascular complications [7]. One of the under researched complications associated with DM is vestibular dysfunction.

The vestibular system originates in the inner ears and includes three semicircular canals and two otolith organs [8]. It projects through the superior and inferior vestibular portions of the eighth nerve to the brainstem and cerebellum and down through the vestibular mediated reflexes [9]. The main functions of the vestibular system are: (1) gaze stabilization, (2) perception of vertical, and (3) static and dynamic balance abilities [1, 7]. The inner ear organs are metabolically very active and do not store energy; therefore, alterations in the blood glucose would compromise the function of this system [10]. Hyperglycemia also affects the brain by causing macrovascular and microvascular complications leading to brain structure and function alterations [9, 11]. Although limited, several studies have documented that adolescents with T1DM exhibited dysfunction of the saccule tested with cervical vestibular evoked myogenic potentials, a widely accepted laboratory test of saccular function. Prolonged P1 and N1 latencies and reduced P1‐N1 amplitude were the main findings shown in adolescents with T1DM [12, 13, 14]. Others researchers explored the visuo‐ocular components of the vestibular system and concluded that children and adolescents with T1DM presented with vestibular‐visual dysfunctions, like asymmetric optokinetic nystagmus, bilateral caloric hyporesponsiveness, spontaneous nystagmus that are not suppressed by visual fixation [12], as measured with videonystagmography. This shows that children with T1DM were shown to have both central and peripheral vestibular dysfunction, detected by standardized vestibular tests.

Dysfunction of the vestibular system has been shown to affect activities and participation in the community [7]. This can manifest in poor balance when other somatosensation abilities are challenged (i.e., on grass or at the beach), which may result in falls and inability to perform activities requiring balance such as riding a two wheeled bike. This can be detrimental for a system with slow healing abilities that can be found in children with T1DM [4]. Another manifestation of a dysfunctional vestibular system is lack of gaze stability that could affect the person's ability to see clearly as they are moving in the world resulting in dizziness and oscillopsia. Vestibular dysfunction may also hinder the ability to play sports with peers, which is an integral part of a child's social and physical development [15]. Early detection through clinical testing and management of vestibular dysfunction through vestibular rehabilitation for individuals with T1DM may lead to avoidance of these manifestations [16, 17].

The literature provided some evidence about the disturbance of the vestibular system, both central and peripheral, in individuals with T1DM but, to our knowledge, no studies have explored functional use of the vestibular system in a comprehensive manner; therefore, the aim of this study is to explore functional use of the vestibular system in children with T1DM aged between 8 and 17 years compared to age‐matched controls using a comprehensive vestibular battery of tests. Our hypothesis is that due to the uncontrolled insulin and glycose levels, the peripheral vestibular system would be compromised in this population.

Methods

Study Design

This study is an exploratory case–control study examining children and adolescents with T1D compared with an age and gender‐matched control group (i.e., typically developing and without T1D).

Ethical Approval and Consent to Participate

Ethical approval for the current study was obtained from the health science center ethical committee at Kuwait university (VDR/EC‐508). The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Signed written informed assent and consent forms were obtained from all participants and their caregivers, respectively.

Population

Children and adolescents with T1DM were recruited from hospitals around the state of Kuwait. To be included in the trial, participants were: (1) diagnosed with T1DM by a physician for more than 1 year, and (2) aged between 8 and 18 years old. Participants with T1DM were excluded if they had a vestibular dysfunction diagnosis from other causes (e.g., severe to profound sensorineural hearing impairment), any surgery within the last 6 months, previous head or neck injury (e.g., concussion), or primary neurological, otological or psychological disorders.

For the control group, age and gender‐matched typically developed children and adolescents were recruited from the community through social media. To be included in the control group, participants were: (1) aged between 8 and 18 years old and did not have a diagnosis of vestibular, neurological, nor chronic illnesses.

Procedure

Participants who met the eligibility criteria (i.e., T1DM or control) were scheduled for a vestibular assessment session during which demographic and medical history information were obtained. The information included date of birth, gender, history of the disease, including lab results (i.e., serum glycated hemoglobin [HbA1C] level within the past 6 months), history of dizziness, history of diabetic ketoacidosis in the past 6 months, and hypoglycemic attacks in the past 6 months, family history of DM, diabetic complications (i.e., retinopathy, neuropathy, nephropathy), insulin regimen (multiple daily injection [MDI] vs. continuous sub cutaneous insulin infusion [CSII]), other medications, and medical co‐morbidities. Height and weight were measured during the testing session for both groups.

Outcomes

Both groups underwent a comprehensive clinical vestibular testing battery with tests completed in the same order for all participants. Testing was done in 1 day by trained physical therapists. Each individual test was conducted by the same therapist for all participants to ensure consistency. The order of testing was as follows.

Height and weight measurement: Height was measured using a portable stadiometer without shoes or socks, with the participant facing away from the rod. Weight was measured using a bioelectrical impedance analyzer.

Modified clinical test of sensory interaction on balance (mCTSIB) [18, 19, 20]: The mCTSIB has been shown to be reliable and valid to detect functional vestibular impairments in children with sensorineural hearing impairment [19] and cerebral palsy [18]. The mCTSIB assesses the child's ability to maintain balance in four conditions, in which the surface (stable/compliant) or the visual feedback (eyes open/eyes closed) was altered. Participants were asked to stand with bare feet together (no shoes or socks) with arms crossed over his/her chest on solid ground with eyes open then closed, followed by standing on a compliant foam surface (Airex AG, Sins, Switzerland) with eyes open then closed, 30 s for each condition. If the participant could not complete the task, they were given two more trials and the average of the time for all trials was calculated and recorded as the score for that condition. The examiner would stop the timer if the participant moved their feet or arms off the position or opened their eyes in the conditions of the eyes closed. The participants were asked to stand in a corner to avoid visual distractions and provide more protection in case of loss of balance. Based on previous studies of the mCTSIB in children with vestibular hypofunction if total of seconds of all four conditions was ≤ 110 s, the test would be considered positive [18, 19].

The clinical dynamic visual acuity (cDVA) test: This is a test shown to be reliable and valid in children with sensorineural hearing loss [19] and cerebral palsy [18] to examine the ability to use the vestibulo‐ocular reflex (VOR) in a functional way. In this test, the participant sat 10 ft away from a Lea Symbols chart (Good‐Lite Company, Elgin, IL, USA). The participant was asked to identify the optotypes with the head still (static) and while being moved in yaw axis (i.e., horizontal) at 2 Hz to the beat of a metronome (240 beats/min) (dynamic). The participants would identify the optotypes down the chart (i.e., getting smaller) until he/she would state that they cannot see the optotypes anymore. The number of incorrect optotypes was noted in each condition [18, 19]. The difference between the static and dynamic conditions was calculated. Based on previous studies of children with vestibular dysfunction, if the score was ≥ 10 optotypes, the test was considered positive [18, 19].

The bucket test of subjective visual vertical (SVV) was then examined using previously published methods [18, 19]. This is a test of perception of vertical, which is primarily mediated by the utricular pathways. Previous studies have indicated that perception of SVV, specifically the variance between trials, is impaired in children with cerebral palsy and adults with concussion [21]. The bucket test was built using an opaque bucket (23.5‐cm diameter) with a printed picture of a computer‐generated straight line on the inside bottom and an inclinometer on the outside bottom. After explaining the task, the examiner asked the child to close their eyes, placed the bucket over the participant's face beyond the external meatus of the ears, then randomly set the line to left or right, at 30°–45° off true vertical. The participant was then asked to open their eyes and to say “stop” when they perceive that the line was “straight up and down like a flagpole.” Ten trials, five to the right and five to the left, were completed and recorded. Mean, variance, and median scores were calculated. Normal scores for SVV mean and variance are ≤ 2.24° and ≤ 2.33°, respectively [18].

The clinical head impulse test (cHIT) was done to assess the horizontal canal's function following previously published methodology [18, 19]. The examiner, with a sticker on their nose, started by sitting at eye level with the participant and asked the participant to keep their eye on the sticker while their head was moved in yaw axis (i.e., horizontal) in an amplitude of 10°–20° and in high velocity (i.e., > 100°/s), after being slightly tipped forward. A corrective saccade to return to the target is considered a positive test. Five trials to the right and five trials to the left were completed. The positive tests and the direction were recorded [18, 19, 20].

Finally, the functional gait assessment (FGA) was conducted to examine the dynamic walking abilities in different tasks that challenge the vestibular system. This tool has 10 items scored on an ordinal scale ranging between 0 (severe impairment) and 3 (no impairment). Higher scores indicate better function (maximum is 30). Some of the more challenging tasks include walking in tandem, walking backwards, walking with eyes closed, and walking while performing vertical and horizontal head turns [22]. The normal score ≥ 27 [22]. The FGA has recently been published and modified for children with sensorineural hearing impairment and vestibular hypofunction [23]. However, this study used methods published for adults [22].

Statistical Analysis

The data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The statistical significance level was accepted as α = 0.05. Categorical variables were presented as count (n) and percentage (%), while continuous variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation (mean ± SD). The positive tests were determined using the cut‐off criteria presented by Christy et al. [19]. Independent samples t‐test and chi‐square were used to compare between groups and within the group based on the type of variable. Pearson correlation was used to assess the correlation between the demographic data and the tests.

Results

A total of 22 children and adolescents participated in this study. Eleven children and adolescents with T1DM (female n = 5 [45.45%], mean age = 13.64 years, SD = 2.69) were compared with 11 age and gender‐matched participants without T1DM. All participants tolerated the vestibular testing battery. The mean HbA1C for the T1DM group was 10.59 ± 2.91. Three participants (27.27%) reported at least one dizziness episode in the last 6 months. Nine participants (81.81%) reported having at least one first degree relative with T1DM. Discussion

The detection of vestibular dysfunction, particularly among pediatric populations (i.e., ≤ 17 years of age) is overwhelmingly insufficient with many children and adolescents going unrecognized and untreated [24]. This study aimed to assess the functional use of the vestibular system in children and adolescents with T1DM compared to an age‐ and gender‐matched group of their peers without T1DM. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to assess this function in children and adolescents with T1DM using a full clinical battery of standard vestibular tests. The results showed that children with T1DM had a significantly lower mCTSIB total score and Condition 4 score. Condition 4 is where participants stand with eyes closed and on a compliant surface (i.e., foam), which challenges the functional vestibular system to resolve the sensory conflict. This finding is contrary to another study that found balance and postural abilities were not affected in children with T1DM using Romberg and Fukuda tests [1]. It is worth noting that both of these tests were found not to be sensitive nor specific in the detection of vestibular dysfunction [25, 26]. Since mCTSIB is a valid and reliable tool to assess balance and postural abilities. Contrary to Romberg and Fukuda tests, mCTSIB is more sensitive for identifying children with vestibular impairments, thus the findings of this study may be more valid. This study did not assess peripheral neuropathy, instead just enquired about it during demographic data interview (i.e., patient reported). This could also cause the disturbed balance shown during mCTSIB test.

Another interesting finding in this study is that HbA1c results had a positive strong correlation with SVV variance which is used to assess perception of verticality, driven by central utricular pathways. El Shafei and colleagues [1] showed that the only statistically significant factor that positively correlated with saccadic latency in children with T1DM was HbA1C. Similarly, Hamed et al. [13] showed that poor glycemic control and it lasting for a long time were predictors of saccular dysfunction children with T1DM with a mean age of 14.05 ± 1.82 years. Both studies showed that uncontrolled T1DM has an effect of the function of the otolith organs in adolescents and children with T1DM. It should be noted that the saccule is one contributor to the postural control system. Noteworthy, within T1DM group analysis revealed that participants reported DKA episodes within the last 6 months had significantly higher SVV variance compared to participants with T1DM that did not. This indicates inconsistency with their SVV, which could be caused by damaged utricles. It is worth mentioning that calculating the clinical SVV variance of the offset score was found to correlate with laser SVV variance of the offset score compared to the mean offset score of SVV [18]. This was due to the high variability noticed with children with compromised SVV (i.e., children with cerebral palsy), which was caused for the mean to appear normal due to the mathematic nature of the mean calculation (i.e., sum of scores divided by the number of trials). Therefore, SVV variance is more reliable to detect utricular pathway damage, compared to the SVV mean.

Examining the variables based on gender showed similar trends between groups; females had significantly worse scores on mCTSIB total score and Condition 4 score in the T1DM group. This could be caused by the hormonal changes experienced by prepubertal and post‐pubertal females [27, 28]. It was shown that during the follicular phase of the menstrual cycle, greater glucose disposal was observed during hyperglycemic–hyperinsulinemic clamp [27]. It was also found that progesterone hinders the negative action of insulin, leading to an increased dose in females with T1DM [27]. Registry‐based studies also observed gender differences where teenage females exhibited higher HbA1c levels compared to teenage males [29, 30]. These phenomena could cause damage to the vestibular system organs, leading to a higher prevalence of vestibular dysfunction among females with T1DM [28, 29, 30], causing balance disturbances evident by significantly worse scores on mCTSIB scores.

It was also found that five participants had positive test results in one or more of the tests, compared to no positive tests shown in the control group. This indicates that these children should be referred for comprehensive vestibular function testing. The accidental discovery of asymptomatic vestibular dysfunction was confirmed by several studies. Moossavi and team found that 80% of their sample of young adults with T1DM had prolonged ocular vestibular evoked myogenic potentials (oVEMPs) latency and 66.67% with a prolonged cervical VEMPs latency [31] indicating dysfunction of the utricle and saccule or along their pathways [20, 32]. Vestibular dysfunction, if remained unmanaged, can lead to further impairments and activity limitations and participation restrictions in this population. Therefore, regular testing of the functional vestibular system in children and adolescents with T1DM should be implemented to intervene early if any impairments were to be detected. This also serves as a baseline for larger studies in the future.

Like any scientific endeavor, this study had limitations. One of which is the small sample size which would hinder the generalizability of these results. Future studies should aim to duplicate this study on a larger sample to confirm the findings. The clinical nature of the tests can limit the objectivity of the results of the study. Lack of assessment for peripheral neuropathy to rule out this comorbidity common with T1DM was another limitation. Additional investigations should compare the laboratory tests along with the clinical tests on the same population to enhance objectivity and delineate the nature of peripheral versus central vestibular dysfunction in the findings of the results.

Conclusion

Impairment with functional use of vestibular information during postural stability can be detected in children and adolescents with T1DM compared to their peers without T1DM. The findings suggest that dynamic visual acuity and functional gait were comparable between groups. Although not significantly different between groups, the variance of perception of verticality was correlated with glycemic control. The functional vestibular system in children and adolescents with T1DM should be tested regularly to intervene early if any impairments are detected. We recommend this screening be performed at least annually, with a focus on mCTSIB and SVV testing as a guide of the evaluation.
