Clinical and mechanical outcomes of robotic-assisted versus conventional total knee arthroplasty: a retrospective propensity-matched analysis of 163,516 patients
Authors: Amir Ghafarian, Muaaz Wajahath, Noah Hodson, Jawad Saad, Ali Mehaidli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTotal knee arthroplasty (TKA) achieves good overall outcomes, yet 15–30% of patients remain dissatisfied, often attributed in part to variability in implant positioning and soft-tissue balance. Robotic-assisted TKA (RA-TKA) aims to improve intraoperative accuracy, but its real-world association with postoperative healthcare utilization and mid-term mechanical outcomes remains uncertain at scale. This study compared 90-day utilization and complication outcomes and 2-year mechanical outcomes of RA-TKA versus conventional TKA (C-TKA) in a large, national propensity-matched cohort.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used the TriNetX US Collaborative Network to evaluate outcomes following primary TKA among adults undergoing TKA between January 2018 and January 2023. Patients receiving RA-TKA were identified and 1:1 propensity-matched to C-TKA patients based on demographics and comorbidities. Primary outcomes were 90-day inpatient hospitalization encounter and 90-day opioid prescription fill. Secondary outcomes included 2-year mechanical outcomes (revision TKA and mechanical loosening diagnoses). Analyses were performed within TriNetX using built-in propensity matching and risk ratio calculations with 95% confidence intervals. After propensity score matching, covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences, with values < 0.10 indicating acceptable balance.ResultsPSM analysis yielded 6,176 pairs of RA-TKA and C-TKA patients (mean age 65.3 ± 8.6 years; 37.5% female; BMI 32.1 ± 8.7). RA-TKA was associated with lower 90-day inpatient hospitalization encounters (4.0% vs. 5.7%, RR 0.69; p < 0.001) and fewer opioid prescription fills (78.4% vs. 85.8%, RR 0.91; p < 0.001). Early medical and surgical complication rates were similar between groups (all p > 0.05). At 2 years, RA-TKA demonstrated lower rates of revision (1.3% vs. 2.2%, RR 0.58; p < 0.001) and mechanical loosening diagnoses (0.28% vs. 0.78%, RR 0.35; p < 0.001), with no difference in manipulation under anesthesia (3.26% vs. 3.22%, p = 0.919).ConclusionIn this large propensity-matched national cohort, RA-TKA was associated with lower 90-day inpatient hospitalization encounters and opioid prescription fills, and lower coded revision and loosening outcomes at 2 years, while early complication rates were similar. These findings should be interpreted as associations given the use of administrative coding and unmeasured confounding.Keywords: Robotic-assisted total knee arthroplasty, Total knee arthroplasty, Propensity-matched analysis, Mechanical complications, Revision surgery, 90-day inpatient hospitalization encounter, Opioid utilization, Real-world evidence, TriNetX database

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is among the most commonly performed orthopedic procedures and provides durable symptom relief for many patients with end-stage knee osteoarthritis. Despite generally favorable outcomes, a meaningful subset of patients, often cited as approximately one in five, report dissatisfaction following surgery, with persistent pain and functional limitations remaining common concerns [1, 2]. Patient selection and baseline clinical factors also influence outcomes, underscoring that “success” after TKA is multifactorial and not solely dependent on implant placement [3].

Multiple factors contribute to dissatisfaction after TKA, including residual pain, stiffness, and instability [1, 2]. Technical factors related to alignment targets and soft-tissue balancing remain central considerations in modern TKA, and variability in execution can plausibly influence postoperative symptoms and mechanical loading [4, 5]. Classic work has demonstrated that achieving appropriate soft-tissue balance is technically challenging even with conventional instrumentation [5]. In parallel, contemporary alignment philosophy continues to evolve (e.g., mechanical vs. functional/personalized concepts), reinforcing that accuracy and reproducibility of executing a selected plan may matter as much as the plan itself [4, 6–8].

Robotic-assisted TKA (RA-TKA) has been adopted to reduce technical variability by improving the accuracy and reproducibility of bone resection and alignment execution, with comparative studies and meta-analyses generally demonstrating improved radiographic accuracy and fewer alignment outliers relative to conventional manual TKA [7–11]. However, clinical differences, particularly patient-reported outcomes, have been less consistent across studies, and reported benefits may be context-dependent [8–11]. Large-scale observational analyses may therefore help clarify whether RA-TKA is associated with differences in short-term postoperative utilization and complications and whether any associations extend to mid-term mechanical outcomes.

Postoperative opioid prescribing patterns are frequently reported after TKA and may reflect pathway-level differences in postoperative recovery and healthcare utilization, although these measures are influenced by prescribing practices and baseline opioid exposure [12–14]. Prior work has shown that chronic opioid use and opioid use disorder are associated with higher complications and increased resource utilization after total joint arthroplasty and TKA specifically, emphasizing the complexity of interpreting opioid-related outcomes [13, 14].

Accordingly, we conducted a retrospective propensity-matched cohort study using a national real-world dataset to compare 90-day inpatient hospitalization encounters, opioid prescription fills, early complications, and 2-year mechanical outcomes between RA-TKA and conventional TKA (C-TKA). We hypothesized that RA-TKA would be associated with lower rates of inpatient hospitalization encounters and opioid prescription fills at 90 days and lower coded mechanical failure outcomes at 2 years.

Methods

Patient selection

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the TriNetX US Collaborative Network, a federated research database aggregating de-identified electronic medical records from health care organizations across the United States. Queries were conducted on May 15, 2025. Adult patients (≥ 18 years) who underwent primary total knee arthroplasty (TKA) between January 1, 2018, and January 1, 2023 were identified, with the procedure date serving as the index date. Robotic-assisted TKA (RA-TKA) was defined as CPT 27,447 performed concurrently with CPT 0054T or 0055T. Conventional TKA (C-TKA) was defined as CPT 27,447 without concurrent robotic assistance codes. Patients with bilateral TKA, revision TKA at index, or insufficient follow-up were excluded. Patients were required to have ≥ 90 days of available follow-up for 90-day outcomes and ≥ 2 years of follow-up for 2-year outcomes. This yielded 6,180 patients in the RA-TKA cohort and 157,336 patients in the C-TKA cohort.

Propensity score matching

To reduce baseline confounding, we performed 1:1 propensity score matching using a greedy nearest-neighbor algorithm without replacement and a caliper of 0.1 pooled standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score. Matching variables included age at index, sex, race, ethnicity, body mass index (BMI), nicotine dependence (F17.2), diabetes mellitus (E08–E13), chronic kidney disease (N18), and liver disease (K70–K77). We used 1:1 matching to maximize covariate balance and comparability between cohorts given substantial baseline group size differences. After matching, PSM analysis yielded 6,176 pairs of RA-TKA and C-TKA patients. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated for all matched variables (continuous and categorical), with SMD < 0.10 indicating acceptable balance [15].

Outcomes evaluated

We assessed outcomes in two timeframes. First, we evaluated 90-day postoperative complication rates, including deep vein thrombosis (I82.4), myocardial infarction (I21), pneumonia (J18), stroke (I63), sepsis (A41.89), prosthetic joint infection (T84.5), wound dehiscence (T81.3), hematoma requiring incision and drainage (CPT 27301), knee arthrotomy (CPT 27310), postoperative inpatient hospitalization encounter (CPT 99221–99223), and opioid prescription fill (VA CN101). Second, we assessed 2-year complication rates including revision TKA (CPT 27486, 27487), manipulation under anesthesia (CPT 27570), and mechanical loosening (T84.032–T84.033). Patients were included in the outcome analysis only if the event occurred after the index date and within the predefined follow-up window.

Data analyses

All analyses were performed using the built-in analytics tools of the TriNetX platform. Continuous variables were reported as means with standard deviations; categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Between-group comparisons were made using independent-sample t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. For each outcome, we computed risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and corresponding p-values. A two-tailed p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For baseline characteristics after matching, covariate balance was assessed using SMD calculations, with values < 0.10 indicating acceptable balance.

Results

Patient cohorts and baseline characteristics

A total of 6,180 patients who underwent robotic-assisted total knee arthroplasty (RA-TKA) and 157,336 patients who underwent conventional total knee arthroplasty (C-TKA) were identified (Fig. 1). Following 1:1 propensity score matching for age, sex, race, ethnicity, body mass index (BMI), and key comorbidities, 6,176 patients remained in each cohort. Baseline characteristics were well balanced after matching based on the SMD value (SMDs < 0.10 for matched covariates) (Table 1).

In the matched cohorts, mean age was 67.3 years in both groups (RA-TKA: 67.3 ± 8.7 vs. C-TKA: 67.3 ± 8.8; p = 0.874). The proportion of female patients was similar (37.5% vs. 37.6%; p = 0.926), and mean BMI was 31.8 ± 6.0 in both groups (p = 0.998). Diabetes mellitus (18.3% vs. 17.7%; p = 0.373) and nicotine dependence (8.0% vs. 7.2%; p = 0.089) were comparable. Small residual differences persisted for chronic kidney disease (7.4% vs. 6.5%; p = 0.043) and liver disease (9.2% vs. 8.0%; p = 0.010), while overall balance remained acceptable by SMD criteria (Table 1).

Ninety-day postoperative outcomes

There were no significant differences between RA-TKA and C-TKA in the incidence of common medical complications within 90 days (Table 2). The rate of deep vein thrombosis was 1.5% in both groups (risk ratio [RR], 1.02; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.77–1.36; p = 0.882). Myocardial infarction occurred in 0.5% of patients in each cohort (RR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.62–1.62; p = 1.000), and pneumonia occurred in 0.6% of both groups (RR, 0.85; 95% CI, 0.54–1.34; p = 0.484). Stroke occurred in 0.3% of the RA-TKA group versus 0.4% of the C-TKA group (RR, 0.78; 95% CI, 0.44–1.37; p = 0.386), and sepsis occurred in 0.2% of both cohorts (RR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.42–2.40; p = 1.000).

Surgical complication rates were similarly low across cohorts (Table 2). Prosthetic joint infection occurred in 0.6% of RA-TKA patients compared with 0.9% of C-TKA patients (RR, 0.72; 95% CI, 0.48–1.09; p = 0.118). Wound dehiscence was reported in 0.9% of RA-TKA patients and 0.7% of C-TKA patients (RR, 1.24; 95% CI, 0.84–1.82; p = 0.276). Hematoma requiring incision and drainage occurred in 0.2% of both groups (RR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.42–2.40; p = 1.000), and knee arthrotomy occurred in 0.2% of each cohort (RR, 0.83; 95% CI, 0.36–1.93; p = 0.670).

RA-TKA was associated with a significantly lower rate of 90-day postoperative inpatient hospitalization encounters compared with C-TKA (4.0% vs. 5.7%; RR, 0.69; 95% CI, 0.59–0.81; p < 0.001). Opioid prescription fills within 90 days were also lower in the RA-TKA cohort (78.4% vs. 85.8%; RR, 0.91; 95% CI, 0.89–0.93; p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Two-year outcomes

At 2-year follow-up, RA-TKA was associated with a lower incidence of revision surgery compared with C-TKA (1.3% vs. 2.2%; RR, 0.58; 95% CI, 0.44–0.76; p < 0.001) (Table 3). Mechanical loosening diagnoses were also less frequent in the RA-TKA cohort (0.28% vs. 0.78%; RR, 0.35; 95% CI, 0.20–0.62; p < 0.001) (Table 3). Rates of manipulation under anesthesia were similar between groups (3.26% vs. 3.22%; RR, 1.01; 95% CI, 0.83–1.23; p = 0.919), indicating no detectable difference in postoperative stiffness requiring intervention (Table 3).

Discussion

In this large, propensity score–matched national cohort of 6,176 pairs, RA-TKA was associated with lower 90-day inpatient hospitalization encounters and opioid prescription fill rates, as well as fewer coded two-year revision and mechanical loosening events, while early medical and surgical complication rates were comparable between RA-TKA and C-TKA. These findings suggest that robotic assistance may be linked to modestly improved downstream utilization and mechanical event profiles without an observable increase in short-term risk, although causal inference remains limited by the retrospective design and the constraints of coded data.

Our findings are consistent with the broader literature demonstrating that robotic platforms can improve the precision and reproducibility of TKA execution, particularly with respect to radiographic alignment accuracy and reduction of alignment outliers [8–11]. While improved alignment metrics do not uniformly translate into large short-term clinical differences, several comparative studies have suggested benefits in early recovery trajectories and discharge-related outcomes in selected settings [16]. In a quasi-randomized trial, Migliorini et al. found no length-of-stay difference and only minimal perioperative differences between robotic-assisted and freehand TKA [17]. In addition, national utilization studies have documented increasing adoption of computer-assisted navigation and robotic assistance over the last decade and have reported broadly comparable perioperative complication profiles relative to conventional techniques, supporting the safety of these platforms in contemporary practice [18, 19].

Meta-analytic evidence further supports that RA-TKA improves alignment accuracy relative to manual TKA, while early clinical outcomes and complication differences are often modest or inconsistent [8–11]. This pattern mirrors our observation that major early medical and surgical complications were similar between groups, suggesting that the principal measurable signal in real-world data may be in utilization and downstream events rather than large short-term complication reductions. Importantly, contemporary work highlights that alignment philosophy is not monolithic; functional and personalized alignment approaches continue to evolve, and the potential role of robotic systems may be to enable consistent execution of whichever alignment strategy is chosen, rather than serving as a single pathway to improved outcomes [4, 6, 7].

The association observed between RA-TKA and fewer 90-day inpatient hospitalization encounters is clinically relevant, as early postoperative utilization is a meaningful quality and cost metric in episode-based reimbursement models. Economic and resource-utilization analyses have suggested that robotic TKA may be cost-effective in high-volume settings and may reduce episode-of-care costs, particularly when post-acute utilization and readmission-related expenses are considered [20–22]. Operating-room workflow and instrument burden have also been studied as system-level drivers of efficiency and cost, and efforts to streamline instrument sets and workflows can contribute to economic value in total joint arthroplasty programs [23]. A focused systematic review of economic outcomes has similarly summarized that resource utilization and value conclusions depend heavily on volume, implementation strategy, and local cost structures [22].

The study also showed fewer opioid prescription fills in the RA-TKA cohort. Opioid prescription fills are a utilization-based surrogate and do not directly measure pain intensity, dose, duration, or actual consumption. Nonetheless, opioid exposure after TKA is an important clinical and health-systems concern. Prior work has shown that chronic opioid use and opioid use disorder are associated with higher complications and greater resource utilization after total joint arthroplasty and TKA, reinforcing that opioid-related outcomes are influenced by baseline patient factors and prescribing practices [13, 14]. Broader reviews of pain control strategies after TKA further underscore variability in opioid use and highlight alternative approaches aimed at minimizing opioid exposure [12].

At two years, RA-TKA demonstrated fewer coded revisions and mechanical loosening diagnoses. Although event rates were low and administrative coding may under-capture mechanical failure mechanisms, the observed directionality is consistent with the mechanical plausibility that improved alignment accuracy and reproducibility could reduce abnormal loading and downstream mechanical failure risk [8–11]. However, longer-term follow-up and outcome validation using implant registries, radiographic endpoints, and patient-reported outcome measures are needed to better define whether these mid-term signals persist or strengthen over time.

Limitations

This study has several limitations inherent to retrospective analyses using a federated EHR/administrative coding platform. First, all exposures and outcomes relied on diagnostic, procedural, and medication documentation, which may vary across institutions and may be subject to misclassification or under-capture, particularly for events managed nonoperatively or outside participating systems. The postoperative inpatient hospitalization endpoint was defined using CPT 99,221–99,223 and should be interpreted as a utilization proxy rather than a validated readmission measure. Opioid outcomes reflected prescription fills (VA CN101) and could not capture inpatient administrations, dose, duration, adherence, or opioid-naïve versus chronic use.

Second, although propensity score matching improved comparability, residual confounding remains likely due to unmeasured clinical and procedural factors not available as standardized structured variables across the network. These include surgeon and hospital volume/experience, learning-curve effects, anesthesia type, discharge pathways and rehabilitation protocols, preoperative functional status/activity and patient-reported outcomes, radiographic alignment and kinematics, severity of deformity, osteoarthritis subtype, inflammatory arthropathy, osteoporosis, implant design/constraint and fixation method, patellar resurfacing, and robot platform type/alignment philosophy. Finally, mechanical outcomes were based on administrative coding (including loosening diagnoses) and follow-up was limited to 2 years; therefore, implant “durability” or long-term survivorship cannot be inferred.

Conclusion

In this large propensity-matched national cohort, RA-TKA was associated with fewer 90-day postoperative inpatient hospitalization encounters and fewer opioid prescription fills, as well as lower coded revision and loosening outcomes at 2 years, while early complication rates were similar between groups. These findings should be interpreted as associations given reliance on administrative coding and potential unmeasured confounding.
