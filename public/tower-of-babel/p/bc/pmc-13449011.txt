Cardiovascular Efficacy of GLP‐1 Receptor Agonists by Kidney Function: An Updated Meta‐Analysis of Randomized Trials Including the SOUL Trial
Authors: Masashi Hasebe, Chen‐Yang Su, Daisuke Yabe, Satoshi Yoshiji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTo evaluate the cardiovascular efficacy and absolute benefit of glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1RAs) by baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR).Materials and MethodsPubMed and EMBASE were searched to 29 April 2026 for randomized placebo‐controlled trials of GLP‐1RAs in adults with type 2 diabetes or overweight/obesity that reported eGFR‐stratified major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE). Hazard ratios (HRs) were extracted for eGFR < 60 and ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2. Random‐effects meta‐analyses estimated pooled HRs within each eGFR stratum and the pooled ratio of HRs (RHR) comparing eGFR < 60 versus ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2. Exploratory absolute risk reduction (ARR) and number needed to treat (NNT) were derived from placebo‐group MACE risk and the corresponding pooled HR.ResultsNine publications from eight trials were included, comprising 70 822 participants; 70 534 had eGFR‐stratified MACE data. GLP‐1RAs similarly reduced MACE risk among participants with eGFR ≥ 60 and < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, with pooled HRs of 0.83 (95% CI 0.77–0.90; p < 0.001; I 2 = 35.6%) and 0.83 (95% CI 0.74–0.93; p < 0.001; I 2 = 40.9%), respectively. The pooled RHR showed no evidence of effect modification (1.02, 95% CI 0.85–1.21; p = 0.84). ARR was larger with eGFR < 60 than ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 (2.6% vs. 1.6%), corresponding to NNTs of 39 (95% CI 26–91) versus 62 (95% CI 46–101).ConclusionsGLP‐1RAs reduced MACE risk similarly across eGFR strata, while lower eGFR was associated with a larger estimated absolute cardiovascular benefit. These findings support GLP‐1RA therapy in individuals with reduced kidney function.

Introduction

Glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1RAs) have become an important therapeutic option for reducing cardiometabolic risk [1]. In a series of randomized controlled trials (RCTs), GLP‐1RAs have reduced major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) among individuals with type 2 diabetes [2, 3], with cardiovascular benefits also shown in individuals with overweight or obesity and established cardiovascular disease but without diabetes [4]. In addition, some GLP‐1RAs have shown favorable effects on heart failure and kidney outcomes [5, 6]. These data have informed guideline recommendations for GLP‐1RA use in individuals with type 2 diabetes who have established cardiovascular disease, high cardiovascular risk or chronic kidney disease (CKD) [7, 8].

Kidney function is an important marker of cardiovascular risk. Reduced kidney function, commonly assessed using estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), is associated with higher risks of cardiovascular mortality and events, independent of conventional cardiovascular risk factors [9, 10]. The recently introduced American Heart Association PREVENT equations also include eGFR among their predictors, underscoring the role of kidney measures in contemporary cardiovascular risk assessment [11]. However, individuals with CKD have been underrepresented in RCTs evaluating cardiovascular outcomes, and treatment effects according to kidney function are often incompletely characterized [12]. This limitation is clinically important because reduced kidney function is associated not only with higher cardiovascular risk, but also with CKD‐related pathophysiological changes, such as vascular calcification and chronic inflammation, that may influence cardiovascular disease presentation and treatment response [13].

Several RCTs of GLP‐1RAs have reported cardiovascular outcomes according to baseline kidney function, but the number of participants with reduced eGFR, conventionally defined as eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, was limited in many trials and the kidney‐function categories used for subgroup analyses varied across studies. Previous meta‐analyses have generally shown lower MACE risk with GLP‐1RAs than with placebo among participants with reduced eGFR, either by focusing on CKD populations or by pooling eGFR‐stratified estimates [14, 15, 16, 17]. However, these studies summarized GLP‐1RA effects within kidney‐function subgroups and did not formally assess whether baseline kidney function modifies the relative cardiovascular treatment effect. Therefore, whether GLP‐1RA cardiovascular benefit is comparable across kidney‐function categories remains uncertain.

To address this gap, we performed an updated meta‐analysis of placebo‐controlled randomized trials, including the recently published SOUL trial [18], examining GLP‐1RA effects on MACE according to baseline kidney function. We first summarized treatment effects within eGFR strata defined by the clinically relevant cutoff of 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 [9, 10] and then compared these effects within trials using ratios of hazard ratios (RHRs), allowing formal assessment of whether baseline kidney function modified the relative cardiovascular effect of GLP‐1RAs. We also estimated exploratory absolute risk reductions (ARRs) and numbers needed to treat (NNTs) to contextualize potential differences in absolute cardiovascular benefit across kidney‐function categories.

Methods

Search Strategies and Selection Criteria

This study was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [19] and was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261381014). We considered RCTs eligible if they: (i) enrolled at least 1000 adults aged 18 years or older; (ii) evaluated GLP‐1RAs, administered either by subcutaneous injection or as an oral formulation, against placebo; (iii) included participants with type 2 diabetes or overweight/obesity; (iv) reported MACE during a follow‐up period of at least 1 year and (v) provided MACE effect estimates stratified by baseline kidney function in categories that allowed comparison between eGFR < 60 and ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2. Trials that did not report MACE outcomes stratified by baseline eGFR in a manner compatible with this threshold were not included. We excluded trials evaluating device‐based continuous‐infusion systems or fixed‐combination products. MACE was defined according to each trial protocol, most commonly as a composite of cardiovascular death, non‐fatal myocardial infarction, or non‐fatal stroke.

PubMed and EMBASE were searched from database inception through 29 April 2026, without language restrictions. The complete search strategies for each database are shown in Figure S1. Two investigators (M.H. and C.‐Y.S.) independently screened records, assessed trial eligibility and selected studies for inclusion. Any disagreements were resolved by discussion until consensus was reached.

Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

We extracted hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for MACE according to baseline eGFR categories from the primary trial reports, their accompanying supplementary materials, or secondary publications. When trials reported multiple eGFR categories within either the ≥ 60 or < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 stratum, we derived a single stratum‐specific estimate by pooling the reported log HRs using a fixed‐effect generic inverse‐variance model [20]. Standard errors were calculated from the corresponding 95% CIs. Event counts and denominators were extracted for each reported eGFR category and summed when categories were combined.

Data extraction and risk‐of‐bias assessment were performed independently by two investigators (M.H. and C.‐Y.S.). Risk of bias was evaluated using Version 2 of the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool [21]. Discrepancies in extracted data or bias assessments were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached.

Data Integration and Statistical Analysis

Using the trial‐level stratum‐specific estimates described above, we performed separate meta‐analyses of HRs for MACE in participants with eGFR ≥ 60 or < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 [22]. To formally assess whether the relative treatment effect differed according to baseline kidney function, we calculated trial‐specific RHRs, defined as the HR in the eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 stratum divided by the HR in the eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 stratum. This within‐trial contrast standardizes the comparison of treatment effects between kidney function strata across trials [23, 24]. An RHR less than 1.0 indicates a relatively greater reduction in MACE in the eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 stratum, whereas an RHR greater than 1.0 indicates a relatively greater reduction in the eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 stratum.

For both HR and RHR analyses, effect estimates were analysed on the logarithmic scale and pooled using random‐effects inverse‐variance meta‐analysis with the DerSimonian–Laird estimator [25]. Trial‐specific RHRs were calculated on the logarithmic scale as the difference between the log HRs for the eGFR < 60 and ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 strata, with standard errors derived from the corresponding 95% CIs [24, 26, 27]. Between‐trial heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran's Q statistic and Higgins's I 2 statistic, with I 2 values interpreted as low, moderate or high heterogeneity using conventional thresholds of ≤ 25%, 26%–50% and > 50%, respectively [28].

To examine the robustness of the findings, we conducted two sensitivity analyses for the pooled HR in the eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 stratum, the pooled HR in the eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 stratum and the pooled RHR. First, we repeated the random‐effects analyses using the Hartung–Knapp method to provide a more conservative approach to inference when between‐trial heterogeneity is estimated with uncertainty [29, 30]. Second, we performed leave‐one‐out analyses by iteratively excluding one trial at a time and recalculating each pooled result to assess whether the findings were driven by any single trial.

To contextualize potential differences in absolute cardiovascular benefit, we conducted an exploratory analysis of ARR and NNT within each eGFR stratum. ARR was estimated using an established approach for deriving absolute effects from baseline risk and relative treatment effects [31, 32, 33]. Specifically, ARR was calculated as the observed placebo event risk within each eGFR stratum multiplied by (1 − pooled HR). The 95% CI for ARR was derived by applying the corresponding 95% CI bounds of the pooled HR to the same placebo event risk. NNT was calculated as the reciprocal of ARR expressed as a proportion, with its 95% CI derived from the corresponding ARR limits. This analysis was considered exploratory because ARR and NNT estimates depend on follow‐up duration and underlying event risk, which vary across trials.

Two‐sided p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant, including Cochran's Q test for heterogeneity. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), primarily with the meta (v 8.0‐1) and metafor (v 4.8‐0) packages.

Results

Study Identification and Trial Characteristics

Study screening and selection are shown in the PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1). After removal of duplicates, 1416 records were screened and 12 articles were assessed in full text. Three articles reporting ELIXA, REWIND and AMPLITUDE‐O [34, 35, 36] were excluded because they did not provide MACE outcomes stratified by baseline eGFR in categories compatible with the prespecified comparison of eGFR < 60 versus ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 (Table S1).

Nine publications reporting data from eight randomized placebo‐controlled trials of GLP‐1RAs, enrolling 70 822 participants, were included: LEADER (liraglutide), SUSTAIN‐6 (subcutaneous semaglutide), EXSCEL (once‐weekly exenatide), Harmony Outcomes (albiglutide), PIONEER 6 (oral semaglutide), SELECT (subcutaneous semaglutide), FLOW (subcutaneous semaglutide) and SOUL (oral semaglutide) [4, 18, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 42]. For FLOW, eGFR‐stratified MACE data were extracted from a secondary publication [43]. Of these, seven were cardiovascular outcome trials, whereas FLOW was designed primarily as a kidney outcome trial in participants with CKD. SELECT enrolled participants with overweight or obesity and established cardiovascular disease but without diabetes; all other included trials enrolled participants with type 2 diabetes. Five of the eight trials evaluated semaglutide, accounting for 37 267 of 70 822 participants (52.6%). Among trials for which baseline albuminuria categories were reported, including in secondary publications [44, 45, 46], most participants in LEADER, SUSTAIN‐6 and SELECT had normoalbuminuria, whereas FLOW predominantly enrolled participants with macroalbuminuria. Reported follow‐up ranged from 1.3 to 4.1 years across trials. Additional trial characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Meta‐Analysis of MACE by Baseline eGFR

Of the 70 822 participants enrolled in the eight included trials, eGFR‐stratified MACE data were available for 70 534 participants (99.6%). This included 53 641 participants with baseline eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 and 16 893 participants with baseline eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2.

In the placebo group, MACE occurred in 2569 of 26 855 participants (9.6%) in the eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 stratum and in 1292 of 8443 participants (15.3%) in the eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 stratum. In the GLP‐1RA group, the corresponding numbers were 2179 of 26 786 (8.1%) and 1117 of 8450 (13.2%), respectively.

In the eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 stratum, GLP‐1RA therapy reduced MACE risk, with a pooled HR of 0.83 (95% CI 0.77–0.90; p < 0.001; I 2 = 35.6%) (Figure 2). In the eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 stratum, GLP‐1RA therapy similarly reduced MACE risk, with a pooled HR of 0.83 (95% CI 0.74–0.93; p < 0.001; I 2 = 40.9%).

Comparison of GLP‐1RA Effects by Baseline eGFR

The pooled RHR comparing participants with eGFR < 60 versus ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 was 1.02 (95% CI 0.85–1.21; p = 0.84), indicating no clear evidence of treatment‐effect modification by baseline kidney function (Figure 3). Trial‐specific RHRs nevertheless varied in direction, ranging from 0.74 in LEADER to 1.47 in FLOW, with high between‐trial heterogeneity (I 2 = 58.2%; p for heterogeneity = 0.019).

Sensitivity Analysis

Hartung–Knapp analyses yielded slightly wider 95% CIs than the primary DerSimonian–Laird analyses, but the pooled HRs for MACE remained statistically significant in both eGFR strata: 0.83 (95% CI 0.75–0.91; p = 0.009) for eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 and 0.83 (95% CI 0.73–0.94; p = 0.003) for eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2. Results for the RHR were also consistent with the primary analysis, with no evidence of effect modification by baseline eGFR (RHR 1.02; 95% CI 0.83–1.25; p = 0.84) (Table S5).

Leave‐one‐out analyses showed similar results, including after omission of SELECT, the only trial enrolling participants without diabetes. Across these analyses, the pooled HRs remained statistically significant, ranging from 0.81 to 0.85 in the eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 stratum and from 0.79 to 0.87 in the eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 stratum (Table 2). The pooled RHR remained close to 1.0 and was not statistically significant in any leave‐one‐out analysis, ranging from 0.98 to 1.08. Heterogeneity in the RHR analysis was attenuated after omission of LEADER but otherwise remained moderate to high, without changing the overall interpretation.

Exploratory Analysis of ARR and NNT

In this exploratory analysis, ARR was estimated by applying the pooled HR to the observed MACE risk in the placebo group within each eGFR stratum. In the eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 stratum, MACE occurred in 9.6% of placebo‐group participants; applying the corresponding pooled HR of 0.83 yielded an ARR of 1.6 percentage points (95% CI 1.0–2.2) and an NNT of 62 (95% CI 46–101). In the eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 stratum, MACE occurred in 15.3% of placebo‐group participants; applying the corresponding pooled HR of 0.83 yielded an ARR of 2.6 percentage points (95% CI 1.1–3.9) and an NNT of 39 (95% CI 26–91). Thus, the similar relative MACE reduction translated into a larger estimated absolute benefit among participants with lower baseline eGFR, reflecting their higher underlying event risk.

Discussion

In this updated eGFR‐stratified meta‐analysis of eight randomized placebo‐controlled GLP‐1RA trials, we found that: (i) GLP‐1RA therapy reduced MACE risk by approximately 17% in both participants with eGFR ≥ 60 and < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2; (ii) formal trial‐level assessment using treatment‐effect RHRs showed no evidence that the relative effect of GLP‐1RA therapy on MACE differed between eGFR strata, although between‐trial heterogeneity was high and (iii) because the observed MACE risk among participants in the placebo group was higher in the eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 stratum, similar relative effects translated into a larger estimated ARR and a lower NNT. Together, these findings suggest that GLP‐1RAs provide consistent relative cardiovascular protection across baseline kidney function strata and may yield greater absolute benefit among individuals with reduced kidney function because of their higher underlying cardiovascular risk.

The current study extends previous literature in three ways. First, although prior meta‐analyses have examined the cardiovascular efficacy of GLP‐1RAs across kidney function subgroups, they have not consistently assessed treatment‐effect modification using formal trial‐level contrasts, nor have they uniformly focused on the clinically established eGFR threshold of 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 [14, 15, 16, 17]. By harmonizing available subgroup data around this threshold and meta‐analysing treatment‐effect RHRs, our study provides a direct comparison of relative cardiovascular efficacy between participants with and without reduced kidney function. Second, we incorporated the recently reported SOUL trial [18], thereby updating the evidence beyond earlier GLP‐1RA cardiovascular outcome meta‐analyses. Third, we examined estimated ARR and NNT to aid clinical interpretation of the observed relative treatment effects. This exploratory analysis suggested that participants with eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 may derive greater absolute cardiovascular benefit because of their higher underlying MACE risk, despite similar relative efficacy across eGFR strata.

Reduced kidney function is associated with distinct cardiovascular phenotypes, including vascular calcification and chronic inflammation, and with a higher burden of competing clinical events such as heart failure, kidney failure and non‐cardiovascular death [13]. These features may influence treatment response and complicate analyses of atherosclerotic MACE because non‐fatal atherosclerotic events may be less frequently observed when competing clinical events occur earlier or dominate the subsequent disease course [13]. In this context, kidney function has been shown to modify the effects of some cardiovascular preventive therapies. For instance, in statin‐based lipid‐lowering trials, proportional reductions in vascular events were smaller at lower eGFR levels [47]. In the present analysis, however, the trial‐level RHR showed no clear evidence that reduced kidney function, defined as eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, attenuated the relative MACE benefit of GLP‐1RAs (Figure 3), consistent with the similar pooled HRs across eGFR strata (Figure 2). Nevertheless, the high heterogeneity in trial‐level RHRs indicates that the pooled result should be interpreted as an average trial‐level contrast and does not exclude more specific kidney‐related effect modification, particularly according to CKD severity or albuminuria status.

The absence of relative treatment‐effect modification does not imply similar absolute benefit. Absolute benefit depends on both the proportional treatment effect and the baseline risk of the population being treated; therefore, similar relative effects can yield larger ARRs in higher‐risk individuals [31]. This principle has been demonstrated in large analyses of blood‐pressure‐lowering and lipid‐lowering therapies, in which broadly similar proportional risk reductions translated into greater absolute benefits among individuals at higher baseline cardiovascular risk [48, 49]. Consistent with observational evidence linking lower eGFR to higher cardiovascular risk [50], participants with eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 in the present study had a higher observed risk of MACE in the placebo group. Thus, with a similar proportional reduction in MACE across eGFR strata, this higher baseline risk translated into a larger estimated ARR and lower NNT in this group. From a clinical perspective, this distinction suggests that GLP‐1RA therapy warrants particular consideration in individuals with reduced kidney function, who may derive greater absolute cardiovascular benefit.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the present study was based on published aggregate data rather than individual participant data. Therefore, patient‐level sources of heterogeneity in the RHR analysis could not be investigated. Second, our analysis was based on the prespecified comparison of eGFR < 60 versus ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2. More granular analyses using lower eGFR thresholds, such as eGFR < 45 or < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2, were constrained by the small number of trials reporting compatible MACE subgroup results. In addition, we could not account for albuminuria, which provides prognostic information independent of eGFR and may identify individuals with distinct cardiorenal risk profiles [9], because MACE results stratified by albuminuria were not consistently reported. Third, the analyses of ARR and NNT should be interpreted as exploratory. Although translating relative treatment effects into absolute terms can aid clinical interpretation, NNTs derived from meta‐analyses require caution because follow‐up duration and the risk of MACE in the placebo group differ across trials [51]. Therefore, ARR and NNT in the present study should be regarded as approximate measures of potential absolute benefit, rather than precise absolute effects applicable to a specific trial population. Fourth, five of the eight included trials evaluated semaglutide, accounting for 37 267 of 70 822 participants (52.6%) and approximately half of the random‐effects weight across the main HR and RHR meta‐analyses (47.1%–52.9%). This large contribution of semaglutide trials should be considered when generalizing the findings to the broader GLP‐1RA class. In addition, a formal subgroup analysis by route of administration was not feasible because data for oral GLP‐1RAs were available only from two trials of oral semaglutide. Fifth, because placebo‐controlled, eGFR‐stratified cardiovascular outcome data remain limited for other incretin‐based therapies, this meta‐analysis was restricted to placebo‐controlled RCTs of GLP‐1RAs. This restriction is important given recent tirzepatide studies showing noninferiority to dulaglutide for MACE and suggesting benefit versus an imputed placebo in an indirect comparison [52, 53]. Further data will be needed to determine whether the relative cardiovascular effects of other incretin‐based therapies are similarly consistent across kidney‐function strata.

In summary, this eGFR‐stratified meta‐analysis of randomized placebo‐controlled GLP‐1RA trials showed that GLP‐1RA therapy reduced MACE risk to a similar relative extent among participants with baseline eGFR ≥ 60 and < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, with no clear evidence of relative treatment‐effect modification by kidney function. Because participants with lower eGFR had a higher absolute risk of MACE, similar relative effects translated into a larger estimated absolute cardiovascular benefit in this group. These findings support the cardiovascular efficacy of GLP‐1RAs across kidney‐function categories and suggest that GLP‐1RA therapy may merit particular consideration for individuals with reduced kidney function, given their potential for greater absolute cardiovascular benefit.
