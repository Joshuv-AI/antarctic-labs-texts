Understanding public confidence in locally-produced mRNA vaccines: Insights for future pandemic preparedness
Authors: Li Ping Wong, Haridah Alias, Hai Yen Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAs local vaccine manufacturing becomes a strategic goal for future pandemic preparedness, investigating public trust in domestic vaccine production is essential for ensuring equitable uptake and long-term success. A cross-sectional web-based survey was conducted to assess confidence in the local scientific community, manufacturers, regulatory authorities, and locally-produced mRNA vaccines. A vaccine confidence index (VCI) captured attitudes toward mRNA vaccine safety, efficacy, and acceptability. In multivariable logistic regression analysis, confidence in the competency of the local scientific community (aOR = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.14–2.10, p < .01), local vaccine manufacturers’ ability to produce high-quality vaccines (aOR = 1.95, 95% CI: 1.46–2.59, p < .001), and trust in the quality of locally-produced vaccine products (aOR = 2.39, 95% CI: 1.79–3.20, p < .001) were significantly associated with greater confidence. Confidence in regulatory authorities also played a key role; participants who believed regulators were competent (aOR = 1.95, 95% CI: 1.44–2.63, p < .001) and trustworthy (aOR = 1.72, 95% CI: 1.22–2.43, p < .01) were more likely to express confidence. Under VCI, perceiving mRNA vaccines as important for children (aOR = 1.63, 95% CI: 1.09–2.43, p < .05), safe (AOR = 2.95, 95% CI: 1.84–4.72, p < .001), effective (aOR = 2.00, 95% CI: 1.26–3.13, p < .01), and aligned with personal beliefs (aOR = 2.31, 95% CI: 1.43–3.75, p < .01) significantly increased confidence. Ethnicity was the strongest demographic predictor, with Malays showing significantly higher confidence (aOR = 1.88, 95% CI: 1.15–3.07) than other groups. Findings underscore the need for transparent, evidence-based, and culturally tailored strategies to build trust and advance Malaysia’s vaccine self-reliance goals.KEYWORDS: vaccine confidence, mRNA vaccines, local production, Malaysia, trust determinants, pandemic preparedness

Background

The development and deployment of mRNA vaccines during the COVID-19 pandemic marked a pivotal moment in global public health. This advancement revolutionized both vaccine technology and delivery. While much attention has been paid to public acceptance of mRNA vaccines developed by international pharmaceutical giants, there remains limited understanding of public confidence in locally-produced mRNA vaccines, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. In Malaysia, the government and scientific community have actively explored local vaccine manufacturing capacity as part of long-term pandemic preparedness. Malaysia has a strong will to achieve indigenous vaccine production by 2030, as outlined in the National Vaccine Development Roadmap.1 However, most important is public confidence in locally-produced vaccines, which determines whether these efforts will translate into successful uptake and community protection. Building such confidence requires a clear understanding of public perceptions toward the safety, effectiveness, and credibility of locally-produced mRNA vaccines.

Vaccine confidence has emerged as a critical determinant of vaccine uptake, especially during the COVID-19 pandemic. According to the World Health Organization, vaccine confidence is defined as the trust or belief that vaccines are effective, safe, and serve the best health interests of individuals and the public, as well as trust in the healthcare providers and systems that deliver them.2 Several global studies have emphasized the role of transparency, scientific credibility, and trust in institutions in shaping vaccine attitudes. For example, large international survey across multiple countries demonstrated that perceptions of vaccine safety, efficacy, and scientific validity significantly shape vaccine confidence and acceptance, with trust in health authorities and government institutions being key determinant3,4 Moreover, research highlights that transparent communication about vaccine development and clear endorsements from credible institutions significantly increase public willingness to vaccinate, while mistrust fueled by misinformation and lack of transparency undermines vaccine acceptance.5 Additionally, building and maintaining trust in scientific and governmental institutions is essential for improving vaccine uptake worldwide.6

In high-income countries such as the United States, there tends to be greater public trust in vaccines manufactured domestically. This confidence is often attributed to strong regulatory oversight, well-established pharmaceutical industries, and robust quality assurance systems. In contrast, vaccines produced in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) may face greater skepticism among local populations due to concerns over manufacturing standards, regulatory credibility, and past experiences with pharmaceutical products.7 A recent study by Leblang, Smith, and Wesselbaum (2024) found that while institutional trust significantly increased vaccination rates and rollout speed in LMICs, this relationship was not statistically significant in high-income countries.8 Notably, this highlights the critical role trust plays in countries where people may have less confidence in institutions.

Beyond vaccines, evidence from consumer research shows that perceptions of “local versus foreign” products shape trust and acceptance. Locally produced goods are often associated with cultural familiarity and national pride, yet foreign products are frequently perceived as safer or higher quality. For example, while studies highlight the value of local foods for quality and economic support, foreign imports are often chosen when reliability is in question. In China, this pattern is clear: imported infant milk formula has long been preferred, especially after food safety scandals, due to greater perceived safety.9 Additionally, Carfora et al. observed that trust in local producers, sustainability, and transparency of product origin are key psychosocial factors driving the choice of local products.10 Drawing on these findings helps explain why trust in domestic scientific institutions, manufacturers, and regulators is a crucial aspect of this study, as similar mechanisms likely influence attitudes toward locally-produced vaccines.

Despite the global significance of these insights, empirical research specifically examining public trust in locally-produced mRNA vaccines in LMICs remains limited, particularly in Southeast Asia. In the Malaysian context, this gap poses a key barrier to understanding and enhancing future pandemic preparedness. Although Malaysia has made strides in strengthening its biomedical and pharmaceutical sectors, public acceptance of locally-produced vaccines, especially those using novel technologies such as mRNA, remains uncertain and is shaped by a variety of interrelated factors. This skepticism may be further influenced by Malaysia’s prior reliance on internationally manufactured vaccines like Pfizer-BioNTech, which may have set a perceived benchmark for quality. Additionally, past shortcomings in the country’s biotechnology initiatives11 may have shaped public perception of government-led vaccine efforts. This could affect how they view and accept mRNA vaccines made locally in the future. As a result, comparing foreign and local vaccines may influence public attitudes and lead to reluctance in accepting domestically produced mRNA vaccines.

Public confidence in locally-produced vaccines is also shaped by trust in multiple interconnected actors within the national vaccine ecosystem. These include the scientific community responsible for research and development, pharmaceutical manufacturers tasked with production, and regulatory authorities overseeing safety and quality assurance. Understanding how the public perceives the credibility, intentions, and capabilities of these stakeholders is essential for identifying barriers to vaccine acceptance and for designing strategies to strengthen public trust in local vaccine initiatives. Confidence in the local scientific community reflects public perceptions of scientists’ competency, transparency, and their perceived motivation to serve the public good.12,13 High levels of trust in scientists have been linked to greater support for health interventions and acceptance of novel technologies.14–16 However, maintaining this trust requires ongoing effort, especially in contexts where scientific communication is lacking or where institutions are viewed as politically biased17 Confidence in local pharmaceutical manufacturers is often driven by perceived product quality, ethical commitment, and track record. When transparency or public visibility is lacking, skepticism can emerge even when the scientific integrity of the product remains uncompromised. Research has shown that trust in local vaccine manufacturers can be undermined by doubts about technical capabilities or concerns about profit-driven motives.18 Confidence in local vaccine regulatory authorities serves as a critical check on public acceptance. Regulatory bodies are responsible for safeguarding public health through the approval of safe and effective vaccines. Trust in these institutions is linked to perceptions of competence, transparency, and independence from political or commercial pressures.19,20 During the COVID-19 pandemic, public trust was significantly undermined by concerns that the FDA’s Emergency Use Authorizations (EUAs) for hydroxychloroquine and convalescent plasma were driven more by political pressure than by scientific evidence, ultimately weakening confidence in the vaccine approval process.21

Confidence in mRNA vaccine technology is also another critical dimension influencing public acceptance of locally-produced vaccines. While mRNA vaccines represent a major scientific breakthrough, their novelty can pose significant communication challenges. Public understanding and acceptance of this technology vary widely and are often influenced by perceptions of safety, effectiveness, necessity, and compatibility with personal beliefs. To capture these key dimensions, the Vaccine Confidence Index (VCI) assesses attitudes across four domains: perceived importance, safety, efficacy, and belief alignment.22 Examining how these perceptions intersect with trust in local scientific institutions, manufacturers, and regulatory bodies is essential for gauging public readiness to embrace domestically developed mRNA vaccines.

Additionally, socio-cultural factors also influence vaccine attitudes, especially in a diverse and multi-religious country like Malaysia. Religious beliefs, especially concerns related to halal certification and the endorsement of vaccines by religious authorities, play a substantial role in shaping public confidence. In light of these dynamics, it becomes increasingly urgent to examine how Malaysians perceive locally-produced mRNA vaccines and what drives or inhibits their confidence. As Malaysia moves toward building vaccine sovereignty by 2030, understanding the socio-psychological, cultural, and institutional factors that shape vaccine confidence is essential for designing effective communication, outreach, and policy strategies that will support local vaccine acceptance and broader public health goals.

Empirical research on public trust in locally-produced mRNA vaccines remain limited, particularly in Southeast Asia and the Malaysian context. This gap poses a significant barrier to strengthening future pandemic preparedness, as the success of local vaccine initiatives depends heavily on public acceptance – rooted in trust and perceived credibility. While Malaysia has made notable progress in developing its biomedical and pharmaceutical sectors, limited evidence exists on how trust in local scientific institutions, manufacturers, and regulatory frameworks influences confidence in domestically produced mRNA vaccines. Existing literature tends to focus on internationally manufactured vaccines or general vaccine hesitancy, leaving a critical void in understanding attitudes toward local vaccine development.

To address this gap, the present study assesses public confidence in locally-produced mRNA vaccines in Malaysia and identifies key influencing factors. It specifically examines trust in the local scientific community, pharmaceutical manufacturers, and regulatory authorities, along with general attitudes regarding the safety, efficacy, importance, and belief compatibility of mRNA vaccines. Aligned with Malaysia’s roadmap to enhance national vaccine development capacity, the findings aim to guide evidence-based strategies for promoting vaccine acceptance and advancing pandemic resilience in Malaysia and comparable LMIC settings.

Methods

Study participants and survey design

A cross-sectional, web-based anonymous survey was conducted using an online questionnaire. The survey link was disseminated and promoted to the public through social media platforms, including Facebook, Instagram, and WhatsApp. The recruitment was based on general postings and voluntary sharing within personal networks; no targeted advertisements or group-specific recruitment strategies were used. Individuals eligible to participate were Malaysian residents aged 18 years and older. Upon completing the survey, participants were encouraged to share the link with their personal networks. Participation was entirely voluntary, and informed consent was implied through the act of completing the questionnaire. The survey was initially developed in English and subsequently translated into Bahasa Malaysia, the national language. All questions were presented bilingually in both English and Bahasa Malaysia. Prior to deployment, the questionnaire underwent content validation by local experts and was pilot tested with 20 individuals to ensure clarity and reliability. No modifications were made following the pilot test, as no issues were identified.

Instruments

The survey consisted of questions (Appendix 1) that assessed 1) demographic backgrounds; 2) confidence toward local scientific community 3) confidence toward local vaccine manufacturers/pharmaceutical companies 4) confidence toward local vaccine regulatory authorities, 5) vaccine confidence index (VCI), 6) reasons influencing confidence in locally-produced vaccines and their manufacturers and 7) confidence toward locally-produced mRNA vaccines

Demographics

Participants provided socio-demographic information, including age group (18–29, 30–39, or 40 years and above), gender (male or female), and ethnicity (Malay, Chinese, Indian, Bumiputera/Indigenous, or others). Marital status was recorded as never married or ever married, with the latter category encompassing those who were currently married, divorced, or widowed. Educational level was classified into two groups: secondary and below, or tertiary. Average monthly household income was reported in four categories: MYR 1,000 and below, MYR 1,001–5,000, MYR 5,001– 10,000, and more than MYR 10,000. Additionally, participants indicated their area of residence (urban, sub-urban, or rural) and their geographic region, categorized as Northern, Central, Southern, East Coast, or Borneo Island regions that form part of Malaysia.

Confidence toward local scientific community

To assess participants’ confidence in the local scientific community, three items were included in the survey. Respondents were asked to indicate their level of agreement with the following statements: (1) “I am confident in the competency of the scientific community in this country” (2), “I have confidence that scientists working in local universities or research institutes carry out their work with the intention of benefiting the public,” and (3) “I have confidence that scientists in local universities and research institutes are open and honest about their findings.” Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from Strongly agree to Strongly disagree. Responses were scored from 5 (Strongly agree) to 1 (Strongly disagree), with higher scores reflecting greater confidence in the scientific community.

Confidence toward local vaccine manufacturers/pharmaceutical companies

Confidence in local vaccine manufacturers and pharmaceutical companies was assessed using three items that evaluated public perceptions of their competency, intentions, and product quality. Participants were asked to rate their agreement with the following statements: (1) “Local vaccine manufacturers have sufficient competency in producing effective, safe, and high-quality products” (2), “The intentions of local pharmaceutical companies are oriented at safeguarding public health,” and (3) “The vaccine products produced by local pharmaceutical companies are effective, safe, and high-quality.” Each item was measured on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from Strongly agree to Strongly disagree. Responses were scored from 5 (Strongly agree) to 1 (Strongly disagree), with higher scores indicating greater confidence in local vaccine and pharmaceutical producers.

Confidence toward local vaccine regulatory authorities

To assess confidence in local vaccine regulatory authorities, participants were presented with a set of four statements designed to capture perceptions of competency, trustworthiness, and regulatory practices. Respondents were asked to indicate their level of agreement with each statement on a 5-point Likert scale (Strongly agree, Agree, Neutral, Disagree, Strongly disagree). The statements included: (1) “If vaccines are developed and manufactured locally, the local regulatory authorities have sufficient competency to offer an assurance of safety and efficacy of the vaccine to the public”; (2) “The local vaccine regulatory authorities are oriented at safeguarding public health” (3); “The local vaccine regulatory authorities are trustworthy to offer an assurance of safety and efficacy of the vaccine to the public”; and (4) “In general, regulatory authorities for vaccines place significant reliance on the reputation of vaccine manufacturers when making their approval decisions. For instance, if the manufacturer is well-known and reputable, they are more likely to receive approval.” These items were developed based on literature and expert input to evaluate public trust and perceived integrity of local regulatory institutions in the context of vaccine approval and safety assurance.

Vaccine confidence index (VCI)

To evaluate participants’ confidence in mRNA vaccines, a Vaccine Confidence Index (VCI) was developed consisting of four items.22 The index was adapted to specifically focus on mRNA-based vaccines. Respondents were asked to indicate their level of agreement using a 5-point scale: Strongly agree, Tend to agree, Tend to disagree, Strongly disagree, and Don’t know. The items assessed various dimensions of vaccine confidence and included the following statements: (1) “mRNA vaccines are important for children to have”; (2) “mRNA vaccines are safe” (3); “mRNA vaccines are effective”; and (4) “mRNA vaccines are compatible with my beliefs.” These items aimed to capture attitudes toward the necessity, safety, efficacy, and personal acceptability of mRNA vaccines.

Structured questionnaires were used to assess the most common reasons influencing: (1) confidence in locally-produced vaccines and (2) confidence in their manufacturers. For vaccine confidence, participants responded to a single-choice question with seven predefined options: clinical trial findings, government approval, positive testimonials, transparent communication from health authorities, technology details, international verification, and educational campaigns. For manufacturer confidence, another single-choice question presented four options: partnerships with reputable scientists/institutions, local track record in drug development, market reputation for community engagement, and collaborations with international biotech companies. In both cases, participants were instructed to select only one factor per question.

Confidence toward locally-produced mRNA vaccine

To assess the confidence toward locally-produced mRNA vaccine, single question was asked “How would you rate your level of confident or trusting in locally-produced mRNA vaccines?” Respondents were asked to indicate their level of confidence using a 5-point scale: Strongly confident, Confident, Neutral, Not confident, Strongly not confident. Higher rating represents higher level of confidence.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were also conducted using SPSS version 22.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the sociodemographic characteristics of the participants and their responses to items measuring confidence in locally-produced mRNA vaccines. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

The responses to the two structured questions, which assessed (1) factors influencing confidence in locally-produced vaccines and (2) factors affecting confidence in their manufacturers, were analyzed using descriptive statistics to determine frequency distributions. To examine factors associated with confidence toward locally-produced mRNA vaccines, responses were dichotomized into two categories: “Strongly confident/Confident” and “Neutral/Not confident/Strongly not confident.” Univariable analysis was first performed using Pearson’s chi-square test to assess associations between independent variables and confidence levels. Variables with a p-value < .05 in the univariable analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression model.

Multivariable logistic regression was then conducted to identify independent predictors of higher confidence levels. Each confidence item including VCI was analyzed independently as a single construct; no composite score was created. Adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. A two-sided p-value of <.05 was considered statistically significant. The goodness-of-fit of the final logistic regression model was evaluated using the Hosmer – Lemeshow test, and model explanatory power was assessed using Nagelkerke’s R2.23

Ethical considerations

This study was implemented according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and granted ethical approval by the Universiti Malaya Research Ethics Committee (UM.TNC2/UMREC–3172). No identifying information was collected from participants in the survey. Respondents were informed that their participation was entirely voluntary. In order to proceed with the study, participants were required to indicate their consent by selecting the option “Yes, I consent to participate in this study.”

Results

Baseline characteristics of the population

A total of 2,028 participants completed the survey between May 2024 and May 2025. The study sample comprised predominantly young adults aged 18–29 years (72.5%), with a higher proportion of females (65.4%) compared to males. The majority of participants were Malay (49.5%), followed by Chinese (39.0%) and Indian (5.7%). Most respondents were never married (76.8%) and had attained tertiary education (83.4%). Regarding income, half of the participants reported a monthly household income between MYR 1001–5000, while 21.2% earned MYR 1000 or below. Two-thirds resided in urban areas (66.2%), and the highest representation came from the central region (44.7%), followed by the northern (21.4%) and southern (13.7%) regions (Table 1).

Confidence toward local scientific community

Approximately 70% of respondents expressed confidence in the competency of scientists in the country, while around 30% remained neutral or expressed doubt. Confidence was even higher regarding scientists’ intentions, with 83.2% agreeing that those working in local universities or research institutes conduct their work with the aim of benefiting the public. Additionally, 73.1% of respondents believed that local scientists are open and honest about their findings, although 26.9% were either neutral or skeptical (Table 1).

Confidence toward local vaccine manufacturers/pharmaceutical companies

About 64.1% of respondents believed that local manufacturers have sufficient competency to produce effective, safe, and high-quality vaccines, while 35.9% were neutral or disagreed. Confidence in the intentions of these companies was stronger, with 75.6% agreeing that they are oriented toward safeguarding public health, and 24.4% expressing doubt or neutrality. Additionally, 66.2% agreed that the vaccine products produced locally are effective, safe, and high-quality, while 33.8% did not fully share this view. These findings suggest that while trust in the public health commitment of local manufacturers is relatively strong, perceptions of their competency and product quality are slightly more cautious. The results show a moderate to high level of public confidence in local vaccine manufacturers and pharmaceutical companies.

Confidence toward local vaccine regulatory authorities

The findings indicate a generally high level of public confidence in local vaccine regulatory authorities. About 69.6% of respondents agreed that these authorities have sufficient competency to assure the public of the safety and efficacy of locally-produced vaccines, while 30.4% were neutral or disagreed. A larger proportion, 82.2%, believed that local regulatory bodies are oriented toward safeguarding public health, with 17.8% expressing less certainty. Additionally, 75.0% of respondents considered local regulatory authorities to be trustworthy in providing such assurances, whereas 25.0% were neutral or disagreed. Notably, 74.1% of participants agreed that vaccine approval decisions tend to rely significantly on the reputation of the manufacturer, suggesting some public awareness or concern about the influence of brand reputation in the regulatory process (Table 1).

Vaccine confidence index (VCI)

The VCI results reveal strong public confidence in mRNA vaccines. A substantial majority of respondents (75.9%) agreed that mRNA vaccines are important for children, though 24.1% disagreed and 12.8% indicated uncertainty. Confidence in the safety of mRNA vaccines was even higher, with 82.7% agreeing they are safe, 17.3% disagreeing, and 12.4% unsure. Similarly, 84.9% believed mRNA vaccines are effective, while 15.1% disagreed and 13.6% responded “don’t know.” Regarding personal belief compatibility, 85.0% of participants agreed that mRNA vaccines align with their beliefs, with 15.0% in disagreement and 11.3% uncertain. These findings suggest a high level of public confidence in the safety, effectiveness, and acceptability of mRNA vaccines, though a notable minority remains hesitant or uncertain (Table 1).

Factors influencing confidence toward locally-produced mRNA vaccines

The univariate and multivariate analyses identified several significant factors associated with public confidence toward locally-produced mRNA vaccines. In the multivariate model, ethnicity showed a strong association with confidence, with Malays (aOR = 1.88, 95% CI: 1.15–3.07, p < .05), showing significantly higher odds of confidence compared to those in the ‘Bumiputra and others’ category. Confidence in the local scientific community was also positively associated with vaccine confidence; respondents who agreed that local scientists are competent had significantly higher odds of being confident in locally-produced mRNA vaccines (aOR = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.14–2.10, p < .001). Furthermore, individuals who believed that local vaccine manufacturers are competent (aOR = 1.95, 95% CI: 1.46–2.59, p < .001) and that their products are effective, safe, and of high quality (aOR = 2.39, 95% CI: 1.79–3.20, p < .001) were more likely to express confidence. Similarly, trust in local regulatory authorities played a significant role; those who perceived the authorities as competent (aOR = 1.95, 95% CI: 1.44–2.63, p < .001) and trustworthy (aOR = 1.74, 95% CI: 1.23–2.45, p < .01) had greater odds of confidence in locally-produced vaccines. Components of the VCI were also significant predictors. Respondents who believed mRNA vaccines are safe (aOR = 2.95, 95% CI: 1.84–4.72, p < .001), effective (aOR = 2.00, 95% CI: 1.26–3.13, p < .01), important for children (aOR = 1.63, 95% CI: 1.09–2.43, p < .05), and compatible with their personal beliefs (aOR = 2.31, 95% CI: 1.43–3.75, p < .01) were significantly more likely to express confidence in locally-produced mRNA vaccines (Table 1).

Other reason reported for confidence in locally-produced vaccines

Figure 1 shows that the most commonly cited reason for increasing public confidence in locally-produced vaccines was the availability of clinical trial findings, selected by 28.0% of respondents. This was followed by positive experiences or testimonials from people who had received the vaccines (23.9%), reflecting the influence of peer experiences in shaping public opinion. Regular and transparent communication from health authorities ranked third (11.0%), indicating the public’s desire for openness and clear guidance from official sources. Other reasons such as government approval, educational campaigns, technical information about the vaccine, and international verification were selected less frequently but still contributed meaningfully to overall vaccine confidence.

Discussion

This study provides novel insights into the factors shaping public confidence in locally-produced mRNA vaccines in Malaysia. The findings emphasize that vaccine confidence is not only a matter of scientific evidence but is deeply intertwined with public trust in national institutions, particularly the local scientific community, pharmaceutical manufacturers, and regulatory authorities.

The study highlights a nuanced landscape of public trust in the local scientific community, where confidence in scientists’ intentions and ethical conduct appears stronger than confidence in their technical capabilities. This pattern reflects broader global trends, where trust in science is more closely tied to perceptions of transparency, honesty, and alignment with the public good than to scientific expertise alone.24,25 Overall, the results indicate a moderately high level of public confidence in the local scientific community. This is consistent with survey findings showing only modest public appraisal of national scientific and medical standards, with just around four in ten respondents rating Malaysia’s technological (38%) and scientific (37%) achievements as above average or among the best globally.26 Bridging the gap requires more than transparency and calls for public engagement that highlights scientific credibility. Initiatives such as open-lab visits, community dialogs, and media features on local scientists can strengthen understanding and trust.

The findings reveal a moderately positive perception of local vaccine manufacturers and pharmaceutical companies, with public trust skewing more toward their intentions than their technical capabilities. A strong majority believe these companies are committed to safeguarding public health, suggesting that their perceived ethical orientation and societal role are well-recognized. However, confidence in their ability to produce high-quality, safe, and effective vaccines is somewhat lower, with only about two-thirds of respondents expressing such trust. This gap between perceived intention and technical competence echoes global trends in trust toward healthcare institutions. According to the Edelman Trust Barometer report, ethical drivers such as integrity, dependability, and purpose account for 76% of the trust capital of a business, while competence contributes only 24%. This means that ethics is more important to company trust than competence.27 In this study, one-third of respondents who remain neutral or doubtful regarding product quality and manufacturing standards reflects a level of cautious skepticism, which could be shaped by limited visibility into production processes, past controversies, or comparisons to international pharmaceutical benchmarks.

A particularly noteworthy finding is that 74.1% of respondents believed regulatory approval decisions are influenced by the reputation of the manufacturer. This perception highlights potential concerns about the transparency and objectivity of regulatory processes. International literature underscores that public trust in regulators is critical for vaccine rollout, as perceptions of political or commercial influence can undermine acceptance of even scientifically sound products. For example, controversies surrounding the U.S. FDA’s Emergency Use Authorizations for COVID-19 treatments raised doubts about whether scientific standards were compromised under external pressure, thereby eroding confidence in subsequent COVID-19 vaccine approvals.19,21 If the public perceives local regulatory bodies as relying too heavily on brand reputation rather than rigorous evaluation, their credibility may be diminished and overall vaccine confidence weakened. Strengthening regulatory trust therefore requires transparency in approval processes, clear communication of decision-making criteria, and visible alignment with international standards. Coupled with this, sustained and targeted efforts are needed to reinforce perceptions of scientific rigor and product reliability in the local pharmaceutical sector. Proactive communication from manufacturers on adherence to international standards, transparency in trial data, and collaboration with trusted regulators, alongside public education campaigns highlighting successful local vaccine development efforts, certifications, and safety records, can collectively reinforce confidence.The findings of this study reveal a generally positive perception of local vaccine regulatory authorities among the public. A significant majority, approximately 70%, believe that these authorities possess the technical competency to assure the safety and efficacy of locally-produced vaccines. Even more notably, 82.2% of respondents perceive these regulatory bodies as being oriented toward protecting public health. These perceptions are consistent with broader global findings that emphasize the importance of institutional trust in shaping public attitudes toward vaccines.8,28 Trust in regulatory agencies is essential especially in the context of new or locally-produced vaccines.29–31 Of important note, the current study found that a considerable minority remains either neutral or skeptical, with 30.4% expressing uncertainty or disagreement regarding the competency of regulatory authorities, and 25.0% questioning their trustworthiness. This uncertainty may reflect public concerns about transparency in regulatory processes. Particularly noteworthy is the finding that 74.1% of respondents believe that vaccine approval decisions are significantly influenced by the reputation of the manufacturer. This suggests a perception that branding and commercial prestige may play a role in shaping regulatory outcomes, potentially undermining confidence in the objectivity and scientific rigor of the approval process. While brand reputation can sometimes act as a heuristic for quality, overreliance on such perceptions may indicate underlying concerns about the fairness or transparency of regulatory practices. To strengthen public trust, local regulatory authorities should enhance transparency in vaccine approval processes and communicate decisions clearly and consistently to the public.32,33 Aligning with international standards is also key to maintaining credibility.

The VCI findings indicate a generally high level of public confidence in mRNA vaccines, with strong agreement on their importance, safety, effectiveness, and compatibility with personal beliefs. Notably, over 80% of respondents viewed mRNA vaccines as both safe (82.7%) and effective (84.9%), while 85.0% found them consistent with their personal beliefs. These results are consistent with previous international studies, which have shown that public perceptions of vaccine safety and efficacy are among the most influential determinants of vaccine acceptance worldwide.34,35 However, the presence of a sizable minority, ranging from 11.3% to 24.1% across the items, who either disagreed or were uncertain indicates that vaccine hesitancy persists despite overall high confidence. This mirrors findings from other low- and middle-income settings, where trust in new technologies like mRNA vaccines can be challenged by misinformation, limited access to accurate information, and socio-cultural factors.36 Specifically, the 24.1% who disagreed on the importance of mRNA vaccines for children suggests that child-specific concerns remain a sensitive area, potentially driven by risk perception or moral considerations. These results highlight the need for targeted communication strategies that not only reaffirm the scientific evidence supporting mRNA vaccines but also address specific concerns among hesitant subgroups. Transparent messaging, culturally tailored outreach, and continued monitoring of public sentiment can help ensure that vaccine confidence remains robust and inclusive.

The multivariate analysis identified several key factors that significantly influence public confidence in locally-produced mRNA vaccines. Ethnicity showed a strong association with vaccine confidence, potentially reflecting a stronger alignment with national identity, greater trust in government-led health initiatives, and positive reception to religious or community-endorsed public health messaging. In Malaysia, the Malay community often shows high responsiveness to state narratives, particularly when health campaigns are endorsed by Islamic authorities, which may contribute to higher levels of perceived legitimacy and trust in local vaccines.37,38 The observed ethnic differences may reflect broader socio-cultural dynamics in Malaysia’s pluralistic society. Nonetheless, future qualitative studies, such as focus groups or in-depth interviews, are warranted to explore these underlying drivers and provide more nuanced insights into ethnic disparities in vaccine confidence.

Trust in the local scientific ecosystem also emerged as a crucial determinant. Respondents who believed in the competence of local scientists, manufacturers, and regulatory authorities were significantly more likely to express confidence in locally-produced mRNA vaccines. This is consistent with global literature emphasizing that perceptions of scientific credibility and institutional integrity are central to vaccine confidence and acceptance, particularly when new technologies or locally-produced products are involved.25,39 Trust was further reinforced by confidence in the product’s quality, including its perceived safety, effectiveness, and high manufacturing standards. These perceptions serve as proxies for institutional performance and are critical in low- and middle-income countries where public trust in locally-produced vaccines.

Additionally, core elements of the VCI, namely beliefs about vaccine safety, effectiveness, importance for children, and alignment with personal values, were also significant predictors of confidence in locally-produced mRNA vaccines. These findings support earlier work showing that vaccine attitudes are not formed in isolation but are closely linked to broader social, cultural, and psychological factors, including moral alignment and perceived societal benefit.40,41 These dimensions resonate with constructs of the Health Belief Model (HBM), where perceived benefits (safety, effectiveness, importance) and perceived barriers (hesitancy around child vaccination or uncertainty about safety) shape attitudes toward preventive health behaviors. Although the present study did not employ HBM as a guiding framework, the observed patterns suggest that vaccine confidence can be partially interpreted through this lens, with cues to action reflected in trust toward scientists, manufacturers, regulatory authorities, as well as positive peer experiences and transparent communication from health authorities. This is in line with other studies. For example, Wong et al. applied the HBM in Malaysia and showed that perceived benefits and lower perceived barriers were significant predictors of intent to receive the COVID-19 vaccine, highlighting the role of belief structures in shaping vaccine confidence.42 Similarly, Zampetakis and Melas found that in Greece, perceived severity of COVID-19 and perceived benefits of vaccination significantly increased willingness to vaccinate, confirming the relevance of HBM constructs across different contexts.43 Beyond individual perceptions, Sturgis et al. demonstrated in a large international survey that higher levels of trust in science and consensus within society strongly enhanced vaccine confidence, underscoring the importance of institutional credibility and social cues to action.44 Collectively, these findings support the interpretation that both individual cognitive evaluations (benefits, barriers) and broader trust-related factors contribute to shaping vaccine attitudes.

The descriptive finding of additional reason for public confidence in locally-produced vaccines is primarily driven by access to clinical trial findings and positive peer experiences, underscoring the importance of scientific transparency and social influence.45,46 Regular communication from health authorities also contributes, alongside factors such as government approval, education, and international verification. For manufacturers, a strong track record of safety and quality is the most valued factor, followed by transparent production, compliance with international standards, and a demonstrated commitment to public health over profit. These findings emphasize that both scientific credibility and ethical accountability are crucial for building trust in local vaccine efforts.

Limitation

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, it focused on measuring confidence in locally-produced mRNA vaccines rather than actual acceptance or uptake. While confidence is an important precursor, it does not necessarily translate into vaccine acceptance or behavioral intent. Second, the web-based survey distributed via social media platforms may have introduced sampling bias, as recruitment was based on general postings and voluntary sharing. This likely overrepresented younger, more educated, and urban populations with better internet access, while underrepresenting older adults, those with lower educational attainment, and individuals in rural areas. Accordingly, the findings should not be generalized to the entire Malaysian population but rather interpreted within the context of the sample characteristics. Future research should consider employing stratified sampling approaches to achieve greater representativeness. Third, as a cross-sectional study, our analysis captures perceptions at a single time point and cannot establish causality between predictors and levels of confidence. Findings should be interpreted as associations, and future longitudinal or experimental research is warranted to clarify causal pathways. Fourth, reliance on self-reported data introduces the risk of recall and social desirability biases, particularly in responses concerning institutional trust and personal beliefs about mRNA vaccine technology. Lastly, the use of a hypothetical scenario to assess acceptance of locally-produced mRNA vaccines may not accurately predict actual decision-making behaviors once such vaccines become available in the market. As confidence does not necessarily equate to uptake, future studies should incorporate measures of vaccination intention and actual uptake to better reflect real-world acceptance.

Conclusion

Public confidence in locally-produced mRNA vaccines in Malaysia is influenced by a multifaceted set of factors, including trust in the scientific community, perceived competence of vaccine manufacturers, product quality, and regulatory credibility. The key actionable insight is that building future vaccine confidence requires not only strengthening scientific and manufacturing capacity but also cultivating trust through transparency, cultural sensitivity, and proactive communication. Among demographic factors, ethnicity emerged as particularly influential, underscoring the importance of tailoring strategies to different cultural and religious contexts. In the future, efforts should prioritize parallel investment in local scientific capacity and transparent, evidence-based engagement with diverse communities, ensuring that when a locally-produced vaccine becomes available, public confidence will be both inclusive and resilient.
