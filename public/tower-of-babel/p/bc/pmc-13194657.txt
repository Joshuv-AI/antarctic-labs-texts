Bovine tuberculosis resulting from infection with Mycobacterium orygis in a closed herd of Indian water buffalo (Bubalus bubalis)
Authors: Babu Lal Jangir, Mohit Kumar, Ramesh Kumar, Mahavir Singh, Tarun Kumar, Vaishali Sehrawat, Pooja Kundu, Premanshu Dandapat, Sukhen Samanta, Abhinaba Sarkar, Manisankar Pathak, Santanu Pal, Sangita Mandal, Molla Zakirul Haque, Ayan Mukherjee, Jeyaprakash Rajendhran, Rameshchandra Pandit, Pallavi Moudgil, Renu Gupta, Douwe Bakker, Vivek Kapur, Naresh Jindal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMycobacterium orygis (M. orygis), a member of the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex, has emerged as an important cause of animal and human tuberculosis in South Asia. Despite the widespread prevalence of bovine tuberculosis (bTB) in India and the major role of over 100 million Indian water buffaloes in dairy production, the pathological and epidemiological features of M. orygis infection in buffaloes are largely unexplored. A closed herd of 279 female water buffaloes in Haryana, India, was screened for bTB by single intradermal cervical tuberculin skin (SCT) test, the comparative cervical tuberculin (CCT) test, and the interferon gamma release assay (IGRA). Detailed post-mortem examinations, cultural isolation, Polymerase Chain Reaction, and Whole Genome Sequencing were methodically performed on reactor buffaloes. The screening identified 26 animals as reactors by SCT. Of these, 20 were confirmed positive for bTB with both CCT and IGRA. Among the 15 reactor animals examined, gross pathological lesions suggestive of bTB were observed in 13 animals, while histopathological lesions in 14 animals. Molecular analyses confirmed the presence of MTBC in nine animals, with eight identified as M. orygis infections and one with mixed infection. Notably, two genetically distinct clusters of M. orygis suggestive multiple introductions into the herd. The study reveals significant pathological and microbiological complexities in bTB infections by M. orygis in Indian water buffaloes. It underscores a need for expanded research, improved surveillance, and introduction of targeted control measures to eliminate bTB in this major farmed animal species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44186-0.Keywords: Bovine tuberculosis, Indian water buffalo, Mycobacterium orygis, Necropsy, Tuberculin skin testSubject terms: Diseases, Genetics, Microbiology, Molecular biology

Introduction

Bovine tuberculosis (bTB) is a chronic bacterial disease that affects both livestock and wildlife. The disease results from infection with any member of the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC), primarily M. bovis, M. caprae, and M. tuberculosis1. More recently, M. orygis has been identified as a causative agent of bTB in various animals such as cattle, monkeys, and rhinoceros in countries like Bangladesh, Nepal, and India2–5 and reported to cause human tuberculosis in patients of South Asian origin6.

Domestic water buffaloes play a vital role in agriculture and food production, particularly in South Asia, where they are extensively reared. While bTB is considered endemic in South Asia4, the role, if any, that M. orygis may pose as a threat to the health and productivity of Indian water buffaloes, as well as public health considerations due to the zoonotic potential of the bacterium remain largely unknown. Most of the published literature on bTB in farm animals in India is based on either skin testing of animals in a few herds or PCR testing of clinical samples7–9. The bTB is primarily caused by Mycobacterium bovis mainly in cattle, however, reports pertaining to the isolation of this bacterium from clinical samples from bovines in India are sparse10–12. There have been a few studies addressing its presence in livestock4 and wild animals5–13. Additionally, a notable recent study has reported the isolation of Mycobacterium orygis from an Indian buffalo14. This expanding body of research underscores the complexity of tuberculosis pathogens in animal populations and highlights the need for continued surveillance and study to understand the full spectrum of tuberculosis infection sources and transmission dynamics.

The diagnosis of bTB typically involves tuberculin skin tests using bovine and avian purified protein derivatives (PPDs), such as the single intradermal cervical tuberculin (SCT) and comparative cervical tuberculin (CCT) tests, complemented by the gamma-interferon release assay (IGRA)15,16. While these methods are established for bTB detection, particularly in the context of international trade1, their efficacy in accurately predicting pathological lesions or determining the microbiological status of bTB in water buffaloes remains unclear. Our recent comparative analysis of performance and diagnostic accuracy of the tests in Indian water buffaloes revealing a lack of concordance between tests such as SCT and CCT, and their individual performance metrics, underscores the complexities involved in bTB diagnostics within buffalo populations17. Moreover, the emerging role of M. orygis as a bTB pathogen in India adds to the diagnostic dilemma, given the current lack of clarity regarding the sensitivity and specificity of these tests for detecting M. orygis infections in buffaloes. Post-mortem diagnosis, relying on visual lesion identification, isolation of bacterium, histopathological examination, and PCR, also poses challenges in establishing definitive diagnoses18. These diagnostic uncertainties reinforce the need for enhanced research efforts and the development of more accurate testing protocols that could better address the specific challenges of diagnosing bTB in buffaloes.

To address this knowledge gap, this study aimed to comprehensively assess the pathological and microbiological features of M. orygis-associated bTB in a herd of Indian water buffaloes. The findings reveal the key pathological and microbiological attributes of the disease, underscoring the critical need for expanded research and enhanced surveillance. Importantly, the study highlights an urgent need for the development and implementation of targeted control strategies aimed at curtailing bTB prevalence in this major dairy species, with the broader goal of safeguarding both animal and human health.

Methods

Ethical approval

The study was approved by both the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (LUVAS/VCC/2161-79; Dated: 17.10.2019) and the Institutional Biosafety Committee (LUVAS/ABT/29; Dated: 07.01.2020) of Lala Lajpat Rai Veterinary and Animal Sciences University (LUVAS), Hisar, Haryana, India. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant institutional guidelines and regulations for the ethical use of animals in research.

Animals included in the study

The study included 279 female animals from a single intensively farmed herd of Murrah breed of Indian water buffaloes (Bubalus bubalis) in Haryana, India. The herd remained closed, except for nine tuberculin skin test-negative female buffaloes introduced during 2018-19. The entire herd was initially tested by SCT, and reactor animals were subsequently retested by CCT and IGRA. A final cohort of 15 non-pregnant animals that tested positive with all assays was selected for detailed post-mortem examination.

Skin testing

The SCT using standard bovine tuberculin (PPD-B; IVRI, Bareilly, India) was performed to determine the reactor status. CCT using bovine PPD (PPD-B, 3000 IU; Prionics; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and avian PPD (PPD-A, 2500 IU; Prionics; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was performed in reactor buffaloes as previously described17. In brief, one hundred microliters of bovine PPD and avian PPD were administered intradermally using McLintock syringes (Bar Knight McLintock Limited, Scotland) to prepared skin sites on the left side in the neck region of the reactor buffaloes. To check for delayed hypersensitivity, differences in skin thickness were measured, and the results were interpreted as positive with an increase in skin thickness of 4 mm or greater as per the WOAH guidelines17.

Interferon Gamma Release Assay (IGRA)

The same tuberculin antigens as applied for the skin test, PPD-B and PPD-A (Prionics; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), were used for in vitro stimulation of whole blood at final concentrations of 300 IU/ml and 250 IU/ml, respectively, according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Bovigam, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Pokeweed mitogen (PWM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used as a positive control. Blood was collected from the jugular vein in lithium-heparinized vials from 26 SCT reactor buffaloes. Whole blood was stimulated overnight (37 °C, 5% CO2) with the antigens (PPD-B and PPD-A) in vitro and the supplied enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay was used to determine IFN-γ concentrations in the whole blood culture supernatants. The results for antigen-stimulated cultures were expressed as the background-corrected optical density (OD) at 450 nm. Animals had an OD value of PPD B minus PPD A ≥ 0.1, considered positive by IGRA.

Pathological examination

A detailed necropsy was performed on the CCT reactor and IGRA-positive buffaloes (n = 15). The buffaloes were euthanized using xylazine and thiopentone, according to the guidelines of the Committee for Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals (CCSEA)19. Animals with focal or multifocal granulomas in the lungs, lymph nodes, or other organs were recorded to have gross pathological lesions suggestive of bTB. Detailed gross and histopathological examination was performed on lungs, liver, spleen, kidneys, intestine, udder and lymph nodes (parotid, mandibular, retropharyngeal, mediastinal, bronchiolar, mesenteric, supra mammary, prescapular, and prefemoral), and lesion severity was scored (Supplementary Table 1).

Tissue samples were collected, fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, and processed for paraffin embedding for histopathological examination using standard procedures20. Tissue sections were stained with haematoxylin and eosin, Ziehl-Neelsen staining, and von Kossa’s method to indicate acid-fast bacilli and tissue calcification. Stained samples were examined under a light microscope and photographed. Microscopic tuberculous lesions were categorized into four stages (Stage I-IV) as per Wangoo et al. (2005) with slight modifications (Supplementary Table 2); ranging from irregular clusters of epithelioid macrophages in Stage I, to multicentric granulomas with extensive necrosis and calcification in Stage IV21.

Mycobacterial culture and isolation

During necropsy, fresh tissue samples were collected from different organs and lymph nodes of each buffalo and transported in a saturated sodium tetraborate solution to Indian Council of Agricultural Research-Indian Veterinary Research Institute (ICAR-IVRI), Kolkata, for mycobacterial culture and isolation. To prevent cross-contamination, samples were processed from each buffalo individually. Samples from a single animal were pooled, homogenized, and decontaminated using Petroff’s sodium hydroxide22 and sulfuric acid methods, involving digestion, washing, and sedimentation. Isolation was performed in the Lowenstein-Jensen (LJ) medium following established protocols23. The sediments from both decontamination methods were suspended in saline and inoculated on LJ medium with glycerol and sodium pyruvate. The inoculated slants were incubated at 37 °C for up to eight weeks, with regular monitoring for mycobacterial growth. Additionally, the sediment from Petroff’s method was cultured in Mycobacteria Growth Indicator Tubes (MGIT) medium containing growth supplement, and incubated at 37 °C for 42 days with daily observations using a BD BACTEC MicroMGIT Reader.

Molecular studies

Individual tissue samples from each of the 15 animals (n = 240) were assayed for detection of MTBC by targeting the IS1081 gene using conventional PCR. DNA was extracted from tissue samples using a ZYMO Research Quick DNA Mini Prep Kit™ according to the manufacturer’s instructions and extracted DNA was stored at −80 °C till further analysis. Conventional PCR was performed using IS1081 gene-specific primers to detect MTBC (forward: 5’-TCGCGTGATCCTTCGAAACG-3’; reverse: 5’-CGCAGCTTGGGGATCGCGAC-3’) following the protocol as described by Nghiem et al. (2015)24. Amplified products were visualized on a 1.5% agarose gel stained with ethidium bromide. DNA extracted from a commercially available BCG vaccine was used as a positive control. The animals were considered positive for MTBC if either single or multiple tissue samples revealed the presence of a specific amplicon of 300 bp size.

Additionally, DNA was extracted from isolates using the QIAamp® DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. This DNA was subjected to multiplex real-time PCR for the differentiation of MTBC species including M. orygis, M. bovis, M. caprae, M. tuberculosis, and M. bovis BCG following the protocols of Halse et al. (2011) and Duffy et al. (2020)25,26. The real-time PCR was performed on a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Inc.) using a TaqMan master mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific™).

Whole genome sequencing

Extracted DNA of isolates were processed for whole genome sequencing (WGS) using the Nextera XT Library Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Qualification of each library was performed with a Fragment Analyser Automated CE System (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Paired end libraries were sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq using the MiSeq reagent kit V2 (500 cycles). Quality check (QC) of FASTQ files was performed using FastQC, and data filtering was accomplished using Trimmomatic by using key parameters including the quality threshold (q), sliding window size, minimum read length, trim leading and trailing bases, adapter sequences, etc. SPAdes genome assembler was used to assemble the raw data. QUAST (Quality Assessment Tool for Genome Assemblies) tool was used for the evaluation and quality assessment of genome assemblies. The resulting assemblies were initially screened using PubMLST and the completeness of genomes assessed with Kbase. Single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) and phylogenetic analyses were performed using the SNP tool developed by the US Department of Agriculture-Veterinary Services (github) with H37Rv (accession number: NC_000962.3) as a reference to define SNP positions. RAxML software (version 8.2; GTRCATI model) was used to construct a maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree using the output SNP alignment. The final tree was visualized and annotated with iTOLv6.827. The eight raw FASTQ sequences have been submitted to the short-read archive (SRA) at the National Center for Biotechnology Information with accession numbers SRR24372788 through SRR24372795 and BioProject identifier PRJNA964597. The Kraken tool was utilized to delineate the taxonomic strata.

Statistical analysis

The data was statistically analyzed to determine the correlation between the factors by using the Pearson correlation test and Chi-square test. The inferences were drawn accordingly.

Results

Skin test and IGRA

The results showed 26 of the 279 (9.32%) studied animals tested positive using the SCT. Of these, 20 animals were also positive with the CCT and the IGRA. Five pregnant buffaloes were excluded from further analyses, resulting in 15 animals with confirmed positive results on both CCT and IGRA tests that represented the final subset for subsequent detailed post-mortem pathological and microbiological investigation (Fig. 1; Table 1).

The 15 selected animals had a median age of 6 years (range 4.5 to 10.5 years) and a median weight of 565 kg (range 475 to 675 kg), with median increases in skin thickness in the SCT of 12 mm (range 7–37 mm) and in the CCT of 9 mm (range 4–26 mm) (Table 1).

Pathological findings

Descriptive gross pathology

Thirteen of the 15 buffaloes showed visible tuberculous lesions at necropsy, 10 of which had lesions in either the lungs or in the lung-, head-, or neck- lymph nodes (Table 2). In seven animals, only one organ or lymph node showed visible lesions while more than one organ or lymph nodes showed visible lesions in six animals. Detailed lesion scores across organs for each animal are provided in Table 2. Total gross visible lesion scores amongst the 13 animals ranged from 1 to 35 (Table 1: Supplementary Table 3). Gross pathological changes were observed in lungs and lymph nodes such as mediastinal and bronchiolar of five animals, mandibular and retropharyngeal of six animals, mesenteric of two animals, and parotid, supramammary and prefemoral in one animal each. The heart, spleen, kidney, intestine or udder tissue of none of 15 buffaloes displayed visible lesions, while the liver revealed typical TB gross lesions in one animal. Two buffaloes had disseminated tubercles in the rib cage that resembled yellow to white pearls of size from 0.3 mm to 1.0 cm in diameter and were either discrete or arranged in typical grape-like clusters attached to the ribs and the intercostal muscles (Fig. 1A). The lesions in the lungs varied from small to large circumscribed, pale yellow or white granulomas containing suppurative exudates. Disseminated tuberculous lesions with yellow to white pearl-like tubercles, either single or grouped, were also visible on the pleural surface. On slicing, the lungs revealed small-to large-sized granulomas disseminated within the parenchyma. Granulomas were characterized by caseous necrosis, the presence of yellow cheesy material and calcification as was evident from the hard consistency and gritty sound heard during slicing.

The affected lymph nodes were enlarged and hard in consistency and showed either solitary or multiple lesions varying from millet size to large multifocal to confluent caseating granulomas containing pale-yellow to white cheesy exudate (Fig. 1B). Similar to the lungs, the lymph nodes in some animals also revealed evidence of calcification within the granulomatous lesions.

Histopathology

On histopathological examination, the lungs of five buffaloes and at least one of the lymph nodes of 14 buffaloes revealed typical lesions suggestive of bTB. The different stages of granulomas were observed. In addition to typical tuberculous lesions in lungs (Fig. 1C), the lungs of these five buffaloes also revealed marked congestion, haemorrhage, edema and emphysema. The lymph nodes showed either discrete or multiple granulomas, most frequently stages III or IV (Fig. 1D), but occasionally also of stages I or II. Besides, typical tuberculous lesions, the lymph nodes also showed congestion, haemorrhages, and depletion of lymphocytes mainly in the cortical region. Udder, liver, intestine, heart and spleen tissue samples from the necropsied buffaloes did not reveal any histopathological change that was suggestive of bTB. Representative sections of the lungs and lymph nodes revealed variable number of pinkish acid-fast bacilli upon (Ziehl-Neelsen) ZN staining (Fig. 1E).

Isolation and identification of MTBC from tissue samples by culture and PCR

Samples (n = 15) representing fresh tissues pooled from a single buffalo were processed for isolation of mycobacteria. Nine of these pooled samples revealed the typical growth of Mycobacterium on LJ and/or in MGIT tubes (Table 1). The observed colonies on solid media were moist, smooth and granular. DNA extracted from nine isolates was subjected to real-time multiplex PCR for identification of Mycobacterium species; eight isolates were M. orygis while one was mixed (positive for both M. orygis and M. tuberculosis).

A total of 37 samples out of 240 tissue samples revealed the presence of a band of 300 bp specific for IS1081gene (Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 1) and represented nine animals (Fig. 2). Notably, one animal (HR-726) did not show gross and histopathological lesions in any of the organs examined; however, its parotid lymph node was positive for MTBC by PCR and tissue pools also microbiologically positive. In contrast, HR-727 showed gross lesions in lungs and six lymphnodes (parotid, mandibular, retropharyngeal, mediastinal, bronchiolar) with highest gross lesion score of 35. PCR analysis confirmed MTBC in both the lungs and all six lymph nodes, and cultures also yielded Mycobacterium for this animal. A total of seven animals tested positive with culture and conventional PCR, while two animals were positive by culture but negative with IS1081 PCR, and conversely, two animals were positive in IS1081 PCR but negative by culture.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the data showed a significant and strong positive Pearson correlation (r = 0.685, p = 0.005, R2 = 0.469) between CCT values and total gross lesion scores. Similarly, a strong positive Spearman correlation (r = 0.688, p = 0.005, R2 = 0.469) was found between the number of PCR-positive tissues and CCT values, while a moderately positive Spearman correlation (r = 0.563, p = 0.029, R2 = 0.172) was observed between the count of PCR-positive tissues and total gross lesion scores.

Additional analysis focused on comparing PCR-positive udders versus PCR-negative udders (Fig. 3). Animals with PCR-positive udders (n = 5) exhibited average SCT and CCT scores (in mm) of 24.8 and 16.4, respectively. This contrasted with the PCR-negative group (n = 10), which had average SCT and CCT scores (in mm) of 15.1 and 9.8, respectively. Examination of PCR udder status alongside CCT amplitude and total gross lesion scores demonstrated a significant difference in the number of PCR-positive tissues between PCR-positive udder (mean = 5.4, SD = 1.8) and PCR-negative udder (mean = 1.0, SD = 1.8) groups (P = 0.002). Notably, the pre-femoral lymph node was the only tissue significantly associated with udder PCR status (Chi-square = 7.3, p = 0.007) (Fig. 4).

Whole genome sequence analysis

Whole genome sequences analysis of the 9 MTBC isolates with next-generation sequencing was done. The statistics of genome assemblies are provided in Supplementary Table 4. The sequence analysis revealed that both forward (R1) and reverse (R2) reads exhibited good quality, with average Phred quality scores of 29.2 and 22.3, respectively (Supplementary Table 5). Using the M. tuberculosis H37Rv (NC_000962.3) genome as the reference, sequencing depth and coverage varied among samples, with an average depth ranging from 36.7X to 101.3X, with isolates exhibiting high-quality coverage (> 97%) of the reference genome indicating good genomic representation (Supplementary Table 6).

By using tools named PubMLST, Kbase, and vSNP for analysis, it was determined that the genetic material of eight samples matched with a species called M. orygis. However, one sample appeared to have a mix of three different bacteria: 87% M. fortuitum, 9% M. orygis, and 2% M. tuberculosis with high SNP coverage (total SNPs 23,384) (Table 1, Supplementary Table 6). Consequently, this sample exhibited a high SNP count (23,384; Table 1 and Supplementary Table 6) along with a larger total assembly size (10,077,688 bp). The Kbase tool confirmed that all the analyzed genomes were more than 99% complete. Through the genetic differences by SNP analysis; it was found that six samples (HR-713, HR-715, HR-722, HR-726, HR-727 and HR-730) grouped together in one category, while two others (HR-706, HR-708) were in a different group (Fig. 5). The SNP counts in isolates obtained from animals (HR706 and HR708) were low as compared to the other six isolates (HR-713, HR-715, HR-722, HR-726, HR-727 and HR-730) (Supplementary Table 5).

Phylogenetic analysis of Mycobacterium orygis isolated from water buffalo with previously published sequences of Mycobacterium orygis and other Mycobacterium tuberculosis around the globe. The sequences showing in two different clusters starting with HR are of the present study. The blue colour indicates distinct lineages of M. orygis of this study.

vSNP analysis confirmed eight of the isolates as M. orygis (Table 1), whereas one (HR-731) represented a mixed culture including 57% M. tuberculosis complex (including 4% each of M. orygis and M. tuberculosis), 42% M. fortuitum, and ~1% other (Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 2). Hence, the sequences corresponding to the MTBC including M. orygis from HR-731 were enriched using a custom pipeline, and the cleaned sequence used for SNP-based comparative genome and phylogenetic analyses.

The results reveal pairwise SNP distances ranging from 0 to 326 with two primary clusters: the first comprised of HR706 and HR708, which were separated by three SNPs, yet exhibited a substantial genetic distance from the second cluster (HR713, HR715, HR722, HR726, HR727, HR730) with differences ranging from 323 to 326 SNPs (Supplementary Table 5). In this second cluster with pairwise distances ranging from 0 to 6 SNPs, isolates HR715 and HR722 were identical, suggesting these isolates are either clonal or share a very recent common ancestor, whereas, HR713, HR726, HR727 and HR730 in this cluster were differentiated by between1-6 SNPs, reflecting a high degree of genetic similarity.

Isolate HR731, noted as a mixed culture, displayed SNP counts that ranged from 32 (with HR730) to 165 (with HR706), suggesting it was potentially intermediate between the two primary clusters. However, given the mixed culture status, these values are interpreted with caution and the exact nature of this relationship needs further detailed investigation.

Phylogenetic analyses of M. orygis sequences of this study with 93 other M. orygis sequences available in the SRA database revealed that the M. orygis sequences from closed herd in this study were in two distinct clades and closely related as well to other M. orygis isolates recovered from bovids, cervids and humans from multiple countries across 4 continents (Fig. 5).

Discussion

The total population of Indian water buffalo exceeds 100 million28 and accounts for a large fraction of the milk and economic value associated with dairy production in India29. Given that bTB is reported to be endemic in cattle and buffaloes in India (Srinivasan et al., 2018), our long-term objective is to understand the disease dynamics in buffalo as an aid to developing better methods for risk assessment and disease control. With this context, the current investigation was undertaken to understand the pathological and microbiological features of the disease in a close herd of buffaloes that were immunologically positive for bTB30.

Our findings from CCT and IGRA positive buffaloes from a single closed herd in Haryana, India, revealed that 13 and 14 of the 15-test positive but apparently healthy adult female buffaloes showed bTB-associated gross and histopathological lesions, respectively at necropsy that was “typical” – and similar to previously described for bTB in cattle infected with M. bovis31–35. Importantly, 9 of the 15-skin reactor buffaloes were also positive by culture and molecular-based detection methods for M. orygis, a relatively recently described “animal” lineage within the closely related M. tuberculosis complex36. M. orygis is increasingly recognized as a cause of tuberculosis in humans and other animals including cattle, ruminants and free-ranging or captive wild animal species [2–13] as well as free-ranging African buffalo (Syncerus caffer)37. Recently, M. orygis has also been reported from buffaloes in India14. The isolation of M. orygis but not M. bovis from Indian buffaloes is consistent with the observation that, unlike other parts of the world, M. bovis is perhaps not a cause of either bTB or zoonotic TB in South Asia, where the disease is likely to result from M. orygis instead. This paucity of M. bovis but the presence of M. orygis was also reported by Duffy et al. (2020) who conducted a study with 940 human isolates and reported no wild-type M. bovis but seven M. orygis isolates from humans across India and South Asia, including some that clustered closely with M. orygis isolates of cattle origin26.

Of the 15 buffaloes necropsied, we were able to identify MTBC by PCR or isolated Mycobacterium in 11 animals, an observation that is consistent with the technical challenges and relatively low sensitivity associated with recovery or detection of members of the MTBC complex from animal tissues. Surprisingly, while the udder tissue from any of the animals did not reveal any gross or histopathological change suggestive of bTB, samples from the udders of 5 animals were MTBC positive by PCR. The results also show that animals PCR positive or negative in the udder had similar average age and body weight across both groups. The consequences of the detection of MTBC from udder tissue are manifold.

We determined the associations among variables. Our results suggested that approximately 47% of the variance in total gross lesion scores can be accounted for by CCT values. Likewise, the association between the number of PCR-positive tissues and total gross lesion scores indicated a weaker but still noteworthy linear relationship. The correlation between the number of PCR-positive tissues and CCT values showed a strong positive Spearman correlation. Collectively, these findings underscore the diagnostic value of CCT in gauging lesion severity and the complex interplay between lesion scores and tissue-specific PCR results in buffalo. Our results further revealed that udder tissue MTBC-positive animals had a higher number of MTBC-positive tissues as compared to MTBC-negative udders suggesting that udder PCR status may be a proxy for broader systemic infection in animals.

The routes and dynamics of transmission of bTB in buffalo herds are poorly understood, but presumably involve introduction into a herd via animal movement or purchase or through contaminated feed, fomites, etc., and followed by within-herd spread through animal-animal contact and exposure. In closed herds with frequent purchase, most of the transmission is expected to be through within-herd transmission or herd breakdowns due to contact with infected free-living species (for example, badgers in the case in the UK or possums in New Zealand) and in rare instances, animal handlers38. In this context, our study identified two genetically distinct lineages of M. orygis circulating within this large buffalo dairy herd with no known contact with other livestock species including cattle, sheep, or goats, or free-living mammals other than humans. To get two clusters circulating in one closed herd of buffaloes is a unique finding of this study. At present we don’t know the potential epidemiological significance of the presence of two clusters. The presence of two distinct clades of M. orygis in the same herd is epidemiologically significant, possibly suggests multiple introduction events or distinct transmission chains may have occurred, even within what is considered a closed herd. Although the herd had no known recent introduction of new animals, such genetic shaping could reflect historical introductions that persisted and diversified over time. The presence of two clades also raises the possibility of micro-evolutionary divergence within the herd, driven by prolonged circulation and host adaptation, as host genetics also play an important role. Importantly, the coexistence of multiple M. orygis lineages underscores the complexity of Mycobacterium transmission in livestock systems and highlights the need for genomic surveillance to resolve cryptic transmission pathways that may not be evident from conventional epidemiological investigations alone. Animals in the herd under study are tested for bTB once in two years by SCT. The inconclusive reactors are kept with the SCT negative animals. The possibility of transmission through these inconclusive reactors cannot be ruled out. In a study, Clegg et al., (2011) reported that 11.8–21.4% of inconclusive reactors slaughtered before being re-tested were infected with M. bovis at necropsy39. Taken together, further studies are needed to understand the transmission dynamics between buffaloes and animal workers, as well as the rate of transmission of M. orygis from reactor buffaloes to susceptible animals in herd settings. Given the substantial differences in husbandry practices, including the strong maternal instinct and need for calf suckling for milk let-down in buffalo, understanding the transmission dynamics of bTB in buffalo herds appears to be a major knowledge gap that needs to be urgently filled.

One of the key aspects of our study is its implications for both animal and human health. In regions like India, where unpasteurized milk is often consumed, the potential for zoonotic transmission of bTB is a concern that warrants attention. This is especially important given the high burden of human tuberculosis in India alongside reports of M. orygis recovered from some human patients, suggesting that elimination of human TB will be made more difficult if pathways for zoonotic transmission are also not similarly addressed. Our study has several limitations, including the fact that we did not test milk samples for the presence of Mycobacterium species. We also did not test the animal handlers and livestock attendants to assess for their status, an area that future research should address to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the disease’s zoonotic or anthropozoonotic potential. Perhaps the greatest limitation is that our study is from a single herd. This limitation restricts the generalizability of our findings, and points to the need for further research involving larger sample sizes and multiple herds to offer a more detailed understanding of bTB epidemiology in India40. Further we used M. bovis and M. avium PPDs in the diagnostic tests (SCT, CCT and IGRA). It would be interesting to note the performance of these tests with M. orygis PPD. We do not know whether use of M. orygis PPD will affect the sensitivity and specificity of the tests. Further studies are needed on these aspects.

In conclusion, this study highlights the importance of multifaceted approach for the detection of bTB in Indian water buffaloes. The whole genome sequencing provides further valuable insights on presence of two distinct clades within the same herd, highlights the coexistence of multiple M. orygis lineages underscores the complexity of Mycobacterium transmission in livestock systems and highlights the need for genomic surveillance to resolve cryptic transmission pathways that may not be evident from conventional epidemiological investigations alone. As the dairy industry continues to grow and intensify, understanding the epidemiology of bTB and implementing methods to control the disease become increasingly important for both animal and human health. Given the importance of human tuberculosis elimination, as well as a focus on improving animal health and dairy production, adopting a One Health approach, which integrates human, animal and environmental health, could be a valuable strategy for the control of tuberculosis from not just humans, but from all domesticated animal species.
