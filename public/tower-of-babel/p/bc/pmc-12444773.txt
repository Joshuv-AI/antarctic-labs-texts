Metastatic Mediastinal Germ Cell Tumour: A Florid and Fulminant Presentation
Authors: Zhong Xhen Khor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA previously healthy teenager presented with acute respiratory distress and constitutional symptoms. Imaging revealed a mediastinal mass with multiple pulmonary nodules, and markedly elevated β‐HCG confirmed metastatic choriocarcinoma. Despite urgent chemotherapy, he succumbed within a week. This case underscores its rarity, diagnostic challenge, and dismal prognosis in tuberculosis‐endemic regions.Keywords: imaging, oncology, radiology, respiratory medicine

A previously healthy 18‐year‐old man presented with progressive dyspnea, dry cough, and fever of 2 days, with constitutional symptoms of weight loss, anorexia, and night sweats over 1 month. On arrival, he was tachypneic and in respiratory distress, requiring high‐flow oxygen support. Lung examination revealed bibasal crepitations; the abdomen was soft without lymphadenopathy or organomegaly. Testes were normal, without any lumps. Tuberculosis was initially suspected.

Chest radiograph showed multiple bilateral pulmonary nodules (> 1 cm); a large mediastinal mass extending into the right lung field was noted (Figure 1). Contrast‐enhanced computed tomography of the thorax confirmed a 6.3 × 5.9 × 7.5 cm anterior mediastinal mass compressing the right superior mediastinum with innumerable pulmonary nodules (Figures 2, 3, 4). There was a markedly elevated serum β‐HCG (> 250,000 mIU/mL; normal range < 5 mIU/mL) and LDH (> 1000 IU/L; normal range 100–280 U/L), consistent with metastatic mediastinal germ cell tumour, likely choriocarcinoma.

Image of CT thorax in axial view shows right anterior mediastinal mass with central hypodensity and compression onto the mediastinal vessels (arrow).

Image of CT thorax in coronal view shows right anterior superior mediastinal mass causing compression onto the right superior vena cava (arrows).

Primary mediastinal choriocarcinoma is exceedingly rare, with fewer than 50 cases reported in the literature. It typically affects young males and carries a dismal prognosis, with cure rates of 5%–10%. Clinical suspicion may be confounded in tuberculosis‐endemic regions, but radiological recognition of a mediastinal mass with large pulmonary metastases (> 1 cm) is crucial to avoid misdiagnosis. Despite urgent chemotherapy initiation, the patient succumbed within 1 week due to refractory respiratory failure [1, 2].
