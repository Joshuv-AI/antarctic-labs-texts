Perturbation of the circadian rhythmic transcription contributes to radiotherapy-induced salivary gland hypofunction
Authors: Shi-Ting Song, Kai-Bin Yang, Xiao-Qi Chen, Zi-Yu Chen, Xing-Zhi Xu, Guo-Yi Zhang, Zhen Huang, Zhi-Cao Yue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSalivary gland hypofunction is a common adverse effect in patients after radiotherapy for head and neck cancers, resulting in the dry mouth syndrome called xerostomia. Previous studies suggested that the functionality of the salivary gland is under the regulation of the circadian clock, however, the extent and scope of this regulation remains unexplored. Here, we profiled the diurnal fluctuation of gene expression in the mouse submandibular gland, and found that about 6% of the transcriptome showed rhythmic expression. We further analyzed the regulatory role of key circadian transcription factors BMAL1, NR1D1 (REV-ERBA), and DBP, which revealed a wide range of potential down-stream target genes. The circadian rhythmic transcription was disrupted upon irradiation in a process related to p53 activation and the increased production of reactive oxygen species. Modulation of the circadian clock by chemical agents including Nobiletin and SR1078 can reduce the damaging effect and improve saliva production. We propose that the salivary gland hypofunction after radiotherapy involves perturbation of the circadian rhythmic transcription, which can be partially rescued by chemical agents that improve the circadian rhythm.Subject terms: Transcriptional regulatory elements, Salivary gland diseases

Introduction

Salivary gland hypofunction is a common adverse effect experienced by patients with head and neck cancer after radiotherapy, the progression of which leads to the dry mouth syndrome called xerostomia1–4. Under severe conditions, patients suffer from impaired swollen and lingual function, and there is currently no effective treatment available5–10. For instance, artificial saliva is often recommended by clinicians to relieve the pain11.

Ionizing radiation (IR) induced considerable damage to the salivary gland through mechanisms that are incompletely understood. Whether the acinar and ductal cells undergo apoptosis post-IR remains a matter of debate12–14. Cellular senescence and chronic inflammation may contribute to the long-term disruption of the salivary gland function1–4,12,15. Other contributing factors may involve hypoxia/hypovascularization, fibrosis, and nitrate metabolism1–4,8.

The circadian clock is the biological rhythmic system that regulates a 24-hour diurnal oscillation of gene expression and biological activities within each cell that is synchronized by the central clock localized in the suprachiasmatic nucleus16,17. Key transcriptional regulatory loops include the E-box binding factors BMAL1/CLOCK, the RORE-binding factor NR1D1 (REV-ERBA) and the D-box binding factor DBP18–21. Circadian regulation of the salivary gland function has been documented, including the rhythmic saliva flow rate and composition, and expression of key circadian transcription factors and functional genes22–29. However, the extent and scope of circadian gene expression in the salivary gland remains unexplored. Moreover, previous work has mainly focused on the time-refined chemo/radiotherapy, or chronotherapy which adjusted the therapeutic schedule according to the circadian clock30–32. It is unknown whether the circadian clock itself is perturbed during radiotherapy.

Here, we show that about 6% of the transcriptome showed rhythmic expression in the mouse submandibular salivary gland. By determining the genomic binding profiles of the key transcription factors BMAL1, NR1D1, and DBP, we found that these factors regulate each other, and cooperate to regulate important functional genes in the salivary gland. IR disrupts the circadian clock within the salivary gland through mechanisms related to p53 activation and the increased level of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Furthermore, chemical agents that improve the circadian clock function can rescue the salivary gland hypofunction via mechanisms independent of cellular senescence. These results suggest that perturbation of the circadian clock contributes to IR-induced salivary gland hypofunction.

Results

Rhythmic gene expression in the salivary gland

A key question regarding circadian regulation of organ function is to what extent the gene expression within the organ is subject to the circadian clock regulation. Here, we analyzed rhythmic gene expression change within the mouse submandibular salivary gland during the day at three time points (ZT3, ZT9, ZT15), and found that among the top 10,000 genes, 609 genes (about 6%; Supplementary Data 1) showed differential expression (over two-fold change, p < 0.05; Fig. 1A). Functional analysis revealed the rhythmic genes are involved in pathways including PI3K-AKT signaling, MAPK signaling, TGF-β signaling, and circadian rhythm (Fig. 1B). In addition, we found that the flow rate of saliva showed diurnal change during the day with higher rate at night (ZT15), which is consistent with the fact that mice are more active during the night (Fig. 1C). Thus, the submandibular salivary gland function, as judged by gene expression and saliva flow rate, is subject to circadian regulation.

The rhythmic expression of key circadian genes including Bmal1, Nr1d1, Dbp, and Per3 was further verified by qRT-PCR, which showed significant diurnal variations (Fig. 1D). It is noticeable that these genes peaked at different time points during the day. The rhythmic expression of the three key transcription factors BMAL1, NR1D1, and DBP was further examined at the protein level by western blot (Fig. 1E) and immunohistochemistry (Fig. 1F), all showed significant variations during the day. Together, it appears that the mouse submandibular salivary gland function is regulated by the circadian clock, which is consistent with previous studies24–29.

Key circadian transcription factors regulate gene expression in the salivary gland

The transcriptional output of the circadian clock is regulated by key transcription factors including CLOCK/BMAL1, ROR/NR1D1, and DBP, which bind to the cis-regulatory elements E-box, RRE and D-box, respectively18–21. To evaluate to what extent the rhythmic gene expression in the submandibular salivary gland is controlled by these core transcription factors, we profiled the genomic binding of BMAL1, NR1D1 and DBP by using the CUT-tag method which is sensitive and requires minimal amount of tissue samples33,34.

We found that the three transcription factors were enriched in the proximal promoter regions of target genes across the whole genome (within ±2 kb of the transcription starting sites, TSS; Fig. 2A). In addition, motif analysis confirmed the binding sequences were in agreement with previous reports18–21 (Fig. 2B). Combining peak location and gene annotation, we found that 7012 genes had BMAL1-binding peaks, 4389 genes had NR1D1-binding peaks, and 6166 genes had DBP-binding peaks. In total, 9144 genes had binding peaks for at least one of the three factors. For the top 10,000 genes identified by RNA-seq, 1862 (18.5%) genes had binding peaks for all three factors, and 5393 (54%) genes had binding peaks for at least one of the three factors (Fig. 2C). For the 609 rhythmic genes, 128 (21%) genes had binding peaks for all three factors, and 374 (61.4%) genes had binding peaks for at least one of the three factors, whereas the other 235 genes did not have binding sites for any of these factors thus may be regulated through indirect mechanisms (Fig. 2D, E). Comparing the BMAL1 binding peaks within the mouse submandibular salivary gland and the mouse liver35, there were 1188 common genes that had BMAL1 binding peaks, corresponding to 17% total genes in the salivary gland and 50% in the liver, and the rest were unique to each organ (Fig. 2F), suggesting organ-specific regulation.

Overall, we found that (1) the binding of key circadian transcription factors does not necessarily lead to rhythmic gene expression, rather, a major portion of these genes are transcriptionally inactive; (2) only a portion of the rhythmic genes in the salivary gland may be subject to direct regulation by the key circadian transcription factors, whereas the others are regulated through indirect mechanisms; and (3) there are unique and common target genes for the core circadian transcription factors among different organs.

Regulation of key circadian genes in the salivary gland

The complex inter-regulation of key circadian genes forms the base of the oscillatory network that manifests as the circadian clock. Extensive effort has been made to develop mathematical models that recapitulate details of this regulation36–38. Based on the genome-wide binding of key transcription factors, we found that there was indeed extensive cross-regulation of the circadian genes. For instance, there were BMAL1, NR1D1 and DBP peaks within the Bmal1, Nr1d1 and Dbp genes (Fig. 3A). These factors also bond to, thus may regulate the expression of Clock, Cry2, and Per3 genes (Fig. 3A). We proposed a model that defines the cross-regulation of key circadian genes in the salivary gland (Fig. 3B).

Regulation of key circadian and functional genes by BMAL1, NR1D1 and DBP.

B Putative regulatory map of key circadian genes as derived from the analysis of transcription factor binding. C Binding of the transcription factors BMAL1, NR1D1 and DBP in the proximal promoter regions of key functional genes in the submandibular salivary gland. D Putative regulation of key functional genes by the circadian transcription factors.

Previous reports suggested that many important functional genes in the salivary gland are regulated by the circadian clock26–28, yet the exact mechanism of regulation remains unclear. Here, we mapped the binding peaks of BMAL1, NR1D1 and DBP on some of the key functional genes including Aqp5, Amy1, Ano1, Egf, Cst3 and Muc1326–28. We found that at least one of the three transcription factors had binding peaks within the proximal promoter regions of these genes (Fig. 3C), suggesting direct regulation by the core circadian clock (Fig. 3D). Together, our data suggest that profiling the genomic binding of key transcription factors offers a powerful tool to dissect the gene regulatory module within an organ.

Ionizing radiation disrupts the circadian rhythmic transcription in the salivary gland

Previous research has mainly focused on tailoring radiotherapy according to the circadian clock to increase its efficacy in cancer treatment and reduce its toxicity. This has led to the concept of chronotherapy30–32. It remains unexplored whether radiotherapy would disrupt the circadian clock. Here, we profiled gene expression within the mouse submandibular salivary gland at 6 hr and 24 hr post-IR, and found that Bmal1, Nr1d1 and Dbp all showed differential expression (Fig. 4A). In total, 91 genes were up-regulated, and 61 genes were down-regulated at 6 hr post-IR, whereas 361 genes were up-regulated, and 1426 genes were down-regulated at 24 hr post-IR (Fig. 4B). Pathway analysis of the differentially expressed genes revealed activation of the p53 signaling pathway, mucin type O-glycan biosynthesis, PI3K-Akt pathway, AMPK pathway, and the circadian rhythm (Fig. 4C). The perturbed expression of Bmal1, Nr1d1, and Dbp was further verified by qRT-PCR analysis (Fig. 4D). In addition, salivary gland hypofunction was revealed by monitoring the saliva flow rate at 1 day, 3-day, 1 week, 2 weeks, and 1 month after a single dose of 5 Gy IR (Fig. 4E). Consistently, the expression of key functional genes in the salivary gland including Aqp5, Amy1, Ano1 and Cst3 all showed reduced expression post-IR (Fig. 4F). These data suggested that IR perturbs the circadian clock within the salivary gland, which may contribute to its hypofunction.

p53 and ROS regulate key circadian genes post-IR

We wondered how IR perturbs the circadian rhythmic transcription within the salivary gland. The well-established down-stream events post-IR include p53 activation and the induction of ROS39–41. We first examined the impact of p53 knockout (KO) on the expression of key circadian genes (Fig. 5A). A single dose of 5 Gy IR induced the expression of Cdkn1a (p21) and Bax, which are well-known downstream targets of p53 (Fig. 5B). Instead of suppression, Bmal1 level was significant increase in p53−/− mice at 6 hr post-IR. Nr1d1 expression was also increased in p53−/− mice, whereas the induction of Dbp expression was abolished (Fig. 5C). These data suggested that p53 signaling has a major impact on the regulation of circadian gene expression.

Tea polyphenol (TP) is a natural anti-oxidant that has been shown to reduce IR-induced tissue damage through its anti-oxidant and anti-inflammatory properties6,42. Here, we found that upon TP injection, the expression levels of Stat1 and Gpx4, which are related to cytokine signaling and anti-oxidant responses, were reduced at 6 hr post-IR (Fig. 5E). The expression of Bmal1 was restored as compared to the no-treatment group (Fig. 5F). TP treatment rescued the expression of both Nr1d1 and Dbp (Fig. 5F). We further confirmed that TP treatment effectively reduced the ROS level in the salivary gland cells post-IR (Supplementary Fig. S1). Thus, the perturbation of key circadian genes post-IR is related to p53 activation and ROS production.

Rescue the salivary gland hypofunction by manipulating the circadian clock

We then explored whether it is possible to improve the salivary gland function post-IR through manipulating the circadian clock. To this end, we tested a few small molecule chemical agents that have been shown to improve the circadian rhythm. Nobiletin is a natural product that enhances the amplitude of the clock43,44. We found that while a single feeding of 100 mg/kg Nobiletin did not show beneficial effect, a single feeding of 150 mg/kg Nobiletin improved the salivary gland function post-IR (Fig. 6A). Similarly, continuous daily feeding of Nobiletin at 50 mg/kg for a week also improved saliva production post-IR (Fig. 6A). Consistently, continuous daily feeding of SR1078, which is another clock-enhancer45, improved salivary gland function (Fig. 6B). In contrast, a 100 mg/kg single dose or 50 mg/kg daily feeding of Genistein, a natural isoflavone with anti-inflammation and anti-oxidant properties, showed no beneficial effect (Supplementary Fig. S2). Nobiletin or SR1078 alone did not impact saliva production in normal mice without irradiation (Supplementary Fig. S2), and Nobiletin itself is not as effective as tea polyphenol in reducing the ROS level in the salivary gland cells (Supplementary Fig. S1). Together, it appears that the salivary gland function can be improved by manipulating the circadian clock function.

We then monitored the gene expression change in the salivary gland post-IR after Nobiletin rescue. Although it appears the expression of Bmal1 showed minimal improvement, the expression of Nr1d1, Dbp and Per3 all improved (Fig. 6C). In addition, the expression of key functional genes Aqp5, Amy1, Ano1 and Cst3 were also improved after Nobiletin rescue (Fig. 6D). Finally, we found that the expression of IL-6 which mediates IR-induced cell senescence in the salivary gland post-IR showed no improvement (Fig. 6E). p21 (Cdkn1a), which is a down-stream target gene of p53 and involved in cell senescence, also showed no improvement (Fig. 6E). Consistently, cell senescence as judged by β-galactosidase staining at 2 weeks post-IR showed no improvement after Nobiletin rescue (Fig. 6F). Thus, Nobiletin did not rescue the cell senescence effect induced by inflammation/cytokine in the salivary gland12,15, suggesting the circadian clock is an independent parameter in the regulation of salivary gland function post-IR (Fig. 6G).

Discussion

In this work, we firstly explored the extent and scope of circadian clock regulation of the salivary gland function, and found that about 6% of the transcriptome showed rhythmic expression. Key circadian genes including Bmal1, Nr1d1 and Dbp, as well as functional genes such as Aqp5, Amy1, Ano1 and Cst3 all showed rhythmic expression. For comparison, about 12% of the transcriptome in the mouse liver46–48, 24% in the cornea49, and 3.9% in the cartilage50,51 are regulated by the circadian clock.

Among the rhythmic genes in the salivary gland, 61.4% had binding peaks for at least one of the three key transcription factors BMAL1, NR1D1 and DBP, which corresponded to the core cis-elements E-box, RRE, and D-box18–21. The rest of these genes are most likely indirectly regulated, i.e. through metabolic and other relevant factors52,53. In addition, among the 9144 genes that had binding peaks for at least one of the three key transcription factors, only 54% were expressed at significant levels in the mouse submandibular salivary gland. Thus, the regulation of circadian genes is complex and involves direct and indirect mechanisms. These data are consistent with previous reports showing that the core circadian clock transcription factors cooperate with additional factors to determine gene expression35,52–54. Our proposed regulatory module is corroborated by a report in cell culture where NR1D is involved in regulating DBP expression55. Defining the rhythmic genes at the whole transcriptome scale, and profiling gene regulation by key circadian transcription factors offer fundamental insights into the regulation of salivary gland function by the circadian clock.

Probably the most interesting discovery in this work is that IR disrupts the circadian rhythmic transcription in the mouse submandibular salivary gland. Previous studies have focused on chronotherapy, whereby chemotherapy or radiotherapy is administered at specific times of day to improve efficacy30–32. The idea is to cooperate the drug effect with the body’s overall metabolism and physiology. However, it is unclear whether radiotherapy might affect the circadian clock, although IR, UV, and chemotherapy have been shown to shift the clock56–59. Here, we found that the key circadian genes Bmal1, Nr1d1, and Dbp all showed differential expression at 6 h and 24 h post-IR, together with the perturbed expression of downstream functional genes. We further showed that this perturbation is related to p53 signaling and increased ROS. How exactly this happens still awaits further investigation – p53 may function as a transcription factor and directly regulates target gene expression; other indirect mechanisms are also possible. Nonetheless, our data raised an important issue for consideration in chronotherapy.

Finally, we found that by manipulating the circadian clock, the salivary gland function can be improved. There has been considerable effort in recent years to develop methods to manipulate the circadian clock, with novel chemical compounds that might be used to treat cancer and metabolic disorders30–32,60. Nobiletin is a natural product that improves the circadian rhythm and has been shown to be beneficial for metabolic disorders and cancer17,43,44. Here, we show that both Nobiletin and SR1078 - another small-molecule agent that improves circadian rhythms - are beneficial for salivary gland function following IR. They both target RORα/γ, key molecules involved in stabilizing the feedback loop of the circadian clock16–21. Interestingly, the rescue effect of Nobiletin is independent of cell senescence, an important contributor of salivary gland hypofunction post-IR12,15. This was confirmed by SA-β-Gal staining, and by the unmodified expression of key senescence-related molecules p21 and IL-6, an inflammatory cytokine. Thus, the circadian clock is an independent contributor for IR-induced salivary gland hypofunction. Although a previous report claimed that the saliva amount is higher in Per2 KO mice26, it is possible that a functional circadian clock is beneficial for the organ in the long term. The therapeutic benefits of modulating the circadian clock in xerostomia after radiotherapy warrants further exploration.

Methods

Experimental animal

All experimental protocols were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Shenzhen University. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. The wild type C57BL/6 mice were purchased from the Gempharmatech Co, Foshan, Guangdong, China. The p53−/− mice were purchased from the JaxMice (# 002101) and genotyped accordingly61. Mice were housed in the Shenzhen University experimental animal facility with free access to food and water under a 12/12 diurnal condition (7:00 am to 7:00 pm).

Irradiation

Male mice aged 2–3 months were irradiated with a RS-200 RAD Source X-ray irradiator (Rad Source Tech, Buford, GA, USA). Briefly, mice were anesthetized with intraperitoneal injection of xylazine hydrochloride at 25 mg/kg body weight (Huamu, Jilin, China). The abdomen and the rest of the body were protected with a 1 cm-thick lead cover; the head region was subject to 5 Gy irradiation at a dose rate of 1.67 Gy/min. The mice were irradiated at 10:00 am (ZT3). For unirradiated control group, the mice were sham treated but not irradiated.

Saliva flow rate

Mice were anesthetized and intraperitoneally injected with pilocarpine at 2 mg/kg body weight (stock solution in phosphate buffer saline 0.5 mg/ml; MCE, # HY-B0726A). The saliva was collected with a pipette from the mouse within 15 min after pilocarpine injection and weighted to determine the flow rate. After collection, the mice were given 500 μL water and returned to the animal facility. For saliva collection at ZT15 (10:00 pm), mice were moved out from the animal room, operated, and returned afterwards.

Drug treatment

Tea polyphenol (Meilunbio, # A0405B; 25 mg/kg intraperitoneal injection), Nobiletin (Meilunbio, # 478-01-3; 50/100/150 mg/kg per oral), Genistein (Meilunbio, # D1101A; 50/100 mg/kg per oral), SR1078 (Macklin, # S864105; 50/75 mg/kg per oral) were dissolved in DMSO as stocks and diluted in PBS before delivery. These drugs were delivered before (for tea polyphenol) or after IR to examine the rescue effect on gene expression and/or saliva production.

RNA-sequencing and data processing

Total RNA extraction was performed according to the manufacturer’s instruction using the RNAex Pro Reagent (Agbio, # A2A1625). RNA-sequencing and data processing has described previously62. Briefly, RNA quality was verified using the Agilent 2100 machine. Samples were sequenced using the Gene-plus 200 pair-end 150 model (Geneplus, Shenzhen, China). After quality check by FastQC, data were mapped onto the mm10 mouse genome using the Tophat program, and rawcount/FPKM were calculated using the HTseq-count program. Differential gene expression was determined using the Cuffdiff program. KEGG pathway analysis was performed (www.genome.jp/kegg) and plotted using the ggplot2 function in the R program. For differential gene expression, a filter of two-fold change with p value less than 0.05 was applied.

CUT-tag and data processing

The detailed method for CUT-tag has been described previously62. Briefly, fresh mouse submandibular salivary gland was minced in PBS and digested using collagenase IV (5 mg/ml) in PBS, 37 °C for 30 min. After filtering the lysate through a 50 μm cell strainer, another round of digestion was performed using the 0.25% Trypsin/EDTA solution for 10 min, 37 °C. The single cell suspension was then centrifuged and washed with PBS. The following kits were purchased from Vanzym, Nanjing, China: VAHTS DNA clean beads (# N411-01), Hyperactive in-situ Chip library prep kit for Illumina (# TD901-01/02), Trueprep Index kit V2 for Illumina (# TD202) and the manufacturer’s guide were followed. The antibodies used include BMAL1 (Proteintech, # 12662-1-AP), NR1D1 (Proteintech, # 14268-1-AP), DBP (Proteintech, # 14506-1-AP). The sequencing data was mapped to the mouse reference genome mm10 using the Bowtie2 program and transformed into bam file using samtools. Peak analysis was performed using the MACS2 progam with a p-value 0.001, model fold 5–50. Peak position was defined using the BETA program and visualized using the IGV 8.2 program. Binding sites analysis was performed using the top 1000 genes and subject to the MEME-chip program and compared with the reference sequence using the compare matrices in the RSAT website.

Protein extraction from the mouse submandibular salivary gland

Mice were sacrificed with dislodging and the submandibular salivary gland was exposed after cutting open the neck skin. The salivary gland was then cut out and washed with cold PBS once, and minced with a scissor. The tissue was then homogenized in a Servicebio CR2103108 machine using the RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio, # R0020; contains 1% protease inhibitor [ApexBio, # K1007], 1% phosphatase inhibitor [Meilunbio, # MB12707], and 1% Triton X-100 [Diamond, # A110694-0100]). The homogenized tissue was then centrifuged at 10000 rpm for 5–10 min and mixed with 5X loading buffer and boiled at 100 °C for 3–5 min.

qPCR, histology, immunohistochemistry and western blot

These experiments were performed using standard protocols. Primer sequences were listed in Supplementary Data 2. Antibodies used: GAPDH (Proteintech, # 60004-1-Ig), BMAL1 (Proteintech, # 12662-1-AP), NR1D1 (Proteintech, # 14268-1-AP), DBP (Proteintech, # 14506-1-AP).

ROS measurement

Salivary glands were collected at designated time points and digested with collagenase I to obtain single cell suspension. Cells were loaded with 10 μM DCFH-DA (# S0033S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 20 min at 37 °C and washed with PBS for three times. Cells were analyzed on a flow cytometer (BD FACSAria III) using the GFP channel. Cells were pre-treated with 0.3% H2O2 for 30 min as a positive control.

SA-β-gal staining

Fresh mouse submandibular salivary gland was collected and cleaned in PBS to remove the attached peripheral tissue under a dissection microscope. The tissue was then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS pH 7.4 at 4 °C overnight. The staining solution contains 2 mM MgCl2, 5 mM potassium ferricyanide, 5 mM potassium ferrocyanide, 1 mg/ml X-gal in PBS pH 7.4. The staining was performed at 37 °C for overnight63. After staining, samples were washed with PBS twice, dehydrated in an alcohol gradient, mounted in paraffin and sectioned at 8 μm. Sections were further counter-stained with eosin, mounted and photographed.

Statistics and reproducibility

All experiments were repeated at least three times. At least three mice were used for each experiment. GraphPad Prism 6 was used to visualize the data and perform statistical analysis. The heatmap was generated by the pheatmap script in the R (64-bit, version 3.43) program. Rhythmic gene expression was determined by the comprehensive value p < 0.0535. Normal distribution was assumed for all data, and unpaired Student’s t-test was applied to calculate the p value. One-way ANOVA was performed when multiple comparisons were necessary. p < 0.05 was considered significant. Error bars: mean ± SD.

The RNA-seq and Cut-tag data were deposited in the NCBI database (GSE 234789). Supplementary Data 1 contains a list of the rhythmic genes; Supplementary Data 2 contains the primer sequences; Supplementary Data 3 contains the source data. Uncropped gel images are supplied in Supplementary Fig. S3.
