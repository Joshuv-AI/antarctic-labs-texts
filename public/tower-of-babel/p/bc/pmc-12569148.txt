Turkish freshwaters as a case of rising invasion risk of aquarium fishes under climate change
Authors: Baran Yoğurtçuoğlu, Ali Serhan Tarkan, F. Güler Ekmekçi, Şerife Gülsün Kırankaya, Irmak Kurtul, Derya Özcan, Onur Karadal, Fikret Öndes, Gürel Türkmen, Hasan M. Sarı, Phillip J. Haubrock, Lorenzo Vilizzi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Climate change is expected to increase the invasiveness of non-native aquarium fish, yet national assessments rarely consider future warming conditions. We screened 46 popular ornamental freshwater fish imported into Türkiye for invasion risk. Each species received a Basic Risk Assessment (BRA) score under current conditions and an adjusted Climate Change Assessment (CCA) score under regional warming scenarios. The BRA classified 12 species (26.1%) as high risk, while under the CCA, this rose to 16 species (34.8%). Four species, namely goldfish Carassius auratus, common carp Cyprinus carpio, vermiculated sailfin catfish Pterygoplichthys disjunctivus, and leopard pleco P. gibbiceps, reached very high-risk status. Climate change adjustment resulted in an increase in the basic scores for 34 species, a decrease for three, and no change for nine. Seven species achieved the maximum increment of + 12 under predicted climate change conditions. Our results show that marginal habitats, such as thermal springs and effluent canals, could become suitable for these species, even as water stress in Central Anatolia threatens both native and invasive species. We recommend dynamic, climate-aware risk protocols, combined with empirical validation via field and eDNA monitoring, and a precautionary ‘whitelist’ import system based on robust risk screening protocols as applied in this study.

Introduction

The global spread of aquatic invasive species is a critical concern for biodiversity and the environment1,2. The human-mediated transport of aquatic organisms through shipping, trade and deliberate introductions has resulted in thousands of species being moved beyond their native range, often with devastating consequences3,4. One of the main vectors is the ballast-water transfer in ships, which has been extensively investigated and has received considerable management attention5,6. Other important vectors include intentional and unintentional stockings, range expansions (e.g. Ponto-Caspian gobies), releases as live bait by recreational anglers7, and escapes from aquaculture facilities8,9.

Similarly, the global ornamental pet trade has emerged as a major and increasingly recognised pathway for the introduction of freshwater fishes, invertebrates and plants10,11. These introductions frequently occur as a result of the release of unwanted organisms, which typically happens when individuals either outgrow their holding facilities or when owners lose interest12,13. The practice of ornamental fish keeping has a history that dates back over a millennium14, yet it was the advent of glass aquaria and the subsequent development of air transport that facilitated the rapid growth of a global trade in ornamental fish3,11. As a result, the industry has grown into a multi-billion-dollar sector. By 2016, it had expanded at an average annual rate of 14% since the 1970s, involving the global circulation of over one billion fish comprising more than 5,300 species15. More recent data confirm that this upward trend has continued: the global ornamental fish market was valued at approximately USD 5.88 billion in 2022 and is projected to reach USD 11.30 billion by 2030, growing at an annual rate of 8.5% between 2023 and 2030. This growth may have been further accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic, as lockdowns and increased time spent at home reportedly led to a surge in pet ownership, including ornamental fish, particularly in the United States16. However, the continued growth of this trade has also raised ecological concerns, as the release of ornamental species into non-native environments poses serious risks to biodiversity and ecosystem integrity. Invasive ornamentals may outcompete native species for resources, disrupt food webs, degrade habitats, and hybridize with native species, leading to loss of genetic diversity and the destabilization of ecosystem functions17.

Considering the threat posed by the global ornamental fish trade, which introduces resilient, high-risk species into new environments, decision-support tools that consider future climate dynamics, such as the Aquatic Species Invasiveness Screening Kit (AS-ISK), are becoming increasingly vital. Recent regional and global studies using the AS-ISK have already identified numerous aquarium fish as potential invaders as waters warm up 18–21.

While these phenomena are evident on a global scale, they also have an impact at a national level, including in regions of high biodiversity and ecological vulnerability, such as Türkiye. The country’s location at the intersection of Europe, the Levant, the Caucasus, the Caspian, and the Gulf, coupled with its wide range of freshwater habitats from the cold headwaters to the warm basins of the Mediterranean and the numerous karstic springs and lakes has resulted in unique ichthyofaunal diversity and endemic species. This diversity, however, is increasingly exposed to biological invasions. Recent reviews have identified 34 non-native freshwater fish species, of which approximately 19 are now self-sustaining22. The successful establishment of invasive species such as tilapias (redbelly tilapia Coptodon zilli and blue tilapia Oreochromis aureus), eastern mosquitofish Gambusia holbrooki, pumpkinseed Lepomis gibbosus and stone moroko Pseudorasbora parva suggests the susceptibility of Turkish freshwater ecosystems to biological invasions, while the aquarium pathway remains comparatively understudied. New evidence indicates that ornamental releases are a significant cause of introductions. Several aquarium species, including armoured catfishes (Pterygoplichthys spp), guppy Poecilia reticulata, green swordtail Xiphophorus helleri and southern platyfish X. maculatus as well as the convict cichlid Amatitlania nigrofasciata have already formed breeding populations in Turkish freshwaters23–27. This tendency is also shown by frequent records of goldfish Carassius auratus from natural habitats; however, true distribution of this species is obscured by widespread hybridisation within the C. auratus complex (including C. auratus, gibel carp C. gibelio, and ginbuna C. langsdorfii) as well as common misidentifications22. Additional occasional detections of giant catfish Pangasius sanitwongsei and red piranha Pygocentrus nattereri suggest ongoing propagule pressure, even when establishment has not yet occurred28.

Türkiye is located within the Mediterranean climate-change hotspot, and is projected to warm faster than the global mean29,30. Multi-model assessments indicate increases in mean temperature of approximately 1.5–3 °C by mid-century and 3–5 °C by late century, depending on the emissions pathway (SSP2-4.5 to SSP5-8.5), alongside more frequent and intense heatwaves31. Summer precipitation is projected to decline, especially in the Aegean and Mediterranean basins, with a marked increase in drought frequency and duration; at the same time, short-duration extreme rainfall is expected to intensify even where seasonal totals decrease 32,33. These signals imply rising water stress nationwide and changing thermal conditions in rivers, reservoirs and springs. These conditions increase the potential habitat of warm-adapted non-native fish.

On the other hand, recent trade data reveal the magnitude and ecological extent of this propagule pressure. In 2025, Turkish imports of live freshwater ornamental fishes amounted to 82,231 kg, with a commercial value of 116,000 USD. The majority of these fish were sourced from tropical or subtropical regions, including Indonesia, Iran, Malaysia, Sri Lanka, and Thailand. Additional deliveries were received from Colombia, Nigeria, and Vietnam, in addition to temperate-origin imports from the USA (Fig. 1). Among the species imported, or even cultivated within the country, cichlids, Carassius auratus and Poecilia reticulata constitute the dominant groups, reflecting their popularity and adaptability in hobbyist markets. These import patterns, which extend across both warm- and cold-adapted taxa, indicate the necessity of evaluating all traded species under both current and projected climate scenarios. Building on this regional context, a growing body of work has demonstrated the value of risk-screening frameworks for fish in the aquarium trade elsewhere, but Türkiye remains largely unexamined. In North America, for example, Stecker et al. 11surveyed tropical species in Pacific Northwest aquarium shops and found that several were capable of overwintering in temperate habitats. Similarly, European screening studies identified a significant proportion of traded species as being at moderate to high risk in Greece34. Other screenings from the USA 35,36 and a global AS-ISK application21 have also revealed that numerous freshwater fish species, including ornamental, are at ‘medium’ to ‘very high risk’, particularly under projected warming conditions. However, Turkish assessments are based on earlier protocols 37 and do not consider climate integration. Even in more recent applications of the latest AS-ISK protocol38, little to no assessment has been conducted on emerging aquarium introductions or ornamental species. Consequently, the invasion risks posed by these species, particularly under future climate scenarios, remain unquantified. Beyond the national case, this approach can be applied to temperate–subtropical transition zones by embedding standardised future climate scenarios in trait- and evidence-based screening, which reshapes species rankings by increasing suitability for thermophilic taxa. Since warming enhances overwinter survival, accelerates life histories and broadens thermal performance ranges, climate-adjusted scores provide earlier and more accurate predictions than static screens. Tools such as AS-ISK + CCA should form the basis of practices for aquarium trade species and other pathways in regions experiencing rapid warming.

The purpose of this study is to address the above gaps by risk screening all popular aquarium fishes imported to Türkiye. The objectives are twofold: firstly, to assign to each species an invasion risk category and evaluate the efficacy of AS-ISK to distinguish between medium- and high-risk species; and secondly, to assess the impact of projected climate warming, in accordance with standard climate change scenarios, on the risk scores and risk ranks of the screened species. It is hypothesized that a significant proportion of tropical and subtropical aquarium fishes will be classified as low or medium risk, but that the inclusion of climate change will result in an elevated risk level for those species adapted to warm waters. The findings can assist in the prioritization of monitoring efforts, the refinement of species watch lists, and the development of regulations concerning the aquarium trade and fish importation. Overall, the integration of climate change into risk screening provides a more solid foundation for the protection of Türkiye’s freshwater biodiversity in an era of rapid environmental change. Amongst the a priori non-invasive species, nine were false positives, and amongst the a priori invasive species, seven were true positives. Of the 28 medium-risk species, 26 were a priori non-invasive and two invasive (Amatitlania nigrofasciata and Chindongo socolofi).

The highest-scoring (very high risk) species (based on an ad hoc threshold ≥ 30) were Carassius auratus, Cyprinus carpio, Pterygoplichthys disjunctivus, and Pterygoplichthys gibbiceps for both the BRA and BRA + CCA (Table 2; Fig. 2A & B). Overall, the proportion of the (very) high-risk species increased from 26.7% to 35.6% after accounting for the CCA and that of the low-risk species decreased from 11.1% to 2.2%. Discussion

The findings of this study demonstrate a broadly applicable result: the integration of future-climate scenarios into screening leads to a substantial shift in invasion risk profiles. A significant proportion of the ornamental freshwater fish imported into Türkiye was identified as potentially invasive, with more than one quarter ranked as high risk under current conditions. This proportion rises to more than one third when future climate change scenarios are considered. Regardless of changes in their risk ranking, climate-integrated assessments (cf. CCA) increased the BRA scores for approximately 74% of the screened species. As temperate regions warm, they are expected to become more compatible with the native ranges of tropical species, resulting in their increased persistence and a greater potential for invasiveness. This projected shift in climatic suitability is consistent with the broader scientific consensus that rising water temperatures and associated climate change will facilitate the establishment, spread, and ecological impact of non-native species39,40, particularly thermophilic species that are popular in the ornamental fish trade41.

Of the four species that achieved ‘very high risk’ status, Carassius auratus, Cyprinus carpio, and Pterygoplichthys disjunctivus have already established self-sustaining populations in Turkish freshwater ecosystems, which validates the predictive power of the screening protocol. Carassius auratus and Cyprinus carpio are globally recognized as typical invasive species, supported by a long history of human-mediated introductions and a suite of ecological traits that contribute to their invasion success 21,42. Both species possess broad thermal and physiological tolerances, generalist diets, and high reproductive output, and these traits are further amplified under warming conditions 43,44. In Türkiye, the invasion dynamics of C. auratus are complicated by widespread hybridization within the Carassius complex, which often leads to misidentification and underestimation of their ecological footprint22,45, a scenario with comparable consequences in North America46. These cyprinids are known to disrupt aquatic ecosystem dynamics through benthic foraging, competition, increasing turbidity, dislodgement of macrophytes, and alteration of nutrient cycling47. This causes a shift from clear-water systems towards degraded, phytoplankton-dominated states. Similarly, C. carpio has been widely introduced for aquaculture and angling, and feral populations are now supposed to exert significant pressure on native biota and habitats47,48. The suckermouth catfishes (Pterygoplichthys spp.) have gained popularity in the ornamental trade due to their ability to consume algae. They have adapted to disturbed environments through a combination of traits, including the capacity for air-breathing, a hardy exoskeleton that reduces predation risk, and burrow-nesting reproductive strategies 49,50. Their extensive excavation of riverbanks has been demonstrated to result in habitat degradation, accelerated erosion, and increased sedimentation. In Türkiye, the establishment of a feral Pterygoplichthys, including probable hybrids between P. disjunctivus and P. pardalis24, has already been documented. The invasiveness of these species has been further supported by previous screenings, which have identified Pterygoplichthys as high-risk invaders in Mexico 51, several European countries34,52, and Türkiye 37,38.

The Poeciliidae, which include numerous species of ornamental fish, represents another group with high invasive potential, particularly under future climate conditions. This study identified three species, namely Poecilia reticulata, Xiphophorus hellerii, and X. maculatus, as high risk, exhibiting significantly elevated risk scores under climate change scenarios. Like their worldwide invader relatives, Gambusia holbrooki and western mosquitofish G. affinis, these livebearing species possess a set of life-history traits including ovoviviparity, high reproductive rates, tolerance of poor water quality, and broad environmental plasticity that facilitate successful colonization and establishment53,54. A particularly remarkable trait is the capacity of females to store sperm for extended periods, enabling a single fertilized individual to establish a population independently55,56. The reproductive and physiological performance of these small-bodied tropical fishes is closely associated with ambient temperature 57,58; therefore, warming conditions in Turkish freshwaters are predicted to accelerate their maturation rate, reproductive frequency, and population growth, hence potentially promoting their dispersal. Of note, Poecilia sphenops, which was initially categorized as medium risk, shifted to high-risk status under climate-adjusted scenarios. This suggests that the wider poeciliid assemblage traded in the ornamental sector merits increased regulatory attention.

In addition to Poecilia sphenops, three other species (namely, Chromobotia macracanthus, Danio rerio, and Gymnocorymbus ternetzi) were reclassified from medium to high risk by achieving maximum CCA increments of + 12. Chromobotia macracanthus, a tropical species native to Indonesia, requires warm flowing water and exhibits thermal sensitivity during early development, with successful embryogenesis occurring only within a narrow thermal window (23.8–30.2 °C) 59. Despite its current absence from invasion records, its elevated risk score under warming scenarios may indicate the potential for future conditions to render Turkish habitats more suitable to the species’ reproduction and persistence. Native to South Asia, Danio rerio has broad experimental interest but also a reputation as an ‘easy fish’ among aquarists. Laboratory and field studies have shown that it can survive temperatures as low as 7.5 °C 60, but not prolonged exposure to 4 °C. Hence, warming may facilitate the species’ survival in thermally buffered habitats such as geothermal streams or aquaculture discharge zones in Turkish freshwaters. Gymnocorymbus ternetzi, conversely, is documented less frequently in extant invasion literature, and no invasion data are available.

A particular illustration of the analytical sophistication of the AS-ISK tool is demonstrated by the reclassification of Pangasianodon hypophthalmus from high to medium risk under climate-adjusted conditions. Contrary to the overall trend of elevated risk for tropical species, this downward adjustment exemplifies the tool’s capacity to consider complex life-history traits that extend beyond thermal tolerance. Although warming may improve physiological suitability, P. hypophthalmus is a potamodromous species whose reproduction is tightly linked to the seasonal monsoon-driven flood pulses of its native Mekong and Chao Phraya river basins61. The successful spawning of this species is determined by specific hydrological cues and habitat features62. However, these conditions are not present in the regulated river systems of Türkiye and the Mediterranean-type climate of the region. Consequently, despite the presence of optimal temperatures, the absence of essential reproductive conditions appears to impede its establishment potential. This conclusion can also be implicated the other catfish, Pangasius sanitwongsei, which has been occasionally reported from central Türkiye28.

Several tropical cichlids, including Andinoacara rivulatus, Astronotus ocellatus, Melanochromis auratus, and Rubricatochromis letourneuxi, were ranked as high risk under current and projected climate conditions. While not yet established in Turkish freshwaters, these species were reported as invasive elsewhere and exhibit traits commonly associated with invasiveness, such as a thermal preference for warm waters, aggressive territorial behaviour, and broad omnivorous diets63–66. Their prevalence in the ornamental fish trade, combined with the ecological success of established cichlid species such as Amatitlania nigrofasciata, Coptodon zillii and Oreochromis aureus, indicates the potential for further introductions and establishment. The current distribution of C. zillii and O. aureus, particularly in Mediterranean coastal areas and brackish environments, suggests the necessity of targeted monitoring and management in these vulnerable regions.

The categorisation of nine a priori non-invasive species as high-risk under the BRA + CCA (five in BRA) scenario does not necessarily indicate misclassification by the AS-ISK tool. Rather, it may indicate the conservative nature of the screening protocol. By prioritising caution, the AS-ISK tool is designed to overestimate rather than underestimate risk, especially when species exhibit traits commonly associated with invasiveness, such as high fecundity, trophic generalism, rapid growth, wide environmental tolerances or documented invasions elsewhere. These traits are well-established proxies for invasion potential66–69, so the tool rightly assigns higher scores to such species, even if they have not yet become invasive in Türkiye. It is important to note that the absence of observed invasiveness in Türkiye for these species may be due to context-specific ecological constraints that are not captured by the AS-ISK protocol. Furthermore, some species may be disproportionately well studied (e.g. Danio rerio), resulting in the identification and scoring of numerous high-risk traits. However, their absence from Türkiye could be due to subtle ecological mismatches or unforeseen barriers that are not considered by global-scale tools such as AS-ISK. These outcomes also reflect the limitations of a priori categorisations, which may be based on incomplete invasion databases or conservative definitions of invasiveness. As noted by Yoğurtçuoğlu et al.70, species absent from databases such as EICAT may be categorised as ‘non-invasive’ a priori; when AS-ISK subsequently scores them as high risk, this results in false positives. Conversely, the single false negative in the present study, Chindongo socolofi, being classified as low risk despite being categorised as invasive a priori, is more problematic and can lead to regulatory oversight and missed opportunities for early intervention. However, the most plausible explanation for this false negative outcome is data deficiency.

Turkish freshwater ecosystems face unique challenges from the introduction of ornamental fish, which are intensified by climate change and trade dynamics. Although generalised climate matching models (applied in CCA) suggest an increased potential for invasion by tropical species, microhabitat variability, for example, geothermal springs, may alter these outcomes. Most ornamental fish imports originate from tropical regions such as Indonesia, Malaysia, Sri Lanka and Thailand (see Fig. 1), with Iran acting as a key transit hub due to tax advantages. This obscures the true origins of species and complicates the implementation of biosecurity measures. While the current establishment is limited to thermal refugia, our climate assessments show that maximum CCA score increments of + 12 could expand the suitable habitat. However, this projection requires critical contextualisation. Türkiye is ranked 32nd globally for water stress by the World Resources Institute and faces severe water scarcity projections71. The closed drainages of Central Anatolia, which support high levels of endemic freshwater fish, will experience dramatic habitat loss due to declining water levels and increasing salinity71,72. Therefore, although the mid-term establishment potential of tropical ornamentals may increase, their long-term dispersal capacity will be severely limited by the disappearance of aquatic habitats as dispersal routes. Despite the decline in ornamental trade volumes reported by stakeholders, the threat to Turkish freshwater ecosystems, particularly in thermal springs, remains significant. The current regulatory framework appears to be inadequate in considering the established populations of multiple high-risk species. However, recent initiatives by the ministry to incorporate risk screening tools hold promise.

The findings of this study demonstrate that climate change can significantly elevate invasion risks, increasing the proportion of high-risk species. Consequently, static risk assessments are inadequate in identifying future threats, particularly in temperate-tropical transition zones such as Türkiye. The urgent requirement for dynamic risk models updated with real-time climate and trade data is widely acknowledged. A periodic re-evaluation mechanism for traded species, aligned with updated climate and ecological data, is essential. Therefore, empirical validation through field monitoring, eDNA, and citizen science can improve early detection. On the other hand, the Turkish authorities should adopt a whitelist system that permits only low-risk species after thorough AS-ISK screening, transferring the burden of proof to importers, as has been successfully implemented in Australia and New Zealand73.

Methods

In total, 46 non-native ornamental fish species, belonging to 12 families and 39 genera and currently present in the Turkish aquarium trade, were selected for risk screening to evaluate their potential invasiveness in Turkish freshwater ecosystems, hereafter referred to as the ‘risk assessment area’ (Table 1). The species were selected based on direct engagement with key stakeholders in the ornamental fish sector, including consultations with major pet importers and aquarium fish traders across the country. This approach aimed to identify species that are actively traded, circulated, or maintained within the national market. While not strictly quantitative, this method aligns with established biosecurity assessment practices that incorporate expert opinion and market research to identify priority taxa when specific trade data are unavailable. The selected species therefore provide a realistic cross-section of non-native ornamental fish with the potential to be released or escape into natural freshwater ecosystems of Türkiye.

Of these 46 species, seven are established in the risk assessment area, 36 are not yet recorded in the wild, and three are recorded in the wild but without successful establishment (Table 1). Screenings were conducted independently by AST, BY, FÖ, FGE, IK, OK, and ŞGK, with each assessor screening a subset of the total number of species. Notably, the screening of an entire set of species for a certain risk assessment area by more than one independent assessor has been shown to provide no advantage in terms of increased level of confidence (see below) compared to screenings carried out by the same assessors on subsets of the total number of species. This approach, which has been followed in this study, has important implications in terms of allocation of resources and cost-benefit analysis74. All screenings were carried out under the supervision of LV, who was responsible for quality control of the biological and ecological data and of the methodological (evidence-based) aspects of the study.

Risk identification was undertaken using the Aquatic Species Invasiveness Screening Kit74–76, which is currently available for download in its v2.4.1 at https://tinyurl.com/ISK-toolkits. This taxon-generic decision-support tool consists of 55 questions: the first 49 comprise the Basic Risk Assessment (BRA) and address the biogeography/invasion history and biology/ecology of the species under screening; the last 6 questions comprise the Climate Change Assessment (CCA) and require the assessor to predict how future predicted climatic conditions are likely to affect the BRA with respect to risks of introduction, establishment, dispersal and impact.

To achieve a valid screening, the assessor must provide for each question a response, a level of confidence for the response (see below), and a justification based on literature sources. The outcomes are a BRA score and a (composite) BRA + CCA score, which is obtained after adding or subtracting up to 12 points to the BRA score or leaving it unchanged in case of a CCA score equal to 0. Scores < 1 suggest that the species poses a ‘low risk’ to become invasive in the risk assessment area, whereas scores > 1 indicate a ‘medium risk’ or a ‘high risk’. The threshold (Thr) value to distinguish between medium-risk (BRA and BRA + CCA scores < Thr) and high-risk (BRA and BRA + CCA scores ≥ Thr) species for the risk assessment area is obtained by ‘calibration’ based on Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis (see Vilizzi et al.74). The discriminatory power of the analysis is defined by the Area Under the Curve (AUC) measured as: 0.7 ≤ AUC < 0.8 = acceptable, 0.8 ≤ AUC < 0.9 = excellent, 0.9 ≤ AUC = outstanding 77. For the species classified as high risk, a further distinction was made of the ‘very high risk’ species based on an ad hoc threshold weighted according to the range of high-risk score values obtained for the BRA and BRA + CCA. Identification of the (very) high-risk species is useful to prioritise allocation of resources in view of a full risk assessment74.

For ROC curve analysis to be implemented, the species selected for screening must be categorised a priori as ‘non-invasive’ or ‘invasive’ using literature sources. The a priori categorisation of species was implemented as per Vilizzi et al.74 (Table 1). where CLQi is the CL for Qi, 4 is the maximum achievable value for confidence (i.e. very high: see above) and 55 is the total number of questions comprising the AS-ISK questionnaire74. The CF ranges from a minimum of 0.25 (i.e. all 55 questions with confidence level equal to 1) to a maximum of 1 (i.e. all 55 questions with confidence level equal to 4). Based on all 55 Qs of the AS-ISK questionnaire, the 49 Qs comprising the BRA and the six Qs comprising the CCA, the CFTotal, CFBRA and CFCCA are respectively computed.

Implementation of ROC curve analysis followed the protocol in Vilizzi et al.74, with the true/false positive/negative outcome distinction not applied to the medium-risk species, as they can be either included or not into a full (comprehensive) risk assessment depending on priority or availability of financial resources. Fitting of the ROC curve was with package pROC78 for R x64 v4.0.5 79 using 2000 bootstrap replicates for the confidence intervals of specificities, which were computed along the entire range of sensitivity points (i.e. 0 to 1, at 0.1 intervals). Differences in CF between components (BRA, BRA + CCA) were tested with permutational ANOVA, with normalisation of the data and using a Bray-Curtis dissimilarity measure, 9999 permutations of the raw data, and with statistical effects evaluated at α = 0.05.
