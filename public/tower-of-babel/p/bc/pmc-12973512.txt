Anastomosing hemangioma of the renal pelvis: The first case report from Vietnam
Authors: Hung Lenh Do, Nguyen Hai Dang Le, Dieu Nhan Van, Thien Tan Tri Tai Truyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Anastomosing hemangioma is a rare, histopathologically distinct, benign vascular tumor that preferentially involves the genitourinary tract and the paraspinal region. AH can be misdiagnosed as a malignancy such as renal cell carcinoma or angiosarcoma. We present the case of a 71-year-old female patient who was initially diagnosed with cystic renal cell carcinoma based on computed tomography findings. The patient underwent robotic radical nephrectomy, but the pathologic result was AH of the kidney. There was no evidence of recurrence or metastasis 6 months after the surgery.

Introduction

Anastomotic hemangioma (AH) is a rare and histopathological distinct benign vascular tumor that preferentially involves the genitourinary tract and paraspinal region. In 2009, Montgomery and Epstein first described AH as a distinct vascular lesion in the kidney and testis.1 Since then, AH has been increasingly reported in the literature, particularly in patients with chronic kidney disease or those who have undergone renal transplantation. Nevertheless, owing to its rarity and nonspecific radiological appearance, AH is frequently misdiagnosed as a malignant renal tumor, most notably renal cell carcinoma or malignant vascular neoplasms, which may result in overtreatment. Clinically, most cases of renal AH are asymptomatic and are incidentally detected during imaging examinations performed for unrelated reasons. When symptoms are present, patients may experience hematuria or flank pain, clinical manifestations that closely resemble those of malignant urinary tract tumors. From a diagnostic standpoint, imaging modalities such as ultrasonography, computed tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging lack specific features for AH, making accurate preoperative diagnosis challenging. Consequently, definitive diagnosis relies primarily on postoperative histopathological examination and immunohistochemical analysis. In this context, we report a case of renal AH to highlight the clinical presentation, imaging characteristics, and histopathological features of this rare entity, and to discuss its differential diagnosis and appropriate management strategies in order to avoid unnecessary aggressive interventions.

Case presentation

A 71-year-old female was referred to the department of urology for an incidentally detected mass in the right kidney on computed tomography (CT). Urinalysis and urine culture were nonspecific. Routine blood examination revealed a standard range of white blood cell count (6.9 x 10^9/L) and a normal hemoglobin level (13.1g/dL). Preoperative blood chemistries were unremarkable, with a serum creatinine level of 63 μmol/L and an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 85.77 mL/min/1.73 m2. Contrast-enhanced CT showed a lesion measuring approximately 3.2 × 3.5 × 3.7 cm with partial extension into about the upper one-third of the right kidney (Fig. 1). The lesion has ill-defined margins, shows substantial contrast enhancement, and increased perilesional vascularity. The CT findings were suggestive of renal cell carcinoma (Fig. 2). We performed robotic radical nephrectomy after detailed discussion because the tumor was too close to the central renal vein to do a partial nephrectomy. Grossly, the cut section of the kidney showed a lesion measuring 2 × 2 cm, with relatively well-defined margins compared with the surrounding renal tissue. The cut surface is relatively homogeneous, brown in color, with areas of vascular malformation. Microscopically, the tumor was composed of small blood-filled vascular spaces with a distinctive interlocking (anastomosing) capillary network set within loose supporting stroma (Fig. 3). The overall histopathological examination confirmed AH of the right kidney. Discussion

AH is a rare benign vascular neoplasm that has been described in various organs, with the kidney emerging as an increasingly recognized site in recent years. Although histologically benign, renal AH poses a significant diagnostic challenge because its architectural complexity and radiological appearance frequently mimic malignant renal tumors, particularly angiosarcoma. This diagnostic ambiguity has been consistently reported in recent Asian case reports and series published between 2016 and 2025, in which renal AH was commonly misinterpreted as renal cell carcinoma or malignant vascular tumors, leading to radical surgical management 2, 3, 4, 5.

Clinically, renal AH lacks specific symptoms. Most reported Asian cases, including those from China, Korea, India, and Hong Kong, were incidentally detected during imaging performed for unrelated indications, while only a minority of patients presented with hematuria or flank pain 2, 3, 6. Similar to these reports, our patient was asymptomatic, and the lesion was discovered incidentally during radiological evaluation. Several studies have also noted a higher prevalence of renal AH in patients with chronic kidney disease or in renal transplant recipients, suggesting that prolonged microvascular hemodynamic alterations may contribute to tumor development.4,7 This association further complicates clinical decision-making, as renal preservation is critical in these patient populations.

From an imaging perspective, ultrasonography, computed tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging typically demonstrate a hypervascular renal mass with contrast enhancement; however, none of these modalities provides pathognomonic features that reliably distinguish AH from malignant renal tumors.2,5,6 In the majority of Asian cases reviewed, preoperative imaging strongly favored a diagnosis of renal malignancy, prompting surgical intervention 2, 3, 4. In our case, radiological findings similarly suggested a malignant renal tumor, leading to surgical resection. This observation is consistent with existing literature, in which definitive diagnosis of renal AH is almost invariably established only after histopathological examination.3,5

Histopathology remains the cornerstone for diagnosing renal AH. Characteristic findings include densely anastomosing small vascular channels lined by bland, flattened endothelial cells without cytologic atypia, necrosis, or increased mitotic activity. Tumor cells exhibit widespread positivity for endothelial markers, including CD31, CD34, and ERG, along with a low Ki-67 proliferation index. This allows for a clear differentiation from angiosarcoma and other malignant vascular neoplasms. In our case, the histology was entirely consistent with that reported in previous Asian studies, confirming the benign nature of the lesion.

Regarding management, most reported cases of renal AH in Asia were treated surgically, either by partial or radical nephrectomy, mainly due to the inability to exclude malignancy preoperatively 2, 3, 4, 5. However, increasing recognition of this entity has led to a growing consensus that nephron-sparing surgery or active surveillance may be appropriate once the diagnosis of AH is established, particularly in small, asymptomatic tumors.6,8 Improved awareness of renal AH is therefore crucial to avoid unnecessary radical surgery and preserve renal function. Finally, our case demonstrated an excellent prognosis with no evidence of recurrence or metastasis at 6-month follow-up, consistent with the current literature documenting the universally benign behavior of anastomosing hemangioma without any reported cases of recurrence or metastatic disease. To the best of our knowledge, this case represents the first reported instance of renal AH in Vietnam.

Conclusion

Renal AH is a rare benign vascular tumor that is frequently misdiagnosed as a malignant renal neoplasm. Accurate diagnosis requires careful correlation of clinical, radiological, and histopathological findings. Our case highlights the importance of considering AHin the differential diagnosis of hypervascular renal masses to prevent overtreatment and support nephron-sparing management.
