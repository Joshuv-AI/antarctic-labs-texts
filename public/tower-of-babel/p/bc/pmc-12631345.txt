Mechanistic Insights into the Coupling of Hydrosilanes and Alcohol-Amines for Hydrogen Production
Authors: Sheila A Martín, Imen Abidli, Abdelhamid Sayari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The production of hydrogen on-demand via the dehydrogenative coupling of hydrosilanes and alcohol-amines, conducted at room temperature without solvents nor catalysts, marks a significant advancement in the pursuit of greener energy sources. This work deals with the reaction mechanism of the dehydrogenative coupling of phenyl silane (PhS) and 2-(methylamino)-ethanol (MEA) as well as the interference of hydrolysis/condensation and disproportionation reactions when alkoxy-containing hydrosilanes, such as triethoxysilane (TES), are used. DFT methods were used to calculate the thermodynamic properties of the reactions involved (Δr G°, Δr H°, and Δr S°), the hydride donating ability of the reactants and intermediate products (HDA) as well as the Mulliken charges. These theoretical data complemented NMR and GC experiments to identify the most likely reaction pathway. Our results suggest the occurrence of multiple reaction pathways involving the nucleophilic attack of OH groups, and to a lesser extent NH groups, on the Si–H bond, leading to the formation of Si–O and Si–N containing products and the release of hydrogen. However, due to the low stability and high entropic contribution in the formation of Si–N containing products compared to their Si–O containing counterparts, their formation seems unfavorable, and no products containing only Si–N bonds were detected. However, the product with mixed Si–N and Si–O bonding, PhSiH­(O−)­(N−), was detected as a minor product. The high hydride donating ability of Si–O containing products led to such species undergoing consecutive nucleophilic attacks on their Si–H bonds by the hydroxyl group in MEA until complete dehydrogenation, ultimately leading to the formation of PhSi­(O−)3 as the main reaction product.

Introduction

As the world seeks cleaner energy alternatives, liquid organic hydrogen carriers (LOHCs) stand as a promising pathway for the adoption of hydrogen as a viable clean energy source. In LOHCs, hydrogen is chemically bonded within organic molecules, allowing it to be readily released through chemical reactions. This method enables on-demand hydrogen production and significantly enhances the practicality, efficiency, and safety of hydrogen storage, handling and transportation, compared to gaseous or solid forms. , Additionally, LOHCs can be integrated into existing fuel infrastructure with minimal modifications, making them suitable for various applications. These features have motivated extensive research into different hydrogen production strategies involving LOHCs. −

One of the least explored LOHC systems for hydrogen production is the coupling of hydrosilanes and alcohol-amines. In contrast, coupling of hydrosilanes and alcohols or amines is a well-known method for protecting alcohol groups or synthesizing silylamines and silazanes. Since the Si–H bond is relatively unreactive toward nucleophilic attacks by amines or alcohols, its coupling requires the presence of catalysts. Many catalysts have been reported to be effective for the dehydrogenative coupling of hydrosilanes and OH-containing compounds, leading to Si–O bond formation and H2 evolution. This includes metal-containing compounds, − and other species such as carbonates CO3 2–, , hydroxide OH–, fluorides F–, , alkoxides RO–, − borane B­(C6F5)3, borohydrides HBR3 –, and even polar solvents such as DMSO, DMF, pyridine, and THF. ,

Interestingly, amines were also found to act as catalysts in the dehydrogenative coupling of silanes and alcohols ,,,,− or amines. The catalytic activity of amines has been confirmed in the presence of hydrosilanes with amine-containing ligands, where intramolecular interaction of the amine group with the silicon center enables the formation of intramolecular pentacoordinated silicon species with activated Si–H bonds to undergo nucleophilic substitution. ,,,, However, when Lukevics and Dzintara , and Voronova et al. investigated the dehydrogenative coupling of nonamine-containing hydrosilanes and alcohols in the presence of free amines, they proposed that rather than a direct Si···N intermolecular interaction, the role of the amine is to deprotonate the alcohol (ROH) generating alkoxide species (RO–), that will then interact with the silicon atom Si···OR. While the proposed activation of the Si–H bond via interaction with alkoxide groups, seems reasonable in the presence of highly basic alkoxides, the formation of alkoxides seems unlikely in the presence of regular alcohols and amines. Nonetheless, whether involving intramolecular interactions with the amine group Si···N, or intermolecular interactions with the alkoxy species Si···OR, there is a general consensus that in this context, the dehydrogenative coupling involves Si–H bond activation through the formation of pentacoordinated silicon complexes, through interaction with nucleophilic species. In pentacoordinated silicon structures, the Si–H bond is widely known to be more reactive toward nucleophilic substitution than in its tetrahedral counterpart, due to increased bond polarization and elongation. , This weakens the Si–H bond and enhances the silicon electrophilic character.

It is worth mentioning that the formation of hypervalent silicon complexes through intermolecular Si···N interactions has also been reported in the literature. For example, Corriu and co-workers indicated the formation of hypercoordinated silicon intermediates during the interaction of hydrosilanes with nucleophiles like N-methylimidazole, while Nakash and Goldvaser demonstrated that the trifluorophenylethynyl silane forms penta- and hexacoordinated complexes with pyridine at 25 °C and below.

In an earlier contribution, we investigated the hydrogen production at room temperature through solvent-free cross-coupling of hydrosilanes and alcohol-amines with no added catalysts. Our work was focused on the scope of the reaction and the origin of reactivity. The main findings were as follows: (i) hydroxyl and to a lesser extent, amine functionalities are both involved in hydrogen production through dehydrogenative coupling with hydrosilanes; (ii) there is no intramolecular synergistic effect between amine and alcohol groups; (iii) the amine groups are much less reactive than the hydroxyl groups toward the hydrogen generation; (iv) the role of amine groups as an internal nucleophilic catalyst is crucial for the reaction to take place. While these findings represent significant contributions to the understanding of these reactions, their mechanism is still unclear.

In dehydrogenative coupling reactions, where subtle changes in electronic structure govern bond formation and cleavage, density functional theory (DFT) enables a deeper understanding of reactivity, selectivity, and thermodynamic feasibility. , Calculated NMR spectra, thermodynamic properties, and hydride donating abilities (HDA) provide critical insights into intermediate species that are often too transient to be fully characterized experimentally. , Recent studies have demonstrated the value of DFT in rationalizing the relative stability of Si–O versus Si–N bonds, as well as in identifying potential energy surfaces and transition states in the dehydrogenative coupling reaction of hydrosilanes with alcohol in the presence of amine.

The objective of the current work was to shed new light into the reaction pathway of hydrosilanes and alcohol-amines using a combination of experimental and theoretical methods. To do so, we investigated the dehydrocoupling of phenyl silane (PhS) and 2-(methylamino)-ethanol (MEA) as a model reaction. To describe the most feasible pathway of this coupling reaction, we carried out DFT calculations focusing on two main targets, namely (i) the reaction thermodynamic properties (Δr H°, Δr S°, Δr G°) associated with the formation of all possible silane products containing Si–N, Si–O, or mixed bonds; (ii), the effect of H– substitution by the amine or alkoxy groups from MEA on the Si–H bond hydricity, which is quantified by the hydride donating ability (HDA). To improve the reliability of theoretical predictions and more closely simulate experimental conditions, solvation models such as SMD were incorporated into the calculations. Experimentally, the dehydrogenative coupling reaction of PhS and MEA was studied using different ratios of reactants and a range of temperatures from −10 to 22 °C. Additionally, the influence of side processes, such as disproportionation and hydrolysis/condensation reactions that occur in the presence of moisture-sensitive hydrosilanes containing alkoxy groups, like triethoxysilane (TES) was also investigated.

Experimental Section

Materials and Experimental Methods

All chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used without further purification. Hydrosilanes and alcohol-amines used in this work are listed in Table . The reaction setup consisted of a 25 mL two-neck round-bottom flask attached to a condenser cooled to −20 °C using a thermostatic bath (VWR 1166D Refrigerated/Heating Circulator). The generated gas was collected in an eudiometer over water at atmospheric pressure. Unless otherwise specified, the coupling reactions were investigated at room temperature (21 °C) and constant magnetic stirring (600 rpm) for 2.5 h, using a 1:1 ratio between the hydrogen atoms of the silane and the total hydrogen atoms in the alcohol and amine groups.

Gas product samples were analyzed using an Agilent 7820A gas chromatograph (GC) equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD) and an Agilent J&W Select Permanent Gases column. Hydrogen was identified by its retention time using the GC signal of pure hydrogen. The amount of produced hydrogen was measured by direct reading of the eudiometer. For kinetic measurements, the level of water in the eudiometer was videotaped for a more accurate reading of the volume versus time. The silylation of alcohol-amines was monitored by proton nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy as well as 29Si NMR with and without inverse gated proton decoupling, 29Si­{1H} and 29Si, respectively. To monitor the evolution of reaction mixtures, in situ 1H NMR measurements were carried out, while the reaction was taking place in the NMR probe at 45 °C during 16 h, in the presence of excess silane and no stirring. All spectra were acquired on a Bruker AVANCE III HD 500 MHz spectrometer equipped with a 5 mm probe and using CDCl3 as solvent. The acquisition time and relaxation delay were 1.49 and 10 s, respectively.

Theoretical Methods

Molecular geometries were optimized using DFT calculations method. The 6–311G­(d,p) basis set was used for all structures, in combination with the B3LYP functional. Frequency calculations were performed on all optimized geometries to confirm the absence of imaginary frequencies, thereby verifying that the structures correspond to the energy minima on the potential energy surface. These calculations also provided thermodynamic properties, including Gibbs free energy, enthalpy, entropy and Mulliken charges. In addition, the optimized geometries for all potential products were used for NMR chemical shift calculations. Thermochemical data and temperature corrections were calculated at 298.15 K for all optimized geometries. Based on optimized gas-phase geometry for each species, solvent effects were considered using the solvation model based on density (SMD) at the same level of theory. All calculations were performed using the Gaussian package 16.

Results and Discussion

The higher reactivity of hydroxyl compared to amine groups and the resulting formation of Ph Si (O−)3 species are consistent with the conclusions reported in our earlier contribution, when investigating the effect of the number of amine and alcohol groups per molecule. The higher reactivity of OH was in line with the observation that enhanced hydrogen production yields were always associated with the use of reactants containing at least one OH group. Reactants containing only amine functionalities were found to be inactive or led to very poor hydrogen yields. As reported in the literature, such reactions require the presence of catalysts.

When investigating the origin of reactivity in the dehydrogenative coupling of hydrosilanes and alcohols in the presence of amines, some authors attributed the catalytic activity to the formation of alkoxide species by deprotonation of alcohols by amines. ,, Such species can coordinate the silicon center, creating a pentacoordinated silicon intermediary with enhanced Si–H reactivity. To investigate the possible role of an alkoxide anion, the dehydrogenative coupling of PhS and alcohol was investigated in the presence of (MeO)2Ca. Methanol (MeOH) and hexafluoro-2-propanol (HFOH) were chosen considering the large difference in their Bronsted acidity, (pK a: MeOH = 9.3; HFOH = 16). Reactions were carried out in the absence of solvent at 21 °C for 30 min, using 1 mmol of PhS, 3 mmol of alcohol, and 1 mmol of (MeO)2Ca. None or negligible amount of hydrogen was generated when using MeOH (0%) or HFOH (4%). However, when the coupling reactions were performed in the presence of 1 mmol of tertiary amine (tetramethylethylenediamine) instead of the alkoxide (MeO)2Ca, the hydrogen production yield was 98% and 100%, in the presence of MeOH and HFOH, respectively. These results demonstrate that under the current conditions, alkoxide anions do not have a catalytic effect on the dehydrogenative coupling of PhS and alcohols. Rather, it appears that the catalytic activity originates from direct interactions between the amine and the silicon. We concur that the reaction proceeds through the formation of activated pentacoordinated silicon species, possibly of the type R4–X H X SiN.

Considering our experimental findings, the overall reaction mechanism could be described in two steps. First, the activation of hydrosilane takes place. It is inferred that the formation of pentacoordinated silicon species due to the interaction of hydrosilanes and electron donor groups, such as amines in our case, activates the Si–H bond, making it more polarized and weaker, thus more susceptible to nucleophilic attacks than their tetracoordinated counterparts. ,,, Then, the attack of activated silane is due mostly to hydroxyl groups, whose high affinity for the silane leads to Si–O bond formation and H2 release. During this process, the nucleophilic attack of amine groups on activated hydrosilane occurs as well, albeit to a very limited extent. Under the current conditions, the consumption of hydroxyl groups proceeds until completion, before amine groups are marginally consumed. The dual role of the amine functionality as an internal catalyst and substrate for the dehydrogenation process affects the rate and yield of this reaction. Theoretical calculations that support the formation of a pentacoordinated silicon species in the transition state with an activated Si–H bond, due to the coordination of the amine group are currently underway.

As mentioned earlier, the hydrogen production from the coupling of PhS and MEA is completed within minutes, in the absence of added catalysts. To calculate the activation energy and initial rate constants, the reaction of 0.5 mmol of PhS and 3 mmol of MEA (excess) was performed at different temperatures in the range of −10 to 22 °C. The reaction profiles in Figure show that quantitative hydrogen yields are obtained quickly (75 s) even at −10 °C. The initial rates were calculated using the slope of the hydrogen yield versus time as close as possible to t = 0. The activation energy, E a, obtained from the Arrhenius plot (Figure S3) was 10.75 kJ mol–1. This low energy barrier allows PhS and MEA reactants to easily reach the transition state, leading to a fast reaction. A very low activation energy also reveals that the reaction mechanism is relatively simple, with the reaction likely occurring in a single step without the formation of intermediates. For a similar reaction, the dehydrogenative coupling of phenyl silane and hexafluoro-2-propanol in the presence of a tertiary amine, Voronova et al. found a higher activation energy of 24.7 kJ mol–1. It is also worth mentioning that many of the reported hydrogen generation systems via transition metal-catalyzed coupling of Si–H and O–H groups also exhibit higher energy barriers compared to the current finding. −

Theoretical Calculations

To evaluate the thermodynamic feasibility of the dehydrogenative coupling of PhS and MEA, all possible products were considered, see Figure . The corresponding Δr G° for the reactions in the gas phase and in acetonitrile solution (modeled using the SMD method) as well as the associated entropic and enthalpic contributions are listed in Table . As seen, in the gas phase, Si–O containing products are more stable than their Si–N containing counterparts. These findings are consistent with the work of Voronova et al., which combines experimental and computational studies demonstrating that Si–O bond formation is thermodynamically favorable, exhibiting lower energy barriers and greater bond stability compared to Si–N containing species. For example, PhSi­(O−)3 was found to be the most favorable product with a Δr G° = −36.7 kcal mol–1, followed by PhSiH­(O−)2 and PhSiH2(O−) with Δr G° = −22.5 and −13.0 kcal mol–1, respectively. In contrast, Si–N containing products such as PhSiH2(N−), PhSiH­(N−)2, and PhSi­(N−)3, exhibit positive free energies of 7.2, 7.5, and 9.7 kcal mol–1, respectively, indicating that their formation is thermodynamically unfavorable. Although the stability of all products was lower in solution than in the gas phase, the overall trend was the same in both phases, PhSi­(O−)3 > PhSiH­(O−)2 > PhSiH2(O−) ≫ PhSi­(N−)3 > PhSiH­(N−)2 > PhSiH2(N−). Mixed Si–O/Si–N containing products exhibited intermediate values. For example, the formation of PhSiH­(O−)­(N−), PhSi­(O−)­(N−)2 and PhSi­(O−)2(N−) corresponds to Δr G° = −8.0, 22.5, and 21.2 kcal mol–1, respectively. Additionally, enthalpic and entropic contributions showed that enthalpy plays a dominant role in the formation of Si–O containing compounds, with up to 82% in the case of PhSi­(O−)3, while the entropy effects dominate in the formation of Si–N containing products. In the literature, Si–O and Si–N bond lengths around 1.64 Å and 1.70 Å, respectively, have been reported. Hence, we found average values of 1.66 Å for Si–O bonds and 1.74 Å for Si–N bonds across all nine products. This is due to the more electrophilic character of the silicon atom and the stronger stabilizing ability of the lone pair of oxygen.

The low stability of Si–N containing products and their unfavorable formation processes compared to Si–O containing products, explains why species such as PhSiH2(N−), PhSiH­(N−)2, and PhSi­(N−)3 were not detected experimentally. Only the compound PhSiH­(O−)­(N−), containing mixed Si–N/Si–O bonds, which has the highest stability and enthalpic contribution value among all Si–N containing products, was detected in limited amount when stoichiometric or excess amounts of PhS were used.

Since the dehydrogenative coupling of hydrosilanes with amine or alcohol groups involves the nucleophilic substitution of the hydride, therefore this reaction depends directly on the silane hydricity, which is expressed as the hydride donating ability (HDA). HDA is defined as the Gibbs free energy change associated with the heterolytic cleavage of a hydride donor (E–H). It was calculated according to HDA = G°(E+) + G°(H–) – G°(E–H), where G°(E+), G°(H–) and G°(E–H) are the free energies of the different species. This parameter offers valuable insight into a species’ ability to donate a hydride ion, and thus undergo dehydrogenative coupling with a proton-donating species. , The lower the HDA value, the more favorable the release of hydride. It should be noted that HDA strongly depends on the operating conditions, especially on the polarity of the solvent. ,

The HDA values for PhSiH3 and all the potential hydrosilane products upon reaction with MEA are listed in Table . It is worth mentioning that the accuracy and reliability of our HDA calculations were tested by running HDA calculations for PhS in both acetonitrile and the gas phase, which allowed comparison with the results reported by Heiden et al. They used the M06–2X/6–31g­(d,p) method and reported HDA values of 265.98 kcal mol–1 for PhSiH3 and 296.91 kcal mol–1 for SiH4. Using the B3LYP/6–311G++(d,p) level of theory, we obtained HDA values of 217.55 and 257.11 kcal mol–1 for PhSiH3 and SiH4 in the gas phase, respectively. As seen, PhSiH3 has the highest HDA value of 217.55 kcal mol–1, which corresponds to the lowest ability to donate H–. However, as the H– ligands are progressively replaced by alkoxide or amine groups, the HDA decreased, indicating increased reactivity. For example, PhSiH3–n (O−) n showed HDA values of 217.55, 201.79, and 191.49 kcal mol–1 for n = 0, 1 and 2, respectively. Similarly, PhSiH3–n (N−) n had HDA of 217.55, 190.61, and 180.75 kcal mol–1. Interestingly, Table shows that PhSiH­(O−)­(N−), the most thermodynamically stable N-containing product (Δr G° = −8.0 kcal mol–1), has the lowest hydride donating ability among all N-containing species. This suggests that, if formed, further dehydrogenative coupling of this molecule would be unlikely.

Mulliken charges on the hydrogen atom bonded to silicon in PhS and all possible hydrosilane intermediates were calculated to complement the HDA data. This charge reflects the local electron density and provides insight into the partial positive or negative character of the hydrogen, thereby indicating its propensity to be released as a hydride. Results displayed in Figure show that the Mulliken charge was −0.13 for PhSiH3 and it becames more and more negative upon nucleophilic substitution to reach −0.17 for PhSiH2(O−) and −0.27 for PhSiH­(O−)2. Similarly, in the nitrogen series, the Mulliken charges were −0.23 for PhSiH2(N−) and −0.29 for PhSiH­(N−)2.

Correlation between Mulliken charge on the hydride bound to the Si atom and hydricity (HDA) for the products of PhS and MEA coupling in the gas phase.

The increasing negative charge on the hydride as successive nucleophilic substitutions take place is consistent with the decreasing HDA values, demonstrating a greater propensity for hydride transfer. This trend is supported by literature data. Krajewski and Lee showed through experimental and DFT analyses that silane hydricity improves with more negative free energy values (ΔG), emphasizing the influence of electronic effects on reactivity. Ilic et al. further confirmed that hydricity correlates strongly with the partial and formal charges on donor atoms, where increasing negative charge enhances hydride donating ability. Similarly, Multem et al. demonstrated that the donor strength of metal–hydride complexes aligns with both M–H bond vibrational frequencies and Mulliken charge distributions, validating the link between Mulliken charge and hydricity across different systems.

Surprisingly, as observed in Table , the HDA of Si–N containing products were lower than that of their oxygenated counterparts, even though oxygen is generally more electronegative. This trend, although counterintuitive, is consistent with the Mulliken charges shown in Figure . It is worth mentioning that although Si–N containing hydrosilanes exhibit higher ability to donate H– compared to their Si–O counterparts, this does not translate in higher likelihood to form Si–N containing products, since their formation processes are very limited thermodynamically, as shown in Table .

In general, the decreasing values of HDA and Mulliken charge observed in the series PhSiH3 > PhSiH2(O−) > PhSiH­(O−)2, combined with their increasing stability PhSiH3 < PhSiH2(O−) < PhSiH­(O−)2 < PhSi­(O−)3, indicate that successive dehydrocoupling steps are increasingly more favorable. This finding suggests that once the nucleophilic attack of Si–H by the alkoxy group takes place, the next nucleophilic attack by the alkoxy group is preferentially directed toward the hydrosilane produced instead of unreacted PhS molecules. Hence, the first PhSiH2(O−) species formed will undergo consecutive substitutions, ultimately leading to fully substituted species PhSi­(O−)3. This finding explains why PhSi­(O−)3 was the only main product and no intermediate products, such as PhSiH2(O−) and PhSiH­(O−)2 were observed, even though considerable amounts of PhS were left unreacted. It is worth mentioning that other fully substituted products such as PhSi­(N−)3, PhSi­(O−)2(N−) and PhSi­(O−)­(N−)2 were not detected, most likely due to their unfavorable formation process, as indicated in Table .

Further analysis of the products PhSiH­(O−)2 and PhSiH­(O−)­(N−) raises an interesting question: if PhSiH­(O−)2 is thermodynamically more stable than PhSiH­(O−)­(N−) with Δr G° = −22.5 versus −8.0 kcal mol–1, why was the latter species detected experimentally, but not the former? The answer may not lie in the thermodynamic data, but in the hydrogen donating ability of the hydrosilanes. Although the formation of PhSiH­(O−)2 is more thermodynamically favorable, its lower HDA (191.49 kcal mol–1) suggests that it more readily transforms into PhSi­(O−)3; whereas PhSiH­(O−)­(N−), with a higher HDA (195.48 kcal mol–1), is less likely to undergo further nucleophilic substitution. The Mulliken charges for all possible products from PhS and MEA coupling are reported in Figure S4. Figure shows that PhSiH­(O−)2 and PhSiH­(O−)­(N−) have similar Mulliken charges of −0.27 and −0.28, respectively. This is consistent with their similar HDA values, and indicates that both species exhibit comparable hydride donating abilities, making them compete for hydride transfer.

Silane Hydrolysis/Condensation and Disproportionation as Competing Processes

We demonstrated earlier that (EtO)3SiH (TES) was also active toward dehydrogenative coupling with alcohol-amines. However, unlike PhS, TES contains moisture-sensitive ethoxy groups, which may lead to rapid conversion into Si–OH groups, followed by condensation into Si–O–Si units. Interaction of water and alkoxy-containing hydrosilane might also compromise the formation of pentacoordinated silicon species, as suggested by Weinmann et al. Therefore, the impact of moisture on the dehydrogenative coupling of MEA and TES was investigated.

Regardless of humidity or the lack thereof, hydrogen was released promptly and vigorously after mixing TES and MEA. Nonetheless, the presence of humidity had an adverse effect on the hydrogen yield. In the presence of ambient humidity, the reaction using stoichiometric amounts of TES and MEA yielded 60% hydrogen, whereas under dry nitrogen in a glovebox, the yield reached 71%. In ambient atmosphere, the side reactions of hydrolysis and condensation compete with the dehydrogenative coupling of TES. 1H NMR data of the products formed under humid and dry conditions shown in Figure , confirmed the formation of new –C H 2–O–Si species at 3.82 ppm, in spectra c and d , respectively, as well as the complete consumption of –C H 2–OH species of MEA molecules, originally appearing at 3.65 ppm (spectrum a). This confirms that the coupling of hydroxyl groups from MEA and TES takes place regardless of the occurrence of humidity. It is also observed in Figure c,d, that a singlet at 4.21 ppm was obtained in both humid and anhydrous conditions. This signal corresponds to the Si–H groups in reaction products and not in unreacted TES. The reason behind the presence of Si–H in the reaction products will be discussed hereafter.

29Si­{1H} NMR data for the reaction mixture in humid conditions, indicated the formation of condensed silicon species associated with the peak at −81.82 ppm in Figure b. This stems from the hydrolysis and condensation processes due to interactions between the silane alkoxy groups (Si–OR) and water. As expected, under dry atmosphere, the formation of condensed silicon species (siloxane groups) was drastically reduced, and the intensity of the corresponding peak at −81.82 ppm was substantially lower, Figure c. The peak at −58.58 ppm, labeled as D, associated with the formation of Si–O bonds via the dehydrogenative coupling of hydrosilane and OH group from MEA, was observed under both humid and dry conditions (Figure b,c). Surprisingly, species D was not generated due to the coupling of −OH groups in MEA with the reactant H–Si­(OC2H5)3, but with H2Si­(OEt)2 (B), which stems from the disproportionation of TES, as shown in Figure . Disproportionation of TES and dehydrogenative coupling of disproportionated species with MEA.

As shown in Figure , TES disproportionation gives rise to silanes with 1 to 4 ethoxy groups (species A, B, and C), through the exchange of substituents on silicon atoms. It was reported in the literature that alkoxy groups and hydrogen atoms are exchangeable substituents, and organic amines can catalyze alkoxyhydrosilane disproportionation. , Other researchers also noted the occurrence of disproportionation reactions while studying reactions involving TES. Although these reaction pathways are not well understood, base-catalyzed disproportionation is widely used on large scale in the organosilicon industry.

Based on the foregoing discussion, the disproportionation of TES is not only possible, but accelerated in the presence of MEA. Presumably, the coupling of −OH groups with all disproportionated hydrosilanes takes place simultaneously, but the reaction with B species occurs to the largest extent. In this case, the disproportionation equilibria likely favor the formation of B. This species is rapidly consumed in the dehydrogenative coupling with MEA, which is facilitated by its lower steric hindrance and higher availability of hydrogen atoms compared to species A and TES, as well as greater electrophilic character of the silicon atom compared to species C. It is important to note in the 29Si­{1H} NMR spectra shown in Figure a,b, that the low intensity of unreacted TES peaks at −58.47 ppm in the product spectra is apparently, not consistent with the moderately low hydrogen yields of both coupling reactions, which are 60% and 71% under humid and dry conditions, respectively. This discrepancy provides further evidence of the short lifespan of TES molecules, which undergo fast disproportionation rather than dehydrogenative coupling with MEA, under both humid and dry conditions. The new disproportionated species turn out to be more reactive toward the dehydrogenative coupling than the starting TES molecule.

The disproportionation of PhS was also documented in the literature. However, contrary to TES, the PhS disproportionated species, such as Ph2SiH2 or Ph3SiH, are inert toward dehydrogenative coupling with MEA, under the current conditions. Thus, they will not interfere with the coupling reaction.

At this stage, it is worth discussing the involvement of amine groups in the dehydrogenative coupling of TES and MEA. Regardless of the occurrence of humidity, the hydrogen production yield was consistently higher than 50%, suggesting that both −OH and –NH functional groups in MEA are involved in the dehydrogenative coupling reaction. However, no peak attributable to Si–N bond was observed under the measurement conditions used for the 29Si­{1H} NMR in Figure . Therefore, additional 29Si NMR without inverse gated proton decoupling (Figure S5) and in situ 1H NMR measurements versus time (Figure S6) were performed to demonstrate the involvement of amine groups in the dehydrogenative coupling. Two sets of doublet signals with 1J(Si–H) of approximately 288 Hz were detected, demonstrating the occurrence of species H Si (O−)3 and H Si (N−)­(O−)2 in the B and MEA reaction products (Figure S5). Additionally, the evolution over time of peaks attributable to –C H 3 and –C H 2– adjacent to the amine group in MEA, as well as the H Si­(O−)3 and H Si­(N−)­(O−)2 peaks, clearly showed the involvement of the amine group of MEA in the dehydrogenative coupling with the hydrosilane (Figure S6). Although amine groups were involved in the cross-coupling reactions, their reactivity was very low compared to hydroxyl groups as we demonstrated earlier. Figures S5 and S6 indicate that in addition to the main product D from B and MEA reaction (Figure ), a double-silylated product was also formed, but in a limited amount.

The product mixture from TES and MEA reaction conducted under anhydrous conditions was also found to be highly sensitive to moisture. As shown in Figure S7, when exposed to ambient atmosphere, the initial liquid mixture transformed over time into a white solid. This finding suggests that ethoxy groups in the silylated alcohol-amines products are as sensitive to moisture as the reactant TES. Hence, extra care should be implemented to avoid condensation reactions. Alternatively, hydrosilanes without ethoxy groups may be used.

Conclusions

The production of hydrogen through the coupling of PhS and MEA at room temperature with no added catalysts or solvents was investigated using a combination of experimental and theoretical methods. The preferential formation of oxygen-containing products over their nitrogen-substituted counterparts stems from the unfavorable formation of the latter. Products of consecutive nucleophilic substitutions formed more and more easily, leading to fully substituted silanes, at the expense of partially substituted products. This was due to the increasingly higher hydride donating ability of successive hydrosilane products. The feasibility of different reaction pathways was discussed in light of the thermodynamic data (Δr G°, Δr H° and Δr S°) of all possible reactions, and the hydricity parameters (HDA and Mulliken charges) of O- and N-substituted hydrosilanes. The influence of side processes such as hydrolysis/condensation and disproportionation reactions when alkoxy-containing hydrosilanes are involved in the dehydrogenative coupling reaction were also assessed.
