Conditional Relative Survival Among Patients With Gastric Cancer Undergoing Surgery: A Hospital-Based Cohort Study
Authors: Hyeree Park, Yo-Seok Cho, Do Joong Park, Hyuk-Joon Lee, Han-Kwang Yang, Yun-Suhk Suh, Seong-Ho Kong, Aesun Shin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeConditional relative survival (CRS) estimates the probability of survival after surgery compared with the general population. We assessed 5-year CRS up to 5 years after gastric resection.Materials and MethodsWe analyzed 15,601 gastric cancer patients who underwent gastric resection between 1996 and 2018. Relative survival (RS) was defined as the ratio of observed survival in cancer patients to the expected survival in the general population. The 5-year CRS was the 5-year RS among patients who had survived a given number of years after surgery.ResultsFive-year CRS increased from 87.2% at one year to 95.4% at 5 years post-surgery. The most substantial increase was observed in stage III cancer, from 53.2% at one year to 85.2% at 5 years. If patients survived 5 years after surgery, their 5-year CRS was 95.4% for those under 40, 96.4% for those aged between 40 and 64, 92.7% for those aged between 65 and 79, and 99.4% for those 80 or older. Recent surgeries showed higher 5-year CRS at the time of surgery and improved CRS during early follow-up. Although there could be concerns about death from postoperative complications, patients in their 80s did not show lower RS than younger patients. Across operation types, total gastrectomy yielded lower 5-year CRS than distal gastrectomy from baseline through 5 years after surgery.ConclusionsCRS improved over time, indicating that with careful candidate selection, surgery is safe, even for elderly patients. Additionally, patients who have undergone total gastrectomy may require nutritional support and long-term care.Keywords: Stomach neoplasms, Survival

INTRODUCTION

Gastric cancer is estimated to be the fifth most common cancer worldwide and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths in 2020 [1]. In Korea, it is the fourth most common cancer in terms of incidence and mortality [2,3]. A total of 26,662 cases of gastric cancer occurred in Korea in 2020, accounting for 10.8% of all newly diagnosed cancers that year. Despite its high incidence, the age-standardized incidence decreased from 86.0 per 100,000 in 1999 to 51.9 in 2020 [2].

The age-standardized gastric cancer mortality rate has also declined in recent years [2]. According to the Korea Central Cancer Registry, the overall 5-year survival rate for patients with gastric cancer has dramatically increased from 43.9% among patients diagnosed between 1993 and 1995 to 78.0% among patients diagnosed between 2016 and 2020 [4]. This significant improvement in the survival rate of patients with gastric cancer can be partially attributed to advancements in treatment, including the development of surgical procedures for tumor resection and lymph node dissection [5,6]. Furthermore, an approximately 50% reduction in gastric cancer mortality is attributable to gastric cancer screening, particularly in individuals over 50 years of age [7,8].

While the 5-year survival estimate is widely used in clinical settings, its relevance to survivors with a good prognosis diminishes over time, as the 5-year survival encompasses the information of all patients, regardless of their prognosis [9]. A typical cancer survival curve displays a steep decline in the initial period that gradually tapers off thereafter. This pattern suggests that the likelihood of survival increases in patients who live during the active treatment. This phenomenon underlines the importance of conditional survival (CS), a metric used to estimate the survival probability of patients who have survived for a specified period after diagnosis. CS is defined as the probability of surviving an additional number of years given that a patient has already survived a specific number of years [10].

Relative survival (RS) is defined as the ratio of the observed survival rate in a group of cancer patients during a specified interval to the expected survival rate in the general population [11]. Because RS accounts for background mortality in the general population matched by age and calendar period, it is a metric frequently employed in cancer statistics [2,12,13].

Conditional relative survival (CRS) provides a time-updated prognosis by expressing the probability of surviving an additional period (e.g., 5 years) given survival to a specified postoperative landmark (e.g., 1–5 years), relative to the expected survival of the matched general population. Although various cancer types have been studied using CRS [14,15,16,17,18], studies on gastric cancer have predominantly employed CS [5,19,20,21] with only a single study applying CRS thus far [9]. Considering the high incidence of gastric cancer in Korea and the crucial role of prognosis, especially in early stage gastric cancer, it is essential to conduct further studies employing CRS. A brief comparison of CS, RS, and CRS is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Hospital-based survival statistics include more detailed clinical information than population-based cancer registries. In this study, we estimated 5-year CRS at annual postoperative landmarks (n=0–5 years) using a hospital-based cohort. We additionally conducted stratified analyses by age at surgery, stage, year of surgery, and postoperative complication status.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This was a retrospective single-center cohort study of patients surgically treated for gastric cancer. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before inclusion in the study. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Seoul National University College of Medicine/Seoul National University Hospital (IRB No. E-2108-116-1245) with a waiver of informed consent due to minimal risk, and complied with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and later versions.

Study population and data collection

A total of 16,681 patients were diagnosed with gastric cancer and underwent surgery at Seoul National University Hospital between January 1996 and December 2018 (Supplementary Fig. 1). For this single-center cohort study, we excluded 8 patients who were under 20 years of age at the time of diagnosis, 3 without age information, 2 without sex information, 10 without a recorded date of surgery, 937 without specified tumor site information, and 120 without stage information. After exclusion, 15,601 patients (10,337 men and 5,264 women) were included in the final analysis. Patients aged over 80 years underwent surgery only if preoperative evaluation deemed them fit for general anesthesia and curative resection; therefore, this subgroup represents a selected surgical population.

We collected a comprehensive set of demographic, postoperative, and pathological data including age, sex, date of surgery, type of surgery, tumor location, tumor size, Lauren classification, and tumor-node metastasis (TNM) stage. Pathological staging was performed according to The American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC; 8th edition) guidelines [22].

Patients were categorized into 4 age groups at the time of surgery: 20–39 years, 40–64 years, 65–79 years, and ≥80 years. The years of surgery were classified into 5-year intervals: 1996–2000, 2001–2005, 2006–2010, and 2011–2018. The study population was followed up from the date of surgery to death or until December 31, 2021, by linking mortality data from Statistics Korea (Korean Statistical Information Service).

Statistical analysis

Patient and tumor characteristics are displayed with frequencies and percentages for categorical variables.

Overall survival with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method (R packages ‘survival’ and ‘survminer’). RS with 95% CIs was computed using Stata’s ‘strs’ command [23] based on national life tables for the general Korean population, matched by attained age (updated with follow-up time), sex, and year, with expected survival obtained via the Ederer II method [11]. The population life table used to calculate the expected mortality rate in the general population was obtained from Statistics Korea for the period of 1996–2021.

Conditional 5-year relative survival (CRS) was estimated using a landmark approach. First, we calculated the 5-year RS at the time of surgery. Then, at each 1-year interval after surgery (n=1, 2, 3, …), we recalculated the 5-year RS only among patients who were still alive at that time. Follow-up was limited to the subsequent 5 years from each landmark. Thus, “5-year CRS at n years postsurgery” represents the 5-year RS among patients who had already survived to n years. For example, the 5-year CRS at 1 year refers to the 5-year RS among patients who survived the first postoperative year. Our analyses were stratified according to age, AJCC stage, year of surgery, and operative type.

All analyses were performed using Stata/SE version 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) or R software (version 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

This rate increased to 87.2%, 93.8%, and 95.4% in patients who survived 1, 3, and 5 years postoperatively, respectively. CRS stratified according to both age group and AJCC stage

The 5-year CRS, stratified according to age group and AJCC stage, is shown in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 2. In stage I, the 5-year CRS exceeded 95% in all age groups. Remarkably, for patients aged 80 years or older, the 5-year CRS reached 107.4% 5 years after surgery, which was higher than that of younger patients. In stage II, patients younger than 65 who had already survived 5 years after surgery showed a 5-year CRS approaching 95%, exceeding their baseline RS. However, for those aged 65 years or older, the 5-year CRS was approximately 85%. Regarding stage III, the 5-year CRS increased across all age groups. Notably, patients aged 80 years or older achieved a rate of 91.5% 5 years after surgery, surpassing the rates observed in younger patients. A similar analysis was conducted exclusively in patients who underwent resection between 2011 and 2018 (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 3 shows the 5-year CRS stratified according to AJCC stage and tumor site. In all stages, the 5-year CRS values at 5 years after surgery were the lowest for tumors located in the upper region: 94.5% for stage I, 90.3% for stage II, and 77.2% for stage III.

To determine the association between operation type and tumor stage, we conducted a secondary analysis based on the AJCC stage (Supplementary Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 4). The 5-year CRS for patients who had already survived for 5 years was lower for patients undergoing total gastrectomy than for patients undergoing distal gastrectomy at all stages. Specifically, in stage III, patients who underwent total gastrectomy had a 5-year CRS of 39.2% at the time of surgery, which increased to 79.5% after 5 years but remained lower than that of patients who underwent distal gastrectomy.

DISCUSSION

This study provides conditional RS estimates for patients with gastric cancer categorized according to age group, AJCC stage, and year of surgery. Our findings indicate an increasing likelihood of survival for an additional 5 years, given that a patient has already survived the first 5 years post-surgery. Specifically, the 5-year CRS for patients with gastric cancer who had already surpassed the 5-year survival mark postoperatively was 95.4%. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the 5-year CRS of patients with gastric cancer in a large cohort. We believe that our findings are useful in clinical practice, as they can serve as a resource to help predict prognosis according to the time after surgery and to explain this to the patient.

Previous studies introduced the concept of a “statistical cure,” indicating that when CS surpasses 95%, such patients can be considered to exhibit minimal excess mortality compared to the general population [24]. In our analysis, the 5-year CRS, conditioned on 5 years of survival, exceeded 90% for both stages I and II, but was observed to be 85.2% for stage III. Although clinical observations with 5 years of follow-up often indicate less favorable prognostic outcomes in older patients, the RS-adjusted population mortality data do not consistently support this assertion.

In the relative-survival framework, estimates are expressed relative to the expected survival of an age-, sex-, and calendar year-matched general population. This implies two features relevant to our results. First, among stage I patients—who have minimal early excess mortality—the conditional 5-year outlook may slightly decrease as the landmark advances, because the subsequent 5-year window increasingly reflects aging and competing risks, narrowing the relative advantage versus the general population. Second, in survivors aged over 80 years who were fit enough to undergo surgery and remain alive to later landmarks, selection and healthy-survivor effects, along with more intensive follow-up, can yield observed mortality lower than that of the matched general population; consequently, CRS can legitimately exceed 100%. Such values should be interpreted as indicating no excess—or even slightly lower—mortality relative to the general population, not that survival literally exceeds 100%.

In patients aged >80 years who are suitable for surgery, the risks associated with gastric cancer treatment do not seem to be notably higher than those in younger patients. The patients included in this study were assessed as being able to tolerate general anesthesia and surgery, indicating that they may not represent the entire population of elderly patients. Nonetheless, the 5-year CRS for those who survived 5 years post-surgery was 107.4% for stage I, 83.8% for stage II, and 91.5% for stage III. Notably, in AJCC stages I and III, older patients exhibited better CRS outcomes than younger patients. During the 1996–2000 period, the baseline 5-year RS for patients over 80 years of age was 47.2%, which was lower than the 71.3% for the overall patient population, which led to hesitancy in performing surgery in elderly individuals. However, the baseline 5-year RS for the 2011–2018 period increased to 93.4%, potentially due to higher life expectancy, improved patient health, and advancements in minimally invasive surgical techniques. Therefore, surgery based on age alone may not be necessary. Considering this observation and the rising trend of cancer diagnoses in elderly individuals, we suggest that older patients with good overall health should be considered suitable for standard treatment [25]. These findings should be interpreted in the context of selection and healthy-survivor effects among elderly surgical candidates and are not generalizable to all patients aged >80 years.

Survival rates following the initial cancer diagnosis have improved in recent years. However, for those surviving the past 5 years, these rates remain comparable to those of earlier periods. The 5-year CRS for patients who reached the 5-year survival mark remained consistent across the intervals 1996–2000, 2001–2005, 2006–2010, and 2011–2018. The increasing trend in the 5-year CRS across all periods suggests an increase in survival during the initial post-treatment years, with more patients making it through the early post-surgery phase. The differences in the 5-year CRS observed in the early phases of the previous periods can be partly explained by changing trends in the stage distribution. Specifically, the proportion of stage I tumors showed a recent increased from 41.1% during the 1996–2000 period to 66.5% in the 2011–2018 period. Since the launch of the national cancer screening program in Korea in 2002, the participation rate in gastric cancer screening has increased, leading to an increase in the detection of tumors at earlier stages [26]. Meanwhile, the consistently high 5-year CRS rates in patients who survived for 5 years after surgery can be attributed to advancements in treatment and reduced recurrence of gastric cancer [27,28]. Surpassing specific post-surgical markers indicates improved survival prospects. This trend provides a positive outlook for patients with gastric cancer.

The improvement in CRS over our study period (1996–2018) likely reflects not only advances in surgery but also evolving chemotherapy practice. Following publication of the ACTS-GC trial results in 2011 [29], the adoption of standardized adjuvant chemotherapy protocols fundamentally changed treatment patterns for stage II–III gastric cancer. The observed increase in baseline 5-year relative survival—from 71.3% (1996–2000) to 91.2% (2011–2018)—likely reflects the widespread uptake of adjuvant therapy during this period, particularly S-1 monotherapy and, later, the XELOX regimen [30].

Compared to distal gastrectomy, total gastrectomy demonstrated a markedly lower CRS, particularly in stages II and III. This discrepancy may be due to the poor compliance with postoperative chemotherapy after total gastrectomy. For instance, in the REGATTA trial, which compared the effects of gastrectomy followed by chemotherapy to those of chemotherapy alone, patients who underwent total gastrectomy received only half the median number of chemotherapy cycles. Conversely, patients who underwent distal gastrectomy showed good compliance with chemotherapy, similar to those treated with chemotherapy alone [31]. Moreover, in the MAGIC trial, only 75.9% of patients who underwent total gastrectomy completed the 3 planned cycles of postoperative chemotherapy [32].

Importantly, because patient-level chemotherapy data were unavailable, the observed differences in CRS by surgical procedure may reflect underlying differences in adjuvant chemotherapy receipt and completion rather than surgical factors alone. This limitation may have introduced bias, particularly for stage II and III patients, for whom adjuvant chemotherapy is standard of care and strongly associated with improved long-term survival. Without chemotherapy data, CRS estimates may be shifted when treated and untreated patients are mixed—potentially overestimating survival among those who did not receive appropriate adjuvant therapy and underestimating the benefit among those who completed planned regimens. The “statistical cure” threshold of 95% CRS observed at 5 years postsurgery may therefore be influenced by this heterogeneity. Prior studies show that completion of adjuvant chemotherapy is an independent prognostic factor (e.g., 5-year overall survival 80.0% vs 42.7% for completion vs non-completion) [33].

Nutritional challenges may also contribute to the reduced CRS observed after total gastrectomy. In stage I, patients who underwent total or proximal gastrectomy exhibited a lower 5-year CRS than those who underwent distal gastrectomy or pylorus-preserving gastrectomy, suggesting that outcomes may be influenced by factors beyond oncology, including nutrition. Malabsorption and malnutrition, which are common after total and proximal gastrectomy, can significantly affect the prognosis of patients with gastric cancer [34,35,36]. In our study, the persistently low CRS in stage III patients 5 years after total gastrectomy may also be substantially influenced by nutritional factors, especially given the rarity of recurrence at this point post-surgery. Therefore, patients who have undergone total or proximal gastrectomy may require more careful and prolonged follow-up for nutritional issues, extending beyond 5 years after surgery.

The primary strength of the present study lies in its use of high-quality clinical data collected from a comprehensive cancer center with a follow-up period of 26 years. This methodology enabled specific evaluation of the clinical stage and type of operation. Additionally, we linked the clinical data with mortality data from the National Statistical Office to minimize follow-up loss. We also standardized the tumor stages based on the sixth, seventh, and eighth editions of the Union for International Cancer Control TNM classification into the most recent TNM-8, thereby correcting for variations in stage classification over time [22,37,38].

This study has several limitations. First, we did not have chemotherapy-related data for the study period. Therefore, we could not assess the potential impact of chemotherapy on patients at stages II and III. Second, while RS considers death as the outcome, it is a concept distinct from disease-specific mortality or progression-free survival. Therefore, it is crucial to interpret its meaning carefully as it represents a narrow definition of survival metrics. Third, information on known modifiable risk factors for gastric cancer that could be associated with cancer survival, such as tobacco smoking and Helicobacter pylori infection, is not available [39,40]. Finally, the patients over 80 years of age in our cohort represent a selected surgical population—individuals deemed fit for general anesthesia and resection at preoperative assessment—and therefore our findings are not generalizable to the broader elderly population.

In conclusion, the 5-year CRS improved over time, particularly among patients deemed to be at a higher surgical risk. Considering that patients aged >80 years who underwent surgery showed CRS rates in stages I and III comparable to those of younger patients, it appears that with a thorough preoperative assessment, the elderly can anticipate safe surgical procedures and long-term survival. Five years after gastric cancer surgery, the CRS reaches a high level of 95%, but relatively low survival rates are observed after total or proximal gastrectomy. Therefore, careful long-term follow-up, including nutritional surveillance, may be necessary for these groups. In essence, CRS will be a useful resource in the clinic for explaining to patients and predicting prognosis based on the timing of their visits.
