Firm structure, organisational capacity, and employer health insurance provision in Oman: a cross-sectional mediation study
Authors: Neetu Kwatra, Nihad Al Zadjali, Sultana Al Sabahi, Zuhair Al Sulti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmployer mandates are a central mechanism for expanding universal health coverage (UHC). In Oman, the Dhamani platform a national digital health-insurance system mandates private-sector coverage under Capital Market Authority Resolution 78/2019. Full national rollout commenced in 2025, creating an urgent need for pre-implementation employer-readiness evidence.ObjectiveTo describe formal employer health-insurance provision rates across Omani private-sector firms, to identify firm structural factors independently associated with provision, and to assess whether organisational resource capacity mediates the firm size–provision relationship.MethodsCross-sectional employer survey (n = 222) conducted prior to full Dhamani enforcement, using stratified purposive sampling by firm size and sector. Multivariable logistic regression (Hosmer–Lemeshow calibration, ROC AUC discrimination, VIF multicollinearity checks, Box–Tidwell linearity test, Firth penalized sensitivity analysis) and bootstrapped mediation analysis (5,000 resamples; bias-corrected 95% CI as primary inferential result) were used.ResultsFormal provision was 36.5% overall (95% CI: 30.2–43.1%), ranging from 10.8% among micro-enterprises to 81.0% among large firms (Cramér’s V = 0.573, p < 0.001). Background

Universal health coverage and employer mandates

For health systems to achieve universal health coverage (UHC), they have to raise revenue, pool it across the population and purchase services equitably and efficiently [18]. Insurance mandates which are employer-based perform all 3 functions. They channel the revenue from insurance premiums through an employment relationship. They pool the risk within and across workplaces. Finally, they link the purchasing with the benefits package. Nonetheless, despite these theoretical advantages, mandate compliance falls sharply with firm size in every documented context. Fixed compliance costs per employee, the need for payroll integration, human-resources administration, liaison with insurers and documentation impose a higher relative burden on micro- and small enterprises than on larger firms. Large firms are better able to spread these costs over a larger payroll and have existing administrative systems [14, 15].

Between the CEBHI programme for compulsory employer-based health insurance in Saudi Arabia, there have been coverage gaps caused by firms with less than ten employees for more than a decade. This has been the case since enforcement cycles taking place over a number of years [2]. The same structural problem might be facing the pre-enforcement settings such as Oman.

The Omani health system and the Dhamani scheme

In Oman, the Ministry of Health is enlisted to manage and deliver essential health services to local citizens and a small number of expatriates. Around 44% of the labour force are non-nationals workers, mostly in the private sector. Private facilities have historically been the recipient of substantial out-of-pocket expenditure [28]. Before the rollout of Dhamani, private health expenditure was roughly one-third of total health expenditure concentrated in a narrow band of the lower wage of the private sector [4].

Dhamani (دماني Arabic for “guarantee” or “security”) is Oman’s national digital health-insurance platform, established under Capital Market Authority Resolution 78/2019 and overseen by the Financial Services Authority (FSA). The scheme requires private-sector businesses to insure the health of their workers, their spouses, and dependent children below 21. The standard Dhamani benefits package comprises: (a) inpatient hospitalization and surgery (annual sub-limit OMR 3000 per beneficiary); (b) outpatient consultations, diagnostic investigations, physiotherapy and prescribed medicines; and (c) emergency services with an overall annual ceiling of OMR 4500 per beneficiary. Dhamani has developed a digital architecture that facilitates the seamless connection between licensed insurers, third-party administrators (TPAs), the MOH, the Ministry of Labor, the Royal Oman Police, and private providers. This interoperable network allows for real-time eligibility verification, standardized claims submission, and monitoring in the Sultanate [25]. According to The Financial Services Authority [32], the platform processed around three million insurance transactions in early 2025. At the time of data collection, full enforcement, which would apply to micro- and small enterprises, had not yet started making the data a pre-enforcement baseline.

GCC regional comparators

Three GCC precedents are directly relevant. Saudi Arabia’s CEBHI (introduced 2006) phased implementation beginning with large employers and expatriate workers before extending to SMEs and Saudi nationals; persistent coverage gaps among small firms have been documented at each phase [2, 3]. The UAE (Dubai 2013, Abu Dhabi 2006) Established digital enrolment infrastructure, graduated penalties and TPA regulation. It has achieved high formal sector rates but lower compliance among micro-enterprises and domestic workers [28]. Workers in Bahrain who lack insurance coverage are entitled to benefits through a “unified digital platform” that is comparable to Dhamani. The scheme has faced issues extending coverage further, with a particular focus on the informal sector. Among the three comparators, we found that phased implementation, digital infrastructure, and targeted SME support were all necessary for broad employer compliance but not individually sufficient.

H1: Firm structural factors (size, sector, ownership) are independently associated with formal health-insurance provision after adjustment for organisational resource capacity.

H2: Organisational resource capacity partially mediates the association between firm size and formal health-insurance provision, as evidenced by a bootstrapped indirect effect (a × b) whose bias-corrected 95% CI excludes zero.

Note on H3: A moderation hypothesis positing that perceived barriers moderate the capacity–provision relationship was included in the original protocol. Because no interaction term was estimated in the logistic regression, moderation cannot be formally tested; H3 is therefore excluded from the formal hypotheses. Perceived barriers are reported descriptively in "Reported barriers among non-insuring firms" section.

Literature review

Health financing transition theory

Kutzins [18] and Carrin [9] proposes a framework that separates three interdependent functions of health financing revenue mobilisation, pooling and purchasing. He further argues that reforms at any one of these functions will merely shift problems to other functions unless these three functions are aligned. Wagstaff [34] re-examines these functions within social health insurance models, arguing that the transition to UHC via employer mandates can be hindered by administrative complexity and negative impacts on formal labor markets. Hadley and Reschovsky [15] argue that employer mandates are effective in revenue raising and pooling when firms are large and have strong administrative capacity,this advantage diminishes as firm size shrinks, however, because per-employee administrative costs rise steeply. The systematic avoidance of compulsory social insurance in the Lao PDR (Alkenbrack, Hanson, Lindelow 2015) is attributed to weak enforcement and administrative complexity, similar to the conditions in Oman pre-enforcement. According to a study by Mathauer, Saksena and Kutzin [20], lack of integration in large unions or employer pools raises per capita administrative costs, distorts risk-sharing efficiency. These outcomes strengthen the benefits of platform-based integration like Dhamani.

Institutional theory and employer compliance

DiMaggio and Powell [11] have described three isomorphic mechanisms for the institutionalism of organisation: coercive pressure (imposition of laws and regulatory sanctions), normative pressure (arising from expectations of professions and industries), and mimetic pressure (imitation of successful peers). As the Dhamani mandate is not yet enforced across all firm sizes, coercive pressure is still low 51.8% of non-insuring firms indicated absence of legal mandate as one of the barriers. The pressure to conform to norms is also limited. Where non provision is the norm, one does not deviate and there is no stigma in non compliance [30]. The interplay of feeble coercive and normative pressures is expected to generate low provision rates that are size-stratified, as evidenced by or in line with the data. Institutional theory thus informs H1’s prediction that structural factors retain a direct association with provision, independent of mediation by resource capacities.

Employer-sponsored health insurance: international evidence

The cross-national literature finds that structural predictor of health-insurance provision is firm size. According to Hadley and Reschovsky [15], US data shows that higher premiums induce non-compliance with the mandate. Buchmueller, DiNardo, and Valletta [8] find Hawaii’s employer mandate did not induce insurance provision – employment-structure adjustments were seen instead, among small firms who could not absorb the premium cost. However, Mendoza [21] notes that even when exempt from mandates, some small firms still offer insurance to remain competitive or meet internal organizational goals, providing a precedent for the baseline provision observed in pre-enforcement settings like Oman. As indicated by Banerjee et al. [5] in Indonesia, the joint proposal of premium subsidies and simplification of administration produced significant improvements in SME enrolment. In the GCC context, under the Saudi CEBHI, AlJohani and Bugis [2] reported persistent coverage gaps for SMEs. As reported in a scoping review by Reka et al. [28], employer-based GCC schemes are proliferating but employer-side capacity constraints remain understudied. This research paper fills that gap in the pre-implementation Omani context.

Organisational resource capacity: RBV and dynamic capabilities

In simpler terms, the Resource-Based View (RBV; [6]) says that, firms do not have the same resources, and it is those differences that determine why some firms can meet compliance and others cannot. To pay for employer health insurance, the relevant resources are: (a) Financial resources: Cash flow or credit sufficient to be able to pay the monthly premiums on time without any disruption to payroll or the organisation’s core operations,(b) Administrative Resources: Staff or systems that are capable of enrolling employees and managing documents, and liaising with the insurers or the TPAs (Third Party Administrator’s); and (c) Information Resources: Internal knowledge of what the regulations require and what kind of insurance products are available. Bigger firms typically have more of each type, and so resource capacity is expected to mediate, rather than just duplicate, the size–provision relationship (H2).

According to the Dynamic Capabilities Theory of [31], regulatory compliance also involves sensing regulatory change, acquiring product knowledge, and reconfiguring administrative routines. Firms with existing dynamic capabilities can carry out these operations faster and cheaper. According to Cashin et al. [10], the analogous logic was applied to reform of public financial management which confirmed that structural input-reform adoption effect was mediated by organisational capacity.

Perceived barriers

Premium costs impose acute cash-flow constraints on small firms, and wage-adjustment mechanisms that might offset mandate costs in other contexts are constrained in Oman by minimum wage floors [13, 23]. Administrative burden the learning, psychological, and compliance costs of regulatory requirements falls disproportionately on employers without dedicated human-resources staff [22]. Both cost and administrative barriers are policy-amenable: Indonesian and Lao PDR evidence demonstrates that subsidies and administrative simplification each improve SME enrolment [1, 5]. These barriers are documented descriptively in "Reported barriers among non-insuring firms" section.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional employer survey was conducted during the Dhamani pilot phase, before full enforcement across all firm sizes, to establish a pre-implementation baseline of formal health-insurance provision and organisational readiness. A cross-sectional design is appropriate for documenting baseline prevalence and identifying structural correlates at a defined point in time [12]. The survey instrument was prepared in parallel Arabic and English versions, pre-tested with ten firms from two sectors, and administered electronically through the Oman Chamber of Commerce and Industry (OCCI), sector-specific industry associations, and corporate networks.

Sampling frame and composition

The sampling frame was the taken from the registered private-sector companies data maintained by the OCCI (Oman Chamber of commerce), supplemented by sector-specific directories. Stratified purposive sampling with maximum-variation principles stratified by (a) firm size and (b) industry sector ensured all analytical subgroups were represented with sufficient density for multivariable analysis. Firm size is determined by the Oman’s Ministry of Commerce, Industry and Investment Promotion definitions: micro (1–10 employees), small (11–50), medium (51–150), and large (> 150). Micro-enterprises were oversampled relative to their national proportion because they represent the highest compliance risk; implications for generalizability are addressed in "Post-stratification weighted coverage estimate" and "Limitations" sections.

A total of 222 firms completed the survey. By size: micro n = 111 (50.0%), small n = 64 (28.8%), medium n = 26 (11.7%), large n = 21 (9.5%). By ownership: nationally owned 182 (82.0%), internationally owned or subsidiary 37 (16.7%), not specified 3 (1.4%). By sector: retail & trade 65 (29.3%), hospitality & tourism 40 (18.0%), construction 29 (13.1%), manufacturing 23 (10.4%), IT & technical services 19 (8.6%), other/mixed 46 (20.7%).

Variables and operationalisation

Outcome variable

Formal employer-provided health-insurance provision was coded 1 if the firm formally enrolled employees in a registered health-insurance product (through Dhamani or an equivalent licensed insurer) and 0 otherwise. Informal reimbursements or ad hoc health allowances were coded 0, as these do not constitute formal insurance under the Dhamani framework.

Structural predictors

Firm size was entered as three dummy variables (reference = Micro). Industry sector was entered as nominal dummies (reference = Retail & Trade). Ownership was coded as nationally owned (reference) versus internationally owned or subsidiary.

Organisational resource capacity

Capacity was operationalised as a composite index (range: 0–1) averaging two standardised Likert-scale dimensions, each scored 0–1 before averaging. Items were rated on a five-point response scale (1 = Strongly Disagree; 5 = Strongly Agree), then standardised to [0, 1] by applying the transformation (score − 1)/4 before computing dimension means.

Dimension 1: Administrative capacity (4 items)

This dimension captures the firm’s self-reported ability to manage insurance enrolment, process required documentation, and communicate effectively with insurers or third-party administrators. Items were adapted from Weiner’s [35] organisational readiness for change instrument and operationalised for the Dhamani compliance context:

Confounder selection and model-building rationale

The covariates were chosen in advance for two reasons: (a) each measured variable has a theoretical relevance, as it has been identified in the literature as a structural or contextual determinant of employer insurance provision; and (b) the survey instrument made them available. The structure was primarily influenced by the size of the organization. The rationale for including the industry sector is that sectors differ systematically by employment contract type, informality of workforce, and visibility of regulatory oversight [1, 2]. International subsidiaries inherit compliance from parent company's jurisdiction. As a result, ownership was included. As a predictor (H1) and a mediator, the capacity of the organisation’s resources was included. All pre-specified variables were included simultaneously in a forced entry model, without conducting a stepwise or data-driven selection. Check the model assumptions. In order to guarantee the linearity of logit, the Box and Tidwell procedure [7] has been applied to the continuous capacity predictor. The interaction term for capacity × ln(capacity) is non-significant (p = 0.34), confirming a linear association in log-odds. All VIFs indices for predictors were below 2.0 (Table 4), indicating no bias in estimates. Influential observations were evaluated for Cook’s distance values greater than 4/n = 0.018. Seven observations had larger values. Examination of the original survey records showed no implausible entries. All were included in the final model.

Bootstrapped mediation analysis

Mediation was evaluated by estimating the bootstrapped indirect effect (a × b) with 95% bias-corrected CI from 5,000 resamples as per MacKinnon, Lockwood, and Williams [19], Preacher and Hayes [26, 27] and recent health services research guidance [29, 33]. The bias-corrected 95% CI excludes zero if and only if the indirect effect is statistically significant, this is the only primary inferential criterion for H2. OLE regression estimates Path A (firm size → capacity),Paths B and C′ are estimated within a logit model. Because the outcome is binary, the indirect effect a × b works at the log-odds scale. Therefore, one should be careful with cross-model coefficient comparisons [29].

Multivariable logistic regression and sensitivity analysis

Formal health-insurance provision was regressed on firm size (three dummy variables, reference = Micro), sector dummies (reference = Retail & Trade), ownership (reference = nationally owned), and organisational resource capacity (continuous, 0–1). Results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (AOR = e^β) with 95% Wald CIs. Model performance was assessed on: (1) calibration Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit; (2) discrimination ROC AUC with 95% CI (good discrimination ≥ 0.80; [17], and (3) explained variance Nagelkerke pseudo-R2. All tests were two-tailed at α = 0.05.

Because medium and large firm strata are sparse (n = 26 and n = 21, respectively), a Firth penalized logistic regression [16] was run as a sensitivity analysis using the logistf package in R to evaluate whether extreme point estimates were artefacts of data sparsity or complete separation. Results are reported in Supplementary Table S3.

Results

Formal health-insurance provision by firm size

Among 222 firms, 81 (36.5%; exact 95% CI: 30.2–43.1%) provided formal health insurance at the time of the survey. Provision rates by size stratum were: micro 10.8% (12/111; 95% CI: 5.7–18.3%), small 50.0% (32/64; 37.4–62.6%), medium 76.9% (20/26; 56.4–91.0%), and large 81.0% (17/21; 58.1–94.6%). The chi-square test was statistically significant with a large effect size (χ2 = 72.88, df = 3, p < 0.001; Cramér’s V = 0.573; Table 2).

Provision by sector and ownership

By sector, provision rates were: manufacturing 47.8%, retail & trade 32.3%, IT & technical services 31.6%, hospitality & tourism 30.0%, and construction 20.7%. The sector chi-square test was non-significant (χ2 = 4.44, df = 4, p = 0.350; Cramér’s V = 0.159). By ownership, internationally owned or subsidiary firms had a provision rate of 67.6% (25/37) compared with 30.2% (55/182) among nationally owned firms (χ2 = 16.92, df = 1, p < 0.001; Cramér’s V = 0.278).

Organisational resource capacity: descriptive statistics

The mean composite capacity score was 0.587 (SD = 0.306; range: 0.04–0.99). The distribution was bimodal: micro-enterprise scores clustered below 0.30 and large/insuring-firm scores clustered above 0.70. The Pearson correlation between the capacity index and formal provision was r = 0.613 (p < 0.001); the correlation between capacity and firm size (ordinal) was r = 0.158 (p = 0.038).

The bootstrapped indirect effect was a × b = 0.413 (bias-corrected 95% CI: 0.189–0.687, p = 0.001, 5,000 resamples). This confidence interval excludes zero, confirming H2. The indirect effect represented 30.3% of the total size–provision association.

Multivariable logistic regression

Table 4 presents adjusted odds ratios from the multivariable logistic regression. The model showed acceptable calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow χ2 = 6.42, df = 8, p = 0.45) and good discrimination (ROC AUC = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.83–0.93). Nagelkerke pseudo-R2 = 0.589. All VIFs were below 2.0, confirming absence of multicollinearity. The Box–Tidwell test confirmed linearity in the logit for the capacity predictor (p = 0.34). Cook’s distance review of seven flagged observations identified no implausible data entries; all were retained.

Compared with micro-enterprises, small firms had AOR = 8.84 (95% CI: 3.17–24.66), medium firms AOR = 42.67 (8.59–212.02), and large firms AOR = 47.68 (8.14–279.38; all p < 0.001). Confidence intervals for medium and large strata are wide owing to small cell sizes (n = 26 and n = 21, respectively). The Firth penalized logistic regression sensitivity analysis confirmed that all size-category associations were consistent in direction with the main model, with point estimates that were attenuated (smaller in magnitude) but qualitatively similar, confirming the main results are not artefacts of sparse data or complete separation (Supplementary Table S3). Organisational resource capacity was the strongest predictor (AOR = 31.51, 95% CI: 11.82–84.01, p < 0.001). Construction was the only sector significantly different from the retail & trade reference category after full adjustment (AOR = 0.14, 95% CI: 0.03–0.64, p = 0.011). Ownership was not significant after adjustment and is omitted from Table 4 for parsimony.

Reported barriers among non-insuring firms

Among 141 non-insuring firms, the most common barrier was absence of a legal mandate (73 firms; 51.8%; 95% CI: 43.4–60.1%), followed by high premium costs (70 firms; 49.6%; 41.3–58.0%). Both barriers were co-cited by 42 firms (29.8% of non-insuring firms). Further barriers were lack of awareness of available options (30 firms; 21.3%; 14.8–29.0%) and limited budget (23 firms; 16.3%; 10.6–23.4%). These data are descriptive only (Table 5); no moderation analysis was conducted.

Coverage characteristics among insuring firms

Among 81 insuring firms, basic coverage (emergency and inpatient only) was most common (49 firms; 60.5%; 95% CI: 48.9–71.3%). Comprehensive coverage (outpatient and preventive included) was provided by 19 firms (23.5%; 14.8–34.1%) and custom limited-benefit plans by 11 firms (13.6%; 7.0–22.9%). Dependent coverage extended to family members by 39 firms (48.1%; 36.9–59.5%). Claims were reported as smooth by 54 firms (66.7%; 55.3–76.7%); the most cited difficulties were claim-approval delays (10 firms; 12.3%) and complex documentation requirements (8 firms; 9.9%).

Post-stratification weighted coverage estimate

Because micro-enterprises were oversampled (50.0% of the study sample versus approximately 78% of all registered private-sector firms in Oman according to OCCI registry data; [28], the unweighted overall provision estimate of 36.5% reflects the study sample composition rather than the national employer population. Post-stratification weights were computed for each firm-size stratum as wᵢ = Nᵢ/nᵢ, where Nᵢ is the estimated national proportion and nᵢ is the stratum proportion in the sample. The 21.9% weighted estimate is considerably less than the unweighted estimate of 36.5%. This is because the micro-enterprises, having the lowest provision rate of 10.8%, are more numerous in the national firm population than in the sample used in the study. All regression and mediation analyses were performed using the observed sample and are thus not adjusted by these weights, which are designed to capture the association structure rather than the population prevalence estimate.

Employer attitudes toward government support

Out of the 222 firms, 182 (82.0%) were in favour of government support in the form of incentives that will lower the cost of insurance. Moreover, 187 (84.2%) of the firms showed interest in group insurance at a lower premium rate. Affordable group health plans were sought after by 80 organisations (36.0%) while SME-targeted subsidies were sought after by 79 organisations (35.6%). Of the 221 firms surveyed, 65.8% (146 firms) supported linking insurance to work visa.

Discussion

Principal findings and their interpretation

Pre-implementation survey of 222 Omani private-sector employers reveals three key trends. Generally speaking, formal health-insurance provision is strongly associated with firm size, ranging from 10.8% among micro-enterprises and 81.0% among large firms (Cramér’s V = 0.573). Insurance compliance is shaped by fixed costs that are found to be in line with a seven-fold gradient. Administrative costs per employee decline with scale, which gives a structural compliance advantage to bigger firms as documented by [15]. The weighted estimate of 21.9% (95% CI: 17.0–26.8%) after post-stratification shows that the unweighted 36.5% reflects oversampling of micro-enterprises in the study with the weighted likely being a better estimate of coverage among national employers.

The study’s mediation analysis revealed that organizational resources statistically accounted for 30.3% of the total size-provision association (indirect effect = 0.413, bias-corrected 95% CI: 0.189–0.687). Consistent with the Resource-Based View [6], larger firms command more financial and administrative resources, which are associated with formal insurance provision. An important direct size effect remains after controlling for capacity (Path C′ OR = 2.58, p = 0.003), which indicates that firm size is related to the provision through channels outside of resources and capacity, confirming coercive and normative pressures of institutional theory [11, 30].

Third, construction is the only sector with a significantly lower adjusted odds of provision relative to retail & trade (AOR = 0.14, p = 0.011), consistent with Saudi CEBHI evidence attributing persistent construction non-compliance to casual employment structures and agency-based labour supply chains [2]. The Firth sensitivity analysis confirmed that all main-model associations were consistent in direction, with attenuated but qualitatively similar point estimates for sparse strata, demonstrating that the principal findings are not artefacts of data sparsity. The model showed good discrimination (ROC AUC = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.83–0.93) and acceptable calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.45).

The SME compliance gap

A large share of non-compliant firms is made up of micro-enterprises whose owners are national. In terms of industry, non-compliance is most concentrated in construction, hospitality, and retail. In 42 firms (29.8% of non-insuring firms) faced both cost and mandate barriers. This situation is consistent with a compliance trap. Employers perceive insurance as unaffordable also claim the absence of a mandate as a justification. Both regulatory and financial support are required simultaneously for a change in behaviour [1]. The finding that 51.8% cited the absence of a legal mandate means that coercive pressure currently at an incipient stage is required for compliance from a sizeable segment of noninsuring employers.

Evidence-tied policy implications

Phased enrollment by firm size is indicated by the size gradient (provision range: 10.8% to 81.0%; Cramér’s V = 0.573). Large and medium firms already approach high provision rates; enforcing the mandate first among them reduces initial compliance friction before extending requirements to micro- and small firms. This phasing mirrors Saudi CEBHI design [3] and Indonesian JKN [5].

Limitations

Cross-sectional design

No causal direction can be established. All associations should be interpreted as correlational; “associated with” is used consistently throughout.

Micro-enterprise oversampling

The unweighted overall provision rate of 36.5% should not be interpreted as nationally representative without the post-stratification correction applied in "Post-stratification weighted coverage estimate" section. Stratum-specific rates are unaffected.

Self-reported measures

All variables, including the capacity index, were self-reported by employer representatives. Social desirability bias may underestimate administrative difficulty and overestimate capacity.

Pre-enforcement baseline

Data collection during the Dhamani pilot phase means some employers may have begun adjusting behaviour in response to nascent regulatory signals, partially attenuating the true baseline.

Sparse strata

Medium (n = 26) and large (n = 21) strata yield wide confidence intervals for their respective AORs. The Firth sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table S3) demonstrates robustness in direction and order of magnitude.

Conclusion

A survey involving 222 employers in the private sector in Oman shows the overall (post-stratification weighted estimate 36.5% (21.9%)) provision of formal health insurance ranges between 10.8% among micro-enterprises to 81.0% among large firms. Capacity of the organisation resource measured using eight-item validated composite index (α = 0.83) was the strongest independent predictor (AOR = 31.51) and a bootstrapped indirect effect of 0.413 (95% CI: 0.189–0.687) confirmed partial mediation of the size–provision link with the first accounting for 30.3% of the total effect. The model effectively discriminates with a ROC AUC score of 0.88. The sensitivity analysis of the Firth confirmed that the main-model estimates and order of magnitude are correct.

Oman may utilize experience of GCC and world. CEBHI in Saudi Arabia illustrated that small employers can remain exposed for a decade without a specific intervention [2].

Indonesia’s JKN showed that premium subsidies and administrative simplifications together accelerate SME enrolment according to Banerjee et al. [5]. The gradual enforcement of mandates in the UAE and Bahrain’s centralized insurance base each serve as nearby GCC models for Dhamani. The three suggestions indicated by evidence stand alone. Trial enrolment by firm size. Subsidising premiums for microenterprises. Ramp up administrative capacity support.

Dhamani’s administrative data, along with quasi-experimental evaluations of specific interventions, will require longitudinal tracking. This will be necessary both to move from mere associations to causal inference. Further, it will help in tracking coverage over time. This is particularly the case as rollout continues.
