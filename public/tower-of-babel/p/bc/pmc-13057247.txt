Integrating attractor dynamics and connectivity features for EEG-based dementia classification
Authors: Sepideh Zolfaghari, Elnaz Gholizadeh, Farnaz Garehdaghi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The precise differentiation between Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and frontotemporal dementia (FTD) presents a clinical challenge, as both conditions share overlapping symptoms yet diverge in their pathophysiological mechanisms and treatment strategies. Electroencephalography (EEG), characterized by its higher temporal resolution and widespread applicability, provides the opportunity to uncover subtle discrepancies in brain dynamics that may be difficult to detect with conventional neuroimaging techniques. This paper presents a system that combines nonlinear attractor-based features derived from phase-space representations with phase-locking value connectivity features to encapsulate both local and global brain dynamics. In this regard, resting-state EEG recordings from 36 AD patients, 23 FTD patients, and 29 healthy controls (HC) were preprocessed and analyzed to extract dynamic and network features. Multiple classifiers were then used to assess these features under stratified 10-fold cross-validation. The results showed that the support vector machine achieved the highest performance for AD vs. FTD (81.7%), logistic regression performed well for FTD vs. HC (81.0%), and gradient boosting reached 82.9% for AD vs. HC. These findings illustrate the capability of EEG as a low-cost diagnostic technique, suggest that attractor dynamics and connectivity can offer complementary perspectives on the brain alterations linked to dementia, and enhance classification performance.

Introduction

Dementia is a clinical syndrome characterized by progressive decline in memory, cognition, and behavior, severely impairing daily living1. While frontotemporal dementia (FTD) is less common (5–10%) and often presents at a younger age (usually 45–65 years)2, Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common subtype of dementia, accounting for 60–80% of dementia cases3,4. AD primarily affects short-term memory and causes visual memory impairment, while FTD causes profound personality changes, language difficulties, and sometimes motor symptoms due to degeneration of the frontal and temporal lobes5,6. FTD typically shows a more rapid progression than AD, with a median survival time of 6–8 years following diagnosis3. Since AD and FTD share overlapping clinical symptoms, this overlap can lead to misdiagnosis, emphasizing the necessity of a careful differential diagnosis.

Dementia impacts more than 50 million people worldwide, and despite its growing prevalence, there is still no definitive cure7. The traditional methods for diagnosing dementia rely on patient history, caregiver observations, neurological examinations, and standardized cognitive tests, such as the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE)8. In addition, various neuroimaging methods, including computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron emission tomography (PET), and single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT), along with more advanced techniques like functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), are frequently used to confirm neurodegeneration and distinguish between different subtypes of dementia9,10. For example, structural imaging often reveals cortical thinning in AD and FTD11. However, these methods have several drawbacks: they can be costly, less accessible, and sometimes have the risks of radiation or contrast agents12. In most cases, the diagnosis of dementia occurs after significant neuronal damage. These challenges highlight the strong need for tools that enable earlier detection, particularly in individuals at higher risk. Early identification of the disease may help to slow its progression and enhance treatment options for patients. In recent years, blood-based biomarkers have emerged as promising tools for screening neurodegeneration. These biomarkers include plasma phosphorylated tau species, especially p-tau217, as well as neurofilament light (NfL) chain and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP)13. Although these biomarkers offer advantages in terms of accessibility and minimal invasiveness, they provide limited information about functional brain dynamics and network-level alterations. In this regard, electroencephalography (EEG) presents a practical alternative. It is a non-invasive, inexpensive, and widely available method that records electrical activity from the scalp14,15. Its temporal resolution allows researchers to examine rapid brain dynamics and connection patterns that traditional imaging methods are often unable to capture16. Despite the constrained spatial resolution of EEG, using network-based studies across several electrodes can partially mitigate this problem17. Taken together, EEG emerges as a promising candidate for the early identification and differential diagnosis of dementia, including AD and FTD18.

Previous studies have extracted a wide range of EEG features to distinguish AD, FTD, and healthy controls (HC). In the time domain, measures such as mean, variance, interquartile range, standard deviation, and zero-crossing rate are among the methods used to capture overall signal dynamics19–21. AD typically shows reduced variability compared to other groups. Also, mean amplitude values in AD are higher in certain regions relative to FTD22. Frequency-domain analysis often reveals decreased alpha–beta activity in dementia, whereas HC generally exhibits higher overall power; additional measures include relative band power and global field power22–24. Connectivity-based features such as cross-correlation, coherence, phase-lag index (PLI), and amplitude envelope correlation (AEC) have revealed altered network interactions, with AD often showing stronger delta-alpha and delta-beta coupling than FTD17,20,25,26. Nonlinear and complexity-based descriptors, including Higuchi’s fractal dimension27, Katz’s fractal dimension, Lyapunov exponent, approximate entropy22, singular value decomposition entropy, and detrended fluctuation analysis21, have also demonstrated discriminative potential, with dementia patients generally showing lower complexity compared to HC28. These features are subsequently employed in machine learning classifiers, including support vector machines (SVM), random forests (RF), gradient boosting (GB), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), and deep learning models for differential diagnosis of AD, FTD, and HC.

The study of EEG signals in the phase space framework dates back several decades, but has attracted renewed attention in recent years, because these signals are inherently nonlinear, non-stationary, and even non-Gaussian, and as a result, their analysis with nonlinear and dynamic methods can capture information that may not be accessible through conventional linear analyses29,30. One of the most important tools in this field is phase space reconstruction (PSR), which is able to reveal subtle changes in the non-stationary EEG. Several applications of this approach have been reported, including seizure detection31, brain-computer interface (BCI)32, and other fields33. Phase space-based methods have also been used directly or indirectly in studies of AD and other dementias. For example, Wang et al. used the combination of PSR with the limited penetrable visibility graph to be able to reveal abnormal patterns and chaotic dynamics in the brains of AD patients34. Also, Zheng et al. used the recurrence complexity plot and extraction of indices such as recurrence Hurst and recurrence rate gradient, and found that recurrent complexity increases in the occipital regions of AD and FTD patients12. In addition, Cruzat et al. utilized the irreversibility index. In this work, it has been reported that the healthy brain, as a dynamic and living system, is always producing complexity and forward flow, while in Alzheimer’s, this property is reduced35.

Despite extensive research on dementia, particularly AD, accurate diagnosis remains a challenge. Most EEG studies on dementia have focused on traditional time-domain and frequency-domain features, perhaps overlooking more intricate, nonlinear patterns and network-level alterations that represent brain functionality. This work takes a different approach by combining two complementary perspectives. On one hand, attractor-based features capture subtle changes in the dynamics of brain activity. On the other hand, phase-locking value (PLV) connectivity measures describe how different brain regions synchronize with each other. Using EEG recordings from individuals with established AD, FTD, and HC, this combined feature set was evaluated across several machine learning classifiers to assess its potential as a supportive tool complementing standard clinical assessments.

This study analyzed EEG signals to extract feature sets and evaluate classification performance in three pairwise comparisons (FTD vs. HC, AD vs. HC, and FTD vs. AD). Data description

The dataset for this study consisted of resting-state EEG recordings collected by Miltiadous et al.4 from 36 patients with AD, 23 patients with FTD, and 29 age-matched HC. Their demographic characteristics are shown in Table 1. The disease duration was measured in months (median = 25), and diagnoses were made based on established clinical criteria for AD and FTD. The MMSE test, an internationally recognized tool with scores ranging from 0 to 30, was used to assess cognitive and neuropsychological functions; lower scores indicate more severe impairment. Clinical diagnoses of AD and FTD were then established by experienced neurologists based on criteria provided by the diagnostic and statistical manual of mental disorders (DSM-III-R/DSM-IV), ICD-10, and the national institute of neurological and communicative disorders and stroke–Alzheimer’s disease and related disorders association (NINCDS-ADRDA).

EEG signals were recorded by 19 electrodes (Fp1, Fp2, F7, F3, Fz, F4, F8, T3, C3, Cz, C4, T4, T5, P3, Pz, P4, T6, O1, O2) positioned on the scalp according to the 10–20 system and two reference electrodes (A1 and A2) placed on the mastoids. The sampling rate was set at 500 Hz with a resolution of 10 µV/mm. According to clinical protocol, subjects were seated comfortably and told to keep their eyes closed during the recordings.

Preprocessing

The EEG dataset was the preprocessed version, in which several steps had been applied to remove artifacts using EEGLAB-based pipelines36. First, signals were filtered with a Butterworth band-pass filter (0.5–45 Hz) and re-referenced to the average of A1-A2 electrodes. Next, the artifact subspace reconstruction (ASR) algorithm was employed to automatically eliminate segments with abnormal or noisy data activity37. To improve signal quality, independent component analysis (ICA) with the RunICA algorithm was performed, decomposing the signals into independent components38. Artifact-related components, such as those reflecting eye movements and jaw artifacts, were identified with the ICLabel tool and removed from the data39.

Additional preprocessing steps were applied to a fixed 4-minute segment (from minute 2 to minute 6) extracted from each cleaned recording. This ensured identical duration across all subjects while providing sufficient data for reliable feature extraction and minimizing potential non-stationarities at the very beginning of the recordings. Signals were filtered in the 8–30 Hz range to target alpha and beta rhythms, then downsampled to 128 Hz. When the signals were plotted, large peaks appeared in several channels (see Fig. 2), which were probably caused by artifacts. To mitigate their influence, a soft outlier reduction strategy was implemented. Instead of eliminating extreme values, this technique subtly reduces their dominance in the data while maintaining the signal’s general shape40. The method is inspired by robust statistical techniques such as winsorization41, logarithmic compression used in EEG preprocessing42 and soft-thresholding ideas in signal denoising. Classification

In this study, classification was performed using a pairwise strategy. Rather than training a single three-class model, independent binary classifiers were trained and evaluated for each diagnostic comparison (FTD vs. HC, AD vs. HC, and FTD vs. AD). For each subject, features were concatenated to form a subject-level feature vector. To prevent information leakage, all features were z-standardized within the training set of each cross-validation fold, and the same scaling parameters were applied to the corresponding test set. Features were sent to a set of classifiers, including SVM, logistic regression (LR), multi-layer perceptron (MLP), KNN, and GB. Hyperparameter values considered for each classifier are reported in Table 3. To ensure class balance across folds, classification performance was assessed with stratified 10-fold cross-validation (random state = 42). In each fold, models were trained on 90% of the data and assessed on the remaining 10%. Predictions pooled across folds were used to compute a confusion matrix and derive standard diagnostic metrics: accuracy, recall, specificity, precision, F1-score, balanced accuracy, 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC). All analyses were conducted in a Spyder environment (Python 3.12) using scikit-learn and related libraries.

Results and discussion

The proposed framework was applied to EEG recordings from HC, individuals with FTD, and patients with AD. Attractor-based and phase synchronization connectivity features were extracted to capture group-specific neural dynamics and were evaluated using machine learning classifiers for their ability to discriminate between clinical groups. To illustrate the dynamical differences, Fig. 3 presents exemplar attractors from a single EEG channel for a representative subject from each group (AD, FTD, and HC). Table 4 summarizes the classification results for distinguishing AD patients from HC. Overall, all classifiers achieved balanced accuracies above chance level, indicating that the proposed EEG-derived features contain discriminative information associated with AD-related neural alterations. Among the evaluated models, the GB classifier demonstrated the strongest performance, achieving the highest balanced accuracy (81.7% ± 11.5) and the largest MCC (66.3% ± 22.9). This superior performance was further supported by a relatively narrow 95% CI for balanced accuracy (75–89%), reflecting relatively stable discrimination between AD and HC subjects. The MLP classifier achieved the second-best performance, with a balanced accuracy of 71.3% ± 14.4 and a 95% CI of 62–79%, indicating moderate but consistent classification capability. SVM and LR showed comparable results, yielding balanced accuracies of 69.2% ± 13.3 and 70.4% ± 17.0, respectively, with overlapping CI. In contrast, KNN exhibited the weakest performance, with the lowest balanced accuracy (67.5% ± 18.3) and the widest CI (56–78%) suggesting greater variability and reduced robustness.

The confusion matrices presented in Fig. 5 provide further insight into classifier behavior. The GB model achieved the most balanced recall–specificity profile, with high recall (85.0%) and specificity (78.3%). The MLP classifier showed moderate balance (recall = 79.2%, specificity = 63.3%), while SVM, LR, and KNN exhibited comparatively lower recall and specificity. To statistically validate these findings, pairwise comparisons were conducted on fold-wise balanced accuracy values using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test from 10 repeats of 10-fold cross-validation, with the Holm correction applied. The results confirmed that GB outperformed SVM, LR, MLP, and KNN (pholm < 0.01), suggesting superior performance for AD vs. HC classification within this framework.

Taken together, the present results suggest that EEG-derived attractor-based features capture salient nonlinear and group-specific neural dynamics relevant to differentiating AD, FTD, and HC. While PLV-based connectivity measures showed limited standalone discriminative power, their integration with attractor features was associated with improved or more stable performance for certain classifiers, particularly in the challenging FTD vs. AD classification task. This pattern indicates that dynamical and connectivity features may reflect complementary aspects of neural function, with nonlinear descriptors characterizing local temporal–geometric properties of EEG signals and connectivity measures capturing large-scale phase synchronization. In this context, comparisons with previous studies (Table 7) are intended to provide a qualitative perspective rather than direct performance equivalence.

To further examine spatial patterns of EEG dynamics, a regional analysis was conducted using the full set of attractor-based features. Feature values were grouped into five standard cortical regions (frontal, central, parietal, temporal, and occipital) for the HC, AD, and FTD cohorts. To mitigate the influence of outliers, values were clipped to the 5th–95th percentile range across subjects before computing group-level averages. Regional means were then calculated, and group-wise differences (HC–AD, HC–FTD, and AD–FTD) were visualized as heatmaps using a binary colormap (Fig. 15).

Regional analysis of attractor features revealed distinct, region-specific patterns across the cortical areas. In the HC–AD comparison, frontal and central regions showed a mix of increases and decreases, while parietal, temporal, and occipital regions mostly showed increases. For HC–FTD, decreases were primarily limited to the frontal region, with other areas showing modest elevations. In the AD–FTD contrast, frontal and temporal regions exhibited decreases, whereas the remaining regions generally increased. These observations broadly align with known pathophysiological trends in dementia, where frontal and temporal cortices are particularly vulnerable. However, given the sensitivity of nonlinear attractor metrics to inter-subject variability and EEG noise, these results should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory indicators rather than definitive regional differences.

From an applied perspective, these findings suggest that the proposed EEG-based framework may serve as an adjunctive decision-support tool alongside established diagnostic modalities, including neuroimaging, neuropsychological assessments, and other biomarkers. The method is not intended to replace existing diagnostic procedures but rather to provide complementary functional information that could assist clinicians in differential diagnosis within memory clinic and neurology settings. In this context, maintaining a balanced sensitivity–specificity profile is clinically desirable, as it helps minimize both missed cases and unnecessary follow-up investigations, consistent with the proof-of-concept nature of the current study.

While early or diagnostically ambiguous cases are of clear clinical interest, the present analysis is limited to individuals with established AD and FTD. Consequently, any potential application of this approach in early-stage populations remains hypothetical and requires validation in future studies, including cohorts with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), early AD, and early behavioral variant FTD (bvFTD).

From a practical standpoint, the proposed pipeline is largely automatable once EEG recordings are available, covering preprocessing, feature extraction, and classification. However, translation to routine clinical practice demands careful attention to operational variability. Differences in electrode montage, channel density, acquisition hardware, and recording conditions may affect feature stability and classification performance. Standardized resting-state protocols, adequate channel coverage, consistent referencing, and robust artifact handling are therefore essential. Validation across heterogeneous datasets is also necessary to identify potential limitations, including low-density systems or recordings contaminated by noise.

Finally, an important clinical limitation is potential confounding by disease stage and cognitive severity. Marked differences in global cognitive impairment between groups suggest that part of the observed classification performance, particularly for dementia versus control discrimination, may reflect general severity rather than disease-specific neural signatures. Hence, the present results should not be interpreted as evidence for early or preclinical diagnosis. Future work using severity-matched or stratified cohorts, or models that adjust for clinical variables such as MMSE, will be needed to clarify the incremental value of EEG features beyond global cognitive decline.

Conclusion

To categorize AD, FTD, and HC using EEG, this study investigated a complementary framework that combines attractor-based dynamics and connectivity measures. The method captures facets of the brain by combining large-scale synchronization features with a nonlinear temporal pattern function that traditional analyses frequently overlook. The results indicated that, depending on the diagnostic scenario, different classifiers tend to be more effective: SVM was the most accurate for AD vs. FTD, LR for FTD vs. HC, and GB for AD vs. HC. This suggests that EEG-based features may provide valuable information for identifying brain alterations associated with dementia, and that matching feature types with suitable classifiers can improve diagnostic accuracy.

However, several limitations should be considered. All recordings were resting-state EEG, which may be less sensitive to disease-specific alterations observable during task-based activity, and the dataset size was relatively small, which might limit generalizability. In addition, functional connectivity was summarized using global PLV-based network measures, which provide a compact representation but may not fully capture spatially localized or frequency-specific interactions. PLV is also known to be sensitive to volume conduction and zero-lag coupling, and the use of a broad 8–30 Hz frequency band may obscure rhythm-specific effects. Additionally, diagnostic labels for AD and FTD were assigned by experienced neurologists based on established clinical criteria; however, the dataset did not provide systematic information on biomarker or imaging confirmation and independent adjudication. This label uncertainty should be considered when interpreting classifier performance and generalizability. Finally, this study focused on classical machine learning methods using nonlinear features, leaving room to explore the effectiveness of deep learning or multimodal approaches. Future research will investigate leakage-robust and band-specific connectivity measures (e.g., PLI or wPLI), analyze longitudinal data to track disease progression, expand validation to larger cohorts, and integrate EEG with other biomarkers.
