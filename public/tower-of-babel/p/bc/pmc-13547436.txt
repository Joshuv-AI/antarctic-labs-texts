Differences in HIV testing may determine the first pillar of the HIV care continuum in a cross‐border setting
Authors: Hanne M L Zimmermann, Sarah E Stutterheim, Dominique van Beckhoven, Wim vanden Berghe, Jessika Deblonde, Sandrine Detandt, Ulrich Marcus, Ben Serrien, Ard van Sighem, Kai J Jonas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn border regions like the EuRegio Meuse‐Rhine (EMR), mobility does not stop at the border. HIV risk may therefore not only be determined by within‐country factors but also by between‐country differences. We explored between‐country differences in HIV indicators in the EMR using two complementary data‐driven approaches.MethodsFirst, we used 2019–2021 surveillance data from the German Robert Koch Institute, the Belgian Health Institute Sciensano and the Dutch HIV Monitoring Foundation to report on the EMR‐specific 95‐95‐95 goals. Second, we used the cross‐sectional EMIS‐2017 survey, including (trans)men who have sex with men (MSM), to report on determinants of HIV testing in the EMR. We employed multilevel multinomial regression modelling to identify sociodemographic factors associated with recent (<1 year), non‐recent (≥1 year) and never testing for HIV, with a random effect for between‐country effects.ResultsBased on 2021 surveillance data, the estimated number of individuals with HIV is 24 895, with 2236 individuals undiagnosed (9%). Countries differ most on the first pillar of the 95–95‐95 goals: 95–93–96 in the Dutch, 90–98‐96 in the German and 96‐94‐97 in the Belgian EMR‐region, respectively. In EMIS‐2017, half of MSM (n = 1335/2669) tested recently, 26% tested not‐recently (n = 693) and 24% (n = 641) never tested for HIV; with 8% of HIV‐testing variance explained by between‐country differences. Non‐recent and never‐testing were both associated with socio‐demographic factors, suggesting that some key populations (e.g., with transgender identity) and lack of resources determined differences in HIV testing.ConclusionTargeted approaches to address regional and socio‐demographic differences are urgently needed, especially to tackle HIV‐testing barriers and reach individuals undiagnosed.Keywords: care continuum, Europe, HIV prevention, HIV testing, sexual behaviour, surveillance

INTRODUCTION

In many Western European countries, the number of new HIV infections has been steadily declining, and the UNAIDS goals to reach 95% of individuals with HIV diagnosed, 95% of diagnosed individuals on treatment, and 95% of individuals on treatment virally suppressed [1] are within reach on the national level. While this is promising, regional differences exist within and between countries. For example, in the Netherlands, more rapid decreases in HIV diagnoses and higher proportions of individuals using pre‐exposure prophylaxis (PrEP), entering care rapidly and achieving viral suppression are often seen in larger cities [2, 3, 4, 5], leaving less‐urban regions behind [4, 6].

A unique context that has received less attention is border regions, where several countries are in proximity. The EuRegio Meuse Rhine (EMR) serves as an example and consists of the province of Limburg in the Netherlands, the region of Aachen in Germany, the province of Belgian Limburg, the province of Liège and the German‐speaking community in Belgium. All share similar characteristics such as the presence of student cities, having an industrial or mining past, and their citizens being of diverse socio‐economic and migration status. Most importantly, in the EMR, mobility does not stop at the border, while health care systems do. This may contribute to different health outcomes, including different HIV risk profiles (e.g., due to differential access to HIV testing or PrEP). For example, in EMR‐GER, HIV testing is available at specialized clinics and community‐based checkpoints in cities and often requires co‐payment, while testing done at a GP in EMR‐GER is often free. In contrast, testing at the GP in EMR‐NL and EMR‐BE requires a co‐payment. Additionally, individuals are often not able to test at the closest location if this is across the border. HIV risk is therefore not only determined by factors such as sexual practices and access to sexual healthcare services within one country, but also by cross‐border determinants such as differential testing policies, greater distance to clinics, language constraints, a reduced sense of community and more stigma as compared to larger urban areas, all of which has been linked to lower uptake of HIV testing [7, 8, 9, 10, 11].

Regional approaches require appropriate interventions targeted to the region's key populations and context. We used a cross‐border approach to propose priorities for improving the HIV response in the EMR. A useful tool to define priorities is the HIV continuum of care (CoC). These indicators are usually reported on a national level only and lack the comparative focus of multiple countries on a regional level of analysis. However, insight is also needed into the underlying behaviour and determinants that may explain differences in CoC indicators across regions. Therefore, we set out to describe region‐specific HIV‐prevention and care indicators using two existing but complementary data sources: surveillance and survey data. Our aim was three‐fold: (RQ1) to provide insight into the CoC and the number of newly diagnosed HIV infections for each specific EMR‐region using surveillance data. In addition, we used existing survey data from the European Men‐Who‐Have‐Sex‐With‐Men (MSM) Internet Survey (EMIS‐2017) to explore differences in HIV/STI testing and HIV prevention between the specific EMR‐regions and their respective countries (RQ2); and to identify socio‐demographic variables associated with recent, non‐recent or never‐testing for HIV (RQ3).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Surveillance data: EMR‐specific HIV continuums of care and newly diagnosed HIV infections (RQ1)

We used aggregated routinely collected surveillance data for 2019–2021 from the German Robert Koch Institute, the Belgian Health Institute Sciensano and the Dutch Stichting hiv monitoring (HIV Monitoring Foundation, SHM) to report on the EMR‐specific CoC and number of newly diagnosed HIV infections. We report on the overall CoC for the EMR and stratified by EMR‐region. We also report on the number of persons with HIV and newly diagnosed HIV infections, stratified by key population (i.e., MSM, all other men and women) and region of origin (i.e., European origin and also as originating from within or outside the respective EMR‐country). We used the CoC working definitions used by each of the country's surveillance institutions. Table 1 describes these definitions and provides comparisons between definitions. For comparability, we limited the HIV CoC to four stages: (1) estimated number of people living with HIV; (2) proportion of those diagnosed and linked to care; (3) proportion of those ever diagnosed using ART; and (4) proportion of those ever treated who had a suppressed viral load (VL) <200 copies/ml at last measurement. The CoCs for EMR‐NL and EMR‐GER extended beyond the formal EMR‐region as defined by official administrative divisions (Supplementary Figure S1) because of data aggregation at this level in the surveillance data.

EMIS‐2017: EMR‐specific sexual behaviour, HIV prevention and testing among MSM only (RQ2 and RQ3)

We used cross‐sectional data from EMIS‐2017 (MSM only) to explore differences in sexual behaviour, HIV prevention awareness and use and HIV/STI testing across EMR‐regions and compared to their respective countries. Additionally, we identified the socio‐demographics associated with recent, non‐recent or never‐testing for HIV. Design and methods for EMIS‐2017 have been described in detail elsewhere [12]. In brief, EMIS‐2017 was a cross‐sectional survey set out to understand changes over time in the behaviour, needs and interventions affecting HIV incidence among MSM in Europe [12]. EMIS‐2017 constitutes a convenience sample without incentive for which participants were recruited online between October 2017 and January 2018. This study includes all EMIS‐eligible participants from Belgium, the Netherlands and Germany, with EMR‐specific responses selected based on available postal codes aligned with the administrative division shown in Supplementary Figure S1 (see Supplementary Table S1 for included postal codes).

Ethical approval for EMIS was obtained from the Observational Research Ethics Committee at the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine (review reference 14 421/RR/8805). All participants provided informed consent.

Measures

Socio‐demographic variables included self‐identified gender (cis‐man/trans men), age‐group (<20, 20–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, ≥60 years), city size (large/medium city ≥100 000 habitants, small city 10 000–100 000 habitants, country‐side <10 000 habitants), being born outside country of residence, relationship status (single, steady partner, not sure/complicated), educational attainment (less than secondary education, at least secondary education, at least tertiary education), employment (employed/unemployed), financial stability (comfortable, neutral, uncomfortable), sexual orientation (gay/homosexual, bisexual, heterosexual/straight, other), outness (not out, partially out, completely out) and HIV‐status (never tested for HIV, HIV‐negative at last test, HIV‐positive).

Sexual behaviour variables included number of sex partners (≥10 vs. <10), number of condomless anal sex (CAS) partners (≤2 vs. >2), chemsex (defined as use of GHB/GBL, crystal meth, mephedrone, ketamine, cocaine around sex) and frequency of paying for or selling sex, all in the past 12 months.

HIV prevention use, awareness and HIV/STI testing were measured among HIV‐negative or unknown HIV status participants, and included timing of the last HIV and STI test (recent: in past 12 months, non‐recent: >12 months, and never‐tested); confidence accessing PEP (yes/no), awareness (yes/no), knowledge (yes/no) and use of PrEP (yes/no); and awareness of (yes/no) and being vaccinated against hepatitis A and B (yes/no). Among participants ever tested for HIV, the location of last HIV test (general practitioner (GP), private clinic, national equivalent of a sexual health clinic (STI clinic, community health service or HIV reference centre), hospital setting, blood bank, home‐based, outreach, elsewhere) was also included. Participants never been tested for HIV were asked if they were ever offered an HIV test (yes/no) and aware of where to test (yes/no).

Data analysis RQ2: Between‐ and within‐country differences in sexual behaviour, HIV prevention use and awareness and HIV/STI testing

In this descriptive analysis, we explored, using EMIS‐2017, between‐ and within‐country differences in socio‐demographic variables, sexual behaviour, HIV prevention use and awareness and HIV/STI testing. We compared these variables between EMR‐regions (BE‐EMR, GER‐EMR, NL‐EMR) and between each EMR‐region and their respective country (BE‐total, GER‐total, NL‐total) using Pearson's χ2 test or Fisher's exact test for categorical data, as appropriate.

Data analysis RQ3: Determinants of recent, non‐recent and never testing for HIV

We employed multilevel multinomial regression modelling to identify socio‐demographic factors associated with recent (<1 year), non‐recent (≥1 year) or never‐testing for HIV in EMIS‐2017, with a random effect to jointly explore between‐country differences. First, to ascertain the variance between EMR‐regions, we fitted a null two‐level model with only a random intercept. Next, to identify determinants, we added all socio‐demographic variables to the two‐level model with only the random intercept. A backward selection approach was applied to select significant explanatory variables. The Akaike information criterion (AIC) was used to assess the goodness of fit of models (AIC null‐model: 6156; AIC final model: 5813). Associations were represented by odds ratios (OR) with 95% CIs. Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated for the final model to estimate the influence of the EMR‐regions.

All analyses were carried out with R software (version R 4.0.4 GUI 1.70).

EMR‐specific HIV care continuum and newly diagnosed HIV infections 2019–2021

Regarding the 95‐95‐95 goals, we estimated that, in 2021, overall, 91% of all people living with HIV were diagnosed and linked to care (+3% since 2019), 98% of those linked to care were on ART (+1% since 2019), and 96% (no change) of those on ART were virally suppressed. EMR‐regions differed most on the first stage of the continuum, with an overall CoC in 2021 of 95%–93%‐96% in the Dutch, 96%‐94%‐97% in the Belgian and 90%‐98%‐96% in the German EMR‐regions, respectively.

MSM accounted for two‐thirds of people with HIV in the EMR between 2019 and 2021, although their relative contribution differed between EMR‐regions (61%–62% in the EMR‐NL, 63%–64% in EMR‐GER, 38%–40% in EMR‐BE, Table 3 and Supplementary Tables S4 and S5). In EMR‐BE, women and MSM each accounted for about one‐third of persons with HIV, while women constituted only about 20% in EMR‐NL and EMR‐GER. The majority of people with HIV were of European origin, but this varied between regions, with 89% in EMR‐GER (76% born in Germany in 2021), 69% in EMR‐NL (61% born in the Netherlands) and 54% in EMR‐BE (45% Belgian nationality) originating from Europe. The proportion of people of European origin was lowest among women with HIV in each of the regions (data not shown).

In 2021, 392 new HIV diagnoses were registered in the EMR (Table 3), marking a 28% decrease compared to 2019 (Supplementary Table S4). Of these, 19 new diagnoses were registered in EMR‐NL (−21%), 70 in EMR‐BE (−28%) and 303 in EMR‐GER (−28%). In all EMR‐regions, most newly diagnosed HIV infections were among MSM and people of European origin. Compared to 2019, the share of new diagnoses decreased in MSM in EMR‐NL and EMR‐GER but increased in EMR‐BE, and in EMR‐NL, also in people with a non‐European migration background.

EMR‐specific socio‐demographic variables among MSM

We included 25 796 MSM from EMIS‐2017 residing in the Netherlands, Belgium or Germany, with 3038 (11.8%) reporting EMR postal codes. Most MSM reported residing in EMR‐GER (n = 2562, 84.3%), followed by 342 in EMR‐BE (11.3%) and 134 in EMR‐NL (4.4%, Table 4). The majority identified as cis‐men (overall: n = 3011, weighted proportion (%w) 99.3%w) and were aged 20–50 years (overall: n = 2149, 90.4%w). Most lived in large/medium cities (overall: n = 2070, 66. %w), although, in EMR‐GER (74.4%), the proportion was significantly higher than in EMR‐NL (35.3%) and EMR‐BE (38.9%, p < 0.001). A small proportion of participants (overall: n = 344, 11.3%w) were born outside their EMR‐country, which differed by EMR‐region (EMR‐NL: 17.9%; EMR‐GER: 10.7%; EMR‐BE: 13.2%; p = 0.020). Most participants were either single (overall: n = 1454, 48.3%w) or had a steady partner (overall: n = 1386, 45.2%w), although relationship status differed between EMR‐regions (p = 0.029). Most participants had at least a tertiary education (overall: n = 2318, 61.1%w), but this proportion was significantly lower in EMR‐BE (n = 172, 54.1%; p = 0.003). Relatively more participants reported unemployment in EMR‐BE (10.6%, p < 0.001), while more financial instability was reported in EMR‐GER (14.4%, p < 0.001). Overall and in each EMR‐region, most participants identified as gay or homosexual (overall: n = 2446, 80.2%w) and slightly more than half were open about their sexuality to (almost) all people (overall: n = 1687, 55.6%w). Overall, 350 (11.3%w) reported being HIV‐positive, 2029 (67.2%w) reported being HIV‐negative, and 641 (21.6%w) were never tested for HIV. HIV status differed between EMR‐regions with higher proportions of never tested individuals in EMR‐BE (21.8%) and EMR‐GER (21.5%) compared to EMR‐NL (14.2%, p = 0.033). In terms of socio‐demographic variables, EMR‐regions were similar to their respective countries, except that more participants lived in large/medium cities outside the EMR‐regions (p < 0.001 for all). Most differences were between EMR‐BE and Belgium, with more participants being born outside Belgium, having lower educational attainment and less financial stability, and higher proportions of never being tested for HIV in EMR‐BE (p < 0.001 for all).

EMR‐specific sexual behaviour, HIV prevention, STI and HIV testing and HIV‐related characteristics among MSM

Among HIV‐negative participants, 50.6% had been recently tested for HIV, and 43.4% for other STIs, 25.1% had not recently tested for HIV, and 19.1% not recently tested for other STIs, and 24.3% had never tested for HIV, and 37.5% had never tested for other STIs. Recent testing rates for HIV and STI were highest in EMR‐NL (64.2% and 62.3%, respectively), followed by EMR‐BE (60.7% and 52.9%, respectively) and lowest in EMR‐GER (47.8% and 40.9%, respectively, p < 0.001 for both). Never testing was slightly less common in EMR‐NL and EMR‐GER compared to the national testing prevalence (15.8% vs. 17.9% and 23.5 vs. 27.1%, respectively), but higher in EMR‐BE compared to the prevalence of testing in all of Belgium (23.6 vs. 15.1%). The location where participants were last tested for HIV differed strongly by EMR‐region (p < 0.001). In EMR‐NL, most HIV tests were conducted at an STI clinic (73.3%), whereas in EMR‐GER and EMR‐BE, HIV tests were more evenly distributed between the GP and sexual health clinics (in EMR‐GER: 36.4% and 36.3%, respectively; in EMR‐BE: 55.2% and 25.9%). More than half of participants reported lacking confidence to access PEP (overall: n = 1418, 55.7%), which was highest in EMR‐NL (63.3%), followed by EMR‐GER (57.3%) and lowest in EMR‐BE (50.2%). Lacking confidence was higher in each of the EMR‐regions when compared to the rest of their respective countries (p = 0.019). About one‐third (34.4%) were unaware of PrEP, with lower PrEP awareness in EMR‐GER (34.1% unaware) and EMR‐BE (35.7% unaware). Awareness in EMR‐NL and EMR‐BE was also lower compared to the national average (p = 0.037; p < 0.001, respectively). PrEP use and having previously been offered PrEP were most common in EMR‐BE (5.3% and 17.0%, respectively). About two‐thirds reported having been vaccinated for hepatitis A and B (overall: 60.1% and 60.4%, respectively), but rates were lower in EMR‐BE (52.5% and 54.6%, p = 0.001 and p = 0002, respectively). Among never‐tested participants, 8.6% had previously been offered an HIV test (n = 54), with the highest proportion in EMR‐BE (17.6%). Additionally, 34.9% (n = 217) reported being unaware of where to test for HIV. Sexual behaviour variables did not differ by EMR regions.

Determinants of recent, non‐recent and never testing for HIV in the EMR among MSM

In the EMR, half of MSM (n = 1335/3038) had been recently tested, 26% were not recently tested (n = 693) and 24% (n = 641) had never been tested for HIV. In the final multilevel multivariable multinomial regression model (Table 5), 8% of the HIV‐testing variance was explained by the multilevel random effect (7.6% for non‐recent; 8.3% for never‐testing) compared to the full model, with both outcomes being significantly more likely in EMR‐GER compared to EMR‐NL (non‐recent: aOR = 2.17, 95% CI = 1.25–3.79, p = 0.006; never: aOR = 1.91, 95% CI = 1.03–3.55, p = 0.040). In addition, non‐recent testing and never‐testing were both associated with age (per 10y increase: non‐recent: aOR = 1.30, 95% CI = 1.19–1.41; never: aOR = 0.83, 95% CI = 0.76–0.91) and relationship status (non‐recent with having a steady partner: aOR = 1.69, 95% CI = 1.35–2.21; never‐testing with a complicated relationship: aOR = 0.51, 95% CI = 0.30–0.85). Both outcomes were less likely among those completely ‘out’ (non‐recent: OR = 0.73, 95% CI = 0.54–0.98; never: OR = 0.21, 95% CI = 0.16–0.28) and among those with more than 2 casual partners in the past 12 months (non‐recent: OR = 0.30, 95% CI = 0.22–0.40; never: OR = 0.37, 95% CI = 0.27–0.49), but more likely among those reporting to be financially struggling (non‐recent: OR = 1.48, 95% CI = 1.06–2.08; never: OR = 1.76, 95% CI = 1.26–2.48). Never‐testing was additionally more likely among MSM living in small cities (OR = 1.84, 95% CI = 1.40–2.41) and in the countryside (OR = 1.72, 95% CI = 1.17–2.54) and among MSM identifying as transman (OR = 4.63, 95% CI = 1.17–18.35).

DISCUSSION

This study set out to map cross‐border specific HIV indicators using two complementary data‐driven approaches and is the first of its kind. The findings serve as input to tailor interventions to the EMR‐context in particular, and can serve as an example to comparable cross‐border regions.

Our first aim was to describe and compare HIV CoCs and the number of newly diagnosed HIV infections across the EMR‐region and by key populations. We found that while the EMR‐regions were close to achieving 95% ART and viral suppression rates, almost one in 10 EMR‐residents with HIV remains undiagnosed. The region‐specific CoCs were relatively comparable but, overall, were slightly lower than national [4, 13, 14] and EU‐wide CoC performance [15]. They also showed differences across the EMR‐regions, with the greatest differences on the first CoC pillar. Key populations also varied across the EMR‐regions for new diagnoses, with MSM of European origin being predominant in EMR‐GER and EMR‐NL, while, between 2019 and 2021, in EMR‐BE, new diagnoses were more common among non‐European heterosexual individuals. The decline in new HIV diagnoses among MSM, compared to other key populations, may indicate the effectiveness of targeted interventions like PrEP, which, to date, have been used primarily by MSM [4, 13, 14]. It should be noted that 2019–2021 coincided with the COVID‐19 pandemic, which has been shown to affect sexual and prevention behaviour and access to care [16, 17, 18]. However, recent surveillance reports showed similar trends of declining HIV diagnoses in MSM that were less pronounced in non‐MSM populations and even increases in HIV diagnoses among MSM more recently [19, 20]. The diversification of key populations across EMR‐regions further underscores that cross‐border collaboration and knowledge exchange are imperative. For example, EMR‐BE was identified as having more initiatives tailored for individuals with a migration background [21], which can serve as a valuable example for other regions.

We used EMIS‐2017 data to complement surveillance with behavioural indicators, which might help explain the differences in the first pillar of the CoC, but only for MSM. These analyses revealed that approximately one in four MSM who participated in EMIS and resided in the EMR had never been tested for HIV, with approximately one in three of those never tested also being unaware of where to get tested. Factors associated with never testing, such as residing outside large cities, financial instability and being less engaged with the MSM community, are often more prevalent in less‐urbanized areas such as the EMR [22, 23, 24]. These factors are often reported as part of a complex interplay of factors that includes socio‐economic position, health literacy and distance from healthcare services, all of which may complicate access to health care services and have also been linked to other health inequalities in the EMR [22]. This could also explain why, for some HIV prevention indicators like PEP, awareness and confidence to access were lower in the EMR‐regions than the national average.

Furthermore, regional differences in testing among MSM existed both between and within countries. For example, with respect to differences within countries, EMR‐BE had notably higher HIV testing proportions than the national Belgian average. With respect to differences between countries, our data showed that 8% of HIV testing variation can be attributed to where someone lives in the EMR alone and even 15% for STI testing (data not shown). While this could potentially be attributed to EMIS‐2017 constituting a convenience sample, this difference could also result from differences in testing access between regions. For example, in EMR‐GER, HIV testing is available at specialized clinics and community‐based checkpoints in cities and often requires co‐payment, while testing done at a GP in EMR‐GER is often free. In contrast, testing at the GP in EMR‐NL and EMR‐BE, however, requires co‐payment. Additionally, individuals are often not able to test at the closest location if this is across the border. Improving cross‐border collaboration in access to HIV‐testing is a crucial step toward improving testing uptake and addressing such disparities. Harmonizing outreach testing activities (e.g., at the same time at different locations) with low‐threshold testing, such as with rapid or self‐tests with referral to prevention and care in case of a positive test, could lower the threshold to get tested.

Our findings should be interpreted in light of some limitations. These limitations are mainly inherent to the use of routinely collected, aggregated surveillance data and adhering to each country's definitions. Consequently, we could only compare a four‐stage continuum and may have underestimated the proportion not linked and not retained in care, except in EMR‐BE. Also, some estimates had high confidence intervals due to small numbers (e.g., German‐speaking community) and should be interpreted with caution, but this underscores the need to harmonize reporting of indicators at the European level in order to enhance and optimize cross‐border collaboration. We also relied on registered HIV diagnoses rather than estimated new HIV infections, which is likely biased towards MSM who are more likely to present for HIV testing. Moreover, EMIS‐2017 employed a convenience sample and only provided insights for MSM, which might not be applicable to the wider population. Also, widespread accessibility to PrEP was still limited during EMIS‐2017, but since then increased over time [25, 26, 27]. Despite these limitations, this study is one of the first to map region‐specific indicators, typically only done at a national level. Accordingly, our data highlights specific differences between and within countries necessary to tailor regional responses. Moreover, combining surveillance and survey data provides a richer and more contextualized picture, because behavioural indicators are rarely integrated in surveillance data, and therefore often lack context. At the same time, there is an urgent need for insight into these indicators for other key populations beyond MSM to inform interventions, particularly given that late diagnoses are more common in non‐MSM populations and new HIV diagnosis rates are not declining as steadily as in MSM populations [4].

In conclusion, surveillance data showed that the EMR‐region is on track to achieve the 95–95‐95 goals. However, at the same time, survey data showed that for MSM, between‐country differences exist in HIV testing, awareness and confidence in accessing HIV testing and prevention options, which may determine the first pillar of the cascade of care. Harmonized cross‐border targeted approaches are thus urgently needed if we are to adequately address these regional and socio‐demographic differences.
