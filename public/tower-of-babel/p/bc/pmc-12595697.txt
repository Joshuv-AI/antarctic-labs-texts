The effect of gluteal Kinesio-Taping on ankle function, dynamic balance, and electromyographic activity of gluteal muscles in male soccer players with chronic ankle instability: a randomized clinical trial
Authors: Hanieh Zarei, Habiballah Lari, Amin Kordi Yoosefinejad, Alireza Motealleh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic ankle instability (CAI) is associated with balance disorders and functional deficits in soccer players. Impaired gluteal muscle function contributes significantly to these issues, as these muscles play a crucial role in maintaining lower limb stability and balance. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of Kinesio-taping (KT) on the electromyographic (EMG) activity of gluteal muscles, ankle function, and dynamic balance in male soccer players with CAI.MethodsIn this randomized clinical trial, 30 male soccer players with CAI were randomly assigned to either the gluteal KT group (case) or the sham control group. Gluteal EMG activity, onset time, ankle function (Single-Leg Hop Test), and dynamic balance (modified Star Excursion Balance Test, mSEBT) were assessed pre-taping, immediately post-taping, and 48 h later.ResultsThe case group demonstrated significant increases in gluteus medius EMG activity (P < 0.05) and reductions in gluteus maximus onset time (P < 0.05) at both immediate and 48-hour follow-ups. Statistically significant improvements were also observed in single-leg hop distance (P = 0.001) and dynamic balance (P < 0.05) in the KT group compared to the control group. However, changes did not consistently meet minimal clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds for the mSEBT (4–5 cm per direction) or single-leg hop test (8–10 cm).ConclusionKT enhances gluteus medius activation, reduces gluteus maximus onset time, and improves ankle function and dynamic balance in soccer players with CAI. While statistically significant, the observed improvements may require integration with other rehabilitation strategies to achieve clinically meaningful outcomes. Future studies should explore long-term effects, diverse populations, and combined interventions to optimize functional gains.Trial registrationIRCT2015021015932N10, Date of registration: 04/11/2015.Keywords: Ankle instability, Electromyography, Kinesio-taping, Balance

Introduction

Chronic ankle instability (CAI) is a musculoskeletal problem characterized by recurrent ankle sprains, persistent pain, and a feeling of instability or “giving way” in the ankle. It often results from an initial ankle injury that hasn’t fully healed, leading to ongoing symptoms and an increased risk of further sprains [1, 2].

CAI is the most common injury among soccer players, accounting for 20% of all injuries, which is due to the stresses placed on the lower extremities and repetitive high-demand maneuvers performed in soccer, such as rapid changes in direction, sudden acceleration, and dynamic movements [3–5].

CAI can result in up to 28 days of missed playing time, depending on the injury’s severity [6]. Despite recent advances in sports medicine and rehabilitation techniques, the management of CAI remains a significant challenge [7].

Soccer players with CAI frequently encounter issues like reduced ankle proprioception, compromised mechanoreceptor function, and impaired dynamic balance, insufficient neuromuscular control as well as diminished ankle functionality, all of which can contribute to a decline in athletic performance [8, 9]. Neuromuscular control deficits during dynamic tasks like the Star Excursion Balance Test (SEBT) have been linked to altered hip and ankle strategies in individuals with CAI, as demonstrated by Jaber et al. [5].

The gluteal muscles play a critical role in balance and maintaining lower extremity stability and optimizing biomechanical efficiency during functional and sport activities [10–12]. Although the main function of the gluteal muscles is to maintain pelvis stability, they also regulate the movement of the femur, which in turn ensures the proper positioning of the foot during functional tasks such as walking [12], and probably running in sports activities. Improper foot placement can increase the risk of ankle injury due to altered normal movement patterns and maldistribution of mechanical loads [13–15]. Furthermore, altered gluteal muscle electromyographic (EMG) activity has been reported in patients with ankle sprain and CAI [2, 16, 17]. Webster et al., specifically identified reduced gluteus medius and maximus EMG activation during functional exercises in individuals with CAI, highlighting a potential neuromuscular deficit [18, 19].

Traditional rehabilitation approaches for CAI often focus on strengthening the ankle and improving proprioception [20–22]. However, recent studies have suggested that core stability exercises and gluteal muscle training play a crucial role in the rehabilitation of CAI [23–25]. Lempke et al. demonstrated that targeted gluteal activation during gait significantly improved functional outcomes in patients with CAI following rehabilitation, supporting the integration of proximal muscle training into rehabilitation programs [26]. Hence, optimizing the activation pattern of gluteal muscles, which serve as core stabilizers, may be considered a complementary strategy in the rehabilitation of CAI.

Kinesio-taping (KT) is a popular and widely used therapeutic technique in musculoskeletal conditions that involves applying elastic tape to the skin to support and stabilize muscles and joints without restricting movement [27].

Research has demonstrated that the application of KT can reduce pain, facilitate muscle activity, improve proprioception and enhance joint dynamic support [27, 28]. A previous study has shown that gluteus medius KT has improved dynamic balance and knee joint proprioception in female athletes with dynamic knee valgus [29]. Moreover, Rajasekar et al. reported that gluteal KT can increase hip abductor muscle strength and improve dynamic knee valgus in collegiate-level athletes [30]. Additionally, gluteus medius KT has been shown to improve plantar pressure distribution and enhance balance in healthy subjects [31]. Despite the popularity of Kinesio-taping, there is limited research on the effects of KT on the EMG activity of the gluteal muscles and its potential benefits for individuals with CAI. Han et al. recently found that individuals with CAI exhibit altered gluteal EMG patterns during static balance tasks, further emphasizing the need for interventions targeting proximal muscle activation [18]. Gluteal facilitatory taping may improve gluteal EMG activation patterns, which could, in turn, enhance ankle function and dynamic balance.

This randomized clinical trial aims to investigate the effect of gluteal facilitatory KT on ankle function, dynamic balance, and EMG activity of the gluteal muscles in male soccer players with CAI. We hypothesize that taping these muscles can improve ankle function, mSEBT scores, and gluteal EMG activity and onset in comparison to gluteal sham taping. By exploring the potential benefits of KT on the gluteal muscles, this study seeks to provide valuable insights into novel rehabilitation strategies for soccer players with CAI.

Patients & methods

Study design and participants

This study adheres to CONSORT guidelines. This parallel-group, single-blinded, randomized clinical trial was approved by the ethics review board of our institute and has been registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials under the registration number IRCT2015021015932N10. Male soccer players with CAI were referred to our physiotherapy clinic by an orthopedic surgeon and subsequently evaluated based on the following inclusion and exclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria were consistent with the International Ankle Consortium’s consensus statement [32] for chronic ankle instability and Included: Men aged 18–40 years old. Diagnosis of CAI confirmed by: ≥2 episodes of “giving way” in the past 6 months, Grade II lateral ankle sprain confirmed via anterior drawer test and talar tilt test [1, 9]. History of at least one significant ankle sprain (lasting > 5 weeks), Participation in soccer ≥ 3 times/week, Self-reported pain intensity 3–7/10 on the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) during functional activities (e.g., running, cutting).

Exclusion criteria were: use of anti-inflammatory drugs or participation in physiotherapy sessions for ankle sprain within the previous three months or during the study period. Concurrent neuromusculoskeletal disorders such as low back pain (with or without radiculopathy), a history of ankle fracture or fracture and surgery involving the spine or lower extremities, flatfoot or other lower limb postural abnormalities, any neuromuscular or neurological disorders affecting muscle function or balance (e.g., multiple sclerosis), or any metabolic or rheumatological condition that could impact function and balance (e.g., diabetes mellitus, rheumatoid arthritis).

Randomization and blinding

Thirty participants who met the study inclusion and exclusion criteria were randomly assigned (1:1) to either the gluteal KT group (case) or the sham control group. All random allocations were generated using computer software, with a block size of 3 and a total of 10 blocks. The allocations were concealed from participants and the statistician responsible for data analysis. The therapist was aware of the participant’s group allocations. Participants were informed they would receive ‘one of two taping methods,’ and post-intervention surveys confirmed successful blinding (100% unable to guess group allocation).

Intervention

In this study, Kinesio-taping tape (Kinesio® Tex Classic, Kinesio Holding Corporation, Albuquerque, NM, USA) was applied by a certified and experienced physiotherapist (Kinesio Taping certification) to the gluteal muscles in both the case and control groups. In the case group, a facilitatory KT technique was applied to enhance gluteal muscle function. To tape the gluteus maximus (Fig. 2a), the patient was positioned in a side-lying position, with the hip in a neutral position. The Kinesio-tape was cut in a Y-shape with two tails, and its base was adhered onto the sacrum (origin of the gluteus maximus) with no tension. The muscle was then stretched to its maximal length through hip flexion and internal rotation, while the knee was in a flexed position. The tails of the Kinesio-tape were pulled down with 50% of its initial tension in a way that the tails embraced the gluteus maximus muscle and then the final part of the tails adhered to the gluteal tuberosity without tension [33].

Kinesio-taping of (a) gluteus maximus; (b) gluteus medius; and (c) sham control

To tape the gluteus medius (Fig. 2b), the base of the Kinesio-tape was placed on the outer surface of the ilium without tension. The muscle was then stretched to its maximal length through hip flexion and adduction. The anterior tail was extended downward from the anterior iliac crest, while the posterior tail was extended downward from the posterior iliac crest to cover the medial gluteal muscle. The junction of the tip tails was placed on the gluteal tuberosity [33].

Sham Taping Procedure: The control group received sham gluteal KT for 48 h. Two parallel I-shaped strips of Kinesio-tape (2.5 cm wide, and 20 cm long) were applied transversely to the middle part of the gluteal muscles without tension or muscle-specific positioning ensuring no mechanical facilitation (Fig. 2.c). This method matched the tactile stimulus of real KT while avoiding therapeutic effects. The taping procedure was performed by an experienced physiotherapist in both case and control groups [33].

Our outcome measures were gluteal muscle activity and onset time (assessed by EMG). Also, the lower extremity function and balance were assessed by the Single-leg hop test and the mSEBT which are valid and reliable methods of assessment [34–36]. All tests and measures were performed before the start of the study, immediately after taping, and 48 h after taping.

Procedures

Electromyography

EMG of the gluteal muscles was recorded using a 16-channel EMG telemetry system (Mega Electronics Ltd., Kuopio, Finland). EMG signals were filtered using a band-pass filter (20–450 Hz). Before electrode placement, if necessary, the hair in the area was shaved, and the skin was cleaned with alcohol and gently abraded with sandpaper to minimize electrical resistance. The gel-electrodes placement was performed based on the SENIAM recommendations [37]. EMG signals were recorded from the gluteus maximus, gluteus medius, and tibialis anterior (TA) muscles. The TA was selected as the reference muscle for the toe-rise task due to its role as the primary dorsiflexor, enabling normalization of proximal muscle onset times. While peroneal muscles are relevant in CAI, this study focused on cross-effects of gluteal interventions on distal ankle control. For the gluteus maximus, the electrode was placed at the midpoint of a line connecting the sacral vertebrae to the greater trochanter, in the direction of the line from the post superior iliac spine to the post aspect of the thigh, while the ground electrode was placed on the C7 spinous process. For the gluteus medius, the electrode was placed at the midpoint of a line connecting the iliac crest to the trochanter. For the tibialis anterior, the electrode was placed at One-third on the line between the tip of the fibula and the tip of the medial malleolus with the ground electrode on the tibial shaft [38]. The electrodes and cables were then secured with hypoallergenic adhesive tape to prevent detachment or movement artifacts.

Evaluation of EMG activity after imposed perturbations is a common method to investigate neuromuscular control [39]. Hence, we evaluated EMG of gluteals following an internal perturbation imposed by a sudden toe-rise task. The toe-rise task was selected to standardize perturbation intensity and replicate the sudden ankle inversion risk inherent to CAI. This method was used for evaluation of EMG activity in previous studies [40, 41], and provides a controlled way to assess the synergistic activation between gluteal muscles and tibialis anterior during dynamic stabilization. while tibialis anterior activation induces dorsiflexion, coordinated gluteal activity provides proximal stability to counteract distal instability, reflecting their functional role in preventing excessive ankle inversion and CAI mechanism [28, 42].

Participants were instructed to stand with their feet shoulder-width apart, hands at their sides, and weight evenly distributed on both feet. Afterward, they were asked to raise their toes and stand on their heels as quickly as possible upon hearing the timer alarm (X-note model 1.5, made in China). EMG data were collected in 5 repetitions (no rest between repetitions), in the frequency range of 25–450 Hz, with a sampling frequency of 1000 Hz, through a 14-bit AD converter.

EMG signals were recorded using Megawin software (version 3.0), stored as ASCII files, and analyzed offline using MATLAB software version 2014. EMG data were rectified and digitally low-pass filtered at 50 Hz (Butterworth filter, 4th order). The onset time of EMG activity for the gluteus maximus and medius muscles was defined as the point at which the amplitude increased by at least three standard deviations from baseline for at least 25 milliseconds. Onset time was determined by computer and confirmed visually from the rectified unfiltered data. To assess neuromuscular timing, the tibialis anterior (TA) was included as a reference muscle for onset normalization during the toe-rise task, as TA is the prime mover for this perturbation. The mean onset time of EMG activity in 5 repetitions was calculated and expressed relative to the onset time of the TA of the toe-rise task. Root Mean Square (RMS) of amplitudes were also analyzed 200 milliseconds after the onset, with a time window of 100 millisecond.

Moreover, for each muscle the level of EMG activity was normalized to its maximum measured during maximum voluntary contractions (MVCs). Participants performed MVCs against manual resistance in standardized positions per SENIAM guidelines [38, 43].

Single hop test

The single hop test was performed as described by Fowler [34], with three trials averaged for analysis. To evaluate ankle function by the single hop test, participants were positioned at a designated starting line, standing on the leg to be tested, while the non-testing leg was raised or held in a comfortable position. Participants were then instructed to hop forward on the involved leg as far as possible, using their arms for propulsion, and to land on the same leg. The distance hopped from the starting line to the toes of the landing foot was measured with a tape measure. The test was repeated two more times, and adequate rest was allowed between trials. Finally, the average distance hopped was used for analysis.

mSEBT

To perform mSEBT three strips of adhesive tape were cut to the length of 180 cm and adhered to the ground to form a “Y” with anterior, posteromedial, and posterolateral axes. The angle between the two posterior axes (posteromedial and posterolateral) was 90 degrees and the angle between each posterior axis and the anterior axis was 135 degrees.

The subject was instructed to place the affected foot on the center of the “Y”, and reach with the other leg the farther distance possible along each axis. Participants completed 3 practice trials per direction to minimize learning effects. During testing, they performed 3 recorded reaches per direction while maintaining hands on hips, with the average reach distance used for analysis. A 30-second rest period between trials prevented fatigue [44]. The reached distance in anterior, posterolateral, and posteromedial directions ‎was measured and normalized to the subject’s leg length. The leg length was determined by measuring the distance from the ‎ASIS to the medial malleolus with subjects in the supine ‎position [45].

Sample size and statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated based on data from a previous similar study [46], with an α level of 5%, a power of 80%, and a 20% drop out rate. A total of 30 participants (15 per group) were deemed sufficient for this RCT.

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS for Windows (version 18). Descriptive data were presented as mean ± standard deviations for quantitative variables and as numbers with percentages for qualitative variables. The ‎Kolmogorov-Smirnov test verified the normal distribution of our data for all ‎variables. Differences within and between groups were analyzed using repeated-measures Analysis of Variance (ANOVA). Whenever an adequate assumption of sphericity was not met, Bonferroni-adjusted paired t-tests were used instead of repeated-measures ANOVA. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Also, based on our findings, the gluteus medius onset time showed no significant difference during the study, neither in the case nor in the control group (Table 3). Additionally, the between-group comparison showed no significant statistical difference in the changes of onsets, between the case and control groups (Table 2).

Moreover, our results showed that gluteus maximus onset times are significantly reduced in the case group immediately and 48 h after the intervention (P = 0.004 and P = 0.002, respectively) in comparison to the pre-intervention values. Yet, these changes in gluteus maximus onset times were not significant in the control group. In the between-group comparison, the change in gluteus maximus onset time at 48 h after the intervention in comparison to the pre-intervention was significantly greater in the case group (P = 0.015) (Table 2).

Single hop test

Compared to pre-intervention values, the mean hop distance in the single hop test increased significantly in the case group 48 h after the intervention but not immediately after the intervention (P = 0.328 and P = 0.001, respectively). In the anterior direction, the mSEBT score significantly increased in the case group 48 h after the intervention (P = 0.007). No significant difference was observed in the anterior mSEBT score in the control group (Table 4). The differences between groups were also not significant (Table 5).

In the posteromedial direction, the mSEBT score increased significantly immediately and 48 h after the intervention in the case group (P = 0.001 and P < 0.001, respectively) but not in the control group (P = 0.277 and P = 0.584, respectively). Discussion

This randomized clinical trial investigated the effects of gluteal Kinesio-taping (KT) on ankle function, dynamic balance, and electromyographic (EMG) activity of the gluteal muscles in male soccer players with chronic ankle instability (CAI). The findings revealed significant improvements in gluteus medius EMG activity, reduced onset time of the gluteus maximus, enhanced single-leg hop test performance, and improved dynamic balance as measured by the modified Star Excursion Balance Test (mSEBT) in the KT group compared to the sham control group. These results provide valuable insights into the potential role of KT in addressing proximal muscle activation deficits and improving functional outcomes in individuals with CAI.

Effects of KT on gluteal muscle EMG activity

The study demonstrated a significant increase in gluteus medius EMG activity in the KT group both immediately and 48 h after taping, while no such changes were observed in the control group. This finding aligns with previous studies showing that KT can enhance muscle activity, particularly in proximal stabilizing muscles such as the gluteals [47–49], For instance, Rajasekar et al. (2018) reported that gluteal KT improved hip abductor strength and corrected dynamic knee valgus in collegiate athletes, likely due to the tape’s ability to facilitate neuromuscular control and proprioceptive feedback [30]. Previously, Han et al. (2024) found altered gluteal EMG patterns during static balance tasks in individuals with CAI, emphasizing the importance of targeting these muscles in rehabilitation programs [18]. Webster et al. (2013), demonstrated that individuals with CAI exhibit reduced gluteal muscle activation during functional tasks [19]. Based on our findings, increased EMG activity of gluteus medius following KT, may be a helpful intervention in managing motor control deficits in patients with CAI. However, contrasting findings were reported by Ataullah et al. (2021), who observed a significant decrease in gluteus medius EMG activity after KT in athletes with CAI [46]. This discrepancy highlights the need for further investigation into the mechanisms underlying KT’s effects on muscle activity. In contrast, the lack of significant change in gluteus maximus EMG amplitude could be attributed to its primary role as a powerful hip extensor rather than a stabilizer like gluteus medius. Moreover, the gluteus maximus is primarily recruited during high-load or explosive movements (e.g., sprinting, jumping), whereas the tasks used in this study (e.g., toe-rise task) may not have been sufficiently demanding to elicit a significant increase in gluteus maximus activation. Our study extends these findings by demonstrating that KT not only enhances gluteus medius activation but also reduces the onset time of the gluteus maximus, suggesting improved timing and coordination of muscle firing patterns.

In contrast, in regard to the gluteus maximus, the EMG level of activity did not show significant changes in either group, which may indicate that KT primarily affects smaller, stabilizing muscles like the gluteus medius rather than larger, more powerful muscles such as the gluteus maximus. The reduction in gluteus maximus onset time observed in the KT group is clinically relevant, as delayed or improper activation of this muscle has been associated with altered lower extremity biomechanics and an increased risk of injury [50]. The delayed activity of the gluteus maximus was noticed by Janda in many musculoskeletal disorders, such as low back pain [51]. Moreover, Bullock-Saxton et al. showed a delayed EMG onset time of gluteus maximus following ankle sprain injuries [52]. Hence, our KT technique may have clinical implications in restoring normal EMG patterns of the gluteus maximus and, subsequently, better neuromuscular coordination and enhanced control of the lower extremities in patients with CAI. By facilitating earlier recruitment of the gluteus maximus, KT may help restore optimal movement patterns and reduce compensatory strategies often seen in individuals with CAI. Interestingly, KT did not improve gluteus medius onset time in our study. If participants in the KT group already had relatively fast gluteus medius onset times at baseline, there may have been limited room for further improvement - a potential ceiling effect. This explanation is supported by the observed improvements in gluteus medius EMG amplitude despite the lack of changes in onset timing. Moreover, the tasks used in this study (e.g., toe-rise) may not have been sensitive enough to detect subtle changes in gluteus medius onset timing. More challenging or task-specific perturbations (e.g., sudden lateral pushes or unstable surfaces) might better highlight the effects of KT on gluteus medius timing parameters.

Effects of KT on ankle function and dynamic balance

The findings of this study demonstrated enhanced single-leg hop test performance and improved dynamic balance, as measured by the mSEBT, in the KT group compared to the sham control group. These improvements may be attributed to neural cross-talk between gluteal muscles and the ankle joint, facilitated by overlapping innervation patterns. Specifically, the gluteus medius and maximus are innervated by the L4, L5, S1, and S2 nerve roots, while the ankle joint and its surrounding musculature receive sensory and motor inputs from the L5, S1, and S2 nerve roots [53]. This shared innervation suggests a potential sensory-motor relationship between the hip and ankle regions, consistent with literature on neurophysiological integration and segmental organization of the nervous system [54, 55]. Thus, KT may modulate these sensory-motor pathways, explaining its remote effects on ankle function and dynamic balance [56, 57]. Although proprioception was not directly measured in this study, our findings align with previous research indicating that KT can enhance joint proprioception and dynamic stability in various populations, including athletes with musculoskeletal injuries and CAI [42]. For instance, Boonkerd et al. (2022) showed that KT improves proprioception in individuals with CAI, while Saki et al. (2022) reported enhanced knee joint proprioception and balance in female athletes with dynamic knee valgus following gluteus medius KT [29]. While Saki et al. study focused on the knee, our results extend this concept to ankle function and dynamic balance in individuals with CAI. Sarvestan et al. (2020) found that KT improved lower extremity kinetics and kinematics, reducing mechanical stress in college athletes with CAI [58]. Similarly, Rajasekar et al. (2018) reported reduced dynamic knee valgus following gluteal KT, underscoring the role of proximal neuromuscular control in distal joint function and biomechanics [30]. The mSEBT results revealed significant improvements in the posteromedial and posterolateral directions in the KT group, reflecting enhanced dynamic balance across multiple planes of motion. These gains may stem from increased gluteus medius activity and earlier onset of gluteus maximus following KT, potentially mediated by enhanced proprioceptive feedback and stimulation of cutaneous mechanoreceptors [59]. Notably, improvements in the anterior direction were less pronounced, likely due to the complexity of integrating proximal muscle activation with distal ankle stability during forward-reaching tasks. Our findings resonate with those of Saki et al. and Sarvestan et al., which highlight similar mechanisms such as enhanced proprioceptive feedback and improved biomechanics [29, 58]. The relationship between gluteal muscle function and ankle stability has been emphasized in prior research. Jaber et al. (2018) demonstrated altered EMG activity and impaired neuromuscular control at the hip during the Star Excursion Balance Test in individuals with CAI [5]. Our findings of improved mSEBT and ankle function after gluteal KT align with these observations, suggesting that earlier activation of the gluteus maximus and enhanced activity of the gluteus medius contribute to better neuromuscular control of lower limb biomechanics, thereby reducing stress on the ankle joint during functional activities. While statistically significant improvements were observed in both the mSEBT and single-leg hop test, their clinical relevance remains uncertain. Using the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for the mSEBT (4–5 cm per direction) in individuals with CAI [60], the magnitude of change in our study fell short of clinically meaningful thresholds, particularly in the anterior direction. Similarly, the single-leg hop test improvements (approximately 6.5 cm from Time 1 to Time 3) did not meet the MCID of 8–10 cm for this population [60]. These findings underscore the importance of interpreting statistical significance alongside clinical relevance. Despite this, the overall enhancement in dynamic balance highlights the potential of KT as a complementary intervention in CAI rehabilitation, warranting further investigation into optimized KT protocols or multimodal interventions to achieve greater functional gains.

Clinical implications

The results of this study highlight the potential clinical utility of KT in the rehabilitation of soccer players with CAI. While statistically significant improvements were observed in both the mSEBT and single-leg hop test, the magnitude of these changes did not consistently meet established MCID thresholds. This suggests that KT alone may serve as a useful adjunctive tool but may require combination with other interventions, such as targeted strengthening exercises or neuromuscular training, to achieve clinically meaningful outcomes. In line with the findings of Lempke et al. (2021), who demonstrated that targeting gluteal activation during gait significantly improves functional outcomes in patients with CAI, the role of proximal muscle facilitation remains an important consideration in CAI rehabilitation [26]. By enhancing gluteal muscle activation and improving dynamic balance, KT may serve as a cost-effective and non-invasive adjunct to traditional rehabilitation approaches. However, given the limitations in achieving clinically significant improvements based on MCID values, future research should explore the long-term effects of KT and its integration into comprehensive rehabilitation programs.

Limitations of the study and future directions

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although participants were blinded to group allocation, the therapist applying the KT was not. This could have inadvertently influenced the application technique or interactions with participants. Future studies employing a double-blind design, such as using placebo taping applied by therapists blinded to the intervention, would help mitigate this potential bias and strengthen the robustness of the findings.

Second, we evaluated gluteus medius muscle activity by surface EMG as a single unit. However, The gluteus medius consists of distinct anterior, middle, and posterior compartments, with different functions. The function of posterior compartment of gluteus medius has been evaluated by needle EMG in a previous study [61, 62]. It is possible that our results might have differed if needle EMG had been used to evaluate the posterior compartment of the gluteus medius muscle. Future studies should consider combining needle EMG and surface EMG to better assess the posterior and other compartments of the gluteus medius, respectively. Third, the study included only male soccer players, which limits the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Future studies should include diverse populations, including females and individuals from different sports backgrounds, to enhance the applicability of the findings.

Fourth, the study did not assess pain levels or subjective perceptions of instability, which could provide additional insights into the therapeutic effects of KT. Future studies should incorporate pain assessments to more comprehensively understand the mechanisms underlying the benefits of KT.

Fifth, the short follow-up period (48 h) precludes conclusions about the long-term effects of KT. Future studies with extended follow-up periods are warranted to clarify the durability of KT’s effects.

Finally, this study did not investigate the combined effects of gluteal KT and exercise therapy, a strategy that is commonly used in the rehabilitation of CAI. Given the potential synergistic benefits of KT and targeted strengthening exercises, future clinical trials should evaluate whether combining these interventions results in greater enhancements in gluteal muscle activation pattern, ankle function, and dynamic balance compared to either intervention alone.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that gluteal KT enhances gluteus medius activation, reduces the onset time of the gluteus maximus, and shows statistically significant improvements in ankle function and dynamic balance in male soccer players with CAI. However, the observed improvements did not consistently meet the MCID thresholds for the mSEBT (4–5 cm per direction) or the single-leg hop test (8–10 cm). Therefore, while KT appears promising as a complementary intervention, its standalone clinical significance remains limited. We recommend integrating KT with other rehabilitation strategies to maximize functional outcomes in soccer players with CAI. Nevertheless, studies with longer follow-up periods and diverse populations are warranted to further validate these results and explore the mechanisms underlying KT’s effects.
