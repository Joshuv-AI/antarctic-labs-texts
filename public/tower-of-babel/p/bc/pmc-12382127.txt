Effectiveness of AI-assisted medical education for Chinese undergraduate medical students: a meta-analysis
Authors: Jingyue Peng, Hongying Zhang, Xiaohua Tu, Xuemei Zhang, Qiuhan Wu, Yijun Wang, Deng Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) has been adopted as an innovative teaching method. However, no studies have comprehensively evaluated the effectiveness of AI in the context of medical education in China. This study aimed to assess the effectiveness of AI-assisted teaching methods versus traditional teaching methods regarding their impact on medical education outcomes in China.MethodsWe followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and retrieved studies published in the Web of Science, PubMed, EMBASE, the Cochrane Library, the Chinese National Knowledge Infrastructure, VIP Database and the Chinese Wanfang Database from January 2015 to December 2024. The standardized mean difference (SMD) with a 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated, and heterogeneity was evaluated using I² statistics, with subsequent meta-regression analysis employed to identify the contributing factors.ResultsTwelve studies involving 824 medical students were included. All studies provided usable data on examination scores. The pooled analysis revealed a significant difference in favor of AI-assisted teaching compared with traditional teaching methods (SMD = 2.06, 95% CI: 1.35–2.76). In addition, AI-assisted teaching significantly increased student satisfaction in eight studies (OR = 5.80, 95% CI: 3.30–10.18). Meta-regression analysis indicated that randomization, study duration and type of AI technologies were the primary factors contributing to heterogeneity.ConclusionThe AI-assisted approach to medical education in China was shown to be more effective than traditional teaching methods in improving examination scores and student satisfaction, offering substantial evidence for the adoption of AI in medical education. These results highlighted the potential advantages of incorporating AI into medical teaching practices. Future research should focus on investigating the effectiveness of AI-assisted teaching across diverse educational systems and applications.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07770-y.

Introduction

The concept of artificial intelligence (AI) was first introduced by John McCarthy in 1955 [1]. With continuous advancements in technology, AI has been increasingly integrated into various healthcare fields, playing a crucial role in assisting medical professionals with disease identification, clinical analysis, data interpretation, and clinical decision-making support [2]. In the 1980 s, AI was explored for its potential in medical education [3]. As a complement to traditional teaching models, AI offers targeted and personalized instruction that helps medical students better grasp and understand complex medical knowledge [4, 5]. In clinical practice lessons, AI integrates diverse medical record data to create standardized virtual patients that simulate realistic diagnostic and treatment processes [6, 7]. This helps students conduct routine consultations and simultaneously collecting teaching feedback to propose new educational suggestions [8]. Moreover, AI’s powerful image-recognition capabilities and extensive teaching resources provide students with more intuitive and comprehensive learning experiences in anatomy and imaging [9]. Overall, AI has emerged as a valuable tool for enhancing the efficiency and effectiveness of medical education [10].

In China, the application of AI in medical education is still in its infancy because of limitations in technology and coverage [11]. With the introduction of the concept of a “new medical education,” the state education department started to strongly support the long-term development of AI in medical education [10]. However, the results of AI-assisted teaching in medical education in China have been inconclusive or inconsistent [12]. Because of the complexity of the Chinese education system, although many medical schools have adopted AI approaches in their curricula, the outcomes have been inconsistent. Whether AI-assisted teaching methods are superior to traditional methods remains unclear. Given the relatively small sample sizes of most existing studies, a quantitative synthesis is required to evaluate the impact of AI-assisted teaching. Under China’s New Medical Education Initiative, most studies on AI in Chinese medical education have been published within the last decade. In this study, we conducted a meta-analysis of 12 studies to derive an overall assessment of the effectiveness of AI-assisted teaching compared with traditional teaching methods from 2015 to 2024. While recent systematic reviews have investigated technology-enhanced medical education in China [13], these encompassed diverse e-learning modalities across multiple learner levels (e.g., postgraduate trainees and practicing physicians). Our study specifically quantifies the efficacy of AI-driven educational interventions—including ChatGPT, virtual patients, and adaptive learning platforms—exclusively among undergraduate medical students. To our knowledge, this constitutes the first meta-analysis synthesizing evidence on the effectiveness of discrete AI technologies in undergraduate medical education within the Chinese context. These findings may also serve as a reference for other countries, especially those with medical education systems similar to that of China.

Methods

This meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [14].

Search strategy

The following databases were searched from January 2015 to December 2024: PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and Embase; and Chinese databases: the Chinese National Knowledge Infrastructure, VIP Database, and Chinese Wanfang Database. The comprehensive search combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH)/Thesaurus terms with free-text keywords. The following terms were used: Controlled vocabulary terms: “Artificial Intelligence“[MeSH] OR “Artificial Intelligence” [Thesaurus]; “Education, Medical“[MeSH] OR “Medical Education“[Thesaurus]. Free-text keywords: (“artificial intelligence” OR “AI” OR “machine learning”); (“medical education” OR “medical training” OR “medical course” OR “curriculum”); (“China” OR “Chinese”). In addition, we searched the references mentioned in the narrative reviews to identify more applicable studies. Two authors independently participated in the retrieval process.

Inclusion criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they evaluated AI-assisted teaching performance. The inclusion criteria for the articles were as follows: (1) English and Chinese studies reporting the performance of AI and traditional teaching in medical education in China; (2) randomized or non-randomized trials; (3) Chinese undergraduate medical students; (4) reported AI technologies such as ChatGPT, AI imaging diagnosis system and adaptive platforms; and (5) detailed quantitative results and available data.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two investigators independently extracted data from each study in accordance with predefined inclusion criteria. The extracted information included the first author’s surname, publication year, course name, sample size, student characteristics, intervention methods, study duration, and outcomes. Following data extraction, the two investigators compared the results to identify any discrepancies. In cases of disagreement, a third investigator reviewed the study and a consensus was reached through discussion among the three investigators. The methodological quality of each study was assessed using the Cochrane Collaboration’s risk-of-bias tool [15].

Statistical analysis

For continuous outcomes, the standardized mean difference (SMD) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated for each study. The studies were then pooled using SMD, as appropriate. Odds ratios (OR) and its 95% CIs were calculated for ordinal outcomes. The overall effect was assessed via the Z test, with a two-sided P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Heterogeneity was assumed using I2 statistics. When the P value was ≤ 0.10 in the I2 test, the random effects model (DerSimonian and Laird method) was employed [16]; otherwise, the fixed effects model was used [17]. A meta-regression analysis based on restricted maximum likelihood was used to further explore the sources of heterogeneity [18]. Funnel plots and Egger’s weighted regression were used to examine the publication bias [19]. The meta-analysis was conducted using Review Manager 5.4 (Cochrane Library Software, Oxford, UK) and STATA 17.0 (STATA Corporation, College Station, Texas, USA).

Results

Search results

During the identification phase, we conducted a comprehensive search of relevant databases using a predefined search strategy. The initial search yielded 588 relevant articles. Following a preliminary review of titles and abstracts, 104 articles were selected for further analysis. Based on the established inclusion criteria, a final total of 12 studies encompassing 824 participants were included in this meta-analysis [20–31]. Study characteristics

The 12 included articles were published between 2019 and 2024, and the effects of AI-assisted teaching were compared with those of traditional teaching in medical courses in China. The sample sizes of the included articles ranged from 18 to 120, with a total of 824 participants. The majors of the students in these studies were clinical medicine and medical imaging, and the duration of the studies ranged from 2 to 52 weeks in nine studies (three studies were not mentioned [22, 25, 31]). The educational system was predominantly a five-year program in almost all studies, although this information was not provided in two studies [27, 28]. The outcomes retrieved from almost all the included articles were examination scores and student satisfaction rates. Examination scores were used to assess the extent to which students had mastered the relevant professional knowledge in all studies. Student satisfaction data were collected through questionnaire-based surveys in eight studies. The characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1.

Quality assessment

A summary of the methodological quality of each study and a graph of each quality item presented as percentages across all studies are shown in Fig. 2. The quality of the included studies was satisfactory. Nine studies employed randomized controlled trial designs, whereas the methodological approach in the remaining three studies was not clearly specified [21, 28, 30]. Regarding outcome data and reporting, all the studies were classified as having a low risk of bias. In addition, ten studies were free of other biases. Information regarding allocation concealment and blinding of participants and outcome assessors was not reported in most studies.

Data analysis

The effects of AI-assisted teaching compared with traditional teaching on examination scores were reported in all 12 included studies involving 824 medical students (experimental group = 414, control group, 410). All studies revealed a statistically significant difference in scores between the AI-assisted group and control groups. Given the high degree of heterogeneity observed across the 12 studies (I2 = 94%, P < 0.00001), a random-effects model was used for the meta-analysis. The pooled data from the forest plots revealed that compared with traditional teaching, AI-assisted teaching significantly increased students’ examination scores (SMD = 2.06, 95% CI [1.35, 2.76], P < 0.00001) (Fig. 3). Student satisfaction, as assessed via questionnaires, was reported in eight studies involving 599 medical students (experimental group = 301, control group = 298). The pooled data from these eight studies exhibited low heterogeneity (I2 = 11%), suggesting the use of a fixed-effects model for the meta-analysis. All studies indicated that AI-assisted teaching significantly increased student satisfaction (OR = 5.80, 95% CI [3.30, 10.18], P < 0.00001) compared with traditional teaching (Fig. 4).

Test of heterogeneity

Because heterogeneity could not be completely avoided in the meta-analysis of examination scores, its source was further investigated. The meta-regression method was used to explore the possible factors. Table 2 shows that the institution type, major, curricula, and total numbers could not explain the source of heterogeneity (P = 0.783; P = 0.405; P = 0.105; P = 0.136, respectively), while the randomization, types of AI technologies, and duration of the study may have contributed to heterogeneity (P < 0.05). Consequently, a subgroup meta-analysis was performed based on time. Figure 5 shows that heterogeneity was significantly lower in five long-term studies (more than 12 weeks) (P = 0.24, I2 = 28%) and four short-term studies (less than or equal to 12 weeks) (P = 0.41, I2 = 0%). Additionally, the pooled analysis revealed that AI-assisted teaching significantly increased examination scores in the fixed-effects model in the long-term group (SMD = 3.27, 95% CI [2.84, 3.71], P < 0.00001) and short-term group (SMD = 1.68, 95% CI [1.40, 1.97], P < 0.00001). Figure 6 shows that heterogeneity was also reduced in the three trials with unclear randomization (P = 0.25, I2 = 29%). Figure 7 shows that heterogeneity significantly decreased in the different types of AI technologies in the AI-assisted diagnosis system group (P = 0.41, I2 = 0%) and the AI adaptive learning system group (P = 0.34, I2 = 8%). Additionally, a pooled analysis revealed that AI-assisted teaching significantly increased examination scores in the fixed-effects model in the AI-assisted diagnosis system group (SMD = 1.68, 95% CI [1.40, 1.97], P < 0.00001) and the AI adaptive learning system group (SMD = 3.35, 95% CI [2.85, 3.85], P < 0.00001).A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis demonstrated robust pooled effect estimates. No single study disproportionately influenced the meta-analysis results, as evidenced by all recalculated estimates falling within the original 95% CI range (Fig. 8).

Discussion

Despite the widespread adoption of AI in global medical education, its application in China remains in its early stages [32]. Most studies on AI in Chinese medical education have been published within the last decade, with a significant increase from 2019 to the present. Because of the short history of AI applications in this field, further scientific validation of AI-assisted teaching in China is required. Considering that different educational systems and cultural backgrounds may influence the effectiveness of teaching methods [33], this meta-analysis focused on Chinese medical students within the Chinese education system. This study aimed to assess whether there are significant differences in quantitative outcomes between AI-assisted and traditional teaching methods.

In this meta-analysis, the efficacy of AI-based interventions was validated through controlled experiments (experimental vs. control groups) employing quantitative metrics, including standardized examination scores and validated satisfaction questionnaires. AI-assisted teaching significantly improves examination scores (SMD = 2.06, 95% CI: 1.35–2.76)and learning satisfaction(OR = 5.80, 95% CI:3.30-10.18) compared to traditional methods. These implementations demonstrated significant improvements in students’ academic performance and diagnostic competencies, while simultaneously optimizing learning experiences and motivation, addressed limitations inherent in traditional pedagogical approaches by enhancing clinical reasoning and practical skills, provided standardized yet personalized learning resources. This positive impact on the examination scores was further confirmed through subgroup and sensitivity analyses, consistent with those of previous studies conducted in Western countries, suggesting that the benefits of AI-assisted teaching are not limited to specific countries or educational systems [34].

However, high heterogeneity was observed among the included studies, and the effect size of examination scores (SMD = 2.45) was large. Meta-regression analyses were conducted to identify potential sources, and the results indicated that institution type, academic discipline, course content, and total sample size did not significantly contribute to the observed heterogeneity. Stratified analyses based on intervention duration demonstrated a significant reduction in heterogeneity, suggesting that the duration of AI exposure plays a critical role in shaping the educational impact. Additionally, the more significant effectiveness observed in the long-term interventions may be attributed to the extended duration, which likely provides students with sufficient time to adapt to AI-assisted teaching methods, thereby facilitating substantial improvements in learning outcomes. Notably, a subgroup analysis stratified by the AI intervention modality revealed a substantial reduction in heterogeneity, indicating that the type of AI tool served as a significant effect modifier in the relationship between AI-assisted education and learning outcomes. This observation suggests that technological characteristics (e.g., AI-assisted diagnosis systems vs. AI adaptive learning systems) may independently influence educational efficacy. The restricted sample sizes across included studies (range: 18–120 participants) pose methodological challenges to this Meta-analysis. As emphasized in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, small sample sizes are inherently more susceptible to random error and effect size overestimation, which may introduce instability in pooled estimates [34]. Sensitivity analyses indicated that no single study disproportionately influenced the meta-analysis results, as evidenced by all recalculated estimates falling within the original 95% CI range. More randomized controlled trials with large-scale studies should be conducted to rigorously evaluate the efficacy of AI-assisted teaching methods and provide robust evidence for their integration into medical education. Moreover, these studies rarely reported instructor proficiency levels (e.g., AI literacy hours and certification status). Prior research indicates that teachers’ self-confidence in AI-integrated instruction directly affects their teaching intentions, and teachers’ familiarity with AI, technical knowledge, and teaching intention directly affect the effective use of AI in the curriculum [35, 36]. More comprehensive observational studies should be conducted to systematically evaluate teachers’ AI adoption levels across different educational contexts and to provide empirical benchmarks for optimizing faculty development programs. While this appears to be the first quantitative synthesis of AI tools for Chinese medical undergraduates, this rapidly evolving field warrants continual reassessment as new evidence emerges.

In this meta-analysis, AI has been applied in various fields of medical education. For example, it has been used to facilitate active learning strategies among students, provide continuous learning opportunities without geographical limitations, and assist in the recognition and diagnosis of medical images. These applications align with previous global studies that have also demonstrated AI’s potential in problem- or case-based teaching, identifying students’ needs, sharing uncommon clinical cases with a broader audience, and enabling self-directed learning without the active presence of teachers [37]. Previous studies have proven that AI can assist students in case discussions, and virtually standardized patients can improve the completeness of inquiries, assist in diagnosis, and enhance operational skills [38–40]. Given these advancements, AI has the potential to expand into new domains of Chinese medical education.

However, their application presents several challenges. First, the designer selects features, metrics, and model structures during AI development, which may introduce bias into the system [41]. This could negatively affect diversity and inclusion in medical education. Additionally, the protection and privacy of medical data are critical concerns [42]. AI systems require large amounts of patient data for training and ensuring the legal rights and privacy of patients is paramount. Data collection must be conducted with explicit patient consent to safeguard personal information. In addition, studies show that the readiness of teachers towards AI is not only dependent on individual capabilities but is also closely related to technical infrastructure, school support, and government training policies. This means that teachers in developed regions have a higher readiness level and more advanced AI equipment and training opportunities than those in less developed areas, which may lead to unfairness of education [43]. Moreover, cultural receptivity to AI in Chinese education should be considered. The traditional Chinese cultural disposition towards “seeking progress while maintaining stability” contributes to educators’ initial resistance to emerging technologies. There is a prevalent tendency towards observational hesitation rather than proactive adoption, with concerns being particularly pronounced among humanities faculty. These educators express a heightened apprehension that AI may undermine both humanistic values and instructor authority, thereby impeding deeper integration of AI within classroom settings [44]. The integration of ideological education into medical curricula has been increasingly emphasized in recent years. This includes fostering empathy, responsibility, and patient-centered thinking among healthcare professionals. Although AI has significantly improved student satisfaction and examination performance, healthcare professionals in China face high daily patient volumes, necessitating the development of empathy and communication skills among medical students. These competencies may require supplementary reinforcement through instructors’ role modeling in traditional clinical teaching methods.

Limitations and future studies

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. Critically, the inclusion of only 12 studies with a cumulative sample size of 824 participants, particularly given the substantial variation in individual study sizes (range: 18–120), poses challenges to the robustness and generalizability of our conclusions. Smaller studies are inherently more susceptible to random errors and effect size overestimation, which may introduce instability into pooled estimates. Although we employed rigorous methods, including heterogeneity assessments and sensitivity analyses, to mitigate these concerns and Egger’s test indicated no significant publication bias, this restricted sample constrained the external validity of our results. Future large-scale studies with standardized sample sizes are imperative to verify these findings before broader clinical application is recommended. Moreover, allocation concealment, blinding of participants, and outcome assessment were not reported in most studies, and student satisfaction was assessed via questionnaire surveys in some studies. The measurement of student satisfaction in the included studies relied heavily on non-standardized, institution-specific questionnaires, and the lack of standardized metrics raises concerns about construct validity, that is, whether different questionnaires truly measure the same underlying satisfaction construct. Reliability was rarely reported in the questionnaire surveys, making it difficult to assess whether the responses were stable across items. In addition, non-standardized tools are more prone to acquiescence and central tendency biases. Therefore, future research should employ standardized study designs and outcome assessments with the adoption of validated instruments, standardized effect size reporting, and full-item wording to address these limitations and provide more reliable and valid evidence.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this meta-analysis demonstrates that AI-assisted methods in medical education are more effective than traditional methods in improving examination performance and student satisfaction in China. These findings highlight the potential benefits of integrating AI into medical teaching, and future studies should focus on exploring the effectiveness of AI-assisted teaching across varying application durations. In addition, given the increasing reliance on AI for educational purposes, the protection and privacy of medical data in AI-assisted teaching must be further explored to ensure ethical and secure implementation. Accounting for institutional variations, disparities in the AI infrastructure, and different levels of teacher training provision, potential approaches to AI-assisted teaching must be explored further to improve teaching quality in medical education in China.
