Molecular design enables color-fluorescence alignment in electrochromic/electrofluorochromic displays
Authors: Ruonan Huang, Ningzhi Cao, Xincai Liu, Caiyun Wang, Danming Chao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The spectral mismatch between the reflective electrochromic (EC) state and the emissive electrofluorochromic (EFC) state fundamentally limits the practical implementation of EC/EFC displays for adaptive visual interfaces. Here, we present a rational molecular-level design that achieves intrinsic color-fluorescence alignment through a single donor–acceptor chromophore. Redox-tuned intramolecular charge transfer enables synchronized modulation of absorption and emission, thus resolving the longstanding EC/EFC performance trade-off. This intrinsic optical alignment is further stabilized within a tailored ionic liquid matrix that suppresses aggregation. The resulting device delivers benchmark performance, including ultralow bidirectional switching voltages (<1.1 V), sub-second response times (<1.0 s), high optical contrast, and excellent stability. To demonstrate practical utility, we develop a low-energy, dual-mode traffic signaling system that seamlessly integrates EC and EFC functions for continuous operation under varying light conditions. This work establishes a generalized strategy for dual-mode optical materials, advancing reliable and energy-efficient next-generation display technologies.

Introduction

The advancement of next-generation display technologies relies on materials with versatile optical functionalities for energy-efficient and adaptable visual interfaces.1 Electrochromic/electrofluorochromic (EC/EFC) bifunctional materials have emerged as a promising platform for adaptive displays, enabling reversible, electrically driven modulation of both visible color and fluorescence emission.2–4 This dual functionality underpins integrated reflective and emissive systems, allowing mode switching for optimal performance under varying conditions. Such systems offer reduced energy consumption and enhanced visual output with consistent color representation across display modes.5–7 Despite these advantages, a major barrier to practical implementation is the lack of intrinsic alignment between the EC color and the EFC emission. Ideally, electrical stimuli should induce predictable and correlated spectral shifts in both optical outputs.8–10 However, these responses arise from distinct molecular mechanisms: electrochromism is primarily governed by ground-state absorption processes,11–15 whereas fluorescence depends on excited-state relaxation pathways, which often lead to spectral mismatches between the two modes.16–19 Conventional molecular design strategies, such as extending π-conjugation,20–22 enhancing molecular planarity and rigidity,23 or leveraging excited-state proton transfer effects,24–26 can effectively tune fluorescence but are insufficient to reconcile EC and EFC responses. This limitation arises from the intrinsic disparity between ground-state absorption and excited-state emission processes, hindering simultaneous harmonization of their optical outputs.27–29 Consequently, current EC/EFC materials face a fundamental trade-off between color consistency and luminescence efficiency, hindering their translation from laboratory demonstrations to robust, application-ready technologies.

The core challenge lies in establishing strong electronic coupling between ground-state absorption centers responsible for visual color and excited-state emissive pathways that dictate fluorescence.30–32 Such coupling ensures that the color generated during the electrochromic process remains consistent with the fluorescence emission, thereby achieving synergistic alignment of optical output across both modes.33–35 Achieving this requires precise molecular engineering to intrinsically link ground- and excited-state processes, guided by a fundamental understanding of how the molecular structure governs ground-state and excited-state dynamics.36–38 Rational design should enable electrochemical potentials to modulate the electronic states governing both absorption and emission, leading to spectrally aligned and synchronized color responses.39–41 In multicomponent systems, minimizing spectral overlap and suppressing undesired energy transfer between chromophoric and fluorophoric units is vital to avoid spectral distortion or quenching.42–44 Furthermore, controlling excited-state dynamics is crucial to favor radiative recombination and maintain fluorescence consistent with the EC state, particularly in narrow-bandgap systems with red-shifted emission.45,46 Maintaining color-fluorescence alignment alongside high fluorescence quantum yields remains a key challenge, highlighting the need for intrinsically integrated molecular architectures that enforce synchronized optical responses across EC and EFC modes.

Here, we present a new class of color-fluorescence-alignment EC/EFC ionic liquids (EEILs) that address the longstanding mismatch between electrochromic and emissive responses. Our strategy employs bottom-up molecular engineering, integrating targeted design principles to achieve synchronized electro-optical responses. Specifically, donor–acceptor (D–A) and donor–π–acceptor (D–π–A) architectures are constructed using a triphenylamine (TPA) core as a robust electron donor. Through rational modulation of linker and acceptor units, we precisely regulate the electronic structure and excited-state dynamics. The resulting materials exhibit striking color-fluorescence alignment across red, green, and yellow spectral regions, with electrochromic color directly matching fluorescence emission, overcoming the fundamental trade-off between EC and EFC. As proof of concept, we further integrate these materials into a traffic signaling system, showcasing reduced energy consumption, operational adaptability, and enhanced visual clarity. This work represents a paradigm shift towards intelligent infrastructure and advanced display technologies.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of EEILs

A rationally engineered molecular architecture, a single chromophore, was designed for achieving synchronized electro-optical responses, where the redox-triggered ICT pathway intrinsically couples ground-state absorption (dictating EC color) with excited-state emission (dictating EFC color). This is achieved via a robust D–π–A framework, utilizing the TPA core as a stable, electroactive electron donor. Crucially, precise control over energy levels and frontier orbital distribution ensures that the same ICT transition is responsible for visible absorption shifts during electrochemical reduction, and also dictates the emission energy in the excited state. This ensures that the visual color in the EC state intrinsically aligns with the emission color in the EFC state, avoiding the spectral mismatches common in existing materials, which often lead to reduced fluorescence quantum yields. To further optimize performance and prevent aggregation-induced quenching, a methylimidazole unit is incorporated as an ionic liquid moiety, enhancing solubility and molecular stability without compromising optical properties. Furthermore, ionic liquids have extremely low vapor pressures and are not easily volatile, thereby avoiding the atmospheric pollution caused by traditional organic solvents. They align with the principles of green chemistry aimed at reducing volatile organic compound (VOC) emissions and are considered environmentally friendly materials.

To systematically tune the spectral characteristics and achieve target absorption and emission wavelengths (specifically red, green, and yellow), we explored the selection of acceptor units for targeted spectral properties. The benzothiadiazole unit, featuring strong electron-withdrawing properties and planarity, effectively lowers the LUMO and promotes ICT, leading to significant red shifts,47–49 making it ideal for red-absorbing and red-emitting chromophores. The cyano group, a small but potent electron-withdrawing moiety with a large dipole moment, enhances the push–pull effect without increasing molecular volume,50–52 facilitating molecular stacking control and enabling the design of yellow-absorbing and yellow-emitting molecules. For green absorption and emission, the thiophene unit, while possessing weaker inherent electron-withdrawing capabilities, excels as a π-bridge by extending conjugation and promoting ICT efficiency.53–55 Its inherent flexibility can also help suppress solid-state aggregation. By strategically integrating these diverse acceptor units within the TPA-based D–π–A framework and coupled with the integration of an ionic liquid moiety for superior processability and photophysical stability, we have successfully synthesized a novel class of EEILs exhibiting exceptional color-fluorescence alignment across the visible spectrum.

As illustrated in Fig. 1a, EEILs (including EEIL-G, EEIL-Y, and EEIL-R) were synthesized through a modular sequence involving Suzuki coupling, Knoevenagel condensation, and ionization/anion exchange reactions, enabling precise control over the molecular structure and functionality. The calculated electrostatic potential (ESP) maps reveal localized positive charges primarily on the imidazole ring, enhancing its electrophilic character and facilitating favorable cation–anion interactions. Comprehensive spectroscopic and thermal analyses, including 1H-NMR, 13C-NMR, FTIR, TGA, and DSC, confirm the successful synthesis and high purity of all three materials (Fig. 1b, c and S4–S8). EEILs were insoluble in water but soluble in DCM, ACN, DMSO, and PC. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations further validate the design. The calculations were performed using the Gaussian16 software package with the cam-wb97xd exchange-correlation functional and the def2-SVP basis set. The results indicate that the HOMO levels are localized on the TPA donor, while the LUMO resides predominantly on the acceptor moiety, confirming the expected push–pull character. The computed HOMO–LUMO energy gaps (4.66 eV for EEIL-G, 5.37 eV for EEIL-Y, and 4.35 eV for EEIL-R) correlate well with the observed absorption maxima and enable fine-tuning across the green, yellow, and red spectral regions. These results collectively demonstrate that our molecular engineering strategy is not merely synthetic but fundamentally physicochemically designed to enforce spectral coherence between EC and EFC states.

To further validate the correctness of the molecular design strategy, DFT calculations were also performed on EEIL-G, EEIL-Y, and EEIL-R. Fig. 1d indicates their HOMO and LUMO energies to be −7.56 eV and −2.90 eV (EEIL-G), −8.10 eV and −2.73 eV (EEIL-Y), and −7.26 eV and −2.91 eV (EEIL-R), respectively, with energy level differences of 4.66 eV (EEIL-G), 5.37 eV (EEIL-Y), and 4.35 eV (EEIL-R). To investigate the weak interactions between the cations of the three ionic liquid molecules (EEIL-G cation (EEIL-G′), EEIL-Y cation (EEIL-Y′), and EEIL-R cation (EEIL-R′)) and the perchlorate anion (ClO4−), binding energies were calculated based on optimized molecular geometries (as shown in Fig. S9). The calculated binding energies are as follows: EEIL-G′ with ClO4− is −451.87 kJ mol−1, EEIL-Y′ with ClO4− is −385.82 kJ mol−1, and EEIL-R′ with ClO4− is −440.55 kJ mol−1. EEIL-G′ exhibits a higher binding energy with ClO4−; it is hypothesized that this may help improve ionic stability and reduce ionic mobility in the solid-state matrix, thereby enhancing the device's durability. This hypothesis was validated in subsequent cycling stability tests.

Additionally, Fig. 1e presents the Jablonski energy level diagrams for EEIL-G, EEIL-Y, and EEIL-R molecules, which outline the formation and decay processes of the molecules' excited state. Taking EEIL-G as an example, according to the energy minimization principle, an electron transitions from the lowest vibrational energy level of the ground state (S0, position 1) to a higher vibrational energy level of the excited state (S1, position 2) upon absorbing a photon of 3.5866 eV. It then quickly relaxes to the lowest vibrational level of S1 through vibrational relaxation (VR), and the electron ultimately returns to the lowest vibrational energy level of the first excited state (S1, position 3) and reverts to the ground state (position 4) via a radiative transition (RT), releasing 2.8569 eV of energy and emitting fluorescence. The molecular electron cloud distribution during each process is also depicted in Fig. 1e. Detailed calculations are provided in Table S1.

Fig. 1f displays the absorption spectra of EEIL-G, EEIL-Y, and EEIL-R molecules in acetonitrile solution, along with their fluorescence emission spectra. The absorption and emission spectra of all three molecules show separation without overlap. Significant Stokes shifts (EEIL-G: 246 nm, EEIL-Y: 262 nm, and EEIL-R: 312 nm) have been determined as shown in Fig. 1f(i, ii, and iii), confirming the validity of the design strategy. Notably, the emission maxima (EEIL-G: 518 nm, EEIL-Y: 562 nm, and EEIL-R: 643 nm) precisely coincide with the reduced-state absorption maxima (EEIL-G: 272 nm, EEIL-Y: 300 nm, and EEIL-R: 331 nm), which demonstrates the intrinsic color-fluorescence alignment at the molecular level and directly results from the shared ICT mechanism. This spectral alignment ensures that the visual color seen under ambient light exactly matches the fluorescent color observed under UV excitation, eliminating perceptual discontinuity during mode switching.

Electrochemical properties of dual-modal devices

To evaluate the electrochemical performance of the designed EEILs in a functional system, dual-modal EC/EFC devices were fabricated using an asymmetric configuration. As schematically illustrated in Fig. 2a, each device consists of two ITO-coated glass electrodes, with one side coated with the respective EEIL (EEIL-G, EEIL-Y, or EEIL-R) gel and the opposite side coated with a p-benzoquinone (BQ) gel as the counter redox material (detailed gel preparation and assembly procedures are provided in the SI). The EFILs//BQ device was designed as a bilayer. This structure simplifies the ionic conduction layer compared to a conventional three-layer device by adding an electrolyte (LiClO4) directly to the EC/EFC layer (EFIL gel) and the ionic storage layer (BQ gel) to bring the EC/EFC layer and the ion storage layer in direct contact. This simplifies the fabrication process of the EFILs//BQ device, reduces the thickness of the EFILs//BQ device, and enables the EFILs//BQ device to have a fast response speed and a bistable nature. The corresponding redox mechanism governing the dual-modal operation is depicted in Fig. 2b. Taking the EEIL-G//BQ device as a representative example, upon application of a positive bias, EEIL-G near the anode undergoes oxidation, losing an electron to form EEIL-G+˙, which induces a distinct color change from green to dark brown (EC response). Concurrently, at the cathode, BQ accepts an electron and is reduced to BQ−˙. Upon reversing the voltage to 0 V (reduction potential), EEIL-G+˙ is reduced back to its original green state, while BQ−˙ is re-oxidized to BQ, completing a fully reversible electrochemical cycle that underpins both color switching and fluorescence modulation.

Electrochemical properties of dual-modal devices. The assembled architecture (a), redox mechanism (b), CV curves (c), EIS curves (d), and Arrhenius conductivity plots (e) of EEIL-G//BQ, EEIL-Y//BQ, EEIL-R//BQ devices. (f) The calendar life of the EEIL-G//BQ device's electrochemical performance.

The electrochemical behavior of the devices was systematically characterized via cyclic voltammetry (CV). As shown in Fig. 2c, well-defined redox couples were observed for all three devices, with oxidation/reduction peaks at 0.8 V/0.15 V for EEIL-G//BQ, 0.45 V/0.35 V for EEIL-Y//BQ, and 0.9 V/0.3 V for EEIL-R//BQ, confirming efficient and reversible charge transfer processes. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was employed to assess the ionic transport properties within the gel electrolyte systems. The Nyquist plots (Fig. 2d) reveal low interfacial resistance and high ionic conductivities of 1.35 × 10-4 S cm−1, 1.21 × 10-4 S cm−1, and 1.32 × 10-4 S cm−1 for EEIL-G//BQ, EEIL-Y//BQ, and EEIL-R//BQ devices, respectively, in the frequency range of 0.1 Hz to 1.0 MHz. To further understand the temperature dependence of ion conduction, Arrhenius plots were constructed from impedance measurements conducted between 0 and 40 °C (Fig. 2e). The calculated activation energies for ion migration were 13.4 kJ mol−1 (EEIL-G//BQ), 8.5 kJ mol−1 (EEIL-Y//BQ), and 5.7 kJ mol−1 (EEIL-R//BQ), indicating favorable ionic mobility and thermally assisted conduction within the devices. Collectively, these electrochemical metrics, including narrow operating voltage windows, high ionic conductivity, and low activation energies, underscore the energy-efficient nature and promising practical potential of the developed EEIL-based devices.

Device operational stability is a critical parameter for real-world applications. The calendar life of the EEIL-G//BQ device was evaluated by subjecting it to repeated cycling (50 cycles per day) at an alternating voltage of 0–1 V over an extended period. As shown in Fig. 2f, the device maintained consistent electrochemical performance for over 90 days, demonstrating exceptional long-term durability. Representative current density switching profiles recorded on the 1st and 90th days (Fig. 2f-i, ii) further confirm the retention of fast and reversible switching characteristics without significant degradation. Similar long-term stability was also observed for EEIL-Y//BQ and EEIL-R//BQ devices, as detailed in Fig. S10 and S11. These results affirm that the molecularly engineered EEIL materials, coupled with a robust device architecture, enable not only dual-modal optical functionality but also remarkable operational resilience, meeting key requirements for sustainable and reliable electro-optical devices.

The EC performance

Building upon the favorable electrochemical properties and molecularly engineered design of EEIL-G, EEIL-Y, and EEIL-R as revealed in Section 2.2, their EC performance was systematically evaluated through UV-vis spectrophotometry combined with real-time voltage control. As shown in Fig. 3a, all three devices, including EEIL-G//BQ, EEIL-Y//BQ, and EEIL-R//BQ, exhibit rapid and pronounced transmittance modulation at applied voltages of 0–1.0 V, 0–0.8 V, and 0–1.1 V, respectively, corresponding to their individually optimized redox thresholds. Upon application of a forward bias, significant decreases in optical transmittance are observed across the visible spectrum, indicating efficient oxidation of the EEIL species at the anode and concomitant formation of colored radical cations (e.g., EEIL-G+˙). The resulting contrast ratios reach 84.75% (EEIL-G//BQ at 584 nm), 86.86% (EEIL-Y//BQ at 530 nm), and 81.64% (EEIL-R//BQ at 774 nm), demonstrating high coloration efficiency and deep modulation capability even at ultra-low driving voltages, which are consistent with the narrow voltage windows and low activation energies reported earlier.

(b) Switching times for coloring and bleaching processes of EEIL-G//BQ, EEIL-Y//BQ, and EEIL-R//BQ devices with a retention time of 5 s at 584 nm, 530 nm, and 774 nm, respectively. (c) The EC display of the patterned devices. (d) The chromaticity coordinates of EEIL-G//BQ, EEIL-Y//BQ, and EEIL-R//BQ devices in the L*a*b* CIE color space. The switching kinetics were further quantified at a 5 second retention time, revealing impressive response speeds: coloring times of 0.8 s (EEIL-G//BQ), 0.6 s (EEIL-Y//BQ), and 0.9 s (EEIL-R//BQ), along with bleaching times of 0.9 s, 0.7 s, and 1.0 s, respectively (Fig. 3b). These sub-second switching behaviors underscore the rapid electron transfer and ion mobility facilitated by the ionic liquid matrix and the well-designed charge redistribution pathway within the D–A conjugated backbone. Notably, the superior speed of EEIL-Y//BQ correlates with its lowest activation energy (8.5 kJ mol−1) and highest ionic conductivity (1.21 × 10−4 S cm−1), reinforcing the direct link between molecular design, interfacial transport dynamics, and macroscopic device performance.

To demonstrate the practical viability of these materials in dynamic visual applications, patterned EC devices were fabricated using photolithography techniques (Fig. 3c). In the initial state, the EEIL-G//BQ, EEIL-Y//BQ, and EEIL-R//BQ devices appear uniformly green, yellow, and red, respectively, without distinguishable patterns (Fig. 3c-i, iii, v). Upon application of localized voltage stimuli, distinct pre-designed patterns, such as “ON/OFF” symbols and geometric shapes, became clearly visible (Fig. 3c-ii, iv, vi), confirming spatially selective and reversible switching. When the voltage was removed, the patterns faded completely, returning to the transparent state, thereby enabling true on-demand display functionality. This capability highlights the potential for integration into smart windows, wearable displays, and information encryption systems.

Further insight into the colorimetric characteristics is provided by chromaticity analysis in the CIE L*a*b* color space (Fig. 3d and Table S2). The coordinates of the three devices are widely dispersed—particularly in the a* and b* axes—indicating distinct and non-overlapping hues: green (EEIL-G//BQ), yellow (EEIL-Y//BQ), and deep red (EEIL-R//BQ). This large spectral separation not only confirms the successful tuning of optical responses across the visible region but also lays the foundation for future full-color EC systems based on color-mixing principles, where combinations of these primary colors could yield a broad palette of secondary hues.

The coloration efficiency (CE), a critical metric for evaluating EC material performance, was calculated from the optical density versus charge density curves (Fig. 3e). The CE values for EEIL-G//BQ, EEIL-Y//BQ, and EEIL-R//BQ reach 587 cm2 C−1, 842 cm2 C−1, and 749 cm2 C−1, respectively, at their respective peak absorption wavelengths (584 nm, 530 nm, and 774 nm). Among them, EEIL-Y//BQ exhibits the highest CE, attributed to its optimal balance of strong electronic transition intensity, efficient charge injection, and minimal parasitic side reactions, further supported by its low activation energy and high ionic conductivity. These values surpass those of many conventional organic EC materials and approach those of their inorganic counterparts, underscoring the effectiveness of our molecular engineering strategy.

Finally, long-term operational stability was assessed through calendar-life testing. Representative results for EEIL-Y//BQ show that after 90 days of daily cycling, 50 full-color switching cycles per day, the average transmittance contrast decreased by only 6.4% (Fig. S12). The switching curves remain sharply defined over time, with no sign of hysteresis or fading, indicating excellent structural and electrochemical robustness. Similarly, EEIL-G//BQ and EEIL-R//BQ exhibit stable performance throughout 90 day testing, as confirmed by Fig. S13 and S14. The above results indicate that the chromophores of EEILs did not suffer from significant irreversible structural damage during the redox process, demonstrating good overall structural stability and photostability. This exceptional durability is attributed to the inherent chemical stability of the imidazolium-based ionic liquids, the absence of volatile components, and the suppression of unwanted degradation pathways due to the closed gel architecture.

Together, these findings confirm that the developed EEIL-based dual-modal devices not only achieve rapid, high-contrast, and energy-efficient electrochromism but also exhibit long-term reliability and patternable functionality, which are essential characteristics for next-generation intelligent optoelectronic interfaces.

The EFC performance

Leveraging the well-characterized electrochemical and EC properties, the EFC performance of the EEIL-based devices was systematically investigated to evaluate their dual-modal optical switching capability. As depicted in Fig. 4a, fluorescence emission spectra of the EEIL-G//BQ, EEIL-Y//BQ, and EEIL-R//BQ devices were recorded at applied voltages of 0–1 V, 0–0.8 V, and 0–1.1 V, respectively, using a fluorescence spectrophotometer integrated with an electrochemical workstation. Under UV excitation at 272 nm, 300 nm, and 330 nm, respectively, the devices exhibited strong fluorescence emission peaks at 518 nm (green), 562 nm (yellow), and 643 nm (red) in the “off” state (0 V). Upon application of the respective forward voltages, the fluorescence intensity was almost completely quenched, demonstrating pronounced EFC behavior that is directly linked to the electrochemical oxidation of the EEIL species to their non-fluorescent radical cations (EEIL+˙). This electroluminescence behavior can be explained by the following fluorescence quenching mechanism: the triphenylamine cation radical is an intrinsic fluorescence quencher with good stability and fast response speed. When a forward voltage is applied to the device, triphenylamine is oxidized to triphenylamine cation radicals, causing a change in the electronic structure of the EEILs, which leads to fluorescence quenching. When a 0 V voltage is applied to the device, the triphenylamine cation radical is reduced back to triphenylamine, and the fluorescence of the EEILs is restored. The corresponding CIE 1931 chromaticity coordinates were determined to be (0.0522, 0.8252) for EEIL-G//BQ, (0.3802, 0.6175) for EEIL-Y//BQ, and (0.7223, 0.2777) for EEIL-R//BQ (Fig. 4b), confirming distinct and saturated emission colors that are well-separated in the color space—an essential feature for multi-color or full-color EFC displays.

The dynamic fluorescence switching behavior was further quantified by measuring the response times under alternating voltage pulses with a 5 second retention interval. As shown in Fig. 4c–e, the fluorescence turn-off times (quenching) were 0.5 s, 0.7 s, and 0.6 s for EEIL-G//BQ, EEIL-Y//BQ, and EEIL-R//BQ, respectively, while the turn-on times (recovery) were 0.6 s, 0.6 s, and 0.4 s. These sub-second switching speeds, coupled with high photoluminescence on/off ratios of 125.3, 96.6, and 114.8 for the respective devices, underscore the efficient and reversible redox-controlled fluorescence modulation enabled by the molecular design of the EEILs. Such rapid and high-contrast switching is attributed to the fast ion transport and low activation energies previously established in Section 2.2.

To demonstrate the practical applicability of these materials in patterned displays, spatially defined EFC devices were fabricated and operated under UV illumination and dark conditions. Initially, the unpatterned devices exhibited uniform green, yellow, and red fluorescence, respectively (Fig. 4f-i, iii, v). Upon application of the respective driving voltages (1.0 V for EEIL-G//BQ, 0.8 V for EEIL-Y//BQ, and 1.1 V for EEIL-R//BQ), the fluorescence in the patterned regions was selectively quenched, resulting in a sharp and reversible appearance of the predefined images (Fig. 4f-ii, iv, vi). This spatially resolved fluorescence switching highlights the potential of these devices for applications in encrypted displays, smart labels, and low-light information interfaces.

Long-term operational stability of the EFC function was assessed through calendar-life testing. Taking EEIL-R//BQ as a representative example, the device was subjected to 50 full fluorescence switching cycles per day over 90 days. As shown in Fig. S15, the fluorescence contrast decreased by only 5.1% after this extended period, indicating excellent cycling stability and UV irradiation stability. A similar robust performance was observed for EEIL-G//BQ and EEIL-Y//BQ devices (Fig. S16 and S17), confirming that the ionic liquid-based gel architecture effectively suppresses photobleaching and electrochemical degradation, thereby ensuring reliable EFC operation over prolonged use.

Based on these findings, the EEIL-based devices exhibit vibrant, voltage-tunable fluorescence with rapid switching and high contrast while maintaining long-term stability—important attributes for their implementation in advanced dual-modal EC-EFC displays and optical devices.

Low-energy-demand traffic signaling system based on EC/EFC displays

Based on their exceptional EC and EFC functionalities, coupled with the inherent advantages of ionic liquids such as low driving voltage, rapid response, and high stability, the EEIL-based devices are exceptionally well-suited for the development of a next-generation intelligent transportation system. Beyond conventional traffic signal lights, the demonstrated EC/EFC dual-modal properties open up unprecedented possibilities for smart, energy-efficient visual communication platforms. These include, but are not limited to, intelligent on-demand displays, dynamic text and road sign presentations, and digital countdown timers, achievable through the fabrication of dot-matrix and pixelized device arrays (Fig. 5a). This synergistic combination of color and fluorescence switching offers enhanced visibility under varying ambient light conditions and provides entirely new paradigms for energy-efficient and reliable smart transportation solutions.

To validate these concepts, a series of prototype devices was meticulously assembled, integrating the EEIL-G//BQ, EEIL-Y//BQ, and EEIL-R//BQ dual-modal elements with essential control and power components such as dry batteries, microcontrollers, UV-LED backlight panels, filters, and back films. The compositional structure and operational principles of this intelligent traffic signaling system are schematically illustrated in Fig. 5b. Fig. 5b-i–iii delineate the working mechanisms of the traffic light control display, the on-demand pixelized display panel, and the digital countdown display panel, respectively. The detailed microcontroller architecture and its control flow are further elaborated in Fig. S18.

The practical implementation of a novel traffic light system is visualized in Fig. 5c-i and ii, representing the device under daylight and UV illumination, respectively. This system effectively harnesses the dual-modal capabilities of the EEIL-G//BQ, EEIL-Y//BQ, and EEIL-R//BQ devices. During daylight hours or in environments with ample ambient light, the desired signal color, for example, green illumination, is achieved by applying appropriate forward voltages to the EEIL-Y//BQ and EEIL-R//BQ devices, transitioning them to their colored black states, while the EEIL-G//BQ device remains in its neutral green state (Fig. 5c-iii). Conversely, under low-light conditions or at night, the system automatically activates the UV-LED backlight. The microcontroller then applies voltages to the EEIL-Y//BQ and EEIL-R//BQ devices to induce their colored black states. Simultaneously, the activated UV-LED excites the EEIL-G//BQ device, which then emits bright green fluorescence (Fig. 5c-iv), providing excellent visibility independent of ambient light. Similar operational logic applies for generating yellow and red illumination signals (Fig. 5c-v through 5c-viii).

Furthermore, to underscore the information-carrying capacity and adaptability of this low-energy paradigm, 10 × 10 pixelized display devices were fabricated. These devices leverage precisely patterned fork-finger electrodes encapsulated in PET frames (Fig. 5d). The matrix structure, with its independent pixel units formed by the overlapping conductive regions of the electrodes, effectively confines ion transport between pixels. This design, as detailed in Fig. 5d and e, ensures precise control over individual pixel illumination and image formation, demonstrating the system's ability for on-demand, high-resolution pixel displays without significant crosstalk. Crucially, the EEIL-G gel and BQ gel were employed, allowing both EC and EFC mechanisms to contribute to dynamic visual communication within each pixel.

In addition to pixelated displays, digital countdown signaling panels were developed, paving the way for the next generation of intelligent traffic management (Fig. 5f). This configuration, comprising ITO glass, etched ITO glass, EEIL-R gel, and BQ gel, allows a microcontroller-programmed sequence to execute precise digital displays, enabling dynamic countdown timers for red lights or pedestrian crossings. The detailed operational mechanisms for both the pixelized and digital display technologies are further elaborated in the SI (Fig. S19 and S20).

The profound implications of this low-energy technology for traffic signaling are underscored by significant potential energy savings. Considering that approximately 300 000 urban road intersections are equipped with traffic signals in China, with an average of 50 signal heads per intersection, upgrading these systems to our proposed low-energy design could result in an annual energy saving of 6.77 × 106 GJ. This is equivalent to the combustion of 2.31 × 105 tons of standard coal, leading to a reduction in carbon dioxide emissions by 6.24 × 105 tons (Fig. 5g). Detailed calculations and analysis of transport losses are provided in Note S1. This substantial energy conservation directly translates to reduced operational costs and significant environmental benefits, aligning with global initiatives for energy sustainability and emission reduction in the transportation sector. In essence, the developed EC/EFC dual-modal devices pave the way for a more visual, informative, and ultra-energy-efficient future for traffic management.

Conclusions

This study presents a rational molecular design strategy to address the intrinsic color-fluorescence mismatching of EC/EFC bifunctional materials. By integrating electroactive chromophores with ionic liquid moieties, we have engineered a class of EEILs that enable simultaneous and tunable control of both color and fluorescence. This integrated molecular architecture ensures precise coupling between ground-state absorption and excited-state emission, leading to enhanced performance and predictability. The resulting devices demonstrate rapid, reversible electrochromic switching with high optical contrast, alongside well-defined and controllable fluorescence emission. These synchronized dual-mode capabilities enable highly efficient and adaptable visual communication systems, as exemplified by a low-energy-demand traffic signaling system with improved visibility for intelligent transportation. Overall, this work establishes a robust platform for designing multifunctional optoelectronic materials, advancing energy-efficient, high-performance display technologies, and next-generation intelligent infrastructure.
