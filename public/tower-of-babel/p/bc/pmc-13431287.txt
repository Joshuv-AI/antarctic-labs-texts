Cross-cohort projection of clinically anchored latent risk enables multi-omics interpretation without refitting

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Linking clinically derived risk signals to reproducible molecular states across independent datasets remains a central challenge in translational bioinformatics. Although high-throughput molecular profiling has enabled increasingly detailed characterization of tumor biology, these data are often not directly aligned with clinically defined risk measures, which are typically derived from imaging, clinical variables, or longitudinal outcomes. As a result, clinical risk assessment and molecular interpretation frequently operate in parallel rather than within a shared analytical framework, limiting cross-cohort comparability and biological interpretability.

A key limitation of existing approaches is their reliance on cohort-specific model fitting. Risk models trained in one dataset are often not directly transferable to external cohorts, particularly when outcome definitions, data modalities, or measurement scales differ. This restricts the ability to relate clinically anchored risk signals to external multi-omics resources, where paired outcome data are usually unavailable. Developing strategies that enable clinically anchored latent risk to be projected into a shared, reusable coordinate system may therefore facilitate the integration of clinical and molecular information across heterogeneous datasets without requiring model refitting.

One potential solution is to construct a clinically anchored latent risk representation and project it into a baseline-computable coordinate that can be applied unchanged across datasets. In this framework, a local cohort is used to define a latent risk axis linked to clinically meaningful outcomes, while a fixed projection function maps baseline variables to this latent representation. Once trained, the projection can be reused without refitting in external cohorts, enabling consistent positioning of samples within a shared risk space. This design avoids dependence on outcome availability in external datasets and provides a basis for cross-cohort comparison and downstream molecular interpretation.

Within such a projection-based framework, multi-omics profiling provides a route to relate clinically anchored latent risk signals to underlying molecular programs. Bulk transcriptomics enables pathway-level interpretation at scale, but it reflects averaged tissue-level signals that may be influenced by cellular composition and compartment mixing [1, 2]. Single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics can help attribute bulk signals to specific cell states and provide tissue-level spatial context, thereby supporting cross-scale interpretation of projected risk signals [3–5]. Together, bulk, single-cell, and spatial transcriptomic data provide complementary interpretive layers for a cautious cross-scale reading of projected risk signals.

A remaining question is whether such molecular interpretations can be made observable in a clinically accessible compartment during treatment. Liquid biopsy, particularly circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) measured within cell-free DNA (cfDNA), offers one such blood-based readout; ctDNA abundance is related to disease burden and has been associated with clinical outcomes in metastatic prostate cancer, suggesting its potential as a complementary layer for projected risk assessment [6, 7].

Using time to castration resistance (TTCR) in metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (mHSPC) as a clinically anchored translational task, we developed a transferable framework for projecting clinically anchored latent risk into interpretable molecular readouts. The framework was anchored in a local imaging cohort and applied to public cross-cohort molecular resources, where consistency across independent datasets and measurement scales was used as a conservative check on interpretability. Conceptually, this framework combines local clinical anchoring, fixed external projection, and cross-scale molecular interpretation to place projected risk signals in a shared context with reproducible molecular states and candidate blood-based readouts.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was designed as a clinically anchored translational framework for cross-cohort molecular interpretation of TTCR-related risk in mHSPC. The framework was anchored in a local institutional cohort (n = 289) with baseline baseline prostate-specific membrane antigen positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PSMA PET/CT) and TTCR follow-up. In this cohort, imaging and TTCR were used to define a locally anchored latent-growth surrogate, which was then projected from baseline clinical variables into a baseline-computable clinically anchored latent risk coordinate, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document}. Cohort definition, PET/CT acquisition, and endpoint adjudication followed the institutional protocol summarized below and are further detailed in the Supplementary Methods and Table S11. The fixed projector was then applied to external bulk transcriptomic datasets to derive projected \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document} for each sample and associated pathway-level summaries, followed by cross-scale interpretation using single-cell and spatial transcriptomic data. Preliminary blood-based observability was further assessed using public cfDNA datasets, with analyses restricted to copy-number proxy readouts rather than clinical-grade variant calling.

Censoring-aware projection model

Because TTCR is unavailable in most public molecular resources, we constructed a clinically anchored latent risk coordinate, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document}, that could be applied unchanged to external cohorts for cross-cohort molecular interpretation. In the local cohort, we modeled the relationship between baseline clinical variables and the imaging-linked latent-growth surrogate, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $r$\end{document}, using a Tobit-like censored normal regression [9]. The baseline variables included age, International Society of Urological Pathology grade group (ISUP), T stage, and N stage. T stage and N stage were encoded as ordinal numeric variables according to their clinical ordering, with T1–T4 coded as 1–4 and N0–N1 coded as 0–1.

We then exported the fitted coefficients and standardization parameters so that the same baseline-to- \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document} mapping could be reproduced in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and other cohorts without refitting. Downstream analyses focused on molecular readouts associated with this projected \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document} rather than on refitting TTCR prediction models in external datasets. We additionally reported a three-level stratification of the continuous projected \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document} (low/intermediate/high), using tertile cutoffs derived from the local cohort. In the independent external cohort, these fixed local cutoffs were reused to evaluate TTCR separation by Kaplan–Meier analysis [10].

Bulk transcriptomic analyses in TCGA-PRAD

Bulk RNA-seq data and associated clinical annotations from The Cancer Genome Atlas Prostate Adenocarcinoma cohort (TCGA-PRAD) were obtained from the Genomic Data Commons [11, 12]. Using the fixed projector described above, we computed projected \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document} in TCGA-PRAD without refitting and treated it as a continuous variable in the primary analyses to reduce information loss and cutpoint dependence associated with arbitrary stratification. We quantified proliferation and androgen receptor (AR)/lineage differentiation axes on DESeq2 variance-stabilized expression data using prespecified gene sets. For each gene set, genes were z-standardized across samples and the resulting z-scores were averaged to obtain a per-sample proxy score. The proliferation proxy was defined using nine core cell-cycle genes (AURKA, BIRC5, CDC20, CDK1, FOXM1, MYBL2, PLK1, TOP2A, and UBE2C), selected as a compact marker set for the MSigDB Hallmark G2M checkpoint program. The AR proxy was computed from nine classic AR-lineage genes (AR, KLK3, KLK2, TMPRSS2, FKBP5, NKX3-1, ACSL3, ABCC4, and STEAP1), consistent with the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) Hallmark Androgen Response annotation and prior AR activity signatures in prostate cancer [13–15]. To account for microenvironmental confounding in bulk profiles, we used Estimation of STromal and Immune cells in MAlignant Tumor tissues using Expression data (ESTIMATE) to quantify stromal content and derive an ESTIMATE-based tumor purity measure. Associations between projected \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document} and transcriptomic proxies were summarized using Spearman correlation. To adjust for stromal score and tumor purity, we estimated partial Spearman correlations using a rank-residual approach [16]. For visualization only, TCGA-PRAD tumors were additionally stratified into low-, intermediate-, and high- \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document} groups using cohort-specific \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${q}_{0.33}$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${q}_{0.67}$\end{document} cutoffs derived from the TCGA-PRAD \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document} distribution.

scRNA-guided deconvolution and cell-state proxies

We constructed the single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA) reference atlas from public prostate cancer scRNA-seq datasets that passed preprocessing and quality-control filtering, including GSE181294 [17] and GSE143791 [18]. Gene symbols were harmonized according to the conventions of the Human Genome Organisation Gene Nomenclature Committee (HGNC) and converted to uppercase, and duplicated symbols were collapsed by averaging expression values. Bulk RNA-seq counts were library-size normalized to counts per million (CPM; scaling factor 106) and log-transformed as log2(CPM + 1) prior to deconvolution. To improve identifiability and robustness, fine-grained reference states were aggregated into a hybrid panel of interpretable compartments. Within epithelial cells, we defined three composite states: luminal AR-high, proliferating epithelial, and epithelial plasticity, where the plasticity state pooled the remaining epithelial programs not assigned to AR-high or proliferating states [19]. Major immune and stromal compartments were also included to capture the dominant microenvironmental lineages. Cell-state fractions were estimated using non-negative least squares (NNLS) [20]. NNLS was selected as the primary deconvolution engine because it provides a transparent, deterministic, and non-negative decomposition with minimal dependence on algorithm-specific hyperparameters. Deconvolution was performed on genes shared between bulk profiles and the hybrid signature, with a minimum of 300 shared genes required for stable estimation. Before fitting, each shared gene in the bulk expression matrix was standardized using its across-sample mean and standard deviation, excluding near-zero-variance genes (SD < 1 × 10−6). The hybrid signature matrix was scaled using the same gene-wise parameters. NNLS coefficients were constrained to be non-negative and normalized to sum to one, yielding interpretable fractional compositions. Deconvolution quality was assessed using the normalized residual. Samples with a normalized residual >20.0 were flagged as invalid, and their deconvolution fractions were set to missing in downstream analyses. Associations between projected \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document} and deconvolved fractions were quantified using Spearman rank correlation.

Spatial coherence assessment

We used a public 10× Genomics Visium HD human prostate cancer spatial transcriptomics dataset as an external spatial coherence assessment layer to test whether the upstream prespecified molecular axes exhibited organized spatial patterns within intact tissue architecture [21]. Spatial coordinates were taken directly from the Space Ranger output and mapped to the tissue image without any geometric warping. Precomputed spot-level program z-scores were imported directly as the spatial readouts of the prespecified molecular axes. To control computational burden while preserving spatial topology, we aggregated spots into non-overlapping square spatial bins (256 pixels per side) in image coordinate space and used bin-level mean program scores for downstream spatial analyses. We computed global Moran’s I on the binned score field to quantify spatial autocorrelation (clustering versus dispersion), using the classical definition introduced by Moran. Spatial weights were defined on the regular bin grid using four-neighbor adjacency. Significance was assessed by permutation testing: bin-level scores were randomly permuted 1000 times under fixed adjacency to generate a null distribution of Moran’s I and an empirical P-value. Program coupling was evaluated using Spearman rank correlation between bin-level program scores. For visualization, program scores were smoothed on a coarse grid and high-score regions were outlined using quantile-based thresholds; these hotspot maps were descriptive and were not used for statistical inference.

Public liquid-biopsy cohort and analytical workflow

We analyzed the public metastatic prostate cancer liquid-biopsy cohort EGAD00001009651, including author-provided BAM files, clinical annotations, and supplementary gene-level copy number (CN) tables [10]. EGAD00001009651 is a controlled-access dataset, and the present analysis was conducted using data obtained under approved access conditions. Because the original study provided the analytical code and processed data, and to avoid unnecessary reprocessing, the present study reports only the lightweight analysis based on the author-provided clinical annotations and supplementary gene-level copy-number tables.

For each patient, one baseline cfDNA sample was selected using a prespecified prioritization scheme that favored hormone-sensitive collection status, quality-control pass, higher tumor fraction (TF), and higher median sequencing coverage. Age, T stage, N stage, and ISUP grade group were parsed into numeric variables according to predefined rules. Projected \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document} was computed using the fixed projector trained in our local imaging cohort, without refitting in EGAD00001009651. The primary CN readout was the RB1 gene-level log-ratio. Additional CN-derived summary measures were constructed for signal characterization and sensitivity analyses, as detailed in the Supplementary Methods. To reduce low-signal false negatives in cfDNA CN profiling, we applied a prespecified detectability gate that combined reported TF with a global CN-amplitude summary measure, as detailed in the Supplementary Methods. The primary analysis evaluated the association between projected \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document} and RB1 using Spearman correlation in the detectable subset and, separately, among detectable baseline mHSPC samples. Sensitivity analyses examined alternative detectability thresholds for the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document}–RB1 association and baseline stage-level comparisons of cfDNA features between mHSPC and mCRPC samples.

Statistical analysis

All statistical tests were two-sided. Associations between continuous variables were primarily quantified using Spearman rank correlation. Group comparisons of continuous variables were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test where appropriate. In the spatial transcriptomic analyses, global Moran’s I was used to quantify spatial autocorrelation, and significance was assessed by permutation testing under fixed spatial adjacency. For deconvolution-based analyses, samples flagged as invalid according to prespecified quality criteria were treated as missing in downstream analyses. Survival analyses in the local and external validation cohorts were summarized using Kaplan–Meier curves and Cox proportional hazards models where applicable. All analyses were performed using Python 3.9 and R 4.5.2 with standard statistical and bioinformatics packages.

Discussion

Using TTCR in mHSPC as a clinically anchored translational task, we developed and evaluated a cross-cohort projection framework for relating clinically anchored latent risk to molecular readouts across external data layers. In this framework, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document} served as a clinically anchored latent risk coordinate that could be projected into external cohorts without refitting. This design provided a common reference for linking clinical progression risk to molecular programs and candidate blood-based readouts when local paired multi-omics data were unavailable. The main contribution of this study is therefore not simply another risk stratification model, but a practical strategy for multi-omics interpretation across independent datasets.

The value of this framework lies not in showing imaging can stratify risk, but in providing a route to translate clinically anchored latent risk into interpretable molecular context across external datasets. Prior PSMA PET/CT and radiomics studies in prostate cancer have primarily focused on diagnostic or prognostic performance, often with limited linkage to reproducible molecular states [22–24]. Conversely, single-cell and spatial transcriptomic studies have advanced the understanding of prostate cancer cell states and microenvironmental organization, but they usually begin from tissue molecular measurements rather than from a clinically defined risk coordinate [25, 26]. Our study bridges these directions by starting from a clinically anchored risk signal and projecting the resulting coordinate into public molecular resources for cross-cohort and cross-scale interpretation.

A further strength of this study lies in how the translational task was formulated. We did not require paired molecular profiling in the local cohort. Instead, the local imaging cohort was used to define an imaging-linked latent-growth surrogate, while baseline clinical variables were used to project this surrogate into the clinically anchored latent risk coordinate, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document}. The Tobit-like, censoring-aware construction allowed censored patients to contribute information, reducing reliance on fully observed progressors and limiting selection toward shorter follow-up. Because paired imaging and TTCR were unavailable in external molecular datasets, downstream analyses focused on molecular readouts associated with projected \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document}, rather than on direct validation of the original imaging signal. This design enabled cross-cohort molecular interpretation without refitting while preserving a clear separation between local clinical anchoring and external molecular interpretation.

The cross-scale analyses help clarify the biology most consistently associated with projected \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document}. In the bulk transcriptomic layer, higher projected \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document} was associated with lower AR-related signaling and higher proliferation-related signaling. In the primary NNLS deconvolution analysis, the more stable compositional association was with reduced AR-high epithelial representation, whereas the proliferating epithelial fraction showed no clear association with projected \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document}. We do not view this as contradictory. Rather, these findings suggest that AR-lineage reduction may be a more robust compositional correlate of projected \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document}, whereas proliferation may be captured more strongly at the level of transcriptional program activity than at the level of deconvolved cell-state abundance. In addition, proliferating epithelial states may be under-resolved by deconvolution because of reference aggregation, sparse marker structure, and QC-dependent identifiability; therefore, the null finding in the primary NNLS analysis may reflect resolution limitations rather than true biological absence. The spatial results further support this interpretation by showing that AR- and proliferation-related programs were not randomly distributed, but were organized within coherent tissue-level patterns.

The cfDNA analysis added a preliminary blood-based readout to this framework. Within the detectable subset, higher projected \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document} was associated with a more negative RB1 copy-number signal, and this direction was retained in detectable baseline mHSPC samples. We do not interpret this finding as evidence that RB1 alone defines the clinically anchored latent risk coordinate. Rather, its value lies in showing that projected \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document}, first anchored through local clinical and imaging data and then interpreted across tissue molecular layers, may also have a measurable blood-based correlate. Although RB1 was not identical to the transcriptomic axes examined in the tissue-based analyses, its direction of association was concordant with the broader interpretation that higher projected \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document} was associated with a more aggressive and less lineage-maintained disease state [6, 27]. This raises the possibility that clinically anchored latent risk states may become partly readable through blood-based molecular measurements in future longitudinal settings. At the same time, this result should be interpreted cautiously. Baseline cfDNA signal quality was highly heterogeneous, and the detectable subset remained modest in size. The detectability rule was specified before association testing and was intended to reduce low-signal false negatives rather than to optimize significance. Accordingly, the liquid-biopsy analysis is better viewed as preliminary translational support for blood-based observability than as definitive validation of a single blood biomarker.

Beyond the specific clinical application considered here, the proposed framework may be viewed as a useful template for linking clinically derived latent variables to external molecular data layers. The key design element is the use of a fixed projection function that maps baseline covariates to a shared latent coordinate, enabling reuse across datasets without requiring outcome redefinition or model refitting. This projection-based paradigm differs from conventional approaches that rely on retraining or harmonization, and instead emphasizes the construction of a transferable coordinate for cross-cohort analyses.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the clinical anchoring component was derived from a single-center retrospective cohort, and selection bias and unmeasured confounding cannot be fully excluded. Second, the external clinical validation cohort and the cfDNA cohort were both modest in size, particularly after stratification and detectable-subset restriction, so some estimates should be interpreted as directionally supportive rather than high-precision. Third, the public bulk, single-cell, and spatial transcriptomic datasets were used for cross-cohort interpretation rather than one-to-one validation against the local imaging samples. In particular, TCGA-PRAD consisted predominantly of primary tumors and therefore remained stage-mismatched to the local metastatic setting. Fourth, the deconvolution framework, spatial analyses, and cfDNA detectability definition depended on prespecified references, thresholds, and filtering rules, which may introduce reference bias or selective detectability. Finally, the overall findings are associative and interpretive in nature and do not by themselves establish a causal chain from clinically anchored latent risk to molecular state change and blood-based readout.

In this context, projected \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document} may represent one example of a broader class of clinically anchored latent variables that can be linked to external omics space. Similar projection-based frameworks could, in principle, be evaluated in other disease settings, imaging modalities, or clinically defined endpoints, provided a suitable local anchoring cohort is available. This design may therefore offer a practical route for future studies seeking to interpret clinically derived risk signals in settings where comprehensive multi-omics profiling is not feasible.

Future work should focus on prospective, multicenter evaluation of the framework, with greater standardization of image acquisition, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document} calculation, endpoint definition, and downstream molecular readouts. In the present study, TTCR in mHSPC provided one concrete example of this strategy. Validation in larger and more rigorously designed cohorts will be needed to determine whether projected \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document} can support reproducible cross-scale biological interpretation across broader clinical settings.

Conclusion

Using TTCR in mHSPC as a clinically anchored task, we developed and evaluated a cross-cohort projection framework for relating clinically anchored latent risk to molecular readouts across external data layers. Projected \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu$\end{document} enabled cross-cohort interpretation without refitting and showed directionally consistent signals across bulk transcriptomic, deconvolution, spatial transcriptomic, and cfDNA analyses. Together, these findings suggest that clinically anchored latent risk can be projected into external cohorts and interpreted in molecular context, even when local paired multi-omics data are unavailable.
