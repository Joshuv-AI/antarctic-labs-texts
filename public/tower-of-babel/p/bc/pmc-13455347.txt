Spatiotemporal dynamics of dengue: a town level study in Guangdong, China
Authors: Yingyin Deng, Chumin Liang, Huifang Lin, Zhe Liu, Ning Sun, Lina Tian, Qianqian Ruan, Ximing Hu, Huimin Chen, Xinxin Li, Yueling Chen, Diemei Liu, Xiaoxue Wei, Wenjun Ma, Qingfeng Du, Jiufeng Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDengue has been prevalent in Guangdong Province, China, for more than forty years and has shown an increasing trend over the past decade.MethodA fine-scale analysis of dengue clusters was conducted. The relationship between virus evolution time and spatial distance was analyzed, and a genetic algorithm was used to quantify the effects of environmental, social, and economic factors on the transmission of dengue.ResultsA retrospective study analyzed 172 confirmed dengue cases (April 2019 to April 2020) at the Seventh Affiliated Hospital of Southern Medical University. Whole-genome sequencing revealed a unique transmission origin in Lishui town, Guangdong Province (99.64% to 100% homology). Regardless of the duration of the outbreak (1, 3, or 6 months), the probability that cases within 5 km belonged to the same transmission chain decreased with distance, with a 60–70% probability observed within 200 m. Population density and income were more influential factors than vegetation and precipitation in terms of virus transmission.ConclusionThe geographical distance among cases and the population density are crucial for determining dengue transmission during fine-scale outbreaks, highlighting important implications for control and surveillance and informing precise prevention strategies for dengue and other vector-borne viruses in China and tropical regions globally as well.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Dengue is an acute infectious disease caused by dengue virus (DENV) transmitted through the bites of mosquitoes, primarily by Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus. Most dengue patients do not exhibit symptoms in the clinic, but among those who do, the most common symptoms are high fever, headache, body aches, nausea, and rash, and the majority of patients recover within 1–2 weeks. However, severe cases can be fatal [1]. DENV is a single-stranded, positive-sense RNA virus classified into four distinct serotypes (DENV-1-4) [2]. Infection with one serotype provides long-lasting immunity against that serotype and temporary immunity against others [3]. Dengvaxia (CYD-TDV), the initial tetravalent DENV vaccine developed by Sanofi Pasteur, was initially authorized by several endemic countries. Recent clinical trials have demonstrated the efficacy of CYD-TDV solely in individuals with pre-existing immunity to DENV. However, seronegative individuals face an elevated risk of severe disease following vaccination, rendering it unsuitable for use with naive populations. Other significant live-attenuated virus vaccine candidates, namely, DENVax-TDV by Takeda and TetraVax TV003/TV005 by the National Institutes of Health (NIH, USA), are being evaluated in ongoing phase III clinical trials. While these vaccines have shown promising results in phase II trials, long-term protection data are not yet available [4]. Notably, clinical treatment options for dengue remain limited. Dengue is listed as the most threatening mosquito-borne viral disease in tropical and subtropical areas, with an estimated 100 to 400 million infections annually, and approximately half of the global population is at risk of DENV infection [5].

Dengue outbreaks have been reported in a number of provinces in China, including Guangdong, Hainan, Guangxi, Fujian, Zhejiang, and Yunnan [6]. In particular, more than 80% of dengue cases in China in the past four decades have been reported in Guangdong [7]. Therefore, the prevention and control of dengue are at the top of the list for local public health authorities each year. During 2019–2022, the COVID-19 pandemic blocked most importations of dengue cases due to quarantine strategies for international travelers to China, while reopening policies resulted in the reintroduction of dengue. Ensuring early detection of imported cases and tracking the dynamics of DENV are essential for controlling local transmission of dengue. However, several gaps exist in determining the transmission chain and influencing factors during dengue transmission on a fine scale (town or community level), e.g., the prior factors during transmission and social, economic or environmental factors, which may guide the implementation of dengue prevention by local public authorities as early as possible.

Archived studies [8] have shown that dengue incidence follows a seasonal pattern and that weather factors—particularly the minimum temperature during the early months of the year and El Niño Southern Oscillation (ENSO) activity in the preceding year—are significant predictors of dengue incidence. This relationship has been observed in Bangkok and other parts of central Thailand, where climate variability was significantly associated with dengue transmission [9]. Similar patterns have been reported in Malaysia [10] and Sri Lanka [11]. Socioeconomic factors, including population density and mobility, ponds, farmland, grasslands, public green space, residential areas and water bodies and local human activities, are closely related to the transmission of dengue [12, 13]. Interestingly, a study in Kassala State, eastern Sudan [14], reported that older age individuals (> 60 years) who reside in houses with indoor bathrooms, use water-based air conditioning, and have family members previously infected with DENV were associated with dengue infection, which indicated that living style, population density, travel history and activities are closely linked to dengue transmission [15]. In China, suitable temperature conditions have been identified as factors that contribute to dengue outbreaks in the Pearl River Delta region of Guangdong, while population mobility might be responsible for the spatial spread of dengue between different cities [16]. Township-level studies [17, 18] conducted in Guangdong have highlighted the influence of socioeconomic and environmental factors on dengue outbreaks, providing valuable guidance for disease control and prevention efforts. Other studies [19] in Guangzhou have emphasized the importance of population density and hospital accessibility, rather than temperature, in dengue transmission. Similar studies by LaCon et al. [20] in Peru and Ong et al. [21] in Singapore also evaluated the factors associated with dengue transmission at the community or grid level and revealed that socioeconomic factors may be important in highly endemic areas of dengue.

Phylogenetic reconstruction and dynamic analysis of pathogen transmission are generally used for tracing the sources of an outbreak and revealing the quantity of dengue transmission chains and the relationships between chains at different spatiotemporal scales, which allows us to infer recent common ancestors and estimate the time of species divergence based on remote historical data, e.g., fossil data. Based on this theory, a number of groups have determined the dynamic transmission of West Nile virus [22], Ebola virus [23], Zika virus [24], and Chikungunya virus [25], as well as the recent SARS-CoV-2 variants [26], and determined the spatial factors involved in virus transmission based on mathematical models. However, almost all those studies were based on epidemiology data, and comprehensive joint studies on both epidemiology and virus evolution data at the individual level are rare.

Lishui, Xinlian, and Ganjiao, the main dengue-endemic sites in Foshan, are located in the Pearl River Delta region, which has a subtropical monsoon climate with warm temperatures (20–24 °C), high humidity (73%–85%), and abundant rainfall (1650–2550 mm annually). These conditions, along with extensive paddy fields, stagnant water, and dense vegetation, support mosquito breeding. The region features low-lying plains surrounded by hills, with many village-in-city areas characterized by overcrowded housing and poor sanitation. Rapid urbanization and high population mobility, driven by migrant workers and industrial expansion, further increase the risk of dengue transmission. Together, these environmental and socioeconomic factors make the region an ideal setting for studying the fine-scale dynamics of dengue transmission. In this study, we compiled a comprehensive dataset encompassing epidemiology, the viral genome, and environmental, demographic, economic, and clinical indicators in the community of the Nanhai District of Foshan city, an area in Guangdong with a high incidence of dengue. Furthermore, we utilized a genetic algorithm to quantitatively analyze the relationship between DENV evolution and the refined spatiotemporal interplay of environmental, social, and economic factors and provide a novel way to trace the dynamics of dengue in real time, which will aid in the precise surveillance and control of dengue in Guangdong, China, as well as other cities that suffer from endemic dengue.

Methods

Ethics statement

The serum sampling protocol for dengue cases was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangdong Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention (Guangdong CDC). The sampling procedures were performed at The Seventh Affiliated Hospital of Southern Medical University in accordance with the humanization regulations. The epidemiological, demographic and clinical data of dengue patients were obtained from the Notifiable Infectious Disease Report System (NIDRS) of China.

Study location and population

This study was conducted at The Seventh Affiliated Hospital of Southern Medical University in Lishui Town, Nanhai District, Foshan City, Guangdong Province, China. All suspected dengue patients were delivered to The Seventh Affiliated Hospital of Southern Medical University regardless of the clinic where they were admitted. All suspected dengue cases among Lishui residents were collected in this study from April 2019 to 2020. The town covers an area of 148.28 square kilometers and has a population of 436,000.

Case definition

Dengue cases were defined according to the Chinese national criteria for dengue diagnosis (WS216-2008) and the 2009 World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines, which contain suspected and confirmed cases [27, 28].

Sample collection and virus isolation

Results of laboratory tests, including blood tests, ABO red blood cell typing, biochemistry, five immune tests/six trace element tests, and C-reactive protein (CRP) tests (various immunological formulas/four myocardial enzyme tests/eight liver function tests), were collected after patient admission. An immunochromatography DENV NS1 antigen detection kit and an immunochromatography dengue virus IgG antibody detection kit (both purchased from Guangzhou Wanfu Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) were used to identify the serotypes of suspected dengue cases. PCR validation was performed to screen positive nucleic acid samples. DENV-positive samples were further used for viral isolation by inoculation into C6/36 cells. When the cytopathic effect was clearly observed, the cells were centrifuged to remove any debris, and the supernatants were collected and stored at ˗80 °C until they were used for genome sequencing.

RNA extraction and Sanger sequencing

Viral RNA was extracted from 200 µL of cell supernatant using a TaKaRa MiniBEST Viral RNA/DNA Extraction Kit Ver.5.0 according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

The library was constructed based on NEBNext® Ultra® Ultratablished following the NEB Illumina® (New England Biolabs, USA) standard process. PE150 sequencing was performed on the constructed library using the Illumina Nova 6000 platform.

Raw sequencing reads were filtered through a quality control pipeline using SOAPnuke (v2.0.5). All low-quality reads with a score > 20, an N proportion < 3%, and a proportion > 40% were filtered out. Adaptors were removed simultaneously through internal parameters. Any repeated reads were determined and removed through readfq (v5.2). All the clean reads from each sample were aligned with the reference genome (NC_001474.2 Dengue virus) by BWA (v). Sequence assemblies were completed using Megahit v1.2.9.

Evolution analysis of DENV

The sequence homology of DENV was compared via BioEdit v7.0.9.0. Mega v6.06 software was used to construct a machine learning (ML) evolutionary tree (GTR + G nucleic acid replacement model), and TempEst software was used to evaluate the genetic distance and time distribution. Bayesian phylogenies were estimated in BEAST v.1.10.4 using relaxed uncorrelated molecular clock (UCLN) models under the GTR nucleotide substitution model (Drummond et al., 2006, Twiddy et al., 2003). The generated set of evolutionary trees was merged using TreeAnnotator v1.8.1 to obtain the final genetic evolutionary tree. The summary phylogenies were visualized in FigTree v.1.4.2 (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software) [29].

A process of discrete state transitioning in evolutionary history is generally modeled using a continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC) model. This is the case for both sequence evolution and discrete trait evolution, e.g., for location traits in phylogeographic inference. Fitting a CTMC model to discrete data results yields model parameter estimates, but the realization of the process remains unobserved. This realization includes all the transitions (Markov jumps) between states along phylogenetic branches and the time (Markov rewards) spent in the states between two transitions. Therefore, it is possible to determine the discrete state transitioning in virus transmission, and Markov jumps and rewards can be utilized to estimate such quantities using stochastic mapping techniques implemented in BEAST (http://beast.community/markov_jumps_rewards).

Transmission network reconstruction

The dengue transmission network was reconstructed by using Population Analysis with Reticulate Trees (POPART) (www.popart.otago.ac.nz) as previously described [30]. The DENV genomes were aligned to the consensus sequence and trimmed to the same length. To be as conservative as possible in mutation calling, gaps and nonidentifiable positions were assumed to have major alleles.

Spatiotemporal transmission of dengue

Microscale dengue transmission dynamics and the resulting competitive interactions between strains drive dengue transmission. Phylogeographic studies mimic the dispersal of dengue at both regional and global scales but provide few insights into the local spatiotemporal structure of endemic transmission, e.g., transmission chains of dengue at the microscale. Referring to the concept from Henrik Salje et al. [31, 32], we estimated the number of circulating transmission chains (i.e., cases separated by a low number of intervening transmission events) at different spatial scales using dengue genome sequence data and evaluated the interactions among endemic time, spatial scales and transmission chains. First, the evolutionary time between each pair of viruses was extracted from Bayesian phylogenetic trees. The uncertainty of the evolution time for each dengue case pair referred to a combination of bootstrapping observations and sampling trees with the posterior.

Factors of dengue transmission dynamics

To explore whether an uncertain and imprecise dependency relationship exists between the factors that influence the microscale evolution of dengue virus, we refer to the concept of biology by using the Reconstruct Ancestral State in Phylogenies (RASP) tool for inferring ancestral states [33]. We aimed to test the effects of a particular state on virus speciation in a phylogenetic model, which is indicated by environmental, social, economic and clinical data on virus transmission time extracted from Bayesian phylogenetic trees.

In addition, the Pearson correlation coefficient was used to validate the linear relationship between the evolution time of each dengue case extracted from the Bayesian phylogenetic trees and the variable factors in environmental, social, economic and clinical laboratory data. The linear relationships between variables were examined using Pearson correlation coefficients. The variables included virus evolution time, difference in domestic gross domestic product (Dgdp), difference in population density (Dpop), difference in farmland area within 500 m and 1000 m of cases (Dframland), difference in forest area within 500 m and 1000 m of cases (Dforest), difference in water area within 500 m and 1000 m of cases (Dwater), and difference in residential area within 500 m and 1000 m of cases (Dliving). Statistical analysis was performed using R software version 4.3.2.

Results

Epidemiological findings

From April 2019 to April 2021, a total of 172 fever cases were retrospectively collected and identified as dengue infection at The Seventh Affiliated Hospital of Southern Medical University. Among the patients, 90 were males (52.33%) and 82 were females (47.67%), with a male-to-female ratio of 1:0.91. The age distribution of the patients ranged from 2 to 82 years, with a median age of 43 years. The cases were distributed within a 148.28 square kilometer area centered on The Seventh Affiliated Hospital of Southern Medical University. Approximately 60% of the cases were located within a 3-kilometer radius of the hospital, while approximately 80% were within a 5-kilometer radius (Fig. 1).

DENV isolation and identification

Blood samples were collected from all 172 patients, and laboratory confirmation was obtained through rapid testing with dengue antigen colloidal gold and fluorescence quantitative PCR. All cases were confirmed as laboratory-confirmed cases of DENV serotype 1 (DENV1). Virus isolation was performed by inoculating the samples into C6/36 mosquito cells, resulting in the generation of 143 DENV strains. Nucleic acid extraction and second-generation sequencing were performed on the isolated strains, and a total of 143 DENV sequences were obtained after assembly. Homology analysis revealed that they belonged to DENV1, whose homology ranged from 99.64% to 100%, with the closest homolog being strain No. 09041 (MG767211), which was isolated from Baiyun District, Guangzhou, in 2017.

Analysis of the molecular evolution of dengue virus

Molecular evolution analysis revealed that 118 of the 143 isolated DENV strains originated from Lishui town (NH2018032) (Fig. 2) and subsequently spread to neighboring towns such as Donggang, where they formed three potential evolutionary branches (Fig. 3). The network relationship diagram constructed based on the virus evolution distance also supported this inference, suggesting that a virus was introduced into the region and led to local transmission, resulting in distinct clusters or chains of evolution (Fig. 4).

We applied a counting method for virus transmission between different geographical areas during molecular evolution. A comparison of the number of transmissions occurring between different geographical regions revealed that the occurrence of importation, exportation, and internal transmission in Lishui, Xinlian, and Ganjiao in Nanhai was significantly greater than that in other areas. These findings suggest that these three areas may serve as the initial sources of local outbreaks (Fig. 5), which is consistent with the results of molecular evolution and network analysis and indicates the presence of three initial outbreak sites or transmission clusters.

Spatial and temporal distribution of DENV

Based on the Bayesian analysis of virus evolution, we combined the time and spatial distance of virus evolution with the duration of the virus epidemic season to explore the impact of local epidemic seasons lasting 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year on the spatial and temporal distribution of the virus. The relationship between the spatial distance of every two dengue-positive patients and the evolution time of their dengue virus is shown in Fig. 6A. The trends for 1 year and 6 months are similar, with the median spatial distance gradually increasing as the evolution time increases, but the linear relationship with time evolution is not significant.

We also estimated the probability of cases belonging to the same transmission chain. The evolution time between viruses and whether they belong to the same transmission chain were influenced by the duration of virus circulation in the local area. We examined the probability that cases from the same geographical area belonged to the same transmission chain and reported that, regardless of the duration of virus circulation (1, 3, or 6 months), the probability of cases residing within a 5-km radius belonging to the same transmission chain decreased as the distance increased. The probability of cases within a range of 200 m belonging to the same transmission chain was less affected by the duration of disease circulation, with a range between 60% and 70%. Within a range of 1 km, the relationship with the duration of the epidemic season was 10%, 13%, and 63%, whereas within a range of 5 km, it rapidly decreased to 2%, 5%, and 45%, respectively (Fig. 6B).

The number of estimated virus transmission chains in relation to distance for different epidemic season durations of 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months is shown in Fig. 6C. The number of transmission chains increases with increasing spatial distance, with the 6-month duration group being the closest to the actual situation based on virus sequencing data, with approximately 3 transmission chains within a 5-kilometer range.

Factors influencing virus evolution

Sixty-three demographic, economic, environmental, and clinical testing indicators from 118 dengue cases were collected. By employing an estimation method based on the evolutionary branching of the virus, we aimed to identify significant factors influencing the transmission of the virus. Our findings revealed that population density and income level per capita significantly affected virus transmission, whereas the effects of vegetation and precipitation were not significant (Table 1). Furthermore, several clinical laboratory test indicators were found to have a significant influence. Parameters such as the mean platelet volume, platelet distribution width, and uric acid concentration were identified as significant factors affecting virus transmission. On the other hand, the platelet count; red blood cell count; white blood cell count; and the levels of sodium (Na), chloride (Cl), and calcium (Ca) were not significantly different. These results suggest that clinical laboratory test indicators may be correlated with disease progression or disease severity. Therefore, our study highlights that the evolutionary process of DENV is more influenced by social factors than environmental factors at a fine spatial scale. These findings contribute to a better understanding of the factors driving the transmission of dengue and provide valuable insights for public health interventions and strategies.

Discussion

Unlike previous studies on the regional transmission of dengue, such as the one conducted in 2014 [27], this research identifies the third historical outbreak of dengue in Guangdong Province as having multiple geographical sources. We emphasized the importance of early detection, case management, and vector surveillance in preventing future dengue fever epidemics in Guangdong Province [28]. In 2019, we employed real-time genomic epidemiology methods to track short-term, medium-scale dengue epidemics [34] and found that early detection of imported cases and monitoring of DENV dynamics were key strategies for preventing local transmission of dengue. In this study, upon retrospectively collecting a total of 172 confirmed cases at The Seventh Affiliated Hospital of Southern Medical University, we refined the spatiotemporal transmission of dengue, which was advantageous for precise lineage tracing of dengue. All outbreak isolates were confirmed as DENV1 serotypes and shared the same origin in Lishui Town, Nanhai, Guangdong Province.

The incidence of dengue is affected by multiple factors, including climatic conditions, human mobility, land use, and socioeconomic status [35]. A study from the United States [36] suggested that strict mosquito control policies and activities should be implemented and maintained in areas that could serve as entry ports for these viruses and become focal points of dengue fever outbreaks in the United States. This may include traditional and biological vector control strategies. Another spatial-temporal analysis conducted in Queensland, Australia [37], revealed that social population and ecological factors provide natural habitats for mosquitoes, whereas increased travel and transportation increase the chances of dengue virus introduction. In terms of control measures, it is necessary to implement mosquito control measures in areas with the highest proportion of returning travelers. Beyond those studies, we focused on conducting a fine-scale spatiotemporal analysis of dengue transmission by utilizing genetic information and a transmission chain model. Our findings revealed spatial and temporal clustering of local dengue transmission, with residential locations of patients playing a significant role in the transmission process. We also investigated the role of different social and environmental factors in the process of viral diversification by evaluating demographic, economic, environmental, and clinical testing indicators and revealed that population density and per capita income level significantly influenced the spread of the virus, while the impact of vegetation and precipitation was not significant. The continuous transmission of dengue typically occurs within households and communities. Our findings align with those of previous research [38] that highlight the influence of socioeconomic factors in cities with similar climatic conditions. Similarly, a case-control study conducted in Guangzhou, China [39], also revealed significant associations between dengue incidence and social factors such as individual behavior, home and work environments, and mosquito prevention measures. However, these findings contradict those of a study conducted in Colombia [40], which revealed that meteorological and environmental variables were more important for predicting dengue within a smaller geographical scope, whereas social population variables were more crucial for larger-scale predictions. This discrepancy may be attributed to the lag effects of weather and environmental conditions on Aedes mosquitoes, with temperature and precipitation typically exhibiting lags of 1 to 4 weeks. The spread of dengue appears to be determined by social relationships [13], as the daily activities of infected individuals and their contacts, such as family members and friends’ homes, are usually similar. Therefore, routine and door-to-door human activities indeed play a crucial role in the transmission of the pathogen in this vector-borne disease at a fine spatial scale.

In addition, we determined the interaction between several clinical laboratory test indicators and the divergence of DENV and revealed a significant effect on disease progression or disease severity. Notably, mean platelet volume (MPV) and platelet distribution width (PDW) were identified as significant factors affecting virus transmission. Previous research [41] has indicated that MPV can be used to assess bone marrow function and to determine the cause of peripheral thrombocytopenia. Additionally, plateletcrit (PCT), which represents the proportion of platelet volume in a unit volume of blood, is associated with both MPV and PDW. PDW helps evaluate the uniformity of platelet volume distribution within a patient, and a sustained increase in PDW indicates heterogeneity in platelet size, which typically increases with increasing MPV. While some studies [42] suggest that MPV is not significantly correlated with disease severity, serological markers, or treatment outcomes, other studies [43, 44] align with our findings and demonstrate significant variations in MPV and PDW among patients. The findings of Dewi et al.‘s study also support the notion that increasing MPV [45] and PDW [46] are considered risk factors for worsening dengue. The assessment of platelet parameters is important in prognostic evaluation. Moreover, uric acid, the most abundant antioxidant in the blood, plays a crucial role. A study [47] revealed that severe dengue patients have uric acid levels 1.5 times lower than moderate patients do, potentially resulting in decreased endogenous antioxidant defense capacity and increased oxidative stress in severe dengue patients. Surprisingly, white blood cell count was recognized as a negative factor for virus divergence, which is normally used as a sign of dengue in the clinic. Nevertheless, Ralapanawa et al. [48] also supports the notion that white blood cell count is unlikely to serve as a predictive marker for dengue. We also found that the levels of sodium (Na), chloride (Cl), and calcium (Ca) have significant effects. These findings are consistent with those of the study conducted by Bano et al. [49], in which they reported a general decrease in serum levels of sodium, potassium, and calcium. Therefore, clinical laboratory test indicators may be correlated with disease progression or case severity and are valuable prognostic indicators for severe illness, facilitating urgent care and interventions to prevent life-threatening complications or death. Some of them may be linked to virus divergence through indirect effects, e.g., MPV, PDW and uric acid.

In this study, we established a fine-scale spatiotemporal research model based on routine large-scale monitoring, enabling precise tracing of viral lineages and systematic evolutionary analysis. By integrating the temporal and spatial distances of viral evolution with the duration of the epidemic season, we investigated the impact of local epidemic seasons lasting 1 year, 6 months, and 3 months on the spatiotemporal distribution of the virus. Furthermore, we compared the probabilities of different cases belonging to the same transmission chain when the virus circulated for 6 months, 3 months, and 1 month in the same area. Our findings revealed spatial and temporal clustering characteristics of local dengue transmission at the county and street levels in Guangdong Province and have significant implications for dengue control measures and active surveillance. Vector control, community engagement, and individual protection in communities with high population density should be enhanced during the endemic season. Additionally, population mobility should be minimized as much as possible to contain the spread of the dengue epidemic over long distances.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that dengue transmission within refined temporal and spatial areas of Guangdong Province is spatially and temporally clustered at the town level. These findings revealed that social factors, rather than natural factors, are the key determinants influencing the spread of DENV, highlighting the crucial role of vector control and population mobility management measures in these cases. Additionally, we have provided novel genetic evidence of dengue transmission at the community level, which may be advantageous for tracing dengue transmission chains in future practical work. In conclusion, we have proven the ability to quantitatively characterize DENV evolution and the nuanced spatiotemporal interplay of environmental, social, and economic factors through hybridization analysis with a genetic algorithm. This will guide further investigations into dengue transmission, incorporating empirical epidemiology and pathogen transmission chain analysis in minimal geographic areas. It will also serve as a powerful supplement for tracing the transmission of highly virulent pathogens (e.g., Ebola and avian influenza) across countries or continents.
