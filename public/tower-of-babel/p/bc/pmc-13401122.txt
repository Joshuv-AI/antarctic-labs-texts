Compartment-Specific Associations of GDF15 With Intraocular Cytokines in Patients With Idiopathic Epiretinal Membrane
Authors: Hiroki Mieno, Masatsugu Hashida, Kengo Yoshii, Munetoyo Toda, Tomoko Horikiri, Kimiaki Urabe, Kazuhiko Mori, Morio Ueno, Chie Sotozono
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe purpose of this study was to characterize the intraocular cytokine context of growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15), a stress-response cytokine implicated in tissue injury and immune regulation, in eyes with idiopathic epiretinal membrane (iERM) by evaluating paired aqueous humor (AH) and vitreous humor (VH) samples and their compartment-specific associations with other soluble cytokines.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 38 eyes of 38 patients with iERM and no history of intraocular surgery. Undiluted paired AH and VH samples were collected at the beginning of pars plana vitrectomy (PPV). Twenty-seven cytokines were quantified using a multiplex bead-based immunoassay, and GDF15 concentrations were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Paired AH–VH comparisons and compartmental correlations were analyzed.ResultsGDF15 was significantly higher in VH than in AH. Ten other cytokines also differed significantly between compartments: eotaxin, IFN-γ, IL-8, IP-10, MCP-1, and RANTES were higher in VH, whereas IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17A, and TNF-α were higher in AH (all P < 0.01). Significant AH–VH correlations were observed for eotaxin, IL-6, IP-10, MCP-1, MIP-1β, RANTES, and GDF15 (all P < 0.01). When VH GDF15 was used as the reference, significant positive correlations were identified with IL-6, IL-8, IP-10, MCP-1, and MIP-1α (all P < 0.01). No significant negative correlations with GDF15 were observed.ConclusionsIn eyes with iERM, intraocular GDF15 showed compartment-weighted associations that were concentrated predominantly on vitreous chemokines, with limited coupling to classical upstream inflammatory cytokines. These findings provide a compartment-specific view of the cytokine context in which intraocular GDF15 is observed.

Growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15, also known as macrophage inhibitory cytokine-1) is a member of the transforming growth factor-β superfamily, and it is induced by inflammation, tissue injury, metabolic stress, and cell damage.1–3 In systemic diseases, the circulating levels of GDF15 are reportedly associated with inflammatory and immune mediators in a context-dependent manner.4–8 Beyond serving as a marker of cellular stress, GDF15 has been implicated in immunomodulatory and cytoprotective responses.

In the eyes, there is increasing published evidence that GDF15 is involved in ocular stress and disease, and studies have reported elevated levels of GDF15 in the intraocular fluids of patients with glaucoma and retinal disorders.9–12 Moreover, the findings in a recent experimental study suggest that retinal neurons and glial cells can express GDF15 in response to stress or injury.13 These findings support the concept that GDF15 is involved in ocular stress-response pathways. However, most previous ophthalmic studies have focused on GDF15 itself, without systematically examining its relationship to the broader intraocular cytokine milieu.

The eye is an immune-privileged organ composed of distinct compartments, most notably the aqueous humor (AH) and vitreous humor (VH), which differ in their cellular sources and molecular environments. Although both compartments are commonly used to assess intraocular inflammatory activity, it remains unclear whether GDF15 has similar or compartment-specific associations with other cytokines. To date, and to the best of our knowledge, there have been no studies specifically focused on systematically comparing the cytokine context of GDF15 between AH and VH in human eyes.

Idiopathic epiretinal membrane (iERM), a disease characterized primarily by vitreoretinal interface remodeling and fibrocellular proliferation, is accompanied by low-grade inflammatory changes rather than overt cytokine-driven activation.14,15 Given the high prevalence of iERM cases, there is now a clinically relevant setting in which intraocular cytokine relationships can be examined without major confounding from acute inflammatory conditions, and a deep examination of the cytokine context of GDF15 may help clarify the intraocular immune milieu in which GDF15 operates.

The aim of this present study was to characterize the intraocular cytokine context of GDF15 in eyes with iERM via examining the relationships between GDF15 levels in AH and VH and a broad panel of intraocular cytokines, including any compartment-specific patterns of association.

Methods

Participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted at Kyoto Prefectural University of Medicine, Kyoto, Japan, and Machida Hospital, Kochi, Japan, and involved 38 phakic eyes of 38 patients undergoing pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) for iERM. All patients had no history of intraocular surgery, and patients with other ocular diseases (i.e. glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy, and uveitis) were excluded from the study. No patients were receiving topical medications for these ocular diseases before surgery.

The protocols of this study were approved by the institutional review boards of Kyoto Prefectural University of Medicine (Approval No. ERB-C-727) and Machida Hospital, and it was conducted in accordance with the tenets set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to their involvement in the study.

Sample Collection and Handling

In all patients, AH and undiluted VH samples were collected from the same eye during 25-gauge PPV. Approximately 0.1 to 0.2 mL of AH was obtained from the anterior chamber at the beginning of surgery using a 27-gauge bent needle after creating a side port. After AH collection, a small amount of ophthalmic viscosurgical device was injected to reform the anterior chamber. Undiluted VH samples were collected using the 25-gauge vitrectomy cutter prior to intraocular infusion to avoid dilution, and the volume of VH collected was approximately 0.3 to 0.5 mL. The collection of all samples was performed by a single experienced vitreoretinal surgeon (author MH), thus ensuring consistency in the procedure. Immediately after the samples were collected at Machida Hospital, they were stored at −60°C and then transported in a dry-ice container to Kyoto Prefectural University of Medicine, where they were then kept frozen until analysis. This strict cold-chain protocol ensured that there was no thawing of the samples during transport, thus minimizing the risk of cytokine degradation.

Multiplex Cytokine Analysis and Measurement of GDF15

For cytokine analysis, each AH and VH sample was first thawed on ice, and then divided into two aliquots. One aliquot was used for multiplex cytokine profiling, whereas the other was used for GDF15 measurement. In both assays, 30 µL of each AH or VH sample was diluted 4-fold prior to analysis to ensure optimal assay performance and maintain measurement sensitivity.

A total of 27 cytokines were quantified using the Bio-Plex Pro Human Cytokine Group I 27-plex Panel (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA), including eotaxin, FGF-basic, G-CSF, GM-CSF, IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-1ra, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-7, IL-8, IL-9, IL-10, IL-12p70, IL-13, IL-15, IL-17A, IP-10, MCP-1, MIP-1α, MIP-1β, PDGF-BB, RANTES, TNF-α, and VEGF. GDF15 concentrations were determined using a Human GDF-15 Quantikine ELISA Kit (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. All measurements were performed in duplicate, and mean values were used for statistical analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Absolute concentrations of 27 cytokines and GDF15 were evaluated in both AH and VH samples. Paired comparisons between AH and VH were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and correlations between variables were primarily assessed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient (ρ).

Cytokines were retained for statistical analyses when detectable concentrations were observed in ≥80% of samples in either AH or VH. Cytokines that did not meet this criterion were excluded due to insufficient variability in both compartments. In total, 13 of the 27 cytokines were not subjected to statistical testing based on this criterion. Undetectable cytokine levels were assigned a value of zero for statistical processing.

In the primary Spearman correlation analyses, associations were considered significant when both |ρ| ≥ 0.4 and P < 0.05 were met. Percentage bend correlation was additionally performed as a robust correlation method to evaluate the influence of extreme observations. Complete results for concentration comparisons, Spearman correlation analyses, and percentage bend correlation analyses are shown in the Supplementary Tables.

All analyses were conducted in an exploratory manner. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified. Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) statistics software.

Discussion

This study on eyes with iERM revealed a compartment-weighted organization of intraocular cytokines, with GDF15 and a chemokine-enriched profile preferentially represented in VH. Concordance between aqueous and vitreous cytokine patterns was limited to a small subset of analytes, suggesting that trans-compartmental alignment is selective rather than widespread. Importantly, GDF15 associations were concentrated on vitreous chemokines, providing a focused context for interpreting GDF15 within the intraocular immune milieu of eyes with iERM.

From a broader perspective, systemic studies provide a useful reference for interpreting these intraocular findings; for example, circulating GDF15 is widely regarded as a stress-responsive cytokine that can be induced by inflammatory and metabolic stimuli, including IL-1β and TNF-α.2,3 However, clinical studies have indicated that circulating GDF15 does not consistently parallel classical inflammatory cytokines across disease contexts.16–18 Rather, GDF15 has been implicated in immunomodulatory and cytoprotective responses, regulating immune cell effector functions during inflammation and contributing to tissue protection in pathologic conditions.6,19 In specific clinical settings, such as cancer-associated cachexia, concurrent elevations of GDF15 and selected circulating mediators including chemokines have been reported.20 Overall, whereas systemic data suggest that GDF15 may rise alongside certain inflammatory signals in defined conditions, the number of systematic investigations focusing specifically on cytokine co-variation with GDF15 remains limited.

Previous ophthalmic investigations have largely established intraocular GDF15 as an intraocular stress–associated marker, with elevated levels reported in several retinal and glaucomatous disorders.9–12 Experimental models further support that ocular tissues can upregulate GDF15 following injury or under cellular stress, consistent with a local stress-response program.9,13 In previous studies, AH GDF15 levels in primary open-angle glaucoma were reported to be 7.4-fold higher than those in non-glaucomatous cataract controls,12 whereas vitreous GDF15 levels were 254 ± 91 pg/mL in eyes with iERM.11 In the present iERM cohort, median GDF15 concentrations were 99.48 pg/mL in AH and 452.10 pg/mL in VH. However, compared with this accumulating single-analyte evidence, information remains limited regarding how intraocular GDF15 relates to other soluble immune mediators within ocular fluids, particularly when aqueous and vitreous compartments are evaluated in parallel.

In this context, the present findings provide new insight into the immunological profile of intraocular GDF15. The correlation pattern suggests that intraocular GDF15 is associated with a selective immune milieu rather than with generalized inflammation. The predominant associations with chemokines involved in monocyte/macrophage recruitment21,22 are consistent with a chemokine-weighted profile, whereas associations with classical upstream cytokines such as TNF-α or IL-1β were limited. This profile may be consistent with previous reports linking GDF15 to posterior segment stress-related responses involving retinal and glial responses.23,24 By demonstrating these relationships through paired aqueous–vitreous analysis in iERM eyes, the findings in this study complement previous single-marker approaches and suggest that intraocular GDF15 is associated with a restricted, chemokine-weighted cytokine milieu.

The marked VH-to-AH gradient of GDF15 may reflect a contribution from the posterior segment, although its basis is likely multifactorial. In our previous paired aqueous–vitreous study, GDF15 was consistently more abundant in VH than in AH and showed AH–VH correlations across retinal disease groups.25 Together with the present findings, these observations suggest some degree of compartmental concordance rather than complete separation. Possible explanations include local posterior production, vitreous retention, and differential clearance or exchange between compartments. In addition, circulating GDF15 has been associated with age in community-based studies of older adults,26,27 and VH GDF15 showed a weak positive association with age in the present cohort, whereas AH GDF15 did not. Thus, VH-predominant GDF15 with AH–VH concordance may represent a compartmental signal related to posterior segment-associated processes, whereas its biological significance and determinants warrant further investigation.

The selection of eyes with iERM as the study population offered several methodological advantages. Because iERM is a prevalent vitreoretinal disorder, it facilitated the recruitment of a sufficient number of surgery-naïve eyes, thus enabling assessment of cytokine relationships without confounding by surgery-related tissue alteration. At the same time, the biological status of iERM eyes has been interpreted differently in the published literature. For example, a proteomic and multiplex analysis reported that a large proportion of cytokines were elevated in iERM compared with macular hole samples, suggesting the presence of active inflammatory and profibrotic mechanisms and advocating caution when considering iERM as a surrogate for healthy tissue.28 In contrast, a previous study that used a comparable multiplex platform with stringent correction for multiple testing found only minimal differences between iERM and macular hole eyes, suggesting that the two conditions may represent broadly similar ocular backgrounds.29 These contrasting views indicate that iERM likely reflects a state of low-grade, disease-related immune activity rather than normal physiology, and the present findings should therefore be understood as defining the cytokine context of GDF15 within this clinically relevant ocular environment.

It should be noted that this study had several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precluded causal inference, and the observed associations between GDF15 and chemokines should be interpreted as co-variation rather than evidence of directional regulation. Second, the moderate sample size may have limited the statistical power and precision of the correlation analyses, particularly for detecting weaker associations. Third, because the study was conducted in eyes with iERM, the generalizability of the observed cytokine relationships to unaffected eyes or other ocular diseases remains to be established. Fourth, retinal structural parameters and systemic comorbidities relevant to GDF15 were not included in the present analysis. These factors may influence the intraocular cytokine profile and should be considered in future studies.

Conclusions

The findings in this study provide a systematic characterization of cytokines associated with intraocular GDF15 using paired aqueous and vitreous samples from eyes with iERM. GDF15 was associated predominantly with vitreous chemokines, whereas its associations with classical upstream inflammatory cytokines were limited. These findings characterize the cytokine context in which intraocular GDF15 is observed within this clinical setting.
