Structure-Activity Relationship Studies Enable Optimization of a Small-Molecule Autophagy Activator and Evaluation of Effects on Cell Death Pathways
Authors: Andrew Dobria, Andrea Arrieche Suarez, Thomas Whitmarsh-Everiss, Ivan Pavlinov, Victoria Soliz, Ryan S. Hippman, Tino Petersson, Zoe A. Petros, Leslie N. Aldrich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Autophagy is a catabolic cellular process that degrades and recycles cellular components. Dysregulation of autophagy can contribute to the development and progression of many different diseases, including cancer and neurodegenerative diseases. Our lab previously completed a high-throughput screen for autophagy activators, and initial structure–activity relationship studies revealed modifications that significantly increased the potency of the original hit. However, this compound has very poor microsomal stability, limiting its utility as an in vivo probe. To overcome this limitation, we developed synthetic strategies to facilitate iterative synthesis of additional analogues with improved potency, stability, and properties. Mechanistic studies revealed that high levels of sustained autophagy activation can lead to autophagy-dependent cell death and enabled differentiation of analogues with off-target effects and analogues with improved selectivity. The most promising analogues have been selected for additional evaluation in disease-relevant models to improve our understanding of the role of autophagy in diverse disease states.

Macroautophagy, hereafter referred to as autophagy, is a catabolic cellular pathway that plays an important role in the degradation and recycling of cellular components. , This process involves the formation of a double-membraned autophagosome, which engulfs a variety of intracellular cargo, and then fuses with a lysosome to form an autolysosome where the cargo is degraded through the action of hydrolases and the acidification of the internal lumen. − The degradation products are then exported back into the cytosol where they are used to fuel ongoing cellular processes. , Because of its role in homeostasis and clearance of toxic cellular components, including protein aggregates and damaged organelles, autophagy is especially important for cellular survival in long-lived, postmitotic cells, like oocytes and neurons. As cells age, there is an associated decline and dysfunction of autophagy, leading to cellular stress and disease. , The impairment in autophagic function can have significant health consequences as evidenced by the links between autophagy and disease, including cancer and neurodegenerative diseases.

The role of autophagy in cancer progression is complex. In established tumors, autophagy has been shown to aid in progression by suppressing antitumor immune activity, lowering the levels of p53, decreasing metabolic stress, and conferring resistance to chemotherapeutic agents. − For this reason, there has been considerable interest in the inhibition of autophagy as a potential treatment strategy. Chloroquine and hydroxychloroquine, which inhibit lysosomal function, have been evaluated for their effectiveness as anticancer agents, but have shown mixed results that may partially be due to nonspecific activity. − Paradoxically, autophagy also serves to protect against tumorigenesis. Prior to tumor development, autophagy has been shown to inhibit cancer progression through the reduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) via the selective degradation of mitochondria, the preservation of genomic integrity via degradation of nuclear components, and the degradation of potentially oncogenic proteins. − This dual role in cancer indicates that the reduction of basal autophagy in healthy cells may contribute to tumor development and the maintenance of healthy autophagy levels may be a viable protective strategy against cancer in high-risk individuals.

Defects in autophagy have also been shown to play a role in the progression of multiple neurodegenerative diseases. Dysfunction in the normal retrograde transport of autophagic organelles from the neuronal axon to the soma has been correlated to the formation of extracellular amyloid plaques, a hallmark of Alzheimer’s disease. , Additionally, deficient or incomplete lysosome acidification and cargo degradation have been shown to decrease the clearance of toxic aggregates and promote Alzheimer’s disease progression. , In Parkinson’s disease, cells with α-synuclein inclusions show decreased autophagic clearance, which may result from defects in autophagosome maturation and improper localization of ATG9A. , Defects in cargo recognition and mitochondrial degradation have also been identified as contributing to disease pathogenesis in Huntington’s disease. − Considerable efforts have been made to develop therapeutics targeting acetylcholinesterase, β- and γ-secretase, and mutant huntingtin protein, but clinical efficacy is limited. − Modulation of the autophagic-lysosomal pathway (ALP) could provide an alternative strategy to reduce toxic protein levels and improve memory and cognition as demonstrated in a number of disease-relevant models. −

Although autophagy modulation has the potential to prove beneficial for the treatment of a variety of diseases, there are major challenges that have limited clinical applications. Selective autophagy modulators with well-defined mechanisms and reduced off-target effects must be developed to accurately assess the role of this pathway in diseases. In the case of autophagy activation, there is also a need to better understand the crosstalk between autophagy and cell death. While autophagy induction may have beneficial effects, overinduction can lead to autosis or autophagy-dependent cell death, which are caspase-independent, nonapoptotic, non-necrotic forms of cell death. − In addition, potent, selective, nontoxic, in vivo tool compounds must be developed to enable efficacy studies in disease-relevant models and ultimately clinical trials.

Recently, our group developed a small-molecule autophagy activator, RH1115, that modulates autophagic flux in neurons, reduces the accumulation of axonal lysosome swellings, increases lysosome movement, and promotes perinuclear clustering of lysosomes. However, despite the increase in potency and the desirable phenotypes observed in neurons, further testing showed that RH1115 had poor metabolic stability in microsomes with a half-life of less than 5 min, indicating that although it is a useful compound for cell-based studies, it would be unsuitable as an in vivo probe. To address this challenge, we developed iterative libraries of analogues to identify the regions of RH1115 that are amenable to modification to further optimize potency and stability to facilitate in vivo evaluation of this promising scaffold. During our optimization efforts, we also observed structure–activity relationships that correlate with cytotoxicity and further studied the mechanism of RH1115 analogues to enable prioritization of compounds with improved metabolic stability, optimal therapeutic indices, and reduced off-target effects.

Results and Discussion

To develop an optimization strategy for RH1115, we first identified which groups would most likely contribute to the rapid metabolism of this compound. We recognized that the hydroxyl group and the cyclohexyl group posed metabolic liabilities, as these groups are known to undergo glucuronidation and oxidation, respectively. Using SMARTCyp, we determined that the most likely sites of metabolism by cytochrome P450 enzymes were the benzylic position on the pyrrolidine ring, the carbon atoms next to the pyrrolidine nitrogen, and the cyclohexyl group (Figure S1). , Based on these observations, we decided to generate an initial series of analogues where the benzyl group was replaced with different sulfonyl groups, eliminating the basic nitrogen and benzylic position to prevent oxidation at this site. To prepare these analogues, we began with the synthesis of precursor molecule 7 (Scheme ). Using our previously established synthetic strategy, 1-Boc-4-piperidinone underwent a Horner–Wadsworth–Emmons reaction to form the α,β-unsaturated ester 4 in good yield (94%). The ester then underwent a [3 + 2] cycloaddition to form spirocyclic intermediate 5. Initially, we used the same method as previously reported, using N-benzyl-1-methoxy-N-((trimethylsilyl)­methyl)­methanamine and catalytic trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) to promote the cyclization. However, we found that the product was very difficult to purify and often required multiple purifications to separate byproducts and obtain appreciable quantities of 5. By implementing a modified protocol developed by Mykhailiuk and co-workers that replaces TFA with LiF, neat at 140 °C, we were able to obtain good yields (84%) with reduced byproduct formation. Additionally, the ease of purification was highly dependent on the supplier of the N-benzyl-1-methoxy-N-((trimethylsilyl)­methyl)­methanamine. After generating the core spirocycle intermediate, the Boc protecting group was removed using TFA to form 6, and the cyclohexyl group was installed via reductive amination with cyclohexanecarboxaldehyde, TFA, and sodium borohydride to provide intermediate 7 in good yield (82% over 2 steps). Next, the benzyl group was removed using catalytic Pd/C and hydrogen gas to afford intermediate 8 which was then immediately sulfonylated using two different sulfonyl chlorides and triethylamine to provide 9a–9b in moderate yield (38–56% over 2 steps). To access the final analogues, the methyl ester was selectively reduced by LiAlH4 at 0 °C with rapid quenching after 2 min to afford analogues 1a and 1b in moderate yield (51–67%).

In addition to sulfonyl analogues, alkyl analogues were also prepared to replace the benzyl group. To access these analogues, intermediate 8 was subjected to alkylation with phenethyl iodide and potassium carbonate to install a phenethyl group in good yield (10, 60% over 2 steps) (Scheme ). Separately, we also performed a reductive amination with 8 using cyclohexanecarboxaldehyde, TFA, and sodium borohydride to install a second cyclohexyl group (11, 35% over 2 steps). Intermediates 10 and 11 were then reduced with LiAlH4 to afford analogues 1c and 1d in 84% and 54% yield, respectively. To assess the importance of the hydroxyl group and to remove a potential site for glucuronidation, we incorporated amide groups and an ether group at this position. To prepare the amide analogues, 7 was hydrolyzed with LiOH to form carboxylic acid 12, which was then coupled with two different amines using 1-ethyl-3-(3-(dimethylamino)­propyl)­carbodiimide (EDC) and 1-hydroxybenzotriazole (HOBt) to afford analogues 1e and 1f in moderate yields (40% and 27%, respectively). To access the ether analogue, we initially attempted to synthesize methyl ethers using sodium hydride and various methyl electrophiles. However, the only products obtained were mixtures of quaternary salts that we were unable to separate. We decided to form a propyl ether instead, as these alkylating agents would be less electrophilic than methylating reagents, to assess the effects of this position on activity and metabolism. Intermediate 7 was reduced using LiAlH4 to yield 13, which was then alkylated using propyl bromide and sodium hydride to afford 1g in low yield (9%).

This first series of analogues (Series 1) was tested in the eGFP-LC3 assay to measure autophagy activation (Table and Figure S2A). Analogues that replaced the benzyl amine with sulfonamides or the primary alcohol with an amide or propyl ether were all inactive (Table ). However, analogues 1c and 1d, which retained alkyl groups at R1, maintained or slightly improved activity relative to RH1115. Because the sulfonamide and phenethyl groups were incorporated to potentially improve the metabolic stability of this series, we evaluated the active phenethyl analogue in mouse liver microsomes. 1c displayed improved stability, with a half-life of 10 min compared to the 3 min half-life of RH1115 (Figure S3). While this was an improvement, these results indicated that the cyclohexyl group was likely the major source of metabolic instability.

The next series of analogues was designed to replace the cyclohexyl group with different heterocycles to evaluate the impact on activity and stability. This series was split into two groups; group 2 retains the benzyl group at R1, while group 3 incorporates either phenethyl or alkyl moieties at R1 to directly compare the activity and stability of analogues that retain or replace the benzyl group to assess the importance of this functional group. To prepare group 2 analogues, reductive aminations were performed with intermediate 6 to install substituents on the piperidine ring using various aldehydes, sodium triacetoxyborohydride, and acetic acid to afford intermediates 14a–14o (15–51% over 2 steps) (Scheme ). These esters were then reduced with LiAlH4 to afford analogues 2a–2o with a wide range of yields (20–92%). To generate group 3 analogues, the benzyl group of 5 was removed via catalytic hydrogenolysis to provide 15, and tertiary amines were formed by alkylations to afford 16a–16c (11–55%) or reductive amination with 3,3-dimethylbutanal to afford 19 in good yield (74%) (Scheme ). Boc-protected intermediates 16a–16c and 19 were then deprotected to afford 17a–17d, which were taken forward through reductive aminations with various aldehydes to afford intermediates 18a–18u in a wide range of yields (13–82%). The final reduction of the ester using LiAlH4 afforded analogues 3a–3u in moderate to good yields (38–94%).

For compounds 3s-3u that retained the cyclohexyl group at R2, we found that replacing the phenyl group in the phenethyl substituent at R2 with a 2-pyridyl (3s), morpholine (3t), or tert-butyl (3u) group significantly reduced activity (Table ). In the next set of analogues (Series 2), we replaced the cyclohexyl group at R2 with various heterocycles and found that the two analogues that incorporated an indole at R2 (2a and 3a) were active (Table and Figure S2B), most notably analogue 3a, which had an EC50 of 3.2 μM, 5-fold lower than RH1115. Based on these results, the next set of analogues (Series 3) incorporated additional nitrogen-containing heterocycles at R2, including alkylated indoles, azaindoles, and imidazoles (Table and Figure S2C). Replacement of the indole with an imidazole abolished activity, but azaindole 3h retained activity, albeit with reduced potency. N-alkylation of the indole produced compounds with comparable or improved EC50 values relative to the parent indole (3a), but generally increased cytotoxicity (CC50) when longer alkyl groups were incorporated (propyl vs methyl). To mitigate increased cytotoxicity and to determine which structure–activity relationships may be predictive of reduced therapeutic index (CC50/EC50), we prepared additional analogues (Series 4) bearing a variety of substituted indoles, alkylated azaindoles, and benzofurans (Tables , , and Figure S2D). Replacement of the indole with benzofuran or azaindole reduced potency, but N-1-methylation of the azaindole (3j) improved potency compared to the parent azaindole (3h). Inclusion of fluorine and methoxy groups at the 5-position of the indole slightly improved the potency of alkylated indole analogues, but overall, most indole analogues possessed EC50 values in the low micromolar range. Additionally, the most potent indole analogues 3l and 3m were also the most cytotoxic analogues, indicating that autophagy activation and cytotoxicity may be correlated. Based on potency in the eGFP-LC3 assay and cytotoxicity profile for 4-, 24-, and 72-h compound treatment, analogues bearing phenethyl and indole (3g and 3o) or azaindole (3j) substituents in place of the metabolically labile benzyl and cyclohexyl substituents, were prioritized for additional evaluation.

Having established the positive effects of incorporating a phenethyl group at R1 and indoles and azaindoles at R2, we sought to develop a deeper understanding of the structure–activity relationships related to cell viability by assessing apoptosis and necrosis following compound treatment. We previously identified lamin A/C and LAMP1 as targets of RH1115, but we did not observe changes in lamin A/C expression levels with compound treatment. Because lamin A/C is a substrate that is cleaved by caspase 6 during apoptosis, we hypothesized that RH1115 might be stabilizing lamin A/C and preventing its cleavage. Staurosporine (Sta) treatment was used to induce apoptosis; however, no reduction in lamin A/C cleavage was observed with RH1115 cotreatment (Figures A, and S8A) or cotreatment with three analogues, 3g, 3j, and 3o (Figures B–C and S8B–C), indicating that the compounds did not prevent or reduce apoptosis. Cleavage of PARP1, which is cleaved by caspase 3/7 during apoptosis, was also assessed, and RH1115 cotreatment did not rescue PARP1 cleavage induced by Sta or chloroquine (CQ) (Figures D and S9A). RH1115 and analogues also did not induce cleavage of either substrate (Figures E and S9A,B), indicating that they do not induce caspase-dependent apoptosis. To determine whether the compounds induce necrosis, permeabilization of the plasma membrane, a key feature of necrotic cells, was assessed by measuring lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release into the cytosol. We observed no significant increase in LDH release from cells treated with 3g, 3j, or 3o for 24 h at any of the evaluated concentrations (Figure F), indicating that necrosis is not the primary pathway responsible for the observed cell death. However, RH1115 treatment did increase LDH release from cells, but only at the highest concentration (Figure F), so necrosis may be contributing to any cytotoxicity observed after longer treatment periods with high concentrations (≥8× the EC50 for autophagy activation) of this compound.

After ruling out apoptosis and necrosis as the primary cell death pathways responsible for cytotoxicity of the analogues, next we evaluated the correlation between autophagy activity and cytotoxicity. We assessed cell viability by nuclear count in eGFP-LC3 HeLa cells at 4 h and using CellTiter Glo to measure ATP production and quantify metabolically active cells in HeLa cells at 24 and 72 h (Tables – and Figures S4–S7), and we observed significant cytotoxicity among the indole series of analogues (2a, 2e, 2f, 2k–m, 3a, 3f, 3g, 3l–o, 3q, and 3r) (Figure , Tables –, and Figures S6–S7). In the eGFP-LC3 assay, we had previously observed that several RH1115 analogues with improved potency produce large perinuclear vacuolar structures at concentrations higher than the EC50 within the 4-h experiment (Figure S10). It is possible that this phenotype is due to the induction of nucleophagy, a type of selective autophagy that recycles nuclear components to maintain cellular homeostasis and safeguard genomic stability. This process forms large, perinuclear autophagosomes and autolysosomes and targets nuclear proteins, including lamins, DNA–protein cross-links, and chromatin fragments for degradation to preserve nuclear integrity. To determine if the observed cytotoxicity and vacuole formation are autophagy-dependent, we treated ATG9A knockout (KO) HeLa cells with compounds, measured viability using CellTiter Glo and vacuole formation by microscopy, and compared the results in HeLa cells and eGFP-LC3 HeLa cells, respectively (Figures A–C, and S10–S11). We expected that cells lacking ATG9A, a transmembrane lipid scramblase involved in expansion of the phagophore membrane, would be unable to form the perinuclear vacuolar structures if they are indeed autophagosomes or autolysosomes and that cytotoxicity would be significantly reduced in this cell line if it is due to autophagy-dependent cell death. After 24-h compound treatment, the effects of 3g, 3j, and 3o on cell viability were significantly reduced or completely abolished in ATG9A KO HeLa cells, whereas the CC50 value for RH1115 was essentially the same in HeLa cells and ATG9A KO HeLa cells. (Figure A). In ATG9A KO HeLa cells treated with 3g or RH1115 the vacuolar structures were observed at the highest concentrations tested for each compound (32 μM and 96 μM, respectively) (Figure C), which also reduced cell viability in both cell lines (Figure A). RH1115 may generally disrupt membrane integrity at very high concentrations, which could contribute to the observed increase in LDH release (Figure F) and explain the increased number of vacuolar structures observed in the ATG9A KO cell line after 24 h (Figure C). In addition, we did not observe significant formation of vacuolar structures in the eGFP-LC3 HeLa cells after 4-h treatment with RH1115 at 96 μM (approximately 8× the EC50 for autophagy activation) (Figure B), which further supports the possibility that the vacuolar structures induced by treatment with this compound may not be dependent on autophagy activation. The indole analogue 3g retained some cytotoxicity and induced perinuclear vacuole formation in the ATG9A KO cell line, albeit a greatly reduced amount compared to the level induced in the eGFP-LC3 HeLa cells, at the highest concentration tested (32 μM, approximately 8× the EC50 for autophagy activation) (Figure A–C). The vacuolar structures observed in eGFP-LC3 HeLa cells treated with 3o for 4 h at 16 μM (approximately 8× the EC50 for autophagy activation) (Figure B) were not observed in ATG9A KO cells, even after 24-h treatment at 32 μM (approximately 16× the EC50 for autophagy activation) (Figure C). Based on these observations, the vacuolar structures formed in cells treated with 3o are likely autophagosomes or autolysosomes that could be involved in protective nucleophagy, but at high concentrations for extended periods of time, 3o may “over-activate” the autophagy pathway and cause autophagy-dependent cell death. Intriguingly, 3o, which is nearly identical to 3g except for the free indole nitrogen instead of N-Me and a 5-methoxy group added to the indole moiety, lost all cytotoxicity and no longer induced vacuole formation in ATG9A KO cells despite being almost two times as potent as 3g in the eGFP-LC3 autophagy assay. The azaindole analogue 3j did not induce vacuole formation in the eGFP-LC3 HeLa cells at 48 μM (approximately 5× the EC50 for autophagy activation) (Figure S11A), but these vacuolar structures were observed at 96 μM (approximately 10× the EC50 for autophagy activation) (Figure B), which is the only concentration tested at which cytotoxicity was observed after 24-h treatment (Figure A). These structures were not observed in the ATG9A KO cells treated with 3j at the same concentration (Figure C). Taken together, these results indicate that some analogues are more selective autophagy activators and that the cytotoxicity observed when cells are treated for longer time periods at high concentrations of these analogues is autophagy-dependent cell death. However, it is important to note that we did not observe formation of the vacuolar structures at the EC50 values for RH1115, 3g, 3o, or 3j in the eGFP-LC3 HeLa or ATG9A KO HeLa cell lines (Figure S11). Treating cells with these compound concentrations robustly activates autophagy and should minimize potential off-target effects and/or autophagy-dependent cell death. Even for our most selective compounds, like 3j and 3o, it will be important to carefully determine the lowest activity level that offers the greatest therapeutic benefit, because excessive levels of bulk autophagy would likely be detrimental to cellular health compared to moderate enhancement of basal autophagy to a level that provides maximum protective effects. Additional investigation into the mechanism of action of RH1115 and analogues is currently underway and will be reported in due course.

One of our primary goals is to develop compounds with improved properties that enable in vivo evaluation, so we selected several compounds based on activity trends and functional group combinations to evaluate metabolic stability in mouse microsomes and blood-brain-barrier (BBB) penetrative ability in a BBB parallel artificial membrane permeability assay (PAMPA). In mouse microsomes, the compound with an amide at R3 in place of the primary alcohol (1f), and the propyl alkylated indoles 2k and 3m performed poorly, with half-lives less than 15 min (Figure A and Table ). The remaining compounds performed significantly better, with five out of ten compounds having a half-life greater than 45 min. Out of the eight compounds evaluated in the BBB PAMPA, only 3j, the methylated azaindole analogue, displayed moderate penetration at the 8-h assay end point (Figure B and Table ). Log P values were calculated using SwissADME, and analogues with consensus log P values between 3.0–3.5 had the greatest effective permeability (Figure C). Due to their improved potency and metabolic stability, range of penetrative ability in the BBB PAMPA, and low off-target cytotoxicity, 3j (AD3112), 3o (501A054), and 3g (AD3036) have been prioritized for evaluation of in vivo metabolism and maximum tolerated dose to inform the design of efficacy studies in disease models to assess the therapeutic benefit of inducing autophagic flux.

Conclusions

Through iterative synthesis and testing of RH1115 analogues, we were able to identify compounds with improved metabolic stability and potency and reduced off-target effects. The replacement of the cyclohexyl group with indole and azaindole moieties provided the most significant improvements, whereas modifications of other regions provided modest improvements or completely abolished activity. These structure–activity relationship studies also enabled evaluation of the impact of structural changes on cytotoxicity. Several analogues induced autophagy-dependent cell death at high concentrations, whereas RH1115 induced a similar cytotoxicity profile in the absence of required autophagy machinery when tested at high concentrations, indicating improved selectivity of these new analogues. Additional mechanistic studies will further assess the activity of RH1115 and analogues and the impact of cytotoxicity and therapeutic index on efficacy and tolerability. Rather than developing the most potent molecule possible with the greatest level of autophagy induction, clinical modulation of this pathway will require a much more nuanced approach. It will be important to carefully assess the ideal level of autophagy induction to achieve therapeutic benefits in model systems, and ultimately clinical settings. Small molecules are especially well-suited for this type of titration and optimization, and the compounds prioritized through our studies will be used to study the potential applications and limitations of autophagy modulation as a therapeutic strategy.

Experimental Section

General Methods

All chemical reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, Ambeed, TCI America, or Oakwood Chemicals and were used without further purification unless otherwise noted. Reaction mixtures were purified on a Biotage Isolera One or a Biotage Selekt automated chromatography system with silica gel columns. All compounds were confirmed to be ≥95% pure by a combination of HPLC, NMR, and TLC analysis prior to evaluation in assays. Reactions were monitored by TLC (Silica gel 60 F254 Glass Backed plates). NMR data were collected on a Bruker AV 500 MHz spectrometer outfitted with a Bruker 5 mm 1H19F/BBO S2 Z-gradient probe, a Bruker Avance III NMR spectrometer equipped with a 3 mm CP TCI probe operating at a frequency of 600 MHz for the 1H nucleus and 150 MHz for the 13C nucleus, and a Bruker Avance III HD spectrometer equipped with a 5 mm CPP TCI probe operating at a frequency of 500 MHz for the 1H nucleus and 125 MHz for the 13C. Data were recorded at ambient temperature and are reported as chemical shift (ppm) relative to solvent peak (1H NMR: CDCl3 = 7.26 ppm, MeOD = 3.31 ppm, DMSO = 2.50 ppm; 13C NMR: CDCl3 = 77.16 ppm, MeOD = 49.00 ppm, DMSO = 39.52 ppm). Spectra were processed utilizing Mestrenova (Mestrelab Research). 1H and 13C NMR spectra are included in the Supporting Information for all isolated intermediates and final compounds. Purity of all final compounds was confirmed to be ≥95% by HPLC analysis and HRMS data was obtained using an Agilent 6520 Accurate-Mass Q-TOF LC/MS System, (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA) equipped with a dual electro-spray source, operated in the positive-ion mode. Separation was performed on Zorbax 300SB-C18 Poroshell column (2.1 mm × 150 mm; particle size 5 μm). The analytes were eluted at a flow rate of 1 mL/min with a 5 to 100% organic gradient over 4 min and holding 100% organic for 1 min, where 0.1% aqueous formic acid was exchanged for 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. The instrument was used in full-scan TOF mode. MS source parameters were set with a capillary voltage of 4 kV, the fragmentor voltage of 150 V and skimmer 65 V. The gas temperature was 350 °C, drying gas flow 12l/min and nebulizer pressure 55 psig. Data were acquired at high resolution (1700 m/z), 4 GHz. To maintain mass accuracy during the run time, an internal mass calibration sample was infused continuously during the LC/MS runs. Data acquisition and analysis were performed using MassHunter Workstation Data Softwares, LCMS Data Acquisition (version B.06.01) and Qualitative Analysis (version B.07.00). Chromatograms for 1c-1g, 2a-2o, and 3a-3u are included in the Supporting Information. IR spectra were recorded as thin films with a Bruker ALPHA II FT-IR spectrometer.

Microsomal Stability Assay

Compounds were diluted to produce a 1.0 mM stock in 0.1 M Potassium Phosphate Buffer (Fisher Scientific, J61413.AP). Duplicate Eppendorf tubes were prepared containing 36 μL of buffer, 2 μL of compound, 2 μL of 0.1 M diphenhydramine (Sigma, D3630) (positive control), 2 μL of 5 mM reserpine (Sigma, 83580), and 5 μL 10 mM NADPH (Sigma, N7505). A negative control tube was prepared by omitting the NADPH. Tubes were incubated at 37 °C for 5 min followed by addition of 2 μL of mouse liver microsomes (Fisher, 50–722–604, Xenotech LLC). Reactions were incubated in a shaking heater and quenched at 0 min, 15 min, 30 min, 45 and 60 min by addition of 100 μL 1:1 acetonitrile:methanol. Tubes were centrifuged at 12,000g for 10 min at 4 °C and supernatant was moved into fresh Eppendorf tubes. Samples were dried down using a centrifugal concentrator (Labconco). Samples were resuspended in water spiked with 0.1% formic acid and analyzed using liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (LCMS). Masshunter was used to identify mass peaks corresponding to compounds and to calculate area under the curve of noted peaks. Areas were compared to the negative control and the 0 min time point. Remaining values were normalized to the internal standard reserpine. This protocol was used to produce data included in Figure S2. Additional microsomal stability studies and BBB PAMPA were performed by BioDuro (www.bioduro.com).

Representative Synthetic Protocols and Characterization

General Synthetic Protocols

General Procedure A

To a flame-dried flask equipped with a stir bar was added crude methyl 2-benzyl-2,8-diazaspiro[4.5]­decane-4-carboxylate (1.0 equiv), the aldehyde (5.0 equiv), and trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) (12 equiv) in methanol (0.5 M). The reaction was heated to 50 °C for 6 h before the addition of sodium borohydride (5.0 equiv). The reaction was stirred for 12 h before being removed from heat. The reaction was quenched with H2O and was extracted with EtOAc and aqueous NaHCO3 (3×). The organic layer was dried with sodium sulfate and concentrated under vacuum.

General Procedure B

To a flame-dried flask equipped with a stir bar was added the desired carboxylate as a solution in CH2Cl2 (0.1 M). The sulfonyl chloride was then added (1.1 equiv) along with triethylamine (1.2 equiv). The reaction was heated to 35 °C for 12 h before being removed from heat. The reaction was extracted with EtOAc (3×), and the organic layer was dried with sodium sulfate and concentrated under vacuum.

General Procedure C

To a flame-dried flask equipped with a stir bar was added the desired carboxylate as a solution in acetonitrile (0.1 M). The alkyl halide was then added (1.2 equiv) along with K2CO3 (2 equiv). The reaction was heated to 60 °C for 12 h before being removed from heat. The reaction was extracted with EtOAc (3×), and the organic layer was dried with sodium sulfate and concentrated under vacuum.

General Procedure D

To a flame-dried flask equipped with a stir bar was added LiAlH4 (1.5 equiv) and a slurry was formed using a small amount of THF and cooled to 0 °C. The ester was then added dropwise as a solution in THF (0.1 M), and the reaction was stirred for 5 min, after which the reaction was quenched with H2O. The reaction mixture was filtered through Celite and concentrated under vacuum.

General Procedure E

To a flame-dried flask equipped with a stir bar was added LiAlH4 (2.0 equiv) and a slurry was formed using a small amount of THF. The ester was then added dropwise as a solution in THF (0.1M). The reaction was stirred for 2 h at room temperature, after which the reaction was quenched with H2O. The reaction mixture was filtered through Celite and concentrated under vacuum.

General Procedure F

To a flame-dried flask equipped with a stir bar was added the carboxylate (1.0 equiv) and TFA (12 equiv) in CH2Cl2 (1 M). The reaction was stirred for 3 h at room temperature and was extracted with CH2Cl2 and aqueous NaHCO3 (3×). The organic layer was dried with sodium sulfate, concentrated under vacuum, and the crude product was used in the next step.

General Procedure G

To a flame-dried flask equipped with a stir bar was added the carboxylate (1.0 equiv), the aldehyde, (1.5 equiv), and glacial acetic acid (1.0 equiv) in CH2Cl2 (0.1 M). The reaction was heated to 35 °C and stirred for 1 h before the addition of sodium triacetoxyborohydride (1.5 equiv). The reaction was stirred for 12 h and then removed from heat. The reaction was quenched with H2O and was extracted with EtOAc and aqueous NaHCO3 (3×). The organic layer was dried with sodium sulfate and concentrated under vacuum.

General Procedure H

To a flame-dried flask equipped with a stir bar was added NaH (1.2 equiv) as a 60% dispersion in mineral oil followed by acetonitrile (0.4 M). The aldehyde (1.0 equiv) was then added and allowed to react for 30 min. The alkyl halide (5.0 equiv) was then added and the reaction was stirred for 1 h. The reaction was quenched with H2O and was extracted with EtOAc and aqueous NaHCO3 (3×). The organic layer was dried with sodium sulfate and concentrated under vacuum.
