Effectiveness, Safety, and Tolerability of Bilastine Up-Dosing in Chronic Spontaneous Urticaria Uncontrolled with Licensed Doses of Other Second-Generation Antihistamines—An Institution-Based, Open-Label, Single-Group Longitudinal Trial
Authors: Sankha S. Chakraborty, Indrashis Podder, Manisha Das, Arunima Dhabal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Bilastine 20 mg daily is a second-generation antihistamine (sgAH) approved for chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU). Up-dosing to 40 or 80 mg daily is recommended in uncontrolled patients.Aim and Objectives:Evaluating the effectiveness, safety, and tolerability of bilastine at 20, 40, and 80 mg daily in CSU patients uncontrolled with licensed doses of other sgAHs.Patients and Methods:In this institution-based, open-label, longitudinal trial, CSU patients were evaluated for demography, angioedema, and symptomatic dermographism. Urticaria disease severity was assessed by urticaria activity score (UAS7) score on days 0, 14, 28, and 42. All patients received bilastine 20 mg daily for two weeks. Non-responders (UAS7 > 0) were up-dosed to 40 or 80 mg. Laboratory tests were conducted at baseline and on day 42.Results:Thirty-five CSU patients (mean age 34.5 years, median duration six months) were included. Bilastine 20 mg reduced mean UAS7 score from 25.7 to 6 at day 14 (P < 0.001), with 16 achieving complete resolution (UAS7 = 0). Among non-responders, up-dosing to 40 mg further reduced UAS7 to 1.9 (P < 0.001), with 15 more patients achieving UAS7 = 0. Only four required 80 mg, showing minimal additional benefit. (P = 0.1). Overall, 94% achieved complete control. Somnolence (22%) and headache (50%) occurred at higher doses, but with good tolerability. A higher baseline UAS7 score increased the odds for up-dosing (OR 1.2, P = 0.001).Limitations:Our study is limited by its small sample size, single-center design, and absence of a control group. Additionally, lack of information on prior antihistamine use or compliance may have confounded the assessment of refractoriness.Conclusion:Up-dosing of bilastine is effective, safe, and tolerable in CSU patients uncontrolled with licensed doses of other sgAHs. Almost 90% patients responded to 20–40 mg, while 80 mg did not show a significant additional benefit. A higher baseline UAS7 score may indicate the need for up-dosing.

Introduction

Chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) is a mast cell-mediated skin disorder characterized by recurrent wheals with/without angioedema, persisting for more than six weeks, without an identifiable trigger.[1] It has a lifetime point prevalence of ~20%.[2] CSU considerably worsens quality of life and imposes a significant societal burden, necessitating appropriate treatment.[3]

Current treatment guidelines recommend second-generation antihistamines (sgAHs), omalizumab, and cyclosporine in a step-wise algorithmic approach.[1] The sgAHs are preferred as first line for their effectiveness, safety, and affordability. Up-dosing of sgAHs to four times their licensed dose is recommended in non-responders.[1] Omalizumab and cyclosporine, the second- and third-line treatments, are expensive with limited accessibility.

Bilastine is a newer, highly-selective H1-antihistamine, with minimal central nervous system penetration and no cytochrome P450 interaction, reducing sedation and drug interaction potential.[4] Its licensed dose is 20 mg daily, and up-dosing to 80 mg daily has been reported as safe and effective.[56] However, real-world studies are limited to its standard dose,[78] and literature concerning up-dosing is scarce.

This study evaluates the effectiveness, safety, and tolerability of bilastine at 20, 40, and 80 mg daily in CSU patients uncontrolled on licensed doses of other sgAHs, and identifies predictors to guide up-dosing.

Patients and Methods

Study design and patient selection

We conducted an institution-based, open-label, single-group longitudinal trial in the Department of Dermatology, College of Medicine and Sagore Dutta Hospital, Kolkata, India, over 12 months (February 2022–March 2023), following ethics approval (Institute/IEC/204/01-2021) and Clinical Trial Register of India registration (CTRI/2021/08/035626). The study obeyed the Good Clinical Practice guidelines, the Declaration of Helsinki, and Schedule Y guidelines.

We screened 43 consecutive CSU patients aged 18–65 years, diagnosed based on the current guidelines,[1] with uncontrolled disease (urticaria activity score UAS7≥7) despite receiving licensed doses of any sgAH other than bilastine. Exclusion criteria included age <18 or >65 years, inducible or chronic urticaria variants, systemic comorbidities, pregnancy/lactation, psychiatric/neurological disorders, substance abuse, illiteracy hindering completion of UAS7, or prior omalizumab use within six months. Written informed consent was obtained from all participating patients.

Evaluation of CSU patients

Eligible participants underwent detailed clinical evaluation, including disease duration, angioedema, dermographism, thyroid dysfunction, atopy, and baseline UAS7 scoring (range 0–42). The UAS7 scoring system evaluates daily wheals (0–3) and pruritus (0–3), summed over seven days (maximum score: 42). Urticaria was classified as absent (0), under control (1–6), mild (7–15), moderate (16–27), or severe (28–42) based on the UAS7 score.

Baseline investigations included a five-part complete hemogram, C-reactive protein, liver/renal function tests, serum immunoglobulin E (IgE), electrocardiogram, and derived neutrophil/lymphocyte or platelet/lymphocyte ratios (NLR/PLR). Safety was assessed at each visit by adverse drug reaction (ADR) monitoring and pill count-based tolerability evaluation.

Flow of study participants

A two-week drug washout period was provided, during which only bilastine 20 mg was allowed as rescue medication.

During the 14 days before baseline, screening for baseline laboratory investigations, UAS7 data diaries, and informed consent were recorded.

At baseline, eligible patients were started on bilastine 20 mg once daily for two weeks.

At first follow-up on day 14, UAS7 was recorded. Complete responders (UAS7 = 0) were excluded from further escalation. Non-responders were up-dosed to bilastine 40 mg once daily. ADRs and tolerability were checked.

At second follow-up on day 28, UAS7 was reassessed. Patients remaining symptomatic (UAS7>0) were up-dosed to bilastine 80 mg once daily. ADRs and tolerability were noted.

At the end of study (day 42), UAS7 was recorded again, and laboratory investigations were repeated. Safety and tolerability were also re-assessed.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using MedCalc v12.5.0. Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ±SD or percentages. The normality of data was tested with Shapiro- Wilk test. Paired t-test and Chi-square test were used for comparisons. Logistic regression identified predictors of bilastine up-dosing. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinico-Demographic profile and laboratory data

Thirty-five CSU patients (17 males and 18 females, mean age 34.5 ± 11.4 years) were included, majority (40%, n = 14) aged 31–40 years. The median disease duration was six [IQR: 4–11] months. Angioedema was seen in 28.6% (10/35), symptomatic dermographism in 22.9% (8/35), thyroid dysfunction in 17.1% (6/35), and atopy in 31.4% (11/35) patients [Table 1]. The mean baseline UAS7 score was 25.7 ± 11.7, with 71.4% (25/35) having moderate-to-severe disease. All patients remained uncontrolled with other second-generation antihistamines in licensed doses [cetirizine (n = 27), levocetirizine (n = 6), and fexofenadine (n = 2)].

The mean neutrophil, lymphocyte, and platelet counts were within normal range. Hyper eosinophilia was observed in 60% (21/35) of patients. Mean NLR and PLR were 4.03 ± 1.56 and 140.8 ± 37.1, respectively. Serum IgE ranged from 199 to 748.7 IU/mL (mean 405.7 ± 128.7), with one-third above 200 IU/mL [Table 1].

Response to bilastine

Following a two-week drug washout, all patients (n = 35) received bilastine 20 mg daily for two weeks. Mean UAS7 reduced significantly from 25.7 ± 11.7 to 6.0 ± 6.8 at day 14 (P < 0.001). Sixteen patients (45.7%) achieved complete control (UAS7 = 0), and six (17.1%) reported well-controlled disease (UAS7 ≤6). Moderate-to-severe disease reduced from 71.4% to 8.6% (P < 0.001). These 16 patients exited the study; the remaining 19 were up-dosed to bilastine 40 mg for two weeks.

At day 28, mean UAS7 declined from 11.1 ± 5.3 to 1.9 ± 4.5 (P < 0.001). Additionally, 15 patients (78.9%) achieved complete control. Only four patients (11.4%) required bilastine 80 mg. Their mean UAS7 declined from 9.0 ± 6.0 (day 28) to 2.0 ± 2.8 (day 42), but this was not statistically significant (P = 0.1). Among those receiving 80 mg (n = 4), two (50%) achieved complete response; two had well-controlled disease. Only two patients (5.7%) remained refractory after four times up-dosing. Overall, 88.6% (31/35) achieved complete control with bilastine 20 mg (n = 16) or 40 mg (n = 15). Response trends are detailed in Table 2, Figures 1 and 2.

Bilastine was well tolerated at all doses. No adverse events occurred with the 20 mg dose. At 40 mg, somnolence (22%) and nausea (5%) were reported. Headache was complained by 2/4 patients on 80 mg. No drug discontinuation was required.

Predictive factors for bilastine up-dosing

On univariate regression, a higher baseline UAS7 score was significantly associated with the need for up-dosing (OR 1.2; P = 0.001). Other factors, such as female gender (OR 1.1), disease duration (OR 1.2), neutrophil lymphocyte ratio (NLR) (OR 1.4), eosinophilia (OR 2.2), higher mean platelet volume (MPV) (OR 1.7), and elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) (OR 1.9), showed trends, but none were statistically significant. No independent predictors were identified on multivariate analysis.

Discussion

This longitudinal study evaluated the effectiveness and safety of bilastine in CSU patients unresponsive to licensed doses of other sgAHs, focusing on bilastine switching and up-dosing in accordance with the international guidelines.[1]

A recent meta-analysis confirmed the superiority of bilastine 20 mg over other sgAHs in chronic urticaria.[9] However, data on switching to bilastine in sgAH-refractory patients are limited. A Phase IV Japanese trial[10] and an Indian study[8] showed clinical improvement with bilastine switching, consistent with our results. In our study, bilastine 20 mg significantly reduced UAS7 scores (P < 0.001), and 45.7% patients refractory to licensed doses of other sgAHs achieved complete control. Thus, switching to bilastine may be considered before up-dosing other ineffective sgAHs. The superiority of bilastine may be attributed to its higher specificity and binding affinity for histamine (H1)-receptors, approximately threefold and fivefold greater than cetirizine and fexofenadine, respectively. Other advantages include longer duration of action due to stronger receptor binding, negligible drug interaction, and minimal sedation as it is a substrate for P-glycoprotein, which limits its passage across the blood–brain barrier.[4]

Current international guidelines recommend up-dosing the same sgAH up to fourfold instead of switching to a different one.[1] This view translates from studies favoring up-dosing to combining different antihistamines, likely due to pharmacodynamic properties and H1 receptor inverse agonism.[1112] However, safety concerns persist with long-term high-dose antihistamine use. Su et al.[13] reported increased dementia risk with cumulative antihistamine exposure, underscoring the need for safer and newer options like bilastine.

In our cohort, bilastine 40 mg significantly improved UAS7 scores (P < 0.001), while 80 mg offered no additional significant benefit (P = 0.1). Similar findings have been reported by Weller et al.[14] and Sarti et al.,[15] while De et al.[16] did not up-dose to 80 mg. So, there is limited efficacy of bilastine beyond 40 mg. Overall, 94.3% of our patients became symptom-free with bilastine, compared to 60–70% complete control rates in Western reports, even after four times up-dosing.[214171819] This disparity may be attributed to genetic or environmental heterogeneity, warranting large-scale multicenter international studies.

Bilastine was well tolerated across all doses. No adverse effects were reported at the licensed dose, while mild symptoms, like somnolence, nausea, and headache, occurred at higher doses, in agreement with the existing literature.[9]

A higher baseline UAS7 score significantly predicted the need for up-dosing (P = 0.001), consistent with previous studies identifying high disease activity, CRP, and D-dimer as predictors of antihistamine refractoriness.[20] No other predictors emerged, possibly due to limited sample-size.

Limitation

Limitations include small sample-size, single-center design, lack of control group, unavailability of D-dimer data, and absence of information on previous antihistamine duration or patient compliance, which may confound true refractoriness. So, our findings should be interpreted with caution, and larger, multicenter trials are needed to corroborate these findings.

Conclusion

Bilastine is an effective, safe, and well-tolerated treatment for CSU, both at its licensed dose (20 mg daily) and when up-dosed. Nearly 50% of patients unresponsive to licensed doses of other sgAHs achieved complete control with 20 mg, and 40 mg further improved outcomes. However, increasing to 80 mg per day offered minimal additional benefit, indicating optimal efficacy at 40 mg. A higher baseline UAS7 score may predict the need for up-dosing.

In our study, ~95% achieved complete symptom control with bilastine alone, significantly higher than the ~60% reported in Western studies, suggesting possible genetic or environmental differences.

Clinicians may consider switching to bilastine before up-dosing other sgAHs. Bilastine can also be safely escalated up to four times its licensed dose, as endorsed by current guidelines.
