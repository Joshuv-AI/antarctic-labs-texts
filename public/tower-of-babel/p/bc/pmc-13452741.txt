Giant Left Atrial Aneurysm Complicated by Multiple Thrombi and Cardiogenic Shock: A Fatal Case
Authors: José Manuel Aguilar Rubio, Ramon Antonio Ruelas Estrada, Luis David Beltrán Ontiveros, Lucero Valenzuela Carvajal, Juliana Patricia Ortiz Jiménez, Fernando Rangel Aello, Marco Antonio Rodríguez Sánchez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A giant left atrial aneurysm is an exceptionally rare but clinically significant cardiac abnormality, most commonly associated with rheumatic mitral valve disease, long-standing atrial fibrillation, and progressive atrial remodeling related to aging. Its clinical relevance lies in the high risk of thromboembolic events, compression of adjacent structures, malignant arrhythmias, and advanced heart failure. We report the case of a 72-year-old woman with a history of persistent atrial fibrillation, chronic heart failure, chronic kidney disease, and a non-reperfused acute myocardial infarction, who presented with progressive dyspnea, precordial chest pain, and peripheral edema. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the chest revealed a giant aneurysmal dilation of the left atrium complicated by multiple intracavitary mural thrombi, severe cardiomegaly, and displacement of adjacent cardiac and extracardiac structures. Despite aggressive medical management, the patient rapidly deteriorated and developed refractory cardiogenic shock, culminating in cardiorespiratory arrest. This case highlights the catastrophic potential of giant left atrial aneurysms and underscores the importance of early diagnosis and timely intervention to prevent fatal complications.

Introduction

Because of the rarity of a giant left atrial aneurysm, no universally accepted diagnostic criteria exist. Most published reports define a giant left atrial aneurysm according to imaging findings, with several studies using an anteroposterior diameter ≥60 mm as a practical threshold [1]. Although left atrial enlargement is frequently encountered in clinical practice, true aneurysmal dilation of the atrial wall remains a rare entity, with most cases reported in association with chronic rheumatic mitral valve disease, persistent atrial fibrillation, or long-standing pressure and volume overload [2].

The pathophysiology involves progressive atrial wall remodeling, fibrosis, and loss of myocardial elasticity, leading to marked chamber dilation and impaired contractility [3]. As the aneurysm enlarges, patients become susceptible to severe complications, including thrombus formation with systemic embolization, compression of adjacent mediastinal structures, atrial arrhythmias, and progressive heart failure [4].

Clinical presentation varies widely, ranging from incidental radiologic findings to life-threatening manifestations such as embolic stroke or cardiogenic shock [5]. Given its rarity and nonspecific presentation, diagnosis is often delayed, limiting therapeutic options and worsening prognosis. Herein, we present a fatal case of a giant left atrial aneurysm complicated by multiple intracavitary thrombi and cardiogenic shock, illustrating the devastating natural history of this condition when diagnosis and intervention occur at an advanced stage.

Case presentation

A 72-year-old woman with a medical history of chronic heart failure, persistent atrial fibrillation, advanced chronic kidney disease, and a non-reperfused acute myocardial infarction presented to the emergency department with oppressive precordial chest pain, dyspnea on minimal exertion (New York Heart Association functional class IV), and progressive bilateral lower-limb edema over several days. Her chronic outpatient medical therapy included furosemide 20 mg once daily, spironolactone 50 mg once daily, sacubitril/valsartan 100 mg twice daily, and acenocoumarol 4 mg once daily. The patient denied any recent changes in medication or poor adherence.

On physical examination, the patient was alert but in moderate respiratory distress. Vital signs revealed severe hypoxemia, with oxygen saturation persistently below 80% on room air, an irregularly irregular heart rhythm, and clinical signs of systemic venous congestion. Cardiac auscultation demonstrated an irregular rhythm without clearly audible murmurs. Pulmonary examination revealed bilateral decreased breath sounds at the lung bases, suggestive of pleural effusion. Additional findings included jugular venous distension and bilateral pitting edema extending to the knees.

Laboratory evaluation showed markedly elevated markers of congestion and end-organ dysfunction, including a B-type natriuretic peptide level of 9,999 pg/mL and cancer antigen-125 of 650 U/mL, consistent with advanced chronic venous congestion. Significant renal impairment was documented, with a serum creatinine level of 3.5 mg/dL and a blood urea nitrogen-to-creatinine ratio of 35. Liver function tests were abnormal, consistent with congestive hepatopathy, including total bilirubin 4 mg/dL (direct bilirubin 2.8 mg/dL), aspartate aminotransferase 250 U/L, and alanine aminotransferase 400 U/L. Additionally, hyperlactatemia (6 mmol/L) was observed, indicating tissue hypoperfusion. The international normalized ratio on admission was 2.6, within the therapeutic range, suggesting that thrombus formation likely reflected profound blood stasis secondary to extreme left atrial enlargement rather than inadequate anticoagulation alone (Table 1).

Detailed transthoracic echocardiographic measurements, including left ventricular systolic function and comprehensive structural assessment, were not available because the patient experienced rapid clinical deterioration requiring urgent stabilization. Consequently, the diagnosis and anatomical characterization relied primarily on contrast-enhanced computed tomography.

Over the following hours, the patient experienced progressive hemodynamic deterioration, evolving into cardiogenic shock, characterized by hypotension to non-perfusing levels and refractory hypoxemia. Vasopressor support with norepinephrine and inotropic therapy with dobutamine were initiated, along with supplemental oxygen via a non-rebreather mask at 15 L/minute. Despite aggressive medical and supportive management, the patient exhibited persistent hemodynamic instability with worsening multiorgan dysfunction.

Cardiothoracic surgery was consulted following the imaging findings. However, after multidisciplinary evaluation, the patient was considered to be at prohibitive surgical risk because of refractory cardiogenic shock, advanced multiorgan dysfunction, chronic kidney disease, and severe hemodynamic instability. Consequently, surgical intervention was deemed unfeasible, and supportive medical management was continued.

Ultimately, the patient suffered a cardiorespiratory arrest. Advanced cardiopulmonary resuscitation was promptly initiated in accordance with institutional protocols; however, return of spontaneous circulation was not achieved, and the patient was pronounced deceased due to complications related to her underlying cardiac condition. Discussion

It is important to distinguish a true giant left atrial aneurysm from a left atrial appendage aneurysm. In the present case, imaging demonstrated diffuse aneurysmal enlargement of the left atrial body rather than an isolated aneurysm of the left atrial appendage. Therefore, the lesion was considered a true giant left atrial aneurysm.

A giant left atrial aneurysm is an uncommon but severe manifestation of advanced atrial remodeling and structural cardiac disease. It is traditionally defined by an anteroposterior left atrial diameter exceeding 60 mm on echocardiographic assessment [1]. Although left atrial enlargement is frequently encountered in clinical practice, true aneurysmal dilation of the left atrial wall is rare, with the first documented case reported in 1962. Published data suggest a predominance in female patients, with a mean age at diagnosis of approximately 31 ± 20 years. Nevertheless, cases have been described across a wide age spectrum, including presentations in the eighth and ninth decades of life, highlighting the heterogeneous clinical course and the potential for delayed recognition in certain patients [2].

While rheumatic mitral valve disease remains the most frequently reported etiology, contemporary series increasingly identify persistent atrial fibrillation, ischemic cardiomyopathy, and age-related degenerative remodeling as predominant contributors, particularly in developed healthcare settings [3,4]. Progressive atrial dilation in these contexts reflects chronic pressure and volume overload, myocardial fibrosis, and loss of atrial contractile function.

The clinical relevance of giant left atrial aneurysms lies in their propensity for life-threatening complications. Progressive atrial dilation promotes blood stasis and endothelial dysfunction, creating a highly thrombogenic environment that predisposes to mural thrombus formation, even in patients receiving chronic oral anticoagulation [5]. This markedly increases the risk of systemic thromboembolism, including ischemic stroke. In addition, massive atrial enlargement may exert compressive effects on adjacent mediastinal structures, resulting in dysphagia, bronchial compression with recurrent respiratory infections, or hoarseness secondary to recurrent laryngeal nerve involvement [6].

In the present case, the extreme degree of left atrial enlargement, with an estimated volume exceeding 1.7 L, underscores the potential for progressive atrial remodeling to reach catastrophic proportions. Beyond serving as a marker of advanced structural heart disease, the massive left atrial aneurysm likely contributed directly to hemodynamic deterioration through several complementary mechanisms. Extreme atrial enlargement results in profound impairment of left atrial reservoir, conduit, and contractile function, substantially reducing effective left ventricular preload and compromising cardiac output. In addition, the aneurysm produced marked distortion of the normal cardiac anatomy and exerted a significant mass effect on adjacent cardiac chambers, potentially limiting ventricular filling and impairing diastolic mechanics. Chronic blood stasis within the aneurysmal cavity further promoted extensive mural thrombus formation, while persistent atrial fibrillation eliminated effective atrial contraction, exacerbating circulatory inefficiency. Collectively, these mechanisms likely acted synergistically with the patient’s underlying ischemic cardiomyopathy and chronic heart failure, contributing to progressive hemodynamic collapse and ultimately refractory cardiogenic shock. The coexistence of persistent atrial fibrillation, ischemic heart disease, and chronic volume overload likely acted synergistically, culminating in severe hemodynamic compromise and refractory cardiogenic shock. This highlights the often insidious progression of this entity, which may remain clinically silent until advanced and irreversible stages are reached.

Although the patient had several established risk factors for cardiogenic shock, including ischemic cardiomyopathy, chronic heart failure, and advanced chronic kidney disease, we believe that the giant left atrial aneurysm substantially contributed to the terminal hemodynamic deterioration rather than acting as an isolated cause. The massive aneurysmal enlargement likely exacerbated left atrial mechanical dysfunction, promoted severe blood stasis with extensive thrombus formation, impaired left ventricular filling through altered atrioventricular coupling, and produced significant distortion of the cardiac anatomy. These mechanisms probably acted synergistically with the underlying ischemic myocardial dysfunction, ultimately precipitating refractory cardiogenic shock and multiorgan failure. Therefore, this case illustrates the multifactorial nature of cardiogenic shock in patients with advanced structural heart disease, in whom a giant left atrial aneurysm may represent an important aggravating factor rather than the sole determinant of circulatory collapse.

The differential diagnosis of marked atrial enlargement includes left atrial appendage aneurysm, atrial septal aneurysm, dilated or ischemic cardiomyopathy with secondary atrial dilation, and congenital atrial malformations [7,8]. Multimodality imaging plays a central role in establishing the diagnosis and assessing disease severity. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging allow precise characterization of atrial anatomy, thrombus burden, wall calcifications, and extracardiac involvement, facilitating accurate differentiation from pseudoaneurysms and other structural abnormalities [9].

Distinguishing a true giant left atrial aneurysm from severe left atrial enlargement secondary to chronic atrial fibrillation or structural heart disease may be challenging, particularly in advanced cases. In the present patient, the diagnosis was based on the combination of imaging findings rather than chamber size alone. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography demonstrated a markedly aneurysmal configuration of the left atrium, with extreme focal enlargement, distortion of the normal cardiac anatomy, displacement of adjacent mediastinal structures, and a well-defined aneurysmal cavity containing multiple mural thrombi and peripheral wall calcifications. These features extend beyond the appearance typically expected in conventional left atrial enlargement associated with chronic atrial fibrillation or ischemic cardiomyopathy and were therefore considered more consistent with a giant left atrial aneurysm.

Management strategies must be individualized based on symptom burden, comorbidities, and surgical risk. Surgical resection of the aneurysm, with or without concomitant mitral valve surgery, is generally considered the treatment of choice in symptomatic patients, particularly those with thromboembolic events, refractory arrhythmias, or compressive symptoms [10]. Conversely, conservative management with long-term anticoagulation and close imaging surveillance may be appropriate in asymptomatic patients or those deemed high-risk for surgical intervention [9]. However, once the disease progresses to advanced heart failure or cardiogenic shock, as illustrated in this case, therapeutic options become markedly limited, and the prognosis is exceedingly poor.

A limitation of this report is the absence of detailed baseline echocardiographic data, including left ventricular systolic function and comprehensive structural assessment, which precluded a more precise evaluation of the relative contribution of chronic ischemic cardiomyopathy and the giant left atrial aneurysm to the patient’s terminal hemodynamic deterioration. Nevertheless, the marked anatomical distortion demonstrated by contrast-enhanced computed tomography, together with the extensive intracavitary thrombus burden, suggests that the aneurysm represented an important aggravating factor acting in conjunction with the patient’s advanced underlying cardiac disease.

Conclusions

A giant left atrial aneurysm is a rare but potentially fatal condition associated with significant morbidity and mortality. The most frequent complications include intracardiac thrombus formation, systemic thromboembolism, compression of adjacent structures, malignant arrhythmias, and progressive heart failure. Early recognition through appropriate imaging and timely intervention, particularly surgical management in symptomatic patients, is essential to prevent irreversible complications and improve outcomes.
