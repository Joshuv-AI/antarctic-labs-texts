Jejunocecal fistula combined with gastro–ascending colonic fistula in a child with chronic diarrhea and weight loss: a case report
Authors: Mengyu Ke, Huan Li, Hongxi Guo, Peng Huang, Jun Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGastrointestinal fistulas in children are rare, and the coexistence of a jejunocecal and gastric ascending colon fistula is exceptional. To our knowledge, no instance of this fistula combination has been reported previously. These patients present with nonspecific symptoms, which may lead to delayed or missed diagnoses.Case presentationWe evaluated a 12-year-old boy with a 3-year history of intermittent nausea and vomiting, and a weight loss of 30 kg. Initially, he was managed as for gastroenteritis; however, his response was minimal. Further investigation included endoscopy and contrast radiography; these studies identified an abnormal communication between the stomach and colon; however, the exact anatomy remained elusive.Laparoscopy identified two fistulae: one between the greater curvature of the stomach and the ascending colon, and another between the jejunum and the cecum. The operation was converted to open due to dense adhesions. Resection of the fistulous tracts and repair (stomach) or anastomosis (small intestine and colon) were performed. Histopathology showed chronic inflammation with mucosal metaplasia but no evidence of malignancy. Retrospectively, the patient and family recalled prior play with magnets, but ingestion could not be confirmed.ConclusionsIn children with chronic symptoms, such as recurrent diarrhea with weight loss, a gastrointestinal fistula should be considered. Use of contrast radiography and endoscopy, together with clinical correlation, may prevent misdiagnosis and delayed in treatment.

Background

Multiple gastrointestinal fistula in children are rare. Their occurrence may follow abdominal surgery (adhesiolysis), or in association with malignancy, Crohn’s disease, diverticulitis, or pancreatitis [1, 2]. Additional etiologies are congenital anomalies, radiation injury, and ingested foreign bodies. Clinical manifestations are nonspecific (diarrhea, vomiting, abdominal pain, malnutrition, and weight loss). We report a child with chronic diarrhea and weight loss, who was initially diagnosed as gastroenteritis, whose actual problem was concurrent jejunocecal and gastrocolic fistulae.

Case presentation

A 12-year-old boy was admitted to the Department of Gastroenterology with recurrent diarrhea (small-volume, loose, yellowish stools) and intermittent belching, nausea and vomiting for 3 years. His appetite was poor and selective. During the preceding year, his weight had decreased from 60 kg to 30 kg. There was no history of abdominal surgery or significant trauma. Allergy, family history, and psychosocial histories were unremarkable.

During the three preceding years, he was evaluated at local hospitals (and also at our institution), and he was repeatedly diagnosed with gastroenteritis. Prescribed treatments included digestive enzyme preparations, probiotics, and traditional Chinese patent medicines; these resulted in no substantial improvement.

On admission, he was alert and oriented. Abdominal examination revealed a soft abdomen without tenderness, rebound, or a palpable mass.

Laboratory tests (Table 1) showed leukocytosis (WBC 12,680/L with 73.2% neutrophils), transaminases were mildly elevated (ALT 52 U/L; AST 51 U/L), hypolipidemia (HDL 0.78 mmol/L, LDL 0.88 mmol/L, total cholesterol 1.94 mmol/L), IgE was markedly elevated (3250 IU/mL), and vitamins A, E, and D were deficient (A 202.78 ng/mL; E 2927.12 ng/mL; 25(OH)D₃ 8.39 ng/mL).

Upper GI endoscopy showed a fistulous opening with mucosa suggestive of small bowel and colon. Colonoscopy suggested a fistula involving the stomach and ascending colon (Fig. 1A, B). Biopsy showed mild inflammation.

Elective surgical exploration was scheduled for etiologic clarification and definitive repair. Intraoperatively, two distinct internal fistulas were identified: (1) between the greater curvature of the stomach and the lateral wall of the ascending colon near the cecum; and (2) between the cecum and the lateral wall of the jejunum approximately 15 cm distal to the ligament of Treitz (Fig. 2A, B,C). Due to dense intra-abdominal adhesions, laparoscopic surgery was converted to open laparotomy, and the fistulous tracts were exposed by meticulous lysis of adhesions. The jejunocecal fistula was approximately 2 cm in diameter. An elliptical excision of the fistulous tract that included a margin of 0.5 cm healthy mucosal to ensure complete removal of the abnormal tissue. The resultant defects (approximately 1.8 cm in diameter) were repaired with a single-layer interrupted suture of polyglycolic acid sutures. The gastrocolic fistula was approximately 3 cm in diameter; there was a protrusion of the ascending colon adjacent to the communication with the stomach. The protrusion was excised, and an end-to-end anastomosis (utilizing a stapler) restored gastrointestinal continuity. An appendectomy was also performed, as the repair was adjacent to the appendix. No foreign bodies were detected.

Postoperatively, the patient was treated with antibiotics, gastrointestinal decompression, acid suppression, and parenteral fluid and electrolytes. Histopathological examination of the tissue showed chronic inflammation and mucosal metaplasia, but no evidence of malignancy or specific infection. His postoperative course was uneventful, and he was discharged on postoperative day 11.

A retrospective history revealed that the patient had played with small magnetic toys (diameter 5 mm) without adult supervision, although ingestion could not be confirmed. Notably, the patient had no history of abdominal surgery, trauma, or invasive abdominal procedures, and intraoperative exploration showed no evidence of iatrogenic injury (surgical adhesions, suture residues, or focal intestinal wall damage consistent with prior interventions). In the absence of evidence for other etiologies (such as malignancy, inflammatory bowel disease, prior surgery, or trauma), magnetic foreign body ingestion was considered a plausible hypothesis.

Discussion and conclusion

We describe a rare combination of GI fistulae in a child: a jejunocecal fistula and a gastro-ascending colon fistula. To our knowledge, this is the first reported pediatric case of concurrent jejunocecal and gastro-ascending colon fistulas, highlighting the novelty and clinical significance of this report. Gastrointestinal fistulae may present with diarrhea, feculent vomiting/eructation, and weight loss, although the full triad is not always present [3, 4]. Associated symptoms may include abdominal pain, jaundice, hematemesis, and melena. Diarrhea may occur when gastric contents bypass normal small-intestinal transit and absorption and enter the distal colon. Feculent vomiting and eructation occur when colonic contents reflux through the fistula into the stomach; this must be differentiated from distal intestinal obstruction [5]. The abnormal gastrointestinal communication causes malabsorption and fluid and electrolyte loss. Ultimately this result in weight loss, hypoproteinemia, anemia, cachexia, and multi-organ (respiratory, hepatic, renal) dysfunction.

There are several potential explanations for our patient’s elevated IgE level (3250 IU/mL).

Without allergen testing and evaluation of immune function, the cause of the IgE elevation remains unclear; however, we surmise that chronic inflammation caused by the fistulas is the most likely contributor. Resolution of IgE elevation after surgical repair of the fistulas would corroborate this hypothesis.

Patients who ingest magnets commonly present with symptoms such as abdominal pain, diarrhea, vomiting, and anorexia. A diagnosis of magnet ingestion is usually established only after complications develop [6, 7]. On imaging, clustered magnets may mimic a single object within one bowel loop, or they may in fact be located in separate loops of bowel and adhere by magnetic attraction across bowel walls; and pressure necrosis causes fistulation [8, 9].

In general, the severity of complications correlates with the number of magnets ingested and their duration in the GI tract. The absence of foreign bodies may indicate elimination by defecation.

Because symptoms are nonspecific and the anatomy variable, diagnosis relies on a combination of endoscopy and imaging [10]. For pediatric patients, this diagnostic combination is tailored to their clinical features: non-invasive ultrasound and CT serve as first-line screening due to children’s limited symptom description, and contrast radiography and endoscopy complement each other—radiography shows the overall fistula course, while endoscopy enables mucosal observation and biopsy, critical for ruling out malignant lesions and specific infections in children. A comprehensive diagnostic approach that includes gastrointestinal endoscopy and contrast radiographs is most apt to lead to the correct diagnosis.

Management depends upon the etiology, anatomy, nutritional status, and complications. Surgery is indicated for fistulae caused by malignancy or Crohn’s disease. Once malignancy is excluded, conservative treatments may be employed. In cases of fistulae complicated by infection, parenteral nutrition may redress malnutrition and create conditions conducive to spontaneous healing. Fistulae associated with peptic ulcer disease may close following appropriate pharmacological treatment. For cases involving foreign body ingestion, prompt endoscopic retrieval or surgical intervention is advised to reduce the risk of perforation [11].

Concurrent jejunocecal and gastrocolic fistulae are rare. In patients who present with GI symptoms complicated by weight loss, clinicians should consider gastrointestinal fistulae. Contrast radiographs and endoscopic evaluations, in addition to careful clinical evaluation, assessing the possibility of foreign body ingestion, may reduce misdiagnosis and facilitate definitive treatment.
