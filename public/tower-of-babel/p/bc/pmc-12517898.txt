Systemic treatment of metastatic castration-sensitive prostate cancer: A meta-analysis of efficacy and safety
Authors: Minhao Zhang, Lilin Wan, Yong Yao, Rongzu Wu, Wenchao Li, Peng Gu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Within the last 10 years, multiple clinical randomized controlled trials in metastatic castration-sensitive prostate cancer patients (mCSPC) have demonstrated that androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) combination therapy is significantly superior to ADT alone. However, there are no guidelines that recommend the best clinical decisions with regard to multi-selective systemic therapy for mCSPC.Methods:Two independent researchers have conducted a systematic literature search of the Cochrane Central, Web of Science, Clinical Trials. gov and EU Clinical Trial Register databases with a search deadline of July 5, 2022. Hazard Ratio (HR) and 95% confidence intervals were analyzed and extracted for overall and progression-free survival and their subgroups, as well as the number of adverse events. Trial quality was assessed through the Risk of Bias Form and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, and systematic reviews and meta-analyses were carried out in strict accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) project.Results:8704 patients from 7 studies participated in trials comparing the effectiveness of 4 ADT combination treatments (including ADT + docetaxel, ADT + abiraterone acetate, ADT + enzalutamide, ADT + apalutamide) and ADT alone (ADT + placebo/no-treatment). The results suggested that ADT combination therapy was significantly better than ADT alone. Furthermore, based on the patient’s initial clinical characteristics and expected prognosis, we tentatively provided relevant optimal treatment options.Conclusions:ADT + ARAT outperformed ADT ± docetaxel in OS/PFS for mCSPC, with strategies tailored to patient profiles. Future work will leverage advanced analytics and standardized reporting; direct combination trials are critical for precise guidance.

1. Introduction

The Global Cancer Statistics 2020 indicated that prostate cancer had become the third most common cancer, representing 7.3% of new cancers, after breast and lung cancer, and accounting for approximately 7% of cancer-related deaths in men worldwide.[1] 76% prostate cancer patients were initially diagnosed as localized cancer,[2] but 30% patients developed cancer recurrence or even metastasis after initial treatment.[3] The primary standard care for recurrent prostate cancer is androgen deprivation therapy (ADT), but after 18 to 24 months of treatment, most progress to castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC).[4–6] Most prostate cancer-related deaths occur in patients who progress to metastases after receiving hormone therapy, that is, metastatic CRPC, which carries a 5-year survival rate of 30%.[2,7]

The challenge of how to effectively prolong patients’ treatment time and delay biochemical recurrence and progressive metastasis has become a pressing problem in the clinical field. Currently, large RCTs provided several valuable therapeutic strategies. The results suggested that the multisystemic therapy combinations were more effective in improving patient prognosis and delaying castration-resistance compared with ADT alone in metastatic castration-sensitive prostate cancer (mCSPC).[8–22] Efficacy differences may be attributed to mutations in androgen receptor pathway-associated genes.[23] Currently, ADT combined with the chemotherapy drug docetaxel,[8–13] the androgen synthesis inhibitor abiraterone acetate,[14–16,19] the androgen receptor inhibitors enzalutamide[17,18,20] and apalutamide[21,22] have been shown to improve mCSPC patient survival and life quality under hormone-sensitive conditions.

Nevertheless, due to the lack of first-level evidence on posttreatment survival indicators, progression indicators and adverse effects, the Prostate Cancer Clinical Guidelines Committee is still unable to recommend a specific drugs combination that targeted to patient characteristics. Consequently, there have been a few meta-analyses that have attempted to explore ADT drug combinations in the mCSPC to clarify the best treatment options.[24–28] However, for some reasons, including publication time,[24–28] disease-related indicators,[24,26] and subgroup analysis, there is no general academic acceptance of the conclusions.

This study is intended to explore and clarify more comprehensively the effectiveness, safety and affordability of various systemic treatment strategies for mCSPC through more survival, progression and adverse effect indicators and their subgroup analysis. Overall, it is expected that the above results will provide a valid theoretical basis for optimal clinical decisions for mCSPC.

2. Methods

This study is based on a systematic review and meta-analysis of PRISMA.[29]

2.1. Search strategy

Two independent researchers searched the Cochrane Central, Web of Science, Clinical Trials. gov and EU Clinical Trial Register databases, respectively, until July 5,2022. The search keywords or formula were (“prostate cancer” OR “Metastatic Castration-Sensitive Prostate Cancer” OR “mCSPC”) AND (“enzalutamide/MDV-3100” OR “apalutamide/ARN-509” OR “abiraterone/CB-7598” OR “docetaxel/RP-56976”) OR (“hormone” OR “castration” OR “androgen”). Additionally, all studies were restricted to human clinical studies and the titles and abstracts of each study were independently reviewed by 2 independent researchers to exclude clinical trials that were not meaningful to us. Since this study was on the basis of published studies, ethical approval was not required.

2.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All included studies were in accordance with Patient-Intervention-Comparison-Outcome-Study design (PICOS) principles.[29] We included clinical RCTs of mCSPC patients receiving first-line treatment and excluded cluster and dose-escalation trials. These mCSPC patients were given ADT in combination with the additional drugs (docetaxel, abiraterone acetate, enzalutamide and apalutamide) or ADT alone (placebo/no-treatment), where ADT included orchiectomy, luteinizing hormone-releasing hormone agonists and antagonists, and estrogen. Patients who have previously received local treatment for prostate cancer without progression or who have been diagnosed with new metastatic disease based on conventional imaging are eligible (Table 1). Furthermore, combinations of noninterest drugs, non-mCSPC patients, lack of or difficult-to-obtain outcome information, registration of trials without results, as well as case reports, letters, meeting abstracts, reviews, animal studies and expert commentaries were excluded.

2.3. Risk of bias assessment

This study applied the Cochrane Collaboration tool Review Manager’s Methodological Quality Assessment Form (Risk of Bias Form) to assess the risk of bias in the valid results of individual experiments. Moreover, we assessed the quality of clinical trials supported by the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), a star rating system (semi-quantitative) that grants up to 9 stars when assessing study quality, including selectivity, comparability and exposure.

2.4. Data extraction and analysis

Two independent researchers separately extracted basic information (Table 1), patient clinical characteristics (Table 2), and indicators of survival (OS, low-volume OS, high-volume OS, rPFS, low-volume rPFS, high-volume rPFS, PSA-PFS), progression (gleason score < 8, gleason score ≥ 8, no metastasis (M0), distant metastasis (M1), visceral metastases, bone metastases) and adverse effects (all grade adverse events, ≥3 grade adverse events, serious adverse events) after treatment from clinical studies.

2.5. Statistical analysis

STATA v15. software is used for statistical analysis of hazard Ratio (HR), including overall survival (OS), low-volume OS, high-volume OS, radiographic progression-free survival (rPFS), low-volume rPFS, high-volume rPFS, Prostate specific antigen-progression free survival (PSA-PFS) and subgroup analysis of every progress indicators.[30] Dichotomous variables were selected for analysis on adverse reaction by Review Manager 5.3 and the combined effect was estimated by the DerSimonian-Laird method. Confidence intervals (CI) for all the above studies were 95% and heterogeneity was evaluated using the Chi-square test or Cochrane Q test, with no significant heterogeneity when I2 < 50% and P > .10, then a fixed-effects model was used to assess the relevant data. Otherwise, a random-effects model was used.[31] Moreover, the risk of bias was assessed for the included studies according to the Cochrane Framework.[32] And all correlation analysis results were considered statistically significant when P < .05.

3. Results

3.1. Trials selection and characterization

The search and selection process is shown in Figure 1, where 2 independent researchers search to identify 10,877 studies, and after excluding 3552 duplicates and 7226 irrelevant articles, 99 articles were eventually downloaded. Seven large clinical RCTs containing 15 relevant articles were identified through manual reading and analysis. The 7 included trials were multicenter phase 3 RCTs published between 2013 and 2022, involving a total of 8704 mCSPC patients treated with ADT alone (4534) or combination (4170) (Table 1). As the main bias was mostly from the open-label study design, significant characteristics of the population included in the trials were reported in Table 2.

3.2. Overall survival and disease-volume subgroup analysis

The OS of the relevant studies were included and initially analyzed in order of treatment effect from highest to lowest: apalutamide (HR = 0.65, 95% CI = 0.53–0.79), abiraterone acetate (HR = 0.66, 95% CI = 0.53–0.78), enzalutamide (HR = 0.66, 95% CI = 0.56–0.78), docetaxel (HR = 0.79, 95% CI = 0.71–0.89) (Fig. 2A). The combination therapy significantly improved the mCSPC patients’ OS compared to ADT alone, and abiraterone acetate, enzalutamide and apalutamide had similar effects. Subsequently, subgroup analysis based on volume of disease revealed that only enzalutamide and apalutamide significantly improved overall survival in low-volume mCSPC, with treatment effects ranging from apalutamide (HR = 0.53, 95% CI = 0.35–0.79) to enzalutamide (HR = 0.55, 95% CI = 0.40–0.77) (Fig. 2A). For high volume disease, all 4 combinations were superior to ADT alone, with efficacy ranked abiraterone acetate (HR = 0.62, 95% CI = 0.52–0.74), apalutamide (HR = 0.70, 95% CI = 0.52–0.88), enzalutamide (HR = 0.71, 95% CI = 0.59–0.85), docetaxel (HR = 0.73, 95% CI = 0.63–0.84) (Fig. 2A). This indicated that for high volume mCSPC, ADT combined with abiraterone acetate was more effective than other combinations.

Analysis of survival indicators. (A) Overall survival and its subgroup analysis. (B) Progression-free survival and its subgroup analysis.

Moreover, we found a crossover factor between ADT alone and combined treatment detailed in the TITAN final results.[21,22] And LATITUDE[16,19] also had a 10% crossover interference, but exact data have not been reported. If the influence of crossover factors was taken into account, apalutamide showed best therapeutic effect regardless of disease volume (OS: 0.52, 0.42–0.64; low-volume OS: 0.34, 0.22–0.53; high-volume OS: 0.61, 0.49–0.78). However, as data on crossover in other experiments have not been reported and thus cannot be compared.

3.3. Progression-free survival and disease-volume subgroup analysis

We found that previous meta-analyses were ambiguous on the definition of progression free survival (PFS). Consequently, we divided it into radiographic-clinical progression-free survival (rcPFS) and PSA-PFS. rcPFS is defined as patients experiencing imaging progression (bone scan suggesting 2 new skeletal lesions; CT suggesting a 20% increase in the total diameter of the target lesion) and clinical progression (worsening of disease-related symptoms or new cancer-related complications), with a predominance of imaging progression.[33] PSA-PFS was described as a 25% increase in PSA progression from baseline values, or an absolute increase of > 2 ng/mL after 12 weeks of treatment, confirmed by a second test result at 3 weeks or more.[33]

All combination treatments were more effective in prolonging mCSPC patients’ rcPFS compared to ADT alone. Their efficacy was ranked from highest to lowest enzalutamide (HR = 0.40, 95% CI = 0.34–0.46), abiraterone acetate (HR = 0.47, 95% CI = 0.39–0.55), apalutamide (HR = 0.48, 95% CI = 0.39–0.60), and docetaxel (HR = 0.67, 95% CI = 0.60–0.74) (Fig. 2B). There are no subgroup analyses of abiraterone acetate due to the absence on disease volume for PFS in the LATITUDE trial.[16,19] The study discovered that enzalutamide (low: HR = 0.25, 95% CI = 0.14–0.46; high: HR = 0.43, 95% CI = 0.33–0.57) was significantly more effective than docetaxel (low: HR = 0.74, 95% CI = 0.61–0.91; high: HR = 0.60, 95% CI = 0.52–0.70) and apalutamide (low: HR = 0.36, 95% CI = 0.22–0.57; high: HR = 0.53, 95% CI = 0.41–0.67), regardless of whether the mCSPC patient’s disease volume was high or low (Fig. 2B). The difference was that the efficacy, as measured by PSA-PFS, was from highest to lowest apalutamide (HR = 0.27, 95% CI = 0.22–0.33), abiraterone acetate (HR = 0.31, 95% CI = 0.27–0.36), enzalutamide (HR = 0.34, 95% CI = 0.29–0.39), docetaxel (HR = 0.58, 95% CI = 0.42–0.79) (Fig. 2B).

3.4. Subgroup analysis of progression indicators related to overall survival

For the first time, this study conduct the survival subgroup analysis of the patients’ Gleason score and metastatic status (M0, M1, visceral metastases and bone metastases). For all initial patients with Gleason score ≥ 8, ADT combination therapy was significantly better than ADT alone. The efficacy of apalutamide, abiraterone acetate and enzalutamide was essentially similar and all were superior to docetaxel (Fig. 3). When the Gleason score was < 8, the efficacy of apalutamide, enzalutamide and docetaxel was similar, while the results for abiraterone acetate were not statistically significant (Fig. 3). Subsequently, the correlation between the patient’s metastatic status at first diagnosis and treatment choice was further explored. The ADT combination in M1 patients had better outcomes, in descending order of abiraterone acetate (HR = 0.61, 95% CI = 0.49–0.77), apalutamide (HR = 0.68, 95% CI = 0.55–0.85),

enzalutamide (HR = 0.69, 95% CI = 0.52–0.91), and docetaxel (HR = 0.76, 95% CI = 0.62–0.92) (Fig. 3). For M0 patients, ADT + apalutamide (HR = 0.39, 95% CI = 0.22–0.69) was superior to ADT alone, while the efficacy of the remaining 3 combinations was not statistically significantly different from ADT alone (Fig. 3). 3.5. Assessment of adverse reactions in ADT combination and alone therapy

Analysis of all grade adverse events to the combination treatment identified increased adverse reactions in patients with enzalutamide (HR = 1.03, 95% CI = 1.01–1.05) and docetaxel (HR = 1.28, 95% CI = 1.17–1.41) compared to ADT alone; the rest were not statistically different (Fig. 4). Subsequently, further analysis of ≥ 3 grade adverse events revealed that docetaxel (HR = 1.78, 95% CI = 1.49–2.13), abiraterone acetate (HR = 1.37, 95% CI = 1.27–1.48) and apalutamide (HR = 1.18, 95% CI = 1.04–1.35) had a higher risk of adverse events than ADT alone (Fig. 4). And no significant trend has been observed for enzalutamide (HR = 1.13, 95% CI = 0.81–1.57). Finally the correlation between treatment options and serious adverse reactions was explored, ranging from the safest to the least safe treatment was enzalutamide (HR = 1.09, 95% CI = 0.83–1.47), abiraterone acetate (HR = 1.28, 95% CI = 1.07–1.54), apalutamide (HR = 1.34, 95% CI = 1.09–1.65) and docetaxel (HR = 29.11, 95% CI = 1.37–620) (Fig. 4).

4. Discussions

Prostate cancer is currently the primary malignancy that threatens men’s lives with a good prognosis for early stage patients,[34] and a high mortality rate for late stage patients due to drug resistance and progressive metastasis.[35] ADT combined with docetaxel or androgen receptor axis targeted agents (ARAT), including enzalutamide, abiraterone acetate and apalutamide, has been proved to have a significantly better prognostic outcome than ADT alone in large randomized controlled trials (RCTs) of mCSPC. Regrettably, despite intensive discussion by the international prostate cancer experts team on the best treatment options for docetaxel, enzalutamide, abiraterone acetate and apalutamide in terms of disease volume, posttreatment recurrence, etc, there is still no general consensus.[36] Consequently, we have conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of clinical RCTs in mCSPC patients’ systemic therapy. Compared to the previous meta-analyses, we discovered several interesting phenomena.

This study has conducted a comprehensive search, rigorous assessment and synthesis in eligible clinical RCTs. Compared to the published relevant meta-analyses, we firstly updated the latest final results of ARCHES 2022[18] and TITAN 2021,[22] which was not available in the previous meta-analyses. Secondly, this study has assessed the phenomenon of important indicators on overall survival at initial diagnosis (including gleason scores, M1, M0, bone metastases and visceral metastases). Thirdly, for every prognostic-related indicators, including OS, PFS and Androgen receptor (AR), we have analyzed them from multiple perspectives and subgroups to obtain a more comprehensive conclusion.

However, due to the limitations of our study, the results should be interpreted within the context of them. Firstly, there are biases in the design and data share of the RCTs, such as differences in open-label trials, study populations and follow-up times, which may have biased long-term efficacy estimates. Secondly, many of our results were dependent on indirect comparisons, which are not a complete substitute for the conclusions obtained when measuring random data. Thirdly, the variability in indicator definitions (rcFPS and volume of disease) and outcome measures across clinical RCTs has limited our confidence in the results of the subgroup analyses. Fourthly, in the comparison of adverse analyses, ARAT had a better safety than docetaxel, but this should take into account the effect of the difference in duration of use between the 2. Finally, given the analysis of crossover in TITAN, we found that crossover in the experimental subgroups had a significant effect on their final conclusions. However, none of the other remaining RCTs have excluded the effect of crossover. Therefore, our discussion of this study results will take place in the context of limitations and effectively incorporate relevant conclusions from other related meta-analyses.

For OS after treatment, the efficacy of ADT combined with enzalutamide, abiraterone acetate or apalutamide was similar, but enzalutamide or apalutamide were more effective in low-volume patients and abiraterone acetate was more effective in high-volume patients. This conclusion was slightly different from the results of Sathianathen et al[26] who considered ADT + enzalutamide to be the first option at either low or high tumor volume. Subsequently, an indepth exploration for progression-free survival has revealed that ADT + enzalutamide has the most significant contribution to delaying patient imaging and clinical progression, with results essentially similar to the study by Brawley et al[24] However, due to the vague definitions and large heterogeneity of rcPFS across studies, we first presented PSA-PFS and discovered that apalutamide better alleviated PSA progression in mCSPC compared to enzalutamide and abiraterone acetate. The patient’s gleason score and metastatic status at primary diagnosis were included and analyzed for the first time in this study as key factors in predicting and assessing mCSPC prognosis. For gleason scores ≥ 8, gleason scores < 8, M1 or bone metastases, ADT combined with enzalutamide, abiraterone acetate and apalutamide was similarly effective and significantly better than ADT + docetaxel or ADT alone. Additionally, ADT + apalutamide had better efficacy in M0 patients, and docetaxel or abiraterone was more effective in patients with visceral metastases. In summary, the combination of ADT was significantly better than ADT alone, and the improvement in outcome was less obvious with ADT + docetaxel (except visceral metastases). The optimal choice of ADT + ARAT is based on the patient’s volume of tumor and the expected direction of prognosis improvement.

Shariat et al[28] explored the different adverse outcomes of ADT combination therapy vs ADT alone, including all grade, ≥3 grade and serious adverse reactions. Their study suggested that there was no statistical difference in the type and incidence of post-treatment adverse effects between ADT combination and ADT alone, but that apalutamide most likely had the lowest rate of all adverse events.[28] Our results differ somewhat from the above in that ADT + docetaxel has been defined to have a higher incidence of adverse reactions (all grade, ≥3 grade and serious adverse reactions) compared with other combinations or ADT alone, which is consistent with the results of Brawley et al[24] Some differences in posttreatment adverse effects may be due to the fact that the article by Shariat et al included 9 large RCTs and the RCTs’ stages were published before 2021, whereas we included 7 large RCTs and the ARCHES and TITAN clinical trials have recently updated their prognostic data. Moreover, ADT + enzalutamide treatment had the lowest incidence of all grades and serious adverse reactions, while apalutamide had the lowest incidence of grade ≥ 3 adverse reactions in the absence of enzalutamide clarity.

As previously mentioned, each combination treatment regimen is optimal for a particular clinical situation or feature. Therefore, based on the results of the meta-analysis, we tentatively provide an optimal treatment decision table based on the initial clinical characteristics and expected prognosis of mCSPC patients (Table 3). However, due to the biased nature of the analysis, the results are for information purposes only and are not strictly indicative. But what could be clear is that the clinical efficacy of ADT + ARAT is significantly higher than that of ADT alone and ADT + docetaxel, and that it assisted urologists and mCSPC patients in making individualized and optimal decisions.

The androgen receptor is a key driver in prostate cancer development and progression. AR inhibitors suppressed tumor growth by blocking the androgen signaling pathway. Related studies have found that the ligand-binding domain (LBD) F877L mutation converts enzalutamide from antagonist to agonist, which activates AR signaling and leads to significant reductions in response rates to enzalutamide[37–40]; the T878A mutation in the LBD enables the AR to be activated by glucocorticoids and bypasses the inhibitory effects of abiraterone, leading to a significant reduction in its efficacy.[39–41] Moreover, mutations in the N-terminal structural domains (NTD, including E255K and Q58L) and DNA-binding domains (DBD, including R629Q and K580R) can affect the AR transcriptional activity, and transcriptional regulation of downstream genes, which ultimately affects the efficacy of AR inhibitors.[40,41] To overcome AR mutation resistance, relevant studies have actively explored multi-drug combination therapeutic approaches, novel AR inhibitors, and new AR mutation downstream targets. Combination of AR inhibitors with PI3K/AKT inhibitors or immune checkpoint inhibitors, such as PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors ARV-110 is a proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTAC) molecule that targets AR with higher affinity for mutant AR, and it is being evaluated in clinical trials for its efficacy against mutant AR.[42] Inhibition of AR-regulated downstream genes (e.g., MYC, PSA) or epigenetic modifiers (e.g., BET) is also an effective strategy. BET inhibitors have shown antitumor activity in AR-mutant prostate cancer models.[43] The emergence of novel therapeutic modalities can effectively ameliorate the drug-resistant phenomenon that occurs after ADT alone or combined therapy, and provide a forward-looking perspective in prostate cancer treatment.

5. Conclusions

The results suggested that ADT combined with ARAT had a higher OS and PFS benefit compared to ADT alone and ADT + docetaxel. Moreover, our results indicated that the choice of specific treatment strategy was highly operational and flexible, depending on the clinical characteristics and prognostic expectations of the mCSPC patients. However, due to unavoidable research limitations, in future studies, our group will utilize machine learning, Bayesian statistics, and other data analysis methods to extract more information from the available data and improve the reliability and comparability of results. And based on reporting guidelines such as CONSORT or STROBE, we hope to establish a standardized reporting framework to ensure comparable results across studies and to standardize the design and presentation of results in combination therapy studies. Moreover, we look forward to the results of clinical RCTs that directly compare preexisting combination treatments in order to facilitate more precise clinical guidance.
