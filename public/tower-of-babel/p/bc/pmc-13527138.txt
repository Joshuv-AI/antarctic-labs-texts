AstroOphthalmology: ocular anterior segment
Authors: Kasra Cheraqpour, Mansoor Shahriari, Mahya Ghazi, Kiana Hassanpour, Sina Asgari, Shaqayeq Cheraqpour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spaceflight can adversely affect ocular health. Most studies in this area are primarily focused on spaceflight-associated neuro-ocular syndrome. Nonetheless, there exist numerous additional potential causes of ocular damage and concern. The purpose of this report is to provide a thorough review of various ocular anterior segment manifestations and concerns that occur during and after spaceflight, including dry eye disease, lens-related problems, glaucoma, and corneal traumatic and emergency conditions.

Introduction

Spaceflight exposes astronauts to an extreme environment characterized by microgravity and elevated cosmic radiation, which induces profound biological adaptations. On Earth, gravity-dependent hydrostatic forces help to preserve normal hydrodynamics, and the magnetosphere protects against harmful cosmic radiation1. Microgravity and cosmic radiation ultimately contribute to a deterioration in ocular health, representing a considerable hazard to astronauts. Notably, visual impairment is recognized as the leading health risk related to spaceflight. Astronauts reported visual changes at rates of 23% for short-duration missions and 48% for long-duration missions, respectively2. The space environment has the potential to impact both the anterior and posterior segments of the eye (Fig. 1). The majority of studies in this regard are primarily focus on spaceflight-associated neuro-ocular syndrome (SANS), a complex of structural changes in the ocular posterior segment and neurologic system. These changes include unilateral or bilateral optic disc edema, chorioretinal folds, thickening of the retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL), and flattening of the posterior globe leading to hyperopia3. Nevertheless, in addition to SANS, there are numerous other potential causes of ocular damage and concerns, such as dry eye disease, that may be distressing and even disabling in space. Eye irritation, foreign bodies, epiphora, conjunctival injection, periorbital edema, puffy eyes/eye fullness, and eye burning sensation are among other conditions reported by the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA)6,7. In One study, ocular symptoms not associated with SANS documented during missions on the International Space Station (ISS) and Space Shuttle (STS) were assessed4. The ISS dataset included data from 63 missions spanning the years 2000 to 2020. The STS dataset featured 135 Shuttle missions between 1981 and 20114. Ocular events were reported in 83 (61.5%) of STS and 41 (65.1%) of ISS missions, respectively. In total, 242 ocular complaints documented across these missions. The most common ocular complaints were eye irritation (n = 80, 33.1%), presence or sensation of foreign body (n = 55, 22.7%), dry eye disease (n = 38, 15.7%), and epiphora (n = 19, 7.85%). Of all ocular problems, 9 (3.72%) were considered to be of higher severity, including keratitis, chemical burn, and corneal abrasion. However, none of these cases necessitated evacuation from a mission4. Based on these statistics and other similar reports, we have decided to allocate the second report of our series, AstroOphthalmology—studying the effects of space on the eye—, to the ocular anterior segment. Previously, we have discussed the basics and pathophysiology of space-induced ocular changes elsewhere8. Although a few reports of corneal diseases are scarce and scattered in the literature, classification of space-induced ocular changes based on anatomical location is a novel idea. We believe this type of classification is more attractive, practical, and informative for ophthalmologists, since it fits their current routine clinical practice and field of interest. The aim of this report is to provide a comprehensive review of various ocular anterior segment manifestations and concerns during and after spaceflight, including dry eye disease, lens-related issues, glaucoma, and corneal traumatic and emergency conditions (Fig. 2). This report will certainly serve as a benchmark for researchers, clinicians, and ophthalmologists, particularly those who specialize in anterior segment (e.g., cornea and glaucoma subspecialists).Fig. 2The triad of microgravity, cosmic radiation, and cabin environment leads to different ocular anterior segment manifestations and concerns.Figure created in Microsoft PowerPoint.

Dry eye disease

Signs or symptoms of dry eye disease (DED) are reported by more than 30% of crewmembers from ISS missions and 15% of Shuttle crewmembers during their mission9. Considering the strict screening and overall good health of spaceflight participants before their missions, the high rate of DED may be connected to microgravity exposure and a set of related factors5,10. Notably, disruption of the tear film leads to a compromised ocular surface and elevates the risk of epithelial injury, abrasions, and ulceration11 (Fig. 3).Fig. 3A schematic image of the ocular surface and tear film.Figure created in Microsoft PowerPoint.

Risk factors

The impact of microgravity on the pathophysiology of dry eye disease is considerable and can be understood as a cascade of direct and indirect physiological alterations. On Earth, gravity facilitates the drainage of tears through the lacrimal puncta and pulls thicker, mucus-rich tears downward. In microgravity, tear drainage becomes less effective. Tears may pool, debris is not cleared by gravity, and the precorneal tear film may become inconsistent12,13. It can cause changes in the distribution of nutrients, cytokines, and inflammatory cells around the ocular surface, potentially creating localized inflammatory conditions that favor the development of DED14,15. On Earth, gravity creates a hydrostatic pressure gradient in the vascular and lymphatic systems, resulting in higher pressure in the lower body vessels compared to the head. Microgravity eliminates this gradient, initiating a domino effect. In the absence of gravitational force, roughly 1.5–2 liters of fluid redistribute from the lower limbs towards the head within the initial hours or days. As a consequence, the veins that drain the orbit become engorged. This leads to periorbital edema and, significantly, edema within the orbital tissues themselves. Swollen orbital fat and tissues can mechanically compress and impair the function of the lacrimal gland, reducing the efficiency of aqueous tear secretion16,17. The meibomian glands may also struggle to secrete the lipid layer of the tear film due to eyelid compression and edema. Another important factor is altered lymphatic drainage. The lymphatic system, which depends on gravity and muscle contractions, becomes sluggish in microgravity. This inefficiency impedes the clearance of inflammatory mediators and metabolic waste from the ocular surface and orbit, thereby exacerbating local inflammation. Additionally, the body’s adaptation to weightlessness involves an alteration in sensorimotor and autonomic control systems. Stress and neural adaptation can disrupt the autonomic nervous system balance, which may in turn affect the basal tear secretion, a process regulated by neural pathways18.

The environments within spacecraft and space stations can affect tear film health through multiple factors. A high rate of air exchange (45–77 times per hour), elevated CO2 levels, artificial lighting, significant screen time, and humidity of around 60% can lead to enhanced tear film evaporation and ocular surface inflammation19–21. It should be noted that 60% humidity exceeds the recommended 30% relative humidity for preventing eye dryness. However, some studies indicate that the risk of dry eye begins to rise significantly even when relative humidity drops below levels as high as 70%22. While higher humidity levels in habitable environments would reduce tear film evaporation rates, they may raise the risk of microbial growth and fire due to water condensation on electrical equipment23.

Adequate sleep is a critical factor for ocular surface regeneration and health24. Sleep deficiency or poor sleep quality can contribute to DED development25,26. Astronauts often report challenges with both falling asleep and sustaining sleep, primarily due to the lack of a natural day-night cycle, and a noisy, bright environment. Besides sleep disorders, the incidence of behavioral and mood disorders increases with longer mission durations. These disorders are related to dry eye conditions and vice versa, although the exact mechanism is still unclear. A possible explanation is that the symptoms of DED interfere with patients’ daily lives, which in turn affects their mood26–28.

Management

Eyedrops are the most frequently utilized treatment for tear-related disorders on Earth. However, they are not suitable for long-duration spaceflights due to payload limitations and shelf life. The use of eye drops in microgravity presents a unique challenge, as the process of instilling them into the eye is difficult. Instead, the fluid must be wicked into the eye by leveraging surface tension through direct contact with the dropper bottle, which contaminates the bottle. Another challenge stemming from the behavior of liquids in a weightless environment is the uncontrolled application process; surface tension prevents the formation of individual droplets. Traditional dispensing methods may result in overdosing or medication wastage11. To mitigate this issue, NASA has collaborated with various companies to develop micro-volume dispensers that effectively regulate the delivery of eye drops in space29. Ointments and gels are simpler to apply and are currently employed on the ISS for infection management, although vision obstruction is a disadvantage. On the other hand, formulations fortified with hyaluronic acid deliver superior lubrication and protection. Notably, high molecular weight hyaluronic acid lessens oxidative stress, possesses anti-inflammatory effects, and enhances tear retention, making it particularly effective for addressing DED in extreme conditions30,31. Lipid-based formulations combat evaporative dry eye by stabilizing the lipid layer and curtailing tear evaporation, which is vital for reducing tear film instability induced by microgravity31. Other therapeutic methods consist of blink exercises, heating and massaging the Meibomian glands, and maintaining cleanliness around the eyes32,33. Overall, it is advisable to explore methods that focus on restoring the tear film’s physiological function9. Nanoparticle-based tear substitutes represent a promising new avenue in the management of DED34.

In line with future perspectives, ocular neurostimulation is a viable option. Intranasal neurostimulation therapy has emerged as a promising alternative to existing treatment modalities for DED. This approach utilizes the nasolacrimal reflex pathway to not only enhance tear production through increased lacrimation but also to target other components of the tear film, including mucin and meibum secretion, thereby promoting tear film homeostasis35. The initial devices were designed to be inserted into the nasal cavity to deliver microcurrents to the anterior ethmoidal nerves36. Current adaptations are less invasive and stimulate the ethmoidal nerve mechanically, rather than electrically, using an oscillating tip placed against the lateral aspect of the nose to induce lacrimation and secretion from the meibomian glands37. Another advantage of the new models over the former technology is the lack of requirement for disposable materials, as modern technology employs external stimulation and one device can be utilized by the entire crew37,38. Only minor side effects, such as headache, sneezing, and occasional nasal discomfort, are reported for these devices39.

Corneal traumatic and emergency conditions

Beyond chronic issues like DED, the unique environment of space also presents significant risks for acute corneal trauma and emergencies. Astronauts aboard the ISS are not immune to medical emergencies, particularly corneal abrasions, perforations, and chemical burns. While they receive extensive training for emergency situations, they face inherent complexities when dealing with eye injuries. Although some astronauts are trained for ocular experiments, more complex ophthalmic procedures, such as the removal of a foreign body using a 25-gauge needle, may be beyond their training scope40. Additionally, the ISS operates with limited pharmaceutical resources, necessitating the proper use of medications and materials to address a range of potential problems41. Teleophthalmology has been described as a potential solution to emergency conditions. However, as missions move beyond the Moon, the limitations of teleophthalmology that relies on Earth become increasingly pronounced, transitioning from a highly effective tool to one that is significantly limited. The light-speed delay to Mars, which varies between 4 and 24 min one way, fundamentally disrupts the model of real-time telemedicine, revealing profound shortcomings. The key advantage of teleophthalmology is its provision of live, interactive guidance. However, this interactive loop is compromised on Mars. A 20-minute round-trip delay results in a simple question-and-answer process taking more than 40 min. Any procedure that demands visual feedback turns into an annoying slow, stepwise process. Thus, the diagnosis and treatment plans may take hours or even a full day to be delivered. In a real ocular emergency, ground-based ophthalmologists are unable to communicate with the crewmembers in real time. Therefore, they must depend on pre-recorded training and potentially AI-guidance software, lacking the ability to ask for immediate clarification. This situation significantly raises the risk of iatrogenic injury. Additionally, the realization that expert help is not just a call away imposes a significant psychological burden. This can result in fear of making mistakes and may delay necessary treatment. The solution does not consist of faster communication, which is physically unachievable. To counter these disadvantages, ophthalmic devices must incorporate AI capable of real-time, onboard analysis. This may allow for immediate, expert-level interpretation. Crewmembers should undergo much more intensive training with high-fidelity simulators that cover a wide variety of pathologies and procedures. Nevertheless, teleophthalmology will continue to be a valuable resource for data review and planning; however, for urgent ocular care on Mars, the mission must be prepared to function independently of Earth, which is one of the most significant challenges of human interplanetary flight.

Corneal foreign body

The process of removing dust from the ocular surface is expected to be increasingly vital in microgravity, where larger dust particles and dense items like metals do not settle42,43. Hence, astronauts often report encountering corneal foreign bodies44.

Risk factors

In microgravity, suspended particles can infiltrate the eyes of crewmembers due to air currents within the spacecraft45. During extravehicular activities (EVAs), they will not be able to manually remove any debris that may be trapped within the suit, which can cause ongoing eye irritation. Moreover, EVAs on celestial bodies may result in dust that adheres to equipment and spacesuits entering the spacecraft, which may then float around the cabin in a microgravity setting40. The dust particles found on the Moon and Mars introduce specific challenges to ocular health as a result of their sharp, abrasive properties, high levels of adhesiveness, and reactive surfaces. Unlike dust found on Earth, which experiences weathering, celestial dust and larger particles maintain their jagged and highly reactive characteristics due to their creation in microgravity and vacuum conditions11. During the Apollo missions, astronauts frequently noted ocular discomfort and irritation stemming from lunar dust that infiltrated the spacecraft after EVAs. Measures taken to brush or vacuum dust off spacesuits before re-entering the cabin are largely unsuccessful, leading to considerable contamination in the cabin environment46,47. The development of visual symptoms and pathologies in astronauts goes beyond being a mere health issue; it poses a critical operational threat that has direct repercussions for mission effectiveness, safety, and success, particularly during long-duration missions. Because visual acuity is essential for navigation, EVA performance, fine-motor operational tasks, reading checklists on suit displays, manipulating complex tools, and conducting meticulous repairs, even brief impairment from foreign body sensation or corneal abrasion carries disproportionate mission risk40,45. Specifically, corneal disorders can induce severe discomfort, photophobia, and reflexive eyelid spasms. Even a minor central abrasion can lead to significant blurring, haze, or glare, which diminishes performance in all visually dependent tasks41. Furthermore, managing a corneal injury demands considerable time and assistance from another crewmember, effectively doubling the operational cost by necessitating two crewmembers to be off-line: one as the patient and one as the medic.

Management

When dealing with minor particles, artificial tears may serve to rinse the eye. If this approach fails, a space eyewash device present on the ISS can be employed, which entails flushing an eyewash solution through swimming goggles. For foreign bodies that are more persistent, the application of a topical anesthetic is recommended, along with gently sweeping the cornea’s surface using a cotton swab. In more critical and complicated scenarios, crewmembers may be instructed on the use of an eye spud or a 25-gauge needle to accurately extract foreign bodies. Following the removal of a foreign body, the use of prophylactic broad-spectrum antibiotic ointment is advised40.

Corneal abrasion

During the Space Shuttle Program and ISS through Expedition 13 in 2006, eye abrasions due to foreign bodies were identified in 70 out of 104 (67.3%) of the recorded ophthalmic medical conditions44.

Risk factors

Arid conditions disturb the delicate balance of tear film stability. A lack of humidity accelerates tear evaporation, resulting in a heightened sensitivity to foreign body sensation, as the tear film fails to adequately protect the corneal epithelium from external irritants. While the ISS is equipped with the Environmental Control and Life Support System (ECLSS) to ensure proper airflow in living quarters, there can be localized accumulations of CO2 around the facial area, especially when individuals are asleep. Prolonged exposure to CO2 may cause slight damage to the corneal epithelium. A diminished tear film increases the likelihood of corneal abrasions48, and these lesions are typically precursors to more serious issues like keratitis40.

Management

Currently, the diagnosis of corneal conditions on the ISS is based on direct ophthalmoscopy, a non-specialized device for this purpose. Although fluorescein strips and a blue light filter are accessible for the identification of corneal abrasions, a slit-lamp biomicroscope is unavailable for more detailed visualization10. Hence, equipping the ISS with a portable slit-lamp biomicroscope seems logical. Topical medications are available on the ISS (Table 1). However, their effectiveness may be affected by altered fluid dynamics in the bottle and a greater risk of contamination. Corneal infection

Infectious keratitis is a serious concern during spaceflight, aggravated by immune suppression, microgravity, and microbial contamination. Cases of keratitis and corneal ulcer are well-documented on the ISS40. Stress-induced immune dysfunction activates the hypothalamic pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and reduces cytotoxic T-cell activity, which compromises the ocular surface’s defenses against pathogens. In line with this concept, recurrence of herpes simplex virus (HSV) may pose a significant challenge during spaceflight, and it is recommended to administer a prophylactic antiviral therapy for astronauts who have experienced recurrent HSV infections or are at a high risk for reactivation49,50.

Microgravity disrupts the tear film, resulting in an increased risk of infection44,51. It has the potential to change the human microbiome, including that of the ocular surface. While there is a scarcity of direct studies focusing on the tear film microbiome in microgravity, terrestrial investigations underscore the critical role of a balanced ocular microbiome in preserving eye health52. Astronauts, whether they are inside a spaceship or a space station, do not have the opportunity to shift to a different environment. As a result, they are continuously exposed to aerosols and bioaerosols which accumulate in cabin53. Microbial biofilms, which are fundamentally more resilient than planktonic bacteria, represent a serious challenge in the confined environment of spacecraft. Studies show that Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Staphylococcus aureus produce denser biofilms under microgravity conditions, exhibiting increased virulence and resistance to antimicrobial treatments54. These adaptations, together with limited air circulation and sterilization difficulties, greatly amplify the risk of ocular infections11.

Corneal burn

Reports of chemical corneal burns during spaceflights are well-documented in the literature4,40,55. According to a review of ocular trauma associated with the NASA Space Shuttle Program and the ISS until Expedition 13 in 2006, there were six reported cases of chemical burns44. In microgravity, unexpected splashing or drifting of liquid particles may occur, thereby increasing the likelihood of ocular exposure and, subsequently, corneal chemical burns. A recent investigation into the components of dust collected by the ISS’s filtration system has found chemicals that may have detrimental effects on human health, with concentrations that exceed those typically found in an average U.S. household53.

The lack of dedicated eyewash stations complicates the prompt decontamination of the eyes after exposure to chemical irritants, which is a crucial management method for corneal chemical burns. The microgravity environment also modifies the natural flow of fluids, impacting the rinsing process and possibly obstructing the removal of chemical agents from the ocular surface. For more severe burns, the limited range of ophthalmic tools and medications available on the ISS may significantly restrict treatment options40. Effective management of chemical burns begins with extensive irrigation to dilute and bring the pH close to 7.0. After this initial phase, mild burns are managed with a treatment strategy similar to that for corneal abrasions. In cases of severe chemical corneal burns, it will be imperative to return to Earth for advanced treatments40.

Corneal perforation

Despite no reports of open-globe injuries in space so far, this absence does not rule out the possibility of such injuries happening40. During splashing down to Earth, crewmembers may undergo forces ranging from 3 to 5 G’s, which can lead to a sudden increase in intraocular pressure (IOP). If a crewmember is returning with a significant perforating eye injury, these increased pressures can exacerbate the existing damage. The rapid rise in IOP can cause additional complications, including hemorrhage, retinal detachment, or a worsening of the perforation, thereby posing serious risks to the already compromised ocular structures.

One of the main barriers to the accurate diagnosis and monitoring of corneal perforations in spaceflight is the absence of a slit-lamp biomicroscope. Also, vital resources, including bandage contact lenses, amniotic membrane, and corneal transplantation, cannot be accessed due to the restricted space and the insufficient number of specialized medical staff. In the case of a severe penetrating corneal injury, astronauts are instructed to apply an eye pad to both the injured and unaffected eyes, followed by the placement of a metal eye shield over the injured eye. Flight surgeons at mission control would subsequently be contacted to facilitate a possible evacuation. Notably, Cyanoacrylate glues may serve as temporary treatment prior to the availability of further interventions. This type of glue is conveniently found on the ISS and is a practical choice for treating small corneal perforations measuring under 3 mm40.

Glaucoma and intraocular pressure dynamics

Glaucoma is a group of eye diseases characterized by damage to the optic nerve, usually caused by elevated IOP56. This pressure is maintained by a fluid called aqueous humor, which constantly flows in and out of the eye. If the drainage system becomes inefficient, fluid builds up, and IOP rises, leading to nerve damage and vision loss. One of the factors with significant importance in this regard is anterior chamber depth (ACD)56.

Intraocular pressure

A temporary rise in IOP has been noted upon entering microgravity2,20. In the initial 15 min of exposure to microgravity, IOP showed a 92% increase relative to baseline measurements57, an extreme but very short-term change that may not reflect steady state conditions in space. Suggested explanations for this early rise in IOP encompass cephalad fluid redistribution, choroidal expansion, and heightened episcleral venous pressure2,3,20,58,59. However, these mechanisms remain hypothetical; direct evidence linking these phenomena to IOP in microgravity is lacking. In one study, IOP was monitored in a Korean astronaut throughout spaceflight60. IOP was recorded every three hours during daylight (six times daily) across two separate days in space, with three measurements taken for each eye at each time point. In total, 72 measurements were obtained during the space mission. To gather control IOP data, the same measurement protocol was utilized on the ground before the astronaut’s spaceflight. A contrasting pattern of diurnal variation in IOP was identified during spaceflight in comparison to ground measurements. The IOP at 7 AM was the lowest in a microgravity environment, while it was the highest on the ground60. In another study, five subjects were evaluated during 2-week missions on the Space Shuttle, consisting of three control individuals, one with glaucoma, and one with ocular hypertension (OHT)61. Baseline IOP was recorded before the flight, during the flight, and after the flight. It was found that IOP increased for all in-flight measurements relative to preflight values across all cases. Significantly, IOP returned to baseline levels postflight for all subjects, with the exception of the astronaut diagnosed with OHT. In the event that such results are confirmed in a broader sample of patients suffering from OHT, it may be advisable to implement prophylactic eye drops for OHT crewmembers to prevent considerable increases in IOP and the related complications of glaucoma61. While suggestive, these findings cannot be generalized without replication in larger cohorts, and no in-flight measurements of anterior chamber angle or aqueous outflow have been performed to substantiate the proposed mechanisms.

Numerous documentation from terrestrial analogues exists regarding acute changes in IOP for studies conducted over short durations, from 2 min to 48 h. In these studies, an immediate increase in IOP was recorded when the body was placed in a recumbent position. The increase in IOP ranged from 2 to 5 mm Hg depending on the various body angles62. IOP also demonstrated diurnal variations at a 10-degree position over the span of 48 h, indicating a decrease in IOP at night62.

Anterior chamber depth

While there have been no documented instances of astronauts developing angle closure glaucoma during or after space missions, considerations in this regard should be taken63. In one study, for astronauts on a six-month mission aboard the ISS, ACD was measured pre- and postflight using optical biometry64. Upon their immediate return from space, the ACD had decreased by a mean of 3%. This reduction in ACD remained below preflight levels for up to one year after returning to Earth. This finding must be interpreted with caution. The authors highlight the necessity for in-flight measurements of ACD and other ocular structural alterations34. Moreover, preflight UBM is capable of identifying those with a reduced angle recess area, a narrow iridocorneal angle, and a shallow ACD to prevent the potential acceleration of angle closure due to spaceflight34. To address the knowledge gap in this regard, terrestrial analogue studies are urgently needed. Optical biometry and anterior segment OCT should be incorporated into head down tilt bed rest protocols to determine whether sustained fluid shift produces measurable ACD shallowing. Furthermore, computational fluid dynamics models of aqueous humor flow, informed by astronaut ocular biometry, could predict the probability of angle closure under various degrees of IOP and lens position change.

The probability of experiencing drug-induced acute angle closure glaucoma (AACG) in a space environment is considerable, given that many anticholinesterase or cholinergic medications utilized by NASA for the treatment of space motion sickness. These agents cause mydriasis and forward displacement of the lens-iris diaphragm, raising the risk of AACG. In addition, a low-light environment can also instigate AACG, which may manifest during space missions65. The other point is lens-induced angle closure. Ionizing radiation experienced during prolonged space missions may lead to the development of cataracts, resulting from exposure to galactic cosmic radiation or solar particle events66. Localized alterations in the structured arrangement of lens cells, triggered by the oxidation of protein sulfhydryl groups and the formation of high-molecular-weight complexes, can result in phacolytic glaucoma63.

When addressing AACG, the primary step involves an immediate reduction of IOP via medical management. As laser iridotomy is unavailable during space missions, it becomes imperative to administer medications that lower IOP. It is recommended that angle assessment be part of all ophthalmic screenings conducted before spaceflight65. This should include detailed medical history, an examination of the optic nerve, and gonioscopy to identify anatomical risk factors for AACG67.

Lens-related issues

Cataract

Cataractogenesis stands out as the most prevalent finding associated with radiation exposure. The NASA Study of Cataract in Astronauts (NASCA) was a longitudinal investigation that examined the severity and development of nuclear, cortical, and posterior subcapsular lens (PSC) opacities68. The astronauts exposed to spaceflight demonstrated an increased rate and variability of cortical cataracts when compared to non-spaceflight-exposed astronauts and control groups, with age being adjusted, which are comparable with findings from another study69. Moreover, it is reported that the cataracts in astronauts were most frequently cortical in nature, while also noting that the cataracts in aviators from the United States Air Force and Navy were predominantly found in the PSC region70. Hence, other spaceflight factors (e.g., oxidative stress from altered metabolism and microgravity-related factors) may also contribute. Terrestrial laboratory models are indispensable for dissecting these mechanisms. Combined radiation-simulated microgravity experiments can test whether microgravity exacerbates radiation damage – a critical question for Mars missions.

Lens stability

Although lens dislocation is not explicitly mentioned in relation to spaceflight, the variety of ocular changes that occur in space may lead to an increased risk of lens dislocation or subluxation. Studies have demonstrated the molecular effects of space travel on the metabolic functions of ocular tissues, which could subsequently influence the laxity of the zonules that support the lens71,72. Notably, direct evidence of zonular biomechanical degradation in microgravity does not exist.

Terrestrial analogue research is urgently required to evaluate zonular integrity under spaceflight relevant conditions. Mechanical testing of zonules after exposure to simulated microgravity or proton radiation could quantify changes in elasticity and tensile strength. Animal models subjected to head down tilt or hindlimb unloading can provide histological and biochemical assessment of zonular structure.

Evaluations have been done on astronauts who had previously undergone cataract surgery with the placement of an intraocular lens (IOL) implant during extended missions: one astronaut had phacoemulsification and the insertion of an acrylic IOL on Earth. After fifteen months, he completed a six-month mission without any recorded changes in the position of the IOL during his spaceflight, maintaining excellent and stable vision during launch73. Also, IOLs appear to be secure for short-term space travel, as demonstrated by a 64-year-old NASA astronaut with bilateral IOLs who maintained stable vision during a two-week mission. The IOLs’ positions were unchanged pre- and post-mission, and subjective visual assessments were rated highly across all flight stages74. Moreover, another report has documented the stability of a single piece, posterior chamber phakic implantable collamer lens (ICL) during a 12-day mission to the ISS75. Conclusion

The anterior segment of the eye is particularly susceptible to the conditions of space. The pathophysiological triad comprising microgravity, radiation, and the cabin ecosystem results in a range of issues, from the persistent discomfort of dry eye to serious corneal infections and urgent traumatic situations. Conducting a comprehensive ophthalmological evaluation prior to flight, creating innovative diagnostic and therapeutic tools for use during flight, and enhancing our understanding of ocular physiology in space are essential for reducing these risks. Protecting the vision of astronauts is not only a medical issue but also a crucial requirement for the success of humanity’s future in interplanetary exploration.
