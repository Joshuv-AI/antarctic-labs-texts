Conclusiveness of biopsy techniques for diagnosis of bone and soft tissue tumours: a retrospective, single-centre observational study
Authors: Maria Anna Smolle, Johannes Rauscher, Florian Alexander Wenzl, Jasminka Igrec, Joanna Szkandera, Bernadette Liegl-Atzwanger, Andreas Leithner, Dimosthenis Andreou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGiven the differences between biopsy techniques, especially regarding the representativeness and amount of tissue obtained upon diagnostic workup of suspicious bone and soft tissue lesions, this study aimed at evaluating the diagnostic conclusiveness of different biopsy techniques at a tertiary sarcoma centre.Materials and methodsOverall, 222 consecutive patients (50.9% males, median age 63.3 [IQR 47.4–71.8] years) undergoing biopsy for a suspected bone or soft tissue lesion at a single centre between December 2021 and December 2023 were included. Biopsy techniques included core needle biopsy in the outpatient setting (without image guidance), sonography-guided and CT-guided and open biopsy. The conclusiveness rate of the first biopsy was defined as a histologically plausible result that enabled subsequent treatment planning.ResultsSoft tissue and bone lesions were present in 114 (51.4%) and 108 (48.6%) patients, respectively. Core needle (n = 85; 38.3%) and CT-guided biopsies (n = 61; 27.5%) were most commonly employed. Median interval from first presentation to biopsy was 14 (IQR 3–29) days for bone and 0 (IQR 0–17) days for soft tissue lesions, attributable to the long delay of CT-guided biopsies (bone: median 28 [IQR 15–47] days; soft tissue: median 18 [14–45] days; p < 0.001). Complications were highest following CT-guided biopsies for soft tissue lesions (n = 2/18; 11.1% [95% CI 1.4%–34.7%]) and sonography-guided biopsies for bone lesions (n = 1/5; 20.0% [95% CI 5.1%–71.6%]). Overall, the first biopsy was conclusive in 88.0% (95% CI 80.3%–93.4%; n = 95/108) and 88.6% (95% CI 81.3%–93.8%; n = 101/114) of bone and soft tissue lesions, respectively, with no difference between biopsy types. A repeated biopsy became necessary in 9.5% of patients (n = 21/222). None of the parameters investigated (BMI, age, lesion size, biopsy type, location) were associated with the conclusiveness rate of bone or soft tissue biopsies.ConclusionsThe time delay from initial presentation to biopsy varies with the biopsy technique employed, as do complication rates. Yet, all biopsy techniques—when applied according to clinical indication—achieve high diagnostic conclusiveness rates.Level of evidenceIV.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s10195-026-00956-0.

Introduction

Biopsy is one key component in diagnosing neoplasms of the musculoskeletal system. Generally, two different techniques can be differentiated, i.e. minimally invasive vs. open biopsies. The former can be performed as a core needle biopsy, with or without imaging guidance (sonography or computed tomography [CT] guided). For open biopsies, depending on the tissue configuration to be investigated, trocars, spoons or rongeurs can be used.

The decision for a biopsy technique depends on the size and location of the lesion, its composition (bone, soft tissues) and the physician’s preference and prevailing local policies [1]. In general, risk of contamination of surrounding tissues is minimized with minimally invasive biopsy techniques, yet material obtained with core needle biopsy is limited. Conversely, open biopsies allow obtaining more material for histopathological examination and biobanking but increase the likelihood of haematoma formation and contamination [2]. Biopsy tracts from open biopsies have to be resected en bloc with the surgical specimen [3], whereas those resulting from core needle biopsies can also remain untouched, especially in case of neoadjuvant treatment [4]. Given the differences between biopsy techniques, especially regarding the amount of tissue obtained and the representativeness of the specimen, it is valuable to know their diagnostic conclusiveness, need for re-biopsy and associated complications.

Thus, the aims of this study were to evaluate the diagnostic conclusiveness of different biopsy techniques for suspected bone and soft tissue lesions within a tertiary sarcoma centre and identify factors associated with conclusive biopsy results.

Materials and methods

In this retrospective observational study, all consecutive patients undergoing biopsy for a suspected bone or soft tissue lesion at a single sarcoma referral centre over a 24-month period (December 2021 to December 2023) were potentially eligible. Patients were excluded if a biopsy was indicated but not performed or if the lesion in question constituted a suspect pulmonary lesion. Potential patients were selected from the house-internal documentation system on diagnostic workup of bone and soft tissue lesions referred to the sarcoma centre.

Parameters obtained included age at biopsy, sex, body mass index (BMI), lesion-specific parameters (size, location, soft tissue/bone lesion) and type of imaging performed prior to referral to sarcoma centre as well as necessity for additional imaging. In addition, the suspected diagnosis, date of biopsy, type of biopsy (core needle biopsy without imaging [in outpatient clinic], sonography-guided biopsy, CT-guided biopsy, open biopsy) and complications related to the biopsy (graded according to the Clavien-Dindo classification [5]) were documented. The time interval between first appointment and initial biopsy was calculated. The histological result of the initial biopsy (insufficient/non-representative material, inconclusive, conclusive), reason for and type of a repeated biopsy, result of repeated biopsy (insufficient/non-representative material, inconclusive, conclusive), occurrence of a treatment delay and surgery for the histologically verified lesion were documented for each patient. All biopsies were reviewed at the multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting, which involved surgeons, pathologists, radiologists and oncologists. A biopsy was considered conclusive by the MDT if the histological workup revealed a plausible diagnosis allowing for subsequent treatment planning. In the case of misalignment between histology and radiology, the decision for a repeated biopsy was reached within the MDT.

During the study period, core needle biopsies at the outpatient clinic of the referral centre were usually carried out at initial presentation under local anaesthesia without image-guidance (device: TEMNO Evolution® Biopsy Device, Merit Medical UT, US); four to six samples were obtained. Open biopsies were scheduled within 2 weeks from initial presentation and performed in the operating theatre of our department under regional or general anaesthesia, with four to six sufficient tissue samples obtained. For sonography- and CT-guided biopsies, patients were subsequently referred to the radiology department [device: Bard® SureCut™ Biopsy Instrument, C.R. Bard Inc., Covington, GA, USA]. Sonography-guided biopsies were performed under local anaesthesia, and four to six tissue samples were obtained. CT-guided biopsies were either carried out under local anaesthesia (soft tissue lesions) or sedation/general anaesthesia (bone lesions), and two to four tissue samples were obtained. All biopsies were performed by consultants with expertise in diagnosis and treatment of musculoskeletal tumours (i.e., orthopaedic surgeons, radiologists) or by resident doctors under consultants’ supervision. The choice of biopsy technique was made according to the discretion of the treating orthopaedic oncologist in collaboration with the radiology department, based on lesion location, imaging characteristics, accessibility and anticipated need for image guidance. Superficial soft tissue lesions were preferentially subjected to outpatient core needle or sonography-guided biopsy, whereas deep-seated or osseous lesions were more commonly referred for CT-guided or open biopsy. All histopathological analyses were carried out by expert pathologists trained in musculoskeletal tumour pathology at a single tertiary sarcoma reference centre.

The initial search of the in-hospital database resulted in 230 patients. Of these, eight were subsequently excluded: two patients had a pulmonary lesions and underwent CT-guided biopsy, three patients underwent direct resection (1 with neurofibromatosis, 1 with intraarticular suspected localised-type tenosynovial giant cell tumour, 1 with a suspicious lymph node) and one received curopsy for simple bone cyst of distal fibula; the biopsy was cancelled for two patients. Statistical methods

Means and medians are presented with corresponding standard deviations (SD) and interquartile ranges (IQR). Numbers are given with valid percentages. Differences between groups were compared with the chi-square and Mann-Whitney U tests, as appropriate. Complication and conclusiveness rates of initial biopsy, as well as repeated biopsy rates, are provided with 95% confidence intervals (CI). For logistic regression analyses, missing variables (Supplementary Table 1) were imputed using random forest algorithm. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to identify factors associated with conclusiveness of biopsy. Multiple comparisons were accounted for using the Bonferroni method. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were carried out with Stata Version 16.1 for Mac (StataCorp, College Station, TX, US) and with R version 4.4.2 (or later).

Results

Of the 222 patients, 113 (50.9%) were male, and median age was 63.3 (IQR 47.4–71.8) years. One hundred fourteen (51.4%) patients had suspected soft tissue lesions, and 108 (48.6%) bone lesions. Most lesions were in the lower extremity (n = 82; 36.9%), followed by the upper extremity (n = 50; 22.5%), pelvis (n = 34; 15.3%) and spine/paraspinal tissues (n = 34; 15.3%). Median lesion size was 6.0 (IQR 3.8–8.6) cm. Soft tissue lesions were larger (median 6.9 [IQR 4.2–10.0] cm) than bone lesions (5.0 [IQR 3.2–7.0] cm; p = 0.002; Table 1). In 154 (69.4%) patients, the suspected diagnosis was a primary tumour, and in 68 (30.6%) patients, a metastatic lesion. The former was more frequently suggested in soft tissue lesions (94.7% vs. 42.6% of bone lesions; p = 0.002; Table 1). The most common biopsy type was a core needle biopsy in the outpatient setting (n = 85; 38.3%), followed by CT-guided (n = 61; 27.5%), open (n = 53; 23.9%) and sonography-guided (n = 23; 10.3%) biopsy. Of note, a treatment delay was observed in 12.6% (n = 26/207) of patients, usually due to an inconclusive biopsy requiring a repeated biopsy (n = 21), followed by initial patient refusal for diagnostic workup (n = 2), anticoagulants not stopped at the appropriate time (n = 1), and limited capacities for CT-guided (n = 1) or open biopsy (n = 1). Demographics were comparable between patients with bone and soft tissue lesions (Table 1). Diagnostic characteristics depending on biopsy type

Complication rates were comparable for all modalities used in soft tissue lesions (p = 0.390; Table 2). The first biopsy was conclusive in 95 (88.0% [95% CI 80.3%–93.4%]) of 108 bone lesions, and in 101 (88.6% [95% CI 81.3%–93.8%]) of 114 soft tissue lesions, with no difference depending on biopsy type (Table 2). Repeated biopsy and definite surgery

Overall, biopsy was repeated in 21 (9.5% [95% CI 2.8–9.3]) of all 222 patients (10 [9.3%; 95% CI 4.5%–16.4%] with bone lesions and 11 [9.7%; 95% CI 4.9%–16.6%] with soft tissue lesions; Figs. 1 and 2, Table 2), while 5 with inconclusive initial biopsy either underwent definite surgery (2 with a bone lesion, 1 with a soft tissue lesion) or active surveillance (1 with a bone lesion, 1 with a soft tissue lesion; Figs. 1 and 2).Fig. 2Flow chart showing patient journey through the diagnostic workup, including initial and repeated biopsies, focusing on patients with soft tissue lesions

Most commonly, the repeated biopsy was open (n = 12; 57.1%), followed by sonography guided (n = 5; 23.8%), core needle (n = 3; 14.3%) and CT guided (n = 1; 4.8%). The second biopsy was conclusive in 16/21 (76.2% [95% CI 52.8%–91.8%]) patients (8 with a bone lesion, 8 with a soft tissue lesion; Fig. 1). No patient underwent a third biopsy.

Altogether, 95 (42.8%) of the 222 patients ultimately underwent definite surgery and the other 127 (57.2%) alternative treatments or active surveillance. Two patients underwent definite surgery elsewhere, but the final histology is unknown. In another patient with confirmed bone metastasis, no histological specimens were obtained during stabilisation of the spine. In the fourth patient, a histologically confirmed ABC was treated with sclerotherapy, and no further tissue was obtained. In the remaining 91 patients, definite histology revealed a primary malignant tumour in 45 (49.4%), a benign lesion in 42 (46.2%) and a metastasis in 4 (4.4%) patients.

Discussion

In the present retrospective, single-centre study investigating patients treated over a limited time period, we observed a conclusiveness rate of 88.0% and 88.6% of the first biopsy for bone and soft tissue lesions, with no significant difference depending on core needle, sonography-guided, CT-guided or open biopsy. Core needle biopsy was the most frequent method employed in soft tissue lesions, while CT-guided biopsy was the prevailing method for bone lesions. The longest time delay from first presentation to the outpatient clinic and initial biopsy was present for CT-guided biopsies, regardless of the underlying lesion type, potentially reflecting scheduling issues due to lesion complexity and logistical constraints.

Patients referred for CT-guided biopsy were more likely to have bony lesions, mirroring the indication for these methodologies as being carried out under (general) anaesthesia and with accurate imaging support. Of note, CT-guided biopsies, but also, to a smaller extent, open biopsies were associated with a time delay from initial presentation compared with core needle and sonography-guided biopsy. Similar findings have been reported by Dirks et al., who found an 8-day mean delay from initial presentation to open biopsy [6]. This delay is probably attributable to limited resources in operating theatres and CT intervention rooms. Although CT-guided biopsies were associated with longer time intervals to intervention, no adverse impact on diagnostic conclusiveness was observed in our cohort. Of note, a delay in treatment following the first biopsy was observed in 12.6% of patients, usually due to an inconclusive initial biopsy requiring repeated biopsy. Although this did not alter the treatment plan since no definite diagnosis had yet been reached, a negative effect on patients’ wellbeing due to prolonged diagnostic workup cannot be ruled out [7].

In addition, each biopsy type entails different complications, which are dictated by the lesion location. We only observed grade I complications according to the Clavien-Dindo classification [5], usually local bleeding from the biopsy tract. High complication rates were observed following CT-guided biopsies for bone lesions, which exceeded those reported in the literature [8, 9]. Interestingly, core needle and open biopsies, respectively, had the lowest complication rates at 0.0% to 1.5% and 0.0%. This contradicts findings from a meta-analysis by Birgin et al., who reported pooled complication rates of 1% and 4%, respectively, for core needle and open biopsies [10]. The reasons for this contradiction are unclear but may be related to our unit's experience with both biopsy techniques.

The overall conclusiveness of the first biopsy was 88.0% for bone lesions and 88.6% for soft tissue lesions, without statistical evidence for differences across the four biopsy types examined. Notably, the biopsy technique was selected based on lesion characteristics, anatomical location and clinical indication. Therefore, the present results should not be interpreted as a direct head-to-head comparison of technique performance but rather as an evaluation of institutional practice within indication-based allocation of biopsy methods. Conclusiveness rates of CT-guided biopsies (77.8–83.7%) [11–14], core needle biopsies (90.0–93.9%) [15, 16], open biopsies (84.6–92.5%) [16] and sonography-guided biopsies (80.0–83.3%) [17] are largely comparable with the literature. Notably, a selection bias must be considered given that three-quarters of CT-guided and open biopsies originated from the bone. However, no significant association between the conclusiveness of the initial biopsy and the method employed, patient age, BMI, lesion size or location was found for either bone or soft tissue lesions, given the number of patients available for our analyses. Yet, these findings should be interpreted with caution, given the limited number of inconclusive biopsies, which may have left the statistical model underpowered.

Finally, following an inconclusive biopsy, be it due to non-representative tumour tissue or discrepancies between radiology and histology, another biopsy is usually recommended to rule out malignancy [18]. Nearly 10% of patients in our cohort required another biopsy, which was usually an open procedure. Slightly lower rates were reported by Kiatisevi et al. following CT-guided or open biopsies [19] and by Strauss et al. following core needle biopsies [20], yet Klein et al. reported comparable rates [21].

Our study encompasses several aspects of added value: we included a contemporary patient population over a limited time period. Thus, potential changes in local policies and physicians’ experience have probably not influenced our findings. Another strength is the single-institution analysis, with biopsies obtained by experienced personnel trained in musculoskeletal oncology and histological samples analysed at the same pathology referral centre by pathologists with expertise in musculoskeletal tumour pathology. In addition, our study reflects a “real-world” clinical practice setting, with indication-based allocation of biopsy techniques.

Some limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, the overall number of included patients is limited, so in-depth subgroup analyses on the diagnostic performance of different biopsy techniques could not be carried out. Second, patients were subjected to the type of biopsy at the discretion of the treating physician. Therefore, a selection bias must be considered, which would weaken the comparability of different biopsy techniques. Third, the retrospective analysis and indication-driven allocation of biopsy techniques limit the ability to draw causal conclusions regarding relative performance of each technique. Furthermore, our study's single-centre design limits the external validity, with local expertise and availability of resources influencing conclusiveness and complication rates as well as time delay.

According to our single-centre experience, time delay from initial presentation to the biopsy itself for suspected bone and soft tissue lesions differs slightly between techniques, as do complication rates. Yet our findings suggest that all biopsy techniques achieve high rates of diagnostic conclusiveness within an indication-based institutional workflow.
