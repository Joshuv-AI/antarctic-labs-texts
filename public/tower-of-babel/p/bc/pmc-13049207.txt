Combined surgical and interventional treatment of a giant superior vena cava aneurysm
Authors: Yonghui Wang, Weihao Li, Xuemin Zhang, Tao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report the diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up of a rare giant superior vena cava (SVC) aneurysm in a 39-year-old woman, managed through a combination of surgical and interventional techniques. The patient underwent off-pump SVC aneurysmorrhaphy without cardiopulmonary bypass, followed by balloon angioplasty to address early postoperative stenosis manifested by facial cyanosis. The interventions resulted in complete symptom resolution and durable vessel patency, as confirmed by computed tomography at 3 months and maintained at 1 year. This case highlights that off-pump aneurysmorrhaphy offers a safe, organ-preserving option for large SVC aneurysms with minimal thrombus, and balloon angioplasty serves as an effective salvage therapy for postoperative stenosis. An integrated surgical-interventional strategy, coupled with standardized anticoagulation and imaging follow-up, may optimize outcomes in this rare vascular condition.Keywords: Superior vena cava aneurysm, Surgical treatment, Aneurysmorrhaphy, Balloon angioplasty, Complication management

Superior vena cava aneurysm (SVCA) is a rare venous anomaly, potentially linked to congenital developmental defects, connective tissue disorders, or acquired factors such as trauma or inflammation.1,2 Its incidence is extremely low, primarily documented through case reports and small series.3 SVCAs are classified as saccular or fusiform, with saccular aneurysms more prone to thrombosis, rupture, or compressive symptoms.4 Asymptomatic small SVCAs may be observed conservatively; however, aneurysms >5 cm are associated with increased risks of SVC syndrome, pulmonary embolism, and potential rupture, and therefore generally warrant intervention.5

Surgical management is the primary approach for large SVCAs, particularly those with expansion or complication risks.6 No evidence-based guidelines exist, and treatment strategies are tailored based on anatomy, physiological burden, thrombus risk, and institutional expertise, including resection/reconstruction, aneurysmorrhaphy, or replacement.7 Postoperative complications such as stenosis or occlusion require vigilance, with interventional techniques like balloon angioplasty serving as effective salvage options.8 This report describes the surgical management of a giant SVCA, supplemented by interventional therapy for complications, and discusses its implications for clinical practice. This study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (2024PHB228-001), and written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report and accompanying images.

Case report

A 39-year-old woman initially underwent chest radiography as part of routine health screening, which revealed an abnormal right superior mediastinal finding and prompted further evaluation with a chest computed tomography (CT) scan. She had no history of hypertension, diabetes, connective tissue disorders, smoking, or alcohol use. Physical examination was unremarkable. Laboratory tests, including complete blood count, coagulation profile, and D-dimer, were normal. Chest radiography demonstrated a well-defined right superior mediastinal widening (Fig 1, A). Contrast-enhanced chest CT scan with axial, coronal, and sagittal reconstructions revealed a 6.9 × 5.5 cm aneurysm of the SVC isolated to the superior mediastinum (Fig 1, B-D). The aneurysm originated from the lateral wall of the SVC, distal to the confluence of the bilateral innominate veins and proximal to the atriocaval junction, without extension into the right atrium. SVC venography confirmed a saccular aneurysm with a maximum diameter of 6.5 cm, no thrombus, and normal flow in adjacent vessels (Fig 2).

After multidisciplinary consultation (involving cardiothoracic surgery, anesthesiology, critical care, and radiology), surgical intervention was planned owing to the aneurysm's size (>5 cm) and potential risks of rupture or thrombosis, despite the absence of symptoms. The procedure involved a median sternotomy to expose the SVCA. Aneurysmorrhaphy was performed: the posterior wall was clamped, and the aneurysm wall was imbricated and sutured with 3-0 Prolene, eliminating the aneurysm (Fig 3). Cardiopulmonary bypass was not used. The operation lasted 180 minutes with an estimated blood loss of 200 mL.

(E) Intraoperative photograph demonstrating exposure of the heart and mediastinum through median sternotomy during the procedure.

The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit. Three hours postoperatively, she developed facial and bilateral upper limb cyanosis and swelling. Neck vein ultrasound examination showed a widened right internal jugular vein (2.0 cm) with sluggish, flow, suggesting SVC stenosis or occlusion. Immediate endovascular intervention was undertaken. Under ultrasound guidance, vascular access was obtained via the left common femoral vein. A diagnostic catheter (MPA 1) was advanced into the right internal jugular vein, and digital subtraction angiography was then performed. The angiogram demonstrated dilation of the right internal jugular vein and a near-occlusive stenosis of the SVC (approximately 95%), localized to the aneurysmorrhaphy segment, with an associated filling defect (Fig 4). Based on the close temporal relationship to the procedure and the angiographic findings, the stenosis was considered most likely related to postoperative edema and suture-related narrowing, rather than involvement of the entire SVC segment. Sequential balloon angioplasty was subsequently carried out using an 8 × 80 mm balloon, followed by a 10 × 60 mm balloon. Completion venography demonstrated resolution of the stenosis with restoration of venous flow, accompanied by prompt clinical improvement (Fig 5). Intravascular ultrasound examination was not used. Stenting was deferred owing to the patient's young age and recent suture line healing, with a plan for close follow-up. Postprocedure anticoagulation (low-molecular-weight heparin, 1 mg/kg every 12 hours for 7 days) was administered, followed by oral rivaroxaban for 3 months.

At the 3-month follow-up, a CT scan confirmed a patent SVC (diameter 2.5 cm) with no recurrence (Fig 6). The 1-year follow-up showed excellent quality of life without complications.

Discussion

Surgical timing and technique selection

Surgical intervention for SVCA is guided by aneurysm size, morphology, and symptoms. Indications include (1) large (>5-6 cm) or progressively enlarging aneurysms, (2) saccular morphology (higher thrombosis/embolism risk), (3) compressive symptoms or recurrent thrombotic events, (4) anatomical rupture risk or inability to ensure long-term follow-up, and (5) young patients seeking long-term maintenance-free outcomes. Approximately 40% of patients with asymptomatic SVCAs undergo surgery, primarily for large or expanding lesions.9 Surgical goals are aneurysm elimination, flow restoration, and complication prevention. Common techniques include the following.

•Aneurysmorrhaphy: Suitable for thin-walled, focal, low-thrombus burden lesions; it is less invasive, preserves physiological compliance, and reduces anticoagulation needs but risks over-narrowing or suture-related stenosis.

•Resection with patch or complete replacement: Preferred for large/complex or high-thrombus lesions; it ensures complete lesion removal but requires patches or grafts, increasing long-term anticoagulation and anastomotic risks.

•Endovascular strategies (eg, covered stents): Rarely reported, mainly for emergent rupture or high-risk inoperable cases; long-term outcomes in benign lesions are understudied.

This case used off-pump aneurysmorrhaphy, informed by recent experiences with mediastinal venous aneurysms. For thin-walled, low-thrombus burden aneurysms in safely accessible locations, aneurysmorrhaphy avoids patch/graft-related risks and long-term anticoagulation. Off-pump surgery reduces complications like systemic inflammation or coagulopathy.10 Literature suggests off-pump techniques achieve >90% success in superior mediastinum, hemodynamically stable aneurysms.11

Postoperative SVC stenosis/obstruction management

Postoperative SVC obstruction is a recognized complication, driven by tight suturing, edema/hematoma, intimal injury, or early vasospasm. Symptoms include facial cyanosis, headache, upper limb edema, and venous engorgement, diagnosable via bedside ultrasound examination and CT scan. Prompt flow restoration is critical. Current consensus favors endovascular management for benign or postoperative SVC obstruction, starting with balloon angioplasty and reserving stenting for persistent issues.12 In young patients with recent sutures, a balloon-first, delayed stenting strategy with short-term anticoagulation and close imaging follow-up is prudent.

Perioperative anticoagulation and follow-up

Owing to the absence of randomized evidence for SVCA management, anticoagulation strategies should be individualized. General recommendations include: (1) intraoperative systemic heparinization coupled with early postoperative mobilization, (2) for aneurysmorrhaphy without evidence of thrombosis, short-term anticoagulation or antiplatelet therapy (eg, 4-12 weeks) may be employed, with gradual discontinuation guided by follow-up imaging,7 (3) in cases requiring endovascular stenting, most centers advocate dual antiplatelet therapy for 1 to 3 months, followed by single-agent maintenance, with shorter durations feasible for benign etiologies compared to malignancy-related SVC syndrome, and (4) follow-up imaging is recommended at 1 to 3 months, 6 to 12 months, and as needed thereafter.

The majority of reported SVCA surgical treatments involve resection with reconstruction, with some cases describing SVC replacement performed on a beating heart.6 In contrast, isolated aneurysmorrhaphy is rarely reported. This case suggests that, for patients with suitable anatomical and hemodynamic conditions, aneurysmorrhaphy can achieve anatomical restoration while preserving physiological compliance. However, preparedness for interventional salvage procedures is essential to address potential early postoperative stenosis.

Limitations

As a single case report, this study lacks generalizability, requiring multicenter validation. Future innovations, such as endoscopic-assisted surgery or bioresorbable stents, may further optimize outcomes.

Conclusions

For young patients with large, low-thrombus burden SVCAs, off-pump aneurysmorrhaphy is a feasible organ-preserving approach. Postoperative SVC obstruction can be effectively managed with percutaneous balloon angioplasty, with stenting reserved for refractory cases. An integrated surgical-interventional pathway, coupled with standardized anticoagulation and follow-up, is critical for enhancing safety and efficacy in this rare condition.
