Development of virtual reality in the upper extremities function in children with Cerebral Palsy. A pilot randomized controlled trial
Authors: Apiphan Iamchaimongkol, Siriluck Sangkrai, Pimtida Alberts, Amprai Surit, Warakorn Charoensuk, Suchada Noopim, Thunyanoot Prasertsakul, Tulyapruek Tawonsawatruk, Sivaporn Vongpipatana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the effect of the Virtual Reality (VR) in upper extremities function in children with Cerebral Palsy (CP).Study designPilot randomization study.MethodsA total of 42 children with CP, aged 10 to15 years, were enrolled and randomly allocated into either an intervention group (n = 21) or a control group (n = 21) using a computer-generated block randomization sequence (block size of 4). There were no statistically significant differences in baseline characteristics between the two groups. The intervention group received a VR training program, whereas the control group underwent conventional occupational therapy. Both interventions were administered in 45-minute sessions, three days per week, over five weeks. Pre- and post-intervention outcome measures included major upper extremity range of motion (ROM), the Box and Block Test (BBT), and the ABILHAND-Kids assessment.ResultsPost-intervention, both the VR and control groups demonstrated improvements across all measured outcomes, including upper-extremity ROM, BBT, and ABILHAND-Kids scores. Although no statistically significant between-group differences were observed in the overall cohort, a subgroup analysis revealed a statistically significant improvement in ABILHAND-Kids scores in favor of the VR intervention among children aged 10 to 12 years.ConclusionThese preliminary findings indicate that the VR intervention does not result in statistically significant improvements in upper-extremity ROM and motor function compared with conventional therapy.

Background

Cerebral Palsy (CP) is a heterogeneous neurodevelopmental condition, caused by a non-degenerative brain disturbance during early childhood. It primarily affects motor abilities and frequently affects other functional domains throughout the individual’s lifespan [1]. CP is recognized as the most common neuromotor disorder causing abnormal movement and posture, thereby significantly impacting a child’s development, functional abilities, and daily activities. Globally, the prevalence ranges from 1.5 to 4 cases per 1,000 live births [2–5]. However, this rate increases substantially among extremely preterm infants, with a pooled prevalence reaching 82.5 per 1,000 in neonates born at a gestational age of less than 28 weeks [6, 7].

Upper extremity dysfunction is a prevalent complication in children with CP, affecting an estimated 60% to 80% of this population [6, 8]. These impairments predominantly manifest as spasticity, muscle weakness, and poor selective motor control [6]. Consequently, such motor deficits significantly compromise their ability to perform activities of daily living and limit overall social participation [9, 10]. To assess upper extremity function in CP, the Manual Ability Classification System (MACS) is commonly used, as it has demonstrated strong validity and reliability. MACS categorizes the manual ability of children with CP. It classifies hand function into five distinct levels based on the child’s self-initiated ability to handle objects, ranging from Level 1 (highest level of independence) to Level 5 (most severe functional limitation) [11].

The severity and clinical presentation of manual dysfunction in CP are correlated with the underlying neuroanatomy of the brain lesions. Bilateral CP is typically associated with diffuse bilateral brain involvement, caused by etiologies such as periventricular leukomalacia, hypoxic-ischemic insults, pre- or postnatal infections, traumatic brain injury, or developmental brain malformations. Conversely, hemiplegic CP frequently results from focal lesions, such as a cerebrovascular accident occurring during the prenatal, perinatal, or postnatal period. In cases of unilateral CP, damage to the corticospinal tract plays a critical role in disrupting sensorimotor development [8].

Enhancing upper extremity function in children with CP requires high-intensity, task-specific active movement therapies to promote neuroplasticity [8, 12–14]. While medical and surgical management, such as Botulinum neurotoxin A, selective dorsal rhizotomy, are frequently utilized to reduce spasticity, their functional benefits are maximized when integrated with physical therapy. To optimize these rehabilitative outcomes and address the motivational barriers to conventional therapy, technology-based modalities such as virtual reality (VR) have been increasingly adopted [12–14].

VR is the use of interactive, computer-generated simulators that allow users to engage with environments that mimic real-world objects and events [15, 16]. Recently, VR has emerged as a therapeutic modality in neurorehabilitation. Its clinical application is base on motor learning theory. Repetitive, task-specific movement practice within virtual environments has been demonstrated to induce neuroplasticity and enhance cortical activation [17, 18]. Furthermore, numerous studies indicate that motor skills acquired during VR training can successfully transfer to real-world scenarios, significantly improving functional abilities such as reaching and activities of daily living [15–19].

In pediatric neurorehabilitation, VR is used to improve upper-extremity motor function. The technology delivers real-time visual and auditory feedback to facilitate precise motor movement while allowing dynamic adjustment of task parameters to maintain an optimal therapeutic challenge. VR addresses the motivational barriers often associated with conventional therapy by converting repetitive motor practice into engaging, reward-based activities. For children with CP, this play-based approach to motor training not only produces beneficial functional outcomes but also maximizes therapeutic adherence and patient cooperation [15, 20].

The systematic review and meta-analysis showed that VR and video games can improve hand and arm function in CP when combined with conventional physical rehabilitation [21–23]. However, the results should be cautiously interpreted. In addition, most studies on interventions for upper extremity function are conducted in unilateral CP. There are quite limited studies examining the results of the intervention in bilateral CP. Recently, the Faculty of Engineering at Mahidol University of Thailand developed a VR system specifically designed for upper-extremity training in CP. The current study aims to evaluate the clinical effectiveness of custom-developed VR intervention in the upper extremity range of motion (ROM) and motor function in CP.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was a single-blinded, pilot randomized controlled trial. It was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee, Faculty of Medicine Ramathibodi Hospital, Mahidol University, Bangkok, Thailand, and was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05328466). The participants were recruited from the Srisangwan School, Nonthaburi, from June to August 2022.

The inclusion of this study were: (1) children with CP aged 10–15 years old; (2) MACS levels 2 to 3; (3) Sufficient cognitive and attention capacity to understand basic instruction; (4) Can cooperate with the therapist and the ability to follow commands; (5) Provided informed consent to participate in the study.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) unable to understand the instruction and follow the task; (2) severe visual or auditory impairment; (3) chemo-denervation injection on the upper extremities within 6 months before participating in the study; (4) received the injection or other treatment options plan on the upper extremity during the study time; (5) wore daytime orthosis on the upper extremity; (6) got epilepsy or convulsive condition (7) Denied to sign an informed consent nor unwilling to continue the study.

Sample size

This study was a pilot randomized controlled trial, so the estimated sample size was based on our preliminary pilot study of improvements in elbow extension ROM. The expected effect size for the calculation was d = 0.99 (a large effect size). To detect this difference with an alpha error probability of 0.05 and a statistical power of 80%, the required sample size was 34 participants. To account for an anticipated dropout rate of approximately 20%, the total sample size was increased to 42 participants.

Intervention

This study recruited children with CP and divided them into two groups; the control group and the VR group. The participants were randomized using a computer-generated block size of 4 randomization. Allocation was concealed using sealed envelopes prepared by an independent researcher. Baseline characteristics, including age, gender, weight, height, underlying disease, medications used, type of CP, and MACS level, were collected. Participants in the control group received a standard rehabilitation program to train bilateral upper extremity function using conventional methods, including repetitive tasks such as sketch board rolling on the table for 10 min, arm bicycling for 10 min, connecting plastic pipes for 10 min, and putting coins into a coin bank for 10 min. In the VR group, participants received upper-extremity training using a VR program. Both groups have training sessions for 45 min per day, 3 days a week, for 5 weeks.

Participants in the intervention group used VR game-based therapy with Kinect sensor technology. The Kinect sensor is a device that combines a camera, an infrared light projector, and a motion detector into a single, compact unit. The Kinect sensor will recognize skeletal gestures and generate a real-time three-dimensional (3D) image on the screen. The invisible infrared light detected gesture motion; therefore, there is no need to record or attach a marker to a body. Furthermore, infrared light is non-invasive, has no side effects, is safe for human tissue, and does not interact with medical devices such as pacemakers or other electrical implants [24]. In many studies, the Kinect sensor was used to assess upper extremity ROM during functional training in rehabilitation therapy, demonstrating high validity and reliability [25–27].

The VR game was designed in collaboration between Mahidol University, medical instructors from Rehabilitation Medicine department, an orthopedic surgeon from the Faculty of Medicine Ramathibodi Hospital, and a biomedical engineering team from the Faculty of Engineering, Mahidol University in Thailand. The overall software system demonstrated excellent performance. The evaluation yielded an accuracy of 0.95, a sensitivity of 0.94, and a specificity of 0.96, indicating a highly reliable system for detecting user movements.

The VR setup consisted of a Microsoft® Kinect sensor positioned approximately 1.5 to 2.0 m in front of the participant at ground level. The intervention was based on motor learning principles, specifically utilizing repetitive, task-specific training combined with real-time visual and auditory feedback to promote neuroplasticity. The games were created to target upper-extremity movements often impaired in CP. The software consists of three mini-games designed to improve the ROM of the major upper extremity joints, specifically shoulder flexion/extension, shoulder abduction/adduction, and elbow flexion/extension.

Before starting each game, each participant must take the calibration process to estimate the maximum and minimum ROM. The active shoulder and elbow in forward flexion and extension, abduction and adduction, and internal and external rotation. Movements have been recorded and calculated to set the difficulty of the game. Therefore, the difficulty of the games depended on each participant’s ability to move. Participants with more upper-extremity disabilities would play the game on easier mode than those with higher function.

After calibration, the training program would start. There are 3 mini-games as follows (Fig. 1).

When completing one task, the game would respond with a reward picture on screen and a delightful sound effect to provide feedback to the participants. There are 20 repetitions in each game. The program will randomly train the more affected side more than the less affected side at a ratio of 60:40. For example, the participant with right upper extremity weakness must move the right side more than the left side in a 60:40 ratio per set. This setting will stimulate participants to train more on the more affected side. In addition, the program also recorded details of training, time period, number of movements on each side, and score per session. This information will be used to evaluate and analyze the progression of each player. All primary and secondary outcomes were assessed by the same assessors, a board-certified physiatrist with over 5 years of clinical experience, both prior to and after training. This study utilized a single-blind design. The outcome assessors were blinded to the group allocation throughout the study.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was measured by active ROM of the major joints of the upper extremity, such as the shoulder and elbow, on both the less affected side and the more affected side. The active ROM can evaluate focal motor function [28, 29]. The ROM of the shoulder includes active shoulder motion in forward flexion, abduction, internal rotation, and external rotation. The ROM of the elbows was active elbow flexion and extension. To measure this test, participants would sit in a supported chair and be asked to move their shoulders and elbows actively. The a standard 12-inch plastic universal goniometer (Baseline Evaluation Instruments, New York, USA) was used.

The secondary outcomes were:

Box and Block test (BBT) is used for evaluating the gross manual dexterity scale. The participants were asked to transfer a block of size 1.5 × 1.5 × 1.5 centimeters from the box size 25.4 × 53.4 centimeters, which was divided into two equal compartments from one side to another side in one minute. The number of completely transferred blocks was recorded [30]. Participants were asked to perform the box and block test in both less affected and more affected sides.ABILHAND-Kids questionnaire is used to measure the perception of children toward their manual ability in daily living. As the manual abilities depend on upper extremity function, cognitive function, emotion, and environment. They are rather better at asking the children’s perception. They must rate the difficulties of the task. Then, the scores were calculated using the probabilistic measurement model, known as Rasch analysis [31].

Statistical analysis

This study analyzed demographic data by using descriptive statistics. The data were presented as the number, percentage, mean, standard deviation (SD), and 95% Confidence Interval (95%CI). The comparisons of outcomes before and after training using STATA version 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Comparing before and after training using the Mann-Whitney test for ROM of the shoulder and elbow, BBT, and ABILHAND-Kids, with a P value < 0.05 for statistical significance.

Results

In this study, 42 children with CP, aged 10–15 years, were included and divided into a control and VR group, with 21 participants in each group. The mean age (SD) was 11.57 (1.66) years in the control group and 11.95 (1.74) years in the VR group. 60% of participants were male in both groups. The number of spastic diplegia was 12 (57.14%) in the control group and 15 (71.43%) in the VR group. MACS level 2 was 12 (57%) and MACS level 3 was 9 (42.86%) in both groups. The number of training sessions in both groups was 6.48 sessions and 7 sessions in the control and VR group, respectively. The baseline characteristics between groups were not statistically significant. (Table 1)

The changes in ROM of major upper extremities for both the less affected and more affected upper extremities demonstrated predominantly negligible to small effect sizes. Discussion

The ROM of the upper extremity

In this study, ROM of the major upper extremity joints was chosen as the primary outcome because adequate joint mobility is a fundamental prerequisite for upper extremity function. Since the VR intervention in this study specifically targets joint kinematics, ROM effectively captures the biomechanical effects of the training, thereby facilitating functional improvements [32, 33]. The results demonstrated that participants in both the control and VR groups showed improvement in the ROM of the major upper extremity joints (shoulder and elbow joints) on both the less affected and more affected sides in CP, with no statistically significant difference between the two groups. Although a minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for upper extremity ROM improvement in CP has not yet been established, improvements in ROM of multiple major upper extremity joints generally facilitate better functional performance. This relationship is supported by previous literature suggesting that increased upper extremity ROM correlates with improved function [29, 32, 33]. Furthermore, the increase in ROM of the upper extremity aligns with the findings of You et al., which indicated that VR can effectively increase movement in CP through the principles of cortical reorganization and practice-dependent plasticity, achieved through repetitive movement training in VR [17]. In addition, we integrated effect size analysis into our interpretation. Although the between-group differences in ROM improvements were not statistically significant, the clinical relevance of the VR intervention was evidenced by functional domains. Specifically, the VR group demonstrated a small-to-medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.45) in the ABILHAND-Kids score compared to the control group. This indicates that the kinematic mobility trained in the VR environment transferred positively to upper-extremity functional ability.

Moreover, these study findings are consistent with the study of Malick et al., who found that VR can improve ROM of the shoulder and elbow, with statistically significant results compared to the control group [34]. However, this study found no statistical difference. These results may be attributed to differences in game formats and a lower number of training sessions, as participants in this study received only approximately 50% of the intended training sessions due to the COVID-19 pandemic period.

The box and block test

The Box and Block Test (BBT) assesses gross manual dexterity through purposeful upper extremity movement and is commonly used in children with CP to evaluate motor function and activities of daily living [35]. This study found that both conventional physical therapy and VR training increased BBT scores on both the less affected and more affected sides, with no statistically significant differences within or between groups. These results are the same as the previous study using the Kinect video games, and there was no significant difference in BBT [36].

Regarding clinical significance, Araneda et al. established the MCID for the BBT as 1.9 blocks for the more affected side and 3.0 blocks for the less affected side [37]. In the VR group of this study, the BBT score for the less affected hand reached 3.0 blocks, meeting the MCID threshold. This supports the hypothesis that VR training for the upper extremity can improve gross manual dexterity in the less affected side. However, a previous study showed that using VR can improve BBT with statistical significance [38]. Moreover, Liang et al. reported that for unilateral CP, the MCID required to impact daily activities on the more affected side is 5.29–6.46 blocks [39]. In contrast, the more affected side in both groups of this study improved by only one block (0.89 in the control group and 1.05 in the VR group), indicating that the BBT improvement in this study has no clinical significance. This may be because the BBT requires hand function, including grip strength for grasping and releasing blocks, as well as eye-hand coordination and sensation. The VR games used in this study primarily focused on major upper extremity movements, such as the shoulder and elbow joints. Therefore, there is no information available on the ROM of the wrist or grip strength. Furthermore, the BBT relies on eye-hand coordination and sensation in the hand. Children with CP often have sensory impairments; however, this research focused on motor function training rather than sensory training and did not collect data on sensory deficits.

ABILHAND-kids and subgroup analysis

The ABILHAND-Kids questionnaire assesses manual ability in CP through activities of daily living. This study found improvement in ABILHAND-Kids in both control and VR groups, but no statistically significant difference was found in the overall population, similar to Hung et al. [36]. However, a subgroup analysis stratified by age revealed that younger children (10–12 years) in the VR group showed a statistically significant improvement in ABILHAND-Kids scores, with a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.17). This indicates that the intervention had a profound clinical impact on this specific age cohort. In contrast, the results for children aged 13–15 years showed no significant advantage over conventional method, yielding a negligible effect size (Cohen’s d = -0.06). This finding aligns with Bleyenheuft et al. report of large changes in younger children (6–12 years) and minor changes in older children (13–15 years) following training. This may be due to younger children having higher neuroplasticity compared to older children, and as they age, they will develop habits that are more difficult to change [40–42]. Furthermore, Bleyenheuft et al. noted that children with CP classified as MACS Level 2 showed the most significant improvement compared to Levels 1 and 3 [40]. This finding is consistent with the current study, in which nearly 60% of participants were classified as MACS Level 2. Additionally, VR may be more effective at increasing attention, providing enjoyment, and promoting motivation in younger children compared to teenagers. This ABILHAND-Kids results are consistent with this study’s findings, which indicate that VR improves ROM of the shoulder and elbow, thereby enhancing functionality. Consequently, it can be concluded that increased ROM contributes to improved function and manual ability in young children with CP who have mild disabilities of the upper extremities.

Safety and side effects

The therapist assessed and monitored participants for any adverse events during the VR training and for 48 h post-intervention. There were no adverse physical or mental effects, such as dizziness, nausea, vomiting, or motion-induced seizure, reported after training with VR.

Limitations

Firstly, regarding the missed training sessions, data collection was conducted at a specialized school for children with disabilities. During the COVID-19 pandemic, there were frequent student absences and intermittent, mandatory school closures directed by health authorities. Consequently, participants completed only 6–7 sessions before the study had to be halted, representing 50% of the total sessions. This truncated dose compromises the study’s construct validity, as the results reflect the efficacy of a short-term intervention rather than a full rehabilitation program. Furthermore, the fewer training sessions resulted in a smaller effect size, increasing the risk of a false-negative results and potentially explaining the lack of statistically significant differences between the two groups. However, because the reduction in sessions was consistent across both study arms, the internal validity of the group comparison remains intact. Future studies should evaluate the full protocol to determine the effects of VR in rehabilitation training.

Secondly, the heterogeneity of CP presentation in this study may influence the results, as individuals with different brain pathologies and pathophysiology of impaired upper limb function may be included. Although we recruited the participants with MACS level 2 to 3, the heterogeneity of brain pathology might affect the results.

Thirdly, this study was a pilot study, and the sample size is small. Thus, it is not enough to detect the change in the outcomes. Moreover, the active ROM, BBT, and ABILHAND-kids may not be sufficient to detect subtle changes. For suggestions, it is recommended to detect small changes using kinematics and kinetics in future studies. Another limitation is the inability to blind participants and therapists to the group allocation, given the nature of the interventions. Although the outcome assessor was blinded, the participants’ knowledge of receiving a VR treatment might have introduced performance bias, potentially altering their motivation and effort levels during the training sessions.

Finally, limitations include the lack of long-term follow-up to assess sustained effects and the absence of a specific established dose for VR training sessions.

Conclusions

These preliminary findings indicate that the VR intervention does not result in statistically significant improvements in upper-extremity ROM and motor function compared with conventional therapy.
