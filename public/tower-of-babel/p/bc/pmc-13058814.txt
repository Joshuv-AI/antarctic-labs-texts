Building Youth Capacity for Climate‐Health Science: Lessons From Implementing The DataJam in Jordan
Authors: Kelly Nielsen, Judy Cameron, Mohammed Salahat, Catherine Cramer, Nathan Gonzalez, Alham Al‐Shurafat, Lamees Alshneikat, Debbie Loomis, Wael Al‐Delaimy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAddressing impacts on human health from climate change will require engaged communities capable of co‐creating actionable science. This is particularly the case in Jordan, one of the most vulnerable countries to climate change with a hot, dry climate and rapidly growing population. A key demographic for building capacity to address climate‐health challenges is youth. To engage Jordanian youth in developing knowledge and skills related to climate‐health science, the Global Center on Climate Change and Water Energy Food Health Systems (GC3WEFH) implemented The DataJam, an annual project‐based data science learning program and competition developed in the United States. The GC3WEFH enrolled 87 students from 21 schools in The DataJam Jordan. Fifty‐four students in teams of three completed 18 projects over a 2‐year period while 33 students started The DataJam but did not complete a project. The aim of the intervention was to build data science capacity to address issues at the intersection of climate and health. To explore the outcomes of this intervention, we used the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research to identify the primary determinants. This analysis revealed that the complexity of The DataJam and the work infrastructure of the implementation impacted communication across the intervention, which shaped the topics students researched and their use of data science. Importantly, the DataJam increased both the confidence and interest of students in engaging in climate change related challenges facing their communities. Therefore, The DataJam is a positive example of engaging youth through the international translation of a STEM learning program.Keywords: community engagement, youth, climate change, health, implementation science, informal STEM

Introduction

Climate change is a major driver of health risks and outcomes. Extreme heat, drought, flooding and other environmental impacts of increasing greenhouse gas emissions negatively impact physical and mental health. Health impacts can be direct, such as deaths from heatwaves and floods, or indirect through effects on basic human needs, such as kidney diseases that are caused by a lack of access to fresh drinking water due to drought (Maslin et al., 2025). While every region is facing the consequences of climate change, Middle Eastern nations are especially vulnerable (Khosravi et al., 2024; Neira et al., 2023). One of the most vulnerable countries in the region is Jordan, situated in a semi‐arid region with a hot, dry climate and a population of over 10 million people and projected to reach over 19 million by 2050 (Al‐Addous et al., 2023). The combination of population growth and climate change adds pressure to Jordan's scarce water resources, which, in addition to dangerous heatwaves and frequent dust events, increase the likelihood of negative health outcomes, particularly for vulnerable populations.

An engaged community with access to relevant scientific knowledge and skills training is better able to co‐produce knowledge and interventions that are more likely to be adopted and sustained (Beier et al., 2016; Chambers et al., 2021; Clark et al., 2016; Gerlak et al., 2023; Harvey et al., 2019, 2021; Mach et al., 2020; Reed, 2008; Talley et al., 2016; Tilt et al., 2024). Youth are both disproportionately vulnerable to health risks from climate change (Clemens et al., 2022; Sanson et al., 2019) and instrumental to addressing those risks (Gasparri et al., 2021). Youth have become increasingly visible at United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change Conferences of Parties (Thew, 2018; Thew et al., 2020). Organizations such as UNICEF have made youth a key pillar in efforts to address climate impacts, engaging millions of young people around the world; in Jordan, UNICEF has engaged over 35,000 youth in more than 200,000 hr of tree planting and recycling (Generation Unlimited, 2025). Programs worldwide have been designed to empower youth to participate in climate‐related policy discussions and engage in participatory research related to climate change adaptation (e.g., Desiderio et al., 2024; Malafaia et al., 2024; Stavrianakis et al., 2025; Trott et al., 2023).

In this article, we evaluate the engagement of Jordanian high school students by the Global Center on Climate Change and Water Energy Food Health Systems (GC3WEFH) using The DataJam, a data science competition based in the United States. The GC3WEFH is a National Institutes of Health (NIH) funded network combining engineering, data science, social science, epidemiology, and public health researchers from the University of California San Diego, Texas A&M University, Hashemite University, and the University of Jordan. A key priority of the GC3WEFH is fostering community engagement to improve local capacity, resilience to climate impacts, and human health and wellbeing. To promote community engagement, the GC3WEFH partners with local non‐governmental organizations such as the Royal Scientific Society (RSS) and Climate Action Network (CAN), which have established relationships with communities across Jordan and within critical government ministries.

The DataJam is an established annual project‐based data science learning program and competition developed in the United States. The DataJam introduces high school students to data science inquiry, visualization, and analytic skills to answer a research question of their own design on a topic that they choose themselves (Haqqi et al., 2018). It builds on the increasingly popular strategy of motivating students to engage in challenging subjects through collaborative team learning and competition (Culha, 2016; Lee & Lee, 2024; Parth et al., 2024; Worker et al., 2023). Small teams of three to eight students are paired with a university student mentor who has been trained to teach data science and presentation skills. Teams work on their project throughout the school year, ultimately presenting their work in an online power point presentation to a panel of judges made up of academics and industry professionals.

As an intervention adapted from an existing program in the United States and led by the GC3WEFH in Jordan, the success of The DataJam in Jordan was measured not only by the standards of The DataJam but also by the priorities of the GC3WEFH, which include building the capacity of Jordanian high school students to research issues related to climate change and health. The following study aimed to (a) assess how successful the implementation of The DataJam was in Jordan based on the aims of The DataJam and the priorities of the GC3WEFH, and (b) identify the factors that impacted implementation using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), a framework designed to assess the contextual factors that determine the effective transposition of interventions between contexts.

Methods

Study Population

To introduce The DataJam in Jordan and recruit participants, the GC3WEFH partnered with CAN to serve as an intermediary between The DataJam in the US and Jordanian high schools. CAN secured approval from the Jordanian Ministry of Education and leveraged contacts with schools where CAN was supporting an existing environmental education program. The DataJam materials were translated into Arabic, including the training curriculum and other resources on The DataJam website. The DataJam also created two Jordan‐specific data guides in Arabic.

In year one, CAN recruited 54 10th grade students to form 18 three‐person teams from high schools in the Irbid and Mafraq governorates. Teams were sex‐segregated consistent with local cultural expectations, which resulted in 12 female and 6 male teams. Rather than working with undergraduate student mentors, the teams were paired with a teacher at their school who would fill the mentor role due to a tight implementation timeline after the decision to implement The DataJam was made in the fall of 2023. Ten teams completed projects that were judged by a local committee from CAN, UN‐HABITAT, and the Ministry of Education using The DataJam judging criteria. The top five teams were chosen to present online over Zoom to US judges, who were all members of The DataJam Board of Directors. These five teams and their teachers were then invited to the annual GC3WEFH conference in Amman where they were given the opportunity to present their projects to an audience of academics, government ministers, and local NGOs.

In year two, a faculty member and a graduate assistant at the Hashemite University (HU) took over managing the DataJam project from CAN after CAN's chief executive officer left the organization. Managing The DataJam from HU facilitated the use of undergraduate mentors, replacing teachers in this role and making The DataJam Jordan consistent with the U.S. model. The HU team recruited 11 three‐person teams (7 male and 4 female) from three schools in the northern Zarqa governorate where HU is located. The teams consisted of primarily 10th and 11th grade students, but two 9th grade and two 12th grade students also participated. After three of the male teams withdrew, including a team with the 12th grade students, the eight remaining teams presented their final projects online to The DataJam judges in the US. The top three teams and their teachers were invited to the GC3WEFH annual conference where they were presented with certificates of achievement.

Data and Analysis

The implementation of The DataJam in Jordan was led by the chief executive officer at CAN in year one and a faculty member and graduate student at HU in year two, all of whom are members of the GC3WEFH. Assessment of the implementation outcomes relied on an analysis of The DataJam Jordan projects including the number of projects that teams completed, topics and questions that teams addressed in their projects, and the judging results. Because the focus of the GC3WEFH community engagement is on the intersection of climate change and health, we considered the extent to which students incorporated these topics into their projects. Judges recorded scores for each team's poster and online presentation. Criteria covered the identification of a variety of data sources, relevant research questions, data analysis, effective communication including data visualization, and other aspects of presenting a research project (for a full list of judging criteria, see Table 1). Three judges scored each poster and presentation on a five‐point scale for each criterion. The average scores for each criterion were then multiplied by a weight given to each criterion to produce a percentage. Finally, the percentages were summed to generate a final score for both posters and presentations.

We also conducted focus groups with students and teachers from the winning teams at the end of each DataJam season at an annual workshop in Amman, Jordan organized by the GC3WEFH in July 2024 and July 2025. The first student focus group included 15 students from five teams. The second student focus group included nine students from three teams. Students were asked about their overall experience with The DataJam, the knowledge and skills they developed to complete their projects, the impact of their teachers and mentors, and the potential impact of The DataJam on their future. Focus groups were also conducted with six teachers in year one and four teachers in year two. Teachers were also asked about their overall experience with The DataJam, their preparation to guide students through the competition, their perception of student learning, and the challenges they encountered. The focus groups were conducted in English and Arabic. They were conducted in‐person by the lead author who speaks English with the aid of a Jordanian translator who speaks both Arabic and English. Some participants spoke English and Arabic while others spoke only in Arabic. The focus groups were recorded and transcribed, and a GC3WEFH member translated any Arabic text into English. The transcribed transcripts were then analyzed by the main author.

Lastly, we conducted a post‐hoc analysis of The DataJam using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) to identify determinants of the implementation in Jordan and the observed outcomes. The CFIR framework includes 76 constructs across five domains: the intervention characteristics, outer setting, inner setting, characteristics of the individuals involved, and implementation process (see Table 2). The CFIR was initially developed for use in healthcare settings (Damschroder et al., 2009; Kirk et al., 2016) but has more recently been applied in different contexts such as restaurants (Fuster et al., 2023; Kerins et al., 2020). The CFIR is a flexible framework for assessing complex intersections between interventions and contexts to guide implementation across multiple settings. In order to evaluate the implementation of The DataJam, the lead author and another member of the research team first interviewed the five people who were primarily responsible for implementing The DataJam in Jordan including the GC3WEFH principal investigator, two members of The DataJam, the CAN chief executive officer, and the faculty member from Hashemite University. Next, the research and The DataJam teams held a series of meetings to review each of the CFIR constructs and input relevant details about The DataJam and its implementation in years one and two. After collectively producing intervention details for all 76 constructs, each of The DataJam team members reviewed the table and added any missing details and made clarifications. Finally, the research and The DataJam teams met to identify the constructs that were most critical to the implementation outcomes. The outcome of this process informs our discussion of the results.

Results

The GC3WEFH enrolled 87 students from 21 schools in The DataJam Jordan, and they completed 18 projects over a 2‐year period. The majority of teams completed their projects. In year one, 10 out of 18 teams completed their projects while eight out of 11 teams completed their projects in year two. Thirty‐three students did not complete The DataJam. Reasons for non‐completion varied. In year two, for example, a team with two 12th‐grade students quit the competition because the two students elected to spend more time preparing for the national Tawjihi exam required for all students who plan to attend university.

The goal of the GC3WEFH is to engage communities and build capacity around the intersection of climate change and health. The expectation for The DataJam Jordan was that teams would complete data science projects that incorporated both climate change and health data. In year one, 10 teams completed projects on a wide range of topics, from the impact of rainfall on student absenteeism to the relationship between rising temperatures and the prevalence of dog bites (see Table 3). Nine out of 10 projects addressed climate change and half of the projects addressed health; only four projects addressed both climate change and health. In the second year, only one project focused on both climate change and health—half of the completed projects focused on health and half focused on climate change as their main theme.

The average final score of the top five teams was similar in year two at 3.1 out of 5. However, the average data analysis score decreased to 2.5 for the top five teams, and 2.1 when we include the three lowest scoring projects ‐ with a low score of 1.3 and a high score of 3.0. The DataJam team confirmed that there was less data analysis presented in year two projects; they were more likely to rely on literature reviews, logical arguments, or descriptive statistics. Comments from The DataJam judges also focused on the lack of data science. As one judge noted after a team presented their project, “no real data analysis; they got numbers and plotted them but did not analyze data. The data on slide 7 is for 2020 but the data for slide 5 is a mean of 2020–2025.” Another judge reported about a different project, “They present numbers of ground water pollution but they did no analyses.” Judging notes for yet another project state, “Presenting Salmonella infections and amount of manure from a specific farm. They show the data but do not do a correlation, but eyeing the data it looks correlated. They collected survey data from workers.” As the scores and judging comments show, year two projects reflected less data science capacity building.

Focus groups with the winning teams revealed a largely positive experience. As one student explained, “we loved The DataJam and gave it all our effort; we sacrificed other things for it.” Another student added, “We don't regret the sacrifices because we saw the positive impact. We loved it, and it was a success.” They emphasized the importance of having math and data science skills for their future careers and the belief that they can personally have an impact on issues related to climate change. They also developed teamwork skills and felt an increased sense of confidence and pride in their work. It helped them “connect with each other as classmates” and they “learned and developed teamwork skills and how to be collaborative with others.” Teachers were similarly positive about the experience, pointing to the practical skills students learned and the support they received throughout the process from The DataJam and CAN. As one teacher explained, “There's a gap between education outputs and the skills students need for real‐life engagement. The DataJam helped close that gap by teaching practical skills.” Another teacher said, “This project was new for Jordan, and we gained valuable experience in research methods. I believe that each year, the students and teachers will improve, and the program will get better over time.”

Discussion

The results of The DataJam Jordan were shaped by the program's implementation. Transposing an informal STEM education program from one country to another and making changes between Year One and Year Two directly impacted students and their projects. To understand the results, we drew on the CFIR to identify determinants of the implementation and the observed outcomes. Three constructs were particularly explanatory relative to the intervention, the implementation process, and the context in which each step was implemented: (a) complexity, (b) work infrastructure, and (c) communication.

Complexity

As part of the innovation domain (refer back to Table 2), complexity refers to the scope or the number and nature of connections and steps in the implementation. For The DataJam, teams of students are asked to create their own data science projects from research design through data analysis and presentation. They are tasked with identifying problems that their communities face that are related to climate change, identifying data sources that might help them address questions related to those problems, learning and applying data science skills, preparing posters that clearly represent their work, and presenting their research to a panel of judges.

Through training, guidance, and data science resources, The DataJam provides structure for teams of students to undertake research projects with significant autonomy. While autonomy gives students the opportunity to pursue research questions that are relevant to them and their particular context, it also means that teams can formulate research questions that do not have sufficient and readily available data. In the U.S., formal project proposals are reviewed by DataJam staff before students are allowed to continue. This includes, importantly, whether the project has a good data set to answer their research question. This was not done formally in Jordan, but as an informal discussion with the teams. And although The DataJam prepared two data guides in Arabic for The DataJam Jordan, teams still had to make use of them.

To support students in navigating this complexity, teams in the U.S. are assigned undergraduate mentors who have received specific training in teaching statistical approaches and presentation skills to high school students. These mentors are overseen by university faculty who teach the DataJam mentor training course and by The DataJam executive director. To ensure that mentors are prepared to support the teams, they are required to go through an elaborate training process that includes undertaking a data science project of their own in The DataJam mentor training course.

To help manage the complexity, The DataJam provides access to a large number of publicly available data sets on The DataJam website and has prepared detailed guides for each step of The DataJam process (i.e., settling on a research question, finding appropriate data sets, applying analytical processes, and putting together posters and presentations). This process was transposed to Jordan. However, the organization of the implementation teams in years one and two shaped how the complexity of The DataJam was managed, which impacted teachers, students, and mentors.

Work Infrastructure

Work infrastructure refers to the staffing levels of the organization implementing the project as well as the distribution of tasks and responsibilities within and between teams. The DataJam Jordan was led by The DataJam executive director and a DataJam board member who is also a member of the GC3WEFH. They worked directly with the innovation deliverers in Jordan—the individuals who are directly or indirectly delivering the innovation. The need for additional innovation deliverers in Jordan added an additional layer between The DataJam leadership and the students, which both facilitated implementation and led to the mixed implementation outcomes. The change from one local team to another from year one to year two further complicated the implementation.

In year one, The DataJam Jordan was implemented by CAN. The decision by the GC3WEFH to use CAN was based on CAN's access to schools. In Jordan, schools are centrally managed by the Ministry of Education, with strict regulations requiring official approval for any activities involving students. CAN integrated The DataJam into an existing program, which already had approvals and community support. The CAN chief executive officer and her staff took the online DataJam training, with the exception of their own data science project, which they condensed into a 4‐week workshop for the teachers who served as mentors in year one. Including teachers as mentors generated buy‐in from the teachers. CAN also maintained close relationships with The DataJam teams throughout the competition and supported their work alongside the teachers.

In Year 2, program implementation was transferred to Hashemite University (HU) where it was led by a faculty member in the Department of Land Management and Environment who was also a member of the GC3WEFH. Moving The DataJam Jordan to the university facilitated the adoption of undergraduate student mentors who came from HU. This brought The DataJam more in line with how the program is run in the U.S. but it also meant starting over with a new implementation team that was unfamiliar with The DataJam. Unlike CAN staff the year before, the innovation deliverers at HU did not take the online training from The DataJam, which meant they were unfamiliar with the training that the mentors were expected to but also did not take. While the implementation team at HU, which also included a graduate student, provided extensive training to undergraduate mentors on critical thinking and data science, the specific logic, goals, and strategies of The DataJam were not delivered via The DataJam training. The lack of training for both the HU leads and the undergraduate mentors, and their lack of experience overseeing undergraduate DataJam mentors, created a context in which teams in year two did not use the available data guides and did little to no data analysis, as defined by The DataJam, in their projects.

Communication

Communication as defined in the CFIR refers to the formal and informal information sharing practices within and across the work infrastructure. There were many necessary points of communication for The DataJam Jordan. Members of the GC3WEFH, The DataJam, CAN, HU, the mentors, and the teachers and students all needed to communicate. The challenges of geographic distance meant that the ability to communicate directly was often limited to narrow windows of time. Language differences meant that some groups were not able to communicate directly, such as The DataJam leaders in the U.S. and the teachers and mentors in Jordan. There was also an impactful communication gap between the leaders of the GC3WEFH and the executive director of The DataJam who was not a funded member of the GC3WEFH but, due to her expertise, became the central person responsible for the delivery of DataJam resources such as mentor training curricula, data set guides, and overall advice.

Communication impacted both years. In year one, the research priorities of the GC3WEFH were not clearly communicated to the Jordan teams, which contributed to fewer than half of the projects incorporating health into their research on climate change. GC3WEFH leaders expected that The DataJam Jordan would make climate change and health the core focus of the students' projects. While the Jordanian DataJam students were directed to focus their research questions on climate change, the more specific topic of climate change effects on health was not communicated to them. This was a significant gap in communication between the GC3WEFH leadership, The DataJam team, and the CAN staff implementing DataJam in the Jordanian schools, with the result that the majority of Jordanian students participating in DataJam in year one did not build the specific capacity that the GC3WEFH was interested in.

In Year two, the GC3WEFH leadership communicated the center's priority of focusing on climate change and health to The DataJam implementation team, which was then communicated to The DataJam executive director. In response, a second data guide in Arabic was developed to focus specifically on the relationship between climate change and health. Like the first guide, it had links to useful data sets publicly available in Jordan. Nevertheless, few projects incorporated both climate change and health. This was due primarily to communication problems between GC3WEFH leadership and the implementation team. In accordance with the directive and broader discussions in the GC3WEFH, the implementation leads in Year 2 emphasized to mentors and project teams that their work should focus on health outcomes, as well as some aspects of climate change and its impacts on Water, Energy, and/or Food systems. This directive did not include, however, a requirement to work on projects that sought to understand the specific relationship between problems caused by climate change and health outcomes. As such, most teams worked on projects that touched on one or two of these themes, but rarely both climate change and health. Breakdowns in communication about the precise goals of The DataJam Jordan therefore led to incomplete instructions being shared with mentors and from them to the project teams.

Additionally, in year two, a failure of communication between The DataJam and HU, and HU and the mentors, contributed to the lack of data analysis in students' projects. For one, The DataJam did not communicate the necessity of completing the online training, which meant that neither the implementation team nor the mentors received the training, which emphasizes the need for students to conduct data analysis themselves such as inferential statistics. Instead, Data science was broadly understood by the implementation team as doing work with data, which could include finding and working with existing and published analyses of data, or descriptive analyses of found or original data. This misunderstanding led teams to work on projects that The DataJam does not consider data science, such as literature reviews or descriptive statistics.

Finally, communication barriers between the implementation team and students and between mentors and students led to implementation issues. Students had limited access to their mentors due to their limited access to computers. For female teams, in particular, communication between students and mentors was limited to conversations facilitated by teachers and during school hours due to cultural norms around gender. This communication chain also required significant teacher buy‐in. While year one incorporated teachers as mentors and therefore secured their buy‐in through training and direct engagement, Year 2 did not include teachers in the same way and therefore encountered a significant constituent that was less attached to the program, which impeded communication between teams and their mentors. Lastly, teachers and mentors were sometimes confused about their different roles in the program, leading to assumptions that one or the other had provided some necessary resource when neither had. Therefore, a lack of communication along the chain from The DataJam through to the mentors and teachers meant that some students lacked necessary resources and direction, which resulted in a lack of data analysis capacity building.

The challenges faced by the GC3WEFH in implementing The DataJam cannot be attributed to the students, mentors, or teachers nor are they a result of the schools, school system, or communities where The DataJam was implemented. While the complexity of The DataJam contributed to the misalignment between expectations and outcomes, the problems of implementation were ultimately problems of organization and communication among those responsible for implementation. Despite the challenges with implementation, the students completed many creative projects with local significance that increased their understanding of and interest in scientific research. In some cases, when students struggled to find existing data, they gathered new data. Although the GC3WEFH encourages community engagement around climate change and health, the openness of a program like The DataJam supports student autonomy to pursue ideas that interest them. Balancing autonomy with the importance of focusing young people's attention on climate change and health is therefore a critical challenge for engaging youth around these urgent topics. For the teams that presented at the GC3WEFH workshop in July 2024, the experience was especially transformative.

Conclusion

Engaging youth to address climate change and its impact on human health is critical for producing actionable science that can help vulnerable communities adapt to intensifying risks. Building youth capacity for research on climate change and health, including developing their data analysis skills, will empower communities to address these challenges into the future. To build youth capacity for research and data science in Jordan, the GC3WEFH implemented The DataJam in 21 high schools with 87 students over two school years. Focus group data suggests The DataJam Jordan had a positive impact on many of the students and their teachers. The DataJam increased Jordanian students' capacity to conduct research and do data analysis, particularly in year one. However, the fact that over half of student projects in year one and all but one project in year two did not focus on the intersection of climate change and health, and that many projects did not include significant data analysis in year two, meant that The DataJam Jordan faced obstacles to meeting expectations.

Implementing The DataJam in Jordan meant adapting the program for a new context. Applying the CFIR framework allowed us to see how the complexity of The DataJam, its work infrastructure with added layers of innovation deliverers that changed from year one to year two, and many points of communication complicated by geographic distance, language and cultural difference, and technology limitations emerged as the primary sources of the intervention's shortcomings. The addition of local innovation deliverers in Jordan to the work infrastructure of The DataJam created a greater need for communication between the implementation teams, and this need grew from year one to year two. Better adherence to The DataJam training process through better communication and greater organizational stability throughout the program will certainly improve the data science that students produce in the future.

Transposing an informal STEM program like The DataJam to a new context is challenging, particularly when managing large distances, multiple languages, and distinct cultures. Having trusted local partners that can facilitate implementation is key. Accessing schools, navigating government regulations, and garnering buy‐in from stakeholders all require local support. The DataJam had trusted partners in CAN and Hashemite University, but it took time to establish an effective working relationship. More intentional relationship building between the different implementation teams in the U.S. and Jordan would have fostered better communication and understanding across the project. Additionally, establishing clearer communication structures early on would have ensured greater adherence to the aims of the GC3WEFH and the processes of The DataJam. Over time, The DataJam Jordan developed communication systems and expectations that should bring the program into better alignment with both organizations.

Some challenges might have been avoided had the CFIR been used prior to implementing The DataJam. Internationally developed and rigorously evaluated programs like The DataJam have the potential to build critical data science capacity and climate‐health awareness among youth in regions vulnerable to the effects of global climate change. However, realizing that promise requires more than exporting a curriculum. It requires clarity around the program's goals, intervention context, and implementation strategy, and regular access to clear two‐way communication. Implementation frameworks like the CFIR can support successful implementation by clarifying program strengths and weaknesses even before they begin. While the implementation of The DataJam in Jordan was successful, its complexity, work infrastructure, and gaps in communication contributed to shortfalls. Anticipating these challenges in advance through the use of an implementation science framework would likely mitigate these obstacles. These lessons will inform future iterations of the program, improving the implementation of The DataJam in Jordan and in so doing supporting Jordan's capacity to respond to the health consequences of climate change.
