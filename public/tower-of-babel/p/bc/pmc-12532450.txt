Translated versions of the English satisfaction with life scale (SWLS) among adult participants: a systematic review
Authors: Razel Bacuetes Milo, Nicole Martinez, Theresa Asmus, Jennie Lee, Kathleen Soon, Jaden Milo, Patricia Calero
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLife satisfaction is the cognitive assessment of an individual’s current life circumstances. Since its introduction, the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS) has been translated into more than 30 languages. However, there is a limited information on the comprehensive synthesis of the psychometric methods of the translated SWLS versions. This systematic review aims to synthesize published studies on the psychometric methodologies of the translated versions of SWLS in the global population.MethodsThis systematic review was guided by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) reporting guidelines. The investigators utilized the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist for systematic reviews and Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) to evaluate the study’s evidence. The manuscript was pre-registered with NIHR PROSPERO.ResultsAll studies, except one (Quechua), demonstrated adequate internal consistency (α = 0.74 to 0.89) of the translated SWLS, with most studies using Cronbach’s α as the sole measure of internal consistency. Few studies explored other forms of reliability (e.g., test-retest). All studies reviewed evaluated various types of construct validity (convergent, discriminant, and measurement invariance) and criterion-related validity (concurrent). Only one study explored content validity.ConclusionFuture studies should prioritize a standardized psychometric methodology when translating and validating the SWLS in languages and regions where research on subjective well-being is still limited.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03498-y.Keywords: Satisfaction with life scale, SWLS, Translated SWLS, Psychometric SWLS, SWLS systematic review

Background

Subjective well-being measurements offer novel insights into societal quality of life, extending beyond traditional social and economic indicators. Comprehensive studies have found that high levels of subjective well-being (SWB) contribute positively to health, longevity, good citizenship, productivity at work, and social relationships [1, 2]. Life satisfaction is the cognitive assessment of an individual’s current life circumstances and personal criteria influenced by their adaptation process. It indicates how well individuals feel their aspirations and achievements were fulfilled [3]. The Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS) measures a person’s global cognitive judgment of their life satisfaction [4]. The SWLS scores correlate with measures of mental health [5]. An increasing number of researchers are focusing on the science of positive psychology and individual strengths to enhance the quality of life and prevent illnesses that arise when life feels empty and without meaning [6]. A study by Diener et al. [7] suggests that individuals who experience greater happiness tend to be healthier and have longer lifespans than those who are angry, depressed, or fearful.

As Diener suggests [8], a national account of well-being can enhance current economic and social indicators that measure the quality of life in countries, a concept that has been adopted in some form by over 40 nations. This systematic measurement of subjective well-being provides insight into circumstances associated with better well-being. It emphasizes subjective quality-of-life data that can identify and support the formulation and adoption of policymaking. Oishi [9] describes societal characteristics that predict high levels of subjective well-being, offering valuable insights for shaping government policies and programs to enhance community well-being. Layard [10] explicitly argues for better mental health programs to increase the subjective well-being of societies. The featured characteristics identified in happy societies include income security programs, healthcare security, and an eco-friendly environment. Economic and income growth are also measures of well-being. It can all help inform policy discussions without a politically affiliated approach. However, mental health remains one of the most underfunded areas despite its benefit to society’s overall health [11].

Since its introduction in 1985, the SWLS has been among the most widely used instruments for measuring the cognitive aspect of SWB, providing an overall assessment of life. SWLS offers insight into the positive aspects of individual experience, allowing individuals to reflect on their lives through self-assessment, independent of their current emotional state [3]. Diener and colleagues designed the SWLS to measure an individual’s well-being and life satisfaction by utilizing an instrument consisting of five items with seven response options on a 7-point Likert scale. It has been used in studies to assess the life satisfaction component of subjective well-being (SWB) [12]. The SWB consists of two primary components: affective and cognitive [4]. The affective aspect measures positive and negative emotions, whereas the cognitive element of SWB, which involves life satisfaction, is a conscious evaluation of one’s life against expectations or personal standards [4]. The SWLS has shown high internal consistency and temporal reliability [13] as a valid and reliable measure of life satisfaction with different age groups and populations [14]. SWLS has been translated into more than 30 languages since its introduction [15, 16]. It has been used in studies of diverse communities and validated in numerous languages, including Spanish [17], French [18], Dutch [47], German [20], Portuguese [21], Turkish [22], Korean [23], and Chinese [24]. Although investigators researched satisfaction with life across various cultures, it underscores the significant lack of standards for psychometric methodology. Additionally, it highlights the research gap within other communities, which remains largely unexplored. This systematic review aims to synthesize published peer-reviewed studies on the psychometric methodologies of translated versions of SWLS in the global population. In this review, the investigators’ aims are the following:

Examine the internal consistency, reliability, and validity of the translated SWLS in the individual studies.

Methods

The investigators synthesized eligible articles using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist for systematic reviews. Using JBI critical appraisal for observational studies assessment (cross-sectional), the investigators appraised the study’s relevance, results, and trustworthiness [25]. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guideline (Fig. 1) was the reporting guideline used in this review for transparent reporting of systematic reviews [26]. The manuscript was pre-registered with the National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR) PROSPERO identification number CRD42024593632. The systematic review protocol is available on the NIHR website, accessible via the review identification number.

Procedure

Search strategy

For the search strategy, the investigators utilized four databases to retrieve relevant articles from August 2024 to January 2025, including PubMed, PsycINFO, OVID, and CINAHL. These databases were used, as most of the studies related to SWLS have been found in these databases. These databases focus on mental health and healthcare studies. For example, CINAHL is a comprehensive database primarily used by healthcare professionals, where information on topics in biomedicine and mental health can be found. PubMed, as part of the National Library of Medicine, publishes biomedical and life sciences literature. For a more focused database search, they utilized PsycINFO, which offers a systematic coverage of the psychological literature. Lastly, for additional searching, they used OVID because it has an expansive collection of biomedical and health-related databases and journals. The timeframe was set to be greater than or equal to 5 years to capture all possible studies, given the limited number of psychometric studies on the translated versions of the SWLS. The mesh terms used were “satisfaction with life scale” (20,511 results), “satisfaction with life scale [title]” (64 results),” psychometric properties satisfaction with life scale” in the previous five years (538 results), “satisfaction with life scale adaptation” (947 results), “satisfaction with life scale psychometric properties” in the last five years (13 results), “satisfaction with life scale SWLS” in the previous five years (11 results), “psychometrics SWLS” (74 results), and “psychometrics + surveys and questionnaires + satisfaction with life (23 results). Six studies were from the references section of the two included articles. After incorporating the exclusion and inclusion parameters, thirty-four articles met the inclusion criteria for further analysis.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The investigators established the study’s inclusion and exclusion criteria based on the reported psychometric properties of the translated version of the SWLS. The criteria for including studies were as follows: studies that translated (or used a translated version of) the Diener 5-item SWLS into a language other than English, used the translated version of the SWLS among populations who spoke the translated language, and performed a psychometric analysis on the translated version of the SWLS. Examples of psychometric properties were Cronbach’s alpha, Kaiser-Mein-Olkin, Pearson’s r, Aiken’s V, and coefficient omega. Exclusion criteria included studies that analyzed the psychometric properties of a different life satisfaction or well-being scale, abbreviated versions of the 5-item SWLS, non-research studies, non-peer-reviewed articles, and studies involving participants under 16 years of age.

Study selection and quality assessment

The investigators selected quantitative articles to establish the psychometric properties of the translated SWLS versions and thoroughly analyze the instruments’ validity and reliability. The study participants were at least 16 years old and represented various groups (in terms of age, gender, education, language, and culture), having consented to participate. The investigators examined the instrument’s criterion, construct, and content validity, as discussed in the study. Furthermore, they examined the studies’ structural and cross-cultural validity for construct validity, as well as the stability of the scores, test-retest results, and correlation coefficients for internal consistency.

Data extraction

The primary and secondary investigators independently extracted and appraised the data using the Covidence software. The extracted data included country of origin, authors’ names, language, settings, study design, population, participant demographics (age, gender, and education), sampling method, instrument validity and reliability, and study outcomes. They independently assessed the reporting bias and compared the extracted data for consensus. A third investigator independently resolved any conflict. Finally, the primary investigator exported the extracted data to Excel for data synthesis.

Data synthesis

The data synthesis includes reviewing the descriptive and inferential statistics (correlation, regression, and factor analyses). The study variables were dichotomous and continuous. For effect measures, the investigators assessed internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s α, McDonald’s ω, and Raykov’s ρ) and stability reliability (test-retest reliability, as measured by Pearson’s r, Spearman’s ρ, or the intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC]) at different intervals. Investigators evaluated criterion-related (concurrent and predictive) and construct-related (convergent, discriminant, and measurement invariance) validity, as well as exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses. The descriptive analyses reported by the studies, including means, standard deviations, and percentages, were also evaluated. The demographic characteristics reported by the reviewed studies were synthesized and compared with those of other studies by the investigators. They examined the translation process, which varies by population, country, and region, as well as the translation validation (fully validated, partially validated, and validated in another study). The methodological process was evaluated and systematically reviewed by the investigators, who assessed the psychometric methods and study invariances.

Bias and certainty assessment

The articles were screened based on the study’s aims and eligibility criteria. The literature synthesis minimized bias by establishing the search protocol, establishing the study’s eligibility criteria, and using the JBI critical appraisal checklist [25]. The investigators independently screened and reviewed the article’s abstract, followed by a full-text review, data extraction, and bias assessment. Other factors considered in the literature review included the participants’ characteristics, data collection process, researcher subjectivity, sampling bias, study design, and flaws in the implementation process. The investigators utilized the risk of bias assessment tool ROBVIS to evaluate the study’s psychometric properties among the 34 articles eligible for full-text review (Fig. 2). Additionally they applied the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework to assess the study’s evidence. GRADE enables investigators to conclude about the quality of the evidence provided by the authors. The GRADE criteria included high (randomized study) and low (observational study) [27]. Using the Covidence program for interrater reliability, the abstract screening interrater reliability random agreement probability was 0.81, and a Cohen’s Kappa of 0.41, indicating moderate agreement between the reviewers. Regarding the full-text review, the random agreement probability was 0.6, and Cohen’s Kappa was 0.40, indicating moderate agreement [28].

Results

Study selection

The research team conducted database searches, and the articles selected for the systematic review were original studies with a primary focus on establishing and evaluating the psychometric properties of the translated SWLS. The electronic database search identified 22,181 records and six studies from the references of the two included studies. After identifying and removing duplicate articles (n = 150), the total yield of the search was 22,037. Further review of the search articles for eligibility criteria excluded articles that did not meet the requirements (psychometric properties of a different life satisfaction or well-being scale, any abbreviation of the 5-item SWLS, non-psychometric, non-research, non-peer-reviewed, and participants under 16). The research team excluded N = 21,995 abstracts (Fig. 1).

The graduate research assistant uploaded all 42 eligible abstracts into Covidence for further review, and the program identified three duplicate abstracts. The primary and secondary investigators screened 39 articles and excluded five ineligible studies (n = 34). The investigators included a total of 34 articles for full-text review. After the full-text review, they excluded fourteen studies for the following reasons: wrong study design (n = 1), wrong scale/instrument (n = 3), abbreviated SWLS version (n = 1), non-peer review article (n = 1), and participant < 16 years old (n = 8). The final number of articles for data extraction was 20 (Fig. 1). A third investigator resolved discrepancies between the first and second investigators during the abstract and full-text review. A statistician entered the study participants’ sociodemographic characteristics into a software package (SPSS 29) for analysis, calculating frequencies and measures of dispersion. The investigators treated the missing data as system missing.

Study design and characteristics

The synthesized studies were observational cross-sectional (n = 19) and prospective (n = 1) study designs. Since most studies are observational (non-random sampling) [17, 22, 23, 30–44, 47], the GRADE criteria assigned are low, and one study had randomized sampling with moderate GRADE [29]. The study countries of origin were Palestine, Malaysia, Israel, Pakistan, Mexico, Lithuania, Turkey, Colombia, Sweden, Germany, Portugal, Peru, Brazil, Spain, and South Korea.

The translated SWLS the investigators examined were Arabic (n = 1), Malay (n = 1), Hebrew (n = 1), Urdu (n = 1), Spanish (n = 5), Lithuanian (n = 1), Turkish (n = 1), Swedish (n = 2), German (n = 1), Dutch (n = 1), Portuguese (n = 3), Quechua (n = 1), and Korean (n = 1). Some studies administered SWLS with other instruments measuring depression (n = 5), anxiety (n = 3), hopelessness/hope (n = 2), self-esteem (n = 2), suicide ideation (n = 1), affect (n = 3), emotions (n = 1), general health (n = 5), psychological/psychiatric health (n = 3), social support/relations (n = 3), self-censor (n = 1), life-orientation (n = 2), flourishing (n = 1), fatigue (n = 1), sleep (n = 2), loneliness (n = 1), happiness (n = 1), well-being (n = 3), and quality of life (n = 1). Six studies administered the SWLS alone. Two scales originated from two major US universities, UCLA (University of California Los Angeles Loneliness Scale) and Duke (Functional Support Questionnaire). Study participants

All participants were at least 16 years old and came from various backgrounds (i.e., psychiatric and medical patients, working adults, correctional officers, elderly, students, retirees, and the general population). Most studies employed non-random sampling (n = 17); one study conducted a random sampling in 20 cities and 29 villages [29]; one study used probability proportion-to-size sampling [23]; and one study used population proportion by region and city size [17]. Nine study samples were from the general population and did not test the instrument in a specific population. By contrast, eleven studies tested the translated SWLS on specific populations [22, 30–33]. One study compared patient findings from two groups: psychiatric and medical patients from a university medical center [31]. Another study compared three groups, including students (n = 547), correctional officers (n = 166), and elderly adults (n = 123) [22]. Six studies recruited specific age groups: eighteen to thirty-one [34], twenty-seven to sixty years old [32], eighteen to eighty years old [41], older than fifty years old [30], student retirees ages forty-eight to 90 years old [33], and twenty-one to sixty-nine years old [17]. In a study by López-Ortega et al. [30], the sample was from the 2012 Mexican and Aging study. Moreover, one study required participants to have a minimum of 10 years of education [32].

The studies’ sample sizes ranged from 51 [42] to 13,220 participants [30]. The participants’ average age ranged between 19 ± 2.18 and 73 ± 8 years. One study did not report the average age [23]; instead, it used a 50-year cutoff to report percentages per age group (54.6% < 50 years and 45.4% ≥ 50 years). Age ranges varied, with some studies spanning from 16 to 75 years [31], 18 to 47 years [42], to 60 to 85 years [22]. The youngest participant was 16 years old, and the oldest was 94 years old. Most studies had a higher proportion of women than men, except for a small sub-study with 56% male participants [42], a study in Palestine with 60% male participants [34], and a study in Sweden with 58% male participants [39].

Among studies that reported education (n = 16), most used categories to define educational attainment, with only three [30, 32, 35] reporting the average years of education (averages ranging from 7.4 to 15.5 years). The education categories varied from 1 to 6 categories. For instance, in one study [22], education was defined as high school education or higher, with no additional details provided. In another study [23], education was dichotomized as less than or equal to a high school education or greater than a high school education. Three studies only indicated the percentage of participants with higher education [34, 35, 42]. Two studies categorized education into three groups using different cutoffs (e.g., Hinz et al. [41] used less than or equal to 10 years, 10 to 11 years, and greater than or equal to 12 years, while López-Ramos et al. [33] used basic, secondary, and higher education). Categories for studies using four [31, 36, 43] and five categories [17, 38] were inconsistent. Over half of the participants in ten studies had a high school education or greater [22, 31, 33–36, 38, 41, 43, 44], with five of the sixteen studies having close to 100% of participants completing high school. Participants in four studies had primarily less than a high school education [17, 23, 30, 41]. Overall, the studies reviewed did not have consistent age ranges or educational groups.

Description of psychometric properties

All but four of the studies evaluated the internal consistency of the SWLS, reporting Cronbach’s alpha. Mamani-Benito et al. [44] reported McDonald’s omega instead, which is based on factor analysis and does not rely on inter-item correlations as Cronbach’s alpha does; this makes McDonald’s omega a more robust measure of internal consistency when assumptions about item correlations are not met [45, 46]. Moreover, Checa et al. [37] reported Raykov’s rho, which accounts for differences in factor loadings, and Silva et al. [40] reported both McDonald’s omega and ordinal alpha for greater accuracy. Only one study [47] did not report on the internal consistency of the translated SWLS, as the study focused on testing multi-dimensional SWLS models. Four studies conducted test-retest reliability analyses—via Pearson’s r, paired samples t-test, and ICC—with time intervals of 2 weeks [39], 30 days [34, 42], and 60 days [35].

The study’s validation techniques varied. Except for one study that only reported criterion-related (concurrent) validity [34], all others reported construct validity with different statistical methods: Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) via principal component analysis (PCA) with Promax rotation (n = 1), Varimax rotation (n = 3) or without rotation (n = 2); confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) via structural equation modeling (SEM) (n = 14) using different fit indices, such as chi-square by degrees of freedom, Comparative Fit Index (CF), Incremental Fit Index (IFI), Normed Fit Index (NFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), or standardized root mean squared residual (SRMR) among others. The other validity methods reported were concurrent, convergent, and discriminant. Concurrent validity was assessed using Pearson’s r (n = 4); convergent validity was evaluated using both Pearson’s r and Spearman’s ρ (n = 6) or the average variance extracted (n = 1). The discriminant validity was assessed using logistic regression (n = 1), Pearson’s r (n = 2), and a paired samples t-test (n = 1). A study in Perú assessed the content validity of the Quechua SWLS via Aiken’s V coefficient [44]. Six studies assessed validity via measurement invariance (configural, metric, scalar, strict) via multi-group CFA by gender [36, 44], gender and age [41], gender and occupation [38], gender, age, education and marital status [37], and gender, age, work activity and economic level [40] (Table 2).

Synthesis of the translation procedure

The translation procedure for the selected study in this review varied by setting, translated instruments, population, and country of origin. Most investigators used back-to-back translation (n = 6) [17, 22, 29, 31, 32, 44]. Other investigators used forward-backward translations, which were translated into the target language by different researchers without any additional translation process or evaluation of their own (n = 14) [23, 30, 33, 35–43, 47]. One study employed forward translation by two independent bilingual individuals, with pilot testing of the instrument conducted among university students [34]. Three translations involved health professionals (2 to 8 psychologists) translating and back-translating the SWLS independently into the target language and English [17, 31, 41]; other translations utilized health professionals (psychologists) and professional interpreters (n = 2) [22, 44]. Three studies used native speakers fluent in English, including students [32, 34, 35]. In one study, the investigators completed the translation [29]. Eleven of the studies used existing translated SWLS instruments in their research: Spanish (n = 3) [30, 41, 43], Portuguese (n = 3) [33, 40, 42], German (n = 2) [41, 47], Swedish (n = 2) [38, 39], and Urdu (n = 1) [36] (Table 3). Two studies conducted a pilot of the final translated SWLS to ensure cultural and semantic equivalence [32, 34].

Synthesis of the psychometric analysis

Measurement of Reliability. Internal consistency for the studies ranged from α = 0.74 for the Mexican Spanish SWLS [30] to α = 0.90 for the Urdu and Swedish SWLS [36, 39]. Although the alpha value for good internal consistency varies depending on the source, many sources consider an internal consistency of 0.7 or higher to be acceptable [48]. In this systematic review, twelve studies reported good internal consistency (α ≥ 0.9), with three studies reporting acceptable internal consistency (α ≥ 0.8) (Table 1). The weighted average reliability coefficient based on sample size for 16 studies with available Cronbach α value was 0.82. In addition to Cronbach’s alpha, two studies included other measures of internal consistency (i.e., McDonald’s ω) with similar results; for instance, the McDonald’s omega for the German SWLS was 0.89 [41] and for the Portuguese SWLS 0.86–0.89 (ordinal α = 0.862–0.889) [40]. The Raykov’s rho for the Spanish SWLS was 0.869 [37]. However, for the Peruvian Quechua, the reliability of SWLS was low (ω = 0.65) [44]. Thus, all the translated SWLS instruments demonstrated good internal consistency, except for the Peruvian Quechua version.

One study [42] conducted a test-retest reliability analysis of the Brazilian Portuguese version of the SWLS with 34 participants at a 30-day interval. The result of the paired samples t-tests showed no significant differences between the means of the first and second SWLS administration (M = 4.35 ± 1.34 and M = 4.45 ± 1.41) and a significant Pearson’s r between them (r = .79, p < .01), indicating high temporal stability of the Brazilian Portuguese version of the SWLS. Similarly, another study assessing the test-retest reliability of the translated SWLS at a 30-day interval also found high temporal stability for the Arabic (Palestinian) instrument, with a Pearson’s r of 0.83 [34], as well as for the Portuguese instrument. Unsurprisingly, temporal stability was evident at a 14-day interval for the Swedish SWLS with an ICC of 0.78 (p < .01) [39] but not for the Spanish (México) SWLS at a 60-day interval with a Pearson’s r of 0.575 (p < .001) [35].

Measurement of Validity. All studies reviewed evaluated various types of constructs (EFA, CFA, convergent, and discriminant), as well as criterion-related (concurrent) and content validity. Almost all studies assessed construct validity: one via EFA [30], twelve via PCA [17, 22, 32, 33, 35–37, 40, 41, 43, 44, 47], and seven via EFA and CFA [23, 28, 31, 33, 38, 42]. The studies using EFA selected this method to identify all sources of variability, and the maximum explained variance with the minimum number of factors (single-factor structure) [49]; the other studies utilized factor analysis to focus only on shared variability using Varimax, Promax, or no rotation depending on the sample size and specific characteristics of their varied samples. Four studies [23, 30, 31, 42] reported eigenvalues and percent variance (all eigenvalues well above one and variances approached 70%), while the other two [29, 33] focused on commonalities and parallel analysis. Regardless of the method, studies confirmed the single-factor structure of the translated Malay, Lithuanian, Spanish (México), Portuguese (Portugal and Brazil), and Korean SWLS; however, a study observed multidimensionality of the Dutch SWLS [47], with individual items capturing unique aspects of individuals’ life satisfaction rather than a source of bias. Though the method used to explore the multidimensionality of the Dutch SWLS is considerably more complex, it may provide a deeper understanding of this psychological construct.

Seventeen studies [17, 22, 23, 29, 31–33, 35–38, 40–44, 47] assessed construct validity via CFA and SEM (Table 1). Although different studies reported different fit indexes—Anaby et al. [32] reported CFI and RMSEA, Dirzyte et al. [29] reported CFI, NFI, and TLI, Duran and colleagues [22] reported CFI, IFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR, Blázquez et al. [35] reported CFI, Goodness of Fit Index (GFI), RMSEA, and SRMR—they depend on the studies’ characteristics. Results confirm the good fit of the unidimensional model of the translated SWLS.

Four studies [22, 31, 33, 34] evaluated the concurrent validity of the SWLS via Pearson’s r correlations, with Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), Life Satisfaction Index (LSI), and Revised Symptom Checklist-90 (SCL-90-R) [34]; Depression Anxiety Stress Scale (DASS-21), Reasons for Living Inventory (RLI), Beck Hopelessness Scale (BHS), Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSE), Positive and Negative Suicide Ideation Inventory (PANSI), Provision of Social Relations (PSR), and Adult Trait Hope Scale (AHT) [31]; with BDI, Positive and Negative Affect Scale (PANAS), RSE, and Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) [22]; and with Geriatric Depression Scale (BDS-15) and University of California Los Angeles Loneliness Scale (UCLA-6) [33]. Results indicate good concurrent validity with measures of life satisfaction, depression, negative affect, loneliness, suicide ideation (protective and risk factors), self-esteem, hope, and perceived social support of the Arabic, Malay, Turkish, and Portuguese (Portugal and Brazil) SWLS (Table 1).

Seven studies assessed convergent validity, using Pearson’s r and Spearman’s ρ with PANAS [42]; PANAS, BDI, and Multidimensional Scale for the Measurement of Subjective Well-being (EMMBSAR) [35]; Life Orientation Test-Revised (LOT-R), Multidimensional Fatigue Inventory (MFI-20), Short-Form Health Survey (SF-8), Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-15 and PHQ-9), Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD-7), Life Orientation Tests (LOT), Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) [41]; Subjective Happiness Scale (SHS), Duke-UNC Functional Social Support Questionnaire (FSSQ), and Subjective Well-Being Inventory (SWBI) [17]; Flourishing Scale (FS), Scale of Positive and Negative Experience (SPANE) and LOT-R [43]; and SWBI and McGill Quality of Life Questionnaire (MQOL) [23] (Table 1); Silva et al. [40] utilized the AVE to assess convergent validity. Some of the studies reviewed reported adequate (r ≥ .50) [50]. The convergent validity of the translated SWLS, e.g., Spanish (México) and Korean SWLS with measures of subjective well-being [23, 35], Portuguese (Brazil) SWLS with measures of life satisfaction [42], and Spanish (Colombian) SWLS with measures of optimism and positive emotions [43], suggest the presence of similar underlying concepts. However, the convergent validity of the German SWLS was inadequate in terms of measures of physical and mental health, as well as optimism, and it showed an adequate level of convergent validity with fatigue [41]. Similarly, the Spanish (Spain) SWLS with measures of happiness and social functioning [17] and the Spanish (México) SWLS with positive emotions [35] showed inadequate convergent validity (Table 1), possibly pointing to cultural and linguistic differences, translation issues, or the complexity of the life satisfaction construct across cultures.

Four studies evaluated the discriminant validity of the translated SWLS using diverse methods and instruments. Studies evaluated the discriminant validity of the Malay SWLS with the PANSI via logistic regression [31], the Turkish SWLS with the Willingness to Self-Censor Scale (WSCS) via Pearson’s r [22], the Portuguese (Brazil) SWLS with the PANAS via paired samples t-test [42], and the Korean SWLS with the SWBI, MQOL, and PHQ-9 via Pearson’s r [23]. The Malay SWLS showed good discriminant validity between participants who attempted suicide and a control group (overall classification accuracy of 85.2%). The Korean SWLS and depression (r = -.31, p < .01) and the Turkish SWLS with an unrelated construct, willingness to self-censor (r = -.08, p = .54), demonstrated good discriminant validity.

Six studies assessed the measurement invariance including the Urdu (scalar; gender), German (configural; gender, age), Quechua (configural; gender), Spanish (metric, scalar; gender, age, marital status), Swedish (configural, metric, scalar; gender, occupation), and Portuguese (strict; age, gender, work activity, economic level). SWLS scalar invariance was observed across genders for the Urdu [36], Spanish [37], and Swedish [38] versions of the SWLS, indicating no difference in the interpretation of the translated measure by men and women. There was no significant gender bias in the translated SWLS for these groups. Similarly, the German SWLS [41] demonstrated configural invariance by gender (ΔCFI < 0.01), indicating men and women understood and responded to the items in a similar manner. Additionally, the Quechua SWLS demonstrated configural invariance. Still, there is no metric or scalar equivalence by gender (ΔCFI = 0.065 and 0.030, respectively), suggesting the general structure of the model is the same for men and women in this population. Yet, they understand the items and respond differently depending on their gender. Strict measure invariance, the most difficult to achieve [51], was also evident across age for the German [41] and Portuguese [40] SWLS and metric invariance across age for the Spanish SWLS [37]. The Portuguese sample had an age range of 18 to 35, while the German and Spanish samples had age ranges of 18 to 80 and 18 to 71, respectively. Age did not impact how these samples across different cultures interpreted and responded to the translated SWLS. Other measures of invariance observed included metric and scalar invariance of the Spanish SWLS across gender, age, and education (not across marital status) [37]; configural, metric, and scalar invariance of the Swedish SWLS across gender and occupation [38]; strict invariance of the Portuguese SWLS across gender, work activity, and economic level [40].

Discussion

This systematic review aimed to synthesize published peer-reviewed studies on the psychometric methodologies of translated versions of SWLS in the global population. The investigators identified thirty-four studies to review psychometric methods and address the study’s purpose. According to the investigators’ knowledge, this is the first systematic review to present a comprehensive analysis of the psychometric properties of the translated SWLS across different populations. The systematic review results show the differences in the study population and the usefulness and relevance of the psychometric properties of the translated SWLS versions within the global population. For example, the high internal consistency of the translated versions demonstrates reliability in responses across these diverse populations. The high internal consistency encompasses the instrument’s measurement reliability, helping to assess the relative consistency in a specific population when measuring the life satisfaction attribute.

Noteworthy is the low internal consistency (McDonald’s ω = 0.65) of the indigenous Peruvian Quechua SWLS despite a rigorous translation process (Table 3). Although the authors conceded that the reliability was lower than that obtained in other studies, they still considered it acceptable, attributing the low reliability to the number of response options for the SWLS used in their study (five vs. seven). Several factors can contribute to the low internal consistency of a translated measure, including the nature of the construct, cultural context, and issues within the scale itself [52–54]. Although the good fit of the single-factor model confirms that the Quechua SWLS measures similar aspects of life satisfaction, the lack of metric invariance by gender and low reliability suggest that the translated SWLS might not be understood in the same way by Quechua men and women. Possible cultural and contextual differences by gender in this population may have significantly impacted the reliability and applicability of the translation.

In this systematic review, only four studies conducted a test-retest [34, 35, 39, 42]. However, only one adhered to the standard two-week interval typically recommended for scale administration, allowing for day-to-day variations in the participant’s life experiences. This interval is generally optimal for minimizing memory effects that can inflate the reliability estimate. Deviating from this interval may compromise the ability to distinguish between score changes resulting from limited instrument reliability. Therefore, the instrument’s reliability, as measured by the test-retest method, is not subject to significant variation. One of the limitations of this method is the lack of a proper parallel design. In addition, if the period between the tests is less than two weeks (too short), the participant may become sensitized to the concepts measured for life satisfaction, change their response based on the first measurement, and appear more consistent than they are [55].

The SWLS demonstrated a one-dimensionality across populations via CFA. It established a good fit of a unidimensional model in clinical and non-clinical populations across Malaysia, Israel, Pakistan, Mexico, Spain, Lithuania, Turkey, Colombia, Germany, Portugal, Peru, Brazil, and South Korea. The Dutch SWLS was the only study reviewed that did not support the single-factor structure [47], as individual items captured unique aspects of participants’ life satisfaction, providing a deeper understanding of this psychological construct. Although the SWLS has a single-factor structure [17, 56], some studies suggest that items may exhibit unique variances, capturing different aspects of life satisfaction [3, 5, 56]. Further study of the SWLS multidimensionality is statistically more complex; however, it may provide a better understanding of life satisfaction.

In the case of translated SWLS, there is a lack of consistency with the statistical methods and translation process. Therefore, the construct validity is significant in establishing the psychometric properties of the translated SWLS. The inconsistency in instrument administration reflects the broader variability in study execution and highlights the lack of standardization across all studies. A potential risk for bias and unavoidable confounding variables exacerbate these challenges, which may contribute to a lack of evidence. Though the SWLS consistently demonstrated strong reliability and validity, evidence of its use in low-resource settings, such as rural regions, is limited.

Some selected studies have compared the translated SWLS’s convergent or divergent validity with that of other mental health instruments. The data patterns identified through the synthesis exemplified a recurring theme: strong positive correlations were evident with numerous other instruments that measured psychological and mental health identifiers. By establishing the construct validity of these studies, the authors measure the SWLS correlation with other mental health instruments (e.g., BDI, PANSI, PANAS, RSE, PSR, PANAS, SRH, RSES, MSPS, SF-8, FSSQ, SWBI, and MQOL). The strong positive correlations with mental health instruments support concurrent validity, demonstrating positive relationships with optimism, positive affect, self-esteem, hope, and perceived social support. In contrast, it suggests negative relationships with loneliness, symptoms of depression, pessimism, suicide ideation, and anxiety. It is important to note that the correlations between translated SWLS and these instruments do not capture the same concept. Thus, clinicians must interpret scores cautiously when using the translated SWLS with any of these instruments to assess a patient’s mental health.

Due to the nature of social science, it is often necessary to establish the direct measure of an instrument, which is, in the case of the criterion validity of the translated SWLS. Three selected studies established the criterion validity [17, 23, 31] by comparing patients with certain conditions (mental health and physical health). Since only three studies determined the criterion validity, there is limited information about the criterion validity of the other translated versions of the SWLS. Regarding cultural context, the rigor and consistency of translation procedures varied notably across studies. Methods included back-to-back translation, forward-backward translation, and the use of professional translators. These variations may affect the comparability of results and lead to measurement bias. Most studies focused on psychometric evaluation, with limitations in longitudinal outcomes. The studies also relied on self-reported measures, which may have introduced bias in the participants’ responses. The accuracy of translations and the impact of cultural differences may need reevaluation in future studies.

Often overlooked sources of variability in validation studies arise from the researcher’s discretionary decisions throughout the research process. Researcher degrees of freedom refer to the choices made during study design, data collection, analysis, and interpretation that can unintentionally or deliberately shape the results of a validation study. The decisions to select specific inclusion criteria, modify statistical techniques, or emphasize certain outcomes can introduce variability that undermines the comparability of validation studies. When studies assess the validity of an instrument or intervention with inconsistent methodologies, it becomes challenging to synthesize findings across research efforts. The lack of standardization in researcher degrees of freedom presented a significant challenge in conducting this systematic review, particularly due to the issue of selective reporting, which is critical in cross-cultural research.

To mitigate the impact of researchers’ degrees of freedom, future efforts should adopt standardized approaches that enhance methodological transparency and consistency. Researchers can use predefined validation frameworks to facilitate comparability across studies. Additionally, encouraging the publication of all methodological decisions, including exploratory analyses, will improve the reliability and reproducibility of validation efforts. Establishing structured reporting templates and adhering to widely accepted validation criteria can ensure that future studies contribute to a more coherent and trustworthy body of research on validation.

Establishing measurement invariance is a foundational step in cross-cultural research, ensuring that a given instrument measures the same underlying construct consistently across different groups. Without establishing invariance, differences in scores may reflect variations in interpretation, cultural norms, or response biases rather than true differences in the instrument’s construct, which is particularly important with the SWLS. Additionally, comparisons between populations’ risk can be misleading, as any observed differences may stem from measurement artifacts rather than genuine disparities in life satisfaction.

The absence of invariance testing is a critical gap that impacts the use of translated versions of the SWLS. Without invariance testing, translated versions may introduce inconsistencies in interpretation or scale functioning, leading to unreliable conclusions. For instance, certain phrases or concepts in the SWLS may carry different connotations depending on linguistic or cultural norms, which can affect response patterns. Consequently, failing to test invariance undermines the credibility of comparative studies, limiting the scale’s effectiveness in cross-cultural assessments and its ability to inform meaningful global insights on well-being. Establishing robust validation procedures, including invariance testing, is crucial to maintaining the integrity of research findings across diverse populations.

The findings from this systematic review reflected reasonable and fair sample sizes, which may enhance the generalizability of the results. These findings provide evidence that the instrument, in its translated form, demonstrates consistency and validity across diverse populations worldwide, and its applicability can yield consistent and reliable results. However, due to the variation in the sampling methods, there is a lack of consistency in the sampling process. Additionally, underrepresented groups, including those with lower literacy or individuals from rural areas, faced limitations.

Implications

This systematic review highlights the SWLS as a reliable and valid instrument that can be effectively adapted cross-culturally in general and clinical populations. While its validation in multiple languages is promising, significant research gaps remain in other underrepresented populations. Future studies should prioritize translating and validating the SWLS using standardized psychometric methods in languages and regions where research on subjective well-being is still limited.

The SWLS is a valuable instrument for assessing a population’s subjective well-being and can serve as a key indicator to guide social and economic advancements. Policymakers can leverage SWLS data to develop targeted interventions to improve the socioeconomic and psychological determinants of well-being. Future research should investigate the utilization of the SWLS across more diverse age groups, particularly adolescents. Ultimately, further studies are necessary to investigate its long-term reliability across diverse populations. Incorporating mixed-methods approaches may complement future research to capture a holistic understanding of life satisfaction.

Limitations

This systematic review provided a comprehensive literature synthesis of the translated SWLS psychometric evaluations across multiple cultures and populations, both in general and clinical contexts. The literature synthesis addressed the study’s purpose and aims. Still, it was limited to the type of study design (non-experimental), sampling methods, and statistical analyses as reported by the selected studies. Some studies discussed their translation process, while others provided little information, which makes it a challenge to develop an evidence-based research protocol for a psychometric study. The differences in statistical methods to establish the reliability and validity of the translated versions are a limitation. In addition, due to the lack of reliable sample size calculation, which is an integral part of a study, the sample size calculation differs. A reliable study design and sample calculation approach would allow future studies to replicate these psychometric procedures in other languages and cultures, requiring further exploration. Therefore, it will create more robust evidence and improve the psychometric evaluations of the translated SWLS version. Future studies should focus on establishing a research protocol for the psychometric methods of the translated SWLS. A standardized approach to psychometric methodology would enable other researchers to consistently and accurately verify the reliability and validity of the translated SWLS in diverse populations from various cultures.

Conclusions

The SWLS is a valid instrument for measuring life satisfaction across diverse populations and contexts. It demonstrates strong psychometric properties, including internal consistency, one-dimensionality, and cross-cultural adaptability. These findings affirm its reliability as an effective instrument for measuring subjective well-being in general and clinical settings. Although limitations include reliance on different study designs, underrepresentation of vulnerable and rural populations, and a lack of longitudinal studies, the implications are significant for research, practice, and policy. Addressing its current limitation will ensure continued relevance and applicability in advancing the understanding of subjective well-being globally. Future research should prioritize inclusivity and real-world applicability by utilizing the SWLS as more than just a research instrument but also as a measure to enhance individual and community well-being.
