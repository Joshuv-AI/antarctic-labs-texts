Enhancing the performance of dental composites with nanomaterial reinforcements via stereolithographic additive manufacturing
Authors: Mitesh Patadia, Tripp T. Lappalainen, Riley Smith, Ana De Leon, Rebekah Downes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimThis study investigates the enhancement of mechanical and morphological properties of dental resin composites through the incorporation of hexagonal boron nitride (hBN) and boron nitride nanotubes (BNNTs) using additive manufacturing techniques.Materials and methodshBN-modified resin (1 wt%) and BNNT-modified resin (0.1 wt%) were prepared separately, with BNNTs pre-dispersed in dimethylformamide (DMF) before mixing into the resin matrix. Stereolithography (SLA) 3D printing was employed to fabricate dental structures. Compression tests were conducted on neat resin, hBN-reinforced resin, and BNNT-reinforced resin, and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was utilized to analyze fracture mechanisms. Finite element method (FEM) simulations further explored the interactions within the composites.ResultsThe compression strength of neat resin, hBN-reinforced resin, and BNNT-reinforced resin averaged 24.93 MPa, 25.92 MPa, and 36.31 MPa, respectively. SEM analysis revealed improved interfacial bonding, leading to enhanced load transfer and fracture resistance. FEM simulations corroborated these findings, highlighting the reinforcing effect of the nanomaterials.

Introduction

Artificial teeth have been used for centuries to address dental deficiencies, evolving significantly in materials and design. Initially, materials such as bone and wood were utilized, but advancements have led to the development of artificial teeth made from composite materials [1, 2]. Dental composites have garnered extensive research attention due to their affordability, non-toxicity, durability, and excellent mechanical and antibacterial properties [3]. Dental resin composites (DRCs) are a primary material in modern dentistry, serving purposes such as crowns, restorative materials, endodontic sealers, and orthodontic devices [1]. These composites consist of an organic resin matrix containing multifunctional monomers, photoinitiators, and a mix of organic or inorganic fillers [4, 5]. Nanofillers are incorporated to enhance the properties of DRCs. These fillers improve the composite’s mechanical strength, dimensional stability under varying temperatures, and other functional characteristics [6, 7]. The choice of filler material depends on the desired attributes. For example, silver is added for its antibacterial qualities, while zirconia provides a white color that aligns aesthetically with natural teeth. The inclusion of multiple additives enables DRCs to achieve superior mechanical performance and meet diverse clinical requirements [8]. Many research has been done on particle-added resin-based composite for dental restorative [9–20].

The strength and durability of dental materials are crucial for the long-term success of resin composites in filling cavities. Boron, a trace element found in bones and teeth, is known for its potential to enhance both the mechanical and antibacterial properties of dental materials. Boron compounds have demonstrated the ability to improve the physical attributes, decay resistance, and surface characteristics of dental composites. Boron nitride (BN), a boron-based compound, is widely used due to its excellent physical and chemical properties. It exists in various crystalline forms, such as hexagonal, cubic, and wurtzite structures, each offering distinct advantages [21–23]. Hexagonal boron nitride (hBN), also referred to as “white graphite” due to its structural similarity to graphite, is frequently used in biomedical applications because of its non-toxicity, mechanical robustness, and thermal stability [24]. Additionally, hBN coatings can act as barriers, preventing harmful bacterial by-products from affecting surrounding tissues [25]. Studies suggest that hBN synthesized from boric acid shows promise for wound healing, bone tissue growth, and neurodegenerative treatments [26].

hBN produces boron nitride nanotubes (BNNTs) When restructured into a tubular form, which exhibits exceptional mechanical properties, including a tensile strength of 33 GPa, Young’s modulus of 1.3 TPa, and a bending modulus of approximately 760 GPa [27]. BNNTs are also biocompatible and non-cytotoxic, and due to their exceptional properties, BNNTs are widely used in different biomedical applications, such as orthopedic dental, to increase their mechanical properties. Also, it was found that it is non-cytotoxic to embryonic kidney cells and neuroblastoma cell lines [28, 29]. BNNTs enhance the mechanical and thermal properties of dental materials and have been shown to improve dental adhesives when used in low concentrations (e.g., up to 0.15 wt%) [30]. Additionally, combining BNNTs with materials like zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO) has demonstrated enhanced antibacterial effects in dental resins.

Advances in manufacturing technologies, such as additive manufacturing, have revolutionized the production of dental materials. Unlike traditional manufacturing methods, which remove material to shape a product, additive manufacturing builds objects layer by layer, using only the necessary material. This approach reduces waste and increases efficiency, making it an ideal technique for dental applications [31]. Additive manufacturing also enables the creation of complex, customized designs that are difficult to achieve with traditional techniques. As a result, it provides greater design flexibility and adaptability to meet specific clinical needs. By optimizing designs, additive manufacturing can produce components that are lighter, stronger, and more durable than conventionally manufactured alternatives [6, 32].

Stereolithography (SLA) and digital light processing (DLP) are prominent additive manufacturing techniques used in dentistry. Both methods cure UV resin layer by layer to form the final product. These processes involve lowering a build platform into a resin tank, where light cures the resin into a solid structure. SLA and DLP printing offer high accuracy, producing parts with details as fine as 30 microns [33]. They are also scalable, allowing for the production of both small and large components. However, the orientation of the printed object can affect its mechanical performance due to the layering process [34]. These technologies have been successfully applied across various industries, including medical, automotive, and consumer goods [35, 36].

This research investigates the integration of hBN and BNNTs into dental resin composites and their effects on mechanical properties. The computer simulation was also performed to analyze the interaction of resin with different nanomaterials. By combining advanced materials with cutting-edge manufacturing techniques, this study aims to enhance dental composites’ durability, functionality, and clinical performance.

Experimentation

Methodology

Two types of nanofillers, hexagonal boron nitride (hBN) and boron nitride nanotube (BNNT), were incorporated into dental resin from Formlabs (Somerville, MA), using distinct methods. Purified multi-walled BNNTs were provided by BNNT materials. Figure 1 illustrates the mixing process for the hBN-reinforced dental resin. Initially, 1 wt% of hBN powder was measured and added to the resin, ensuring the correct powder-to-ink ratio for optimal properties. The mixture was then placed in a Thinky mixer set to 2000 rpm for 5 min for thorough blending. This high-speed blending ensures uniform dispersion of the hBN particles throughout the resin, which is crucial for achieving superior print quality and maximizing the effectiveness of the hBN additive. Once the mixing process was completed, the well-dispersed hBN dental resin was ready for printing.

Fig. 1hBN mixing process into the dental resin

hBN mixing process into the dental resin

Figure 2 depicts the process for mixing and printing the BNNT-reinforced dental resin. Firstly, 0.1 wt% BNNTs were weighed and dispersed into DMF, ensuring the accurate ratio of both materials for controlled solvent evaporation in later steps. The BNNT-DMF mixture was stirred using a magnetic stirrer (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) at 120 rpm for 96 h to achieve a uniform dispersion and prevent agglomeration of the BNNTs. After stirring, bath sonication was applied in Cole-Parmer Ultrasonic Cleaner (Vernon Hills, IL) for 1 h to further break down any BNNT clusters and improve dispersion. Once the mixture achieved a homogeneous distribution, the dental resin was gradually added to the BNNT-DMF solution, ensuring even incorporation of the resin. The resulting blend was then placed on a ThermoFisher hot plate with a mixer processed at 80 °C and 200 rpm for 120 h, which enabled thorough integration of the BNNTs into the resin. After mixing, the solvent evaporation was confirmed by weighing the final product to verify the complete removal of DMF. This carefully controlled process results in a stable BNNT-reinforced dental resin with enhanced properties, which is ideal for advanced applications.

Fig. 2Steps for preparing and manufacturing BNNT dental resin

Steps for preparing and manufacturing BNNT dental resin

After the printing process, which was carried out using the Formlabs Form 2 SLA 3D printer (Somerville, MA), all three types of teeth—neat, hBN-reinforced, and BNNT-reinforced—were cleaned by immersing them in an acetone bath for 5 min using the Formlabs Form Wash equipment. Following the cleaning process, the printed teeth were cured at 80 °C for 120 min in the Form Cure equipment to finalize the treatment.

Results and analysis

Mechanical testing

Compression tests on all three types of teeth were performed using the Shimadzu AGS-X testing machine. In this test, the sample was placed between two parallel plates, as shown in Fig. 3(b), and the test was conducted at a crosshead speed of 20 mm/min, utilizing a 5 kN load cell. The teeth were subjected to compression until fracture occurred, as shown in Fig. 3, at which point the test automatically stopped.

Fig. 3Testing process (a) 3D printed teeth, (b) compression testing, (c) fractured teeth

Testing process (a) 3D printed teeth, (b) compression testing, (c) fractured teeth

The mechanical testing of dental teeth samples with various fillers reveals distinct differences in strength, brittleness, and flexibility. As shown in Fig. 4, neat dental resin teeth, with an average strength of 24.93 MPa and strain of 14.2%, are more ductile than hBN-filled dental resin teeth, which show slightly higher strength (25.92 MPa) but a reduced strain of 12.2%. The lower strain in hBN-reinforced dental resin teeth reflects increased brittleness, as hBN particles limit deformation and cause earlier failure under stress. In contrast, BNNT-reinforced dental resin teeth demonstrate a balanced performance, combining the strength of 36.31 MPa with a strain of 13.9%, resulting in greater strength and reduced brittleness. The addition of 1% hBN results in a modest 4% increase, while the inclusion of 0.1% BNNT leads to a remarkable 46% improvement as compared to neat resin teeth, highlighting the superior reinforcing effect of BNNT fillers in enhancing both strength and structural integrity in the resin teeth.

Compressive strength of all types of composites

Additionally, despite the varying filler concentrations, the standard deviation among the samples remains minimal, indicating the consistency and reliability of the 3D printing technique used to manufacture the teeth. The modulus measurements also reveal a positive trend with the incorporation of hexagonal boron nitride structure, as shown in Fig. 5. Neat dental resin teeth samples show an average modulus of 1.96 ± 0.22 MPa, while hBN-reinforced samples have a slightly higher modulus of 2.12 ± 0.06 MPa, suggesting that the hBN enhances stiffness. The highest modulus is observed in the BNNT-reinforced teeth samples at 2.78 ± 0.31 MPa, indicating that the nanotube structure further improves the material’s stiffness. Overall, the progression from neat dental resin to hBN to BNNT demonstrates an increasing trend in both strength and modulus, underscoring the influence of the structural organization of hexagonal boron nitride on the mechanical properties of the dental resin. Studies also show that adding BNNTs into the polymer matrix shows an increase in mechanical properties [30, 37].

Fracture analysis

The Helios G4 scanning electron microscope (SEM) was used to analyze the fracture surfaces and dispersion of nanofillers in dental resin. The SEM analysis revealed intriguing insights into the fracture surface of the neat dental resin teeth. With the presence of inclusions akin to nanofillers within the teeth, a notable absence of crack resistance was observed. Consequently, cracks propagated along a linear trajectory, depicted in Fig. 6. This phenomenon underscores the diminished strength exhibited by the material in contrast to its nanofiller-infused counterparts, which is particularly evident in dental applications.

Fracture pattern analysis of neat teeth

The hBN-reinforced teeth show a rougher surface than the neat counterpart, visualized in Fig. 7 (a).

Fig. 7Fracture pattern of hBN teeth (a) well distribution of hBN into the resin (b) crack resistance by hBN

Fracture pattern of hBN teeth (a) well distribution of hBN into the resin (b) crack resistance by hBN

Within the microstructure, the dispersion of hBN is evident in Fig. 7(a), along with noticeable clusters of hBN. It’s noteworthy that well-dispersed hBN particles are renowned for significantly enhancing material strength. This increase in strength is due to the hBN particles connecting the dental polymer together. This increase in strength from neat to hBN teeth samples can be explained by the powder latching onto the dental resin, creating chains that improve the strength of the overall hBN-reinforced teeth and provide an inexpensive solution for stronger dentures.

However, the presence of hBN clusters introduces complexities, or poor wettability of hBN to the matrix [38], potentially diminishing the properties of the dental resin. Figure 7(b) unveils a compelling insight: the crack propagation through the matrix encounters hurdles when interacting with hBN clusters. As the crack reaches a cluster, it diverts its path around it, highlighting the crack-resisting capability of hBN clusters. This phenomenon, also described as matrix debonding, occurs around the hBN cluster, which leads to the failure of the teeth. This phenomenon underscores the detailed role of hBN in altering fracture behavior.

In summary, the incorporation of hBN particles into dental resin showcases a modest enhancement compared to neat teeth. Yet, the intricate interplay between well-dispersed particles and clusters emphasizes the delicate balance required for optimizing material performance.

The incorporation of BNNTs significantly enhances the mechanical properties of dental resin by improving both load transfer and crack resistance. As shown in Fig. 8 (a), the even distribution of BNNTs within the resin matrix is crucial for their role in load transfer, allowing the nanotubes to effectively strengthen the material. In Fig. 8 (b), the observed nanotube bridging between cracks further demonstrates the BNNTs’ ability to inhibit crack propagation by forming a bridge across the crack surfaces, preventing further failure. This bridging phenomenon not only improves fracture toughness but also enhances the material’s resilience to dynamic loading, extending its service life and improving its reliability under challenging conditions. Additionally, BNNTs foster a more efficient energy dissipation pathway, reducing the likelihood of catastrophic failure and enhancing overall safety. These combined benefits make BNNTs a valuable addition to composite materials, offering significant improvements in both mechanical performance and safety.

Fracture patterns in BNNT-modified resin: (a) Arrows highlight BNNT distribution within the resin. (b) Arrows indicate nanotube bridging across cracks, enhancing stress transfer and structural integrity

The addition of hBN or BNNTs to the dental resin results in distinct differences in fracture behavior due to their unique structures and interactions with the matrix. Incorporating hBN primarily improves toughness through crack deflection and energy dissipation. The layered structure of hBN particles allows them to act as crack stoppers, deflecting and slowing crack propagation, though their weaker interfacial adhesion limits load transfer. On the other hand, BNNTs significantly enhance toughness via nanotube bridging and pull-out mechanisms. These tubular structures effectively distribute stress and hold the resin together after crack formation, dissipating more energy during fracture. Other studies also shows that how BNNT bridging mechanism enhance mechanical properties of the composite [39, 40]. BNNTs also provide better load transfer due to their higher surface area and stronger bonding potential. While hBN is effective for crack deflection and simpler to incorporate, BNNTs offer superior toughness improvements at the cost of more complex dispersion challenges.

Modeling and simulation

Computer simulations and modeling of nanocomposites play a crucial role in comprehensively understanding the relationship between structure and properties, as well as the interaction mechanisms between nanofillers and the matrix that influence the performance characteristics of the final composite materials [41–43]. The FEM was conducted to observe stress distribution on hBN and BNNT-based dental resin. Two representative volume elements (RVEs) were created, one with hBN and the other with BNNT incorporated into the resin, as shown in figure Figs. 9(a) and 10(a), respectively. The hBN particle model, developed using Simcenter MultiMech software, provided detailed insights into the fracture behavior of the resin matrix under the boundary conditions illustrated in Fig. 9(b). The isotropic linear elastic material properties used in the hBN RVE model are summarized in Table 1, with the Poisson’s ratio for the resin obtained from the literature [44]. MultiMech includes an in-built feature for generating RVEs with various filler shapes, such as cylinders, irregular shapes, ellipsoids, and spheres. To accurately replicate the irregular geometries of hBN particles, the irregular particle generation feature was employed in this project. The simulation incorporated the same quantity of hBN used in the experimental setup, ensuring consistency in material representation. A model comprising 508,861 tetrahedral elements was developed to simulate the hBN particles, enabling a detailed analysis of their influence on the composite’s mechanical behavior. A cut plane, as shown in Fig. 10(b) within the matrix, revealed that stress was primarily concentrated around the hBN particles, with localized matrix cracking initiating in these high-stress zones. This cracking propagated outward, leading to debonding at the particle-matrix interface. Notably, the stress concentration patterns and the observed debonding closely matched experimental fracture analyses, reinforcing the validation of the simulation.

Conclusion

Three types of dental resins—neat, hBN-modified, and BNNT-modified—were fabricated and tested. The neat resin had a compressive strength of 24.93 MPa, while adding 1 wt% hBN increased this to 25.92 MPa (+ 4%). The incorporation of BNNTs achieved a significant improvement, with compressive strength rising to 36.31 MPa (+ 46%). SEM analysis revealed that hBN particles and BNNTs enhanced toughness, with BNNTs acting as bridges within the matrix to improve cohesiveness.

Key findings from the modeling section provided crucial insights into the stress distribution and failure mechanisms within the composites. FEM simulations revealed that in the hBN-modified resin, stress was concentrated around the hBN particles, initiating localized matrix cracking and propagating outward, leading to debonding at the particle-matrix interface. For the BNNT-modified resin, stress concentration was observed near the ends of the BNNTs, likely influenced by their geometric characteristics. These areas experienced matrix cracking and debonding at the BNNT-matrix interface, emphasizing the importance of interfacial strength in determining composite performance.

In conclusion, the integration of hBN and BNNTs into dental resin composites represents a transformative approach in dental and composite material science. These nanomaterials significantly enhance the mechanical performance of the resins, addressing prior limitations and paving the way for stronger, more durable dental restorations with longer lifespans and minimized waste. The straightforward process of incorporating hBN and BNNTs offers a cost-effective and promising method for developing high-performance dental composites, with potential applications extending beyond dentistry to other advanced material systems.
