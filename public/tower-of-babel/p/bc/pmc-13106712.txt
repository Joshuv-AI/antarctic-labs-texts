Analyzing fourteen deleterious nsSNPs of CFTR as promising genetic markers for cancer prognosis
Authors: Seyyedha Abbas, Maria Shabbir, Yasmin Badshah, Fizzah Abid, Khushbukhat Khan, Amna Hafeez, Naeem Mahmood Ashraf, Janeen H Trembley, Tayyaba Afsar, Altaf khan, Sadia Fatima, Suhail Razak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) is a chloride channel protein involved in trans-epithelial transport of salt and the regulation of intracellular ion concentrations. The present study aims to investigate the relationship between CFTR non-synonymous SNPs (nsSNPs) and cancer. The study also seeks to elucidate the structural and functional consequences of the deleterious CFTR variants on the protein. The CFTR nsSNPs were retrieved from the Ensembl genome browser, and the deleterious nsSNPs were filtered out using PredictSNP2, CADD, FunSeq2 and GWAVA. The most pathogenic nsSNPs were shortlisted for further analysis. By employing I-TASSER, ProjectHOPE, I-Mutant, MUpro, DynaMut and MutPred, the influence of SNPs on the function and structure of the protein was assessed. Secondary and tertiary structures were predicted through PSIPRED and AlphaFold. Normal Mode Analysis (NMA) were performed through IMOD software, while the RMSD values of the mutated structures were determined through myPresto. Genotyping analyses were conducted through Tetra-primer ARMS-PCR (Amplification Refractory Mutation System). Fourteen nsSNPs of CFTR were identified as deleterious, significantly decreasing protein stability and potentially contributing to carcinogenesis. The pathogenic variants were mapped onto the ATP-binding cassette domain, indicating a strong association with altered protein function. Structural analyses indicated that the nsSNPs could induce changes in residue interactions with neighboring residues, thereby affecting the protein stability and molecular interactions. Normal Mode Analysis confirmed that these variants could perturb the CFTR’s conformational integrity. The genotyping analysis indicated that the heterozygous (AT) genotype of rs556880586 was highly prevalent in patients (70%) in comparison to controls (19%). Testing through Hardy–Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE) showed significant deviation in both patient and control groups for the nsSNPs, rs556880586 and rs949472192. Outcomes of the current study identified two deleterious nsSNPs in the CFTR gene that can possibly contribute to cancer progression. Moreover, these variants could act as preliminary biomarker candidates for clinical validation. Introduction

The human genome contains more than ten million nucleotide positions that vary among individuals within any population1. A single SNP occurs approximately every 300 base pairs2. Moreover, 1% of the whole genome consists of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). Approximately 90% of all genomic variations are SNPs. Among these variants, 0.12% may be pathogenic according to the genome-wide analysis. However, not all SNPs are associated with the disease; some of the SNPs, called non-synonymous SNPs, are related to genetic diseases. nsSNPs account for approximately 50% of pathogenic mutations in human genetic diseases. nsSNP occurs when an amino acid is substituted by another that causes a change in the amino acid sequence of the protein which can lead to disease. In contrast, a nonsense variant inserts a stop codon. This results in premature termination of the process of protein synthesis and leads to truncated protein formation2. A majority of the disease-associated nsSNPs are situated in the conserved regions involved in protein folding and regulation mechanisms. Alterations in protein charge, geometry, hydrophobicity, stability, dynamics, translation and interactions can result in deleterious protein variants3–5. Moreover, nsSNPs may influence protein-protein interactions6. CFTR is a cAMP-dependent chloride channel protein of the ATP binding cassette transporter family7. CFTR channels mediate trans-epithelial transport of salts and regulation of ion concentration. Altered CFTR expression can lead to diseases such as pulmonary fibrosis, chronic pancreatitis and cancer8–10. Recent studies have demonstrated an association between CFTR and tumors. The expression of CFTR was reportedly downregulated in various cancers, such as intestinal cancer, lung cancer, breast cancer, prostate cancer and pancreatic cancer11–15. Upregulation of CFTR expression was registered for gastric, ovarian and cervical cancer16–18. The CFTR gene spans 190 kb of genomic DNA and contains 27 exons, which encodes 1,480 amino acid proteins with 170 kDa19,20. The abnormal expression also leads to pulmonary fibrosis, chronic pancreatitis and cancers8–10. Limited literature is available regarding the CFTR gene role in cancers. Furthermore, nsSNPs of CFTR have not been studied in detail. Determining the pathogenicity level of particular nsSNPs is crucial for disease prognosis, but there remain challenges because hundreds to thousands of nsSNPs in candidate genes must be evaluated. This challenge is addressed by bioinformatics tools that assist in prioritizing and selecting target variants. To estimate the structural and functional effects of the nsSNPs identified in the coding region of the CFTR gene, the current study has employed different bioinformatics tools. Genotyping analysis was also part of the study to determine frequency differences of narrowed down deleterious nsSNPs between patients and controls. Moreover, HWE tests were performed to evaluate the accuracy of the genotyping data. The nsSNPs identified through this study can be used as genetic markers for diagnosing people at risk and may be useful in explaining inter-individual differences associated with cancer susceptibility. Also, these genetic markers could be used for personalized medicine, where a therapy could be prescribed according to the genetic makeup of an individual. The identification of high-risk variants of CFTR is a stepping stone in the early diagnosis of cancer, particularly in conditions with multifactorial genetic contributions.

Methods

In-silico method

Data retrieval and prediction of pathogenicity of nsSNPs

The nsSNPs of the CFTR gene were obtained from the Ensembl genome browser (https://www.ensembl.org). nsSNPs under Transcript ID ENST00000003084.11 were extracted from the variant table (Supplementary File, Table S1). The data were downloaded as an MS Excel-supported file, which listed all the nsSNPs of the CFTR gene. nsSNPs were further categorized as neutral or damaging by the bioinformatics tools, including PredictSNP2, CADD, FunSeq2 and GWAVA21–23. These tools helped in interpreting the functional effects of nsSNPs. The pathogenicity of the identified nsSNPs was further evaluated using a combination of predictive tools, including SIFT, PolyPhen-2, CADD, Mutation Assessor, and MetaLR22–24. Combining the scores from these tools enabled the identification of potentially damaging variants for further study.

Structural and functional analysis

The functional and structural analysis of nsSNPs was performed using various tools, including SDM, MuPro, MutPred, Project HOPE and DynaMut25. SDM and MuPro provided information on the effects of the nsSNPs on the CFTR function and stability by calculating changes in free energy and stability scores based on thermodynamic principles. These programs employ machine learning techniques to analyze the consequences of mutations on protein function. Project HOPE and MutPred were used to evaluate the effects of amino acid substitutions on the protein activity, spatial structure and potential interactions. Lastly, DynaMut was used to evaluate the impact of mutations on protein motion and flexibility, providing insights into how these mutations may influence conformational changes. Moreover, the functional and structural impacts of the possible pathogenic nsSNPs were evaluated using these bioinformatics tools.

Secondary and tertiary structure prediction and domains identification

The protein sequence of CFTR was obtained from the ENSEMBL genome browser. The secondary structure was determined through PSIPRED (http://bioinf.cs.ucl.ac.uk/psipred/), while the tertiary structure was predicted by AlphaFold26. The domains of CFTR were identified through InterPro27.

Normal mode analysis

The in silico mutagenesis was carried out in PyMOL28. The variant structures were obtained by modifying amino acid positions in the wild-type protein. These mutated structures were analyzed using PyMOL to visualize the effect of the mutations. These mutated variants were then optimized for Normal Mode Analysis, which are computational techniques that predict the atomic and molecular motions in a system over time. Normal Mode Analysis allows the estimation of structural stability, dynamics, and function of the mutated variants, as well as their biological activity and interactions in a physiologically relevant environment. The Normal Mode Analysis was performed online through IMOD software (https://bio3d.colorado.edu/imod/). Default settings were used for running Normal Mode Analysis in IMOD software. The atomic model was selected as CA (Cα atoms accounting for whole residue mass). The JSmol plugin was selected as HTML5. The number of modes were chosen as 20. The fixed angles ratio was kept as 0.00. The Elastic Network Model (ENM) was chosen as a Sigmoid. The ENM cut off, clusters and deformability values were kept as default. RMSD values of the nsSNPs were determined after performing MD simulations in myPresto (https://www.mypresto5.jp/en/). The wild and mutated structures were superimposed in PyMOL, and RMSD values were calculated to identify structural differences between the wild and mutated proteins. The global minimization steps were selected as 500. The steepest descent and generalized born methods were chosen. The MD simulation steps were kept as 500. The simulations were run at 300,000 K. The time steps were kept as 2,000 fsec. The cut-off length was selected as 14.00 (A). The MD energy and coordination steps were kept at 20. The velocity of running steps was 200.

Experimental method

Blood samples collection

Blood samples were collected from 100 patients diagnosed with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and 100 healthy controls with no prior history of liver disease or other related conditions. Before the collection of the blood samples, written informed consent was taken from the participants. The blood was primarily collected from the Polyclinic Hospital, Islamabad. Ethical approval for conducting the study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Atta-ur-Rahman School of Applied Biosciences (ASAB), National University of Sciences and Technology (NUST). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki29.

Genomic DNA extraction for genotyping analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from the whole blood using the phenol-chloroform method, which included a stepwise lysis and precipitation procedure30. The quality of extracted DNA was confirmed using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (A260/A280 = 1.8). For genotyping analysis, ARMS-PCR was performed. For PCR, 25 µL of HOT FIREPol EvaGreen HRM Master Mix was prepared. The cycling conditions included preliminary denaturation (95 °C, 5 min), 35 cycles of denaturation (95 °C, 30 s), annealing (63 °C for rs556880586, 62 °C for rs949472192, 30 s), extension (72 °C, 30 s), and final extension (72 °C, 8 min). A 3% agarose gel was prepared to visualize the DNA bands. The images of the DNA bands were captured using a UV Transilluminator.

Primer designing

Genotyping analysis was performed for 2 nsSNPs. The primers for the CFTR nsSNPs rs556880586 (A/T) and rs949472192 (C/T) were designed using PRIMER1 (https://primer1.soton.ac.uk/primer1.html). PRIMER1 generates allele-specific primers, inner primers and a pair of outer primers to amplify the target region. These primers are used for Tetra-primer ARMS-PCR31. For rs556880586, primers were designed to differentiate between A and T alleles and in the case of rs949472192, the primers were designed to differentiate between C and T alleles (Table 1). The genomic sequences flanking each SNP were downloaded from the Ensembl genome browser based on the GRCh38.p14 human genome assembly. A 300–400 bp flanking region around each SNP site was extracted and submitted to the PRIMER1 web interface. The position of the SNP from the start of the sequence was defined. The allelic variations were specified. The rest of the parameters, such as primer size, melting temperature range, GC content and product size, were specified according to standard guidelines to ensure primer efficiency, specificity and stability32. The primers designed using PRIMER1 were validated by OligoAnalyzer (https://sg.idtdna.com/pages/tools/oligoanalyzer). The possibility of hairpins, dimers and unintended interactions within the secondary structure of primers was determined.

Statistical analysis

Genotypic data were analyzed using the chi-square test. Relative risk (RR) and odds ratio (OR) were calculated using the Chi square test33. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, indicating that there exists an association between a specific genotype and HCC.

Results

Data processing and filtration

In total, data related to 1843 non-synonymous SNPs were obtained from the Ensembl genome browser (Supplementary File, Table S1). We subjected the nsSNPs to various tools for filtration. PredictSNP2, CADD, FunSeq2 and GWAVA were used to filter the 14 most pathogenic nsSNPs. The nsSNPs were further analyzed by six different tools, including SIFT, CADD, Polyphen-2, REVEL, MetaLR and Mutation Assessor (Table 1; Fig. 1). Variants with SIFT scores within the range of 0.00–0.05.00.05 were identified as deleterious. Variants with a score of 0.05 were classified as tolerant. PolyPhen-2 values of 0.25 were marked as ‘Benign’. Variants with values between 0.25 and 0.8 were characterized as ‘Possibly Damaging’. Variants with values higher than 0.8 were specified as ‘Probably damaging’. Variants with values lower than 0.8 were delineated as ‘Neutral’. Mutation Assessor characterized variants with values between 0.8 and 0.9 as ‘Low Deleterious. Variants with values greater than 1.9 and less than or equal to 3.5 were marked as ‘Medium Deleterious’, while variants with values above 3.5 were outlined as ‘Highly Deleterious’. Variants with CADD’s score higher than 30 were delineated as damaging. The REVEL marked variants with values less than or equal to 0.5 as benign, while the values between 0.6 and 0.9 were specified as disease-causing. Similarly, the MetaLR gave 0.5 or lower scores to non-deleterious variants while damaging variants were given scores above 0.5. The values of short-listed variants were between 0 and 0.97. The nsSNP predicted to be the most deleterious was rs397508297, as it had the highest pathogenicity score of 0.97. The variant IDs, chromosomal locations, substituted alleles, amino acids (AA), and coordinates of AA were studied and stated in Tables 2 and Fig. 2. The chromosome positions of the variants were also analyzed and documented in Table 3.

Effect of nsSNP on protein stability and structure

The structural and functional analysis was performed using MutPred, Project HOPE, MUpro, SDM and DynaMut as documented in Tables 4, 5 and 6. The results revealed alteration in protein stability and function due to single amino acid substitution. The highest pathogenicity value predicted by Project HOPE was 0.977 for nsSNP, rs397508297 (E588V), which was found to be present in the ATPase Domain, which is a highly conserved region in CFTR protein channel. MutPred2 analysis demonstrated that amino acid change of E588V at position 588 can result in loss of Allosteric site at residue 587 with a significant p-value of 0.03, indicating the potential role of this SNP in altering the function of the protein. MUpro, SDM and DynaMut predicted decreased stability with DDG values of −0.17, -−1.430 kcal/mol and − 0.456 kcal/mol for the nsSNP rs397508297 (E588V), further strengthening its pathogenic potential.

The 2D structure of CFTR was identified. The sequences of CFTR contributing to helix, coiled, strand formation, extracellular domains, metal binding, putative domain boundary, re-entrant helix, membrane interaction, cytoplasmic region, transmembrane helix and signal peptide were indicated by PSIPRED (Fig. 4a). AlphaFold was used to predict the 3D structure of the CFTR (Fig. 4b). Alphafold provided the pLDDT scale of 0–100. The majority of residues had the pLDDT values greater than 90, indicating greater confidence in the predicted positions of the amino acids within the 3D structure (Fig. 4c). Furthermore, the positional errors within the CFTR structure were also determined, and those residues that were colored green had minimal positional error compared to those residues that were colored light green (Fig. 4d). IMOD analysis

The Normal Mode Analysis for the 14 selected nsSNPs was performed. Normal Mode Analysis results indicated the three-dimensional structure, deformability plot, b-factor comparison (NMA vs. PDB), cumulative variance, cross-correlation map, elastic network model (ENM) and eigenvalue distribution of the mutated and wild proteins. Normal Mode Analysis of the wild-type protein (Fig. 5) was performed to act as a reference, so that the variant structures could be compared. NMA simulations of the wild-type structure indicated the normal flexible positions of the protein (Fig. 5a). Within the deformability plot (Fig. 5b), the green areas indicate the higher flexibility in the structure. Larger values of deformability suggested loops or linker regions, which were generally associated with functional motion or disorder. A comparison of atomic B-factors obtained from Normal Mode Analysis and atomic B-factors from the PDB file was performed (Fig. 5c). NMA (red) indicated the theoretical B-factors computed from the structure while PDB (gray) indicated the observed thermal motion of the structure as indicated by X-ray crystallography (calculated from experimental data). Such differences could highlight disparities between experimental and theoretical estimations. Cumulative variance was explained in the graph (Fig. 5d). The first few modes (purple bars) represented most of the total variance, which explains their dominance in capturing the larger scale motions. The green modes had a lesser part in the overall variance, hence indicating lower or local oscillations. The cross-correlation map was studied (Fig. 5e). Heatmap indicated the atomic fluctuations calculated for each residue. Positive values were represented by the red color, meaning that motions of the corresponding residues were co-correlated (i.e., residues move in the same direction). Blue color represents anti-correlated motions (i.e. the residues move in the opposite direction). High correlation values could define functionally significant sets of residues, including the active center or a hinge. The Elastic network model was analyzed (Fig. 5f). The shaded areas and circles represented substantially higher elastic connections between the atoms. This map showed the level of coupling (spring constants) between the molecules. Interconnected regions were usually stiffer and hence enhanced the structural integrity of the protein. Eigenvalue distribution was also evaluated (Fig. 5g). The relative contribution of each mode to the flexibility of the molecule was described by the eigenvalues. Smaller eigenvalues were associated with slower motions, which were generally more functional in nature. Higher eigenvalues corresponded to rapid, small-scale motion.

Genotyping analysis

In order to determine the nsSNPs rs556880586 or rs949472192 with dominant allele or genotype, the genotype frequencies and allele distributions of the SNPs in the patient and control groups were assessed. For the SNP rs556880586, the genotype frequencies in the patients were as follows: AA = 11%, TT = 19%, and AT = 70%. In controls, the genotype frequencies were AA = 63%, TT = 17% and AT = 19%. The frequency of heterozygote (AT) among patients (70%) was much higher than that of controls (19%). The calculated allele frequencies in patients were 46% and 54% for allele A and T, respectively, while in controls, the percentages were 73% and 27% for allele A and T, respectively. In the case of rs949472192, the genotype frequencies were much more balanced. CC = 31%, TT = 31%, and CT = 38% among patients and CC = 32%, TT = 34%, and CT = 34% in controls (Supplementary File, Table S3).

The HWE analysis was done to determine the genotype distribution of SNP rs556880586 among patients and controls. The genotype frequencies observed among the patient group were AA: 11, TT: 19 and AT: 70. The allele frequencies were computed as p (A) = 0.46 and q (T) = 0.54. The predicted genotype frequencies under HWE were AA: 21.16, TT: 29.16 and AT: 49.68. The chi-square test gave a value of 16.75 with one degree of freedom, which meant that there was a statistically significant deviation of HWE (p < 0.003). In the control group, the observed frequencies were AA: 63, TT: 17 and AT: 19. The estimated frequencies of the alleles were p = 0.725 and q = 0.275, and expected genotype frequencies were AA: 52.56, TT: 7.56, and AT: 39.88. Chi-square test yielded a value of 24.77, which showed that there was a significant deviation from HWE (p < 0.001). When the data of both groups (AA: 74, TT: 36, AT: 89) were added together, the frequency of allele p and q were obtained as 0.593 and 0.403, respectively. The expected frequencies of HWE were AA: 70.31, TT: 32.48, and AT: 95.44 and the chi-square value was 0.89, which indicated no significant deviation of the chi-square value of HWE (p ≈ = 0.35).

The genotype frequencies observed in the controls were CC = 32, CT = 34 and TT = 34. The frequencies of the alleles were found to be p (C) = 0.49 and q (T) = 0.51. Using these allele frequencies, the genotype frequencies expected under HWE were 24.01, 49.98 and 26.01 for CC, CT and TT, respectively. The chi-square test gave the value of 10.22. The p-value that was obtained using one degree of freedom was about 0.0014. The genotype frequencies for rs949472192 in the case group were CC = 31, CT = 38, and TT = 31, with the allele frequencies p(C) = q(T) = 0.50 in a sample size of 100. The expected genotype frequencies under HWE were 25, 50 and 25 for CC, CT and TT, respectively. The chi-square test gave 1 as the degree of freedom and 5.76 as the value of the test, with p-value near to 0.016. The genotype frequencies of the combined data (n = 200) were CC = 63, CT = 72 and TT = 65. The allele frequencies that were calculated were p(C) = 0.495 and q(T) = 0.505. The predicted genotype frequencies of the HWE were 49.005, 99.99 and 51.005 for CC, CT and TT, respectively. The chi-square for the combined data gave the p-value of < 0.0001 (Table 7).

Discussion

Early detection of cancer is possible through genetic screening of nsSNPs. nsSNPs affect different parameters from staging to treatment. Genetic screening assists in the early detection of individuals with cancer, and therefore helps in the early management and personalized treatment. Research indicates that nsSNPs are crucial to determine the risk of diseases34. The research demonstrates the effectiveness of genetic testing to identify hereditary risk factors for cancer treatment and management. The presence of high-risk nsSNPs within the genome could alter the CFTR gene and thereby the phenotypes35.

The following study has proven successful in determining the deleterious/benign nsSNPs of CFTR. A total of 14 SNPs were determined by SIFT, PolyPhen, CADD, REVEL, MetaLR and Mutation Assessor36. The integrated analysis of CFTR nsSNPs aids in classifying functional types with detailed detrimental effects associated with genes. The mutation at position 588 from glutamic acid to valine could result in the loss of Allosteric site at F587 (P = 0.03). The two residues vary in size and charge. This influences protein structure and spatio-temporal dynamics between proteins. MutPred predicted the score of 0.603 with the loss in function due to the mutation. The changes could perturb the interactions with ATP binding in the cleft/pocket of the catalytic domain, effecting favorable interactions. In total, Project HOPE, MuPro, DynaMut, SDM and MutPred, used in the study indicated changes leading to thermodynamic instability, revealing potential consequences of nsSNPs22.

The transformation in residues causes perturbed folding interactions, which lead to production of bumps in structure. Variation in charge and sizes between wild-type and mutant residues. These point mutations affected the intermolecular interaction. The observed results indicated differential charge distribution of residues. This additional charge changes protein folding. Point mutation as a result changes the angles of torsion, which are essential for the stability of proteins. The instability in structure causes disruption and misalignment. The location of residue binding changes along with intermolecular bonds. The change in size and discrepancies in charge result in the formation of hydrogen bonds, which impart elevated hydrophobicity levels to the residues.

The majority of 14 nsSNP mutations were identified between the ABC membrane and ABC transmembrane domains between 84 and 1374 amino acids. The structural analysis revealed high-risk nsSNPs of amino acid residue substitutions in the CFTR domain, which cause a deleterious impact on the ABC transporter and initiate cystic fibrosis. The amino acid residues that have been modified are rs556880586 (D373Y), rs1416245806 (D373V), rs748461979 (R487K), rs1562898548(E528G), rs764644021(D984N), rs397508465(D985N), rs141150961(M1137), rs1800120(R1162L), rs573448832(W1310G), rs779591474(V1415G). The secondary and tertiary structure of the CFTR was identified through PSIPRED36 and AlphaFold26. These tools gave the idea of the amino acid residues that form different domains and helices of the proteins. The residues with their high confidence scores were also indicated. The confidence scores indicated the correct position of the residues within the structure of the protein. The majority of residues had high pLDDT values that indicated that the majority of residues positioned within the CFTR structure, as predicted by AlphaFold, were correctly positioned. This information helps researchers to understand the reliability of the predicted protein structure at a local level. The presence of nsSNPs could disrupt the structure of CFTR and the adjoining residue positions.

Normal Mode Analysis was performed for the 14 selected nsSNPs by IMOD software37. Normal Mode Analysis results indicated the deformity within the mutated structure because of the presence of the variants. The results of the Normal Mode Analysis were compared with the wild structure. The change in the flexibility of the protein was indicated by Normal Mode Analysis. The MD analysis indicated the likely effect of the change in residues on the interactions and conformation of the CFTR protein.

The genotyping analysis gave information about the possible association of nsSNPs, rs556880586 and rs949472192 with the HCC. The differences in genotype frequencies between patients and controls, especially for SNP, rs556880586, indicated that the SNP had a considerable possibility of being involved in the pathology of the HCC. In particular, the heterozygous genotype (AT) of rs556880586 was significantly over-represented in patients (70%) as compared to controls (19%). This is supported by the change in the frequency of the alleles with the allele T being more prevalent in the patients (54%) than in the controls (27%).

Conversely, the genotype frequencies for rs949472192 in patients and controls showed slight variations. Nevertheless, the HWE tests showed that both the controls (p 0.0014) and the cases (p 0.016) were significantly deviated, and so much more in the combined data (p < 0.0001). The Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE) p values differ for the SNPs, rs556880586 and rs949472192 in the patients and controls. Likewise, one of the possible reasons out of other reasons for deviations in HWE38 could be that the nsSNP was possibly linked with the disease or is in linkage disequilibrium with a disease-causing variant. If a certain genotype is protective or susceptible, its frequency will be different in the expected cohort or population. This study could be extended at the population level to determine the nsSNPs of CFTR that could act as the genetic markers for disease association studies. Treatment approaches could be tailored that are in accordance with the genetic profile of patients. Even though these nsSNPs are not yet established genetic biomarkers, the changed genotypic frequencies of these nsSNPs in patients in comparison to controls indicate their potential as promising biomarkers for future studies after clinical validation.

Moreover, the presence of deleterious nsSNPs, including rs556880586 and rs949472192 could influence the conformational stability of CFTR and affect its functions, including chloride and bicarbonate transport. Because of the presence of nsSNPs, genes that connect with CFTR in different signaling pathways, including Wnt/b-catenin, PI3K/AKT, and NF-kB would be affected39. CFTR also facilitates epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), which increases the tumor invasiveness and potential to metastasize40. In addition to intracellular signaling, CFTR dysfunction may also cause changes in the tumor microenvironment (TME). The destabilization of CFTR through the presence of nsSNPs in this study can disrupt CFTR’s ability to maintain ionic and redox balance, creating the microenvironment that supports tumor growth and development. Further research incorporating transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic studies will be useful in clarifying the role of CFTR-associated variants in the pathogenesis of HCC and in the development of diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers.

Conclusion

This is the first study that has narrowed down fourteen nsSNPs of CFTR and investigated their role in relation to cancer. Previous studies have reported that the altered expression of CFTR can lead to cancer; however, none of the reported nsSNPs in this study have been previously studied in relation to cancer. The positions of these fourteen nsSNPs were identified on different domains of the transmembrane, possibly affecting their function. It was noted that altered structural dynamics could malfunction the protein. The motion disorder and local oscillations could be predicted because of the presence of nsSNPs. These nsSNPs may alter the conformation of the protein that can lead towards pathogenic conditions, including cancer. The changed amino acid positions disrupted the interactions of nearby amino acids. The research findings indicated the possible deregulation of the signaling pathways downstream of CFTR because of the presence of nsSNPs. Moreover, the 2 deleterious nsSNPs narrowed down through this study were used for genotyping analysis. The genotypes (heterozygous, homozygous) of the nsSNPs were identified within the HCC patients and controls. Also, the association of the specific alleles with the disease onset was determined.
