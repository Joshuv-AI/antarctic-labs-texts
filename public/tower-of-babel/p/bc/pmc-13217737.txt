Self-directed teaching system driven by video analysis feedback improves surgical skill training for clinical interns: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Boqiang Liu, Bin Gong, Xiaochen Zhang, Chen Lu, Bin Zheng, Jin Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinical skills training in surgical clerkships relies heavily on faculty supervision, yet traditional models often face time constraints, lack of individualized guidance, and the inability to provide timely feedback. Technology-enhanced, learner-centred approaches, such as video-based self-directed systems, may help address these limitations. The Self-directed Teaching System (SDTS) integrates multi-angle video recording, self-paced practice, and structured feedback to support autonomous learning, but its effectiveness compared with that of conventional instructor-led teaching remains unclear.ObjectiveThis randomized controlled trial aimed to (1) evaluate the effectiveness and efficiency of SDTS versus traditional teaching in training basic surgical skills and (2) compare three SDTS feedback modalities (self-, peer-, and instructor-feedback) in influencing learning outcomes.MethodsSixty-two surgical interns were randomized to either a traditional teaching group (fixed 3-hour session with real-time faculty guidance) or an SDTS group (self-directed practice with multi-angle video recording and delayed feedback). Within the SDTS arm, participants were further randomized into self-feedback (n = 10), peer-feedback (n = 10), or instructor-feedback (n = 11) subgroups. All students completed pre- and post-training Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCEs) on two skills: incision/suturing and suture removal/dressing change. Total training duration for the SDTS group, including video learning, practice, and feedback activities, was logged through the backend system data.ResultsFifty-six interns completed the full protocol. For incision and suturing, SDTS shows no statistical difference compared with traditional teaching (P > 0.05). However, for suture removal/dressing change, a more complex, step-intensive procedure, the traditional group scored significantly higher than SDTS group (P < 0.05). Despite this, SDTS participants required substantially less total training time in both modules. The three SDTS feedback subgroups demonstrated no statistical differences in post-training OSCE performance. Learners reported high levels of satisfaction with the video-based feedback process and perceived improvement in procedural skills.ConclusionsThe SDTS provides a flexible, technology-supported approach to surgical skills training and can deliver learning outcomes comparable to traditional methods for basic procedures while enabling more flexible use of training time. However, for more complex tasks, fully self-regulated practice without the required minimum training time may risk premature disengagement, before participants could fully refine their skills. While the feedback modality did not significantly impact outcomes, studies with a larger sample size are needed to verify subgroup effects. The SDTS shows strong potential as a scalable, learner-centred model for clinical education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09104-y.Keywords: Self-directed Teaching System, Video Analysis Feedback, Self-Determination Theory, Self-Regulated Learning, Surgical Skills Training

Introduction

Clinical clerkships provide medical students with an essential transitional phase before they enter clinical practice, particularly in surgery, a discipline that places strong emphasis on hands-on skills training. During this period, students learn fundamental surgical skills such as making incisions, wound closures, suturing and knot tying, suture removal, and wound dressing. These core competencies form the foundation of surgical proficiency.

Mastery of such skills requires extensive practice, and timely and constructive feedback is critical for developing the necessary movement coordination [1]. Traditionally, feedback is provided by clinical faculty members during training sessions. While these feedback is highly useful, the faculty are often burdened with multiple clinical responsibilities, limiting their availability for real-time supervision and feedback [2]. This challenge is common across medical schools worldwide and can lead to insufficient guidance during practice, delayed correction of errors, and delayed skill acquisition among clerkship students in surgery.

Simulation-based training has been widely adopted in surgical education and has been shown to improve technical performance, patient safety, and learner confidence by allowing repetitive practice in a controlled environment without risk to patients [3–5]. Previous studies have demonstrated that video-assisted learning and video-based feedback can enhance procedural accuracy, support reflective practice, and facilitate skill acquisition across a range of clinical tasks [6, 7].

However, many existing video-based or simulation-enhanced training models heavily depend on instructors and require substantial faculty time for supervision, assessment, and feedback delivery. This reliance limits scalability and may reduce accessibility in busy clinical settings, especially during clerkship training. Moreover, few studies have systematically examined whether structured video-based feedback can support effective self-directed learning while maintaining educational quality comparable to traditional faculty-led instruction. Evidence comparing different feedback sources, such as self-feedback, peer feedback and instructor feedback, within a unified video-based training framework remains limited, particularly for basic surgical skills training during clinical clerkship [8]. These gaps underscore the need for innovative, resource-efficient training systems that can deliver high-quality feedback while reducing dependence on faculty availability.

To address these challenges, a novel Self-directed Teaching System (SDTS) has been developed to provide performance feedback through video analysis. The SDTS captures students’ practice sessions using multiple cameras from various angles, enabling comprehensive post-practice review and analysis. Video analysis offers several key advantages: it allows for detailed evaluation of procedural steps from multiple perspectives, which is not always possible in real time; it enables both students and instructors to revisit and critically assess each performance; and it supports self-paced learning, empowering students to refine their skills based on feedback given by the SDTS [9, 10].

To this end, this study aimed to compare the effectiveness of SDTS with that of traditional instructor-led teaching in training basic surgical skills, including incision, suturing, suture removal, and dressing change. We hypothesized that compared with the traditional teaching approach, SDTS would result in better training outcomes.

The introduction of the SDTS enables three sources of feedback: self-feedback, peer feedback, and instructor feedback. Instructor feedback is provided by faculty members after they review performance videos post-training. In contrast, self-feedback and peer-feedback do not require faculty involvement; students independently review their own performance videos (self-feedback) or review each other’s videos (peer-feedback). These video-based feedback differ from traditional real-time feedback delivered by faculty surgeons during in-person training sessions.

The second objective of this study is to compare the effectiveness of the three different feedback modes based on SDTS in basic surgical skills training. We hypothesize that compared with self-feedback or peer-feedback, feedback provided by instructors will result in better skill acquisition.

Methods

Participants

Participants were senior medical students under their clerkship (internship) in the surgery program at the Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, which is affiliated with Zhejiang University School of Medicine. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation.

To answer the first research question, we enrolled a total of 62 participants in September 2023 and assigned them to either the control group (traditional teaching group, n = 31) or the experimental group (SDTS group, n = 31) via a random number method. Within the experimental group, participants were further randomly divided into three subgroups, each corresponding to self-feedback (n = 10), peer-feedback (n = 10) and instructor-feedback (n = 11), in order to answer the second research question.

Self-directed teaching system

The SDTS is an educational platform designed to support self-directed procedural skills training. The conceptual framework of the system is grounded in principles of self-directed learning and video-based feedback, which comprises three core components: (1) a structured self-directed learning module for procedural skill acquisition; (2) a hands-on practice and multimodal recording module; and (3) an online video review and structured feedback module.

The system was deployed in a 9 m² training room equipped with four ceiling-mounted 2MP wide-angle cameras, a head-mounted trainee-view camera, and a wireless array microphone covering 10–100 m². The hands-on practice module integrated wearable and fixed multiangle recording devices to capture multimodal data, including synchronized video and audio streams, during procedural performance (Fig. 1).

Trainees accessed the system via a dedicated mobile application and scheduled self-guided practice sessions. During training, procedural performance was recorded in real time. The online review module allows asynchronous playback, structured evaluation, and feedback (Fig. 2). Trainees could immediately review recordings on site or remotely, refine operational details according to predefined assessment criteria and feedback models, and repeat practice without time restriction. After achieving self-satisfactory performance, trainees uploaded the final procedural video for evaluation.

The SDTS was co-developed through collaboration between our research group and a domestic educational technology company in China. Our team provided academic and clinical input during system development, including optimization of wearable device design, provision of standardized teaching materials and assessment criteria, and iterative testing within clinical teaching settings. The SDTS is currently used for routine undergraduate and postgraduate clinical skills training at our institution. The system is used exclusively for educational and research purposes and is available for non-commercial academic collaboration.

Task and training procedure

This prospective randomized controlled study began with baseline data collection, including gender age, academic performance, and pre-training Beck Anxiety Scale scores. Beck Scale scores were collected to confirm baseline psychological comparability between groups. Assessment of learners’ anxiety status before procedural training is routinely performed for two main reasons. First, epidemiological data indicate that the prevalence of anxiety among medical students has increased substantially in recent years [11]. Second, elevated anxiety levels negatively affect learning efficiency, attention, and procedural performance [12]. For Beck Anxiety Scale, scores of 0–14 indicate no anxiety, 15–25 indicate mild anxiety, 26–35 indicates moderate anxiety, and ≥ 36 indicates severe anxiety. All participants subsequently underwent a pre-training assessment of two core clinical skills, incision/suturing and suture removal/dressing change, using an Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE). Scores were recorded as baseline measures.

All students received training materials 1 week prior to the course, including the learning objectives, standardized procedural videos, and evaluation checklists, for independent study.

The control group followed a traditional teaching model: a 1-hour in-person session (instructional videos and faculty explanations) followed by 2 h of practice. During the practice session, students performed skill training with the instructor overseeing the session, providing necessary guidance, and correcting errors as needed.

The experimental group utilized an SDTS model. Upon entering a dedicated skills training room, students proceeded to a designated training area, which was equipped with recording devices, and began practising while being recorded. The instructional video continued to play in the background and was available for review at any time. After each practice attempt, students could either choose to review their performance immediately on a small screen or proceed based on their own perception of performance. There was no limit on the number of trials before uploading the final video. Once they felt satisfied with a particular trial, they could confirm submission of their best performance using the “one-click upload” function on the screen.

After uploading their video, all three subgroups (self-feedback, peer-feedback, and instructor-feedback) accessed the final performance videos on either a desktop platform or a mobile app for subsequent review and evaluation, offering students maximum flexibility in reflecting on their performance (Fig. 2). This feedback process was conducted after the training session, with flexible timing.

Specifically, in the self-feedback subgroup, the students used predefined evaluation criteria alongside the instructional video to assess their performance and identify areas for improvement. The peer feedback and instructor feedback subgroups followed the same process, differing only in the source of feedback: students paired up for mutual evaluation in the peer-feedback subgroup, whereas a dedicated instructor provided evaluation in the instructor-feedback subgroup.

One week post-training, all the students underwent a second OSCE examination to evaluate the effectiveness of the various training methods in enhancing skill proficiency (Fig. 3).

Clinical skill evaluation

Performance was assessed using the incision/suturing and suture removal/dressing change modules from the Zhejiang University Clinical Skills Evaluation Assessment, an institutionally established scoring system routinely applied for undergraduate surgical skills assessment. Evaluations were conducted in three separate classrooms under standardized conditions, with a 15-minute time limit per task. Before individual assessment, 3 experienced examiners fully communicated the detailed rules of the scoring table and aligned the score deduction standards. All OSCE examiners were blinded to the group allocation of participants.

Training duration tracking

The control group received a 3-hour training session (1-hour lecture + 2-hour practice). The experimental group utilized a self-directed system, with training durations monitored via data logging. The total duration for the SDTS group includes the time spent watching instructional videos, engaging in self-directed practice, uploading operation videos, and participating in feedback activities. The feedback process was conducted after each practice session, allowing flexible access for the learners.

The total feedback time encompasses three components: (1) the evaluator’s review time, including self-, peer-, or instructor-assessment of the submitted performance videos and generation of targeted feedback; (2) the learner’s time spent reviewing the feedback provided through the platform; and (3) the learner’s time spent rewatching their own performance videos via the mobile app or desktop system for reflection. All durations were objectively recorded through the SDTS backend logs, ensuring that the total recorded time accurately reflected the pedagogical exposure for each learner. No mandatory minimum practice time was imposed on the experimental group.

Questionnaire survey

After the training course, students in the experimental group evaluated their satisfaction with the course using anonymous survey, focusing on two main aspects: (1) whether they agreed with the feedback content based on video analysis and (2) whether the video analysis-based SDTS contributed to the improvement in clinical procedural skills.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS 26.0. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Normality-tested data were analysed using independent t tests or ANOVA for group comparisons; nonparametric data were analysed using Mann‒Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were compared using χ² tests. A two-tailed P < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

Six participants in the traditional teaching group were excluded due to scheduling conflicts (two students missed the training because they participated in emergency surgeries, and four students missed the session because they were required to attend scheduled teaching rounds). As indicated, the attrition resulted from conflicting clinical duties rather than personal time-management issues or a lack of motivation. All the trainees in the SDTS group completed the study protocol.

In total, 56 participants were included in the final analysis. There were no significant differences between the traditional teaching and SDTS groups with respect to sex, age, academic performance, psychological metrics, or baseline skill scores, indicating adequate baseline comparability between groups (Table 1).

In the traditional teaching group, the post-training test score was 95.36 ± 3.46, representing an average improvement of 18.18 ± 11.93 points. In contrast the SDTS group achieved a post-training test score of 88.42 ± 6.95, with an average improvement of 10.81 ± 15.66.

Self-directed teaching system enables flexible utilization of training time

In the traditional teaching group, students participated in in-person training, which included video learning (a 12-minute video for incision and suturing and a 15-minute video for suture removal and dressing change), explanations, and hands-on practice, with a total instructional duration of 180 min. In contrast, the SDTS group engaged in self-scheduled practice sessions that included video learning, self-directed practice, and performance feedback.

In the suture removal and dressing change module, the average number of practice sessions of the SDTS was 2.65 ± 1.33, with an average total duration of 84.94 ± 20.64 min. For the incision and suturing module, the mean number of practices sessions was 2.21 ± 1.05 practice sessions, with an average duration of 77.34 ± 22.00 min (Fig. 5A).

Thus, the total recorded instructional time in the SDTS group was significantly shorter, reflecting greater flexibility in training time utilization under the self-paced SDTS model. Post-training survey results showed that 97% (30/31) and 100% of the students agreed with the feedback provided for the suture removal/dressing change modules and the incision/suturing modules, respectively, Furthermore, 97% (30/31) and 87% (27/31) of students, respectively, believed that the SDTS improved their operational skills.

Discussion

Primary findings

Innovations in medical education are embracing learner-centred and technology-supported approaches [13, 14]. Our study demonstrates that the Self-directed Teaching System (SDTS), driven by video-based feedback, can achieve learning outcomes comparable to those of traditional instruction for basic procedures such as incision and suturing while offering substantially greater flexibility in training time. However, for more complex suture removal and dressing procedures, the performance of the SDTS group was lower than that of the traditional group. This may reflect that fully self-regulated practice, even when supported by video review and structured feedback, offer less real-time guidance for procedures involving multiple nuanced steps. Our secondary analysis revealed no statistically significant differences among the three feedback modalities.

Self-determination theory and self-regulated learning

The SDTS approach not only enables repeated, high-fidelity practice with detailed, multi-angle feedback but also reinforces core educational principles such as autonomy, competence, and relatedness, which are key components of self-determination theory (SDT). SDT posits that for learners to be motivated and engage in effective learning, their needs for autonomy (the ability to make choices), competence (the feeling of mastery), and relatedness (the sense of connection with others) must be met [15, 16]. By fulfilling these needs, SDT enhances intrinsic motivation, which is crucial for sustained engagement and self-confidence, leading to more effective learning outcomes.

Another important strength of the SDTS model lies in its alignment with the principles of self-regulated learning (SRL). SRL emphasizes learners’ active involvement in planning, monitoring, and evaluating their own performance, allowing them to take ownership of their learning process [17]. In our study, the SDTS provided students with flexible training time, the autonomy to repeat practice attempts as needed, and the ability to review their performance videos at any time during practice. These features closely reflect key SRL components, including self-monitoring, self-evaluation, and self-directed decision-making. The video-based feedback system further supports SRL by enabling learners to identify performance gaps, compare themselves with expert standards, and engage in reflective judgement after each attempt.

Rather than imposing a mandatory minimum practice duration or requiring learners to reach a predefined proficiency threshold, the SDTS encourages students to progress at their own pace based on their perceived competence. This learner-centred approach may promote intrinsic motivation and deepen engagement, while also highlighting the potential risks of premature disengagement when learners misjudge their readiness, an issue observed particularly in more complex tasks, such as suture removal [18].

By integrating SDT and SRL, the SDTS model promotes a flexible and personalized learning experience, where learners take control of their training, reflect critically on their performance, and implement targeted improvements. Such a learner-centred, self-directed approach is crucial as the demands of clinical practice evolve, offering a more adaptive and efficient way to train medical professionals. As these innovative paradigms continue to emerge, they are likely to become essential components of the future landscape of medical education.

Advantages and risks of a fully self-paced approach

The efficiency observed in the SDTS group must be interpreted with caution. Without the required duration of practice or instructor oversight, some learners may have prematurely terminated training due to overestimating their readiness—a known risk in SRL-driven models. This likely contributed to lower scores in the suture removal module, where more nuanced steps and stricter evaluation criteria demanded additional repetition and expert feedback.

Additionally, structured feedback (self, peer, or instructor) occurred after the students uploaded their final practice video. Thus, the performance measured in the second OSCE reflects delayed feedback, not iterative feedback-guided refinement. This distinction may partially explain the weaker results in complex tasks, where real-time corrective input is typically beneficial.

Nevertheless, the design of the SDTS offers important practical benefits. It supports flexible scheduling, reduces dependence on faculty, and enables learners to utilize fragmented time periods for training and reflection—advantages that are particularly relevant in busy clinical settings.

Comparison with prior work

Traditional teaching methods in surgical education are associated with several limitations. Owing to constraints in time, location, and training resources, some students are unable to afford sufficient hands-on training opportunities. The absence of video recording and playback features in conventional classrooms prevents students from reviewing their performance, making it difficult to engage in self-assessment, peer evaluation, and effective error correction. In addition, instructors are typically responsible for supervising multiple students simultaneously, which makes it challenging to monitor each learner closely and provide tailored, real-time feedback.

In contrast, the SDTS addresses many of these challenges by offering greater flexibility and enhanced learning tools. By breaking down traditional classroom content and making full use of pre-class, in-class, and post-class periods, SDTS allows trainees to schedule their own training sessions, reducing conflicts with clinical responsibilities.

In fact, six students in the traditional teaching group were unable to complete their training because of scheduling conflicts. This issue can largely be mitigated by the flexibility of the SDTS model. Another key advantage of SDTS is that it enables repeated, multi-angle reviews for thorough and accurate assessment by both peers and instructors. Moreover, our study demonstrated that the feedback delivered through SDTS was highly consistent among surgical trainees and was associated with increased engagement and enthusiasm, indicating that the system was effective at promoting learning.

Compared with existing research on video-assisted or self-directed skills training, the SDTS used in our study provides a more integrated structure by combining real-time video review, flexible scheduling, and structured delayed feedback within a single platform. Prior work has typically examined these elements in isolation, whereas our design allowed them to work together in a unified learning process. This integrated approach may explain the efficient performance observed in basic tasks and clarify where self-directed learning may fall short in more complex procedures.

However, in the present study, the educational outcomes of SDTS in suture removal and dressing change matched the performance levels achieved by the traditional teaching group. This discrepancy may be explained by several factors. These procedures are more complex, involving numerous steps and subtle evaluation criteria that may not be adequately emphasized in a self-directed format. Additionally, the SDTS group operated entirely on a self-paced basis without a mandatory minimum training duration. Consequently, compared with those in the traditional group, some students in the SDTS group received lower assessment scores because of their long performance time.

Limitations and future directions

First, this study was conducted at a single centre with a relatively small sample size, which may restrict the generalizability of the results. An international multicentre study incorporating clinical interns from diverse educational backgrounds could provide a more robust dataset. Stratified analyses across different centres might yield additional insights and enhance the overall validity of the conclusions regarding self-directed teaching methods.

Second, our study focused on basic surgical skills, which do not encompass the full range of skills required in clinical practice. It remains uncertain whether the effectiveness of SDTS is similar when it is applied to more complex surgical procedures. Future research should broaden the scope of evaluation to include advanced surgical tasks to determine whether the benefits observed in basic skills training extend to more complex procedures.

Third, the evaluation of learner performance was limited to the first week after training. Moreover, feedback was provided only after final video submission and therefore, did not inform iterative practice prior to the post-training OSCE. This delayed, non-iterative feedback design may have attenuated the impact of feedback, particularly for more complex procedural skills. Although the immediate post-training improvements are encouraging, the absence of both feedback-guided repetition and long-term follow-up prevents the evaluation of skill refinement, retention, and transfer. Future longitudinal studies incorporating iterative feedback cycles and tracking skill maintenance and application in real clinical settings are needed to fully assess the educational impact of self-directed instruction.

Fourth, 6 participants in the traditional teaching group were unable to complete the training due to unavoidable clinical duties (emergency surgeries or mandatory teaching rounds). Because OSCE outcomes could not be meaningfully imputed for learners who did not complete the intervention, an intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis was not feasible. Although we verified that baseline characteristics such as academic performance and psychological status did not differ significantly between the included and excluded students, the resulting per-protocol analysis may still introduce some bias.

Fifth, the subgroup comparison of feedback modalities involved small sample sizes. Although no statistically significant differences were detected among self-, peer-, and instructor-feedback, the study was likely underpowered to detect small or moderate effects.

Finally, although psychological constructs such as learner confidence, grit, and autonomy are highly relevant to self-directed and technology-enhanced learning, these variables were not formally assessed in this study. The absence of such measures limits deeper exploration of the mechanisms underlying the observed training outcomes and should be addressed in future research.

Hence, future research should include multicentre designs, address the power limitation, incorporate iterative feedback loops, and examine more complex procedures to better define the scope of SDTS implementation.

Conclusions

The SDTS model, grounded in SDT/SRL principles and supported by video-based feedback, presents a flexible and learner-centred approach to surgical skills training. While effective for basic procedures and allowing flexible utilization of training time, augmentation—such as minimum practice requirements—may be needed to optimize outcomes for more complex tasks. With further refinement, SDTS offers substantial promise as a scalable, technology-enhanced solution for modern medical education.
