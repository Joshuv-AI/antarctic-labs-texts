Rethinking therapsid phylogeny through Bayesian and cladistic approaches
Authors: Alienor Duhamel, Brenen Wynd, April Marie Wright, Atashni Moopen, Julien Benoit, Bruce Rubidge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Therapsids are early-diverging synapsids that thrived during the Permian and Triassic periods, and ultimately gave rise to mammals. They include six major groups, which already exhibited considerable diversity at their first appearance in the fossil record. Their early to middle Permian origins remain poorly understood due to a scarcity of fossils with clear therapsid morphology in the early Permian and the presence of an interval of poor fossil preservation near the early to middle Permian boundary (Olson’s Gap). This limits our insight into the phylogenetic relationships among the main middle Permian groups. This study presents a novel approach to therapsid phylogeny, using probabilistic frameworks. In performing a phylogenetic analysis based exclusively on cranial characters, using both RevBayes and MrBayes, this study challenges understanding of the early diversification of Therapsida. We recover Neotherapsida, with Anomodontia branching as the sister taxon to Theriodontia. Biarmosuchia and Dinocephalia form a clade. The positions of taxa such as Sinophoneus and Biseridens are reconsidered. The application of Fossilized Birth-Death models suggests that while Therapsida originated in the early Permian, currently known therapsid groups diversified around 281 to 272 million years ago and subsequently underwent rapid radiation into five clades: Biarmosuchia, Dinocephalia, Anomodontia, Gorgonopsia, and Eutheriodontia.

Introduction

Non-mammalian therapsids are among the most diverse groups of Permian and Triassic tetrapods. Therapsida evolved from a sphenacodontoid ‘pelycosaur’ grade ancestor between the middle and end of the early Permian and rapidly diversified into six taxonomic groups: Biarmosuchia, Anomodontia, Dinocephalia, Gorgonopsia, Therocephalia, and Cynodontia1. Current data suggest that they evolved at some point in the 5- to 10-million-year-long fossilization hiatus known as Olson’s Gap2–9. During Olson’s Gap, Permian tetrapod faunas underwent considerable changes in both diversity and faunal assemblage7, from a ‘pelycosaur’ dominated world in the early Permian (palaeoequatorial faunal assemblages) to a therapsid one in the middle and late Permian, recorded in eastern and southern Africa, Brazil, Russia, Europe, India and China2. This limited fossil record from the middle Permian has historically challenged the study of the origins of the Therapsida. The earliest recorded therapsids were already an extremely diverse and organised in a well-nested clade from their first appearance in the fossil record5,10,11. As sphenacodontids are the sister taxon to therapsida, the latter would have originated in the early Permian and the first step of their evolutionary radiation is no recorded.

Most recent phylogenetic analyses of Therapsida have focused on individual new specimens12–15. Few studies have focused on resolving the relationships between the six clades of therapsids, and have yielded conflicting results due to difficulties in resolving deep phylogenetic nodes while lacking morphological data1,5,16–21. For instance, Biarmosuchia has variously been considered closely related to dinocephalians, gorgonopsians, or anomodonts16,19,21–23, while dinocephalians have often been considered closely related to anomodonts19,24 or as the earliest-diverging Therapsida21. In some analyses, anomodonts form a clade with eutheriodonts, excluding gorgonopsians from Eutheriodontia25. Provocatively, it has also been suggested that Anomodontia and Therocephalia may form a clade16. In addition, recent phylogenetic analyses found that therocephalians might be paraphyletic26,27. The Biarmosuchia, Gorgonopsia, and Anomodontia have also been placed in a basal unresolved polytomy5. The clade Neotherapsida, including Anomodontia and Theriodontia was also proposed1. These inconsistent results are symptomatic of the lack of clear early-diverging therapsids and of fossiliferous horizons dating from or correlated to Olson’s Gap. Furthermore, the currently known synapsid fossil record in the early Permian is from equatorial paleoecosystems, while the fossil record in the middle to late Permian is almost exclusively from higher latitudes.

The most widely accepted phylogenetic framework currently places Biarmosuchia at the base of the tree and Cynodontia as the clade including crown mammals1. Dinocephalia, Anomodontia, Gorgonopsia, and Therocephalia branch in a pectinate manner as successive sister groups to Cynodontia1,28 (see Supplementary Fig. A3).

Until now, published phylogenetic analyses have predominantly relied on maximum parsimony. However, the absence of specimens from the earliest stages of therapsid evolution makes it difficult to achieve a fully resolved phylogeny. The possible rapid radiation of non-mammalian therapsids hinders the accumulation of distinctive synapomorphies necessary for reconstructing their phylogenetic relationships. This rapid evolution may also have led to the development of homoplasies, further complicating hypotheses about therapsid relationships. These factors have resulted in a difficult-to-resolve, deep polytomy at the base of the therapsid evolutionary tree, posing a challenge for traditional phylogenetic approaches29–33.

Probabilistic approaches provide a possible solution to this issue30,34,35 and Bayesian inference of phylogeny has shown promising results for resolving problematic phylogenetic relationships36–38. Traditionally applied to molecular data39, Bayesian inference is widely used with a combination of molecular and morphological data, and with a focus on estimating branch lengths and dating trees40,41. Advances in this field have enabled its application to morphological datasets, with the development of the Mk model, which modifies the assumptions of the simplest molecular model, JC6936,38,42–44. The advent of the Fossilized Birth-Death (FBD) model45 provides further opportunities to estimate divergence rates given fossil-based data. Bayesian inference of phylogeny itself may outperform parsimony46–48, but more importantly, its application in combination with a clock model provides insight into divergence times44,45,49.

For Therapsida, the application of probabilistic approaches, coupled with the accurate dating of their fossil record, has the potential to shed new light on the phylogenetic relationships of the clade. It may also clarify their early divergence during the early Permian and Olson’s Gap. In this research, the FBD model is applied to a new morphological dataset of therapsid cranial characters, in combination with relaxed-clock models. The goal is to help resolve the deepest phylogenetic nodes, which are critical to reconstruct the early diversification of Therapsida.

Materials and methods

Taxon sampling

Tab. A1 in the Supplementary material lists the specimens used for the phylogenetic analyses and the stratigraphic ranges associated. The literature used to score the taxa and to obtain the stratigraphic ranges is also mentioned (Supplementary material Tab. A1). Raranimus and the earliest-diverging genera and species of each of the six major clades of Therapsida were selected for the study (Tab. A1), such as Biseridens, Sinophoneus, Biarmosuchus, Patranomodon, Suminia, Nochnitsa, Phorcys, Lycosuchus, Simorhinella, Abdalodon, and Charassognathus. Specimens and taxa such as DA21/17-01-0121, Anomocephalus16,50, Tiarajudens50,51 or Galeops52 were not considered due to poor preservation and lack of informative cranial characters, and were replaced with contemporary representatives from the same clades. As our study focused on resolving deep nodes in the therapsid evolutionary tree, late-diverging taxa were not included.

Character selection

Cranial morphological characters were selected from the literature28,53–55 and rewritten if necessary. The scoring was confirmed (or updated when appropriate) using firsthand observations of specimens. Parsimonious non-informative characters for our dataset were not considered. Postcranial characters were not included for this study, given the lack of postcranial elements in numerous taxa from the dataset (most biarmosuchians, Raranimus, Biseridens, Australosyodon, Styracocephalus, Simorhinella, Phorcys, Nyaphulia, Patranomodon, Ulemica). This resulted in a total of 99 characters. The full list of characters can be found in the Supplementary material.

Bayesian inference of phylogeny

General model

Bayesian phylogenetic analyses were run with a matrix of 99 characters and 42 OTUs, using the developer version of MrBayes and RevBayes v.1.2.1. In MrBayes, the gamma model of among-character rate variation was used. In MrBayes, the lognormal model of among-character variation was tested, and gave identical phylogenetic results but with lesser statistical support. The characters were randomly sampled and considered unordered. Haptodus garnettensis is the outgroup. All taxa, excluding Haptodus and Dimetrodon were constraint in a ingroup. The analysis was run for 50 million generations, with four chains, two runs, two swaps, a burnin option set to 25%, and a sampling frequency set to 500 generations.

In RevBayes v 1.2.1, we applied the same model. RevBayes performs multiple Markov Chain Monte Carlo moves per iteration, thus, the two chains were run for 1 million generations. We sampled every 100 generations and removed 25% of the chain as burnin. The script is available in the Supplementary material.

Clock analysis, tip dating and Fossil-birth-death model

For the time-calibrated analyses in MrBayes, two relaxed-clock models were tested, both using a FBD model to calibrate the tree45,56 and a uniform distribution for the age of the OTUs. To set the tree age prior, the age of the most recent ancestor of the tree was defined using offsetexponential function between 307 and 310 Ma21. A uniform distribution was chosen to calibrate the prior on the age of the fossil tips, and the lower and upper bound values were set based on the earliest occurrence of the corresponding taxon.

Two relaxed-clock models were tested with MrBayes: the uncorrelated relaxed clock (IGR, Independent Gamma Rate, see57) and the Thorne-Kishino (TK02) model, which is an autocorrelated relaxed clock model58–60. Other relaxed clock models available in the literature are not suitable for morphological datasets61. The TK02 model58–60 assumes that the substitution rate in a lineage is influenced by the rates in the neighbouring branches, so that the evolutionary rate is not completely independent, so that a daughter branch’s rate is influenced by its parent branch. In contrast, the IGR model assumes that rates are independent among all branches.

The IGR model was set with a branch-length rate that follows a gamma distribution, in which the variance increases following an exponential function of 10. Similarly, in the TK02 model, an exponential prior probability distribution of 10 was chosen for the variance of the rate multiplier. To calibrate the nodes in both models, a normal distribution was used, with a mean \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu$$\end{document} of 0.001 and a standard deviation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma$$\end{document} of 0.0138.

A clear preference was given to the IGR model with regard of marginal likelihood values, obtained from the MrBayes analyses (-1412.06 for TK02 and -1375.36 for IGR, Bayes Factor of 73.28), average effective sample size (ESS) values (less than 100 in several parameters for the TK02 analysis), and better average standard deviation values (0.003418 for IGR and 0.004142 for TK02).

Two analyses were performed with RevBayes, one with an uncorrelated lognormal clock and a second with an autocorrelated lognormal clock. The uncorrelated clock assumes branch-specific rates of evolution are drawn independently from a lognormal distribution58,59. This is substantially similar to the TK02 model in MrBayes, but importantly the RevBayes analysis also employs a gamma parameter with four rate categories. Both clock model results are presented in this paper. Under the FBD model45,62, we set priors on speciation, extinction, fossilization rates, as well as the origin time (uniform between 307 and 310 million years ago). The results of this phylogenetic analysis were then used in a downstream analysis of extinction rates over time in RevBayes. In this analysis, the tree was divided up into 100 intervals of equal size. On the fixed tree from the prior analysis, we estimated the extinction, speciation, and fossilization rate parameters of the FBD model in each time slice (see Supplementary material A2). While it would be computationally intractable to infer the tree under this model, to infer these rates on a fixed tree is possible. We used log Bayes factors to determine if there was evidence for elevated extinction rates in any of the 100 intervals63. We visualised these rates over time in the R package RevGadgets64. Importantly, this analysis does not indicate magnitude of extinction or speciation, but only the probability of Extinction outpacing Speciation or vice versa.

Parsimony analysis

To run the parsimony analysis, TNT (Tree analysis using New Technology65) version 1.566 was used, with 99 morphological characters and 42 OTUs. A traditional search was performed. The starting tree was a Wagner tree with a random seed of 10 and 100 addition sequence replicates. The tree bisection reconnection (TBR) algorithm was selected with 100 trees saved per replication. Trees were not collapsed after the search but existing trees were replaced. Synapomorphies were listed and the Bremer indices were calculated (derived from suboptimal trees).

A second parsimony analysis was performed in RStudio v2025.05.0, using the MaximizeParsimony function in the TreeSearch package. The results were used to identify rogue taxa and to calculate tip instability values with the Rogue package (see the Supplementary material for the script). RStudio was also used to perform a Phytools stochastic character map analysis with the mcc trees produced by MrBayes and RevBayes to search for clade support. The script for the Phytools analysis and the mcc trees are available in Supplementary material.

Results

Phylogenetic results

The parsimony analysis resulted in a relatively well-resolved strict consensus tree (Fig. 1). Tetraceratops is the sister taxon to Therapsida. Anomodontia forms a clade with Theriodontia. The relationship between Raranimus, the Biarmosuchia, Dinocephalia and Anomodontia + Theriodontia is unresolved (Fig. 1). Moreover, in this analysis, the relationship between the gorgonopsians Phorcys and Gorgonops; the anomodonts Niassodon, Diictodon and Eodicynodon; the biarmosuchians Leucocephalus, Lende and NMQR 1702; and the cynodonts Charassognathus, Thrinaxodon, Galesaurus and Procynosuchus, is unresolved. Similarly, the phylogenetic position of Simorhinella relative to other Eutheriodontia is uncertain in the cladistic analysis. In the majority rule consensus, Biarmosuchia and Dinocephalia form a clade, with Raranimus as the sister taxon.

The IGR analysis performed with MrBayes provides a fully resolved Maximum compatibility tree (Fig. 2), with Tetraceratops as the sister taxon to Therapsida. Raranimus is recovered as the sister taxon to a clade including Biseridens, Biarmosuchia and Dinocephalia.

A third and fourth Bayesian analyses were conducted with RevBayes67, employing respectively an autocorrelated and an uncorrelated lognormal clock model. The resulting Maximum compatibility trees are presented in Figs. 3 and 4. The two trees differ primarily in the position of Raranimus and the therocephalian specimens. Under the autocorrelated clock, Raranimus is recovered as the earliest-diverging therapsid, and therocephalians form a paraphyletic grade relative to cynodonts (Fig. 3). By contrast, the uncorrelated clock recovers Raranimus as the sister taxon to Biseridens, Biarmosuchia, and Dinocephalia (mirroring the IGR results in MrBayes), with therocephalians forming a clade (Fig. 4). These contrasting placements of Raranimus in turn influence the inferred divergence times of later-diverging therapsid clades.

Phylogenetic relationships and monophyly of therapsid clades

The monophyly of Therapsida is supported, consistent with previous studies1,4,5,16,17,19,28, with a high posterior probability of 0.88 and a divergence time at around 278.38 Ma, setting the origin of the clade in the early Permian (Figs. 1 and 2). The uncorrelated clock analysis performed with RevBayes supports the clade with a posterior probability of 0.68 and a divergence date of 281.96 Ma (Fig. 4). In contrast, the RevBayes autocorrelated tree provided stronger support for the clade (posterior probability of 0.95) and a slightly younger divergence date (275.54 Ma, Kungurian, see Fig. 3). All major therapsid subclades are recovered as monophyletic (Figs. 2, 3 and 4), with the exceptions of Dinocephalia (average posterior probability = 0.67; 0.50 and 0.55 in the RevBayes trees) and Therocephalia, which is paraphyletic to Cynodontia (Fig.4).

The strict consensus resulted from the parsimony analysis left most derived nodes unresolved and failed to clarify the positions of Raranimus, Biarmosuchia, and Dinocephalia (Fig. 1). While several nodes are poorly supported, the Bayesian analyses provided a full resolution of the deep nodes, and challenged traditional views on therapsid evolution (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). Notably, the Bayesian analyses support a clade that groups Biarmosuchia and Dinocephalia with a low posterior probability of 0.47 in the IGR analysis conducted with MrBayes (Fig. 2) and low posterior probabilities of 0.29 and 0.35 in RevBayes (Figs. 3 and 4). This close relationship between Biarmosuchia and Dinocephalia has been previously suggested in taxonomic studies, though it has not been supported by modern phylogenetic analyses1,23,68–70. Clock analyses in MrBayes and the uncorrelated clock on RevBayes suggest that the clade including Biarmosuchia and Dinocephalia would have started to diversify around 272.93 Ma (Roadian) and 275.63 Ma (Kungurian), respectively (Fig. 2 and 4). The autocorrelated clock indicates a diversification time at 269.52 Ma (Fig. 3). Interestingly, Biseridens branches at the base of this clade contrary to the current phylogenetic framework that places it as an early-diverging anomodont19, though the low node support (0.28, 0.14 and 0.24) warrants caution in interpreting this result.

Biarmosuchia were previously regarded as gorgonopsians71, but subsequent studies established them as a distinct therapsid group1,17,21,22,72. Their monophyly is supported in both the current cladistic and Bayesian analyses (Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4). The IGR clock performed with MrBayes indicates a possible diversification date for the clade around 265.28 Ma, during the Wordian (Fig. 2). This suggests that early biarmosuchian evolution involves more than 7 million years of unsampled time with their last common ancestor with Dinocephalia, which would require further investigation. The RevBayes analyses give a similar divergence time at 266.38 and 267.90 Ma, suggesting a ghost lineage of 7 to 12 million years (Figs. 3 and 4). Among Biarmosuchia, Lemurosaurus is the earliest-diverging burnetiamorph and is phylogenetically distinct from NMQR 1702 in all analyses, a biarmosuchian specimen that was previously assigned to Lemurosaurus73. Burnetiamorpha is strongly supported as monophyletic, with posterior probabilities of 0.85 (MrBayes), 0.92 (RevBayes autocorrelated), and 0.90 (RevBayes uncorrelated) (Figs. 2, 3 and 4).

The placement of Dinocephalia within the therapsid evolutionary tree has long been debated, with some studies positioning them stemward of Anomodontia1,4, while others suggest a more derived position5. In all our phylogenetic analyses, Dinocephalia is recovered as monophyletic (Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4), with either Sinophoneus or Biseridens as the earliest-diverging taxon. The parsimony analysis places Biseridens as the earliest-diverging dinocephalian (Fig. 1) while the position is held by Sinophoneus in Bayesian analyses (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). The IGR tree generated with MrBayes suggests that dinocephalians diversified approximately 271.47 million years ago from a common ancestor shared with Biarmosuchia (node at 272.93 Ma). The node is supported by a posterior probability of 0.67. The RevBayes analyses, on the other hand, yielded average posterior probabilities of 0.55 and 0.50 and an age of 268.91 and 273.90 Ma (Figs. 3 and 4). Bayesian and parsimony analyses suggest that the anteosaurs are paraphyletic, with Sinophoneus placing either as an early-diverging dinocephalian (Fig. 1 and 2), or as the sister taxon to Biarmosuchia (with a weaker supported node, see Figs. 3 and 4). Australosyodon, Syodon, Anteosaurus and Titanophoneus form a clade that diversified 266.75 million years ago (267.16 Ma in the RevBayes tree), with posterior probabilities of 0.62 in the IGR tree and 0.44 in the RevBayes analysis (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). Styracocephalus is consistently recovered at the base of Tapinocephalidae.

All phylogenetic analyses performed support the monophyly of Neotherapsida1 with low to moderate support (Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4). In both parsimony and Bayesian phylogenetic analyses, Neotherapsida is weakly supported by a Bremer index of 1 and posterior probabilities of 0.30 (IGR model with MrBayes), 0.09 and 0.15 (RevBayes) (Figs. 1 and 2). Caution is required in the interpretation of these results. These findings, recovering the monophyly of Neotherapsida, align with previous studies1,4,74, but contradict others that place Biarmosuchia as the sister group to Theriodontia21, or Dinocephalia as the sister group to the Dicynodontia24,75. The latter hypothesis was subsequently rejected, so that the Dicynodontia are now considered derived anomodonts and the Anomodontia and Dinocephalia are distinct76–78, forming a clade in some phylogenetic analyses19. The age estimate of the neotherapsid node is 275.31 Ma, late Kungurian in the MrBayes tree (Fig. 2), and 270.17 and 279.39 Ma in the RevBayes trees (Figs. 3 and 4). Subsequently, anomodonts are estimated to have diversify in the middle of the Roadian, according to the MrBayes analysis (270.43 Ma, see Fig. 2). The RevBayes analyses suggest a diversification around the late Roadian (autocorrelated clock, 267.83 Ma) or Kungurian (uncorrelated clock, 273.75). Both RevBayes clocks show contemporaneous diversification times for Anomodontia, Theriodontia and Dinocephalia. The inclusion of Nyaphulia as the earliest-diverging dicynodont suggests that Dicynodontia diversified approximately 5–8 million years (giving the different estimations) after the origin of the anomodont clade (Figs. 2, 3 and 4).

In the literature, Gorgonopsia has been considered outside Theriodontia69,74,76, whereas Anomodontia has sometimes been placed within Theriodontia74. Although alternative hypotheses have been proposed79, modern phylogenetic analyses consistently recover Gorgonopsia as part of the monophyletic group Theriodontia1,4,17,53,77. Our analyses strongly support the monophyly of Theriodontia (exclusive of Anomodontia), with posterior probabilities of 0.96 (MrBayes, see Fig. 2), 0.87 (RevBayes autocorrelated, see Fig. 3), and 0.82 (RevBayes uncorrelated. see Fig. 4). The IGR clock suggests the clade diversified around 270.98 Ma (Fig. 2) while the RevBayes autocorrelated model gives 267.32 Ma (Fig. 3). The uncorrelated model gives an older date at 274.17 Ma (4).

Gorgonopsia is placed as the sister taxon to Eutheriodontia (Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4). In the parsimony analysis, Nochnitsa falls outside Gorgonopsia, although with weak support (Bremer index = 1; Fig. 1). Rogue analysis, however, identifies Nochnitsa as a highly unstable taxon (Supplementary Fig. A5). In Bayesian analyses, Nochnitsa is instead recovered as the earliest-diverging gorgonopsian (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). According to the MrBayes and RevBayes analyses, the diversification date of Gorgonopsia is between 267.30 and 264.27 Ma (Roadian-Wordian), and the clade is supported by posterior probabilities ranging from 0.84 to 0.99 (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). This implies a ghost lineage of around 3–7 million years separating their last common ancestor with Eutheriodontia from the estimated diversification of Gorgonopsia (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). The recently described gorgonopsian specimen DA21/17-01-01 from Mallorca may represent material that could bridge this gap21. The current analysis of the specimen and geological context suggest a Roadian to Wordian age, with the possibility to be older21.

The monophyly of the Eutheriodontia is widely accepted1,4,17,21,25,53,74,79, and is supported in our analyses by strong posterior probabilities of 1 (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). The IGR and the uncorrelated RevBayes clocks suggest that, as in Gorgonopsia, there is a 5 million-year gap between the diversification of Theriodontia and that of Eutheriodontia (Figs. 2 and 4). By contrast, the autocorrelated model on RevBayes does not support this gap (Fig. 3).

The uncorrelated RevBayes clock analysis supports the monophyly of Therocephalia, and the group branch in a polytomy with cynodonts in the parsimony analysis (Figs. 1 and 4). Therocephalian monophyly is weakly supported (posterior probability of 0.34), and the uncorrelated clock analysis suggests a diversification time at 267.88 Ma (Fig. 4). The monophyly of Therocephalia is questioned in recent literature4,26,27,53. Studies with a primary focus on early cynodonts suggest that therocephalians are paraphyletic with regards to cynodonts26,27. This hypothesis is supported by the IGR and the autocorrelated RevBayes analyses (Figs. 23), and they provide a Wordian age for the diversification of Eutheriodontia (around 266 Ma), and a late Permian age for Cynodontia (259.37 and 258.09 Ma, see Figs. 2 and 3). Discussion

Review of the Dashankou specimens: Raranimus, Biseridens and Sinophoneus

Raranimus, the earliest-diverging therapsid?

Raranimus dashankouensis is known from a single specimen that only preserves the snout (IVPP V15424), but possesses some classic synapomorphies diagnosing Therapsida, such as a contact between the prefrontal and maxilla, and a deeply troughed vomer5,9. Its unique combination of plesiomorphic and derived characters suggests an early-diverging position in the therapsid evolutionary tree5,9. The initial description placed Raranimus as the earliest-diverging therapsid5, a hypothesis that was later corroborated with additional data and CT-scanning9,19,21. The parsimony analysis places Raranimus in an unresolved position at the base of the tree (Fig. 1). Across the 48 most parsimonious trees recovered in the parsimony analysis, Raranimus alternates among three positions: as the earliest-diverging therapsid, as sister to Biarmosuchia + Dinocephalia, or as the earliest-diverging biarmosuchian.

The autocorrelated clock analysis positions Raranimus as the earliest-diverging therapsid, and supports previous phylogenetic studies5,9,21. The node is strongly supported by a posterior probability of 0.95 (Fig. 3). The IGR relaxed clock performed in MrBayes places Raranimus as the sister taxon to Biseridens and the Biarmosuchia + Dinocephalia clade (Fig. 2), but with a low posterior probability (0.25). The uncorrelated clock performed with RevBayes gives a similar result, with a poorly supported node (0.15, see Fig. 4).

The phylogenetic position of Raranimus varies considerably depending on the analysis performed (Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4). Because of its fragmentary nature (only 20 characters out of 99 could be scored), Raranimus may acts as a wildcard (or rogue) taxon, partially preventing the full resolution of the deep nodes in the phylogenetic analyses. We used the Rogue package in R to test our dataset; the results identified Raranimus as a wildcard taxon. In addition, we found that Raranimus exhibits a greater tip instability than other taxa in the dataset (over 0.06, compared with most taxa, which fall between 0.02 and 0.05; see Supplementary Fig. A5). When Raranimus was excluded from the analysis, the strict consensus of sixteen most parsimonious trees recovered full resolution of the deep nodes without any major changes in the topology (see Supplementary material Fig. A1).

Biseridens, an anomondont?

Both parsimony and Bayesian phylogenetic analyses place Biseridens outside Anomodontia (Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4). In the parsimony analysis, it is placed as the earliest-diverging dinocephalian, with a low Bremer index of 1 (Fig. 1).

In Bayesian analyses (MrBayes and RevBayes), Biseridens is placed as the sister taxon to Biarmosuchia and Dinocephalia, with low support of 0.28, 0.15 and 0.24 (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). In regards of those results, Biseridens, like Raranimus, would represent an early-diverging therapsid outside of the main known clades. Biseridens ressembles both Biarmosuchia and Dinocephalia as the vomerine process of the premaxilla is absent (character 5) and the dorsal nasal process of the premaxilla is long and reach the level of the canine (character 1), the ventral surface of the vomer is deeply throughed (character 47), the anterior ramus of the pterygoid bears teeth (character 60).

Recent phylogenetic studies commonly regard Biseridens as the earliest-diverging anomondont19,21,80. Its skull and mandible lack several key anomodont characters, such as the mandibular fenestra and a short ventral process of the tabular. Instead, it exhibits dinocephalian traits, including a posteroventral extension of the temporal fenestra (postorbital scoop) and a posteroventrally curved canine, features otherwise found only in Anteosauria. The revised description proposed that the elevated position of the zygomatic arch relative to the maxillary tooth row supports the placement of Biseridens within Anomodontia19. However, as noted in the initial description81, the zygomatic arch is actually level with the maxillary tooth row. These authors further suggested that Biseridens resembles Syodon and the putative ‘Eotitanosuchus’ (a synonym of Biarmosuchus). In this context, Biseridens may represent the earliest-diverging dinocephalian (Fig. 1).

Sinophoneus and the monophyly of the Anteosauria

Sinophoneus is recovered as the earliest-diverging dinocephalian in the Bayesian analyses and as a basal dinocephalian in the cladistic analysis (Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4). These placements differ from those in previous studies20,21,82–86. The description of the holotype placed Sinophoneus within Anteosauria82, but did not provide a phylogenetic analysis. Initial phylogenetic studies placed Sinophoneus within the derived Anteosaurinae83,84. Subsequently, Sinophoneus was proposed as the earliest-diverging anteosaur85,86.

Sinophoneus comprises specimens of various ontogenetic stages85. The only fully grown adult specimen (collection number GMV160185) is incomplete, and pachyostosis obscures some morphological features. This may have caused discrepancies in character scoring, since previous studies used juvenile specimens, whereas we considered only the adult. These factors may explain the inconsistencies between our tree topologies and those of previous phylogenetic studies21,83,85.

A clade uniting Biarmosuchia and Dinocephalia?

All three Bayesian analyses recovered a clade that includes Biarmosuchia and Dinocephalia (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). However, the posterior probabilities do not exceed 0.47, which warrants caution in interpreting this results. The strict consensus of the cladistic analysis could not recover the clade nor the list of synapomorphies (Fig. 1). However, while analyzing the forty-eight most parsimonious trees, all trees recovered a monophyletic relationship between Biarmosuchia and Dinocephalia. The node collapses in the strict consensus tree due to Raranimus acting as a wildcard with high leaf instability. We performed a second parsimony analysis on TNT without Raranimus, which found sixteen most parsimonious trees and provided a full resolution of the deep nodes (see Supplementary material Fig. A1). The strict consensus tree confirms a monophyletic relationship between Biarmosuchia and Dinocephalia.

Previous phylogenetic analyses did not recover a clade including only Biarmosuchia and Dinocephalia. Instead, the two groups were retrieved in a pectinate arrangement relative to other therapsid groups, with Biarmosuchia as the earliest-diverging taxon1,4,5. However, the most recent phylogeny focusing on Therapsida, placed Dinocephalia as the earliest-diverging therapsid, with Biarmosuchia forming a clade with Theriodontia21.

No clade including solely Biarmosuchia and Dinocephalia, with their modern definitions, has been taxonomically introduced in the literature. Names such as ‘Alphatherapsida’ and ‘Eotheriodontia’ have been introduced in taxonomic studies but would either include the Gorgonopsia (‘Alphatherapsida’) or exclude Tapinocephalidae (‘Eotheriodontia’)68,69.

If our results are confirmed by further phylogenetic analyses with stronger node supports, it might be advisable to refer to the clade as Dinomorpha, first introduced by Ivakhnenko (2008)87, but including modern definitions of Biarmosuchia and Dinocephalia. This clade could be redefined as stem-based, including all therapsids more closely related to Biarmosuchus and Tapinocaninus than to Patranomodon or Eriphostoma.

Simorhinella and the monophyly of Therocephalia

The monophyly of Therocephalia, originally proposed in 198677, has gained broad acceptance1,17,53,88. The analysis produced in RevBayes with the uncorrelated clock supports this hypothesis (Fig. 4). However, the IGR and autocorrelated clock analyses from MrBayes and RevBayes challenge this classification, suggesting that therocephalians are paraphyletic, with Simorhinella as the closest relative to cynodonts (Figs. 2 and 3).

The hypothesis that Therocephalia gave rise to Cynodontia, first proposed in 193089 and subsequently supported90,91, has remained a topic of debate53. Although most studies consider therocephalians monophyletic1,17,53,88, the recent morphological analyses of the basal cynodont, Charassognathus, have revived the hypothesis of therocephalian paraphyly26. A recent 2024 study focusing on Eutheriodontia phylogeny further supports therocephalian paraphyly, showing Alopecognathus, Lycosuchus, Theriognathus and Olivierosuchus branching off at the base of Cynodontia27. Two of the three Bayesian analyses presented in this paper (IGR and the autocorrelated clocks) place Simorhinella, one of the oldest therocephalians from the South African upper Tapinocephalus Assemblage Zone (262.0 to 260.259 Ma), as the sister taxon to Cynodontia, with posterior probabilities of 0.58 and 0.84 (Figs. 2 and 3). These results, supporting therocephalian paraphyly, suggest that Cynodontia may have originated from a therocephalian ancestor, with an estimated divergence between 261.56 and 261.97 Ma (Figs. 2 and 3).

While all other therapsid groups emerged during the early to middle Permian stages, the earliest cynodonts are found in late Permian faunal assemblages. This results in a ghost lineage of approximately 8 to 14 million years between the earliest therapsids (Raranimus in the Roadian, Patranomodon, Nyaphulia, and Eodicynodon from the South African Eodicynodon Assemblage Zone, with a first occurrence at 266 Ma) and the first recognized cynodont (Charassognathus and Abdalodon) from the upper horizons of the Endothiodon Assemblage Zone (258.5 Ma)92. Reassessing middle Permian therocephalian specimens as early-diverging cynodonts could account for this ghost lineage, and shift the timing of cynodont emergence to the middle Permian. This will be the subject of a forthcoming paper.

Exploring the timing and impact of Olson’s Gap on the synapsid fossil record

The study of the early evolution of Therapsida is problematic. They may have originated at the Carboniferous–Permian transition, yet the earliest fossil record of major known clades dates to the middle Permian5,19,85,93. A substantial portion of their evolutionary history during the early Permian is therefore missing (the gradual evolution hypothesis4,94). Another factor in their missing fossil record is Olson’s Gap. Spanning the early–middle Permian transition, it is characterized by a sparse tetrapod fossil record, particularly of synapsids2. This interval coincides with a major faunal turnover, shifting from a ‘pelycosaur’-dominated world to one dominated by the Therapsida. The timing of this event has been variably estimated based on geological strata correlations2,8,95–97. The Gap A hypothesis8, originally proposed in 20012, places the Russian Kazanian fauna in the Wordian and the North American Chickasha and San Angelo formations in the late Kungurian, creating a stratigraphic gap during the Roadian2,98–100. The Gap B hypothesis8 suggests that the Russian Kazanian fauna could instead be correlated to the Roadian, shifting the gap to the late Kungurian101. Subsequent studies have proposed that the Russian Kazanian fauna, the North American Chickasha and San Angelo formations, and the Chinese Dashankou fauna are all Roadian in age, thereby bridging and eliminating the gap9,102. Furthermore, the transition between the early and middle Permian may have been marked by an extinction event7,8,95, with higher Bayes Factor and likelihood values supporting the extinction hypothesis over either Gap A or Gap B8. In our study, both Gap A and Gap B are indicated on the phylogenetic trees (Figs. 2, 3 and 4), and fossil age estimations were taken from the most recent stratigraphic dating for each taxon (see Supplementary material Tabs. A1 and A). Because most taxa originate from the South African Karoo Beaufort Group, that is well-constraint in term of chronostratigraphy, the precise stratigraphic placement of the gap is expected to minimally affect age estimations generated by the Fossilized-Birth–Death model.

Our fixed-tree analysis with RevBayes detected variations in FBD parameters over time. Speciation and fossilization appear relatively constant through time (Fig. A2-A and B). In contrast, the extinction rate fluctuates and shows periods where heightened extinction rates are supported (Fig. A2-C). The outputs of our fixed-tree FBD rate estimates (Fig. A2-B in the Supplementary material) suggest steady fossilization rates through the Gap. We identify two pulses of increased extinction probability (metric is agnostic to extinction magnitude), one during Olson’s Gap and another around 222-225 million years ago. These findings support Olson’s Gap extinction hypothesis and likely reflect the extinction of sphenacodontine synapsids.

The earliest known therapsid fossils appear just after Olson’s Gap (or are likely contemporary, such as the Dashankou fauna), suggesting that major therapsid clades may have originated during or even before this interval. Despite uncertainties, our analysis indicates an increase in diversification rates immediately after Olson’s Gap. We propose that new fossil discoveries of early-diverging therapsids could push the timing of this diversification back into the early Permian.

The Bayesian analyses suggest that multiple diversification and divergence events occurred during and prior to Olson’s Gap (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). Models estimate that therapsids originated before Olson’s Gap, in the early Permian (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). The clade comprising Biarmosuchia and Dinocephalia likely diversified during the late Kungurian to Roadian, around 275.63 to 269.52 Ma, and originated before that in the early (Fig. 4) to late Kungurian (Figs. 2 and 3). Within the timeframe of the Gap B hypothesis of Olson’s Gap, several diversification events are indicated in the dinocephalian lineage, while biarmosuchians arose shortly after Olson’s Gap (Figs. 2, 3 and 4).

Furthermore, the clock models suggest that both Anomodontia and Theriodontia originated and diversified during Olson’s Gap B hypothesis, with clock models differing on the timing of their common origin—either during Olson’s Gap B, Gap A, or prior to the Gap (Fig. 3, 2, 4). Anomodontia is estimated to have diversified around 273.75 to 267.83 Ma ago, while dicynodonts began their diversification around 265–267 Ma (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). Additionally, early theriodonts likely diversified between 274.17 to 267.32 Ma ago (with an origin in the late Kungurian to the middle Roadian). Eutheriodontia and Gorgonopsia diversified shortly after (middle Roadian to Wordian).

Two models of therapsid diversification have been proposed: The rapid evolution hypothesis posits that therapsids developed their defining characteristics over a brief evolutionary period, with diversification occurring within just 3 to 6 million years4,94.The gradual evolution hypothesis suggests that therapsids acquired their unique characteristics gradually from a ‘pelycosaur’-grade ancestor, with early forms of therapsids barely distinguishable from other early Permian tetrapods4,94.Some evidence suggests that Therapsida may have originated in the Carboniferous, based on uncertain “therapsid” material103. In contrast, our relaxed clock models within a Bayesian framework place their origin in the early Permian (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). Apart from Olson’s Gap, the study of early therapsids is hindered by geological sampling issues. In the early Permian, the entire synapsid fossil record comes from paleoequatorial environments, while most middle and late Permian synapsids have been found in paleo high latitude21. A large part of the Permian synapsid evolutionary history is missing, questioning the timeline and place of origin of therapsids. As ’sphenacodontids’ are the sister taxon to Therapsida, it is likely that therapsids emerged in the early Permian1,104 and their early evolutionary history is not recorded. The recent discovery of a gorgonopsian specimen in Mallorca, could shed new light, as the specimen might be as old as the Artinskian, pushing all divergence and diversification events to the early Permian21. In their description of the specimen, the authors provided a Maximum compatible tree from a relaxed morphological clock Bayesian analysis that shows similar divergence and diversification dates as our Bayesian analyses21.

The IGR model suggests an 8-million-year gap between the diversification of currently known therapsid clades (278.38 Ma, see Fig. 2) and the earliest known therapsid fossils (Raranimus, Sinophoneus, and Biseridens from the Dashankou fauna), while the RevBayes analysis suggests a longer 10 to 11-million-year gap. This evidence supports the hypothesis that currently known therapsid lineages underwent rapid radiation predominantly during the transition from the early to middle Permian, as previously proposed4,23,105. However, on the autocorrelated model, most of the early diversification events take place in the Roadian (Fig. 3), and all models suggest that the currently known therapsid clades emmerged during the Roadian and Wordian (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). Our results suggest that the first phase of therapsid evolution likely occurred during the early Permian. This phase involved the gradual acquisition of distinct characters, making it difficult to distinguish them from their sphenacodontoid stem group, and leaving no clear trace in the fossil record. It is possible that undocumented early therapsid lineages were affected by Olson’s extinction, as suggested by our analysis of FBD parameter variation through time (see Supplementary material Fig. A2). The second phase, at the transition from the early to the middle Permian, was characterized by a rapid diversification that gave rise to the therapsid clades known from the middle Permian. We propose that this rapid radiation and morphological divergence may have been linked to Olson’s extinction, which created new ecological niches that favored therapsid diversification7,8. Lastly, the analyses are based on currently known middle Permian material, and the rise of Therapsida could be pushed further back with the discovery of distinct early Permian therapsid material.

Conclusion

The time-calibrated trees estimate an early Permian origin for Therapsida, with major clades—including Dinocephalia, Theriodontia, and Anomodontia—diversifying during Olson’s Gap (Gap B hypothesis). This suggests that the transition from the early to the middle Permian was at least partially a period of diversification and proliferation for the synapsid lineage. Although concurrent extinctions affected other early Permian tetrapod lineages7,8,95, therapsids may have undergone adaptive radiation, as suggested by the time-calibrated phylogenetic trees.

Both the parsimony and Bayesian analyses challenge the phylogenetic position of the Dashankou taxa Raranimus, Biseridens, and Sinophoneus. Whereas Biseridens may be the earliest-diverging dinocephalian, Sinophoneus occupies a basal position, raising questions about the monophyly of the anteosaurs. Therocephalians are recovered as paraphyletic in most trees, suggesting that Cynodontia may have originated from a therocephalian-grade ancestor.
