Integrating Non-Invasive Physical and Behavioral Indicators Into Routine Health Check-ups for Detecting Early Depression in Older Adults Living Alone
Authors: Hyun Woo Jung, Jae-Hyun Kim, Jung Jae Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs South Korea faces a rapidly aging population, older adults living alone are becoming increasingly vulnerable to depression. This study aimed to identify non-invasive and easily measurable physical and behavioral indicators that are associated with depression in this high-risk group. We proposed a novel screening framework—Depression Prediction based on Anthropometric and Clinical Signs—designed for integration into existing health check-ups to support early detection.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study among 1,093 older adults living alone in South Korea. The Depression Prediction based on Anthropometric and Clinical Signs was developed by incorporating vital signs, anthropometric measures, physical function tests, and health behaviors. Multiple logistic regression analyses were used to examine the association between these indicators and depression, with subgroup analyses by sex.ResultsSeveral indicators of Depression Prediction based on Anthropometric and Clinical Signs were significantly associated with depression. Larger head circumference and sensory impairments were associated with a higher risk of depression. In contrast, better neurological gait performance, more frequent meals, and longer exercise duration were associated with lower risk. Sex-specific patterns were identified, among which visual, olfactory, and gustatory dysfunctions were notable predictors. Among men, elevated respiratory rate and gustatory dysfunction were prominent. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis showed fair discrimination for pooled (area under the curve = 0.74, cut-off = 0.304) and female (area under the curve = 0.73) models, whereas the male model demonstrated lower performance (area under the curve = 0.62).ConclusionSimple, non-invasive physical and behavioral measures may serve as effective indicators for identifying depression risk in older adults living alone. Incorporating these indicators into routine health check-ups could support early detection and referral, particularly for those who are unlikely to seek psychiatric care. The Depression Prediction based on Anthropometric and Clinical Signs model offers a feasible approach to enhance mental health screening in aging populations, although further validation and longitudinal studies are warranted.Keywords: Anthropometry; Vital Signs; Health Behavior; Depression; Older Adults, Living Alone

INTRODUCTION

Depression is a major mental health condition that significantly impacts global disease burden, affecting > 280 million individuals worldwide as of 2020.1,2 It is also a serious factor that causes suicide risk and other physical illnesses, ultimately leading to disability and mortality.1 Although early detection and intervention are critical in preventing its progression, access to psychiatric care remains challenging, particularly among individuals without a prior history of mental illness. This difficulty is due to various factors, including societal stigma surrounding psychiatric treatment and the subjective and internalized nature of psychiatric symptoms, which are not easily observable as external signs.3

Despite the seriousness of this problem, the current pathway to psychiatric care relies heavily on self-recognition of symptoms and voluntary presentation at medical facilities. According to the National Center for Mental Health in South Korea, only 35.3% of psychiatric patients seek care due to external prompting, while the remaining 64.7% voluntarily visit psychiatric services based on their own awareness of mental health difficulties.4 This reliance on self-initiated care poses a substantial barrier for older individuals living alone, who often lack the ability to objectively assess their mental state and the close social connections that could facilitate help-seeking.

Many Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development countries, including South Korea, operate national health screening programs aimed at early detection of various physical conditions.5 These screenings typically involve simple examiner-assessed measures (e.g., body mass index [BMI], vision and hearing tests, and blood pressure) and self-reported health behaviors (e.g., meal frequency, smoking, alcohol consumption, and exercise habits).6,7 However, mental health assessments, particularly for depression, are incorporated only once every 10 years under Korea’s national system.8 Given the limitations of relying solely on self-awareness for mental health care access, there is a critical need to explore whether fundamental physical examinations could also serve as a preliminary screening tool for depression.

To address this need, it is essential to identify the basic vital signs, anthropometric measures, functional markers, and health behaviors that are meaningfully associated with depression. Building upon this rationale, the present study theoretically conceptualized a new depression screening package—Depression Prediction based on Anthropometric and Clinical Signs (DEPACS)—using data collected from the Intensive Managed Care for Elderly Living Alone (IMCELA) program initiated by the Dementia Care Center (DCC) in Chungcheongnam-do.

DEPACS includes simple, non-invasive measures that overlap with existing health check-up items and do not require expensive diagnostic equipment, yet are theoretically supported to be relevant to depressive symptoms. The package comprises assessments of heart rate, respiratory rate, head circumference, neck circumference, BMI, hearing impairment, visual impairment, olfactory dysfunction, gustatory dysfunction, neurological gait performance, and self-reported lifestyle behaviors, including meal frequency, smoking, alcohol intake, and exercise frequency. The theoretical rationale for each selected variable is detailed in the measurement section.

In summary, this study hypothesized that the variables included in the DEPACS package will show significant associations with depressive symptoms among older individuals living alone, offering a potential avenue for enhancing mental health screening accessibility through integration into general health check-ups. Additionally, we conducted sex-specific subgroup analyses given the inherent physical and psychological differences between males and females that may affect depression risk and its associated factors.

METHODS

Participants

This study included 1,093 older adults who agreed to participate in the IMCELA initiative at the DCC in Chungcheongnam-do. The participants were selected from a pool of 1,427 individuals enrolled in the Basic Care Service for the Elderly Living Alone, a program initiated by the Cheonan Senior Welfare Center. Enrollment into the IMCELA program was determined through evaluations conducted by a committee comprising the DCC director (a psychiatrist) and three researchers. Data were collected between July 2015 and March 2018.

Measurements

Definitions and codes of variables are available in the Supplementary Data 1 (Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2).

Statistical analysis

To identify the factors associated with depression, we examined vital signs, anthropometric measurements, health status, demographic variables, and health behaviors. Multiple logistic regression analysis was performed to identify significant predictors after adjusting for covariates, and subgroup analyses were conducted according to sex. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors, all of which were < 5, indicating no concerns. Descriptive statistics (frequency, proportion, mean, standard deviation, and range) are provided in the Supplementary Table 2. Bivariate analyses were conducted to explore unadjusted associations between each variable and depression, and the results are presented in the Supplementary Table 3. For each model (pooled, female subgroup, and male subgroup models), we conducted a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to evaluate model performance. Additionally, to enhance clinical utility, optimal cut-off points for key continuous variables were estimated using the Youden Index in each model. Area under receiver operating characteristic (AUC) curves are presented in the main text, while detailed cut-off point estimations are provided in the Supplementary Data 1. All analyses were performed using Stata/MP version 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Ethics statement

Studies involving human participants were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Dankook University (DKUH 2025-05-003). All the patients provided written informed consent to participate in this study.

RESULTS

Determinant factors of depression

This section presents results of the multiple logistic regression analysis to identify the factors associated with depression among older adults living alone. Significant associations were observed across all domains including demographic characteristics, physical status, functional abilities, and health behaviors. Among demographic factors, age was inversely associated with depression, and older participants were less likely to experience depressive symptoms (odds ratio [OR], 0.967). Regarding anthropometric measures, a larger head circumference was positively associated with depression (OR, 1.147). Regarding physical function, hearing impairment (OR, 1.544) and gustatory dysfunction (OR, 1.551) increased the likelihood of depression, whereas better neurological gait performance was protective (OR, 0.885). Among health behaviors, a higher number of daily meals (OR, 0.559) and longer weekly exercise duration (OR, 0.991) were associated with reduced odds of depression (Table 1).

Subgroup analysis

Results of the sex-based subgroup analyses are shown in Tables 2 and 3, which present only statistically significant findings. In the female subgroup, multiple logistic regression results largely mirrored those of the entire sample (Table 2). Among women, older age was significantly associated with a lower risk of depression (OR, 0.963). Higher odds of depression were observed with a larger head circumference (OR, 1.182), visual impairment (OR, 1.574), olfactory dysfunction (OR, 1.471), and gustatory dysfunction (OR, 1.479). In contrast, better neurological gait scores (OR, 0.893), more meals per day (OR, 0.526), and longer weekly exercise duration (OR, 0.992) were associated with a lower depression risk.

In contrast to the female subgroup, male subgroup showed a distinct pattern of depression-related risk factors (Table 3). Notably, higher respiratory rates were strongly associated with an increased risk of depression among older men (OR, 12.437), a factor that was not significant in women. This may reflect sex-specific physiological responses or the underlying conditions associated with respiratory distress. Gustatory dysfunction also predicted depression in men (OR, 3.385), reinforcing the female group findings and suggesting a stronger effect. Additionally, better gait performance significantly reduced the risk of depression (OR, 0.735), which was consistent with the female subgroup results. These differences imply that although some factors (e.g., gait impairment) broadly affect mental health in older adults living alone, others, such as respiratory rate, may reflect male-specific pathways. This highlights the need for sex-sensitive strategies in managing late-life depression.

ROC curve analysis

The pooled logistic model demonstrated a fair discriminatory ability, with an AUC of 0.7408 (Fig. 1A). The optimal probability cut-off point, estimated based on the Youden Index, was 0.304 (Fig. 1B), yielding a sensitivity of 68% and specificity of 70%. The female subgroup model showed a comparable discriminatory performance, with an AUC of 0.7288 (Fig. 1C). The optimal cut-off point was 0.312 (Fig. 1D), corresponding to a sensitivity of 65% and specificity of 71%. In contrast, the male subgroup model demonstrated lower discriminatory ability, with an AUC of 0.6165 (Fig. 1E). The optimal cut-off point was estimated to be 0.537 (Fig. 1F), yielding a sensitivity of 60% and specificity of 65%. Additionally, further analyses were conducted to estimate the optimal cut-off values for key continuous factors (e.g., age and head circumference) using ROC curves. DISCUSSION

This study examined the relationship between vital signs, anthropometric measures, basic physical functions, health behaviors, and depression in older adults living alone. The target population was community-dwelling older adults living alone, a group known to be at an increased risk of social isolation and depression.9 The analysis identified several significant associations between measured health factors and depression.

One key finding was that older age was associated with a lower likelihood of depression in all the participants (Table 1).10 Although this may seem counterintuitive given the increase in health problems with age, it aligns with reports showing lower depression prevalence among oldest-old compared with younger older adults.11 One explanation is a survivor effect or greater resilience among those living independently into their late 70s or 80s.12 Those that were more vulnerable to depression may have developed symptoms earlier and moved to assisted living, leaving a relatively healthier group.11 Nonetheless, it should be noted that although severe depressive disorders may be less common among oldest-old, subthreshold depressive symptoms can still be prevalent and problematic in this age group.13

Anthropometric factors were also associated with mental health. A larger head circumference was associated with a higher risk of depression,14 which was unexpected. Further sensitivity analyses of the association between head circumference and depression are provided in the Supplementary Table 2. This contrasts with a study on brain reserve and dementia, in which a smaller head circumference (indicating lower brain volume) is linked to cognitive impairment and Alzheimer’s disease.15 Our finding may indicate that head circumference acts as a proxy for other factors. One possibility is that individuals with a larger body size or greater nutrition during development (hence, a larger head) might also have had different life conditions that affected their mental health.16

Another potential biomechanical explanation is that age-related muscle loss may cause older adults with larger head sizes to experience greater physical strain in supporting head weight, leading to fatigue or a tendency towards more sedentary behavior. This reduced activity may gradually affect mood and psychological well-being later in life. It could also be a spurious association or reflect unmeasured variables; for example, if males generally have larger heads and depressed males are slightly over-represented, that may drive this association. However, the head size effect remained significant, even after adjusting for sex, and this was specifically observed in women as well. However, although biomechanical explanations are plausible, there is still no well-established biological mechanism directly linking head circumference to mood disorders. This novel finding warrants further investigation.15

Sensory impairment has emerged as a significant correlate with depression. Visual impairment was notable, as difficulty in seeing can limit daily activities, hobbies, and independent navigation, leading to reduced confidence and greater dependence, which may contribute to depression. Olfactory and gustatory dysfunctions are also associated with depression. Gustatory dysfunction is significantly linked to a higher risk,17 while olfactory dysfunction showed an overall trend and was significant in women. Loss of smell and taste is common in older age and may reduce appetite, cause weight loss, and diminish the pleasure of eating,18 contributing to nutritional deficiencies associated with depression.19 Emerging evidence also suggests that such dysfunctions may be early markers of neurological changes or inflammation affecting mood regulation.19

From a biological standpoint, the olfactory system is closely linked with limbic brain regions involved in emotion, such as the amygdala and hippocampus. Therefore, it is plausible that degeneration in olfactory receptors or central processing may have emotional side effects.19 Gustatory dysfunction often co-exists with smell loss. The effect of taste loss on depression might also be mediated by poor nutrition and the social aspects of eating; older adults who cannot taste food well may derive less pleasure from meals and might start eating alone or skipping meals, which can exacerbate loneliness and depression.20 Indeed, clinical observations suggest that loss of taste and smell can contribute to depressive symptoms in older adults.21 Our findings highlight the need to pay attention to sensory health, beyond vision and hearing, and the “hidden” senses of smell and taste are also important for predicting depression among older adults.

A decline in neurological gait score was another factor strongly associated with depression. We found that a better gait score (indicating a more stable and normal gait) protected against depression. Difficulties in walking can limit an older person’s independence and ability to participate in social or pleasurable activities (such as going outdoors, visiting friends, or exercising), which can lead to a vicious cycle of further physical decline and worsening mood.22 Potential biological mechanisms include underlying cerebrovascular disease or white matter changes in the brain that can present with both gait disorders and mood depression as dual symptoms of an overarching aging process or pathology. Regardless of cause, maintaining mobility and providing assistive devices for those with gait impairment may have an added benefit of protecting mental health.23

Health behaviors were also significantly associated with depression in this study. Skipping meals or poor nutrition can directly worsen energy levels and brain function, and over time malnutrition may predispose to depression.24 It is also likely that depression itself causes loss of appetite and irregular eating; therefore, this association could be bidirectional.25 However, even after adjusting olfactory and gustatory dysfunction, a higher meal frequency was a protective factor, suggesting that ensuring older adults receive adequate nutrition through regular meals could support better mental health.24 There is a psychosocial element as well: eating regular meals is often tied to having a routine and, potentially, eating in the company of others.25 Older adults living alone who lack motivation to cook or who eat alone might skip meals or just snack, which can both reflect and aggravate depressive states.25 This finding aligns with a study that showed that eating alone is associated with depression in older adults, whereas communal meals and good nutrition are protective.26

Finally, physical activity levels were inversely related to depression. Although many of our participants were older adults, those who managed to perform more minutes of exercise per day experienced a significantly lower risk of depression. This concurs with a large body of evidence that regular exercise has antidepressant effects in older adults.27 Exercise affects multiple biological pathways beneficial to mood, including the release of endorphins and neurotrophic factors, reduction of inflammatory markers, and improvement of circadian rhythms and sleep quality.27 A general review of numerous trials and cohort studies concluded that exercise interventions consistently reduce depressive symptoms in older adults across various settings.28

The subgroup analysis of women, which comprised most of the participants (Table 2), showed results largely consistent with the overall findings. Patients with visual impairments, as well as those with olfactory or gustatory dysfunction, were more likely to experience depression. These associations may be stronger in women owing to social and biological factors. As women tend to live longer than men, they are more prone to sensory decline and may be more emotionally affected. Older women often engage in social and household activities, which can be disrupted when sensory abilities decline.29 For example, impaired vision may interfere with reading, sewing, or recognizing loved ones, which can be demoralizing.30 Although such impairments are associated with depression in both sexes, some studies suggest that women report greater emotional distress related to vision loss.30 Biologically, the relationship between olfactory dysfunction and depression may be affected by hormonal or genetic differences, although studies in this area are still emerging.19 One study also noted that the social effect of olfactory loss was particularly pronounced in older women.21

Additionally, other significant predictors in women, including—age, head circumference, gait, nutrition, and exercise were similar to those in the overall population. Notably, hearing impairment, although significant in the combined analysis, was not a significant independent predictor among women in the adjusted subgroup analysis. This could imply that when other variables are accounted for, hearing loss might be somewhat less explanatory in depression among women compared with the strong role of vision and taste/smell. This might also reflect that hearing impairment was so common in this cohort (57.73% had some hearing loss) that it did not differentiate between depressed and non-depressed women as clearly as other senses. Head circumference remained significant in women, indicating that this association was not merely due to head sizes in men, suggesting an intrinsic link that is still unclear.15

The subgroup analysis of older men (Table 3) revealed some distinct patterns, despite the smaller sample size, reflecting the demographic reality that fewer men live alone until old age. The most notable factor was tachypnea, which was identified through respiratory rate. A high respiratory rate in older adults often indicates underlying chronic health issues such as reduced lung function, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), heart failure, or anxiety-related hyperventilation.31 Chronic lung diseases, such as COPD, are well known to be closely associated with depression, which is the most common psychiatric comorbidity in these patients.32 This link may be driven by persistent physical discomfort, activity limitations, and systemic effects of chronic hypoxia or inflammation in the brain.32 Given that older men are more likely to have a history of smoking or COPD, this association was not unexpected. These findings suggest that for older men living alone, monitoring respiratory rate may help identify individuals at risk not only for physical illness but also for depression.33

In this study, older males also showed a strong association between gustatory dysfunction and depression, which was even stronger than that observed in females. One possible explanation is that for males who live alone, losing the sense of taste might drastically reduce their motivation to prepare proper meals (since they might already be less inclined to cook for themselves than females). This can lead to nutritional deficiencies and weight loss, which can directly worsen mood.24 Additionally, there might be a common underlying neurodegenerative process in some males; for example, Parkinson’s disease can present with loss of smell and taste and also has a high incidence of depression.34

Based on these findings, it is possible to suggest improvements in public health and clinical practice to manage late-life depression. Notably, our results support the idea of revising national health screening protocols for older adults to incorporate the types of measurements used in this study.31 Current geriatric health screenings often focus on chronic disease markers (blood pressure, glucose, cholesterol, etc.) and basic questions regarding mood or cognitive function. However, adding simple assessments of sensory function (hearing, vision, smell, and taste),19,30 mobility (gait tests),22 and lifestyle factors (nutrition and exercise habits)23,27 could significantly enhance the ability to identify older adults at risk of depression. For example, a “vital sign” of high respiratory rate31 or a finding of significant sensory loss during a check-up should prompt consideration of a patient’s mental well-being.35 ROC analysis further informed clinical applicability. The pooled model achieved fair discrimination, the female model showed comparable performance, and the male model had lower accuracy. In practice, the pooled model may serve as an initial screening tool, with a sex-specific model applied in secondary assessments to enhance detection efficiency.

The results of the present study advocate for the establishment of a more proactive referral system. If an older adult’s routine health check uncovers problems, such as impaired gait,36 malnutrition (low BMI or meal frequency),24,37 or sensory deficits,17,38 healthcare providers could automatically arrange a follow-up screening for depression or even a referral to a geriatric psychiatrist or counselor. Vital signs and physical function tests can be used as gateways for mental health evaluations. This approach helps overcome some barriers to mental health care by leveraging the regular health check-ups that older adults are already undergoing. Given the stigma and under-reporting of depression among older adults, especially those without a history of psychiatric illness,39 such a system, could identify vulnerable older individuals.

In South Korea, where this study was conducted, there is a structured national health examination for seniors. Integrating our findings would create a DEPACS package within that examination. If certain thresholds in the package are met (e.g., notable gait impairment or multiple sensory losses), that individual will be flagged for a comprehensive mental health assessment. This could facilitate early identification of and intervention for depression in the growing population of older adults living alone.

It is also important to note that individual predictors may have insufficient explanatory power when considered separately. Our findings suggest that the combined interpretation of multiple physical indicators, such as sensory decline, gait impairment, and head circumference, may offer a clinically meaningful understanding of the risk of depression in older adults. For example, although age was not significant in the univariate analyses (Supplementary Table 3), it became important within the multivariate context (Table 1), possibly reflecting confounding effects. This reinforces the rationale for including theoretically important variables, even if they do not reach statistical significance in univariate models, especially in the context of building a comprehensive screening tool such as DEPACS.

There are several limitations to consider when interpreting our findings. First, because this was a cross-sectional study, all the measurements and depression assessments were conducted at a single time point. Consequently, causality could not be inferred, and the directionality of these associations remains unclear. For example, although poor physical health may contribute to depression, it is also possible that depression leads to adverse health behaviors or worsened physical performance. Longitudinal studies are required to clarify these temporal relationships.

Furthermore, there are some limitations related to measurement accuracy and scope. Self-reported variables, such as exercise and meal frequency, are subject to recall or social desirability biases. Although clinical measures (e.g., head circumference and gait assessment) were conducted using standard tools, measurement errors and inter-rater variability remained possible. Depression was treated as a binary outcome; more nuanced insights may have emerged, and symptom severity was examined on a continuum. Nonetheless, data collection at a single institution using standardized protocols likely enhances consistency, although this may limit generalizability to other settings.

This study examined the associations between vital signs, anthropometric measures, physical function, health behaviors, and depression in older adults living alone. Several easily measurable physiological and lifestyle factors, such as respiratory rate, head circumference, sensory impairments, gait performance, meal frequency, and physical activity, have been significantly linked to depression risk, with some sex-based variations. These findings support the revision of national health screening protocols to include physical function and health behavior assessments alongside traditional indicators for earlier detection of at-risk individuals. Establishing referral pathways from general screening to psychiatric evaluations based on these markers may enhance access to mental healthcare. Despite the cross-sectional design and potential measurement limitations, the use of standardized protocols within a single institution strengthens internal validity. Future studies should aim to validate the DEPACS package and explore longitudinal relationships between physical indicators and depression later in life.
