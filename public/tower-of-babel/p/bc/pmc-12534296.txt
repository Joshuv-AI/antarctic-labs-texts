Changes in ferroptosis-related molecular expression in macrophages of community-acquired pneumonia patients and their associated with the disease progression
Authors: Huan-Shao Huang, Jia-Xin Chi, Le-Yao Xiao, Jia-Jun Wang, Shi-Ying Lai, Lan Chen, Jiang Pi, Yan-Guang Cong, Yi-Ming Shao, Jun-Fa Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the expression of ferroptosis-related molecules in macrophages of community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) patients and explore their association with disease severity. This study provides novel insights into the role of ferroptosis in CAP pathogenesis by integrating transcriptome sequencing, RT-qPCR, Western blot, and flow cytometry analyses.MethodsFerroptosis-related molecules were analyzed in CD14⁺ monocytes and CD11b⁺ macrophages from CAP patients using transcriptome sequencing, RT-qPCR, Western blot, and flow cytometry. Clinical data from CAP patients (n = 46) and healthy controls (n = 63) were compared.ResultsCAP patients exhibited elevated levels of ferroptosis markers (PTGS2, Fe²⁺, and lipid peroxides) and downregulation of inhibitors (TP53 and GPX4). Transcriptome analysis showed activation of the ferroptosis pathway, with significant changes correlating with disease severity. Elevated neutrophil counts and decreased lymphocyte levels were also observed in CAP patients.ConclusionFerroptosis is intricately involved in CAP pathogenesis, with altered expression of key molecules contributing to disease progression. Our findings highlight the potential of targeting ferroptosis-related molecules (e.g., TP53, GPX4, and PTGS2) as a novel therapeutic strategy to mitigate inflammation and tissue damage in CAP. Future studies should validate these findings in larger cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00011-025-02116-2.

Introduction

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) remains a substantial infectious disease burden worldwide, causing an alarming number of over 3.5 million deaths annually [1, 2]. Elderly individuals are disproportionately affected by this illness, underlining the gravity of the public health challenge [3, 4]. The complexity of CAP arises from its diverse pathogen spectrum, including bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites, which pose diagnostic and therapeutic difficulties [5, 6]. This highlights the urgency of understanding its pathogenesis and developing effective therapeutic strategies. Despite advances in antimicrobial therapy, the complexity of CAP, arising from a diverse range of pathogens, continues to pose diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.

Understanding and addressing CAP requires a deep investigation into its pathogenesis. Macrophages, which are key effector cells of the innate immune system, play a critical role in managing microbial infections [7–9]. In particular, CD14+ monocytes derived from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and CD11b+ macrophages isolated from bronchoalveolar lavage mononuclear cells (BLMCs) are essential components of the immune response to respiratory pathogens [10–12]. These cells not only recognize and phagocytose foreign particles but also present antigens and secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby orchestrating the body’s defense mechanisms. Additionally, macrophages regulate immune responses through various cell death mechanisms such as apoptosis, autophagy, necroptosis, pyroptosis, and, more recently, ferroptosis [13].

Ferroptosis, a unique form of iron-dependent, lipid peroxidation-mediated cell death, has attracted significant attention in recent years, particularly within the context of inflammatory diseases [14]. Notably, ferroptosis induced by bacterial or viral infections has been closely linked to the occurrence and development of inflammation. For instance, Staphylococcus aureus infection in mouse lungs induces severe oxidative stress, leading to lipid peroxidation and reduced GSH levels [15], while host cell arachidonic acid and lipid peroxidation products can counteract and kill the bacteria [16]. Pseudomonas aeruginosa exacerbates disease severity by causing cell ferroptosis; it expresses lipoxygenase to oxidize arachidonic acid phosphatidylethanolamine in human bronchial epithelial cells [17]. Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection of macrophages induces ferroptosis by reducing the activity of GSH and GPX4, while increasing levels of ferrous ions, mitochondrial superoxides, and lipid peroxides, thereby evading the immune killing effect of cells [18, 19]. Similarly, SARS-CoV-2 infection may trigger ferroptosis by downregulating GPX4 expression, inhibiting the NRF2 antioxidant pathway, and elevating serum ferritin levels, which could potentially lead to lung damage in COVID-19 patients [20]. Emerging evidence suggests that ferroptosis may be intricately involved in the pathological process of CAP [21]. Alterations in the expression of certain ferroptosis-related molecules may influence the occurrence and progression of CAP.

Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), a crucial negative regulator of ferroptosis, maintains cellular homeostasis by inhibiting lipid peroxidation [22, 23]. On the other hand, prostaglandin-endoperoxide synthase 2 (PTGS2), whose upregulation is associated with inflammatory responses and oxidative stress, has been linked to the promotion of ferroptosis [24, 25]. TP53, also known as p53, is a tumor suppressor gene that plays a central role in maintaining genomic stability and preventing cancer development [26]. Beyond its well-established role in cancer biology, TP53 has recently been implicated in the regulation of ferroptosis. Specifically, TP53 can transcriptionally regulate genes involved in glutathione metabolism, including GPX4, which acts as a key inhibitor of ferroptosis [27–29]. By modulating GPX4 expression, TP53 influences the cell's susceptibility to ferroptosis, thereby regulating cell survival and death pathways.

Despite growing interest in ferroptosis, the expression of ferroptosis-related molecules in macrophages of CAP patients and their correlation with disease severity remain poorly understood. This study aims to address this gap by systematically analyzing the expression of ferroptosis-related molecules in PBMCs and BLMCs from patients with mild and severe CAP. Using a comprehensive approach that includes transcriptome sequencing, RT-qPCR, Western blot, and flow cytometry, we investigate the role of ferroptosis in macrophage function and its potential as a therapeutic target for CAP. Our findings highlight the potential of targeting ferroptosis-related molecules to mitigate inflammation and tissue damage in CAP, providing a new therapeutic direction for this complex disease. Our study is the first to utilize RNA sequencing to analyze the activation of the ferroptosis pathway in CAP patients, providing novel insights into the pathophysiology of the disease and a foundation for developing targeted therapeutic strategies.

Material and methods

Study subjects and grouping

According to the diagnostic criteria established by the Respiratory Disease Branch of the Chinese Medical Association in 2016, CAP inpatients (≥ 18 years) from Dongguan First Hospital Affiliated to Guangdong Medical University were selected as the experimental group. Patients were categorized into mild and severe groups based on the CURB-65 scoring system (Confusion, Urea > 7 mmol/L, Respiratory rate ≥ 30 breaths/min, Blood pressure systolic < 90 mmHg or diastolic ≤ 60 mmHg, Age ≥ 65 years old) [30–32]. Each item of CURB-65 is 1 point, and the total score ≤ 2 is the mild CAP group, and > 2 is the severe CAP group. Age-matched (≥ 18 years) healthy individuals undergoing physical examination at Dongguan First Hospital Affiliated to Guangdong Medical University served as the normal control group. Demographic and clinical characteristics of participants, including age, gender, and comorbidities, were recorded.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The collection and use of peripheral blood and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Medical University with the approval number YJYS202304005. All donors provided written informed consent.

Sample collection and processing

Peripheral blood and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) samples were collected from CAP patients, while only peripheral blood samples were taken from healthy individuals undergoing physical examinations. PBMCs from the blood samples and BLMCs from the BALF samples were isolated using the Ficoll density gradient centrifugation method. Subsequently, CD14+ monocytes and CD11b+ macrophages were sorted using immunomagnetic beads according to the instructions provided by the EasySep™ Human CD14 Positive Selection Kit II and EasySep™ Human CD11b Positive Selection and Depletion Kit, respectively, for further analysis and detection. In brief, 100uL of Selection Cocktail was added to the cell mixture and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. Following this, the mixture was vortexed for 30 s, and then 100 uL of RapidSpheres™ was added and incubated at room temperature for an additional 3 min. Subsequently, 2.5 mL of EasySep™ Buffer was added, and the mixture was gently pipetted up and down 2–3 times. The tube (without a lid) was then placed in a magnet and incubated at room temperature for 3 min, followed by the removal of the supernatant. This step was repeated three times. Finally, the cells were resuspended in culture medium for future use.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Reverse transcription was performed with the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was then performed using the SYBR PrimeScript RT-PCR Kit (Takara) to analyze gene expression. The 2−△△CT method was employed for quantification, with GAPDH used as an internal control. Western blot

CD14+ cells from PBMCs of healthy individuals and CAP patients, as well as CD11b+ cells from BLMCs of CAP patients, were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer. Centrifuge the protein extracts at 4 °C and 12,000 rpm for 10 min, then collect the supernatant and use a protein assay kit to determine the protein concentration. Load equivalent amounts of protein onto an SDS–polyacrylamide (SDS-PAGE) gel for separation and transfer the proteins to a PVDF membrane. Block the membrane with 5% skimmed milk at room temperature for 60 min. Add diluted primary antibodies for Cox2 and GPX4 (1:1000) to the membrane and incubate overnight at 4 °C. Wash the membrane three times with TBST. Incubate the membrane with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse/rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:5000) at room temperature for 1 h. Use a chemiluminescent solution to visualize the protein bands and analyze the protein levels quantitatively using ImageJ software.

Intracellular Fe2+ measurement

Intracellular Fe2+ levels were determined using the FerroOrange reagent (Dojindo), a novel fluorescent probe that enables fluorescent imaging of Fe2+ in living cells. FerroOrange specifically detects unstable iron (II) ions (Fe2+), localizing in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). Upon reaction with Fe2+, it irreversibly generates a fluorescent product (Ex = 543nm, Em = 580nm). After isolating PBMCs and BLMCs using Ficoll separation medium, BLMCs were incubated with anti-human CD11b+ antibody. Following two washes with 1xPBS, a serum-free medium containing FerroOrange (1 µmoL/L) was added to the cells, which were then cultured in a 37°C, 5% CO2 incubator for 30 min before detection on a flow cytometer.

Intracellular lipid peroxide measurement

After isolating PBMCs and BLMCs using Ficoll separation medium, BLMCs were incubated with anti-human CD11b+ antibody, followed by two washes with 1xPBS. BDP 581/591 C11 working solution was added, and the cells were cultured in a 37°C, 5% CO2 incubator for 30 min. After removing the working solution and washing twice with HBSS, HBSS was added again, and fluorescence was detected using a flow cytometer.

Transcriptome sequencing and PPI network analysis

Total RNA was extracted from samples using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen), and strand-specific libraries were constructed. The libraries were quantified using Qubit, and their quality and size were assessed using the Agilent 2100. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq x-ten platform at Shanghai Bohao. Raw data were filtered to obtain clean data by removing adapter sequences, primers, and low-quality data. HISAT2 [33–35] was used to align the clean data to the GRCh38 genome, and gene expression levels were estimated by counting reads using Stringtie to calculate FPKM [36–38] values for each gene. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using edgeR [39], controlling the FDR [40, 41] (False Discovery Rate) to determine the p-value threshold. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were selected based on an FDR ≤ 0.05 and a fold-change ≥ 2. Utilize Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) to assess the activation status of the ferroptosis pathway, and present the expression levels of ferroptosis-related molecules in the form of violin plots. Subsequently, to further investigate the interactions among the differentially expressed genes, a Protein–Protein Interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) and visualized with Cytoscape software.

Due to the low yield of CD11b⁺ cells obtained from the BLMCs of individual CAP patients, cells from three patients within the same CURB-65 severity stratum (either mild or severe) were pooled to ensure sufficient material for transcriptome sequencing. Sequencing was performed by a commercial service provider. No further stratification by pathogen or comorbidity was applied, as such grouping may obscure heterogeneity related to specific pathogens or comorbidities; the implications of this pooling strategy are addressed in the Discussion. For the transcriptome sequencing samples of CD14+ cells in PBMCs, we employed a random selection approach. Specifically, we randomly chose CAP patients and healthy individuals to ensure a diverse and representative sample set for transcriptome sequencing. This optimized approach ensures that we make the most of the available cells for sequencing while maintaining a rigorous and representative sampling strategy.

Statistical and bioinformatics data analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 27.0 and Graphpad prism 9 software. For transcriptome sequencing data, raw reads from each library were normalized to FPKM. For normally distributed quantitative data, the Student's t-test was employed, with results are reported as mean ± standard deviation (x̅ ± s). Conversely, for non-normally distributed data, non-parametric tests were utilized, and the outcomes are presented as median (Q1, Q3), where Q1 represents the 25th percentile and Q3 represents the 75th percentile. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Transcriptome sequencing analysis of ferroptosis-related molecular expression in CD14+ cells from PBMCs of CAP patients

We successfully isolated CD14+ cells from PBMCs of CAP patients and healthy controls using immunomagnetic beads (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Transcriptome sequencing was performed on the isolated CD14+ cells from PBMCs of CAP patients (n = 8) and healthy controls(n = 9). To explore the potential mechanisms of ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of CAP, we conducted Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) on the ferroptosis pathway. The results indicated that the ferroptosis signaling pathway was activated in CD14+ cells of PBMCs from CAP patients (Fig. 1A). Further, we analyzed the expression levels of ferroptosis-related molecules, presented in the form of violin plots (Figs. 1B-G and Supplementary Fig. 2). Among them, the expression level of the ferroptosis markers or drivers PTGS2, SLC3A5, CD82 and SMAD7 showed an upward trend (Figs. 1B-E), while the expression levels of ferroptosis inhibitors TP53 and GPX4 were significantly down-regulated (Figs. 1F-G). Expression correlation analysis revealed that PTGS2 expression was negatively correlated with TP53(Fig. 1I), while TP53 and GPX4 expression were positively correlated (Fig. 1H). With the criteria of |logFC|≥ 2 and P < 0.05, we identified 662 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 414 upregulated and 248 downregulated genes. These DEGs were imported into the STRING database, resulting in a PPI network map with 505 nodes and 524 edges (Fig. 1J). In the PPI network, p53 (encoded by TP53) played a central role, interacting extensively with many proteins in various ways. These interactions spanned multiple biological processes and signaling pathways, highlighting the broad influence and core function of p53 in cells. By analyzing the number of interactions, types, and network centrality, the central position of TP53 in the PPI network was clearly demonstrated. Additionally, raw count data were obtained from the publicly available GEO datasets GSE160329 (Fig. 1K) and GSE196399 (Fig. 1L). Both studies enrolled adults (≥ 18 years) with community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), as confirmed by clinical and radiographic criteria; healthy controls were frequency-matched to CAP cases by age (± 5 years) and sex. Differential gene expression analysis using volcano plots revealed significant downregulation of TP53 and GPX4 mRNA expression, alongside significant upregulation of PTGS2. Discussion

The present study offers several novel insights into the role of ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of CAP. First, we systematically analyzed the expression of ferroptosis-related molecules in monocytes/macrophages from both peripheral blood and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid of CAP patients, revealing distinct expression profiles between mild and severe cases. This is the first study to demonstrate the activation of the ferroptosis pathway in CAP patients using transcriptome sequencing, highlighting the potential contribution of ferroptosis to disease progression. Second, our findings underscore the differential expression of key ferroptosis regulators (e.g., TP53, and GPX4) and markers (e.g., PTGS2, Fe2⁺ and lipid peroxides) in CAP patients, suggesting that targeting these molecules may offer therapeutic benefits. These results not only fill the gap in understanding the role of ferroptosis in CAP but also provide a foundation for future research and therapeutic development.

Our clinical data analysis revealed distinct immune cell distribution abnormalities in CAP patients, characterized by elevated neutrophil and monocyte counts and a marked reduction in lymphocyte counts. These findings indicate heightened inflammatory responses in CAP patients, aligning with previous studies that associate neutrophilia and monocytosis with severe respiratory infections [42]. Additionally, elevated levels of AST and ALT suggest liver function impairment, likely due to systemic inflammatory stress responses associated with CAP [43]. Furthermore, the differential distribution of pathogens between mild and severe CAP patients, particularly the prevalence of Mycoplasma pneumoniae in mild cases, highlights the complexity of CAP and suggests that pathogen burden may influence disease severity and immune response dynamics.

Ferroptosis, characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, has emerged as a significant mechanism underlying inflammatory diseases, including CAP. Our results demonstrate that CAP patients exhibit significantly elevated levels of ferroptosis markers, such as Fe2⁺ and lipid peroxides, in both monocytes and macrophages. This finding is consistent with previous studies showing that infections with pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus and Mycobacterium tuberculosis can induce ferroptosis through oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation [15, 18]. The increased ferroptosis activity observed in CAP patients suggests that this cell death mechanism may contribute to the inflammatory pathology and tissue damage associated with the disease.

We also explored the potential relationship between ferroptosis and disease progression. Severe CAP patients exhibited higher levels of ferroptosis markers and more pronounced downregulation of TP53 and GPX4 compared to mild cases. This suggests that ferroptosis may play a more significant role in severe disease outcomes and could serve as a indicator of disease progression. However, further studies with larger sample sizes and longitudinal follow-up are needed to establish a definitive correlation between ferroptosis and disease progression.

Regarding antibiotic treatment, it is plausible that antibiotics may influence ferroptosis and disease progression in CAP. Antibiotics primarily target pathogens, reducing the infectious burden and subsequent inflammatory response. By alleviating the infection-induced oxidative stress, antibiotics may indirectly modulate ferroptosis. For instance, reducing the pathogen load could decrease the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [44], which are known to promote ferroptosis through lipid peroxidation. Additionally, some antibiotics possess antioxidant properties that may directly inhibit ferroptosis [45]. However, the specific mechanisms by which antibiotic treatment affects ferroptosis and its subsequent impact on disease progression remain to be elucidated. Future studies should investigate the interplay between antibiotic therapy, ferroptosis, and clinical outcomes in CAP patients to better understand the therapeutic implications.

The altered expression of key ferroptosis-related molecules, TP53, GPX4, and PTGS2, further supports the involvement of ferroptosis in CAP. TP53, a tumor suppressor gene, plays a crucial role in regulating cellular responses to stress and has recently been implicated in ferroptosis regulation [46]. Our study shows that TP53 expression is significantly downregulated in both CD14⁺monocytes and CD11b⁺macrophages from CAP patients. This downregulation may disrupt the normal inhibitory functions of TP53 on ferroptosis, thereby enhancing cellular susceptibility to this cell death pathway. Additionally, GPX4, a well-established negative regulator of ferroptosis [23], is also downregulated in CAP patients. As GPX4 is essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis by inhibiting lipid peroxidation, its reduced expression likely exacerbates ferroptosis and contributes to the inflammatory response. Conversely, PTGS2, which is associated with inflammation and oxidative stress [47], is upregulated in CAP patients. This upregulation may further promote ferroptosis and contribute to disease severity. The correlation analysis between these molecules further highlights their interconnected roles in modulating ferroptosis pathways.

Although our transcriptome sequencing data of CD11b⁺ macrophages showed a consistent trend, the differences in mRNA expression of key ferroptosis regulators (e.g., GPX4, PTGS2) between mild and severe CAP patients did not reach statistical significance. This discrepancy between mRNA and protein/functional outcomes may be attributed to post-transcriptional regulation, protein stability, or the limited sample size available for sequencing. To investigate alternative explanations for the observed Fe2⁺ accumulation, such as broader metabolic compromise or other cell death pathways, we performed additional GSEA on our RNA-seq data. Notably, pathways for apoptosis, pyroptosis, necroptosis, and core metabolic processes like oxidative phosphorylation and glycolysis were not significantly enriched (Supplementary Fig. 5). This analysis reinforces that ferroptosis, among other pathways, is specifically relevant in the context of severe CAP.

The differential expression of ferroptosis-related molecules and the activation of ferroptosis pathways in severe CAP cases compared to mild cases underscore the potential contribution of ferroptosis to disease progression. Severe CAP patients exhibit higher levels of ferroptosis markers and more pronounced downregulation of TP53 and GPX4, suggesting that ferroptosis may play a more significant role in severe disease outcomes. This finding is supported by our transcriptome sequencing analysis, which shows that the ferroptosis signaling pathway is more active in severe CAP patients. The involvement of ferroptosis in severe CAP may be related to the complex pathogen spectrum and the associated inflammatory responses, as severe cases often involve more complicated infections and higher levels of systemic inflammation.

An intriguing finding of our study is that ferroptosis-related alterations, including elevated Fe2⁺, lipid peroxides, and downregulation of GPX4, were already evident in CD14⁺ from CAP patients compared to healthy controls. This suggests that systemic inflammatory signals may induce a pre-ferroptotic state in monocytes prior to their recruitment into the lungs. These “pre-activated” cells might then be more susceptible to further ferroptotic stimuli within the infected pulmonary microenvironment, thereby exacerbating tissue damage and contributing to disease progression. This systemic dimension of ferroptosis highlights its potential role not just as a local event but as a disseminated pathological process in CAP.

Our study also highlights the potential therapeutic implications of targeting ferroptosis-related molecules in CAP. Modulating the expression or activity of TP53, GPX4, and PTGS2 may offer new strategies for mitigating ferroptosis and reducing inflammation in CAP patients. For instance, restoring TP53 function or enhancing GPX4 activity could inhibit ferroptosis and protect against tissue damage. Conversely, targeting PTGS2 to reduce its pro-ferroptotic effects may also provide therapeutic benefits. Future studies should focus on exploring the mechanisms underlying the dysregulation of these molecules and developing targeted interventions to modulate ferroptosis in CAP.

While this study has made significant progress in elucidating the link between macrophage ferroptosis and CAP pathogenesis, there are still many aspects worthy of further exploration. The sample size, especially for severe CAP patients, was relatively small, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. Future studies should include larger cohorts to validate these results and explore the variability among different populations. Moreover, while our study focused on macrophages, other immune cell types such as neutrophils and dendritic cells also play critical roles in CAP and may interact with ferroptotic pathways. Investigating these interactions could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the immune landscape in CAP. Additionally, the mechanistic pathways by which TP53, GPX4, and PTGS2 regulate ferroptosis in macrophages require further elucidation. Utilizing gene knockout or overexpression models in vitro and in vivo could help delineate the precise roles of these genes and their downstream effectors in ferroptosis and CAP pathogenesis. Combining these molecular studies with clinical interventions in animal models and eventually in human trials will be essential to translate these findings into therapeutic applications.

We explicitly acknowledge that pooling CD11b+ cells without stratification by pathogen or comorbidity may have obscured pathogen-specific inflammatory signatures or comorbidity-driven metabolic reprogramming. For example, Mycoplasma pneumoniae (dominant in MCAP) and polymicrobial sepsis (more frequent in SCAP) elicit distinct macrophage polarisation patterns and iron-handling pathways. Future RNA-seq studies with larger, stratified cohorts are warranted to dissect whether ferroptosis signatures are universal or restricted to specific pathogen/comorbidity combinations. Meanwhile, our current design provides a proof-of-concept that the ferroptosis axis is globally activated in severe CAP, but should not be interpreted as pathogen-specific.

In summary, our study demonstrates that macrophage ferroptosis is intricately involved in the pathogenesis of CAP, with TP53, GPX4, and PTGS2 playing critical roles in modulating ferroptosis pathways. The findings highlight the potential of targeting ferroptosis-related molecules as a novel therapeutic strategy for CAP. Further research is needed to elucidate the detailed mechanisms and validate these molecules as effective therapeutic targets.

Conclusion

This study provides novel insights into the crucial role of ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of CAP by systematically analyzing the expression of ferroptosis-related molecules in macrophages. Our findings reveal distinct molecular profiles between mild and severe CAP patients, highlighting the potential contribution of ferroptosis to disease progression. Specifically, the activation of the ferroptosis pathway, as demonstrated by transcriptome sequencing and validated by experimental assays, underscores the importance of targeting ferroptosis-related molecules such as TP53, GPX4, and PTGS2 to mitigate inflammation and tissue damage. Elevated levels of ferroptosis markers, including Fe2+ and lipid peroxides, confirm the involvement of this mechanism in CAP’s inflammatory pathology. These results offer a new perspective on the pathophysiology of CAP and provide a foundation for developing targeted therapeutic strategies to reduce ferroptosis, inflammation, and improve clinical outcomes. Future research should focus on elucidating the underlying mechanisms of dysregulation and validating the therapeutic potential of these molecules in larger cohorts. This study advances our understanding of CAP’s molecular drivers and offers new hope for the management and treatment of this complex disease.
