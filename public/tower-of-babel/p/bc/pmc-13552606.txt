Improving Medication Safety Following Neonatal Discharge: A Feasibility Study of a Parent Medication Education Intervention in Ireland
Authors: S Giva, B Cleary, F Gaffney, L Bracken, A Gill, C Sutton, J Clark, V Gray, N McCallion, F O'Brien
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimNeonates discharged home on medications remain at risk of medication errors. The PADDINGToN programme previously developed parent co‐designed resources to support safer medication administration following discharge. The aim of this study, PADDINGToN‐2, is to evaluate the feasibility of recruiting and retaining parents in a study assessing these resources in order to support safer medication administration at home.MethodsProspective single‐centre feasibility study in a level 3 neonatal unit between March and December 2025. Parents of infants discharged on ≥ 2 medications were recruited before discharge, and followed for 12 weeks. Primary outcome was recruitment and retention using predefined progression criteria. Secondary outcomes included assessment of parental anxiety and confidence in medication administration.ResultsIn all, 35 parents were recruited. Retention was 42.8%, meeting predefined ‘Amber’ progression criteria indicating feasibility with protocol modification. Anxiety scores decreased from a median of 9 at baseline to 5 at follow‐up.ConclusionsRecruitment was feasible, and retention met predefined criteria, supporting progression to a larger multi‐centre implementation study with optimised retention strategies.

Aim

Parents of newborns admitted to a neonatal unit (NNU) report high levels of stress and anxiety worsened by the inability to provide routine infant care, emotional and physical barriers to early bonding and parent–infant interaction [1]. Parents report insufficient understanding of their infant's condition, possible complications and outcomes. These experiences may be associated with feelings of fear, guilt and helplessness, with negative consequences for parental mental health [2].

This is particularly prevalent among parents of preterm infants. Care models that prioritise parental empowerment and involvement, supporting parents as active NNU partners in their infant's care through structured education and engagement [1, 2, 3, 4] impact positively on parental mental health, self‐confidence and self‐efficacy, enhancing preparedness for infant discharge [4, 5, 6].

Preterm birth is a significant global public health issue, with 13.4 million babies (1 in 10) born prematurely each year [7]. In the Republic of Ireland, an estimated 4000 infants are born before 37 weeks' gestation annually [8]. Welcome improvements in survival rates among extremely preterm infants means more families are facing the challenges of transitioning from highly supported NNU care to independent but complex caregiving at home [3].

The period immediately preceding discharge from the NNU is particularly stressful for parents, who frequently report feeling overwhelmed, often describing a sense of being unprepared to assume full responsibility for their infant's care, particularly with minimal prior education or hands‐on experience [3]. Structured parental education incorporated into the discharge pathway has been shown to improve parental knowledge and understanding, enhancing both confidence and self‐efficacy at the point of discharge [3]. However, the content and focus of this discharge education varies widely.

Most existing discharge education programmes focus primarily on core newborn care such as feeding, breathing and sleeping, with medication‐related information typically embedded within broader materials rather than addressed as a distinct and complex domain [3, 9, 10]. To our knowledge, no prior neonatal discharge studies have evaluated a medication‐specific parent education intervention designed specifically to support medication administration following discharge home from the NNU.

Medication errors (MEs), defined as any preventable event that may lead to inappropriate medication use or patient harm, represent a well‐recognised patient safety concern [11, 12, 13]. While the incidence, types and contributing factors associated with MEs have been extensively studied within neonatal inpatient settings [14], comparatively little attention has been given to MEs occurring in the home environment following discharge from the NNU. MEs in this setting are common [13, 14, 15, 16] with rates as high as 66% among infants following discharge from the NNU, with the majority related to dosing inaccuracies [13]. These findings highlight the vulnerability of neonates during the transition from hospital to home and the need to support parents in safe medication administration. While educational interventions can improve medication practices, parents continue to report unmet needs in structured medication education on discharge from the NNU [12]. Parents often report feeling underprepared, lacking confidence, and experience to administer medications to their infants after discharge from the NNU [17].

The PADDINGToN (Parent co‐Designed Drug INformation for parents and Guardians Taking Neonates home) programme emerged as a collaborative research initiative between Alder Hey Children's Hospital in the United Kingdom and the Royal College of Surgeons in Ireland. The first phase of the programme, PADDINGToN‐1, worked with parents and healthcare professionals, to co‐design educational resources, aimed at enhancing parental knowledge and confidence for safe medication administration following neonatal discharge [17, 18].

The primary outcome of Irish PADDINGToN‐2, a pilot single site feasibility study, was parent recruitment and retention, with findings informing a future, larger multi‐centre trial.

Parental involvement was key to ensure that materials and documents used during the feasibility study remained accessible and outcomes remained relevant to parental needs [19]. An Irish PADDINGToN Parent Advisory Group (PPAG), parents with lived experience of neonatal care, supported study design, wording and delivery but did not act as study participants.

Methodology

Study Design and Setting

The Irish PADDINGToN‐2 was a prospective, single‐centre feasibility study designed to assess the practicality, acceptability, and methodological robustness of implementing parent co‐designed medication information resources (intervention) in a clinical setting for families of neonates being discharged home on medications. The study was conducted at the Rotunda Hospital, Dublin, a level 3 NNU. The primary purpose of the study was to inform the design and delivery of a future larger multi‐centre trial by evaluating recruitment, retention, data collection procedures, and intervention fidelity in an Irish healthcare setting.

Ethical approval was obtained from both the Rotunda Hospital Research Ethics Committee and the Royal College of Surgeons in Ireland (RCSI) – Reference number REC‐2024‐022.

Eligibility Criteria

Parents and guardians of neonates admitted to the NNU for a minimum of five days were eligible for inclusion if the infant was expected to be discharged on two or more medications. A minimum of one parent or guardian per infant was required to participate. Where both parents or guardians consented to participate, each was considered an individual study participant and assigned a unique identifier linked to the infant. Responses were analysed at the participant level for all outcomes.

Participants were excluded if infants' medications were discontinued before discharge, if infants were transferred to a different hospital, or due to language barriers. Although the PADDINGToN resources are now available in multiple languages, they were not available at the time this study was conducted.

Recruitment

Recruitment was between March and December 2025. The clinical team were approached to identify eligible families approaching discharge. A member of the research team approached potential participants for informed consent.

Sample Size

As a feasibility study, no formal power calculation was undertaken. The target sample size was 35 participants.

Intervention

Participants were introduced to the PADDINGToN medication information resources by the researcher after signed consent was obtained; parents received the printed PADDINGToN booklet containing an overview of the available resources and QR codes linking to a website hosting the A‐Z medicines information [20].

Measurement Tools

Parental anxiety was assessed using the Generalised Anxiety Disorder 7‐item scale (GAD‐7), a validated self‐report instrument widely used to screen and quantify symptoms of anxiety in clinical and research settings [21, 22, 23]. Selection of the GAD‐7 followed a systematic review of validated tools used in parents of hospitalised infants (in press) and was informed by consultation with the PPAG, healthcare professionals, and a clinical psychologist to ensure suitability and acceptability in the neonatal discharge context.

Parental confidence was assessed using a study‐specific questionnaire comprising Likert‐type items with ordinal response options; this was developed in collaboration with the PPAG to capture parents' self‐reported confidence and anxiety in relation to medication administration.

Acceptability of the intervention and study procedures was further explored through semi‐structured interviews and open‐text feedback, allowing in‐depth assessment of perceived burden, usefulness, and engagement.

Data Collection Procedures

All participants were assigned a unique study identification code for pseudonymisation. Identifiable information was stored separately on a secure, encrypted hospital server in accordance with GDPR requirements.

Pseudonymised data was collected at multiple time points: baseline demographic data, a pre‐discharge (baseline) parental anxiety assessment using the GAD‐7 questionnaire, a Likert scale questionnaire assessing medication administration specific parental confidence and anxiety completed by participants at 1–2 weeks post discharge, 4–6 post discharge and again at 8 weeks post discharge, and finally, a repeat GAD‐7 assessment at 10–12 weeks post discharge. An optional post completion participant feedback was also available to participants.

Midway through the study, an additional question was incorporated into the post discharge questionnaires to assess the usability and acceptability of the PADDINGToN resources. This was introduced to inform refinement of data collection tools and outcome selection for future research. Pseudonymised participant data was used to generate socioeconomic demographic data using the Pobal HP Deprivation Index (2022) [24].

To support participant retention, follow‐up questionnaires were distributed twice at one‐week intervals. Where no response was received within two weeks, a telephone message reminder was issued, including a reiteration of voluntary participation and the option to withdraw at any stage.

Study Outcomes

The primary feasibility outcome was participant recruitment and retention. Retention was defined as completion of the baseline and final follow‐up assessment and at least one interim follow‐up questionnaire. Retention to proceed for further phases of the study was evaluated using a predefined Red–Amber–Green (RAG) progression framework:

Data Analysis

As feasibility study, reporting was informed by the CONSORT extension for pilot and feasibility studies [25].

Quantitative data were analysed descriptively. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 11.0.2 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA); continuous variables were summarised using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR), with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Data were analysed on an available‐case basis at each follow‐up time point.

Qualitative data, including semi‐structured interviews and open‐text feedback, were analysed using a descriptive approach. Responses were reviewed iteratively to identify recurring themes relating to usability, acceptability and perceived value of the intervention. Findings were synthesised to inform refinement of subsequent study procedures and intervention delivery.

Ethical Considerations

Expert advice was sought from the local perinatal mental health team on the management of participants who reported moderate or severe anxiety scores, demonstrated by a score of 10 or higher in the GAD‐7 questionnaire; participants were provided with information on appropriate support services and encouraged to seek follow‐up with their general practitioner or relevant healthcare professionals. All data handling complied with institutional and EU GDPR requirements, and data were anonymised following study completion.

Results

Participant Flow and Recruitment

A total of 61 infant‐family dyads met inclusion criteria and were eligible for participation. Of these, 57.4% (n = 35) were approached for participation and 35 individual parents (25 infant‐family dyads) consented to participate in the study, including 24 mothers or birthing parents and 11 fathers or non‐birthing parents. 22.9% (n = 8) declined participation. Reasons for refusal included parents reporting confidence in medication administration (n = 3) and perceived lack of time following discharge (n = 2); the remaining 3 families did not provide a reason. Medication Education

In all, 74.3% of participants (n = 26) completed the medication education questionnaire. Thirteen (50%) respondents reported receiving a medication schedule outlining when each medication should be administered, while eleven (42%) had not yet received a schedule, and two (8%) were unsure.

Eight respondents (31%) reported being shown a medication checklist by a member of staff before discharge, seven (27%) reported that they were not shown a checklist. Nine respondents (35%) indicated that their infant was being discharged on nutritional supplements only.

Pre‐discharge knowledge of medication‐related information varied across domains. Nineteen respondents (73%) reported knowing the names of all prescribed medications and twenty‐one (81%) reported knowing the indication for all medications. Fewer respondents reported knowing the timing or frequency of all prescribed medications (58%), exact dose or quantity (58%), how to store each medication (62%), or how to measure the exact dose of each medication (50%). Knowledge of how to manage medication administration complications for all prescribed medications, such as vomiting after administering the medication, was reported by fewer than half of the respondents (42%). Recruitment Data

Of the 35 participants who consented to participate in the study, 77% completed the baseline GAD‐7 anxiety questionnaire before discharge, demonstrating strong initial engagement following recruitment.

Retention was pre‐defined as completion of both baseline and final anxiety assessments and at least one interim follow‐up questionnaire. Retention rate was 42.8% (15/35; 95% CI 26%–60%). According to the predefined Red–Amber–Green progression criteria, this retention rate falls within the Amber category, indicating that progression to a larger study is feasible with protocol modifications.

Of the retained participants, questionnaire completion was generally high; only one participant missed a single interim questionnaire, while the remainder completed all scheduled assessments. An additional four participants (11.4%) completed all interim questionnaires but did not complete the final GAD‐7 anxiety assessment at 10–12 weeks post discharge.

Parental Anxiety (GAD‐7)

Baseline GAD‐7 anxiety scores were available for 27 participants, while 15 participants completed the follow‐up GAD‐7 questionnaire at 10–12 weeks following discharge.

At baseline, the median GAD‐7 score 9 (IQR 4.5–13; 95% CI 5–12), indicating overall mild to moderate anxiety among participants before discharge. At follow‐up 10–12 weeks post discharge, overall anxiety levels were lower among respondents at that time. The median GAD‐7 score fell to 5 (IQR 4–7; 95% CI 4–7), with fewer participants reporting moderate or severe anxiety at follow‐up. Parental Confidence in Medication Administration

Parental anxiety and confidence was assessed using a structured Likert‐type questionnaire. Participants rated how anxious and confident they felt when managing different aspects of medication administration following discharge (e.g., preparation, administration, post‐administration). The same questionnaire was administered to the participants at approximately 2 weeks, 4–6 weeks and 8 weeks following discharge from the neonatal unit to explore changes in anxiety and confidence over time.

Parental anxiety and confidence scores were available for 21 of 35 participants at the first follow‐up point and 19 of 35 participants at both subsequent follow‐up timepoints. Figure S1 illustrates the trajectory of median anxiety and confidence scores over time.

Median anxiety scores decreased from 8 (IQR 4–16; 95% CI 4–16) at 2 weeks after discharge to 5 (IQR 0–8; 95% CI 0–8) at 8 weeks after discharge. Median confidence scores showed a modest upward trend, rising from 7 (IQR 6–8; 95% CI 6–8) at 2 weeks after discharge to 8 (IQR 6–10; 95% CI 6–10) at 8 weeks after discharge.

Engagement With the Intervention

Questions relating to engagement with the PADDINGToN medication information resources were introduced midway through the study following early participant feedback, in order to assess the usability and acceptability of the resources. These questions were subsequently included in all follow‐up questionnaires.

Across follow‐up time points, a proportion of participants reported accessing the resources after discharge with the highest reported access taking place between 1 to 2 weeks and 4–6 weeks after discharge (40% each) and subsequently reducing to 33% at 8 weeks after discharge. By the end of the study, 55% (n = 6) of respondents indicated that they had accessed the resources at any stage and 45% of respondents (n = 5) reported that they found them useful.

Qualitative Feedback (Acceptability and Usability)

Parental feedback indicated that the medication information resources were generally perceived as useful, with suggestions including improved organisation of medication information at discharge and increased availability of digital formats.

Discussion

Main Findings

This feasibility study evaluated the practicality of recruiting and retaining parents of neonates discharged home on medications into a structured research study examining the PADDINGToN medication information resources. Recruitment targets were achieved within the planned timeframe, demonstrating that parents are willing to participate in research addressing medication management during the transition home. Retention over the 12‐week follow‐up study period of 42.8% met the predefined ‘Amber’ feasibility criteria, indicating progression is feasible with targeted protocol optimisations, particularly addressing retention of participants and outcome measures.

Patterns of participant engagement highlighted important practical consideration when planning parent‐centred follow‐up approaches in studies involving parents caring for medically vulnerable infants. Integrating follow‐up with routine clinical encounters may enhance participant engagement and retention, while reducing participant burden. Parents participating in neonatal follow‐up research frequently experience competing caregiving demands, sleep deprivation and ongoing medical appointments, which may contribute to attrition in longitudinal studies following neonatal discharge. These findings will directly inform the design of a future multi‐centre trial, where follow‐up assessments will be integrated into scheduled neonatal appointments to support participation and optimise data collection.

While participant anxiety reduced over time, this outcome is likely influenced by a range of factors, highlighting the value of incorporating outcome measures more closely aligned with medication‐specific knowledge and behaviours in future studies. Subsequent phases of the programme will therefore focus on more targeted measures of parental medication knowledge and understanding, including awareness of medication indication, dosing, administration, storage and management of common issues. These measures are expected to provide a more direct evaluation of the educational value of the PADDINGToN resources and their potential role in improving medication safety following neonatal discharge.

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of this study is its prospective design and focus on evaluating the feasibility of implementing a co‐designed education intervention in a real‐world neonatal setting, before launching a larger multi‐centre RCT informed by lessons learned. Collaboration with parents through the PPAG ensured that study materials and outcomes were informed by lived experience. In addition, the use of predefined progression criteria (RAG criteria) allowed structured evaluation of recruitment and retention outcomes to guide the design of subsequent phases of the research programme.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. As expected in a single‐centre feasibility study without a control group, the sample size was modest; however, this allowed detailed evaluation of study processes to inform future multi‐centre implementation. Remote follow‐up provided important insights into participant engagement, directly informing optimisation strategies for subsequent PADDINGToN studies. While anxiety and confidence improved over time, incorporating outcome measures more closely aligned with the impact of medication information resources and education will provide a more direct assessment of the intervention. The absence of a control group reflects the feasibility design of this study; subsequent phases will incorporate comparative approaches to more robustly evaluate intervention effects.

Finally, the participant cohort was relatively socioeconomically advantaged and highly educated, which may limit generalisability.

Implications for Future Research

This study provides valuable insights into the feasibility of conducting research with parents of neonates after discharge from the NNU. It emphasises the importance of integrating research procedures into routine clinical encounters to improve retention and reduce participant burden. It also highlights the need to select outcome measures that more directly reflect the intended impact of the intervention. The next phase of the programme, PADDINGToN‐3, will build on these findings by evaluating the implementation of the intervention across multiple NNUs and assessing its impact on parental medication knowledge and self‐efficacy using a pre‐ and post‐intervention design.

Ultimately, strengthening parental education and support around medication administration has the potential to improve medication safety for infants during the critical transition from hospital to home. Evidence generated through the ongoing PADDINGToN research programme will support the establishment of a more parent‐centred framework incorporating structured medication education within the neonatal discharge process.

Conclusion

This feasibility study demonstrated that recruitment and retention of parents of neonates discharged home on medications into a structured research study is achievable and provides clear direction for optimising participant engagement in future multi‐centre studies.

The findings support selection of outcome measures that directly reflect the impact of the intervention in future studies.

These insights will inform the design of a future, larger, multi‐centre study evaluating the benefit of medication information resources across different neonatal units.
