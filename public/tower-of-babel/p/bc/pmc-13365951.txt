IFPHPOS0057 The Relationship Between Income-Generating Activities of Women, Dietary Behaviours and Feeding Practices of Infants and Young Children in Urban Ghana
Authors: Jedidah Mould, Emily Rousham, Katherine Gough, James Esson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalnutrition remains a major public health concern in low- and middle-income countries, including Ghana, particularly among children aged 6–23 months, a critical window for growth and development. Maternal income-generating activities (IGAs) may influence infant and young child feeding (IYCF) practices through improved income but also reduced caregiving time. This study examined the relationship between maternal IGAs, dietary behaviours, IYCF practices, and child nutritional status in urban Ghana.MethodsA mixed-methods cross-sectional study was conducted among 323 mother–infant pairs in Oyarifa, near Accra. Quantitative data included IYCF indicators: minimum meal frequency, minimum dietary diversity, minimum acceptable diet, and exclusive breastfeeding and anthropometric measures: length-for-age z-score, weight-for-age z-score, and weight-for-length z-score. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed. Qualitative data from focus groups and interviews were analysed thematically.Results54% of mothers engaged in IGAs. Children experienced a double burden of malnutrition (10% underweight, 5% stunted, 13% wasted), while 47% of mothers were overweight or obese. Mothers in IGAs were more likely to meet minimum dietary diversity (58% vs 39%, p < 0.001) and minimum acceptable diet (53% vs 36%, p = 0.003). Maternal IGA remained a significant predictor of both outcomes. However, IGA participation was also associated with higher consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages, fried snacks, and infant formula. Children of IGA mothers had lower length-for-age and weight-for-age z-scores and higher stunting prevalence. Qualitative findings highlighted time constraints, limited childcare support, and environmental influences.ConclusionMaternal IGAs have a dual impact on child nutrition, improving dietary diversity while potentially compromising caregiving and nutritional outcomes. Integrated interventions are needed.
