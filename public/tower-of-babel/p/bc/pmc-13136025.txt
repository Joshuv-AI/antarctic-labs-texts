Trisomy 8 alters chromatin conformations and activates Y chromosome genes in stem cells to drive a pre-leukemic state
Authors: Jie Bai, Kimi Araki, Daisuke Kurotaki, Eerdunduleng, Supannika Sorin, Kei Hiramatsu, Narumi Uno, Ai Hamashima, Mihoko Iimori, Kenta Kikuchi, Minoru Terashima, Sho Kubota, Kensaku Kohrogi, Gang Huang, Minetaro Ogawa, Mitsuo Oshimura, Yasuhiro Kazuki, Goro Sashida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe mechanistic role of trisomy 8 in the development of myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) remains poorly defined. Here, we generated a trisomy 8 mouse model by transferring a human chromosome 8 into murine embryonic stem cells and prospectively examined the effects on hematopoietic stem cells (HSC) by trisomy 8. The expression of inflammatory genes was enhanced, and hematopoietic programs mediated by transcription factors and polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) were dysregulated in trisomy 8 HSC, which impaired their self-renewal and balanced differentiation. Trisomy 8 HSC altered the chromatin accessibility and conformations and activated Y chromosome genes, such as Uty/Kdm6c epigenetic modifier, which is known to demethylate histone H3K27me3 modification. The Uty gene facilitated the activation of PRC2-target and Runx1-target genes in leukemogenesis and drove the proliferation of human trisomy 8 leukemic cells. Since the RUNX1 gene is frequently mutated in patients with trisomy 8 MDS, its deletion attenuated the enhanced expression of inflammatory genes and mitigated the impaired self-renewal of trisomy 8 HSC in mice. Our findings reveal that trisomy 8 altered the transcriptional programs and chromatin conformations in HSC and drove a pre-malignant state through activating the expression of Uty, suggesting a route for the development of trisomy 8 MDS.Subject terms: Myelodysplastic syndrome, Chromosomes, Cancer stem cells

Introduction

Myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) arises from mutated hematopoietic stem cells (HSC) and is characterized by cytopenia, cellular dysplasia, and a predisposition to acute myelogenous leukemia (AML) [1]. Recent sequencing studies provided the landscapes of genetic mutations and clonal evolution dynamics in patients with MDS [2, 3] and revealed the molecular mechanisms by which these genetic mutations drive the initiation and progression of MDS using murine models and human xenograft models [4, 5]. Numerical chromosome anomalies and deletions, such as monosomy 7 and deletion 5q, drive the pathogenesis of MDS due to the loss of tumor suppressor genes [6, 7]. In contrast, an extra chromosome has been suggested to induce the gene dosage effect that drives myeloid leukemia in Down syndrome [8]. In patients with MDS, trisomy 8 is a recurrent anomaly and is associated with an intermediate risk based on previous studies, including the IPSS-R system [6, 9, 10]. In addition, congenital trisomy 8 mosaicism shows developmental disabilities and increases the risk of developing MDS and AML later in life [11, 12], suggesting that trisomy 8 has the potential to drive the development of myeloid malignancies. While the MYC gene is located in chromosome 8 and is a potent oncogene, the expression of MYC is typically not up-regulated in MDS stem cells with trisomy 8 [13]. Indeed, the genes and regions in chromosome 8 that are commonly amplified in trisomy 8 tumor cells and may account for the pathogenesis of MDS have not yet been identified [13, 14].

Systemic inflammation due to chronic infections and autoimmune diseases has been suggested to drive the clonal hematopoiesis of a mutated driver gene in HSC in the elderly, leading to MDS [15, 16]. Somatic trisomy 8 is frequently found in MDS patients presenting with autoimmune diseases, such as Beçhet’s disease [17, 18]. In addition, transcriptomic studies demonstrated that the expression of inflammatory genes in hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPC) was enhanced in trisomy 8 MDS patients [19, 20], suggesting that trisomy 8 blood cells induce systemic inflammation or that inflammation confers a selective advantage to trisomy 8 MDS HSPC. Although trisomy generally reduces the proliferation of somatic cells, such as mouse embryonic fibroblasts, which were spontaneously immortalized by the loss of Tp53 in a long culture period [21], it has yet to be established whether trisomy 8 alone is sufficient to induce the activation of inflammatory genes and develop blood cancer under in vivo conditions. Genome sequencing studies revealed that MDS and AML cells with trisomy 8 preferentially mutated RUNX1, ASXL1, EZH2 and DNMT3A, which are known to drive the progression of MDS [22], suggesting that these genetic mutations and trisomy 8 may cooperate to drive the development of MDS. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that trisomy 8 induces epigenetic changes and dysregulates the transcription of genes, such as inflammatory genes, in HSC to initiate transformation driven by the acquisition of genetic mutations.

Previous studies reported that the incidence of trisomy 8 MDS was higher in males than in females, and also that median survival was shorter in males [10, 23]. To investigate the mechanisms by which trisomy 8 affects hematopoiesis in vivo, we herein generated a new trisomy 8 mouse model by transferring a human chromosome 8 into male murine embryonic stem cells. We found that trisomy 8 impaired adult hematopoiesis but did not develop full-blown MDS in mice. The expression of inflammatory genes was up-regulated in trisomy 8 HSC, and chromatin accessibility enriched with binding motifs of transcription factors (TFs), such as Runx1 and Gata2, was changed, which appeared to impair the self-renewal capacity and balanced differentiation of HSC. The expression of polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2)-target genes was up-regulated in trisomy 8 HSC. Furthermore, chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) sequencing revealed that trisomy 8 not only markedly altered the conformations of all chromosomes, but also induced an active chromatin compartment in the Y chromosome, accompanied with increased expression of its genes, such as Uty/Kdm6c, a demethylase of histone H3K27me3, which may dysregulate the function of stem cell TFs and drive the proliferation of trisomy 8 leukemic cells. Therefore, the transduction of trisomy 8 was sufficient to induce the activation of inflammatory genes and the chromatin conformation changes in HSC. Finally, based on the loss-of-function mutation of RUNX1 frequently found in trisomy 8 MDS, we demonstrated that the deletion of the Runx1 gene attenuated the activation of a subset of inflammatory genes and mitigated the impaired self-renewal capacity of trisomy 8 HSC in mice. Overall, the altered chromatin conformations by trisomy 8 drive a pre-malignant state through activating the expression of Uty, suggesting a UTY-specific function in MDS and a route for the development of trisomy 8 MDS.

Methods

Generation of trisomy 8 mouse ES cells by microcell-mediated chromosome transfer (MMCT)

To generate trisomy 8 mouse embryonic stem (ES) cells, we performed MMCT to introduce human chromosome 8 from hybrid mouse A9 cells into the wild-type (WT) male ES cell line, 6NK7 [24]. MMCT was performed as previously described [25]. Donor A9 cells carrying a human chromosome 8, tagged with an EGFP-neo resistance gene as previously described [26]. Briefly, murine ES cells were fused with microcells prepared from A9 cells carrying the human chromosome 8 and selected with G418 (Promega, 250 µg/ml). We isolated four trisomy 8 ES clones (#7, #11, #23, and #30) and one control ES clone (#21), which spontaneously lost both arms of the human chromosome 8, but retained its centromere with EGFP-neo resistance gene. Because this study used male ES cells, it is unknown whether the findings are relevant for female cells.

FISH analysis

ES clones were evaluated by conventional cytogenetics fixed by Carnoy’s solution (methanol/acetic acid-3:1) and a fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) analysis using digoxigenin-labeled human COT-1 probe (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and biotin-labeled EGFP probe as previously described [25].

Mice

ES cells were aggregated with an ICR morula to generate chimera mice, as previously described [27]. Fetal livers (FL) were collected from chimeric mice on embryonic day 14.5 (E14.5) after aggregation. WT control mice were chimeras derived from the control ES cell clone (#21). C57BL/6 mice congenic for the Ly5 locus (CD45.1) were purchased from Sankyo-Lab Service. NSG mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory. Adult male or female mice (8-12 weeks) were used as recipients for experiments. All experiments using these mice were performed in accordance with our institutional guidelines for the use of laboratory animals and were approved by the Review Board for Animal Experiments of Kumamoto University (Kumamoto, Japan). Reference numbers were A2020-050, A2022-022 and A2024-059. All mouse experiments were performed without randomization and blinding.

Cell culture

The C57BL/6N-derived ES cell line 6NK7 was cultured in liquid medium supplemented with mLIF (Sigma), as previously described [24]. CD150+CD48-Lineage-Sca-1+c-Kit+ HSC were cultured in RPMI-based medium supplemented with 2% Lipid-Rich BSA (GIBCO), 10 ng/ml mouse stem cell factor (SCF; PeproTech), and 100 ng/ml mouse thrombopoietin (TPO; PeproTech). THP-1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Wako) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). SKK-1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and 10 ng/ml human GM-CSF (BioLegend). SKK-1 cells transduced with shRNA lentivirus vectors were isolated by flow cytometry and cultured. Mycoplasma contamination was tested in all cell lines by PCR.

ES cell differentiation

ES cell differentiation was induced as previously reported [28]. Briefly, 8 × 103 ES cells were inoculated on OP9 bone marrow (BM) stromal cells and cultured for 6 days in a medium to which Activin A was added for the first 4 days. A total of 2.5 × 104 CD45-CD31+CD41+ hemogenic endothelial (HE) cells were sorted and cultured on OP9 cells. HE cells were cultured in a medium supplemented with 20 ng/ml mIL-3, 2 U/ml hEPO, 20 ng/ml hG-CSF, and 20 ng/ml mSCF for myeloid/erythroid cell differentiation and 50 ng/ml mSCF, 50 ng/ml hFlt3L, and 20 ng/ml mIL7 for B cell differentiation. All in vitro experiments were conducted without randomization and blinding.

Flow cytometry and antibodies

Flow cytometry and cell sorting were performed using the following antibodies purchased from BioLegend or eBioscience (clone and catalogue numbers): B220 (RA3-6B2, 103212), CD3e (145-2C11, 100304), CD4 (L3T4, 100526), CD8α (53-6.7, 100714), CD34 (MEC14.7, 11-0341-85), CD41 (MWReg3, 133928), CD45.1 (A20, 110730), CD45.2 (104, 109820), CD48 (HM48-1, 103443), CD105 (MJ7/18, 120414), CD135 (A2F10, 135306), CD150 (TC15-12F12.2, 115924), c-Kit (2B8, 105812), FcγRII-III (93, 101308), Gr-1 (RB6-8C5, 108404), IL-7Rα (A7R34, 121104), Mac1 (M1/70, 101208), Sca-1 (D7, 108114), and Ter119 (116204). The lineage mixture solution contained biotin-conjugated anti-Gr-1, B220, CD3e, CD8α, Ter119, and IL-7Rα antibodies, but did not contain anti-CD4 and Mac1 antibodies. All flow cytometric analyses and cell sorting were performed using FACSAriaII, FACSCantoII (BD), or MoFlo XDP (Beckman Coulter) [29].

CRISPR/Cas9 transduction

Designed sgRNA and Cas9 (Alt-R® S.p. HiFi Cas9 Nuclease V3) were purchased from Invitrogen and IDT, respectively. Two sgRNA-directed Runx1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, CRISPR33531_SGM and CRISPR32292_SGM of A35533) were mixed at a 1:1 ratio for transduction, and sgRNA non-targeting (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A35526) was used as a negative control. The electroporation of Cas9-sgRNA RNPs was performed as previously reported [30]. The electroporation of HSPC was conducted under optimized conditions (1700 V, 20 ms, and one pulse) using the Neon transfection system (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

shRNA virus vectors and transduction

Lentivirus vector (CS-RfA-EF-mRFP1) expressing three shRNA-directed UTY (shUTY #1, #2, and #3), two shRNA-directed UTX (shUTX #1, #2) and a control shRNA-directed Luciferase (shLuc) were constructed. The target sequences were designated as follows: shLuc, 5’-CGCTGAGTACTTCGAAATGTC-3’; shUTY #1, 5’-GGATGCTTTACAGGCATATAT-3’; shUTY #2, 5’-GGCAACTAGCACTGGTATTAA-3’; shUTY #3, 5’-GGCAACTAGCACTGGTATTAA-3’. shUTX #1, 5’- AAGACCACTCAGATAGTGAAT-3’; shUTX #2, 5’- GCAGCACGAATTAAGTATTTA-3’. VSV-G pseudo-type lentiviral supernatants were produced by calcium phosphate transfection into LentiX cells. SKK-1 cells were infected with viral supernatants in the presence of protamine sulfate (10 μg/ml; Sigma).

Genomic PCR and quantitative RT-PCR

Genomic PCR amplification was performed using KOD Fx Neo polymerase (TOYOBO). Quantitative RT-PCR was performed on LightCycler 480 (Roche) using Luna Universal qPCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs). Expression levels were normalized to those of mouse B2m or human B2M. Primers for PCR are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

RamDA sequencing

RamDA sequencing was performed as described in our previous study [29]. Sequencing libraries were generated using the Nextera XT DNA Library Prep kit (Illumina) and sequenced on NextSeq500 (Illumina). Kallisto (v0.43.1) was used to calculate transcripts per million. R software was employed for statistical analyses and a k-means clustering analysis. Sequencing data were deposited in the DNA Data Bank of Japan (DDBJ) under accession numbers DRA019676, DRA016699, DRA019955, and DRA18137.

Assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC sequencing)

ATAC sequencing libraries were prepared using the Omni-ATAC protocol [31]. Briefly, cells were lysed and incubated with transposase solution containing 1 μL of Tagment DNA Enzyme (Illumina). Transposed DNA was purified and amplified using a NEBNext High Fidelity 2× PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs) with indexed primers. The prepared libraries were sequenced on NextSeq500 (Illumina) with 38-bp paired-end reads. Bowtie2 (v2.2.6) was used to map reads to the reference genome (UCSC/mm10). Peak calling and motif analyses were performed using HOMER (v4.9). Heatmap clustering was conducted by deepTools (v3.5.0). Sequencing data were deposited in the DDBJ under the accession numbers DRA016701.

Hi-C sequencing

Hi-C sequencing was conducted as previously described [32]. In brief, isolated cells were fixed with 1% formaldehyde. Nuclei were isolated with lysis buffer, and DNA digestion was performed at 37 °C using DpnII. DNA overhangs were filled with biotin-labeled deoxyadenosine triphosphate using Klenow DNA polymerase, followed by proximity ligation with T4 DNA ligase at 16 °C. Ligated DNA was purified by phenol extraction and subsequently sheared using Covaris M220. Biotin-labeled DNA fragments were enriched by streptavidin C1 beads. A KAPA hyper prep kit was used for the generation of Hi-C libraries. Each Hi-C library was sequenced on Illumina HiSeq X. Hi-C sequencing reads were mapped according to the instructions provided on the Homer website (http://homer.ucsd.edu/homer/interactions2/HiCtagDirectory.html). To analyze changes in nuclear compartments, a primary component analysis of Hi-C data was conducted using the Homer runHiCpca.pl module. Sequencing data were deposited in the DDBJ under the accession numbers DRA018138 and DRA018357.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation with sequencing (ChIP sequencing)

SKK-1 cells were cross-linked with 3 mM disuccinimidyl glutarate (Thermo Scientific) at RT for 30 min and then cross-linked with 1% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 30 min. Cross-linked cells were lysed and sonicated 10 times at a 50% amplitude for 10 s. Samples were incubated with an anti-UTY antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #48779) conjugated to Dynabeads protein A/G at 4 °C overnight. Inputs and immunoprecipitates were incubated at 65 °C for 4 h for reverse cross-linking, and DNA was purified using the MinElute PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen). Sequencing libraries were generated using the ThruPLEX DNA-seq kit (Rubicon Genomics) and sequenced on NextSeq500 (Illumina). Bowtie2 (v2.2.6) was used to map reads to the reference genome (UCSC/hg38). Peak calling and motif analyses were performed using HOMER (v4.9). Heatmap clustering was conducted by deepTools (v3.5.0). Sequencing data were deposited in the DDBJ under the accession number DRA018139.

Western blotting

Whole cell lysates were used for Western blotting. The following antibodies were used: Runx1 (Abcam, ab23980; Sigma, R0406) and actin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, C4). Uncropped immunoblot images are available in Supporting Data.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism version 9 (GraphPad) was used for statistical analyses. The significance of differences was measured by an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test and an ANOVA for multiple comparisons test. A P value < 0.05 was considered to be significant. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes for animal studies.

Results

Generation of a new trisomy 8 mouse model

To investigate the mechanisms by which trisomy 8 affects hematopoiesis in vivo, we attempted to generate a new trisomy 8 mouse model. Since homologous genes in human chromosome 8 are separately located in eight murine chromosomes (Supplementary Fig. 1A), we introduced human chromosome 8 as an extra allele of genes in murine diploid cells (Fig. 1A) [25]. Published datasets on MDS patients revealed that the incidence of trisomy 8 was higher in males than in females, and also that median survival was shorter in males (Supplementary Fig. 2), suggesting that sex differences may affect the pathogenesis of trisomy 8 MDS. Therefore, we transferred human chromosome 8 into murine male ES cells, successfully isolated four trisomy 8 ES clones, and generated one control ES clone that spontaneously lost both the long and short arms, but maintained the centromere region expressing EGFP, hereafter referred to as the WT control (Fig. 1B). Genomic PCR confirmed the absence of human genes on both arms in control cells (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Since trisomy 8 mosaic humans are rarely born and are predisposed to develop hematological malignancies in later life [11, 12], we injected an aggregate mixture of trisomy 8 ES cells and ICR morulae into pseudo-pregnant female mice (Fig. 1C). On E14.5, the number of alive chimeric embryos derived from distinct trisomy 8 ES clones was slightly lower than that from control ES clone (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. 1C, D). The frequency of GFP+ cells in fetal liver (FL) blood cells was lower in trisomy 8 chimera embryos than in control embryos (Fig. 1E). A flow cytometric analysis of FL cells revealed the emergence of lineage-Sca-1+c-Kit+ (LSK) HSPC in both trisomy 8 chimera and control embryos (Fig. 1D), whereas trisomy 8 chimera contained significantly fewer GFP+ cells in CD150+CD48-LSK HSC than control embryos (Fig. 1F). In addition, under culture conditions using OP9 stromal cells, we generated blood cells using the CD31+CD41+ hemogenic endothelial (HE) cells differentiated from parent ES cell and these ES clones (Fig. 1G). While parent ES cells differentiated into blood cells, trisomy 8 ES clones differentiated into HSPC, myeloid cells, and B lymphoid cells at lower frequencies than control ES clone (Fig. 1H). Therefore, we successfully generated a new trisomy 8 chimera mouse model that was able to generate blood cells at lower frequencies under both in vivo and in vitro conditions.

To understand how trisomy 8 affected the transcriptome at different stages of development, we performed RNA sequencing of ES cells, HE cells, and fetal HSC. By using a combined reference of human chromosome 8 with the murine genome (mm10), principal component analyses (PCA) of RNA sequencing revealed the overall similar transcriptional profiles between WT and trisomy 8 cells during the development (Fig. 1I). In addition, human genes in chromosome 8 and their homologous murine genes showed a linear correlation of expression levels in trisomy 8 cells (Supplementary Fig. 1E), suggesting that the human chromosome was transcriptionally regulated in murine cells in a similar manner to homologous regions in murine chromosomes. Despite the overall similarity in the transcriptome, a hierarchical clustering revealed that trisomy 8 cells from different stages clustered independently (Supplementary Fig. 1F), suggesting that trisomy 8 induced transcriptional changes in blood cells.

Trisomy 8 impaired the self-renewal of HSC and failed to develop MDS in mice

To determine whether trisomy 8 affected adult hematopoiesis in a cell-intrinsic manner, we transplanted trisomy 8 FL blood cells into WT mice and assessed the hematopoietic function. We transplanted 5 × 105 GFP+ FL cells isolated from E14.5 embryos into lethally irradiated Ly5.1+ mice together with 2 × 105 WT BM cells (Fig. 2A). We used two trisomy 8 clones (clones 7 and 23) and the WT control (clone 21) for transplantation (Fig. 1D). We found that the frequencies of GFP+ cells in myeloid and lymphoid cells in PB and HSC and multipotent progenitor cells (MPP) in BM three months after transplantation were similar in trisomy 8 mice and control mice (Fig. 2B, C). We then investigated whether trisomy 8 HSC maintained their self-renewal capacity by performing a competitive secondary transplantation of LSK HSPC isolated from the primary recipients of trisomy 8 cells. The frequencies of GFP+ cells in both mature cells in PB and HSC and MPP in BM were lower in trisomy 8 mice than in control WT mice (Fig. 2D, E). In order to examine the possibility of human genes on the extra chromosome 8 to express xenoantigens, which may eliminate trisomy 8 cells in immunocompetent mice, we transplanted trisomy 8 HSPC into NSG immunodeficient mice together with Ly5.1+ WT BM cells (Supplementary Fig. 3A). NSG recipients showed that the frequencies of GFP+ cells in HSC and MPP in the BM and mature myeloid cells in the PB were markedly lower in trisomy 8 mice than in control WT mice (Supplementary Fig. 3B–D). Therefore, trisomy 8 impaired the self-renewal capacity of HSC in a cell-intrinsic manner.

We next investigated whether trisomy 8 cells were sufficient to induce malignant disease in the primary recipients. While trisomy 8 mice mostly lost GFP+ blood cells in PB 3 to 4 months after primary transplantation, 3 out of 148 transplanted mice developed lethal disease, showing anemia and dysplastic blood cells in the maximum observation period of one year (Supplementary Fig. 4A–C). To assess the proliferative capacity of trisomy 8 disease cells in these mice, we performed the secondary transplantation of 1 × 106 BM cells isolated from two disease mice into sub-lethally irradiated WT recipients (Supplementary Fig. 4D). Consistent with the impaired self-renewal capacity of trisomy 8 HSC (Fig. 2D, E), trisomy 8 disease cells maintained the extra chromosome 8 and lost their repopulation capacity (Supplementary Fig. 4E–H), resulting in a few GFP+ cells in the PB of secondary recipients (Supplementary Fig. 4I), suggesting that trisomy 8 alone was not sufficient to develop full-blown MDS in vivo, although trisomy 8 induced transcriptional changes in blood cells and rarely developed lethal blood diseases in the primary recipients.

Trisomy 8 HSC dysregulated the transcriptional programs by stem-cell regulators

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which trisomy 8 impaired the self-renewal capacity of HSC and hematopoiesis, we performed RNA sequencing on HSC and granulocyte/monocyte progenitor cells (GMP) isolated from the primary recipients two months after transplantation. The expression of 261 genes was higher, and that of 20 genes was lower in trisomy 8 HSC than in control HSC, while the expression of 196 genes was higher and that of 41 genes was lower in trisomy 8 GMP than in control GMP (Fig. 3A, B and Supplementary Dataset 1). A hierarchical clustering map showed that the expression levels of genes in human chromosome 8 were higher in trisomy 8 HSC than in control HSC, and were not shared with up-regulated genes in ES cells and HE cells harboring trisomy 8 (Fig. 3C), supporting that transduced human genes followed tissue-specific transcriptional regulation (Fig. 1I). A gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that in comparison with control HSC, trisomy 8 HSC (clones 7, 23, and 30) showed positive enrichments in both stem cell and myeloid signature genes (Fig. 3D). In addition, trisomy 8 HSC positively enriched the canonical target genes of stem cell regulators, such as Runx1, PU.1, Gata2, and Mecom TFs, and that of polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2), in which Ezh2 is critical for differentiation via the modulation of histone H3K27me3 modifications [33, 34] (Fig. 3D). In contrast, in comparison with control GMP, trisomy 8 GMP negatively enriched the target genes of PU.1 TF (Fig. 3D), suggesting that trisomy 8 activated both stem cell and myeloid signature genes in HSC, but repressed the transcription of myeloid signature genes at the GMP stage. Although the MYC gene is located in human chromosome 8, the canonical target genes of Myc were not activated in trisomy 8 HSC and GMP (Fig. 3D).

We found a mild elevation of Mecom expression in trisomy 8 HSC among those dysregulated stem cell regulators (Supplementary Fig. 5A). Among those dysregulated TFs, we focused on Runx1 TF because loss-of-function mutations in RUNX1 have been most frequently found in patients with trisomy 8 MDS [35, 36]. While trisomy 8 HSC increased the expression of Runx1-target genes identified in murine progenitor cells (Fig. 3D), trisomy 8 HSC and GMP showed similar Runx1 expression levels to those in WT HSC and GMP (Fig. 3E). We also found that trisomy 8 mononuclear cells (MNC) showed similar Runx1 protein expression levels to those in WT MNC (Fig. 3F), suggesting that the expression change of Runx1 may not account for the dysregulated expression of Runx1-target genes. In comparison with control HSC, among canonical Runx1-target genes [37], we found that down-regulated genes in trisomy 8 HSC were enriched in cluster 1 and up-regulated genes were enriched in clusters 2 and 3 (Fig. 3G). Gene ontology (GO) analyses revealed that cluster 1 genes were involved in biosynthesis and metabolism, while cluster 2 and 3 genes were involved in immunity, inflammation, and differentiation pathways (Fig. 3H). These results suggest that trisomy 8 HSC induced the activation of inflammatory genes and dysregulated the transcriptional programs by stem cell TFs, including Runx1, which appeared to impair the self-renewal function of HSC and hematopoiesis.

Trisomy 8 altered the chromatin structures in genome-wide and combinatory manners

Chromatin accessibility is a hallmark of active elements that regulate the transcription of genes in stem cells for their differentiation [38]. Based on the dysregulated programs for the differentiation by TFs, such as Runx1 and PU.1, in trisomy 8 HSC and GMP cells (Fig. 3D), we performed ATAC sequencing on WT and trisomy 8 HSC. ATAC sequencing revealed that trisomy 8 induced open chromatin and closed chromatin in HSC (3829 and 14371 out of 75685 peaks) (Fig. 4A). TF-binding motif enrichment analyses revealed that in comparison with WT HSC, trisomy 8 HSC enriched binding motifs of the RUNX family in both open and closed chromatin (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Dataset 2), which may have contributed to the activation or repression of individual Runx1-target genes (Fig. 3G). In addition, trisomy 8 HSC enriched binding motifs of SpiB/PU.1 related and CEBPE in closed chromatin, which may have impeded the transcription of PU.1-target genes in GMP (Fig. 3D). Therefore, trisomy 8 dysregulated chromatin accessibility at the binding regions of Runx1 and myeloid TFs in HSC, leading to the repressed expression of myeloid genes in GMP cells.

Based on the alterations in chromatin accessibility and transcriptional programs mediated by TFs in trisomy 8 HSC, we investigated whether trisomy 8 changed the conformation of chromatins, since the conformation of chromatins facilitates the process of transcription [39]. To achieve this, we performed Hi-C sequencing on HSPC isolated from primary transplanted mice two months after transplantation. Nuclear compartments were captured by the value of the first principal component (PC1) in a principal component analysis of the Hi-C contact map. In comparison with control HSPC, trisomy 8 HSPC showed 1707 genomic regions with increased PC1 values and 2760 genomic regions with decreased PC1 values in intra-chromosomal structures (Fig. 4C). These chromatin conformation changes were observed in all chromosomes to similar degrees, but were lower in chromosome 9 (Fig. 4D). GSEA revealed that up-regulated PC1 regions correlated with the higher expression of the corresponding genes in trisomy 8 HSC than in WT HSC (Fig. 4E and Supplementary Dataset 3), suggesting that trisomy 8 activated chromatin compartments, accompanied with the increased expression of genes in HSC, consistent with the previous finding [39]. To determine whether those changes in the chromatin accessibility and chromatin conformation in trisomy 8 cooperated to dysregulate the transcription of genes, we analyzed Hi-C and ATAC sequencing data in combination. We found that trisomy 8 HSPC increased PC1 values in open chromatins more than closed chromatins, compared to control HSPC (Fig. 4F), suggesting that the formation of open chromatins may be facilitated by active chromatin compartments in trisomy 8 HSC. Therefore, trisomy 8 altered the chromatin structures in HSPC in genome-wide and combinatory manners, which may lead to the altered transcriptional programs for hematopoiesis.

Trisomy 8 up-regulated the transcription of Y chromosome genes and facilitated the activation of Uty-target genes

We attempted to determine which altered chromosome regions contributed to the impaired hematopoiesis by trisomy 8. Among the 73 genes with increased expression in active compartments (Fig. 4E), we found that only the Y chromosome containing Kdm5d, Eif2s3y, Uty/Kdm6c, and Ddx3y genes showed a significant enrichment by the DAVID analysis (p = 0.025). The Hi-C sequencing revealed that a centromere-proximal region in the Y chromosome containing those genes showed higher contact levels forming a topologically associating domain (TAD) and a more active compartment than other chromosome regions, relative to WT HSPC (Fig. 5A, B). As the expression levels of the Kdm5d, Eif2s3y, Uty/Kdm6c, and Ddx3y genes were increased in the active region (Fig. 5C), Q-RT-PCR confirmed that trisomy 8 HSC variably increased the expression levels of Uty and Kdm5d genes, relative to WT HSC (Fig. 5D). In addition, we performed Hi-C of SKK-1 human leukemic cells presenting 47, XY + 8 originated from a male patient with MDS [40] and THP-1 non-trisomy 8 leukemic cells. The Hi-C analysis also revealed a significantly stronger TAD formation containing the UTY gene (Supplementary Fig. 6A) in SKK-1 cells than in THP-1 cells, suggesting that both murine and human trisomy 8 blood cells induced an active TAD formation and increased expression of the UTY gene.

Based on that, trisomy 8 HSC did not change the expression levels of PRC2 components but positively enriched the target genes of PRC2 (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig. 5A). Among the up-regulated genes in the Y chromosome, we focused on Uty because human UTY is known to demethylate histone H3K27me3 in vivo despite its weak enzymatic activity compared to UTX, its homologue on the X chromosome [41]. We found that the expression of UTY in HSPCs was higher in a subset of male MDS patients than in healthy males, whereas UTX was not activated in murine trisomy 8 HSC or MDS HSPCs in male and female patients (Supplementary Fig. 7A, B). To identify the direct targets of UTY, we performed UTY ChIP sequencing on SKK-1 trisomy 8 cells and found that UTY directly bound to the promoter and enhancer regions of stem cell genes, including RUNX1 and SPI1/PU.1 (Fig. 5G and Supplementary Fig. 6B; Supplementary Dataset 4). UTY-binding regions were enriched by active histone modifications, such as H3K4me1, H3K4me3, and H3K27ac, and the RUNX1 peak was higher than the H3K27me3 repressive mark, which were identified in human CD34+ cells and monocytes using published datasets (Fig. 5E and Supplementary Fig. 6C). A motif enrichment analysis of UTY-binding peaks showed significant enrichments in the binding motifs of RUNX, GATA, and ETS family TFs (Fig. 5F). In addition, UTY-binding annotated genes markedly overlapped with the Runx1-binding genes and canonical PRC2-target genes identified in murine HSPCs [37, 42] (Fig. 5G). By utilizing these UTY- and Runx1-binding gene sets (Supplementary Dataset 4), we found that the expression of both UTY-target and common target genes, such as MECOM/EVI1, which are involved in stem cell regulation and leukemia-related pathways (Fig. 5H and Supplementary Dataset 5), was significantly higher in trisomy 8 HSC than in WT HSC (Fig. 5I). By analyzing published datasets of patients with MDS, the expression levels of the common target genes in HSPC were higher in trisomy 8 MDS than in disomy 8 MDS (Fig. 5I), suggesting that the Uty gene activated the expression of PRC2-target genes, leading to the impaired hematopoiesis and the leukemogenesis by trisomy 8.

Finally, in order to investigate the role of UTY in trisomy 8 MDS, we knocked down the expression of the UTY gene in SKK-1 cells using three distinct shRNA vectors and confirmed lower expression levels in these cells than control-vector transduced cells (Fig. 5J). In a culture condition, the knockdown of UTY markedly repressed the growth of SKK-1 cells despite the expression of UTX (Fig. 5K and Supplementary Fig. 7C) and showed a similar immature morphology to the control shLuciferase-transduced cells (Fig. 5L), suggesting that UTY drove the proliferation of trisomy 8 MDS cells, which may not be compensated for by UTX. Interestingly, the knockdown of UTX mildly repressed the growth of SKK-1 cells in a culture condition, compared to control shLuciferase-transduced cells (Supplementary Fig.7D, E). Therefore, trisomy 8 up-regulated the expression of Y chromosome genes, including Uty, thereby facilitating the activation of its target genes, which may drive the transformation and the proliferation of MDS cells.

Deletion of Runx1 facilitated the activation of Uty-target genes and drove a pre-MDS state in trisomy 8 HSC

Trisomy 8 induced chromatin conformation changes and increased the transcription of Runx1-target genes involved in inflammation, proliferation, and differentiation in HSC. Since trisomy 8 was insufficient to develop full-blown MDS in mice and the RUNX1 gene is most frequently mutated in MDS patients with trisomy 8 [35, 36], we investigated whether the deletion of the Runx1 gene completely transformed trisomy 8 HSC in mice. By using a CRISPR/Cas9 system to delete the Runx1 gene [30], we transduced sgRNA-directed Runx1 into 1000 HSPCs isolated from primary transplant recipient mice and transplanted them into lethally irradiated mice together with 2×105 WT BM cells (Fig. 6A). We confirmed a reduction in Runx1 protein levels in BM blood cells after transplantation (Fig. 6B). Four months after transplantation, sgRNA-Runx1 transduced WT mice showed a similar donor chimerism in whole cells in PB to that in sgRNA-control transduced WT mice, but with a slightly higher number of myeloid cells (Fig. 6C), which is consistent with the mild myeloproliferative phenotype in Runx1 mice [43]. In contrast, control trisomy 8 mice all showed markedly lower donor chimerism in both the myeloid and lymphoid fractions of PB, while the deletion of Runx1 increased donor chimerism in myeloid and B cells in PB in 4 out of 10 trisomy 8 mice (Fig. 6D). Control trisomy 8 mice showed very low chimerism in HSC and MPP in BM, while the deletion of Runx1 induced a detectable chimerism in HSC and increased that in MPP in the secondary transplant recipient mice (Fig. 6E); however, we did not find evident malignant diseases in compound mutant mice in the one-year observation period, suggesting a pre-malignant state.

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which the deletion of Runx1 mitigated the impaired self-renewal capacity of trisomy 8 HSC, we performed RNA sequencing on HSC isolated from transplanted mice one year after transplantation. Principal component analyses revealed that sgRNA-Runx1 transduced trisomy 8 HSC was located between WT HSC and control trisomy 8 HSC (Fig. 6F). Among differentially expressed genes from control WT HSC, we found that up-regulated genes in control trisomy 8 HSC were enriched in cluster 4, in which the expression of genes was markedly repressed in sgRNA-Runx1 transduced trisomy 8 HSC (Fig. 6G). GO analyses revealed that cluster 4 genes were involved in cytokines, complements, and differentiation, while cluster 1 genes were involved in T cell-related signaling (Fig. 6H and Supplementary Dataset 6), suggesting that the Runx1 deletion repressed the expression of a subset of inflammatory genes activated in trisomy 8 HSC. Furthermore, GSEA revealed that the expression of stem cell signature genes and UTY-target genes was higher in sgRNA-Runx1-trisomy 8 HSC than in control trisomy 8 HSC (Fig. 6I). Therefore, the deletion of the Runx1 gene attenuated the activation of a subset of inflammatory genes and facilitated the activation of UTY-target genes in trisomy 8 HSC, which appeared to provide the competitive fitness of the mutant clone in BM and induce a pre-malignant state.

Discussion

By transducing human chromosome 8 into male ES cells and generating a new trisomy 8 mouse model, we were able to prospectively examine the functional effects on and the process of transformation of HSC by trisomy 8. In cancers with a loss of chromosomes, such as monosomy 7, a haplo-insufficiency of tumor suppressor genes has been shown to promote pathogenesis through studies using mouse models and human specimens. In contrast, in patients with trisomy 8, neither commonly amplified regions nor oncogenes on chromosome 8 that drive the progression of MDS have been identified [13, 14]. Previous studies reported that Down syndrome and somatic trisomy 21 induced global changes in the transcription of genes and their chromatin accessibilities in normal and malignant cells [44]. We consistently found that the transduction of trisomy 8 was sufficient to change chromatin accessibility and conformations in HSC, leading to the aberrant activation of TFs, including Runx1. Notably, the present study revealed that trisomy 8 markedly increased the expression of Uty/Kdm6c and, in turn, activated the transcription of genes involved in stem cell and cancer-related pathways. Previous studies revealed that trisomy 8 was associated with a shorter median survival in both male and female MDS patients [10, 23], suggesting the tumor-promoting role of trisomy 8 in MDS regardless of sex. The mechanisms by which trisomy 8 drives the development of MDS in females independent of the Y chromosome have yet to be elucidated. Nevertheless, trisomy 8 reduced the self-renewal capacity of HSC and impaired the hematopoiesis, while the deletion of the Runx1 gene resulted in a pre-MDS state of trisomy 8 HSC, accompanied by the enhanced function of Uty, suggesting a route for the development of trisomy 8 MDS in men.

The region on the Y chromosome included the Kdm5d, Eif2s3y, Uty/Kdm6c, and Ddx3y genes. Among these genes, we focused on the Uty gene because Uty has been shown to activate target genes through its H3K27 demethylation-dependent and -independent functions [45]. We identified UTY-binding regions in human trisomy leukemic cells showing the enrichment of promoters and enhancers, which significantly overlapped not only with RUNX1-binding peaks, but also with H3K4me3 and H3K27ac modification peaks in normal human HSPC, supporting the UTY-dependent transcriptional activation of genes in blood cells. Murine trisomy 8 HSC significantly activated these UTY-target genes, including leukemia-related genes. In addition, the knockdown of UTY clearly repressed the cell growth capacity of SKK-1, a human trisomy 8 MDS cell line, suggesting that the increased expression of UTY contributed to the initiation and maintenance of trisomy 8 MDS. We had focused on the Uty gene on the Y chromosome; however, it is interesting to investigate whether this homologue on the X chromosome, Utx, contributes to the pathogenesis of trisomy 8 MDS in both male and female patients. The Uty gene was previously shown to inhibit the progression of AML in the absence of Utx in mouse and human models [46], suggesting the functional redundancy between the Uty and Utx genes in the context of AML. Although the expression levels of UTX were not increased in murine trisomy 8 cells or human MDS cells, further studies are needed to examine the function of UTX in the development of MDS.

Somatic mutations in RUNX1, ASXL1, EZH2 and DNMT3A are significantly more prevalent in human MDS with trisomy 8 than in MDS without trisomy 8 [35, 36]; however, the reasons why trisomy 8 HSC preferentially acquire mutations in these genes remain unknown. RUNX1 TF is also essential for differentiation, and its mutations increase the risk of developing MDS and AML [43, 47]. ASXL1 is an epigenetic modifier that is critical for normal hematopoiesis through its modulation of EZH2/PRC2 functions, and ASXL1 mutants were shown to promote the development of myeloid malignancies via PRC2-dependent and -independent functions [48]. Loss-of-function mutations of EZH2 are often found in patients with myeloid malignancies [2]. In the present study, the expression levels of Ezh2/PRC2 target genes were significantly elevated in trisomy 8 HSC, suggesting that trisomy 8 inhibited the function of Ezh2/PRC2 and increased the expression of target genes, thereby impairing the differentiation of HSC and promoting a pre-malignant state. Transcriptional changes in PRC2-target genes have been suggested to promote the expansion of HSC with loss-of-function mutations in RUNX1, which may result in the development of MDS, based on previous findings showing that a RUNX1 mutant and the loss of Ezh2 resulted in the development of MDS in mice [42, 49]. We demonstrated that the deletion of Runx1 and the extra chromosome 8 reduced the enhanced inflammatory response in HSC and induced a pre-MDS state; however, this combination was not sufficient for the development of MDS, suggesting that additional genetic and epigenomic changes in trisomy 8 HSC are required to develop MDS in mice and humans. Although somatic trisomy 8 is likely a second event following driver genetic mutations in MDS, such as ASXL1 and DNMT3A, the present results suggest that RUNX1 mutations do not occur by chance in patients and mosaic trisomy 8 humans, but may be selected to compensate the impaired self-renewal capacity of trisomy 8 HSC and dampen the enhanced inflammatory response, leading to increasing the fitness in BM and driving the development of MDS.

This study has a number of limitations that need to be addressed. We transduced a human chromosome 8 vector into mouse ES cells to reproduce trisomy 8 because homologous genes are present on multiple chromosomes in mice. Since human genes are mostly regulated in transduced murine cells in the same manner as their mouse homologous genes, our model revealed the molecular mechanisms by which trisomy 8 affects chromatin structures and transcription, other than its gene dosage effect. Although genes in chromosome 8 that are elevated in trisomy 8 cells and account for the pathogenesis of MDS have not been determined by this study, further studies are needed to examine the potential contribution of its gene dosage effects on the phenotype and chromatin structural changes we observed in our model. In addition, the indirect effects of human genes cannot be completely ruled out due to the aberrant expression and/or unpredictable functions of proteins caused by differences in coding sequences and their regulatory regions between mice and humans. A comparative verification with human trisomy 8 cells is considered necessary in the future. In addition, trisomy 8 HSC showed activated inflammatory genes and chromatin structural changes that accumulated in the binding motifs of AP-1 and RUNX TFs. Exogenous factors have been shown to contribute to enhanced inflammatory signaling in MDS; however, an analysis of inflammatory cytokines is unlikely to show apparent elevations in the BM of trisomy 8 mice at the pre-MDS stage. Alternatively, we observed that cytoplasmic DNA originating from the murine genome was increased in trisomy 8 blood cells and embryonic fibroblast cells, which may activate the cGAS-STING pathway to provoke inflammation [50]. Further studies are needed to determine the mechanism of the enhanced systemic inflammation in patients with trisomy 8 MDS, in which MDS stem cells likely evade deleterious effects of the inflammation through the acquisition of additional mutations, as we demonstrated the Runx1 deletion that attenuated the enhanced inflammation in HSC.

Nevertheless, the present study revealed that trisomy 8 altered the chromatin structures in the genome-wide and combinatory manners and dysregulated transcriptional programs in HSC, at least in part, due to the activation of Uty. Therefore, trisomy 8 impaired the functionality of HSC and the hematopoiesis, while trisomy 8 followed by the deletion of the Runx1 gene resulted in a pre-MDS state.
