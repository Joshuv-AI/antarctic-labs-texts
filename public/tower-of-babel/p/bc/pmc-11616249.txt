Factors associated with breastfeeding rates at 1 month of age: Focusing on newborn feeding behaviours in a Japanese maternity hospital
Authors: Aya Tomita, Emi Tahara‐Sasagawa, Kaori Yonezawa, Yuriko Usui, Megumi Haruna
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study investigated the relationship between newborn feeding behaviour and feeding type among month‐old babies and explored maternal and neonatal factors associated with breastfeeding rates at 1 month of age.MethodsThis observational study was conducted in Japan with healthy mothers and full‐term newborns. Newborn feeding behaviours were assessed using the Japanese Infant Breastfeeding Assessment Tool (IBFAT) within approximately 1 day of birth. Obstetric and newborn characteristics, including newborn feeding behaviours, were investigated in relation feeding type (breast milk or mixed milk) at 1 month of age.ResultsThis study included 176 mother–newborn pairs. IBFAT scores were significantly higher in the breast‐milk group (Median (M) = 10.5, Interquartile Ranges (IQR): 9.0–11.0) than in the mixed‐milk group (M = 10.0, IQR: 7.5–11.0) (P = 0.046). Additionally, the sucking pattern of breastfeeding was showing a greater distribution of higher scores in the breast‐milk group (M = 2.0, IQR: 2.0–2.5) than in the mixed‐milk group (M = 2.0, IQR: 1.5–2.5) (P = 0.015). Compared to mothers who received epidural analgesia or synthetic oxytocin, primiparas who did not receive them were more likely to breastfeed (epidural analgesia: (33.3% vs. 65.8%, P = 0.004); synthetic oxytocin: (42.0% vs. 78.6%, P = 0.018)). For multiparas, Apgar and IBFAT scores were associated with feeding type at 1 month of age.ConclusionsThis study found that newborns' effective and sustained sucking within 1 day of birth is an important factor for breastfeeding at 1 month of age. When assessing feeding type, maternal factors such as epidural analgesia should be the focus for primiparas, while neonatal factors such as feeding behaviour should be prioritised for multiparas.Keywords: breastfeeding, breastfeeding status, Infant Breastfeeding Assessment Tool, newborn feeding behaviours

Maternal breast milk is considered the optimal source of nutrition for almost all infants. 1 , 2 It provides maternal immune factors and appetite‐regulating hormones and facilitates the establishment of a gut microbiota unique to breastfed infants. 3 , 4 Furthermore, infants' acute health outcomes are related to breast milk, which contains long‐chain polyunsaturated fatty acids, such as docosahexaenoic and eicosapentaenoic acids; these are essential for infant growth and brain development. 1 , 5 The practice of breastfeeding as frequently as possible, not just exclusive breastfeeding, has these effects on infant growth and health. 6

Given the demonstrated benefits of breastfeeding, the World Health Organisation (WHO) and United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF) recommend starting breastfeeding within 1 h of birth and continuing exclusive breastfeeding for at least 6 months. 7 The Infant Nutrition Survey 2015 by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare in Japan reported that more than 90% of pregnant women intend to breastfeed their babies. 8 However, exclusive breastfeeding rates in Japan were only 51.3% and 54.7% at 1 and 3 months, respectively, in 2015, 8 which do not meet WHO recommendations and breastfeeding preferences for pregnant women in Japan. Appropriate breastfeeding support should be provided to mother‐and‐newborn pairs as early as possible after birth based on their individual breastfeeding needs.

Breastfeeding involves an interactive relationship between the mother and infant, whereby the mother recognises and responds to the infant's hunger and feeding cues. 7 It is important for breast milk secretion and the continuation of breastfeeding that mothers acquire breastfeeding techniques and have the intention to sustain breastfeeding. Newborns also play an important role in breastfeeding. 9 , 10 While many studies have already identified maternal factors that affect breastfeeding rates, there has been less focus on factors influencing breastfeeding rates for healthy full‐term newborns. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 The first few hours and days of a newborn's life are especially critical for providing mothers with the support they need for successful breastfeeding according to the newborn's instinctive feeding behaviours. 7 An Infant Breastfeeding Assessment Tool (IBFAT) of scales assesses the feeding behaviour of newborns, focusing on primitive reflexes. 18 The IBFAT comprises four items: (i) infant readiness to feed or arousability, (ii) rooting reflex, (iii) time latching on and (iv) sucking pattern. The score for each item ranges from 0 (low) to 3 (high). The total score ranges from 0 to 12, with higher scores indicating more vigorous and effective suckling. 18 The IBFAT also has a Japanese version, and its reliability and validity have been verified. 19 Assessing the newborn's feeding behaviours properly using an assessment tool and supporting breastfeeding practices may contribute to successful breastfeeding and extending the duration of breastfeeding. 20 Prior to the present study, we investigated factors associated with the feeding behaviours of newborns on the first day of birth using the IBFAT. 21 Based on the factors associated with newborn feeding behaviours that were identified, we thought it necessary to continue to further investigate how feeding behaviours are related to breastfeeding.

The study (i) investigated whether newborn feeding behaviours on the first day of birth were associated with breastfeeding rates at 1 month of age using the Japanese version of the IBFAT and (ii) explored the maternal and neonatal factors, including medical intervention, associated with breastfeeding rates at 1 month of age.

Methods

Study design and participants

This observational study was part of a prospective study conducted between June 2021 and October 2022 at the Artemis Women’s Hospital in Tokyo, Japan. This hospital promotes breastfeeding but is not certified as baby‐friendly hospital. Women who visited the hospital for prenatal checkups after 30 weeks or those who were admitted for delivery were recruited. The inclusion criteria were Japanese‐speaking women aged ≥18 years at the beginning of the study who wanted to breastfeed their babies for at least 1 month. Mothers and their newborns with multiple and caesarean births, newborns <2.5 kg, or those aged <37 gestational weeks were excluded. Newborns placed in incubators because of unstable respiratory status or other reasons, and whose mothers were unable to directly breastfeed them, were excluded.

Measurements

Newborns' feeding behaviours

We used the Japanese version of the IBFAT, which focuses on infant primitive reflexes, to assess newborn feeding behaviours. 18 , 19 Midwives or nurses at the hospital used the IBFAT to assess newborn feeding behaviours. After breastfeeding during the first 2 h of life, the newborn was kept in the neonatal room, and breastfeeding instruction and rooming‐in began after 10 a.m. the next day. Newborn feeding behaviour was thereby assessed twice during the daytime, between 10 a.m. and 5 p.m., within approximately 1 day of birth. The average values of the two scores were used in the analysis to ensure the accuracy of the newborns' feeding behaviour.

Breastfeeding rates at 1 month of age

Breastfeeding rates was defined based on a previous study: newborns with a breast milk feeding rate of ≥80% were defined as belonging to the ‘breast‐milk’ group, and those with a feeding rate of <80% were defined as the ‘mixed‐milk’ group. 11 , 22 The breastfeeding rate was calculated based on the number of breast milk and formula milk feeding sessions per day determined by the mothers' breastfeeding records. We adopted this definition because when breastfeeding is recommended, mothers are advised to breastfeed as much as possible, if not exclusively.

Data collection

After informed consent was obtained, data on demographics and labour medications were obtained from the medical or nursing records: maternal age, breast milk or mixed milk intention, mode of delivery, duration of labour, postpartum bleeding, with or without epidural analgesia (EDA), dose and duration of epidural fentanyl, with or without synthetic oxytocin, dose and duration of synthetic oxytocin, birth weight, Apgar score (1 and 5 min), umbilical blood gas, with or without skin‐to‐skin contact (SSC), and duration of SSC.

Statistical analyses

A comparison was made between breast‐milk and mixed‐milk groups. With reference to previous studies, 11 , 19 the sample size was calculated using G‐power software to find meaningful differences in breastfeeding rates among the two groups. The minimum sample size required to achieve a power of 0.80, a moderate effect size of 0.50, and a probability level of 0.05 was 67 participants in each group, for 134 participants in total. The breast‐milk and mixed‐milk groups were compared at 1 month of age using the χ 2 test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables because histograms and scatterplots did not suggest a normal distribution. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies (percentages), whereas continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). After presenting the results for all participants, a subgroup analysis was conducted dividing them into primiparas and multiparas.

Two‐tailed P‐values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 28.0 software for Windows (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Graduate School of Medicine, the University of Tokyo (No. 2020425NI). All the participants provided written informed consent.

Results

Of the 187 women approached for consent at the obstetric outpatient and delivery wards before delivery, 7 declined to participate, so 180 participated in this study. Ultimately, 176 women were included in the analysis because one infant was born prematurely, and 3 women underwent a caesarean section. Details of the flowchart can be found in the paper about factors associated with the IBFAT. 21

Characteristics and IBFAT scores according to breastfeeding rates at 1 month of age (all participants)

Table 1 shows the characteristics and IBFAT scores by breastfeeding rates for all participants at 1 month of age. The variables that showed statistical significance between the breast‐milk and mixed‐milk groups were parity, breast milk or mixed milk intention, mode of delivery, Apgar score at 5 min, and IBFAT scores. IBFAT total scores were higher in the breast‐milk group (Median (M) = 10.5, IQR: 9.0–11.0) than in the mixed‐milk group (M = 10.0, IQR: 7.5–11.0) (P = 0.046). Additionally, the medians and IQRs of the sucking pattern (fourth item) on the IBFAT items were 2.0 (IQR: 2.0–2.5) and 2.0 (IQR: 1.5–2.5) for the breast‐milk and mixed‐milk group, respectively. The distribution of histograms for IBFAT scores was slightly different between the two groups, with the mixed‐group showing a greater distribution of lower scores than the breast‐milk group (P = 0.015). However, the difference between the two groups based on IBFAT scores was not considered clinically significant. Multiparas (74.2% (69/93)) were more likely than primiparas to breastfeed at 1 month of age (48.2% (40/83)) (P < 0.001).

Breastfeeding rates differed significantly between primiparas and multiparas. Therefore, we analysed primiparas and multiparas separately. Table 2 shows the characteristics and IBFAT scores according to breastfeeding rates for primiparas at 1 month of age. No significant differences in the IBFAT scores were found between the breast‐milk and mixed‐milk groups (P = 0.819). Characteristics and IBFAT scores according to breastfeeding rates at 1 month of age (multiparas)

Table 2 shows the characteristics and IBFAT scores according to the breastfeeding rates of multiparas at 1 month of age. IBFAT scores were significantly higher in the breast‐milk group (M = 10.5, IQR: 9.0–11.0) than in the mixed‐milk group (M = 8.8, IQR: 6.6–10.5) (P = 0.011). Furthermore, the breast‐milk group had significantly higher scores for the readiness to feed (first item) (M = 3.0, IQR: 2.5–3.0) and sucking pattern (fourth item) (M = 2.0, IQR: 2.0–2.5) on the IBFAT items than for the readiness to feed (M = 2.0, IQR: 1.6–3.0) (P = 0.017) and sucking pattern (M = 2.0, IQR: 1.5–2.0) (P = 0.004) in the mixed‐milk group. Apgar scores were significantly higher with the breast‐milk group at 1 min (M = 9.0, IQR: 8.0–9.0) and 5 min (M = 9.0, IQR: 9.0–9.0) than for the mixed‐milk group at 1 min (M = 8.0, IQR: 8.0–8.8) (P = 0.008) and 5 min (M = 9.0, IQR: 9.0–9.0) (P = 0.006).

Discussion

In this study, the IBFAT score was associated with breastfeeding rates at 1 month of age, indicating that the sucking pattern had a more positive impact on breastfeeding. Other factors related to breastfeeding rates at 1 month of age were parity, intention to breastfeed, mode of delivery, and Apgar score at 5 min. These results are similar to those of previous studies. 10 , 12 , 13 In the analysis stratified by parity, the breastfeeding rates of primiparas at 1 month of age was associated with EDA and synthetic oxytocin administration and the dose and duration of epidural fentanyl but not with the IBFAT score. Conversely, for multiparas, Apgar and IBFAT scores were associated with breastfeeding rates at 1 month of age.

From the perspective of the newborns, Apgar and IBFAT scores were associated with breastfeeding rates, indicating that it is important for breastfeeding rates that newborns exhibit healthy and effective vigorous feeding behaviour after birth. Effective vigorous feeding behaviour means that the newborn spontaneously turns its head to the nipple when placed on the breast and immediately begins sucking. This may be because newborns' effective vigorous feeding behaviours lead to more frequent breastfeeding, stimulating breast milk secretion. Randomised control trials on breastfeeding rates have reported that effective feeding behaviours of newborns are associated with successful breastfeeding, and mothers can be motivated to breastfeed by achieving effective breastfeeding much earlier. 23 A newborn's successful suckling at the breast could shape the mother's perception of whether the newborn is enjoying breastfeeding, which, in turn, may influence her decision to continue breastfeeding. Furthermore, our results showed that the sucking pattern had a more positive impact on breastfeeding, indicating that sustaining effective sucking is particularly important for breastfeeding. If a newborn's sucking is not effectively sustained, the stimulation of the breast is weakened, which does not lead to increased endogenous oxytocin secretion or breast milk production. When newborn sucking is not effectively sustained, it may be necessary to maintain breast milk production with assistance, such as by pumping breast milk.

Regarding the other factors affecting breastfeeding rates at 1 month of age, multiparas tended to breastfeed more successfully than primiparas, as other studies have indicated. 10 , 13 , 15 This may be because multiparous mothers experience less physical damage during childbirth and are better prepared for breastfeeding.

Mothers reporting an intention to breastfeed during pregnancy were more likely to breastfeed at 1 month of age. Breastfeeding mothers may encounter challenges due to a lack of knowledge or support, potentially hindering their ability to maintain breastfeeding motivation. Women who intended to breastfeed were more likely to persist in breastfeeding, even under such difficult circumstances. Providing all mothers with perinatal education and more professional support would help motivate them to breastfeed.

Mothers with instrumental births were less likely to breastfeed almost exclusively at 1 month of age. This finding is consistent with those reported by other authors. 10 , 13 , 24 Women with instrumental vaginal births are more likely to experience physical damage than those with spontaneous vaginal births. Tissue damage from instrumental delivery, perineotomy, or laceration takes time to repair and may delay the initiation of breastfeeding. Mothers with instrumental deliveries must be supported during breastfeeding while their pain is controlled.

For primiparas, the maternal factor, EDA administration, rather than IBFAT scores, was associated with breastfeeding rates at 1 month of age. This is probably because primiparas breastfeed for the first time and it takes longer for them to acquire breastfeeding techniques if their bodies are damaged during delivery. Thus, they could not breastfeed easily because of postpartum physical conditions caused by the medical interventions they received during childbirth. Primiparas with EDA are likely to experience greater pain, fatigue and stress postpartum due to increased medical interventions such as instrumental deliveries. 16 Psychophysical factors in women requiring EDA may also affect breastfeeding outcomes, and they may experience pain and discomfort associated with breastfeeding. 17 Although our previous study has shown that IBFAT scores increased with SSC duration, primiparas with EDA may have difficulty breastfeeding due to the anxiety and discomfort associated with breastfeeding, even if their newborn feeding behaviour increases. Therefore, an alternative pain relief method, such as mindfulness, massage, or warm packs, may be recommended to women who request EDA due to anxiety about labour pain. Additionally, to help prolong breastfeeding, providing breastfeeding education and care both postpartum and during pregnancy is important for coping with anxiety and discomfort associated with breastfeeding.

For multiparas, only the Apgar and IBFAT scores, which are neonatal but not maternal factors, were associated with breastfeeding rates. In our study which examined the factors associated with the IBFAT, IBFAT scores were associated with Apgar scores, gestational weeks, and fentanyl dosage. Therefore, healthy newborns are more likely to have higher IBFAT scores, thereby leading to higher breastfeeding rates for multiparas. This may be because multiparas are better prepared in terms of breastfeeding techniques and milk secretion. If newborns tend to be sleepy and the development of effective feeding behaviour is delayed, it may hinder subsequent breastfeeding in multiparas. Multiparas should prioritise breastfeeding according to the newborn's awakened state and must ensure that the newborn's feeding behaviour is appropriately assessed.

Limitations

This study has three main limitations. First, it was conducted at a single hospital in Japan; thus, the findings may not be generalisable to other hospitals because breastfeeding support and staffing levels vary among hospitals. Second, this study was conducted during the COVID‐19 pandemic; therefore, SSC could not be actively implemented due to staff shortages and restrictions on the partner's presence during childbirth. SSC should be actively conducted because it has many benefits, including increased breastfeeding secretions and enhanced newborn feeding behaviour. 23 Finally, we did not investigate whether IBFAT scores were associated with longer breastfeeding duration, such as breastfeeding rates, at 6 months. Further studies are necessary to investigate the relationship between longer breastfeeding duration and newborn feeding behaviour.

Conclusions

Our study suggests that effective vigorous feeding behaviour is associated with breastfeeding rates at 1 month of age. Sustained effective sucking in newborns is important for successful breastfeeding at 1 month of age. In order to improve breastfeeding among primiparas, equal attention should be paid to both maternal and neonatal factors, and primiparas who had used EDA and synthetic oxytocin should be given extra support. Alternative pain relief options should be considered instead of EDA. For multiparas, the focus must be on the newborns' breastfeeding ability while awake and sustain effective sucking; therefore, prioritising the assessment of the newborn is crucial rather than relying on reports on newborn feeding among multiparas.
