From prestige to solidarity: why WHO collaborating centres must become a political force
Authors: Tiago Correia, Natasha Azzopardi-Muscat
Published: 2026

WHO Collaborating Centres (WHOCCs) are often treated as markers of prestige. In more stable times, that may have seemed enough. It is not enough now.

Global health is being reshaped by war, funding shocks, disinformation, political intimidation, and retreat from multilateralism. In this context, WHOCCs cannot remain a loose collection of respected institutions linked to WHO through largely vertical relationships. They must become an organized force within multilateral public health.

This is not simply an institutional question. It is a political one. When universal norms are attacked and funding is suddenly withdrawn, the effects are immediate: delayed outbreak detection, weaker preparedness, more fragile access to medicines and vaccines, and further erosion of the institutions on which global health depends [1].

Formally, WHOCCs are institutions designated by the WHO Director-General to support the WHO’s work at country, regional, and global levels. There are now >800 centres across >80 countries. That is a substantial infrastructure. Yet much of its collective value remains unrealized. If collaborating centres continue to operate mainly as separate bilateral designations attached to specific workplans and officers, they will remain politically peripheral.

That is why the first Global Forum of WHO Collaborating Centres, held in Lyon in 2026, matters. Its importance lies not in the meeting itself, but in what it made possible. The forum aimed to strengthen collaboration, align priorities, and shape the future role of centres in advancing global health in line with GPW14. More importantly, it suggested that WHOCCs could play a more collective role within a changing global health architecture [2].

WHOCCs do not simply need better coordination. They need a different model. Rather than functioning as isolated nodes, they should be reorganized into clustered communities of practice built around thematic areas, shared responsibilities, and horizontal solidarity. The language may sound managerial, but the point is political and practical [3]. What WHO needs is not another structure in which initiative is drawn upwards and stalled at the top. It needs stronger multilateralism built from below.

A clustered model would allow WHOCCs to work across institutions and regions rather than simply through reporting lines. It would turn isolated excellence into shared capability. Above all, it would allow WHO to mobilize not only expertise, but solidarity.

If WHOCCs are to matter politically, they must be willing to stand with centres and countries under strain: those facing conflict, fiscal coercion, attacks on science, collapsing trust, political intimidation, and failing public institutions [4]. It is not enough for centres in more stable and better-resourced settings to publish papers, host meetings, and enjoy symbolic proximity to the WHO. A serious WHOCC community must ask a harder question: what do stronger centres owe those operating under pressure?

This question is no longer abstract, given that the WHO has reported major disruption in country-level functions after cuts in official development assistance. Public health has also become a battleground for politicized narratives, while misinformation and disinformation continue to weaken trust and institutional legitimacy. In fragile and conflict-affected settings, technical weakness and political vulnerability reinforce each other. These are precisely the conditions in which a stronger WHOCC community should be able to mobilize peer support, training, visibility, rapid technical assistance, and collective advocacy across borders.

Europe has a particular responsibility here. Not because it should dominate the future of WHOCCs, but because it hosts a large share of them and because it knows, or should know, the consequences of allowing universal norms to erode. Europe cannot defend multilateralism in speeches while remaining comfortable with a WHOCC system that is still too elitist, too cautious, and too unequal in practice. Its role should be to help organize reciprocity across regions.

Thematic clustering is where this ambition becomes concrete. A health workforce cluster could connect labour market intelligence, planning tools, policy design, and training. A preparedness cluster could sustain cooperation on surveillance, emergency operations, and resilient implementation. A cluster on trust, communication, and misinformation could help public health move beyond exhausted, top-down messaging. A One Health cluster could provide an operational platform for collaboration across human, animal, and environmental health.

WHO already recognizes networks of collaborating centres as a way to strengthen relationships, use expertise more effectively, and build capacity. The next step is to give that logic more substance through clearer purpose, greater density, and stronger political direction.

However, the obstacles are real. Bureaucratic inertia may protect familiar vertical arrangements. Competition between institutions can reduce willingness to share methods, credit, and visibility. Some governments may welcome the prestige of designation while resisting the obligations that should come with it. Inequalities in resources between centres can make the language of community ring hollow unless mutual support is built into the model itself. WHO, too, may face constraints. Solidarity is easy to endorse in principle. It becomes harder when it requires action in politically sensitive settings. Support for a centre or country under pressure may no longer be read as technical cooperation alone. It may be interpreted as political positioning. That risk is real. But it is not a reason to avoid the issue. It is a reason to confront it honestly.

There is also a more familiar danger: that clustering becomes another layer of meetings, branding, and procedural activity. Global health already has too much of that. The point is not to produce another polite architecture of declarations. It is to organize capacity so that centres can act together when systems come under strain.

Responsibilities therefore need to be assigned clearly. WHO headquarters should streamline designation and redesignation and provide the coordination backbone for clustering through clearer mandates, shared platforms, cross-cluster priorities, and mechanisms for identifying when centres or countries are under acute strain and need organized support. Regional offices should deepen professional partnerships and use WHOCCs more consciously as strategic infrastructures for regional action. Governments can do more to support collaborating centres as part of a broader commitment to national and regional public health capacity. Host institutions must also change their understanding of designation. WHOCC status cannot remain mainly a branding asset. It should carry practical duties: data sharing, training, mentorship, peer support, technical assistance, and contribution to collective goods.

Funding arrangements must change too. Shared financing mechanisms that pool resources to support joint functions across clusters, rather than isolated projects alone, could help turn this agenda into durable practice.

But ultimately the issue is not only organizational. It is normative. If collaborating centres are serious about WHO’s mission, they must visibly embody the principles global health now most needs to defend: universality, reciprocity, shared responsibility, and practical solidarity. That means standing with centres and countries under pressure, not merely admiring those already thriving. It means treating knowledge as something to circulate, not hoard. And it means accepting a difficult truth: when one part of the system is politically weakened, the rest are not spectators. They are implicated.

Lyon should mark a turning point. WHOCCs can remain prestigious affiliates, respected but politically peripheral. Or they can become part of the organized infrastructure that multilateral public health now urgently needs. The second path is harder. It is also the only serious one.
