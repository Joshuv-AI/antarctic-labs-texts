Characterizing high cognitive performance in persons with mood disorders and their unaffected relatives: associations with emotional cognition, functioning, and clinical outcomes
Authors: Hanne Lie Kjaerstad, Bjørn Ole Barkholt Nordseth, Maj Vinberg, Lars Vedel Kessing, Kamilla Woznica Miskowiak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMood disorders are characterized by considerable cognitive heterogeneity. However, little is known about high cognitive performance and its associations with relevant clinical outcomes. High cognitive performance in persons with mood disorders and high-risk unaffected relatives (UR) may be an expression of resilience to illness-related neuropathology.MethodsCross-sectional data from persons with mood disorders (n = 212), unaffected relatives (UR) (n = 96), and healthy controls (HC) (n = 152) were pooled from two cohort studies, during which participants completed neuropsychological test batteries comprising both non-emotional and emotional cognition. Participants were grouped into ‘high’ or ‘normal cognitive performance’ subgroups based on their global cognitive performance. Groups were compared to investigate high-performing persons with mood disorders and relatives, respectively, in demographic and clinical variables and emotional cognition.ResultsResulting subgroups were persons with mood disorders with high (P-HP = 67) and normal cognitive performance (P-NP = 135), UR with high (UR-HP = 41) and normal cognitive performance (UR-NP = 52) and HC with high (HC-HP = 86) and normal cognitive performance (HC-NP = 66). P-HP were characterized by fewer experiences of physical abuse, fewer mood episodes and improved functioning compared to P-NP. Both P-HP and UR-HP experienced more overall childhood trauma, compared to HC-HP. All cognitively high-performing subgroups exhibited faster recognition of emotional faces compared to their corresponding normally performing group.ConclusionHigh cognitive performance was associated with favorable clinical outcomes and improved functioning in persons with mood disorders and high-risk UR. Given this association, above-average enhancement of cognition beyond normalization may aid functional recovery.Keywords: Mood disorders, Cognition, Psychosocial functioning, Emotional cognition, Childhood trauma

Introduction

Mood disorders, including major depressive disorder (MDD) and bipolar disorder (BD), are often debilitating psychiatric disorders, characterized by recurrent episodes of depression and/or (hypo)mania (Belmaker and Agam 2008; Carvalho et al. 2020). Affecting more than 300 million persons globally (Ferrari et al. 2022), mood disorders are among the most prevalent mental disorders and constitute a significant socioeconomic burden (Oliva et al. 2025). In addition to mood symptoms, approximately half of persons with mood disorders also exhibit cognitive impairments across several domains including attention, verbal memory, and executive functions (Bourne et al. 2013; Van Rheenen and Rossell 2014), during both acute mood episodes and periods of remission (Kurtz and Gerraty 2009). Studies have identified cognitive impairment to be associated with pronounced familial risk, as unaffected first-degree relatives (UR) may exhibit milder but similar patterns of cognitive impairment, particularly persistent across executive functions, over time (Bora 2017; Kjærstad et al. 2022). Cognitive symptoms are strongly associated with poorer quality of life and decreased functioning (Tse et al. 2014), contributing significantly to the overall socioeconomic burden of mood disorders and constituting an essential target for research and intervention (Miskowiak et al. 2017).

The identification of distinct neurocognitive subgroups across mood disorders has so far supported associations between cognitive impairment and poorer clinical outcomes (Burdick et al. 2014; Cotrena et al. 2016; Jensen et al. 2016; Pu et al. 2018). However, the clinical correlates of high cognitive performance in mood disorders remain largely unexplored. Cluster-analytical studies investigating cognitive heterogeneity in mood disorders consistently identify a ‘cognitively intact’ subgroup (40–50% and 30–40% of persons with MDD and BD, respectively) who present with cognitive performance comparable to healthy controls (HC) (Pu et al. 2018; Russo et al. 2017), and occasionally superior performance to HC within selected domains, such as social cognition and verbal fluency (Burdick et al. 2014; Kjærstad et al. 2021). ‘Cognitively intact’ persons with mood disorders are typically characterized by fewer mood symptoms, episodes, and hospitalizations (Burdick et al. 2014; Rabelo-da-Ponte et al. 2022), higher premorbid IQ, and improved psychosocial functioning compared to cognitively impaired persons with mood disorders (Lima et al. 2019; Montejo et al. 2022; Pu et al. 2018). Some studies even identify a subgroup characterized by above-average cognition (Bora et al. 2016; Chakrabarty et al. 2021; Jiménez et al. 2017; Roux et al. 2017), which, although this group is typically modest in size (13–25%), supports a hypothetical neurocognitive subgroup of persons with mood disorders exhibiting high cognitive performance.

Research in healthy individuals has shown that high cognitive performance in aging populations is associated with greater thickness and volume of cerebral cortex, anterior cingulate cortex and hippocampal structures, as well as decreased age-related atrophy (Dekhtyar et al. 2017; Fjell et al. 2006; Harrison et al. 2012), suggesting a relationship between overall structural preservation of the cortex and cognitive performance. Although studies on mood disorders have not directly assessed clinical correlates of high cognitive functioning, a substantial proportion of persons with mood disorders show increased accelerated brain aging, as well as increased risk of dementia, suggesting an important relationship between structural preservation and overall functioning in mood disorders (Fries et al. 2020). Furthermore, several studies indicate an association between global cortical preservation and neuronal plasticity (Borelli et al. 2018; Heuninckx et al. 2008). Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), a growth factor essential for neuronal development and synaptic connectivity has been identified as a salient marker of neuroplasticity in persons with mood disorders and healthy populations (Madsen et al. 2024), linking higher levels of BDNF to both improved cognition and less severe mood symptoms across mood disorders (Cavaleri et al. 2023; Fernandes et al. 2015; Shimizu et al. 2003). Higher levels of BDNF are thus hypothesized, through interaction with the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis (Carlino et al. 2013; Watson and Mackin 2006), to be a protective factor against accelerated brain aging and cognitive dysfunction, underlining the importance of neurocognitive functioning in mood disorders (Schmidt et al. 2011).

Together, these findings suggest high cognitive performance as a potential resilience marker in mood disorders. Nevertheless, no study in MDD or BD has yet examined possible underlying factors of high cognitive performance, or its associations with emotional cognition and childhood trauma, factors presumed to be heavily implicated in cognitive and psychosocial dysfunction in mood disorders (Barczyk et al. 2023; Leppänen 2006). This area of investigation may be central to the mapping of favorable illness trajectory in mood disorders and its associations with cognition, which may have implications for the utilization of familial risk- and resilience markers in the study and design of prophylactic interventions. Moreover, the findings may guide development of pro-cognitive treatments, that could alleviate cognitive impairment and improve overall clinical progression in persons with mood disorders (Miskowiak et al., 2022b).

The present study is part of a large clinical cohort seeking to identify reliable biomarkers of cognition in mood disorders. The aims of the present study were twofold. First, we aimed to investigate what characterized cognitively high-performing persons with mood disorders in terms of demographic and clinical variables, emotional cognition, and childhood trauma, to identify mechanisms supporting cognitive resilience. Specifically, we aimed to compare cognitively high-performing persons with mood disorders (P-HP) with (I) normally performing persons with mood disorders (P-NP) and (II) high-performing healthy controls (HC-HP), to elucidate whether differences can be ascribed to high cognitive functioning or diagnosis, respectively. Secondly, we aimed to investigate whether the findings could be replicated in high-risk unaffected relatives by comparing high-performing UR (UR-HP) with (III) UR with normal cognitive performance (UR-NP) and (IV) high-performing HC (HC-HP). Lastly, to elucidate potential findings and account for baseline contributions of high cognitive performance, (V) high-performing HC (HC-HP) were compared with HC with normal cognitive performance (HC-NP).

We hypothesized that (1) cognitively high-performing persons with mood disorders would be characterized by more favorable clinical characteristics, including less severe mood symptoms and higher psychosocial functioning, as well as improved emotional cognition and less childhood trauma compared to normally performing persons with mood disorders - but less favorable characteristics compared to high-performing HC - and (2) that high-performing UR would exhibit patterns of psychosocial functioning, emotional cognition and childhood trauma similar to high-performing affected relatives.

Experimental procedures

Study design

Cross-sectional neurocognitive data was pooled from two studies conducted at Psychiatric Centre Copenhagen between 2014 and 2022: the Bipolar Illness Onset (BIO) study (Kessing et al. 2017) and the Neurocognition and Emotion in Affective Disorders (NEAD) study (Ottesen et al. 2018), resulting in a total sample of N = 763 participants: 380 persons with mood disorders, 158 first-degree UR and 225 HC. The data was pooled on the basis of shared participant samples, recruitment criteria, and substantial overlap in cognitive battery including both non-emotional and emotional paradigms. Finally, data from both studies were collected at the Copenhagen Affective Disorder research Centre (CADIC) within the same timeframe.

Both studies were approved by the Regional Ethics Committee (BIO: H-7-2014-007; NEAD: H-3-2014-003) and The Danish Data Protection Agency Capital Region, Denmark (RHP-2015-023; 2014-331-0751). Written informed consent was collected from all participants. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association (Helsinki Declaration of 1975; 2008).

Participant recruitment and screening

Persons with newly diagnosed BD from the BIO-study were recruited from Copenhagen Clinic for Affective Disorders, Psychiatric Centre Copenhagen (Kessing et al. 2017). Affected monozygotic twins from the NEAD study were recruited from the Danish Twin Registry, the Danish Psychiatric Central Research Register and the Danish Civil Registration System (Ottesen et al. 2018). Inclusion criteria for persons with mood disorders were 15–65 years of age, an ICD-10 (World Health Organization, 1993) diagnosis of MDD or BD verified with the Schedules for Clinical Assessment in Neuropsychiatry (SCAN) (Wing et al. 1990), and full or partial remission (i.e. Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS-17) (Hamilton,1960) and Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS) (Young et al. 1978) score ≤ 14).

Unaffected relatives comprised offspring and siblings of persons with BD from the BIO-study and monozygotic twins discordant for MDD or BD from the NEAD-study. UR were between 15 and 40 years of age and had no personal history of mood disorders or schizophrenia spectrum disorders ascertained with the SCAN.

Age-and-sex matched HC were recruited via the Copenhagen University Hospital Blood Bank (BIO) or as healthy monozygotic twins via the Danish Twin Registry (NEAD). Exclusion criteria for HC were a personal or first-degree relative with treatment-required psychiatric disorder or current substance abuse. Exclusion criteria for all participants were current mood episodes (< 14 on HDRS-17 or YMRS), severe somatic illness, brain injury, pregnancy, or current substance abuse.

Measures

Neurocognition

Overlapping non-emotional cognitive tests for both studies included the Danish Adult Reading Task (DART) (Nelson and O’Connell 1978), to estimate premorbid verbal intelligence, and the Trail Making Test Parts A & B (TMT-A; TMT-B) (Army Individual Test Battery 1944). Additionally, the BIO-study assessed neurocognition with the Rapid Visual Information Processing (RVP) test and Spatial Working Memory (SWM) test from the Cambridge Neuropsychological Test Automated Battery (CANTAB), Coding and Digit Span from the Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS) (Randolph et al. 1998), the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT) (Schmidt 1996), Verbal Fluency (letters ‘S’+‘D’) (Borkowski et al. 1967), and the Letter-Number Sequencing test from the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS)-III (Wechsler 1997). The NEAD-study implemented the Screen of Cognitive Impairment in Psychiatry (SCIP-D) (Purdon 2005) in the assessment of corresponding neurocognitive domains.

Emotional cognition

Processing of emotional facial expressions was assessed with the Facial Expression Recognition Task (FERT). Participants viewed pictures of faces showing one of six basic emotions: anger, disgust, fear happiness, sadness or surprise, morphed between neutral face (0%) and full emotion (100%) (Ekman 1976). Participants viewed 250 randomly ordered pictures of faces, composing four pictures of each emotion at varying intensity and were instructed to indicate which emotion was shown (Harmer et al. 2004).

Emotion regulation and reactivity were assessed with the Social Scenarios Task (Goldin et al. 2009; Kjærstad et al. 2016), in which participants were presented with short written descriptions of negative or positive social scenarios, accompanied by self-beliefs statements. Each scenario comprised 11 descriptive sentences and 10 self-beliefs followed by 10 emotion ratings, during which participants were instructed to evaluate their discomfort/sadness or pleasure/happiness. During each scenario, participants were asked to either react naturally or dampen their emotional responses.

Psychosocial functioning

Overall functioning was measured using the Functioning Assessment Short Test (FAST) (Rosa et al. 2007), a scale assessing six domains of functioning: autonomy, occupational functioning, cognitive functioning, financial issues, interpersonal relationships and leisure time.

Childhood trauma

Participants’ history of childhood trauma was assessed with the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ), a scale assessing the frequency of five subsets of childhood trauma: sexual abuse, physical abuse, emotional abuse, physical neglect and emotional neglect (Bernstein et al. 2003).

Statistical analysis

Preprocessing of cognitive data

Participants’ raw scores on neuropsychological tests were standardized into z-scores (M = 0, SD = 1) based on HC’s score using the formula: (test score - HC test M) / HC test SD. In order to account for directionality of measurements, the z-scores for TMT-A, TMT-B, RVP (latency), and SWM (strategy; between-errors) were inverted so that poorer performance reflect lower scores. To eliminate outliers, extreme z-scores (> 4SD ± HC’s M) were truncated to z = ± 4.0. Distinct non-emotional neurocognitive domains were established using averaged z-scores: (i) attention and psychomotor speed (TMT-A, RVP (accuracy; latency), Coding, Digit Span Forward), (ii) verbal learning (RAVLT (trial IV; trial VI), recognition, and delayed recall), (iii) working memory (WM) and executive function (TMT-B, SWM (strategy; between-errors), Letter-Number sequencing), and (iv) verbal fluency (letters ‘S’ + ‘D’). A global cognition composite z-score was derived from averaging z-scores of every non-emotional neurocognitive domain with equal weighting, consistent with prior studies deriving global cognitive performance from multi-domain z-score composites, to provide a transparent and domain-general summary measure.

For recognition of emotional facial expressions, reaction time (RT) was log-transformed prior to analysis, while discrimination accuracy (d’) was measured using the formula provided by Corwin (1994): ((number of hits + 0.5) / (number of targets + 1) - ((number of false alarms + 0.5) / (number of distractors + 1). Measures of overall RT and accuracy (d’) of emotional facial expression recognition were derived from averaging participants’ responses to every facial expression. Downregulation of emotion was analyzed for both positive and negative conditions in the social scenarios task and was measured by subtracting emotion ratings of the ‘dampen’ conditions from emotion ratings of the ‘react’ conditions.

Classification of participants

Participants were divided into one of two cognitive subgroups based on their performance on the global cognition composite measure. Participants scoring between 0 and − 0.5 SD below the norm were characterized as exhibiting ‘normal cognitive performance’. These thresholds for the subgroups were chosen on the basis of recommendations by the International Society for Bipolar Disorder (ISBD) targeting cognition task force (Miskowiak et al. 2017 b). The ISBD recommends a score of ≥ 0.5 SD below the norm as the threshold for cognitive impairment, as a conservative threshold may detect even subtle difficulties.

Participants scoring ≥ 0.2 SD above the norm were characterized as exhibiting ‘high cognitive performance’. While there is presently no broadly accepted formal conceptualization of high cognitive performance, a positive change of ≥ 0.2SD has been recommended as a marker of clinically relevant cognitive improvement associated with functional outcomes in persons with mood disorders (Miskowiak et al. 2017 b). This threshold thus serves as an empirically anchored cutoff for defining subgroups of participants with correspondingly high cognitive performance.

Cognitive test scores for persons with mood disorders typically reflect greater variability towards impairment when compared to HC, a pattern that is also evident in the present sample (see Fig. 1). The applied thresholds were chosen to reflect normal and high cognitive performance, specifically within these groups, aiming to account for this shift towards impairment, and to establish categories that are both intuitively interpretable for clinicians and comparable to categories utilized in the surrounding literature. Participants scoring either ≥ 0.5 SD below or between 0 and ≤ 0.2SD above the norm were not included in the analyses.

Group comparisons

Statistical significance was set to p<.05 (two-tailed). Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to explore data normality distribution. Demographic, clinical, and cognitive variables were compared between groups using Mann-Whitney U or independent samples t-tests, respectively, for continuous variables, while chi-square was used for categorical variables. In order to identify characteristics of cognitively high-performing persons with mood disorders (aim 1), the following groups were compared: (I) P-HP vs. P-NP and (II) P-HP vs. HC-HP, respectively. To identify characteristics of cognitively high-performing UR (aim 2), comparisons were (III) UR-HP vs. UR-NP and (IV) UR-HP vs. HC-HP, respectively. Finally, (V) HC-HP vs. HC-NP were compared. Due to the exploratory nature and design of the study, results were not adjusted for multiple comparisons. In order to account for a possible adverse effect of subsyndromal symptoms on cognition, results were adjusted for subsyndromal mania and depression symptoms (YMRS- and HDRS-17 total scores as covariates). Post-hoc Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to investigate associations between high cognitive performance and the demographic, clinical, and cognitive variables that were found to characterize cognitively high-performing persons with mood disorders or UR, respectively. To address potential age-related heterogeneity in risk-status within the UR groups, we conducted a sensitivity analysis restricted to URs aged ≥ 30 years as a conservative proxy for having passed the typical mood disorder onset window. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 25.

Results

Participants

The pooled sample included data from N = 763 participants: 380 persons with mood disorders, 158 UR and 225 age- and sex-matched HC. Of these, 6 UR were excluded due to MDD diagnosis. Participants were divided into either a ‘high’ or ‘normal’ cognitive subgroup of their respective superordinate group, resulting in a final total of N = 447 participants (post-exclusion) of which n = 67 persons with mood disorders and high cognitive performance (P-HP), n = 135 persons with mood disorders and normal cognitive performance (P-NP), n = 41 UR with high cognitive performance (UR-HP), n = 52 UR with normal cognitive performance (UR-NP), n = 86 HC with high cognitive performance (HC-HP), and n = 66 HC with normal cognitive performance (HC-NP) (Table 1).

Aim 1: Characterizing high cognitive functioning in persons with mood disorders

Persons with mood disorders and high cognitive performance (P-HP) vs. persons with mood disorders and normal cognitive performance (P-NP)

Demographic and clinical variables

P-HP displayed less subsyndromal depressive symptoms (p=.004) and greater premorbid IQ (p<.001) than P-NP. P-HP had also experienced fewer episodes of depression, hypomania and total number of mood episodes (ps≤0.02) compared to P-NP. Additionally, P-HP were characterized by less impairments in psychosocial functioning within all domains (lower scores on FAST scores) (ps≤0.02) with the exceptions of domains of leisure and occupational functioning (ps≥0.87). There were no significant differences between P-HP and P-NP in sex, age years of education, subsyndromal manic symptoms, comorbid anxiety disorders, illness duration, hospitalizations, suicide attempts, or medication use (ps≥0.06) (Table 1).

Cognitive performance

With regards to neurocognition, P-HP performed better than P-NP in global cognition and all cognitive subdomains (ps<0.001), which remained significant after adjusting for subsyndromal depressive symptoms and IQ, respectively (ps<0.001) (Table 2).

With regards to emotional cognition, P-HP exhibited lower emotional reactivity in neutral social scenarios (p=.03). Discussion

This study first aimed to characterize high cognitive performance in persons with mood disorders and investigated associations with demographic and clinical variables, emotional cognition, and childhood trauma. In line with our first hypothesis, P-HP exhibited more favorable clinical and functional outcomes compared to P-NP. Specifically, P-HP presented with fewer depressive symptoms, experienced fewer mood episodes, and were characterized by better overall functioning. Regarding emotional cognition, P-HP were more successful at downregulating negative emotions in social scenarios and showed both faster responses and greater accuracy during emotional facial expression recognition compared to P-NP. Compared to HC-HP, P-HP were characterized by poorer functioning but higher verbal fluency performance. When comparing to HC-HP, P-HP reported more overall as well as specific childhood trauma, except for sexual abuse or physical neglect. They also reported less physical abuse than P-NP. Our secondary aim was to characterize high cognitive functioning in high-risk unaffected relatives. UR-HP were characterized by older age than both UR-NP and HC-HP, as well as more years of education and higher IQ than UR-NP. Supporting our second hypothesis, UR-HP also showed both faster recognition and greater accuracy of emotional facial expressions compared to UR-NP, with results remaining significant following sensitivity analyses restricted to URs ≥ 30 years. Regarding childhood trauma, UR-HP reported an overtly similar pattern of overall and specific childhood trauma when compared to HC-HP. UR-HP were also characterized by poorer functioning than HC-HP.

No previous study has investigated high cognitive performance and associated clinical characteristics in persons with mood disorders using predefined cutoffs. Nevertheless, our findings align with previous studies on neurocognitive heterogeneity identifying subgroups of cognitively intact or high-performing persons with mood disorders who are characterized by fewer mood symptoms and episodes (Burdick et al. 2014; Martin et al. 2020; Rabelo-da-Ponte et al. 2022) and improved functioning (Roux et al. 2017) when compared to cognitively impaired or low-performing persons with mood disorders. Notably, the improved functioning observed in P-HP compared to P-NP suggests that the principal relationship between cognition and functioning extends beyond impairment and into non-impaired populations. Correspondingly, the scope of treatment could thus extend beyond alleviating cognitive symptoms, to actively enhancing cognitive performance, potentially leading to improved daily functioning and paving a way towards more comprehensive functional recovery. Taken together, engaging patients in active cognitive training could be a valuable approach to achieving this outcome, underlining the importance of the investigation and development of pro-cognitive treatment on functional recovery.

Interestingly, participants in every cognitively high-performing group (P-HP, UR-HP, HC-HP) exhibited faster recognition of emotional faces compared to their corresponding normally performing group (P-NP, UR-NP, HC-NP), with high-performing persons with mood disorders and UR additionally exhibiting greater accuracy. This association between improved emotional facial expression recognition and high cognitive performance may reflect a more comprehensive relationship between non-emotional and emotional cognitive processes. While there is currently no agreement on a particular directionality between the two, non-emotional neurocognitive processes may constitute a prerequisite for several emotional cognitive processes (Van Rheenen and Rossell 2016). Studies in persons with BD suggest that impairments in theory of mind and emotional facial expression recognition may be mediated by non-emotional neurocognitive impairment (Bora et al. 2005; Konstantakopoulos et al. 2020; Varo et al. 2019). Studies additionally show persons with MDD presenting with both heightened vigilance towards negative stimuli and poorer performance in otherwise non-emotional neurocognitive tasks, showing no direct link between depressive symptom severity and non-emotional cognitive performance (Dam et al. 2021). While the observed negative bias itself was not entirely separable from depressive symptoms, this underlines the complex interrelatedness of the two domains (Roiser and Sahakian 2013). Indeed, persons with MDD and BD exhibit hypoactivity in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex during both emotional and non-emotional cognitive tasks (Kjærstad et al. 2024; Siegle et al. 2007; Zarp Petersen et al. 2022), suggesting overlapping neural circuits implicated in both emotional and non-emotional cognitive processing. The present findings suggests this relationship may persist beyond impairment.

Both P-HP and UR-HP reported more overall childhood trauma compared to HC-HP, underlining childhood trauma as a risk factor of mood disorders in general (Barczyk et al. 2023). Nevertheless, the less reported physical abuse of P-HP compared to P-NP aligns with previous findings linking physical abuse to the severity of mood symptoms (Kullberg et al. 2021) and cognitive impairments in mood disorders (Aas et al. 2012; Miskowiak et al. 2023). Both P-HP and UR-HP were comparable to HC-HP within domains of physical neglect and sexual abuse – subtypes of childhood trauma associated with cognitive and functional impairments in mood disorders (Aguiar et al. 2023). However, the prevalence of childhood trauma in the present sample was low and the limited findings do not furnish any solid interpretations. Previous studies have found subgroups of cognitively high-performing persons with BD to report fewer experiences of overall childhood trauma compared to cognitively impaired subgroups (Chakrabarty et al. 2021; Ehrlich et al. 2022; Jiménez et al. 2017), warranting future investigations of distinct subtypes of childhood trauma, that may be particularly detrimental to optimal cognitive development. These findings further emphasize the importance of early prophylactic interventions in vulnerable families, as cognition has been consistently identified as a key moderator of prognosis both in persons with mood disorders and high-risk groups (Miskowiak et al. 2022a; Vinberg et al. 2013).

As UR-HP largely differed from HC within the same parameters as did P-HP, the findings do not furnish an interpretation of any dissociable resilience markers. The findings did however show UR-HP to be uniquely characterized by older age than both UR-NP and HC-HP, as well as having undergone more years of education than UR-NP. Given previous studies finding high-risk relatives to be characterized by both poorer cognitive performance and fewer years of education compared to healthy controls, higher age and education may particularly benefit UR-HP as protective factors against the negative effect of illness on cognition (Christensen et al. 2006; Ponsoni et al. 2020). Indeed, given the relatively high age of the UR-HP in the present study (mean 37 ± 7), it is likely that this group of high-risk relatives has surpassed the threshold of likely age of illness onset (Solmi et al. 2022). While this study, given its cross-sectional design and choice of comparative analyses, does not explain why the two UR groups remain healthy despite heightened familial risk, UR-HP’s intact emotional cognition may be an expression of familial resilience. Indeed, whereas their high-performing affected counterparts exhibited heightened emotional reactivity in negative scenarios and were less successful at downregulating their emotions in positive scenarios compared to controls, these difficulties were driven by subsyndromal symptoms in persons with mood disorders and - given their absence in relatives - are likely a feature of the mood disorder diagnosis.

A strength of this study was the relatively large sample of participants, encompassing persons with mood disorders, unaffected high-risk relatives, and controls, and that participants were in full/partial remission, minimizing the potential risk for confounding effects of mood symptoms on test performance. Another strength was the comprehensive neuropsychological battery assessing both non-emotional and emotional neurocognition. Limitations include the cross-sectional design of the study, hindering causal inferences of potential associations between high cognitive performance and long-term illness course. Furthermore, although the non-emotional neurocognitive battery was extensive, the two studies from which the data was pooled differed on the particular neuropsychological tests used for assessment, impeding direct comparison of performance on the specific non-emotional cognitive measurements. Moreover, the selected tasks for assessment of emotional cognition focused on a limited range of domains and thus did not assess other areas of emotional cognition, in particular complex social cognitive functioning, e.g. reward processing, mentalization, and theory of mind (Bora et al. 2005; Roiser and Sahakian 2013). However, the tasks were specifically chosen, as they encompass the primary domains of affective cognition, and are among the most extensively studied in mood disorders (Miskowiak et al. 2019; Miskowiak and Carvalho 2014). Although certain domains, particularly verbal memory, have previously demonstrated stronger associations with psychosocial functioning (del Mar Bonnín et al. 2014), all domains were weighted equally in constructing the global cognitive composite to avoid introducing sample-specific bias and to maintain comparability with prior multi-domain composite approaches; however, differential weighting of domains may be relevant in future studies specifically targeting functional outcomes. Regarding childhood trauma, the CTQ, as a retrospective self-report measure, is to some extent vulnerable to measurement bias. While this may challenge the interpretative value of distinct trauma subtype measures, psychometric validations have found the Danish version of the CTQ to be a both valid and reliable instrument in clinical samples (Kongerslev et al. 2019). Moreover, because cognitive performance was standardized relative to a rigorously screened healthy control group (a majority, 84%, being blood donors without first-degree familial psychiatric history), the defined ‘high’ cognitive performance group reflects performance relative to this comparison sample rather than to population-based normative data, which may limit generalizability to the general population. Lastly, the thresholds defining high and normal cognition respectively, may constitute a limitation, as the cut-offs excluded different segments of each participant group, although they allowed for desired comparisons, specifically between the two groups of persons with mood disorders (see Fig. 1). Importantly, these thresholds were selected to serve the exploratory framework of the study, by setting conservative yet data-anchored starting points (Miskowiak et al. 2017) for pragmatic distinctions between population groups (“high” and “normal” cognitive performance respectively) relatively uncommon in the surrounding literature.

In conclusion, cognitively high-performing persons with mood disorders were characterized by favorable clinical outcomes, improved functioning, successful downregulation of negative emotion and facial recognition, and fewer experiences of physical abuse compared to cognitively normally performing persons with mood disorders. The findings support previous studies suggesting a fundamental association between cognitive ability and illness course in mood disorders, seemingly extending beyond impairment and into non-impaired populations. Enhancement of cognition beyond simply normalization may thus aid functional recovery and constitute a salient target for the design and implementation of future pro-cognitive interventions. Given the cross-sectional design of the study, our findings are presented as hypothesis-generating evidence. Future longitudinal studies may assess the predictive validity of the present ‘high cognitive performance’ construct, to causally infer potential associations between high cognitive performance and long-term illness course.
