Simulated microgravity weakens wheat root microbial network against pathogens
Authors: Jingjing Cui, Zhenyu Chen, Shaocheng Yan, Liting Zhao, A G Degermendzhi, Hong Liu, Yuming Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFungal pathogens are well-recognized biotic stressors for plants under terrestrial gravity and also pose risks to space crop production, but their effects on root-associated microbial networks under microgravity conditions remain poorly understood. Here, we profiled bacterial and fungal communities in wheat seedlings with or without Fusarium graminearum infection under normal gravity and simulated microgravity, and linked network properties to plant growth and hormone profiles. Although bacterial and fungal α-diversity showed no significant differences among treatments and Bray–Curtis β-diversity showed limited separation, co-occurrence networks revealed that infection disrupted bacterial–bacterial and bacterial–fungal networks more strongly under simulated microgravity than under normal gravity, whereas fungal–fungal networks were largely unchanged. Bacterial network characteristics explained more variation in plant performance than bacterial–fungal network characteristics. Structural equation modeling showed that simulated microgravity reduced endosphere bacterial network stability, which was positively associated with plant performance, especially jasmonic acid and cytokinin levels. Random forest analysis identified Paenibacillus and Microbacteriaceae-related taxa as key predictors of bacterial network stability. These findings support microbiome-based strategies to enhance plant resilience in space systems. Subject terms: Microbiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

The complex interactions within plant microbial communities are crucial for supporting plant growth, enhancing disease resistance, and improving the plant’s ability to withstand environmental stress through the modulation of hormone networks1–3. These ecological relationships can be represented as co-occurrence networks, where species are nodes, and their interactions are represented by links4,5. The configuration and distribution of these links are essential for characterizing species interactions, especially during the seedling stage when plant health and adaptability are most vulnerable6,7. Recent studies have linked these network structures with the resistance of rhizosphere and endosphere microbial communities to pathogen invasion and abiotic stress1,8. Expanding our understanding of microbe–microbe interactions can deepen our understanding of microecosystem functions and ecological processes and guide research to enhance plant health, pathogen resistance, and resilience to abiotic stress.

Microgravity is considered a typical abiotic stress in the space environment and can alter plant physiological processes and defense-related gene expression9, potentially rendering plants more vulnerable to pathogen infection10. For instance, soybean plants grown under microgravity conditions show increased susceptibility to fungal pathogens11. Rhizosphere and endosphere microbial communities serve as critical defenses against pathogens, and the network structures—comprising bacterial–bacterial, fungal–fungal, and bacterial–fungal interactions—play significant roles in determining pathogen invasion and overall plant health1,12–14. High microbial diversity is associated with greater community stability, which enhances resistance to pathogen invasion1, but diversity indices alone may not capture changes in microbial interaction architecture. Additionally, complex microbial communities can trigger faster activation of the plant immune system, providing stronger defenses against pathogens15. Thus, understanding the dynamics of microbial communities under pathogen attack in microgravity is essential for developing strategies to mitigate disease risks in space. Our previous study demonstrated that both the diversity and network stability of bacterial communities decline under simulated microgravity16, suggesting that microbial network disruption may be one mechanism by which microgravity weakens plant-associated defenses. However, little is known about how pathogen infection affects the complexity and stability of microbial co-occurrence networks under microgravity conditions.

Recent studies have increasingly highlighted the roles of plant-associated microbial communities and their network interactions in influencing plant growth and defense against pathogens1,12,14. Plant hormones, key mediators of microbe–plant interactions, can be modulated by microbial interactions under both abiotic and biotic stress17. Understanding the interplay between network characteristics and plant hormones is crucial for identifying key microorganisms that can be targeted to optimize microbial community interactions, thereby enhancing plant disease resistance and growth. However, our knowledge of the relationship between microgravity, network structure, and plant hormones is still limited, hindering our ability to design engineered microbial communities for enhancing plant defense and growth in space environments.

Hub taxa, which interact disproportionately with other members of microbial communities, are central to maintaining network stability and shaping community composition and function18–21. As a distinctive environmental stressor associated with spaceflight, microgravity has the potential to destabilize microbial interaction networks and alter the ecological roles of these key taxa. Because access to real microgravity experiments remains limited, simulated microgravity platforms have been widely adopted to investigate plant and microbial responses to altered gravity. We therefore hypothesize that simulated microgravity exacerbates plant susceptibility to pathogens by disrupting hub-taxa-mediated microbial network stability and function. If supported, this hypothesis would suggest that targeted manipulation of key taxa to preserve or restore network complexity may offer a strategy to alleviate the combined impacts of microgravity stress and pathogen pressure.

However, direct empirical evidence for this hypothesis remains limited. It is unclear whether microgravity selectively disrupts network modules centered on hub taxa, whether pathogen invasion under simulated microgravity drives microbiome reorganization in a manner distinct from that under normal gravity, and whether such structural shifts are mechanistically associated with plant hormone signaling and plant health outcomes. To address these questions, we combined network theory with high-throughput sequencing to evaluate how root-associated microbial community networks respond to pathogen invasion under different gravity conditions. Specifically, this study aimed to: (i) assess how pathogen invasion reshapes the root microbiome network under simulated microgravity; (ii) elucidate how gravitational conditions and network architecture jointly influence plant hormone responses and plant performance; and (iii) identify candidate biomarker taxa associated with network properties linked to plant health. This work provides an empirical basis for microbiome engineering strategies aimed at modulating network structure to enhance plant growth and disease resistance in microgravity environments.

Methods

Experimental design

The overall experimental design is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. All plant culture procedures and operations were conducted in a sterile environment. The experiment comprised four groups: normal gravity healthy group (1-g, − P), simulated microgravity healthy group (mμ-g, − P), normal gravity infected group (1-g, + P), and simulated microgravity infected group (mμ-g, + P), with six biological replicates per group. The pathogen strain selected for the experiment was Fusarium graminearum PH-1, a wheat pathogen. Each simulated microgravity group was rotated about both a vertical axis and a horizontal axis at a random angular velocity between 1.5° and 15° per second by random positioning machines (RPMs)22. To minimize potential confounding effects caused by rotation, the light source was mounted directly above the samples in both setups; in the RPM groups, the light source rotated together with the seedlings and culture bottles, maintaining a consistent relative orientation between the light source and the plants throughout the experiment. Under these conditions, the wheat in the RPMs experienced a maximal residual centrifugal acceleration of less than 10−5 × g, which is below the threshold acceleration for gravity responses in plants22.

Plant cultivation and sampling

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) seeds were surface-sterilized using a 0.1% (W/V) KMnO4 solution for 30 min, followed by five rinses with sterilized ddH2O. Next, surface-sterilized seeds were placed on moist, sterile Petri dishes and cultured for 24 h. Subsequently, the germinated seeds were aseptically transferred using sterile forceps into culture bottles pre-filled with sterile mineral salt medium (composed of 2% agar powder in 1× Hoagland’s nutrient solution) for continued cultivation. For the pathogen infection groups, at the first leaf stage (day 4), 1.5 mL of Fusarium graminearum spore suspension with a concentration of 1 × 105 spores mL−1 was inoculated into the roots of the infected groups (the preparation method of spore suspension is described in Supplementary Note 1). Following a 12-h incubation period, both infected and healthy groups were returned to the same sterile mineral salt medium and cultured for 14 days under simulated microgravity and normal gravity, respectively. All treatments were maintained under a 12-h light/12-h dark photoperiod, with a light intensity of 200 ± 10 μmol m−2 s−1 measured at the top of the culture bottles. The temperature was maintained at 22 ± 1.0 °C, and the relative humidity was 55 ± 5.0%. After the 14-day cultivation period, root and stem length as well as root and stem biomass were measured.

Root tissues were sampled for analysis of bacterial communities in both the rhizosphere and endosphere. For each biological replicate, pooled roots from five plants were collected into 15 mL Falcon tubes containing 5 mL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and shaken at 180 rpm for 20 min. The supernatant (PBS washing buffer) was stored as the rhizosphere compartment until DNA extraction. For endosphere samples, the root samples described above were surface-sterilized by consecutive immersion for 1 min in 75% ethanol, 3 min in 3% sodium hypochlorite, and 30 s in 75% ethanol, followed by three rinses in sterile distilled water. The samples were then ground using a sterile mortar. To measure plant hormones, fresh wheat leaves were harvested, weighed, and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. Leaf and root samples were processed entirely independently; leaf samples were used exclusively for plant hormone quantification and were not included in any stage of microbial community analysis. All samples were stored at −80 °C until further processing.

DNA extraction and amplicon sequencing

The rhizosphere and endosphere microbial DNA were extracted using the MN NucleoSpin 96 Soil kit (MN, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The bacterial 16S rRNA V3–V4 regions (338F: 5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3’ and 806 R: 5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’) were amplified23, while the fungal ITS1 region was amplified using primers ITS1F (5’-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3’) and ITS2 (5’-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3’)24. High-throughput sequencing of the PCR products was performed on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (Biomarker Technologies Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), and 250 bp paired-end reads were generated.

Sequence data analysis

Amplicon sequences were analyzed using the QIIME 2 (version 2020.6)25. The DADA2 pipeline26 was used for quality filtering, denoising, and inference of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Sequences with an average quality score < 5, invalid primer or barcode sequences, ambiguous bases, or length < 250 bp were removed during quality filtering. After removing sequences identified as representing chloroplasts and mitochondria, a total of 11,665,748 bacterial and 23,156,931 fungal high-quality reads were obtained from all valid samples. The number of reads per sample is provided in Supplementary Table 1. The number of high-quality reads per sample ranged from 15,990 to 40,881 (median: 28,638) for bacterial 16S rRNA gene sequencing and from 39,481 to 61,818 (median: 59,935) for fungal ITS sequencing. The bacterial and fungal sequences were classified using the SILVA (v132) and UNITE (v8.0) databases, respectively27,28. The relative abundance of ASVs was calculated by dividing the reads per ASV in a sample by the sum of the usable reads in that sample. Relative abundance normalization was used primarily for community-level comparisons, including β-diversity and related composition-based analyses, for which proportion-based normalization has been shown to perform well in ecological microbiome comparisons29. In the present dataset, sequencing-depth variation across samples was relatively limited (Supplementary Table 1), reducing the likelihood that library-size differences substantially distorted these comparisons.

ASVs were retained for downstream analysis if they were present in more than half of the samples within any given treatment group, resulting in 368 bacterial and 1999 fungal ASVs included in the final dataset. The Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated for each pair of ASVs based on their relative abundances. Thresholds were set at P < 0.05 and |r| > 0.6. An adjacency matrix was generated for each correlation matrix, containing only the correlations with absolute coefficient values (correlation strengths) that were greater than or equal to the threshold. Retained ASVs were visualized in co-occurrence networks, where ASVs were represented as nodes and significant pairwise correlations were represented as edges. Network analysis was performed using the R “igraph” package30 and visualized with Gephi software31. Network topological properties, including node degree and eigenvector centrality, were calculated for each microbial network, where node degree was defined as the number of edges connected to a node and eigenvector centrality quantified the relative importance of a node based on both its direct connections and the connectivity of its neighboring nodes. Robustness was defined as the network’s resistance to node loss and its ability to maintain topological integrity under perturbation. It was quantified using natural connectivity, which simulates random or targeted removal of nodes (species) and calculates the decay rate of connectivity in the remaining network32. We also calculated cohesion, an abundance-weighted, null model-corrected metric based on pairwise correlations across taxa33. Total cohesion was defined as the sum of positive cohesion and the absolute value of negative cohesion. The ratio of negative to positive cohesion was used as an indicator of network stability19, reflecting the balance between competitive/inhibitory interactions (negative cohesion) and cooperative/facilitative interactions (positive cohesion) within the community.

Plant hormone analyses

To extract the metabolites, 50 mg of the frozen wheat leaves were ground into powder in liquid nitrogen and mixed with 1 mL of methanol/water/formic acid (15:4:1, V/V/V) containing isotope-labeled internal standards (e.g., 13C- or 2H-labeled phytohormone analogs). The combined extracts were evaporated to dryness under a nitrogen gas stream, reconstituted in 100 μL of 80% methanol (V/V), and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter for subsequent quantitative analysis by UPLC–ESI–MS/MS, as described in a previous study34. Absolute quantification was achieved using isotope-labeled internal standards to correct for extraction losses and matrix effects. Hormone contents were normalized to fresh weight (FW) and are reported as ng g−1 FW.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were carried out in R35 (version 4.3.0). A nonparametric Wilcoxon test was performed to evaluate differences in network topology parameters and community relative abundance of healthy and infected wheat under different gravity conditions. For bacterial and fungal communities, α-diversity was estimated using the Shannon index. β-diversity was evaluated by principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrices. Differences in community composition among treatments were statistically tested using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA). To integrate the multidimensional phytohormone data into a holistic indicator of plant physiological status, we constructed a composite variable termed “Plant Performance”. Specifically, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the measured concentrations of all quantified hormones. The first principal component (PC1), which captured the largest proportion of variance across treatments, was extracted and used as the composite score for “Plant Performance” in subsequent structural equation modeling. We used structural equation modeling (SEM) to evaluate the direct and indirect effects of gravitational conditions and network stability on plant performance. SEM was conducted using the “lavaan” package in R36. The main predictors of network structures were identified using a random forest (RF) analysis37, via the rfPermute package in R38. Preparation methods for SEM and RF are described in Supplementary Note 2. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Plant performance and baseline microbiome structure under pathogen infection and simulated microgravity

Pathogen infection significantly reduced root biomass under both gravity conditions, whereas root length showed a decreasing trend that did not reach statistical significance under either gravity condition (Fig. 1a). In contrast, both stem biomass and stem length were significantly reduced in infected plants under both gravity conditions. Representative phenotypes are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2, where infected seedlings exhibited clear stem rot symptoms, including browning and reduced shoot elongation.

At the diversity level, we observed no clear treatment-associated differences. Bacterial and fungal α-diversity (Shannon index) did not differ significantly between healthy and infected plants under either gravity condition (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). Bray–Curtis β-diversity analysis likewise showed no clear separation among treatment groups in either compartment (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d, PERMANOVA, P > 0.05). At the phylum level, bacterial communities were consistently dominated by Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Firmicutes in both the endosphere and rhizosphere, while fungal communities were primarily composed of Ascomycota (Fig. 1b, c). Across treatments, overall bacterial composition remained comparable, and the relative abundances of dominant phyla did not differ significantly among groups (Supplementary Fig. 4c). At the genus level, bacterial communities were mainly represented by Pseudomonas, Stenotrophomonas, and Sphingomonas (Supplementary Fig. 4a). However, fungal taxonomic resolution at the genus level was limited, with a substantial fraction of fungal ASVs remaining unclassified (Supplementary Fig. 4b). Within the classified fraction, Penicillium, Fusarium, and Gibberella were among the dominant genera, although each represented only a minor fraction of the total fungal community. Most dominant bacterial genera did not differ significantly among treatments (Supplementary Fig. 4d). Notably, Pseudomonas showed an increasing trend in both compartments, with the increase reaching statistical significance only in the rhizosphere under simulated microgravity following infection. Given the limited treatment-associated signal in diversity and composition, we next investigated whether microbial interaction networks were nonetheless reconfigured by pathogen infection, particularly under simulated microgravity.

Bacterial and fungal networks in response to fungal pathogen infection under different gravity conditions

To evaluate how pathogen infection affects bacterial and fungal interactions under varying gravity conditions, we initially conducted bacterial–bacterial and fungal–fungal network analyses (Supplementary Fig. 5). Our findings revealed that both bacterial and fungal networks exhibited small-world characteristics (Supplementary Table 2) and short geodesic distances (average shortest path length) ranging from 1.167 to 4.868 (Supplementary Table 3). Analysis of network complexity revealed that fungal pathogen infection significantly reduced the degree and eigenvector centrality of endosphere bacterial communities under simulated microgravity, but no significant changes were observed under normal gravity. In rhizosphere bacterial networks, these metrics decreased under both gravity conditions (Fig. 2a). Moreover, fungal pathogens reduced the total cohesion of both rhizosphere and endosphere bacterial communities under simulated microgravity (Fig. 2c). However, most network complexity parameters of fungal communities did not exhibit significant trends when infected by fungal pathogens under either gravity condition (Fig. 2b).

Next, we simulated species extinction through either random species loss or targeted removal of module hubs to assess the robustness of microbial networks across treatments. Infected groups had lower robustness than healthy groups in both endosphere and rhizosphere bacterial communities (Fig. 2d). Under simulated microgravity, pathogen infection appeared to reduce bacterial community robustness more strongly than under normal gravity. Additionally, pathogen infection under simulated microgravity reduced the robustness of rhizosphere fungal communities (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Bacterial–fungal networks in response to pathogen infection under different gravity conditions

To assess the effects of pathogen infection on interkingdom interactions between endosphere and rhizosphere bacteria and fungi under simulated microgravity and normal gravity, we performed bacterial–fungal interaction network analyses (Fig. 3). Our results showed that within the endosphere networks, bacteria comprised a higher proportion compared to fungi, whereas in the rhizosphere, fungi were more dominant (Fig. 3a). Further analysis of network complexity parameters of endosphere interdomain networks indicated that closeness centrality was significantly higher in the infected samples than in the healthy samples under normal gravity, but markedly decreased in the infected samples under simulated microgravity. The degree significantly decreased in the simulated microgravity infected group, while no significant changes were observed under normal gravity. A similar pattern was observed in the rhizosphere interdomain networks, where both closeness centrality and degree followed the same trend (Fig. 3b). Post-infection, total cohesion increased under normal gravity but significantly declined under simulated microgravity in both endosphere and rhizosphere microbial networks (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). Collectively, these results suggest that, unlike normal gravity, fungal pathogens significantly reduced network complexity under simulated microgravity.

Network stability analysis subsequently demonstrated that infection by fungal pathogens significantly reduced the robustness of rhizosphere and endosphere microbial communities under simulated microgravity (Fig. 3c). Similar to the changes in bacterial networks, bacterial–fungal networks showed a smaller magnitude of change under normal gravity compared to simulated microgravity. These results suggest that pathogen infection induced greater disturbance to the natural connectivity of microbial communities under simulated microgravity, resulting in lower stability in bacterial–fungal interaction networks.

Plant growth and defense hormones in response to pathogen infection under different gravity conditions

To determine whether microbial network characteristics were related to plant growth and defense capacity, we analyzed the effects of pathogen infection on plant hormone profiles under different gravity conditions (Fig. 4). Under normal gravity, pathogen infection significantly increased several defense-related hormones, including jasmonic acid (JA), jasmonoyl-isoleucine (JA-Ile), methyl jasmonate (MeJA), and abscisic acid (ABA). Under simulated microgravity, JAs and ABA responses to infection were retained, whereas JA-Ile and MeJA no longer showed significant changes, suggesting a partial attenuation or reduced consistency of some jasmonate-related responses under simulated microgravity. In contrast, many cytokinin- and auxin-related compounds decreased markedly in infected plants under both gravity conditions, although the magnitude and significance of these changes varied among individual hormones. Overall, these results indicate that pathogen infection broadly perturbed plant hormone homeostasis, while the specific response patterns depended on both gravity condition and hormone type.

The complexity and stability of microbial networks related to the wheat growth and defense ability

Next, we employed structural equation modeling (SEM) to assess the direct and indirect effects of gravity conditions and network characteristics, including the stability and complexity of bacterial–bacterial and bacterial–fungal networks, on plant hormone dynamics (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 8). These findings revealed that bacterial–bacterial network characteristics have a higher R² in explaining plant performance compared to bacterial–fungal networks. Gravity conditions may directly affect plant performance or indirectly influence it by significantly impacting the stability of endosphere bacterial communities and the complexity of rhizosphere bacterial communities. Specifically, (i) simulated microgravity significantly reduced the stability of endosphere bacterial networks, which in turn positively influenced plant performance (P < 0.05); (ii) simulated microgravity significantly decreased the complexity of rhizosphere bacterial networks, which also positively affected plant performance (P < 0.05). Furthermore, we observed that the correlation between gravitational conditions and endosphere bacterial network stability (−0.856) is stronger than that between gravitational conditions and rhizosphere bacterial network complexity (−0.535). Similarly, the correlation between endosphere bacterial network stability and plant performance (0.784) is higher than that between rhizosphere bacterial network complexity and plant performance (0.713). Moreover, correlation analysis between bacterial network stability and plant performance revealed that the stability of endosphere bacterial networks is significantly associated with plant hormones such as CKs, JA, and auxins. These findings suggest that gravitational factors influence plant growth and defense-related processes by modulating bacterial network characteristics, particularly the stability of endosphere bacterial networks.

Potential biomarker taxa correlated with endosphere bacterial network stability under simulated microgravity

To investigate how gravity influences the structure of microbial networks through specific endosphere microbes, and to identify strains that potentially enhance plant health and adaptability, we used random forest analysis to identify key microbes affecting network stability within bacterial communities (Fig. 6a). The minimum cross-validation error identified nine significant taxa, which were defined as biomarker taxa in our model. Strains from the genera Paenibacillus, Rhodococcus, Microbacterium, Methylobacterium, and Massilia were identified as key predictors of endosphere bacterial network stability. Additionally, Spearman analysis showed significant positive relationships between ASV1 (Paenibacillus sp. T17-23), ASV2 (Paenibacillus sp. MAFF550216), ASV7 (Microbacteriaceae bacterium strain DB-2402), and the stability of endosphere bacterial networks (Fig. 6b). Notably, the abundance of Paenibacillus sp. T17-23 and Paenibacillus sp. MAFF550216 was reduced in the pathogen-infected group. Furthermore, the relative abundance of Paenibacillus sp. MAFF550216 was lower under simulated microgravity than under normal gravity (Supplementary Fig. 9).

Discussion

Plants in space microgravity environments are highly susceptible to pathogen infections, likely due to changes in microbial community structure12,39. Understanding the dynamic nature of root microbial co-occurrence networks and their role in modulating plant defense and overall performance under simulated microgravity is crucial for advancing our knowledge of microbial ecology in extreme environments. This knowledge lays the theoretical foundation for developing strategies to optimize plant health and productivity in space through the manipulation of microbial communities, including the use of probiotics. However, the response of root endosphere and rhizosphere microbial networks to pathogen infection in microgravity and their ecological relationship to plant performance remains largely unexplored. To address this gap, we combined network theory with high-throughput sequencing to assess how root-associated microbial community networks respond to pathogen invasion under varying gravity conditions.

Our results show that pathogen infection reduced wheat growth, with stronger suppression under simulated microgravity (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 2). In contrast, bacterial and fungal α-diversity, Bray–Curtis β-diversity, and phylum-level composition showed only limited treatment-associated changes, with only modest genus-level shifts such as an increase in Pseudomonas in the rhizosphere under simulated microgravity following infection (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). These results indicate that, in our system, the major effects of pathogen invasion under simulated microgravity were expressed more strongly at the level of microbial interaction networks than as broad compositional restructuring.

Consistent with this pattern, pronounced reorganization was evident in microbial interaction networks under simulated microgravity. Our findings indicate that bacterial–bacterial, fungal–fungal, and bacterial–fungal interaction networks exhibit small-world characteristics (Supplementary Table 2), which facilitate the rapid propagation of perturbations through the network, thereby enhancing system efficiency40 and potentially influencing microbially mediated functions and stability41. Following fungal infection, the bacterial–bacterial network experienced a significant decrease in both degree and eigenvector centrality under simulated microgravity, while the fungal–fungal network remained largely unchanged (Fig. 2). Previous studies have demonstrated that bacterial networks are more vulnerable to stress-induced interference compared to fungal networks42–44. Together, these patterns suggest that under simulated microgravity, pathogen infection may more readily destabilize root microbial networks, potentially weakening microbiome-mediated buffering of plant defense. Additionally, fungal infection reduced the complexity of the endosphere bacterial–bacterial network under simulated microgravity, a trend not observed under normal gravity. This observation is consistent with the idea that simulated microgravity could favor endosphere bacterial network topologies that are less robust to additional biotic perturbations. Overall, endosphere bacterial interactions appeared more disruption-prone under simulated microgravity than under normal gravity.

To quantify network stability, we simulated species extinction and calculated network robustness (resistance to node loss) for bacterial and fungal networks45. Under simulated microgravity, the infected group exhibited lower stability compared to the healthy group in the endosphere bacterial community, rhizosphere bacterial community, and rhizosphere fungal community (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 6), with a greater decrease in robustness than under normal gravity. These findings suggest that pathogen infection significantly exacerbates the destabilization of microbial networks under simulated microgravity, with a particularly pronounced effect on bacterial communities. Notably, these stability losses occurred despite limited differences in α-diversity and Bray–Curtis β-diversity among treatments (Supplementary Fig. 3), suggesting that the dominant effects of pathogen invasion under simulated microgravity were expressed more strongly as disruption of microbial interaction architecture than as broad compositional shifts. Therefore, safeguarding the stability and complexity of bacterial networks—rather than relying solely on taxonomic diversity—may be important for fortifying plant disease resistance in microgravity.

Fungal and bacterial species frequently coexist within the rhizosphere and endosphere of plants, and the interdomain relationships between these microbial communities have attracted significant attention46. To further explore these interactions, we conducted bacterial–fungal co-occurrence network analysis (Fig. 3). Our analysis revealed that fungal–bacterial associations were more complex and tighter in the infected samples compared with those in the corresponding healthy samples under normal gravity. However, under simulated microgravity, the complexity of the infected microbial network was reduced. Previous studies have shown that interactions between rhizosphere and endosphere fungal species exhibit more intricate network structures following fungal pathogen infection than in healthy plants under normal gravity14,47. We hypothesize that the combined effects of microgravity and pathogen stress intensify disturbances in microbial community interactions, leading to reduced community complexity and stability under simulated microgravity. Compared to single stress factors, microgravity and fungal pathogens, when combined as high environmental stressors, impose stronger selective pressures on specific adaptive microorganisms, triggering a series of dynamic changes within microbial communities. A previous study indicates that high environmental stress can reduce network complexity and stability of the network19, primarily through two mechanisms: (i) loss of antagonistic interactions and (ii) increased environmental filtering. Our findings support this mechanism, as we observed a significant reduction in negative cohesion within microbial networks under simulated microgravity, indicating a loss of microbial antagonism (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). Additionally, earlier research has suggested that microgravity and pathogen infection can selectively influence specific microorganisms1,16. This suggests that both microgravity and fungal pathogens function as deterministic filters, imposing selective pressures on particular adaptive microorganisms, and thereby initiating a series of dynamic alterations within the microbial community. These findings have substantial implications for preserving intricate and stable microbial networks that are vital for plant growth and defense in real microgravity environments.

In recent years, there has been increasing interest in studying the complexity and stability of microbial networks to understand how microorganisms influence plant growth and defense1,47,48. Understanding the relationship between microbial network characteristics and plant growth and defense performance is crucial for enhancing plant growth and defense under simulated microgravity by manipulating microbial interaction networks. Therefore, another objective of our study was to elucidate the relationships among gravitational conditions, network structure, and plant performance, thereby advancing our understanding of the ecological mechanisms through which microbial networks impact plant performance. We observed that pathogen-induced defense responses were weaker under simulated microgravity compared to normal gravity (Fig. 4). In non-infected plants, simulated microgravity significantly reduced JA-Ile and caused a decreasing trend in JA (Supplementary Table 4). These results are consistent with the view that simulated microgravity may function as an abiotic perturbation capable of affecting plant physiological and hormone-related responses49,50. Based on the analysis of network characteristics, we hypothesize that the observed decrease in defense levels under simulated microgravity may be due to an exacerbation of the damage caused by pathogen infection to the stability and complexity of bacterial–bacterial and bacterial–fungal networks.

We further constructed a structural equation model (SEM) to explore the relationships among gravitational conditions, network stability and complexity, and plant performance (Fig. 5, and Supplementary Fig. 8). Our results demonstrate that bacterial–bacterial network characteristics exhibit a stronger correlation (higher R2) with plant performance than bacterial–fungal networks. This may reflect that bacterial communities—and especially their co-occurrence networks—are more sensitive to microgravity-related perturbations than fungal communities in this system51. Moreover, simulated microgravity negatively impacts the stability of endosphere bacterial networks and the complexity of rhizosphere bacterial networks, which may ultimately influence plant performance. Previous studies have shown that microgravity reduces the stability and complexity of microbial communities16,52. Another study suggests that more stable microbial networks contribute to plant health and adaptability53. Furthermore, we found that the stability of endosphere bacterial networks has a stronger correlation with gravity conditions and plant performance than the complexity of rhizosphere networks. Stable microbial networks are more resilient to environmental fluctuations20, ensuring continuous microbial support for plant physiological processes under various conditions, thereby more effectively supporting plant health and performance. In highly stable communities, all species in the resident community can use resources similarly, potentially increasing the consumption of every individual resource and reducing pathogen invasion success by limiting opportunities for pathogen growth1. Stable networks characterized by balanced interactions prevent pathogenic or non-beneficial microbes from dominating, ensuring that plant-supportive microbes predominate and function effectively1,54.

Additionally, we provide evidence that the stability of endosphere bacterial–bacterial networks shaped by gravitational conditions is significantly associated with the plant hormones JA and CK. Stable microbial interactions may enhance the plant’s ability to modulate hormone pathways, thereby contributing to improved growth and defense responses under stress conditions55,56. Previous work has shown that defense phytohormones, particularly JA, can shape root microbiome assembly by modulating the colonization of specific bacterial taxa57. In this context, the hormone alterations observed in our study may plausibly contribute to the limited taxon-specific shifts associated with endosphere network stability under simulated microgravity and pathogen stress, although direct causal links remain to be tested. These observations suggest that plant hormone–mediated microbial recruitment may represent a plausible mechanism in this system, although it requires further experimental validation. This evidence supports the hypothesis that enhancing the stability of endosphere bacterial networks could be a promising strategy for optimizing plant growth and defense mechanisms in microgravity environments.

Next, we identified potential biomarker taxa associated with endosphere bacterial network stability using Random Forest (RF) analysis (Fig. 6). The results indicated that Paenibacillus sp. T17-23, Paenibacillus sp. MAFF550216, and Microbacteriaceae bacterium strain DB-2402 emerged as crucial predictors of endosphere bacterial network stability, showing significant positive correlations. Previous studies have shown that biocontrol rhizobacteria belonging to the genus Paenibacillus can enhance the stability of bacterial networks by increasing competitive interactions among the microbial community members48,58. Additionally, the Microbacteriaceae family was identified as a critical keystone taxon, bridging the maximum number of nodes and associations in the endosphere network59,60. Furthermore, we observed that Paenibacillus sp. T17-23 and Paenibacillus sp. MAFF550216 exhibited higher abundance in pathogen-free groups, with the relative abundance of Paenibacillus sp. MAFF550216 being lower under simulated microgravity than under normal gravity. This phenomenon suggests that simulated microgravity may impact the stability of the endosphere bacterial–bacterial community by reducing the abundance of Paenibacillus strains, thereby increasing disease susceptibility under simulated microgravity. Therefore, supplementing Paenibacillus strains presents a potential solution for reducing disease susceptibility and supporting plant growth and defense by enhancing the stability of bacterial networks in microgravity environments.

Although SEM and RF analyses provide evidence for key root-associated microbial regulatory network features that could promote plant growth and defense under simulated microgravity, culture-based experiments are required to verify these findings. Specifically, key microorganisms should be isolated and their ability to regulate bacterial network stability should be tested in vivo to enhance plant growth and defense under both simulated and real microgravity environments. In addition, pathogen load was not directly quantified in this study, and the inoculated pathogen F. graminearum could not be reliably resolved from the ITS dataset because of limited fungal taxonomic resolution. Therefore, the ITS-based fungal community profiles should not be interpreted as direct evidence of pathogen biomass or pathogen pressure. Future studies combining pathogen-specific biomass quantification, such as qPCR, with systematic disease severity assessment would help clarify whether microbial network destabilization is associated with pathogen proliferation, disease development, or both under different gravity conditions.

Taken together, our study shows that the effects of pathogen invasion under simulated microgravity are expressed less as broad compositional shifts and more as disruption of microbial interaction architecture, particularly within bacterial networks. This network-level destabilization was associated with reduced plant defense performance, links to hormone dynamics, and the loss of taxa related to endosphere network stability, including Paenibacillus-associated groups. These findings support a view of the root microbiome in microgravity as an interaction system whose stability may be critical for plant resilience. More broadly, they provide a basis for microbiome-informed strategies aimed at maintaining plant health and productivity in space-associated environments.
