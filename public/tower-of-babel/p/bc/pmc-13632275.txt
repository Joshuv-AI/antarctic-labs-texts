Disrupting the adiposopathy-inflammation loop: a translational immunometabolic framework for inflammatory disease
Authors: Salvatore Corrao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdipose tissue dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a biologically active contributor to chronic inflammatory disease beyond its traditional role in energy storage. In the context of obesity, adiposopathy is characterized by persistent immunometabolic alterations involving adipokine dysregulation, macrophage infiltration, insulin resistance, and chronic low-grade inflammation. These processes intersect with inflammatory signaling pathways central to immune-mediated diseases, supporting the concept of an immunometabolic phenotype in which metabolic dysfunction and immune activation converge to influence disease expression and therapeutic responsiveness. This framework does not merely restate the association between obesity and inflammation; rather, it proposes adiposopathy as a measurable immunometabolic state that may amplify inflammatory signaling, influence therapeutic responsiveness, and identify patients in whom combined metabolic–immune targeting could be biologically rational. The recent TOGETHER-PsA trial provides an important clinical proof of concept for this framework, demonstrating that adding tirzepatide to IL-17 inhibition achieved greater disease control than cytokine blockade alone in psoriatic arthritis among those with overweight or obesity. Rather than representing definitive evidence of disease modification, these findings suggest that targeting adipose tissue dysfunction may influence inflammatory disease activity in selected contexts. Extrapolation from this single, disease-specific trial should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating and requires validation in other inflammatory conditions. Emerging evidence further indicates that incretin-based therapies may exert anti-inflammatory effects extending beyond weight reduction alone. Experimental studies support potential direct immunomodulatory roles of GLP-1 and GIP receptor signaling within immune and myeloid cell populations, although the relative contribution of direct receptor-mediated pathways versus secondary metabolic improvement remains incompletely understood. Within this emerging translational framework, adiposopathy may represent a therapeutically actionable upstream regulator of inflammatory signaling. Further mechanistic and clinical studies are needed to clarify how modulation of adipose tissue dysfunction may complement conventional immune-targeted therapies across chronic inflammatory diseases.Keywords: Adiposopathy, Immunometabolism, Incretin-based therapies, Tirzepatide, GLP-1/GIP receptor signaling, Psoriatic arthritis, Visceral adiposity, Chronic inflammation, Obesity-related inflammation, Translational medicine

Introduction

Obesity-associated adipose tissue dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a central regulator of systemic inflammatory tone and therapeutic responsiveness across chronic diseases. Beyond its role in energy storage, adipose tissue functions as an active immunometabolic organ whose pathological expansion—adiposopathy—generates a biologically active state characterized by insulin resistance, dysregulated adipokine signaling, chronic low-grade inflammation, and impaired tissue adaptability [1–4]. In this context, adiposopathy should not be interpreted merely as excess adiposity, but rather as dysfunctional adipose tissue biology capable of sustaining maladaptive endocrine, inflammatory, and metabolic signaling. The novelty of this commentary lies in reframing adiposopathy not as a parallel comorbidity or passive consequence of obesity, but as a potentially measurable upstream immunometabolic amplifier that may shape inflammatory disease activity, therapeutic response, and cardiometabolic risk in selected phenotypes.

Within this framework, adiposopathy may contribute to a context-dependent immunometabolic phenotype in which metabolic dysfunction and immune activation converge to influence disease expression, treatment responsiveness, and cardiometabolic risk. Recent conceptual work based on large-scale AI-driven mapping of the literature further supports the possibility that adipose tissue may retain a form of persistent immunometabolic imprinting, or “obesogenic memory,” beyond weight loss alone, potentially contributing to chronic inflammatory vulnerability [5]. At present, obesogenic memory should be regarded as a hypothesis-generating concept rather than established clinical biology.

The randomised TOGETHER-PsA trial provides an important proof of concept for this paradigm. Although conducted in psoriatic arthritis, the study is best read as a disease-specific, hypothesis-generating signal that targeting adipose tissue dysfunction may influence inflammatory disease expression, a possibility that requires validation before it can be considered generalisable to other inflammatory diseases. In this phase 3b, 52-week randomised trial conducted in adults with active psoriatic arthritis and overweight or obesity, the addition of tirzepatide, a dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist, to IL-17 inhibition with ixekizumab resulted in superior disease control compared with cytokine blockade alone [6].

Rather than establishing a definitive disease-modifying paradigm, these findings suggest that metabolic interventions targeting adipose tissue dysfunction may influence immune-mediated disease activity in selected contexts. This emerging framework may, if validated in other conditions, support increasing integration between metabolic medicine and inflammatory disease biology.

Adiposopathy as an immunometabolic driver of inflammatory disease

Psoriatic arthritis provides a clinically relevant model through which the immunometabolic phenotype may be observed and experimentally modulated. While the core pathogenesis of psoriatic disease is driven primarily by dysregulation of the IL-23/IL-17 axis [7], the coexistence of visceral adiposity introduces an additional layer of biological complexity.

Psoriatic arthritis represents a particularly informative model for this framework because it combines a dominant IL-23/IL-17-driven immune axis with a high burden of obesity, visceral adiposity, insulin resistance, and cardiometabolic comorbidity. This convergence allows the interaction between adipose tissue dysfunction and immune-mediated inflammation to be examined in a clinically meaningful setting, without implying that the same mechanisms operate with equal strength across all inflammatory diseases. Indeed, obesity and central adiposity have been associated with greater disease activity and impaired treatment response in psoriatic disease and other immune-mediated conditions, reinforcing the clinical relevance of this model [8].

Expansion of visceral adipose tissue promotes adipocyte hypertrophy, macrophage infiltration, altered adipokine secretion, and chronic inflammatory signaling through pathways including NF-κB, JAK/STAT, and mTOR [1–3]. These pathways intersect directly with immune circuits involved in Th1 and Th17 polarisation, thereby amplifying systemic inflammatory signaling.

Rather than a simple M1/M2 macrophage switch, obesity-associated adipose inflammation is increasingly understood as a spectrum of metabolically programmed immune-cell states. Adipocyte hypertrophy, local hypoxia, lipotoxic stress, extracellular matrix remodelling, and impaired efferocytosis can promote innate immune activation and myeloid-cell reprogramming. These processes may support a form of trained inflammatory responsiveness in which monocytes, macrophages, and tissue-resident immune cells acquire persistent metabolic and epigenetic changes that amplify cytokine production and antigen-presenting functions [9]. In parallel, adipokine imbalance, insulin resistance, and nutrient excess may favour Th17-skewed responses and impair regulatory immune circuits, thereby linking adipose tissue dysfunction with inflammatory pathways relevant to psoriatic disease.

More specifically, these events may be connected as a biological continuum: adipocyte hypertrophy and stress generate local hypoxia and lipotoxicity, which can activate innate immune sensors and promote secretion of interleukin (IL)-1β and IL-6. This cytokine milieu, together with adipokine imbalance and insulin resistance, may favour Th17 skewing while impairing regulatory T-cell function, thereby amplifying the inflammatory pathways that drive psoriatic disease. Through this sequence, adiposopathy could biologically amplify rather than merely accompany immune-mediated inflammation, sustaining the self-reinforcing and bidirectional adiposopathy–inflammation loop depicted in Fig. 1. Although each step is individually supported by experimental data, their integrated contribution in human inflammatory disease remains to be confirmed.

Importantly, this relationship is unlikely to depend solely on total body weight. In fact, adiposopathy reflects qualitative alterations in adipose tissue biology, including inflammatory activation, extracellular matrix remodelling, impaired tissue plasticity, and persistent immunometabolic signaling [4, 5]. In this sense, adipose dysfunction may act as an upstream biological amplifier of inflammatory disease rather than merely representing a parallel cardiometabolic comorbidity.

Real-world evidence from our group demonstrated that central obesity correlates with disease activity, physical function, and patient-reported outcomes in psoriatic arthritis [10]. Additional evidence suggests that metabolic factors may influence responses to biologic and targeted synthetic disease-modifying therapies across rheumatologic and dermatologic diseases [11].

More broadly, similar interactions between adipose dysfunction and immune activation have been reported across multiple chronic inflammatory conditions, supporting the possibility that the immunometabolic phenotype may be a generalisable translational construct rather than a disease-specific observation.

Incretin-based therapies and disruption of the adiposopathy–inflammation loop

Within this emerging framework, incretin-based therapies may exert effects extending beyond glucose lowering and body-weight reduction alone. Tirzepatide may be among the earliest clinical examples suggesting that targeting adipose tissue dysfunction can modify the expression of inflammatory disease in selected immune-mediated conditions.

By reducing visceral adiposity, improving insulin sensitivity, and attenuating adipose inflammatory signaling, dual incretin receptor agonism may act upstream of conventional immune-targeted therapies [6, 12]. However, the relative contribution of weight reduction versus direct receptor-mediated immunomodulation remains incompletely defined in humans.

Importantly, the broader immunometabolic implications of incretin-based therapies are not restricted to tirzepatide. Similar anti-inflammatory signals have also been observed with GLP-1 receptor agonists alone, including reductions in C-reactive protein associated with semaglutide across the STEP clinical trial programme [13]. These findings suggest that modulation of inflammatory tone may represent a broader property of incretin-based therapies, although the mechanisms underlying these effects remain incompletely understood.

In parallel, accumulating evidence suggests that incretin receptors may participate directly in immune regulation. Both GLP-1 and GIP receptors are expressed on immune and myeloid cell populations, supporting the possibility of receptor-mediated immunometabolic effects independent of weight loss alone [14–16].

Preclinical evidence further suggests that GIP receptor signaling in immune cells may contribute to metabolically beneficial anti-inflammatory responses within adipose tissue, although the translational relevance of these mechanisms in humans remains incompletely established [14]. Recent experimental studies additionally support a role for GIP receptor signaling in the modulation of inflammatory pathways across metabolically active tissues [15].

At the same time, the biological role of GIP signaling remains complex and potentially context dependent. Current evidence does not yet clearly separate the relative contribution of direct incretin receptor signaling from secondary metabolic improvements associated with reductions in adiposity. Consequently, mechanistic interpretation should remain appropriately cautious.

Direct versus indirect immunometabolic effects

One of the central unresolved questions in this field concerns the relative contribution of direct immunological effects versus secondary consequences of weight reduction itself.

Weight loss alone is already known to improve inflammatory activity and cardiometabolic risk across multiple obesity-associated conditions. Therefore, the anti-inflammatory effects observed with incretin-based therapies may partly reflect reductions in visceral adiposity, improved insulin sensitivity, and attenuation of adipose tissue inflammation.

Nevertheless, several observations suggest that the biology may be more complex. Experimental evidence suggests that GLP-1R- and GIPR-expressing immune cells may modulate macrophage polarisation, inflammatory signaling, and tissue immune responses through receptor-mediated mechanisms [14–16]. Recent translational evidence further indicates that GIP receptor signaling is functionally active within adipose tissue itself. Human adipocyte studies have demonstrated that tirzepatide modulates adipocyte nutrient metabolism through sustained GIP receptor activation, suggesting that incretin-based therapies may directly influence adipose tissue biology beyond weight reduction alone [17]. Moreover, some clinical analyses have suggested temporal dissociation between early inflammatory improvements and later weight-dependent metabolic changes, raising the possibility that at least part of the observed anti-inflammatory signal may occur independently of weight reduction.

At present, however, definitive mechanistic separation between direct receptor-mediated immunomodulation and indirect metabolic improvement remains unavailable in humans. This distinction represents a major translational priority for future research.

Future directions and translational implications

The emerging concept of an immunometabolic phenotype carries potentially important implications for translational medicine and disease stratification.

Future studies should clarify the relative contribution of weight loss, visceral adipose tissue reduction, adipokine remodeling, and direct incretin receptor signaling in mediating anti-inflammatory effects. Mechanistic translational studies integrating imaging of visceral adiposity, adipose tissue phenotyping, immunophenotyping, adipokine profiling, and longitudinal inflammatory biomarkers may help define the immunometabolic phenotype more precisely.

A realistic translational roadmap could proceed across complementary designs. Prospective, biomarker-enriched cohorts could test whether baseline immunometabolic markers predict disease trajectory and treatment response. Deep-phenotyping studies combining visceral-fat imaging, adipose tissue sampling, immunophenotyping, and adipokine profiling could characterise the phenotype at tissue level. Mechanistic translational studies could dissect the relative contribution of weight loss versus direct incretin receptor-mediated immunomodulation in humans. Ultimately, adaptive, phenotype-stratified clinical trials that enrol patients selected by immunometabolic criteria, rather than by obesity alone, would be needed to establish whether combined metabolic–immune targeting confers benefit beyond conventional immune-directed therapy.

In parallel, additional work is needed to determine whether persistent adipose dysfunction or obesogenic memory influences long-term inflammatory vulnerability, therapeutic responsiveness, or relapse propensity after metabolic improvement [5]. This concept should be interpreted as a hypothesis-generating model rather than an established clinical entity, and its relevance will require testing in longitudinal mechanistic cohorts.

From a clinical perspective, assessment of adipose tissue dysfunction may eventually become an integral component of inflammatory disease stratification, identifying patients more likely to benefit from combined metabolic–immune therapeutic approaches.

Operationalising the immunometabolic phenotype

For translational use, the immunometabolic phenotype should be operationalised through layered, clinically accessible variables rather than by body weight alone (Table 1). A pragmatic framework could combine central adiposity measures, body composition or visceral fat imaging where available, insulin resistance indices, systemic inflammatory markers, adipokine profiling, and disease-specific activity measures. In this model, waist circumference or waist-to-height ratio, HOMA-IR, triglyceride/HDL ratios, CRP, leptin/adiponectin ratio, and selected adipokines such as resistin or chemerin may help identify patients with biologically active adipose tissue dysfunction [1–4, 8]. More specialised markers, including Th17/Treg balance, myeloid activation phenotypes, or tissue-level adipose inflammatory signatures, should currently be considered research tools rather than routine clinical criteria [9]. Disease-specific instruments such as DAPSA, PASDAS, CRP-linked activity, and patient-reported outcomes remain essential to anchor any immunometabolic stratification to clinically meaningful endpoints [18].

Candidate patients for combined metabolic–immune targeting

Potential candidates for combined metabolic–immune strategies are therefore not all patients with inflammatory disease, but those with a demonstrable immunometabolic phenotype: central or visceral adiposity, insulin resistance, low-grade systemic inflammation, cardiometabolic comorbidity, and persistent disease activity or incomplete response despite appropriate immune-targeted therapy [10, 11]. A disproportionate inflammatory burden relative to articular or cutaneous findings may further support this phenotype. Conversely, lean inflammatory disease, metabolically healthy obesity, or inflammation driven predominantly by non-metabolic immune mechanisms may not be expected to respond in the same way. In all cases, metabolic intervention should be regarded as complementary to, rather than a replacement for, immune-targeted therapy. Two caveats are central to this framework: not all obesity reflects adiposopathy, as metabolically healthy adiposity may lack the inflammatory activity that defines the phenotype, and not all inflammatory disease is immunometabolically driven [1–4]. Phenotype-guided patient selection, rather than reliance on body weight or diagnosis alone, is therefore essential to identify those most likely to benefit from combined metabolic–immune strategies.

Limitations and unresolved questions

Several limitations should temper interpretation. First, TOGETHER-PsA provides a disease-specific and hypothesis-generating signal rather than definitive evidence that adiposopathy-targeted therapy modifies inflammatory disease across conditions [6]. Second, the relative contribution of weight loss, visceral-fat reduction, improved insulin sensitivity, and direct incretin receptor-mediated immunomodulation remains unresolved in humans [13–17]. Third, inflammatory diseases also occur in lean individuals and in patients without overt metabolic dysfunction, indicating that adiposopathy is unlikely to be a universal driver. Fourth, the concept of obesogenic memory remains an unproven conceptual model whose relevance to inflammatory disease relapse, persistence, or therapeutic resistance is not yet established [5]. Finally, candidate biomarkers and stratification algorithms require prospective validation, and combined metabolic–immune targeting carries implications for cost, safety, and treatment sequencing that must be evaluated before clinical adoption.

Conclusion

Emerging evidence increasingly supports integration of metabolic and immunological perspectives within a unified pathophysiological framework. Within this paradigm, adiposopathy may represent a therapeutically actionable upstream regulator of systemic inflammatory signaling rather than merely a passive metabolic comorbidity.

The TOGETHER-PsA trial provides an important early clinical signal suggesting that targeting adipose tissue dysfunction may influence inflammatory disease expression in selected immune-mediated conditions [6]. More broadly, the convergence of adipose biology, immunometabolism, and incretin-based therapeutics supports the concept of an immunometabolic interface linking visceral adiposity to chronic inflammatory disease.

Although substantial mechanistic uncertainty remains, this framework may help guide future translational research exploring how correction of adipose tissue dysfunction could complement conventional immune-targeted therapies.
