Thermal modulation of genetic resistance to infectious salmon anemia virus: Transcriptome analyses of Atlantic salmon families (Salmo salar) at 10 °C and 20 °C
Authors: E Misk, SK Whyte, SL Purcell, L Groves, D Michaud, WC Cai, BL Langille, AF Garber, MD Fast
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding the interaction between genetic resistance and environmental temperature is critical for managing infectious salmon anemia virus (ISAv) in Atlantic salmon aquaculture. Building upon an experimental infection trial of 20 families with ISAv HPR4 at 10 °C and 20 °C, we conducted RNA sequencing of head kidney samples from the two most resistant (average survival: 81% at 10 °C, 93% at 20 °C) and two most susceptible families (average survival: 32% at 10 °C, 54% at 20 °C). Transcriptomic analysis revealed that resistance mechanisms were dramatically temperature-dependent, with 3,690 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between resistant and susceptible families at 10 °C compared to only 64 DEGs at 20 °C. At 10 °C, resistance was characterized by coordinated downregulation of proteasome, DNA replication, and translation initiation pathways, alongside upregulation of receptor internalization mechanisms including flotillin proteins, β-arrestins, and NEDD4 E3 ubiquitin ligase. Temperature comparisons revealed fundamentally different molecular strategies: resistant families (7,156 DEGs) prioritized DNA replication, cell cycle processes, and oxidative phosphorylation, while susceptible families (8,004 DEGs) emphasized RNA processing, ribosome biogenesis, and proteasome-mediated protein degradation. Genome-wide association analysis identified the translation initiation factor EIF4G1 on chromosome 14, which was significantly downregulated in resistant fish (log₂FC = -0.51, FDR = 0.03). These findings demonstrate that ISAv resistance relies on broad cellular resilience strategies rather than classical antiviral responses, and that these mechanisms interact substantially with thermal conditions.

Introduction

Infectious salmon anaemia virus (ISAv) causes a devastating disease in farmed Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar L.), triggering hemorrhages and severe anemia that can result in high mortality rates if not controlled [[1], [2], [3]]. The first report of ISAv outside of Norway [4], was from New Brunswick, Canada, in 1996 [5], with subsequent reports from Maine (USA), Chile, Scotland, and the Faroe Islands. In Chile, for example, the first detection of ISAv in Atlantic salmon was in the winter of 2007 [6], which resulted in the loss of approximately 1 billion USD over the succeeding four years [7]. Clinical disease has only been reported in Atlantic salmon. However, the virus has been isolated from other species with no clinical disease or with mild symptoms, including brown trout (S. trutta L.), rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss Walbaum) [3], Pacific coho salmon (O. kisutch Walbaum) [6], and Amago trout (O. masou Günther) [8]. The virus' potential to cause epidemics and severe outcomes in aquaculture was recognized in the early 1990s, leading to its classification as a reportable disease by the World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH).

Infectious salmon anaemia virus (ISAv) belongs to the family Orthomyxoviridae, genus Isavirus. It is an enveloped virus characterized by mushroom-shaped surface projections of hemagglutinin-esterase (HE) and fusion glycoprotein (F). The HE protein functions both as a receptor-binding molecule and as a receptor-destroying enzyme through its esterase activity [1,9]. Strain designation of ISAv isolates to a specific genotype is based on a number of deletions in the highly polymorphic region (HPR) of the HE protein, located on segment 6 of the viral genome [10]. HPR4 is a virulent isolate of ISAv belonging to the North American genotype and has been associated with mortalities in the Bay of Fundy [11,12].

Temperature is a risk factor involved in primary ISAv outbreaks. Outbreaks show increased risk in the spring and late autumn/early winter with higher incidence associated with extended periods where temperature remains below 12 °C [13]. Groves et al. [14] demonstrated that temperature significantly impacts disease progression and mortality kinetics in Atlantic salmon, with mortality peaked at approximately 300 degree-days post-challenge in both temperature treatments, the disease mortality curve differed markedly. At 20 °C, mortality was characterized by an acute peak occurring from days 28–31 post-infection, resulting in rapid but ultimately lower cumulative mortality. Conversely, at 10 °C, the mortality peak was delayed to days 34–37, creating a prolonged period of moderate mortality that ultimately resulted in higher overall losses. These temperature-dependent mortality patterns likely reflect the complex interaction between viral replication kinetics, host immune capacity, and thermal physiology. Atlantic salmon families exhibit substantial variation in thermal tolerance [15], which is a polygenic trait for which no significant quantitative trait loci have been identified [16,17]. Previous transcriptomic studies of heat stress have demonstrated temperature-induced changes in immune-related gene expression [18], suggesting that thermal stress may modulate antiviral responses and disease susceptibility.

It is well established that family genetics can influence progression in many aquatic animal diseases. However, only a few studies have focused on the genetic resistance of Atlantic salmon families to ISAv infection [14,19,20]. Groves et al. [14] highlighted the interplay between rising temperature and familial genetic resistance by infecting Atlantic salmon from susceptible and resistant families with ISAv HPR4 at 10 °C and 20 °C. The results confirmed the presence of familial resistance to ISAv infection and showed different mortality patterns and immune responses at both temperatures. At 10 °C, there was a higher viral load and overall mortality, along with a stronger innate antiviral response, evidenced by the differential regulation of specific genes, such as mx1, isg15, vprn, il12rb2, il10, and il4/13a.

This study builds upon the findings of Groves et al. [14]. We conducted further analysis to identify transcriptomic differences between North American Atlantic salmon infected by ISAv at 10 °C and 20 °C, with an emphasis on familial resistance. We utilized samples from the top two resistant and bottom two susceptible families at both temperatures. The goal of this study was to identify potential interactions between genetic resistance and temperature that may uncover key pathways involved in resistance.

Methods

Description of study samples

In this study, we used bulk RNA sequencing (RNAseq) to generate data from head kidney samples of Atlantic salmon, as detailed by Groves et al. [14].

In the original, study the husbandry practices were in accordance with the guidelines of the Canadian Council of Animal Care (Protocol # 20–017) and was approved by the University of Prince Edward Island Animal Care Committee.

Briefly, approximately 100 fish from each of 20 Atlantic salmon families were exposed to infectious salmon anemia virus (ISAv) via cohabitation at two temperatures—10 °C and 20 °C. Forty head kidney samples were collected at the initiation of mortality which is expected to correspond to the highest viral load in the experimental fish.

Degree days (the cumulative sum of daily water temperatures) post-exposure were approximately 302–312 at 10 °C and 288–308 at 20 °C, aligning with peak mortality events in each temperature group. We collected head kidneys of five fish from each of the two families with the highest survival rates (termed 'resistant') and the two families with the lowest survival rates (termed 'susceptible') from each temperature, yielding 40 RNA samples in total. Families 7 and 17 (Resistant) exhibited average survival rates of 81% and 93% at 10 and 20 °C, respectively, while Families 8 and 15 (Susceptible) had average survival rates of 32% and 54% at 10 °C and 20 °C, respectively. Transcriptome analysis with RNAseq

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from head kidney samples (approximately 50–100 mg per fish) using a modified Tri-Reagent and metal beads method [21]. The quality and integrity of the RNA were assessed using Thermo-Fisher’s Nanodrop 3000, followed by electrophoresis on 1.5% agarose gels. Each RNA sample (2–6 µg) was subjected to TURBO® DNase treatment to eliminate any residual contaminating DNA then submitted to Genome Quebec (https://genomequebec.com/en/) for library preparation (TruSeq3), and 100 bp paired-end Illumina HiSeq sequencing.

All sequencing result datafiles are uploaded to the public NCBI SRA database (PRJNA1359443).

Raw sequence read quality was assessed using FastQC [22] and summarized across all samples using MultiQC [23]. All samples yielded high-quality sequencing data suitable for downstream analysis.

The trimming of sequence data was performed using Trimmomatic v0.39 [24]. The following conditions were used to remove sequences: adaptors with a seed mismatch of 2, palindromes longer than 30 bases, simple sequences longer than 10 bases, low quality bases in the leading and trailing ends with quality score of 3, any 4 bases with an average quality below 15, and reads less than 36 bases [25].

All sequences were aligned to Salmo salar reference genome version Ssal_v3.1 (GenBank: GCA_905,237,065.2, RefSeq: GCF_905,237,065.1) by the Norwegian University of Life Sciences (Apr 21, 2021) using the STAR aligner [26,27]. Reads, provided in compressed gzip format, were decompressed during the alignment process as the output formatted as coordinate-sorted BAM files [28].

RNA sequencing yielded an average of 44.2 million reads per sample (range: 30.4–57.2 million), with an average mapping rate of 78.4% to the Atlantic salmon reference genome (range: 71.4–81.7%).

The quantification of gene expression levels from RNA-seq data was performed using the featureCounts tool, part of the Subread package [29]. Gene-level quantification was guided by the gene identifiers (gene_id) within the Salmo salar genome annotation in GTF format. The row count data are provided in Supplementary File 1.

A design matrix was created using the model.matrix() function specifying no intercept to independently estimate expression levels for each group identified in the RNA-seq data. The count data were filtered to exclude low or inconsistently expressed genes using the filterByExpr() function [30], which implements appropriate filtering for the experimental design. Count data were normalized for variations in library sizes and other systematic discrepancies among samples using the calcNormFactors() function [34] implementing the Trimmed Mean of M-values (TMM) approach. Dispersion parameters were estimated using the estimateDisp() function [35] to calculate trended and tagwise dispersion estimates essential for generalized linear model fitting.

Differential expression testing was performed using the quasi-likelihood framework implemented in glmQLFit() and glmQLFTest() functions. Contrast vectors were constructed to test the following four comparisons: (1) Resistant vs Susceptible at 10 °C (contrast: [0, 0.5, −0.5, 0, −0.5, 0, 0.5, 0]), (2) Resistant vs Susceptible at 20 °C (contrast: [0.5, 0, 0, −0.5, 0, −0.5, 0, 0.5]), (3) Resistant 10 °C vs 20 °C (contrast: [−0.5, 0.5, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0.5, −0.5]), and (4) Susceptible 10 °C vs 20 °C (contrast: [0, 0, 0.5, −0.5, 0.5, −0.5, 0, 0]). Genes were considered significantly differentially expressed at false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple testing. Results were extracted using the topTags() function [36] and sorted by p-value.

Functional annotation, cluster and pathway analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using clusterProfiler v4.0 [37,38] with the Salmo salar organism database (AH117347) obtained from AnnotationHub (Bioconductor). Over-representation analysis was conducted using the enrichGO() function for three ontologies: Biological Process (BP), Molecular Function (MF), and Cellular Component (CC). Enrichment testing used the hypergeometric distribution with Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple testing (p-value cutoff = 0.05, q-value cutoff = 0.05). All genes detected in the RNA-seq experiment served as the background universe for statistical testing.

GO enrichment analysis was performed for six distinct gene sets: (1) genes differentially expressed between Resistant and Susceptible fish at 10 °C, (2) genes differentially expressed between Resistant and Susceptible fish at 20 °C, (3) core resistance genes (significant at both temperatures), (4) genes responding to temperature only in Resistant fish, (5) genes responding to temperature only in Susceptible fish, and (6) genes responding to temperature in both resistant and susceptible fish.

Network cluster analysis was conducted to visualize functional relationships between GO terms identified by analysis. The cluster analysis was performed on the resistant vs susceptible at 10 °C and temperature-responsive groups to identify the distinct clustering patterns. The analysis incorporated all significantly enriched GO terms (FDR < 0.05) from all gene sets.

Networks were constructed using a gene-sharing approach where GO terms were connected based on overlapping gene memberships. Specifically, edges were created between GO terms when they shared at least 2 genes and exhibited a Jaccard similarity coefficient greater than 0.1 [39], calculated as the ratio of intersecting genes to the union of genes between term pairs. Node attributes included GO term descriptions, ontology categories (BP, MF, CC), statistical significance (FDR values), gene counts, and fold enrichment values. Network construction and visualization were performed using Cytoscape v3.10.3 [40].

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis [41] was conducted using the enrichKEGG() function from clusterProfiler [38], targeting the Atlantic salmon (organism code: 'sasa'). Gene identifiers were converted from Ensembl IDs to Entrez IDs using biomaRt v2.58.0 [42]. Pathway enrichment testing used q-value cutoff < 0.05 with minimum and maximum gene set sizes of 3 and 500 genes, respectively. Enrichment analysis was performed for the same six gene sets as the GO analysis to identify significantly enriched metabolic and signaling pathways.

Data visualization

Several R packages and software were used for visualisation including Volcano plots (Wickham, 2016), UpSetR package [43], VennDiagram v1.7.3 [44], enrichplot [45], pheatmap v1.0.12 [46], and RCy3 for Cytoscape [40,47].

Genome wide association study of genomic data

Phenotypic data and pedigree variance component estimates

A total of four different phenotypic measurements were used for this study: (1) resistant and susceptible at 10 °C, (2) resistant and susceptible at 20 °C, (3) resistant at 10 °C and 20 °C, or (4) susceptible at 10 °C and 20 °C. Using the phenotypic data, we evaluated the significance of fixed effects (namely tank and sex) using a univariate model in ASReml-R 4.1 [asreml flag in asreml package; 48] in R version 4.3.0 [49]:yijkl=μ+Tanki+sexj+βweightk+al+eijkl,where y is the data of the phenotypic measurements (i.e. either resistance [resistant or susceptible], or temperature [10 °C or 20 °C]), μ is the overall mean, Tanki is the fixed effect corrected for the tank that fish were in, and sexj is the fixed effect of sex (j = 1: male, 2: female, 3:unknown). weightk is the fixed covariate included in the model to account for the relationship between phenotype traits and pre-smolt weight of the kth individual, where β is the regression coefficient. The al is the random additive genetic effect, a ∼ N(0,Aσa2) of the lth animal, where A is the numerator relationship matrix, and eijkl is the random residual effect, e ∼ N(0, Iσe2), where I is the identity matrix. All fixed effects were statistically tested using the Wald test with the wald.asreml flag.

Genotyping and GWAS

A total of 81 samples from four families were successfully genotyped for a ThermoFisher Axiom genotyping array containing 55,725 single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers distributed evenly across the North American Atlantic salmon genome (50 K Axiom SNP array; fully described by [50]). DNA extraction from fin clips and SNP array genotyping was performed by IdentiGEN (Dublin, Ireland). Quality control filtering was performed in PLINK v1.90 (http://pngu.mgh.harvard.edu/purcell/plink/ [51]) according to the following criteria: retained markers with minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.05 (–maf flag), removed markers and individuals with > 10% missing data (–geno and –mind flags, respectively), and removed individuals that had pi-hat values over 0.9 as they were likely duplicates (–genome flag). The final dataset included a total of 41,411 SNPs and 81 individuals.

The package ASRgwas is a linear mixed model approach, which is used in tandem with ASReml-R to estimate genomic variance components and identify the genomic regions associated with ISAv resistance. The same linear mixed model used for the preliminary pedigree variance component estimates was used to estimate genomic variance components, except that the numerator relationship matrix (A) was replaced with a genomic relationship matrix (G) constructed using VanRaden [52]. To run the GWAS analysis, two additional parameters were added to the variance component estimation model. We fitted the regression of eigen vectors and the marker effect for each trait using the function gwas.asreml. The number of PC (npc) axes was included in the analysis; determined by a screeplot that was generated from the GWAS pre-processing step (pre.gwas). The best number of PC axes was four, which is where the curve in the screeplot leveled out. In this step, we also changed the MAF to 0.30 as the sample size we have is small and therefore, we did not want false positives (value chosen based on results from [53]). The p-value threshold (pvalue.thr) for significant loci was set to 5.0e-6 and incrementally lowered until the false discovery rate was under 5% (evaluated at the end of each run). The original threshold was under the 5% false discovery rate after the first run and was therefore left unchanged. Quantile-quantile (Q-Q) and Manhattan plots were generated using the qqplot and manhattan function of the qqman package in R [54].

Gene region discovery

Once the gene regions of interest were determined from the GWAS, rapid Ensembl v.109 [55] was utilized to find all associated genes (including a 50 K region either side of outlier SNPs) with S. salar. The Ensembl gene lists were then run in Metascape (http://metascape.org) [56] to generate a list of overlapping gene ontology (GO) terms.

Results

The following results for all comparative groups reveal many differentially expressed genes, for which the terms ‘upregulated’ and ‘downregulated’ refer to the contrasting familial resistance or temperature, not to the infection. At 10 °C, resistant fish displayed extensive differential expression relative to susceptible fish, with 3690 genes significantly regulated (1920 upregulated, 1770 downregulated). In contrast, at 20 °C, only 64 genes were differentially expressed between resistant and susceptible groups, with a bias toward downregulation (21 upregulated, 43 downregulated). Temperature comparisons revealed much broader transcriptomic shifts: resistant fish showed 7156 differentially expressed genes between 10 °C and 20 °C (3321 upregulated, 3835 downregulated), while susceptible fish exhibited an even larger response, with 8,004 genes regulated (3634 upregulated, 4370 downregulated). Mechanisms of resistance at 10 °C and 20 °C

Resistant vs susceptible families at 10 °C

Differential gene expression revealed 3690 DEGs significantly regulated (1920 upregulated and 1770 downregulated) when resistant families were compared to susceptible families at 10 °C (Fig. 3). A complete list of all DEGs is provided in Supplementary File 1.

GO enrichment analysis of the significant DEGs identified 67 enriched biological processes, 11 molecular functions, and 14 cellular components. (Supplementary File 2). The most highly enriched terms were DNA unwinding involved in DNA replication (6.07-fold enrichment), ATP metabolic process (3.63-fold), and aerobic respiration (2.50-fold) among biological processes; proteasome-activating activity (5.21-fold enrichment), RNA helicase activity (2.88-fold), and ATP-dependent activity acting on RNA (2.84-fold) among molecular functions; and proton-transporting two-sector ATPase complex catalytic domain (6.15-fold enrichment), proteasome regulatory particle (4.92-fold), and proteasome accessory complex (4.21-fold) among cellular components.

Network analysis using Cytoscape of the 92 significantly enriched GO terms (FDR < 0.05) from genes differentially expressed between resistant and susceptible salmon at 10 °C revealed eight distinct functional clusters based on shared gene memberships between GO terms (Fig. 4, Supplementary File 2). The network clustering algorithm grouped GO terms that share significant numbers of overlapping genes, creating interconnected modules representing coordinated biological processes. The largest cluster (C1, 59 terms, 64% of all terms) formed a highly interconnected network dominated by nucleotide and energy metabolism processes. The second largest cluster (C2, 16 terms, 17%) connected nucleic acid processing functions, particularly DNA replication and RNA processing activities. A tightly connected proteasome complex cluster (C3, 7 terms) contained highly significant protein degradation machinery terms. The remaining smaller clusters included protein folding chaperone complexes (C4, 3 terms), supramolecular assembly components (C5, 2 terms), RNA 3′-end processing (C6, 2 terms), endocytic machinery (C7, 2 terms), and receptor internalization (C8, 1 term). Resistant vs susceptible families at 20 °C

Differential gene expression revealed 64 DEGs significantly regulated (21 upregulated and 43 downregulated) when resistant families were compared to susceptible families at 20 °C (Fig. 1). A complete list of these DEGs is provided in Supplementary File 2. This represents a dramatic decrease in transcriptional distinction between resistant and susceptible families at the higher temperature compared to 10 °C. GO enrichment analysis and KEGG pathway analysis of the significant DEGs revealed no significantly enriched biological processes, molecular functions, cellular components, or pathways, likely due to the small number of genes and their diverse functional roles.

Shared genes from resistant vs susceptible families at 10 °C and 20 °C (Core resistance)

A total of 34 DEGs were consistently regulated in both temperature comparisons, representing core resistance genes that are temperature-independent (12 upregulated and 22 downregulated) (Table 2). A complete list of these core resistance DEGs is provided in Supplementary File 1. GO enrichment analysis identified four significantly enriched molecular function terms, though these enrichments are likely driven by the small gene set size. The enriched terms involved two genes: acvr1l (activin A receptor, type 1 like), which was associated with three related terms involving TGF-β receptor activity and transmembrane receptor protein serine/threonine kinase activity, and grm3 (glutamate receptor, metabotropic 3), which was associated with glutamate receptor activity (Supplementary File 2). KEGG pathway analysis revealed no significantly enriched pathways, consistent with the limited number of genes and their diverse functional roles.

Modulation of transcription by temperature in resistant and susceptible families

We found temperature modulated transcriptional responses in both resistant and susceptible families, with distinct patterns between the two groups (Fig. 2), where complete lists of temperature-responsive DEGs are provided in Supplementary File 2. These distinct transcriptional profiles (Fig. 2, Fig. 3) were further characterized through GO term enrichment analysis (Table 3, Supplementary File 2) and KEGG pathway analysis (Supplementary File 4).

Discussion

The virulence, pathogenicity, and replication success of ISAV virus in Atlantic salmon are heavily influenced by viral strain variations and host immune responses [57], as well as environmental factors such as water temperature and season [1]. There are very few studies to date that have compared familial resistance mechanisms using transcriptomic responses in infected Atlantic salmon, which could provide important insights when dealing with ISAv infection in aquaculture settings [19,20,58]. Our results demonstrate that host transcriptomic responses vary dramatically with both family genetics and temperature, highlighting the complex interplay between these factors, and possibly others, in determining disease outcomes.

Resistant and susceptible families at 10 °C: GO antiviral responses

The transcriptomic distinction between resistant and susceptible families were dramatically temperature-dependent. There were 3690 differentially expressed genes between resistant and susceptible families at 10 °C compared to only 64 genes at 20 °C.

This approximate 58-fold reduction may be due to several potential mechanisms including reduced viral persistence at elevated temperature [14,59], energetic constraints that limit complex immune responses such as adaptive immunity [19,20,58] or the extensive temperature-induced changes in both phenotypes (7156–8004 DEGs) that may have overlapped resistance response.

In Groves et al. [14], at 10 °C, a test of an immune gene panel including Myxovirus (influenza virus) resistance 1 (mx1), Interferon stimulated gene 15 (isg15), and others, showed that individual families elicited robust antiviral immune responses before and after infection (Time 0 to Time 1), with minor individual variations between families. This suggests that these genes are important antiviral mechanisms, but they do not explain the differential resistance between families. This was also true in Dettleff et al. [19], where they found increased expression of Mx transcripts in the susceptible families and similarly suggested their minor role in determining the outcome of infection. In this study we report extensive transcriptional changes that could explain the observed familial resistance, which is not achieved only by direct antiviral response but rather by complex host-pathogen interactions. At 10 °C, we described 92 enriched GO terms grouped into 8 independent clusters based on shared genes and 15 enriched pathways. For the scope of this paper, we will focus on terms with relevance to direct antiviral response. There was clear major downregulation of metabolic and energy processes in the resistant families at 10 °C (Fig. 4, Cluster 1). Some of the most highly enriched terms include proton-transporting two-sector ATPase complex, catalytic domain, ATP biosynthetic process, electron transfer activity, proton motive force-driven ATP synthesis, ATP metabolic process, and pyridine nucleotide catabolic process. All of these terms and the overwhelming majority in this cluster showed strong downregulation in their constituent genes. This was also further demonstrated by KEGG pathway analysis (Fig. 5, Supplementary File 4). The enriched pathways highlighted the critical down regulation of energy metabolism (including glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, citrate cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation.

This metabolic suppression is significant because many viruses, including orthomyxoviruses, are known to enhance host anabolic pathways (such as glycolysis, ATP synthesis, and the TCA cycle) to generate the energy and building blocks needed for viral replication. Influenza virus (Orthomyxovirus) has been shown to upregulate glycolysis and manipulate ATPase activity in host cells, directly linking metabolic changes to increased viral replication [60]. Moreover, treatment with drugs like BEZ235, which blocks PI3K and mTOR signaling pathways, reduced cellular glucose metabolism and impaired influenza virus production, leading to improved survival rates in infected mice [61]. Therefore, our results suggest that metabolic reprogramming is a possible scenario during ISAv infection, and it may be less successful in hijacking the cellular metabolic machinery in resistant families compared to susceptible ones. Reduction of the energy and resources available for DNA and RNA replication is observed in the significantly downregulated DNA replication and RNA processing activities and many other cellular activities in general in the resistant fish compared to the susceptible fish, which directly reflect the impact of viral replication.

In our study we noticed, among others, significant down regulation in most of the genes contributing to DNA unwinding, DNA duplex unwinding, DNA geometric change, RNA helicase activity, ATP-dependent activity acting on RNA, single-stranded DNA binding, helicase activity, DNA-templated DNA replication, and RNA splicing. The KEGG pathway analysis showed enriched pathways that included DNA replication, mismatch repair, and spliceosome (Fig. 5, Supplementary File 4). This corroborates results by Gervais et al. [58] revealing an overall downregulation in head kidney DEGs (genes involved in DNA processing, mRNA stability, RNA splicing, glycolysis, and nucleotide synthesis) at 7 days when resistant fish were compared to susceptible fish.

Our results revealed downregulation of proteasome genes in resistant families compared to susceptible families (Fig. 5 and Supplementary File 4). The downregulated genes were predominantly associated with the 19S lid and base (Regulatory Particle) and the 20S core particle, in addition to proteasome maturation protein (POMP), which activates the standard 20S proteasome into the immunoproteasome (Fig. 6). The proteasome plays a crucial role in processing viral proteins for presentation to CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) via major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I), thereby mounting an antiviral immune response [62]. However, proteasome regulation is complex, as several studies have highlighted the intricate interplay between viruses and the host ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) [63,64]. Other orthomyxoviruses, such as influenza virus, have demonstrated the ability to manipulate the host UPS for viral advantage by targeting entry inhibitors and immune receptors for degradation [65]. Proteasome system inhibition has been identified as an effective strategy to reduce viral replication [66], with similar responses reported for other fish viruses such as Singapore grouper iridovirus (SGIV) [67]. While we see resistant fish downregulating proteasome genes compared to susceptible fish, it is unclear if ISAv is able to recruit proteasome for its own advantage.

The GO analysis results show a highly significantly enriched upregulation in the receptor internalization biological process. The group contained eight genes, where seven were significantly upregulated: flot1a (flotillin 1a), an unknown gene ENSSSAG00000087860, arr3b (arrestin 3b/X-arrestin), sagb (S-antigen/arrestin b), ramp2 (receptor activity modifying protein 2), another unknown gene ENSSSAG00000104204, and NEDD4 (NEDD4 E3 ubiquitin protein ligase), and one was significantly downregulated: flot1b (flotillin 1b).

These genes likely play critical roles during viral infection. Flotillin proteins (flot1a and flot1b) form membrane lipid rafts that serve as specialized signaling hubs on cellular membranes [68], including phagolysosomal membranes in macrophages, which are key antimicrobial defense systems. (RAMP2) is a single-pass transmembrane protein that modulates the function of G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) by altering their trafficking, ligand binding, signaling specificity, and downstream cellular effects [69]. β-Arrestins (S-Antigen and Arrestin b) have an important role in termination of G-Protein Coupled Receptors (GPCRs) signaling by desensitization and internalization of the receptor. Hu et al. revealed that NF-κB-mediated accumulation of bile acids during viral infection activated the TGR5-GRK-β-arrestin signaling pathway, which subsequently activated SRC kinase [70]. SRC kinase then phosphorylated multiple critical antiviral signaling proteins including RIG-I, VISA/MAVS, MITA/STING, TBK1, and IRF3, thereby amplifying the cellular antiviral response through coordinated tyrosine phosphorylation of key immune signaling components. NEDD4 is a E3 ubiquitin ligase, that regulates protein stability, trafficking, and signaling by attaching ubiquitin to specific substrate proteins. NEDD4 tags target proteins with ubiquitin, marking them for degradation by the proteasome or altering their cellular fate [71,72]. NEDD4 can regulate antiviral proteins such as IFITM3, which increase cellular resistance to influenza virus infection [73] and enhance autophagy and host defense against intracellular bacteria, supporting the innate immune function [74].

Beyond the pathway-level analysis, examination of individual gene regulation patterns provides additional insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying ISAv virus resistance. To contextualize our findings within the broader research landscape, we compared our results with genes specifically highlighted by Dettleff et al. [19]. Our analysis revealed several convergent regulatory patterns, particularly in immune receptor regulation and adherent junction pathways. Among the genes Dettleff et al. (2017) highlighted, we found consistent downregulation of interleukin 13 receptors in resistant fish (il13ra2; il13ra1) and interferon gamma receptor 1a (ifngr1a). We also shared adherent junction upregulation, in which multiple cadherin family members were upregulated (cdh24b, FAT4, celsr2) along with protocadherin 19. We also confirmed upregulation of integrin family members (itgav, itgbl1) and found latent transforming growth factor beta binding protein 1 (ltbp1). These multiple gene family matches provide confirmatory evidence for similar regulatory pathways among the genes emphasized in their study. However, some differences emerged; while Dettleff et al. [19] reported downregulation of complement C7 in resistant fish and upregulation of claudin-4, we found upregulation of related complement components (C4; C8g) and downregulation of claudin family members (cldnf; cldn5b).

Resistant vs susceptible families at 20 °C

The resistant vs susceptible comparison specific to 20 °C identified 64 genes with no significant gene ontology terms or pathways. The increased temperature likely produced a broader transcriptional response that overlapped between resistant and susceptible groups, as demonstrated by our results showing 3245 shared genes that were regulated in both groups' temperature responses.

Core resistance: shared genes from resistant vs susceptible

The core resistance genes regulated at 10 °C and 20 °C included only 34 genes and was represented by 4 molecular functions that contained only 2 genes: activin A receptor (acvr1l) and glutamate receptor, metabotropic 3 (grm3). ACVR1 is a TGF-β superfamily receptor critical for bone, cartilage, and organ development across vertebrates [75,76]. In humans, ACVR1 mutations cause Fibrodysplasia Ossificans Progressiva (FOP), where injury triggers localized heterotopic ossification at trauma sites. However, zebrafish studies using the ortholog acvr1l revealed species-specific differences: fish carrying the FOP-associated Q204D mutation did not develop heterotopic ossification at injury sites following cardiotoxin injection or fin clipping, but instead showed ectopic ossification at distant sites including the body cavity and spine [77]

Beyond acvr1l and grm3, only seven of the remaining 32 core resistance genes could be assigned functional annotations through biomaRt, while 25 (74%) lacked gene-level annotation, reflecting the incomplete characterization of the Atlantic salmon genome. Among the annotated genes, DNA replication and repair functions were represented by rad54b, involved in homologous recombination, and rfc2 (replication factor C subunit 2), a component of the DNA replication machinery, both downregulated in resistant families. SNORA75, a small nucleolar RNA that guides chemical modifications of ribosomal RNA, was the most significantly differentially expressed core resistance gene (FDR = 8.24 × 10⁻¹⁸). Lipid metabolism was represented by tm6sf2b (transmembrane 6 superfamily member 2b), downregulated in resistant families; as ISAv is an enveloped orthomyxovirus that acquires its lipid bilayer envelope from host cell membranes during budding [78,79], differential regulation of lipid-associated genes may influence viral propagation efficiency. The remaining annotated genes included ppfia3 (PTPRF interacting protein alpha 3), involved in cell adhesion, which was upregulated, while limd2 (LIM domain-containing protein 2) and slc26a5 (solute carrier family 26 member 5) were downregulated. The functional diversity of these core resistance genes, combined with the high proportion of unannotated genes and absence of significant pathway enrichment, indicates that temperature-independent resistance to ISAv is mediated by distributed, non-redundant molecular mechanisms rather than a single coordinated pathway, consistent with the polygenic architecture identified in our GWAS analysis.

Transcriptomics meets genomics

Our transcriptomic data revealed that familial resistance to ISAv involves broad regulatory responses encompassing multiple molecular mechanisms. This finding is strongly supported by our genomic analysis, which demonstrated a polygenic architecture with no major QTL detected. These results align with those reported by Gervais et al. [20], who similarly found no major QTL but identified one significant QTL on chromosome 13. Notably, both studies highlighted the eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4 gamma 1 (EIF4G1) gene, though our genomic analysis showed this gene as suggestive rather than significant. In our study, EIF4G1 is located in a QTL locus on chromosome 14 that contained a genomic region harboring four notable genes: thymocyte selection-associated high mobility group box (TOX), protein phosphatase 1 regulatory subunit 7 (PPP1R7), UBX domain protein 7 (UBXN7), and EIF4G1. Importantly, EIF4G1 was significantly downregulated in resistant fish compared to susceptible fish (logFC = −0.51, FDR = 0.03). Given EIF4G1's crucial role in protein synthesis initiation, its downregulation in resistant fish may limit viral protein production by reducing overall translation efficiency, thereby contributing to the resistance phenotype.

Temperature and Atlantic salmon ISAv resistance

Familial response to temperature and high heritability of thermal tolerance in Atlantic salmon have been well documented [15,17,80], demonstrating that fish respond to temperature differently based on their genetics. Our results reveal that ISAv resistance also interacts with temperature response, as both resistant and susceptible families exhibited distinct transcriptional responses when comparing 10 °C and 20 °C conditions. However, since previous temperature tolerance studies have not examined disease resistance as a factor, it remains unknown whether the differential temperature responses observed in disease-resistant versus disease-susceptible fish translate to actual differences in thermal tolerance capacity.

High temperature up to 20 °C, approaching Atlantic salmon's thermal limit, was found to have no negative impact on innate immune response, including both antibacterial and antiviral functions in head kidney tissue. Previous research demonstrated that constitutive transcript expression levels of anti-bacterial genes (il1β, il8-a, cox2, hamp-a, stlr5-a, and irf7-b) were up-regulated by high temperature, while anti-viral genes (viperin-b, mx-b, and isg15-a) were either unaffected or down-regulated under these conditions [81]. An in vivo study, from which our samples originated, showed that Atlantic salmon at high temperatures actually exhibited higher survival rates (Table 1) following ISAv infection [14]. Although the mortality curve differed between temperatures, with peak mortality occurring earlier and at higher daily rates at 20 °C but then subsiding more quickly, the overall survival was better at the higher temperature. This improved survival at 20 °C may be partially explained by reduced viral fitness at elevated temperatures, as ISAv has been shown to have decreased survival in vitro at 20 °C compared to lower temperatures [59]. Here we document transcriptome observations, as most of the differentially expressed gene groups in both resistant and susceptible fish are not dominated by direct antiviral response pathways, suggesting that elevated temperature does not directly impact immune function but rather modulates cellular responses in a more complex way.

The qPCR data from Groves et al. [14] further support the role of temperature in modulating viral persistence, as head kidney samples collected at peak mortality showed a higher frequency of non-amplification (indicating viral clearance) at 20 °C compared to 10 °C, particularly in resistant families. We propose that the significant reduction in transcriptomic differences between resistant and susceptible families at 20 °C (64 DEGs) compared to 10 °C (3,690 DEGs) can be explained by an interaction between temperature, viral persistence, and host metabolism. At 10 °C, sustained viral replication maintained prolonged pressure on host cellular machinery, allowing the distinct molecular strategies of resistant and susceptible families to fully manifest, including the metabolic suppression, proteasome downregulation, and DNA replication changes described above. At 20 °C, despite aggressive initial viral replication reflected in earlier and higher peak daily mortality, the virus could not sustain replication, resulting in a compressed infection cycle with rapid viral clearance. This reduced duration of host-pathogen interaction limited the time for divergent transcriptomic strategies to develop between resistance categories. Additionally, the extensive shared temperature response (3245 DEGs common to both resistant and susceptible families) likely further masked any remaining resistance-specific transcriptional signals. This model remains a hypothesis that warrants further investigation through time-course viral load quantification paired with transcriptomic sampling at both temperatures.

Temperature exposure revealed fundamentally different molecular strategies between resistant and susceptible Atlantic salmon families. Resistant families responded to temperature change by engaging DNA replication, cell cycle processes, and chromosome organization pathways (including chromosome organization, and DNA unwinding involved in DNA replication), alongside energy metabolism through oxidative phosphorylation and ATP synthesis, with significant enrichment in valine, leucine and isoleucine degradation and DNA replication pathways. In contrast, susceptible families prioritized RNA processing machinery, including spliceosome function, ribosome biogenesis, and rRNA metabolic processes (particularly rRNA metabolic process, and rRNA processing), coupled with proteasome-mediated protein degradation pathways, showing significant enrichment in spliceosome and proteasome pathways. These divergent responses suggest that resistant fish employ a growth-oriented, energy-intensive strategy during temperature stress, while susceptible fish focus on RNA quality control and protein turnover mechanisms (Fig. 8).

In conclusion, this study documents significant transcriptomic differences between resistant and susceptible Atlantic salmon families, where mechanisms of ISAv resistance do not rely on specific antiviral responses but rather on broad resilience mechanisms involving upregulated receptor internalization pathways (including flotillin proteins, β-arrestins, and NEDD4 E3 ubiquitin ligase) and coordinated downregulation of the proteasome KEGG pathway, DNA replication processes, and translation initiation machinery, including EIF4G1 (logFC = −0.51, FDR = 0.03), which was also identified in genomic QTL analysis on chromosome 14. These resistance mechanisms intersect with differential temperature responses, as resistant families regulate DNA replication and energy metabolism pathways while susceptible families focus on RNA processing and protein degradation. This intersection suggests that ISAv familial resistance and thermal adaptation likely share common molecular pathways, with potential implications for selective breeding programs targeting both traits simultaneously.
