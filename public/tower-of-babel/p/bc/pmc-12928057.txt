A Study on the Validity and Reliability of the Turkish Translation of the Self‐Report Wender‐Reimherr Adult Attention Deficit Disorder Scale
Authors: Selin Karakaya, İrem Akyol Ertekin, Burçin Çolak, Bedriye Öncü
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesAttention‐Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) in adults extends beyond inattention and impulsivity, encompassing challenges in emotion regulation and organization. This study aimed to evaluate the validity and reliability of the Turkish version of the Wender‐Reimherr Self‐Report Scale (SR‐WRAADDS), developed based on the Utah Criteria for adult ADHD.MethodsThe study included 172 adults diagnosed with ADHD and 92 healthy controls. Participants completed the SR‐WRAADDS, Wender Utah Rating Scale (WURS), Adult ADHD Self‐Report Scale (ASRS), and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS).ResultsThe SR‐WRAADDS total score was significantly higher in the ADHD group (p < 0.001). Factor analysis demonstrated a four‐factor structure: attention deficit, emotional dysregulation, impulsivity/hyperactivity, and temperament, explaining 61.8% of the variance. Internal consistency was acceptable for all subscales (Cronbach's α = 0.67–0.86). SR‐WRAADDS scores correlated positively with WURS and ASRS, but not with HADS subscales. Test–retest reliability ranged from moderate to strong.ConclusionThe Turkish SR‐WRAADDS is a valid and reliable self‐report instrument that captures the multidimensional nature of adult ADHD, including emotional dysregulation. Its robust psychometric properties and diagnostic utility make it a powerful tool for both clinical practice and future research.

Introduction

Attention Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized primarily by patterns of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity, frequently observed in adolescents and young adults. These symptoms often lead to developmental and functional impairments. However, the symptomatology of ADHD extends beyond these core domains. Difficulties with planning, sustaining focus, memory, effort regulation, motivation, and the regulation of emotions and behaviors are among the most commonly reported challenges by individuals diagnosed with ADHD (Thapar and Cooper 2016).

Current evidence suggests that ADHD symptoms persist into adulthood in a substantial proportion of individuals diagnosed during childhood. The disorder continues inapproximately 60%–85% of cases during adolescence and in 40%–60% of cases in adulthood (Barkley 2015). In adults, ADHD is reported to have a prevalence rate of approximately 2.5%–4.9% in the general population and ranks among the most common psychiatric disorders (Simon et al. 2009).

The diagnosis of ADHD is clinical and should be established based on a detailed history of childhood symptoms, comprehensive psychiatric evaluation, and careful differential diagnosis (Öncü 2008). In addition to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) and Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders—5 (DSM) criteria, which primarily emphasize inattentive symptoms, the Utah Criteria have been established for diagnosing ADHD in adults (Wender 1971). According to these criteria, ADHD is characterized by seven core symptom clusters: inattention, hyperactivity, mood dysregulation, irritability and quick temper, reduced stress tolerance, disorganization, and impulsivity. The most notable difference between the Utah Criteria and current ADHD diagnostic criteria is that emotion dysregulations are considered not as a comorbid symptom but as an integral part of the clinical presentation.

Emotional dysregulation in individuals with ADHD denotes a disturbance in the capacity to accurately identify, express, and modulate emotional responses in accordance with situational demands. Clinically, this may present as severe temper outbursts, rapid mood fluctuations, low frustration tolerance, disproportionate reactions to minor stressors, and a pervasive sense of emotional overwhelm (Barkley 1998; Shaw et al. 2014). In large‐sample study conducted in 2020, emphasized the presence of emotional dysregulation in individuals with ADHD and highlighted the importance of its assessment (Reimherr et al. 2020). Although emotional dysregulation is not currently incorporated into the formal diagnostic criteria for ADHD, a growing body of literature conceptualizes it as a core dimension of the disorder rather than a mere comorbid or secondary feature (Mayer et al. 2022; Roselló et al. 2020; Sanabra et al. 2022; Welkie et al. 2021).

The Wender‐Reimherr Adult Attention Deficit Disorder Scale (WRAADDS) is a clinician‐rated instrument developed to assess the severity of ADHD symptoms in adults. It is based on the Utah Criteria for diagnosing ADHD in adulthood. Consequently, the WRAADDS focuses on adult symptomatology rather than childhood symptoms, covers a broader range of symptom domains, and presents ADHD from a dimensional perspective (Marchant et al. 2013). For more practical use in clinical settings, the SR‐WRAADDS was developed based on the WRAADDS (Marchant et al. 2015). The self‐report scale has been translated into languages such as German (Retz‐Junginger et al. 2017) and French, and has begun to be used internationally (Weibel et al. 2022).

The aim of this study is to determine the validity and reliability of the Turkish version of the Wender‐Reimherr Adult Attention Deficit Disorder Self‐Report Scale, which has a comprehensive capacity to assess emotional dysregulation, and to make the scale available as a supportive tool in the diagnosis of adult ADHD.

Method

Translation Process

The study was reviewed and approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Ankara University Faculty of Medicine (Approval No: I04‐185–22). Following the approval of ethics committee, the original English version of the SR‐WRAADDS were independently translated into Turkish by two members of the research team. The preliminary Turkish version was then administered to a pilot sample consisting of 20 healthy controls and 20 individuals with ADHD in order to assess clarity and comprehensibility. Subsequently, the Turkish form was back‐translated into English by an independent expert. Based on the feedback obtained from the back‐translation process, necessary adjustments were made, and the final version of the scale was used by the research team.

Participant Characteristics

The study included patients aged 18 to 65 who were diagnosed with ADHD according to DSM‐5 and Wender Utah criteria, and who applied to the Department of Psychiatry at Ankara University Faculty of Medicine. A control group consisting of individuals without active psychiatric complaints was also recruited. Patients with intellectual disability (IQ < 80), those diagnosed with schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder, and patients with bipolar disorder currently experiencing depressive, manic, or hypomanic episodes were excluded. Nine participants from the control group were excluded from the study due to psychotropic medication use. All participants were thoroughly informed about the study, and those who provided written informed consent were included. Participants were first administered the Wender Utah Rating Scale (WURS), the Adult ADHD Self‐Report Scale (ASRS), and the SR‐WRAADDS to evaluate ADHD symptoms.

Measures

Self‐Report Wender‐Reimherr Adult Attention Deficit Disorder Scale

The SR‐WRAADDS is a 61‐item self‐report scale assessing ADHD symptoms, using a five‐point Likert format (0 = No or very mild, 4 = Very severe). In the original scale, seven domains were defined (attention difficulties, hyperactivity/restlessness, temper, emotional over‐reactivity, affective lability, disorganization, impulsivity, not otherwise specified), and a summary score was calculated for each domain. Subsequently, a two‐factor structure was identified through factor analysis for these domains. One factor includes the domains of attention difficulties and disorganization, while the other factor encompasses hyperactivity/restlessness, temper, affective lability, emotional over‐reactivity, and impulsivity. The scale includes additional domains assessing oppositional behaviors, social functioning, academic difficulties, and other miscellaneous symptoms. Although clinically informative, these are not part of the original domains, as they do not represent the core features of the disorder. As the score increases, the severity of ADHD symptoms increases. The structure of SR‐WRAADDS allows for item‐level or domain‐based analysis, making it suitable for both clinical assessment and research purposes. In a study conducted by Marchant et al. in 2015, it was shown to correlate with the WRAADDS and to be suitable for clinical use (Marchant et al. 2015).

Wender Utah Rating Scale

The Wender Utah Rating Scale is a self‐report instrument developed by Ward et al. in 1993, designed to retrospectively assess childhood ADHD symptoms and aid in the diagnosis of ADHD in adults (Ward et al. 1993). The short form consists of 25 items rated on a five‐point Likert format (0 = Not at all, 4 = Very much). The factors of the Turkish version of the WURS consist of irritability, depression, school problems, behavioral problems/impulsivity, and inattention. The validity and reliability of the Turkish version were established in a study conducted in 2005 (Öncü et al. 2005).

Adult ADHD Self‐Report Scale

The Adult ADHD Self‐Report Scale was developed by the World Health Organization for screening purposes (Kessler and Üstün 2004). The scale consists of two subscales, each containing nine items: “inattention” and “hyperactivity/impulsivity.” The questions assess the frequency of each symptom within the past 6 months. It evaluates 18 DSM‐based symptoms. The Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted in 2009 (Doğan et al. 2009).

Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale

The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale was developed by Zigmond and Snaith in 1983. It is a brief self‐report measure designed to quickly and reliably assess levels of anxiety and depression (Zigmond and Snaith 1983). Its Turkish adaptation and validity‐reliability study were conducted by Aydemir et al. in 1997, demonstrating that the scale is suitable for Turkish culture (Aydemir 1997).

Data Analysis

The construct validity of the SR‐WRAADDS was examined through factor analytic procedures. First, item scores were summarized within each domain, consistent with the original scale structure, which includes 30 ADHD‐related items representing the seven core domains. A factor analysis was initially conducted based on these domain scores. Subsequently, a Principal Component Analysis with Varimax rotation was performed on the ADHD‐related items. However, an interpretable factor structure could not be obtained. Therefore, a second factor analysis was conducted at the item level for the 30 ADHD items.

Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach's α coefficients for the total scale, subscales, and additional supplemental domains. Test–retest reliability was assessed in a subsample of participants from both the ADHD and control groups who completed the SR‐WRAADDS twice with a 4‐week interval. Spearman's rank correlation coefficients were calculated to determine the strength of the relationships between the first and second administrations of each subscale and the total score.

Convergent validity was evaluated using Spearman's correlation analyses between the SR‐WRAADDS, ASRS, and WURS scores. For discriminant validity, Mann–Whitney U tests were used to compare SR‐WRAADDS scores between the ADHD and control groups. Non‐parametric tests were preferred due to non‐normal score distributions. Additionally, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to determine the scale's ability to distinguish individuals with ADHD from controls. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

Statistical analyses were conducted using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 14 (SPSS Inc).

Results

The study included 172 individuals diagnosed with ADHD and 92 control individuals without active psychiatric complaints. No statistically significant difference was found between the groups in terms of mean age (ADHD group: 25.2 ± 6.43; control group: 25.80 ± 4.61; p = 0.39). The gender distribution was similar in both groups. (p = 0.48). Likewise, no significant difference was observed between the groups regarding marital status (p = 0.41). Although the proportion of university graduates was high in both groups, the rate was 52.9% in the ADHD group and 72.8% in the control group, and this difference was statistically significant (p < 0.001). Factor Analysis

Bartlett's Test of Sphericity was significant (χ 2 = 5333.01, df = 435, p < 0.001), indicating that the data were appropriate for factor analysis.

To evaluate the construct validity of the scale, Principal Component Analysis with Varimax rotation was performed. The analysis revealed that the SR‐WRAADDS consists of a four‐factor structure, explaining 61.8% of the total variance. The identified factors were classified as attention deficit, emotion dysregulation, impulsivity/hyperactivity, and temperamental traits. Internal Consistency Analysis

The internal consistency of the SR‐WRAADDS was evaluated using Cronbach's α coefficients. In all sample high levels of internal consistency were obtained for the subscales of attention deficit (0.75), emotion dysregulation (0.76), and impulsivity/hyperactivity (0.67). and temperament traits (0.81), The results are presented in Table 3. Corrected item‐total correlations ranged from 0.58 to 0.98 across all subscales and domains, indicating that the items showed adequate correlations with their respective subscales and domains. Cronbach's α if item deleted values were also examined to determine the contribution of each item to the reliability of its subscale, confirming satisfactory internal consistency across the entire scale. The total score for ADHD factors demonstrated a Cronbach's α of 0.81, indicating good overall reliability.

Test‐Retest Correlation

To evaluate the temporal stability of the scale, a test‐retest procedure was conducted with 34 control and 35 ADHD individuals, with a 4‐week interval between the two administrations.

Test–retest reliability was assessed using Spearman's rank‐order correlations(ρ) in ADHD group. The coefficients indicated satisfactory temporal stability across the scale dimensions, with values of ρ = 0.79 for attention deficit, ρ = 0.77 for impulsivity/hyperactivity, ρ = 0.66 for emotion dysregulation, ρ = 0.63 for temperamental traits, ρ = 0.58 for oppositional impairment, ρ = 0.73 for academic problems in adulthood, and ρ = 0.62 for social adaptation in adulthood. For the total score comprising 30 items, the correlation was ρ = 0.74 (p < 0.001). Collectively, these findings provide evidence for moderate to high test–retest reliability, supporting the robustness of the instrument over time.

Test–retest reliability was also assessed using Spearman's rank‐order correlations (ρ) in the control group. In the correlation analyses, significant positive correlations were observed between the initial and repeated administrations for the SR‐WRAADDS inattention subscale (ρ = 0.499, p = 0.003), the temperament traits subscale (ρ = 0.375, p = 0.02), the impulsivity/hyperactivity subscale (ρ = 0.351, p = 0.04), and the emotional dysregulation subscale (ρ = 0.401, p = 0.01). For the SR‐WRAADDS total score, a moderate correlation was also found between the test and retest administrations (ρ = 0.359, p = 0.03).

Correlation Analysis

Since the data in the ADHD group were not normally distributed (Kolmogorov –Smirnov test, p < 0.05), Spearman's rank correlation coefficients (ρ) were used to examine the convergent and discriminant validity of the SR‐WRAADDS. Correlations were calculated between the SR‐WRAADDS total and subscale scores and established external measures of ADHD symptoms and related traits (the ASRS and WURS subscales). The analysis revealed significant positive correlations between the SR‐WRAADDS and the ASRS and WURS subscales supporting the convergent validity of the instrument. The SR‐WRAADDS total score, calculated by including only the items retained in the factor structure, was found to be very strongly correlated with the ASRS total score (ρ = 0.764, p < 0.01), further confirming its construct validity.

At the subscale level, a particularly high correlation was observed between the SR‐WRAADDS attention deficit subscale and the ASRS inattention subscale (ρ = 0.719, p < 0.01). The SR‐WRAADDS temperament traits subscale showed significant correlations with the WURS irritability (ρ = 0.601) and WURS depression (ρ = 0.456) subscales (p < 0.01). ROC Analysis

The ROC analysis indicated that the total score of the 30‐item SR‐WRAADDS, based on factor analysis, had strong discriminative ability in differentiating individuals with ADHD from controls (AUC = 0.857, SE = 0.023, 95% CI = 0.813–0.901, p < 0.001). The optimal cut‐off score was determined at 57.5 based on the maximum Youden Index, yielding a sensitivity of 75.0% and a specificity of 89.1%. For practical clinical application, this threshold may be rounded to 58.

Discussion

In this study, the validity and reliability of the Turkish version of the SR‐WRAADDS were evaluated, and the findings demonstrated that the scale possesses adequate psychometric properties for assessing adult ADHD.

Previous research and clinical experience indicate that individuals diagnosed with ADHD in childhood often continue to experience symptoms into adulthood; however, differential diagnosis of adult ADHD remains challenging. Even in subthreshold presentations, functionality may be impaired in adults with ADHD. Therefore, it is recommended that, during assessment, the focus be placed less on the number of diagnostic criteria met and more on the individual's functional impairment and the effort required to maintain that level of functioning (Tufan and Yaluğ 2010).

It has been reported that at least 65% of adolescents diagnosed with ADHD have a comorbid psychiatric disorder (Öncü 2008). Since comorbidities are considered the rule rather than the exception in ADHD, clinicians need to review multiple disorders and conduct a detailed, high‐quality psychiatric assessment for accurate diagnosis (Asherson et al. 2016; Biederman 2004). Due to developmental characteristics and comorbidities, the symptoms and problem areas observed in adults differ significantly from those seen in children (Öncü 2016).

The use of only DSM‐5 or ICD‐11 diagnostic criteria limits the assessment of ADHD in adults to a restricted list of symptoms. As longitudinal studies have shown, problems related to hyperactivity and impulsivity tend to decrease in adults, while issues associated with inattention persist more permanently. Moreover, unlike children, adults experience difficulties across nearly all areas of life (Öncü 2008; Ramsay 2017; Taylor et al. 2011). The most frequently overlooked issues, however, are symptoms of inattention and emotional problems (Taylor et al. 2011).

It is estimated that up to two‐thirds of individuals with ADHD exhibit impairments in emotional regulation capacities (Shaw et al. 2014; Surman et al. 2013). These deficits have been associated with more severe clinical presentations, an increased risk of psychiatric comorbidities, and significant impairments in overall functioning (Mayer et al. 2022; Roselló et al. 2020; Sanabra et al. 2022; Welkie et al. 2021).

In the original SR‐WRAADDS, as in the WRAADDS, factor analysis was conducted using summary scores for the seven domains (attention difficulties, hyperactivity/restlessness, temper, affective lability, emotional over‐reactivity, disorganization, and impulsivity). However, when we applied the same scoring system as the original scale, factor analysis could not be performed due to the results being highly similar. This difference in factor structure may be attributable to the characteristics of the Turkish sample. Therefore, in order to obtain a structure more suitable for the Turkish population, the 30 items of the original scale were included in the factor analysis, and the items loaded onto four factors consistent with the original instrument. The factors were named in a way that corresponded to the item distribution in the original scale.

Unlike other ADHD scales, this version appropriately assessed temperament and emotional dysregulation. The SR‐WRAADDS stands out as a uniquely comprehensive self‐report instrument for assessing ADHD in adults, with a particular emphasis on emotion dysregulation—a feature absent in other scales available in the Turkish language. The scale demonstrated high internal consistency coefficients, and confirmatory factor analysis substantiated its four‐factor structure within the Turkish sample. As in the original scale, oppositional impairment, academic problems in adulthood, and social adaptation in adulthood were not included in the factor analysis; however, the items assessing these domains demonstrated high internal consistency. These items may provide useful information for clinicians in the treatment and follow‐up of individuals with ADHD.

Regarding criterion validity, significant and robust correlations were observed between the SR‐WRAADDS and both the ASRS and WURS scales, at the levels of total scores and subscales. These findings underscore the clinical utility of the SR‐WRAADDS as a valid and functional tool for the assessment of adult ADHD. Importantly, no significant correlations were found between SR‐WRAADDS scores and anxiety or depression scores measured by the HADS, indicating that the SR‐WRAADDS operates independently of comorbid mood symptoms.

To further support its clinical applicability, a cut‐off score analysis was performed to aid diagnostic decision‐making. Since no definitive cut‐off value has been established for the total score of the 30 ADHD items in the original SR‐WRAADDS, the present findings should be interpreted as provisional and specific to the current sample. Nonetheless, given the heterogeneity of clinical populations, a cut‐off score of 58 may be pragmatically employed by clinicians as a supportive diagnostic criterion.

This study has several limitations. The sample was recruited from a single center, and the control group was composed of volunteers, which may introduce selection bias. The presence of comorbidities and medication status was not assessed in the ADHD group. Additionally, some items exhibited closely similar factor loadings, possibly due to the limited sample size. The Turkish version of the SR‐WRAADDS, whose validity and reliability have been demonstrated, is expected to serve as a valuable tool in enhancing the understanding of adult ADHD—particularly in relation to the emotion dysregulation domain—and to provide clinical guidance throughout monitoring and follow‐up processes. Moreover, it can be regarded as a qualified assessment instrument suitable for use by researchers in future studies. In this context, future research is planned to employ the SR‐WRAADDS to monitor treatment and evaluate response in adults with ADHD, with particular attention to capturing changes in emotional dysregulation.
