Species Distribution, Antifungal Resistance, and Risk Factors of Candida Bloodstream Infections in a Tertiary Care Hospital in Iran: A Two-Year Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Hadis Jafarian, Fatemeh Ghasemi, Parisa Badiee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnderstanding trends in Candida infections and their resistance patterns is essential for improving patient outcomes and guiding effective treatment strategies. This study aimed to investigate the distribution of Candida species, their resistance to antifungal medications, and the risk factors associated with infection in a tertiary care hospital over a two-years period.Patients and MethodsWe performed a cross-sectional study at Professor Alborzi Clinical Microbiology Research Center, Shiraz University of Medical Sciences. Blood samples from patients suspected of having bloodstream infections were examined using the BACTEC system, and Candida species were identified using PCR-RFLP. Antifungal susceptibility testing was performed following CLSI guidelines.ResultsNinety-five cases were confirmed as candidemia. The most common species identified was Candida albicans (n=52, 54.7%), followed by Candida parapsilosis complex (n=18, 18.9%) and Nakaseomyces glabratus complex (n=12, 12.6%). Antifungal susceptibility testing indicated that, although C. albicans remained mainly susceptible to treatment, non-albicans Candida species showed reduced susceptibility, especially to azoles. The overall mortality rate was 57.8% (n=55), with the highest number of deaths occurring in intensive care units (41.8%, n=23/55). In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, increasing age, intensive care unit admission, and higher white blood cell count were independently associated with mortality.ConclusionNon-albicans Candida species with reduced azole susceptibility were common. Mortality was associated with intensive care unit admission, older age, and elevated white blood cell counts, while rapid antifungal therapy remained critical for improving survival.

Introduction

Candidemia remains a serious concern in hospitals, particularly in critically ill and immunocompromised patients.1 It is the fourth most common healthcare-associated bloodstream infection in the United States.2 Pooled European data indicate an incidence of around 3.9 cases per 100,000 population based on a meta-analysis of 107 European studies.3 Also, the candidemia occurrence in Asia reported as 1.2 episodes per 1000 patients, based on data from 25 hospitals across five countries.3 Its rate has increased by over 50% in the last decade4 owing to the widespread use of broad-spectrum antibiotics, invasive medical procedures, and immunosuppressive treatments.5 Identifying the most common etiologic agent and its sensitivity patterns is crucial for effective treatment and improvement of patient outcomes.

Several risk factors predispose patients to candidemia, including central venous catheters, broad-spectrum antibiotics, parenteral nutrition, abdominal surgery, chemotherapy, and gastrointestinal barrier disruption from medical interventions.6,7 These risk factors combined with antibiotic-induced dysbiosis, immunosuppression, chronic diseases, advanced age, and biofilm formation impair mucocutaneous barriers and promote opportunistic Candida invasion. These conditions contributing to substantial mortality.8,9 Candidemia often progresses to invasive infections and imposes a heavy economic fee. Mortality has risen significantly, caused by factors such as cirrhosis, malignancies, and ICU stay at diagnosis.10,11

The Candida species responsible for candidemia vary in different hospitals and regions. Recently, non-albicans Candida species have become more prevalent in many tertiary care centers.12 Resistance to antifungal medications, especially azoles, makes treatment more challenging.13 Despite advances in medical care, mortality rates of candidemia remain high, highlighting the need for early diagnosis and targeted treatment strategies.14

This study examined the prevalence of different Candida species, their resistance patterns, risk factors linked to infection, and patient outcomes over two years in a tertiary care center. By better understanding these factors, we can help improve antifungal stewardship and ensure better care for patients at risk for candidemia.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Population

This cross-sectional study was conducted at Professor Alborzi Clinical Microbiology Research Center, Shiraz University of Medical Sciences, for two years (from November 2023 to November 2025). Patients were included from different hospital wards. The Ethical Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (IR.SUMS.REC.1401.67 and IR.SUMS.REC.1401.672) approved the study protocol. The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the patients provided written informed consent. Demographic data, including sex, age, underlying disease, and risk factors (patients receiving broad-spectrum antibiotics, immunosuppressive therapy, parenteral nutrition, and hematopoietic transplantation), were collected from electronic medical records.

Isolation and Identification of Candida Strains

Blood samples from patients suspected of having a bloodstream infection were sent to the laboratory in BACTEC vials (Becton Dickinson, USA). Depending on clinical indication, one or more blood culture samples were obtained per patient. In cases where multiple cultures were collected from the same patient during a single infectious episode, these were considered as one case for analysis to prevent duplication. Candidemia was defined as the isolation of a Candida species from a blood culture of a patient exhibiting temporally associated clinical signs and symptoms, such as fever, chills, or hypotension. This test was repeated for patients whose cultures grew to more than one species.

After the positive alarm of the BACTEC system, each vial was subcultured on blood and eosin methylene blue (EMB) agar plates (Merck, Germany) in the microbiology lab. Plates that tested positive for yeast were referred to the mycology lab. Each isolate was subcultured and purified on Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) (Merck, Germany).

Molecular Identification

DNA of the isolated species was extracted as described by Lõoke et al15 Briefly, one or two freshly purified colonies were picked up and suspended in a solution containing 200 mM lithium acetate (Sigma, Germany) and 1% SDS (CinaClon, Iran). The mixture was incubated for 5 min at 70°C, and 300 µL of absolute ethanol was added. The microtubes were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 2 min, and the supernatant was discarded. An equal volume of 70% ethanol was added to the DNA pellet, and the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 2 min. Finally, the DNA pellet was dried at room temperature and suspended in 100 μL of deionized water.

Correspondingly, isolated Candida species were identified via PCR-RFLP according to Mirhendi et al.16 Briefly, the internal transcribed spacer region was amplified using ITS1 and ITS417 primers (1 μL each primer), Taq DNA Polymerase 2× Master Mix RED with 1.5 mM MgCl2 (Ampliqon, Denmark), and 2 μL extracted DNA (25 μL total volume). After restriction digestion with MspI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), the products were separated on a 2% agarose gel (SinaClon, Iran).

Antifungal Susceptibility Testing

Antifungal susceptibility testing was performed using corresponding CLSI protocols (M27 and M59).18,19 Amphotericin B (Sigma-Aldrich), fluconazole (Sigma-Aldrich), caspofungin (Sigma-Aldrich), voriconazole (Sigma-Aldrich), itraconazole (Sigma-Aldrich), posaconazole (Sigma-Aldrich), isavuconazole (Sigma-Aldrich), and luliconazole (Sigma-Aldrich) were tested using the broth microdilution method. RPMI 1640 medium (with L-glutamine, without sodium bicarbonate and phenol red) (Sigma Aldrich, USA) buffered with MOPS (3-N- morpholinepropanesulfonic acid) (Sigma Aldrich, USA) was prepared. Drug dilutions were prepared and distributed in 96-well plates at final concentrations ranging from 32 to 0.064 μg/mL for fluconazole; 8 to 0.016 μg/mL for amphotericin, caspofungin, voriconazole, itraconazole, and posaconazole; and 4 to 0.008 μg/mL for isavuconazole and luliconazole. The fungal inoculum was prepared from a 24-hour culture incubated at 35°C, standardized to 0.5 McFarland, and diluted in RPMI medium (1:20 and 1:50 dilutions). This was added to the serial dilution of the drugs (incubation for 24 h at 35 ◦C). The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of the drugs were determined according to M59.19 Breakpoints were used to categorize the isolates as sensitive, susceptible, dose-dependent, or resistant according to CLSI breakpoints where available. Candida parapsilosis ATCC 22019 was used as a quality control strain.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics software version 27 (USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic data. Continuous variables were presented as means and ranges, while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Associations between Candida species distribution and categorical variables were evaluated using the chi-square test. Fisher’s exact test was used when the expected frequency in any cell was less than five. Antifungal susceptibility results were interpreted according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M27-A4 guidelines. Minimum inhibitory concentration values were determined for each antifungal agent, and resistance rates were calculated for individual Candida species. Multiple logistic regression analysis was performed to identify the best predictors of mortality. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient Demographics and Clinical Characteristics

During the two years, blood culture samples from Namazi Hospital were received from the microbiology department of Professor Alborzi Clinical Microbiology Research Center. Candida species were identified in 95 blood cultures. The mean age of the affected patients was 43 years (range: 5–90 years). These patients included 59 (62.1%) males and 36 (37.9%) females. The mean length of hospitalization was 28.7 days. Seventy-seven of the 95 patients were hospitalized for seven days or more; of these, 64 were diagnosed with candidemia after seven days of hospitalization.

There was no significant relationship between corticosteroid use and Candida species distribution (P = 0.218). Regarding patient outcomes, 55 (57.89%) patients died, whereas 40 (42.1%) survived (Table 1). The highest crude mortality rate occurred in intensive care units (23, 41.8%). In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, increasing age (OR 1.034, 95% CI 1.013–1.055, P = 0.001), ICU admission (OR 5.45, 95% CI 1.34–22.12, P = 0.018), and higher WBC count (OR 1.111, 95% CI 1.004–1.231, P = 0.042) were independently associated with mortality. Antifungal treatment was associated with a significantly lower risk of death (OR 0.175, 95% CI 0.053–0.580, P = 0.004). In contrast, severe underlying disease and Candida species distribution were not independently associated with mortality after adjustment.

Distribution of Candida Species

Among the 95 Candida isolates, Candida albicans was the most prevalent species, accounting for 54.7% of cases (n=52). This was followed by the C. parapsilosis complex in 18.9% of cases (n=18) and Nakaseomyces glabratus in 12.6% of cases (n=12). Less frequently isolated species were C. tropicalis (6 cases, 6.3%), Kluyveromyces marxianus (1 case, 1.1%), Pichia norvegensis (1 case, 1.1%), C. lusitaniae/intermedia (1 case, 1.1%), and C. inconspicua (1 case, 1.1%) (Figure 1). The distribution of Candida species did not vary significantly across different age groups, sex, and underlying health conditions (P = 0.24, P = 0.68, and P = 0.14, respectively). Discussion

This study provides insights into the epidemiology and antifungal susceptibility patterns of Candida bloodstream infections at a tertiary care hospital over two years. Candida albicans, C. parapsilosis complex, C. tropicalis, and N. glabratus complex together often constitute the majority of isolated species in many settings. Although Candida albicans remains the most frequently isolated species worldwide, a clear shift toward non-albicans Candida has been observed. In the current study, C. albicans was the most frequently isolated species, accounting for 54.7% of cases. Similar to our study, C. albicans was the most commonly isolated species, accounting for approximately 49.5% to 54.5% of candidemia cases in previous studies in Iran.20,21 Regional data further demonstrate important epidemiological variability. In the Asia-Pacific region, C. albicans is prevalent in Australia, Japan, and Southeast Asia, while C. tropicalis dominates in Pakistan and India.22 Similarly, the ECMM Candida III study reported that C. albicans (47.1%) and C. glabrata (currently named Nakaseomyces glabratus) (22.3%) were the most prevalent species in Europe.23 However, the notable prevalence of non-albicans Candida species highlights the shift in the epidemiology of candidemia. This shift is clinically significant because non-albicans Candida species often exhibit reduced susceptibility to commonly used antifungal agents, particularly azoles, and in some cases emerging resistance to echinocandins, which may limit therapeutic options.5,21 In addition, the timely diagnosis of candidiasis remains a significant clinical challenge. Blood cultures, as the current standard diagnostic method, often require several days to yield positive results and may fail to detect deep-seated infections.24 Also, it can take time to provide species identification and susceptibility results. This delay may postpone the start of appropriate antifungal therapy. Although nonculture-based tests such as (1–3)-β-D-glucan and mannan assays can support earlier diagnosis, their sensitivity and specificity vary across clinical settings.24 For this reason, ongoing monitoring of Candida species distribution and antifungal resistance patterns, together with the adoption of faster, more accurate diagnostic tools, is essential to improving patient outcomes.

The observed mortality rate of 57% is concerning and may be attributed to factors such as delayed diagnosis, underlying comorbidities, and potential antifungal resistance. Chen et al reported a mortality rate of 32.8% in 134 patients.25 This difference is likely attributable to greater clinical severity in our study, as evidenced by the independent associations of ICU admission, older age, and elevated white blood cell count with mortality. In contrast to Chen et al,25 we did not observe an independent effect of comorbidities or Candida species distribution on outcomes. It suggests that the host’s clinical status, rather than microbiological factors, played a dominant role. Although antifungal therapy was protective in both studies, differences in patient severity and management may further explain the higher mortality in our population. A similar high mortality rate (59.26%) has also been reported in a cohort from Turkey including 81 adult patients, in which older age, ICU admission, and acute renal failure were identified as independent predictors of mortality.26 It is noteworthy that in the present study, 66 patients had positive fungal cultures after more than seven days of hospitalization. This pattern may suggest possible healthcare-associated acquisition, although the exact source of infection could not be confirmed, as molecular typing and epidemiological linkage analyses were not performed. Prolonged hospitalization has been linked to an increased risk of invasive procedures, broad-spectrum antibiotic use, and central venous catheterization, all of which are known risk factors for candidemia.2 The P-value of the age group and Candida species suggests a potential relationship between ICU admission and Candida species (P=0.06). Likewise, the P-value of infection after seven days and Candida species suggests a possible association, such as ICU admission (P=0.07). More data or a larger sample size may clarify whether this association is significant. Moreover, a significant relationship was observed between antifungal prophylaxis and the distribution of Candida species. This is the only significant result observed. This suggests that antifungal treatment may influence the predominant Candida species.

Antifungal susceptibility tests revealed species-specific variations. Candida albicans isolates were generally susceptible to caspofungin (MICGM = 0.030 µg/mL) and fluconazole (MICGM = 0.602 µg/mL), consistent with prior surveillance data.27,28 Compared to our previous studies, a higher MIC90 (1 µg/mL)29 against fluconazole and a lower MIC90 (0.5 µg/mL)30 against amphotericin were observed among C. albicans isolates. In contrast, C. parapsilosis exhibited a higher MICGM for fluconazole (3.299 µg/mL), indicating reduced susceptibility. Nakaseomyces glabratus showed elevated MICGM values for fluconazole (5.339 µg/mL) and itraconazole (1.059 µg/mL). Although C. albicans isolates are generally susceptible to antifungal agents, non-albicans species exhibit reduced susceptibility, particularly to azoles.20,21 Resistance to fluconazole ranges from 6.8% to 15%, with C. tropicalis showing non-susceptibility rates of 5.7–11.6% in China.22 Echinocandin resistance remains rare, but fluconazole resistance is observed in 12% of N. glabrata and 17% of C. parapsilosis isolates.23 Given the increasing reports of cross-resistance among azoles, luliconazole was included in this study because of its distinct imidazole structure. The low MIC values observed even among isolates with reduced susceptibility to other azoles suggest that luliconazole may retain activity despite resistance patterns commonly seen with conventional azole agents. Despite alarming trends in candidemia, some studies suggest that the overall level of antifungal resistance remains low. However, the increasing prevalence of non-albicans species and their associated resistance patterns necessitate improved diagnostic and treatment protocols to effectively manage candidemia. Accordingly, empirical antifungal therapy should be carefully selected based on local susceptibility patterns. Echinocandins are recommended as first-line therapy for invasive candidiasis due to their broad-spectrum activity and favorable safety profile.31 However, the emergence of resistance among non-albicans Candida species, particularly C. parapsilosis and N. glabratus, highlights the need for routine susceptibility testing and antifungal stewardship programs to guide appropriate therapy and prevent the development of resistance.32

Several limitations of this study should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, this was a retrospective study conducted at a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the results to the broader population of patients with candidemia. In addition, Candida species were identified using PCR-RFLP, a method with acceptable discriminatory power. However, this technique cannot reliably distinguish closely related species within certain Candida species complexes.

Conclusion

This study confirmed that C. albicans remains the primary cause of candidemia in our tertiary care center. However, the considerable prevalence of non-albicans Candida species, particularly C. parapsilosis complex and N. glabratus, highlights a growing epidemiological trend. The reduced susceptibility of these species to azoles, together with the emergence of echinocandins resistance among N. glabratus isolates, suggests that empirical fluconazole therapy may be insufficient in our setting. Mortality was associated primarily with ICU admission, older age, and elevated WBC counts rather than with the infecting species or underlying comorbidities. These findings emphasize the importance of early recognition of severe disease and timely supportive management. Notably, antifungal treatment was the only adjustable factor independently associated with improved survival. This is supporting the need for early initiation of appropriate antifungal therapy. Taken together, our findings support the need for continuous local surveillance of species distribution and antifungal susceptibility patterns, strengthened antifungal stewardship programs, and broader implementation of rapid non-culture diagnostic approaches.
