SLC7A11-mediated cystine accumulation and Rac1-WRC-Arp2/3-driven actin cytoskeletal folding synergistically induce disulfidptosis in acute myocardial infarction
Authors: Dongpu Shao, Dongmei Chen, Wenli Ma, Xinjie Wang, Huifeng Wang, Shunfang Zuo, Shuting Feng, Zihao Zhang, Han Sun, Yanbing Wu, Meng Cai, Jialin Chen, Wenwen Fu, Huali Xu, Honglei Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Disulfidptosis, a newly identified form of cell death, holds significant biological relevance; however, its role in cardiovascular diseases remains poorly characterized. To investigate this, disulfide bond formation was evaluated using non-reducing Western blot, while cytoskeletal alterations were assessed via phalloidin staining. In vivo experiments using a rat model of myocardial infarction (MI) and in vitro experiments using H9c2 cardiomyocytes subjected to oxygen‑glucose deprivation (OGD) both demonstrated upregulation of SLC7A11, NADPH depletion, and cystine accumulation under OGD or MI conditions. Notably, increased disulfide bonds in actin cytoskeletal proteins were observed, leading to cytoskeletal collapse. Inhibition of SLC7A11 rescued disulfidptosis across all models. Cellular knockdown of Nckap1 (a core component of the Rac1-WRC-Arp2/3 pathway) suppressed disulfidptosis, whereas overexpression of Rac1, Nckap1, or Wave2 promoted it. Spatial transcriptomic analysis of human infarcted myocardium further validated the concurrent upregulation of SLC7A11 and Rac1-WRC-Arp2/3 pathway components. Collectively, these findings indicate that MI induces disulfidptosis—a novel cell death modality mediated by activation of the SLC7A11 and Rac1-WRC-Arp2/3 signaling pathways.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of global mortality and morbidity, with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) being the most lethal condition1–3. AMI leads to decreased blood flow to the myocardial region and causes oncosis and necrosis of cardiomyocytes, whose loss primarily drives cardiac dysfunction4,5. Thus, reducing cardiomyocyte death during MI is essential for enhancing treatment outcomes and long-term prognosis4,6,7. ‌While current AMI therapies like thrombolysis, percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), and bypass surgery have markedly improved survival, their unpredictable complications underscore the urgent need for novel approaches to preserve myocardial function and prevent heart failure progression8–10. In recent years, new forms of cell death, such as ferroptosis, cuproptosis, and disulfidptosis, have been discovered11–14. ‌While ferroptosis is well-established in myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury, and upregulation of the SLC7A11-GSH-GPX4 axis combats ferroptosis and mitigates cardiomyocyte damage15–17, it remains unclear whether SLC7A11 upregulation is necessarily cardioprotective during MI development6.

Disulfidptosis, first proposed by Liu et al. in 2023, is a novel form of cell death driven by elevated SLC7A11 expression, which increases cystine levels13. This process necessitates substantial NADPH consumption to transform cystine into cysteine. However, when the glucose supply is limited, NADPH production is insufficient, leading to cystine accumulation and disulfide bond stress. This, in turn, causes folding and aggregation of the actin cytoskeleton, ultimately resulting in cell death. Therefore, high SLC7A11 expression and NADPH depletion are essential conditions for the occurrence of disulfidptosis. During AMI, the increased demand for reduced glutathione forces up regulation of the NRF2/SLC7A11 signaling pathway, increasing cystine and cysteine levels to produce more reduced glutathione and maintain the body’s redox balance18,19. Concurrently, glucose deprivation experienced by myocardial cells during AMI results in reduced NADPH production. Insufficient NADPH hinders cystine reduction to cysteine, causing cystine buildup and potential disulfide bond stress in cardiomyocytes, potentially initiating disulfidptosis. Given that research on disulfidptosis is still in its early stages, with most studies focusing on tumor cells20,21, there is a lack of investigation into its role in cardiovascular diseases. This presents significant opportunities and challenges in advancing our understanding of pathophysiological changes in MI and in the search for effective pharmacological treatments.

‌This study aimed to investigate the occurrence of disulfidptosis in cardiomyocytes during MI and to elucidate the associated molecular mechanisms. We first employed bioinformatic analysis to confirm the elevated expression of SLC7A11 at the onset of MI. Subsequently, we performed in vitro and in vivo experiments, including non-reducing western blot (WB) analysis, protein disulfide bond modificomics, and phalloidin staining, to confirm the increase in disulfide stress during MI. This stress leads to the folding and aggregation of the actin cytoskeleton, ultimately resulting in disulfidptosis. We also employed the oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD) model and utilized knockdown and overexpression techniques to validate that inhibiting the Rac1-WRC-Arp2/3 pathway could alleviate disulfidptosis. Ultimately, the key genes implicated in disulfidptosis were successfully validated in human MI cardiac tissues using both spatial transcriptomics and single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq). Collectively, we demonstrate that disulfidptosis—a novel cell death modality—occurs in MI. This study fills a research gap regarding disulfidptosis in the cardiovascular field.

Results

In vitro OGD model demonstrates intracellular cystine accumulation and NADPH depletion

To determine the expression of regulatory genes related to disulfidptosis in an AMI model, we performed a differential analysis of transcriptome sequencing data from a rat MI model. We found that six key genes positively regulating disulfidptosis were highly expressed in the MI model, while two genes negatively regulating it showed decreased expression (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Table 1). Among them, the two key proteins responsible for cystine transport, SLC7A11 and SLC3A2, were highly expressed in the MI model. Interestingly, TXNRD1, a crucial enzyme that reduces cystine to cysteine, also showed increased expression in the MI model. In the presence of cystine stress, Txnrd1 was upregulated, which was consistent with previous reports22. Nckap1, which encodes Nck-associated protein 1, serving as a core constituent of the WAVE regulatory complex (WRC), plays a role in regulating the actin cytoskeleton and displays a marked upregulation of expression in MI tissues13. We performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of the differentially expressed genes. When investigating the pathophysiological changes in MI, cardiac tissues are typically divided into three zones: IZ, BZ, and RZ23. High expression of Slc7a11 serves as a prerequisite for the occurrence of disulfidptosis. To determine in which zone disulfidptosis mainly causes severe damage to myocardial tissues, we used spatial transcriptomics to study the spatial distribution of Slc7a1123. This provided a certain basis for our subsequent experimental validation. We categorized clusters from day 1 and day 7 post MI (pMI) groups into three zones: IZ, BZ, and RZ. In the day 1 pMI group, clusters 0, 2-3, and 6–9, which were characteristic of genes involved in the mitochondrial functional pathway, such as Cox5a, Atp2a2, Ndufb9, and mt-Co3, were localized within the RZ (Fig. 1C–E). Cluster 4, which was associated with functions related to the response to muscle stretch, including Ankrd1, Csrp3, and Nppa, was specifically localized within the BZ. Clusters 1 and 5, which exhibited the expression of collagens, extracellular matrix, and inflammatory response genes such as Mmp9, S100a8, Flnc, and Il1rn, were localized within the IZ. For the day 7 pMI group, clusters 1–3 and 5-6, which are characteristic of genes involved in the mitochondrial functional pathway, such as Cox7a1, mt-Nd3, and mt-Co3, were localized within the RZ (Fig. 1C–E). Cluster 4, which demonstrated functions associated with the response to muscle stretching, including Ankrd1 and Nppb, was localized within the BZ. Lastly, cluster 0, which was characterized by the expression of collagen and extracellular matrix genes such as Col3a1 and Postn, was specifically localized within the IZ. The results demonstrated that 24 h pMI, the expression of Slc7a11 was significantly elevated compared to the sham group (Fig. 1F). Moreover, it was clearly observed that the expression of Slc7a11 was predominantly concentrated in the IZ, in contrast to the BZ and RZ (Fig. 1F, Supplementary Table 2 and 3). This suggests that disulfidptosis exerts severe damage on myocardial tissues within the IZ. Consequently, we focused our research on the IZ. However, on the day 7 pMI, the differential expression of Slc7a11 was mainly concentrated in the BZ and RZ regions (Fig. 1F).

To validate the hypothesis of cystine accumulation in the MI model, we initially conducted experiments using an in vitro OGD model. Our findings demonstrated a significant upregulation of Slc7a11 gene expression in this model, with an even more pronounced increase observed at the protein level (Fig. 1G–I). This upregulation suggests an enhancement of intracellular cystine transport. Furthermore, we measured the intracellular cystine levels and found them to be elevated in the model group, further supporting our hypothesis (Fig. 1J). We further investigated NRF2, the upstream transcriptional regulator of SLC7A11, and observed a significant elevation of NRF2 in the OGD model (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). This finding partially elucidates the mechanism underlying the increased expression of SLC7A11.

Under normal circumstances, the accumulation of cystine typically triggers a compensatory upregulation in the reducing capacity of the thioredoxin system. However, our results revealed a significant decrease in the key reducing agent NADPH within the thioredoxin system in the OGD model, accompanied by a notable increase in the NADP+/NADPH ratio (Fig. 1K–M). We hypothesize that this reduction in NADPH levels is attributed to both increased NADPH consumption and decreased glucose availability, which inhibit the pentose phosphate pathway, subsequently limiting the substrate necessary for NADPH production.

To validate this hypothesis, we assessed the levels of G6P and the activity of G6PDH. In the OGD model, we observed decreased levels of G6P and reduced activity of G6PDH (Fig. 1N, O). In instances of glucose insufficiency, inhibition of the glycolytic pathway may prompt a shift in energy metabolism towards the pentose phosphate pathway, thereby augmenting NADPH production. To substantiate this hypothesis, we introduced 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2DG), a glycolysis inhibitor, into the OGD model. The findings revealed that glycolysis inhibition led to elevated NADPH levels and a notable decrease in the NADP+/NADPH ratio (Fig. 1P–R). Glutathione, which constitutes a vital reducing system in the body, is closely associated with the thioredoxin system22. Our experimental data indicated that the GSH/GSSG ratio remained relatively unchanged in the model group, implying that reduced glutathione is unable to promptly alleviate the pressure exerted by cystine accumulation (Fig. 1S–U). Therefore, we identified a continuous accumulation of cystine and an increase in stress in the cellular OGD model.

Increased disulfide stress in the in vitro OGD model induces disulfidptosis

Non-reducing western blot analysis was used to check for disulfide bond formation in actin cytoskeleton proteins under OGD. Disulfide bonds are known to alter protein electrophoretic mobility under non-reducing conditions13. Notably, after 6 h of OGD in H9c2 cells, several actin cytoskeleton proteins showed diffuse banding patterns. Furthermore, some proteins displayed exceptionally high molecular weight bands in proximity to the stacking layer under these specific conditions (Fig. 2A). Conversely, these proteins failed to exhibit similar migration patterns under reducing conditions, emphasizing the distinct role of disulfide bond formation during glucose deprivation. This finding suggests that multiple intermolecular disulfide bonds are formed within these actin cytoskeleton proteins under OGD conditions. Furthermore, the reducing agent 2ME mitigated the retardation of the migration of actin cytoskeleton proteins under glucose starvation in these cells. We have previously shown that treatment with 2DG augments NADPH production via the pentose phosphate pathway. The increased NADPH can alleviate disulfide stress, which is manifested as a reduction in band tailing in non-reducing western blot. The administration of reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavengers, like Tempol or Trolox (Supplementary Fig. 2A, B), failed to mitigate the band tailing phenomenon in non-reducing western blot. To demonstrate that cell death due to increased disulfide stress is a novel form of cell death, we tested the efficacy of various common inhibitors in rescuing cell viability. In the OGD model, inhibitors of ferroptosis (ferrostatin-1 and deferoxamine), necroptosis (necrostatin-1), and autophagy (chloroquine) did not significantly improve cell viability in H9c2 cells (Fig. 2B). Notably, the apoptosis inhibitor (Z-VAD 10 µM) was able to rescue cell viability to a certain extent. However, apoptotic inhibitors did not prevent actin cytoskeleton folding or separation of the cytoplasm from the cell membrane (Fig. 2C).

Phalloidin staining indicated that in glucose-cultured H9c2 cells, actin filaments (F-actin) were mainly organized in the cell cortex and stress fibers. OGD notably altered the cell morphology, leading to cell shrinkage and F-actin contraction (Fig. 2C). Staining with F-actin and the membrane dye CellMask revealed that OGD caused F-actin to detach from the plasma membrane of H9c2 cells (Fig. 2C). The alterations in actin cytoskeleton morphology observed in these cells following OGD were dependent on SLC7A11 and could be reversed by treatment with erastin2 (a selective and potent SLC7A11 inhibitor), 2DG, or 2ME, whereas Tempol or Trolox had no such effect (Fig. 2C). Collectively, our data suggests that SLC7A11-mediated uptake of cystine, in conjunction with OGD, triggers severe disulfide stress, ultimately leading to aberrant disulfide bonding in actin cytoskeleton proteins in a ROS-independent manner.

To investigate the evolutionary process of actin contraction, we chose to observe the morphological changes in actin contraction at the 3 h mark of OGD. Initially, we assessed the cellular viability after 3 h of OGD and observed significant inhibition (Fig. 2D). At this point, the actin cytoskeleton had already undergone contraction, accompanied by mild folding and aggregation, and the plasma membrane began to detach. To further validate the correlation between cytoskeletal folding and abnormal accumulation of cystine, we supplemented the culture medium with additional cystine. When the cystine concentration reached 400 μM, the actin cytoskeleton exhibited pronounced folding and aggregation, with distinct plasma membrane detachment (Fig. 2E). As the cystine concentration escalated to 800 μM, the actin cytoskeleton fully folded and aggregated into clusters, the plasma membrane was completely detached, and the cells gradually underwent necrosis. Intriguingly, when the cystine concentration reached 800 μM, and either the SLC7A11 inhibitor (erastine2) or 2ME was introduced, the morphological alteration of actin cytoskeleton folding and aggregation was reversed (Fig. 2E). In summary, current evidence suggests that abnormal cystine accumulation leads to increased disulfide bond stress in actin cytoskeletal proteins, subsequently causing folding and aggregation of the actin cytoskeleton, ultimately resulting in disulfidptosis.

Validation of disulfidptosis occurrence in the infarcted region of an in vivo rat MI model

Since the varying estrogen levels in female rats at different stages of the estrous cycle may potentially influence the infarct size and cardiac function in myocardial infarction models, we exclusively selected male rats for the relevant in vivo experiments24,25. We confirmed the successful creation of an MI model in rats through ligation of the left anterior descending coronary artery (LAD) (Supplementary Table 4, Supplementary Fig. 3A–I). In the rat MI model, the myocardial region we selected remains the infarcted area. We analyzed the expression levels of the SLC7A11 gene and protein in a rat MI model. Aligned with the in vitro model findings, SLC7A11 gene and protein expression levels were elevated (Fig. 3A–E). And the expression of NRF2 protein, an upstream transcription factor of SLC7A11, is significantly elevated in the MI group (Supplementary Fig. 1C, D). Subsequently, we measured NADPH levels and NADP+/NADPH ratio. In line with the in vitro findings, NADPH levels decreased, whereas the NADP+/NADPH ratio increased (Fig. 3F–H). Furthermore, we assessed cystine levels, which were elevated in the MI model (Fig. 3I). We assessed the GSH/GSSG ratio to examine the effect of the glutathione reduction system on cystine metabolism and found no significant alterations in its reductive capacity (Fig. 3J–L). Using non-reducing WB techniques, we conducted a semi-quantitative analysis of the disulfide bond numbers. The results revealed a notable tailing phenomenon in the electrophoresis bands of the five actin cytoskeletal proteins in the MI model (Fig. 3M, N). Increase in disulfide bonds of actin cytoskeleton-associated proteins in infarcted rat tissue validated by disulfide bond modification proteomics

We conducted protein modification proteomics analyses to quantitatively assess disulfide bond sites in MI tissues, with the objective of testing the hypothesis regarding the formation of intra-protein disulfide bonds as well as inter-protein disulfide bonds in actin cytoskeletal proteins. These findings indicated an increase in the formation of 20 disulfide bond linkages (Supplementary Table 6). Seven actin cytoskeletal proteins contributed to the formation of 20 disulfide bonds (Fig. 4A). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of proteins involved in the 20 disulfide bonds revealed that significant enrichment in pathways associated with the cytoskeleton, actin cytoskeleton, and protein binding (Fig. 4B, C). Additionally, the number of different cysteine sites involved in the disulfide bonds formed by ACTB increased the most (Fig. 4D). Protein-protein interaction network analysis revealed that the actin cytoskeleton was the primary interaction cluster (Fig. 4E). Due to technical limitations, we could not detect disulfide-linked peptides between MYH9, FLNA, FLNB, and TLN1 proteins; however, we identified seven other disulfide bond linkages in cytoskeletal proteins, including ACTB C288-ACTB C275, ACTA2 C287-ITGA5 C960, ACTB C275-ITGA5 C960, ACTC1 C12-ACTB C377, NDUS1 C727-ANK2 C362, S100A9 C91-YY1 C357, and FBN1 C923-ZIC3 C359 (Fig. 4F–L, Supplementary Table 6). In addition to the increased disulfide bonds in cytoskeletal proteins, we also identified increased formation of disulfide bonds in 16 other types of proteins. Firstly, we confirmed that in the OGD model of H9c2 cells, the expression levels of NCKAP1, WAVE2, and Rac1 were all upregulated in the experimental group (Fig. 5B–E). Subsequently, we utilized small interfering RNA (siRNA) to knock down Nckap1 in H9c2 cells. We validated the knockdown efficiency of three siRNAs targeting NCKAP1 at the mRNA level. The results revealed that siRNA number 2 exhibited the most pronounced knockdown effect. Consequently, siRNA number 2 was selected for subsequent NCKAP1 knockdown studies in our research (Fig. 5F). The knockdown results demonstrated a significant reduction in NCKAP1 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 5F–H). Notably, the knockdown of Nckap1 also led to the suppression of WAVE2 expression (Fig. 5I). The results from the CCK8 assay indicated that the knockdown of Nckap1 could enhance cell viability to a certain extent, suggesting that Nckap1 exacerbates cardiomyocyte injury in the OGD model (Fig. 5J). Non-reducing Western blot analysis confirmed that knocking down Nckap1 significantly reduced disulfide stress and alleviated the band-smearing phenomenon (Fig. 5K). Importantly, phalloidin staining clearly demonstrated that Nckap1 knockdown inhibited the folding and aggregation of the actin cytoskeleton, thereby suppressing disulfidptosis (Fig. 5L).

Phalloidin staining also verified that Nckap1 overexpression promoted the folding and aggregation of the actin cytoskeleton (Fig. 5R).

Finally, we validated in a rat MI model that NCKAP1 expression was significantly upregulated in the infarcted area (Fig. 5S–V). Importantly, immunohistochemical analysis showed that NCKAP1 expression exhibited a clustered pattern in the infarcted region, which aggravated disulfidptosis in cardiomyocytes (Fig. 5U).

WAVE2 serves as the catalytic subunit within the WRC13. It functions by binding to Arp2/3 through its C-terminal VCA domain, thereby promoting Arp2/3-mediated actin polymerization and lamellipodia formation31. To validate the pivotal role of this regulatory relationship in disulfidptosis, we concurrently overexpressed the WT Wave2 and its ΔVCA mutant (which is incapable of interacting with Arp2/3 and regulating actin polymerization). The overexpression efficacy of both constructs was confirmed at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 6G–I). The results from the CCK8 assay indicated that the overexpression of WT Wave2 could decrease cell viability to a certain extent (Fig. 6J). Its ΔVCA mutant had no significant impact on cell viability. In non-reducing Western blot, overexpression of WT Wave2 increased disulfide stress, as evidenced by the exacerbated band smearing phenomenon compared to the OGD group (Fig. 6K). In contrast, overexpression of the its ΔVCA mutant, which lacks the ability to interact with Arp2/3 and regulate actin polymerization, did not produce this effect. Phalloidin staining results revealed that overexpression of WT Wave2 promoted the folding and aggregation of the actin cytoskeleton (Fig. 6L). In contrast, overexpression of the its ΔVCA mutant did not facilitate actin aggregation. These findings confirm the role of the WRC in regulating actin polymerization and lamellipodia formation, and further demonstrate that this effect is mediated through the modulation of its interaction with Arp2/3.

Moreover, we also conducted immunohistochemistry for Arp3 using tissue samples from the infarcted regions of rats (Fig. 6M). The results demonstrated a significant upregulation of Arp3 protein expression in the infarcted areas (Fig. 6N). Importantly, Arp3 exhibited a pronounced spatial aggregation phenomenon, which exacerbated the folding and aggregation of the actin cytoskeleton and contributed to the occurrence of disulfidptosis.

The combination of spatial transcriptomics and snRNA-sequencing data reveals spatiotemporal expression patterns of disulfidptosis-related genes

By leveraging previously published data, we employed an integrative single-cell genomics approach that combined snRNA-seq with spatial transcriptomics from the same tissue32. This enabled us to map human cardiac cells in both homeostatic conditions and following MI at an unprecedented spatial and molecular resolution (Fig. 7A–C). We explored the expression patterns of disulfidptosis-related genes from both spatial and temporal dimensions, providing a theoretical basis for future targeted and precise therapies. Spatially, we primarily focused our analysis on the IZ and BZ. Temporally, we divided the IZ into two groups based on time points: less than 3 days and less than 14 days post-injury (Fig. 7C). This allowed us to observe the temporal changes in key genes and infer the occurrence of disulfidptosis over time. We analyzed a total of 11 samples derived from 8 individuals. These included 4 non-transplanted donor hearts as control samples, along with tissue samples obtained from the necrotic regions (IZ and BZ) of patients with AMI (Fig. 7A–C). Following the exclusion of low-quality nuclei, we successfully isolated 83,582 nuclei from all samples, which were deemed suitable for snRNA-seq. Initially, we clustered the cells based on the integrated snRNA-seq data obtained from all samples, after correcting for batch effects. Subsequently, we annotated the resulting clusters using carefully curated marker genes sourced from the literature, facilitating the identification of 11 distinct primary cardiac cell types (Fig. 7D, Supplementary Table 7). Subsequently, we delineated the expression profiles of the key disulfidptosis-related genes across various cell types (Fig. 7E). In the three groups of MI samples analyzed, it was observed that, when compared to the control group samples, the expression level of SLC7A11 was significantly elevated in cardiomyocytes and fibroblasts (Supplementary Fig. 4A–C, Supplementary Tables 8–10). Similarly, compared with the control group, the expression of WAVE2 was also upregulated in cardiomyocytes and fibroblasts in the MI group (Supplementary Fig. 4A–C, Supplementary Tables 8–10). After aggregating all samples, we conducted quantitative analysis on the expression proportion and expression level of each gene across different cell types. Since each spatial transcriptomics spot comprises a cluster of cells, we successfully enhanced the spatial resolution by deconvoluting the cell-type compositions within each spot using annotated snRNA-seq data derived from the same biological samples (Fig. 7F). Notably, we observed a remarkable spatial colocalization of genes implicated in the Rac1-WRC-Arp2/3 signaling pathway and SLC7A11 (Fig. 7F, G). This compelling observation strongly supports our conclusion that the upregulation of SLC7A11, in conjunction with the activation of the Rac1-WRC-Arp2/3 signaling pathway, collectively drives the occurrence of disulfidptosis.

Moreover, we utilized the AddModuleScore method to estimate the activities of disulfidptosis signaling pathways (Supplementary Table 11) for each spot based on spatial gene expression data. By correlating the spatially resolved pathway activities with the estimated cellular abundance at each spot, we established a direct association between spatial cell composition and cellular function at the tissue slide level. For example, within 3 days following MI, in IZ, we detected a positive correlation between elevated disulfidptosis pathway activity and an increased abundance of cardiomyocytes and myeloid cells (Fig. 7F). Within 14 days following MI, in ischemic regions, we identified a positive correlation between elevated disulfidptosis pathway activity and an increased abundance of fibroblasts and myeloid cells (Fig. 7F). These findings imply that disulfidptosis plays a crucial role in both the acute and repair phases of MI.

To gain a deeper understanding of the diverse states of cardiomyocytes, we aimed to decipher the molecular heterogeneity of cardiomyocytes following MI. We integrated the snRNA-seq data obtained from cardiomyocytes into a unified low-dimensional space and then clustered the cells (Fig. 7H, Supplementary Table 12). This method revealed five distinct cell states of ventricular cardiomyocytes (vCM 1–5), which were present across multiple samples and data modalities. These cardiomyocyte states reflect different cellular stress conditions during the acute phase of MI. Specifically, vCM1 represents a ‘non-stressed’ state, vCM2 indicates a ‘pre-stressed’ state, and vCM3 denotes a ‘stressed’ state32. Differential gene expression analysis revealed a significant upregulation of SLC7A11 in vCM2. Similarly, NCKAP1 and WASF2 exhibited distinct upregulation in vCM2 (Fig. 7I, J, Supplementary Fig. 5A–C, Supplementary Tables 13–15). These findings imply that early intervention strategies aimed at promoting the downregulation of SLC7A11 and modulating the Rac1/WRC/Arp2/3 pathway in the vCM2 state may potentially mitigate disulfide death in cardiomyocytes.

Discussion

Disulfidptosis, a recently identified form of cell death marked by elevated cystine levels and NADPH depletion, was initially detected in tumor cells13,14,20,21. However, its role in cardiovascular diseases has not yet been investigated. Our study demonstrated that elevated SLC7A11 expression in AMI models leads to cystine overload and disulfide stress, which subsequently trigger the misfolding and aggregation of the actin cytoskeleton, plasma membrane detachment, and ultimately, disulfidptosis. Furthermore, we validated that downregulating SLC7A11 or inhibiting the Rac1/WRC/Arp2/3 signaling pathway can suppress disulfidptosis in MI.

In this study, we employed cellular OGD models and male rat models of LAD ligation to replicate the pathophysiological changes of myocardial infarction as observed in clinical settings. The use of male rats helped us avoid the confounding effects of varying estrogen levels on the pathophysiological changes associated with myocardial infarction25. The essence of disulfidptosis is the increase in disulfide bonds within actin cytoskeletal proteins. ‌The gold standard for determining whether cardiomyocytes undergo disulfidptosis comprises three main criteria. The first and most crucial criterion involves the upregulation of SLC7A11 expression, which leads to an increased intracellular cystine content. Notably, exogenous addition of cystine can produce an equivalent effect to SLC7A11 upregulation. The cystine/glutamate antiporter system, composed of SLC7A11 and SLC3A2, facilitates the exchange of extracellular cystine with intracellular glutamate in a 1:1 ratio21. In the normal physiological state, SLC7A11 protein exhibits minimal active transport of cystine. This observation has been validated through our spatial transcriptomic analysis of MI tissues in mice, as well as in spatial transcriptomic studies of human MI samples (Figs. 1F, 7J). The second criterion is characterized by glucose deprivation, which disrupts NADPH-dependent cystine reduction, consequently triggering cystine stress (Figs. 1K–M, 3F–H). The third criterion, as demonstrated in our OGD models, involves Rac1-WRC-Arp2/3 pathway-mediated actin polymerization and subsequent lamellipodia formation (Fig. 5A). This process creates a favorable environment for the formation of disulfide crosslinks between actin cytoskeletal proteins. NCKAP1 and WAVE2 are essential for WRC, which regulates cytoskeletal restructuring by facilitating actin polymerization. The lack of either protein can destabilize the complex. Rac1 can activate WRC to facilitate lamellipodia formation. Given that the Rac1-WRC-Arp2/3 pathway may also exert regulatory effects on other proteins, it is plausible that disulfidptosis involves the disulfide bonding of multiple proteins, not limited to actin cytoskeletal proteins alone. Further investigations are warranted to elucidate the roles of other proteins in the process of disulfidptosis (Fig. 4A). In the future, as research on disulfidptosis progresses and deepens, it is possible that any cellular death events triggered by an increase in disulfide bonds could be defined as disulfidptosis. This broadened definition of disulfidptosis may gain widespread acceptance among researchers. Ultimately, a key limitation in developing disulfidptosis-targeted therapies is the absence of specific biomarkers for quantifying this process, similar to how cleaved caspase-3 serves as an apoptosis marker21.

When we employed spatial transcriptomics to analyze myocardial tissues 11 days pMI in humans, we observed persistent high expression of SLC7A11 in fibroblasts. This observation led us to hypothesize that disulfidptosis might also occur during the pMI repair phase (Fig. 7F). However, it is important to note that these conclusions are specifically applicable to the study of myocardial ischemia. When shifting the research focus to ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) injury, the scenario changes significantly. The primary mechanism of I/R injury involves myocardial damage induced by oxidative stress. Moreover, following reperfusion, the restoration of glucose supply may enable NADPH to adequately reduce the intracellularly accumulated cystine. Consequently, for I/R injury, further experimental validation is imperative to ascertain the occurrence and potential implications of disulfidptosis.

Currently, the majority of research on disulfidptosis is concentrated within the field of oncology, where studies primarily focus on inducing cystine stress in tumor cells to inhibit their growth and metastatic potential20,33,34. Unlike the field of oncology, maintaining the stability of cardiomyocytes necessitates a delicate balance among various types of cell death, with each gene regulation potentially playing a pivotal role in different types of cell death, thereby avoiding unintended widespread effects. Cystine, cysteine, and GSH play crucial roles in disulfidptosis, ferroptosis, and cuproptosis, which are three distinct modes of cell death11–14,34. It is reasonable to study these three types of cell death together14,34. As a key gene involved in all three modes of cell death, artificial intervention to upregulate or downregulate SLC7A11 may disrupt cellular homeostasis14. Previous studies have reported that upregulating SLC7A11 expression can inhibit ferroptosis to a certain extent, but this intervention may potentially promote disulfidptosis35. We cannot solely aim to protect against one type of cell death while potentially causing another. Therefore, when the glucose supply to cardiomyocytes is sufficient, and NADPH can promptly reduce cystine, upregulating SLC7A11 to inhibit ferroptosis is reasonable. However, when cardiomyocytes are glucose- and oxygen-deprived, the pentose phosphate pathway is inhibited, and energy metabolism shifts towards anaerobic glycolysis. Under these conditions, upregulation of SLC7A11 can induce cells to undergo disulfidptosis. This indicates that whether the upregulation of SLC7A11 is beneficial to the organism heavily depends on glucose supply. Our experimental results prove that during the progression of AMI, upregulation of SLC7A11 does not confer a protective effect on dying myocardial cells; on the contrary, it exacerbates myocardial injury. While ferroptosis research has profoundly advanced our understanding of cardiovascular pathogenesis, its therapeutic translation remains scarce. ‌This problem may stem from the previously unrecognized detrimental effects associated with SLC7A11 upregulation. Our study provides a critical mechanistic addendum: in non-oncological contexts (e.g., MI), interventions targeting SLC7A11/GSH axis upregulation must ensure concomitant NADPH sufficiency. Failure to maintain this metabolic prerequisite paradoxically subverts cytoprotection, potentially explaining prior translational setbacks36. This redefines NADPH as a gatekeeper for ferroptosis modulation, establishing a new framework for future therapeutic design.

As previously reported, we believe that disulfidptosis is likely an excessive regulation of cells in response to redox imbalance14. This finding is consistent with our cardiomyocyte subtype analysis: SLC7A11 upregulation occurs predominantly in the vCM2 subpopulation, with the vCM2 phenotype indicating a distinct ‘pre-stressed’ functional state (Fig. 7I, Supplementary Fig. 5A). To maintain redox homeostasis, cells increase the compensatory transport of cystine. However, cells are unable to finely regulate the balance between cystine and NADPH, leading to a gradual increase in cystine levels and ultimately cystine stress. To mitigate the occurrence of disulfidptosis, we can reduce cystine transport or supplement NADPH to alleviate intracellular disulfide bond pressure. On the one hand, our study demonstrates that inhibiting SLC7A11 can, to a certain extent, mitigate disulfidptosis and improve cardiac function. However, there may be a potential risk of inducing ferroptosis, although the role of ferroptosis in ischemic injury is not as prominent as that in I/R injury. As mentioned earlier, it is unreasonable to rescue one type of cell death while inducing another. On the other hand, utilizing nanomaterials to specifically target and deliver NADPH to myocardial tissue could potentially supply additional NADPH, thereby effectively alleviating cystine stress37,38. Interestingly, if the cellular demand for glutathione, a metabolic derivative of cysteine, decreases, it can, to some extent, downregulate the expression of SLC7A11, thereby alleviating disulfide bond stress. N-acetylcysteine (NAC) is a chemically stable cysteine supplement that inhibits intracellular ROS levels by promoting GSH synthesis and reducing the accumulation of intracellular disulfide bonds through disulfide bond exchange22,39. The potential efficacy of NAC in suppressing disulfidptosis remains to be definitively established and warrants further investigation. Key genes regulating the actin cytoskeleton, such as Rac1, Nckap1, Wave2, and Arp2/3, may be more suitable for mitigating the occurrence of disulfidptosis with minimal impact on other types of cell death. These approaches may offer new directions for the pharmacological treatment of MI. However, whether the regulation of the actin cytoskeleton causes damage to normal cardiomyocytes requires further research and confirmation. Consequently, precise targeting of cells with aberrant molecular expression represents a pivotal avenue for future therapeutic development.

In summary, our study has validated the existence of a novel form of cell death—disulfidptosis—in MI. This finding opens an entirely new research avenue for the study of MI and, more broadly, for the field of cardiovascular research. Secondly, targeted inhibition of either SLC7A11 or the Rac1-WRC-Arp2/3 signaling axis can attenuate disulfidptosis, providing a mechanistic foundation for future precision-targeted therapeutic strategies. Critically, this study validated that under glucose deprivation, SLC7A11 upregulation acts as a double-edged sword—suppressing ferroptosis while inducing disulfidptosis. This finding warrants caution in future ferroptosis research.

Methods

Bulk RNA-seq data acquisition and processing

The GSE240847 datasets were sourced from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. This dataset (GPL22396) includes bulk RNA-seq data from rat heart tissue samples, comprising three sham controls and three MI samples. The data, initially in transcripts per million (TPM) format, underwent log2 transformation for further analysis. The ‘limma’ R package was utilized to identify differentially expressed genes between the two groups. Genes were identified as differentially expressed if they had an FDR-adjusted p-value below 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change exceeding 0.5. Differentially expressed genes were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses using the DAVID platform40. Data visualization was facilitated by the bioinformatics platform at https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn41 A set of 20 disulfidptosis-related genes (Slc7a11, Slc3a2, Nckap1, Rpn1, Ndufa11, Nubpl, Lrpprc, Oxsm, Ndufs1, Gys1, Rac1, Cyfip1, Wave2, Abi2, Hspc300, Arp2/3, Txnrd1, Txnrd2, Gpx4, and Nrf2) was identified based on a previous report (Supplementary Table 11)13,22.

Spatial transcriptome data processing of disulfidptosis-related genes

The spatial transcriptomic data were sourced from the GEO database (GSE176092)23. Specifically, GSM5355663 pertains to the mouse sham operation model, whereas GSM5943190 represents changes observed in mice 24 h post- MI. Additionally, GSM5943193 signify changes noted in mice 7 days post-MI. Raw FASTQ files and histological images of each sample were processed using Space Ranger software (version 1.2.1, 10X Genomics). Spatially resolved RNA-seq analysis used Seurat (v4.3.0) with sequential component transformation (SCT) correction to integrate the samples. Dimensionality reduction was performed using the RunPCA function, whereas graph-based clustering was conducted using the FindClusters function. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was applied using the RunUMAP function. Marker genes for all subpopulations were identified using the FindMarkers function in Seurat, with a minimum percentage threshold (min.pct) greater than 0.25 and a p-value below 0.05. Genes were considered to be differentially expressed if the average change in expression exceeded 0.5. The infarcted heart is typically categorized into three distinct zones: infarct zone (IZ), Ischaemic border zone (BZ), and remote zone (RZ). The different clusters were divided into different regions according to the identified marker genes.

Cell culture and the OGD model

H9c2 cells, originating from rat embryonic cardiomyoblasts, were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, USA). Cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #C11995500BT), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, #10099-141) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, #15140-122). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ and passaged at 80–90% confluence using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA. All experiments were performed using cells between passages 3 and 5 to ensure consistency and reproducibility.

For the establishment of the OGD model, H9c2 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5.0 × 10³ cells per well and incubated for 24 h under normoxic conditions. After reaching ~70–80% confluence, the culture medium was carefully aspirated, and cells were gently washed once with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) to remove residual growth factors and glucose. The medium was then replaced with serum-free, glucose-free DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #11966-025). Of note, the concentration of cystine in both the standard high-glucose DMEM and the glucose-free DMEM was 201.3 µM, allowing for controlled evaluation of glucose deprivation effects independent of cystine availability.

Cells were immediately placed into a multi-gas incubator (SANYO Electric Co., Ltd., Japan) and subjected to hypoxic conditions (94% N₂, 5% CO₂, and 1% O₂) for 6 h to induce OGD. Control cells were maintained under normoxic conditions in high-glucose DMEM with 10% FBS for the same duration.

Assessment of cell viability

H9c2 cell viability was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (DOJINDO, #CK04). H9c2 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5.0 × 10³ cells per well and incubated for 24 h under normoxic conditions. After incubation, the cells were either subjected to OGD as described in Section 4.3 or left untreated as controls.

To investigate the mechanisms underlying OGD-induced cell death, various pharmacological inhibitors were applied. The inhibitors used in this study included chloroquine (HY-17589A, MedChemExpress), Necrostatin-1 (HY-15760, MedChemExpress), Z-VAD-FMK (HY-16658B, MedChemExpress), Ferrostatin-1 (HY-100579, MedChemExpress), deferoxamine (HY-B0988, MedChemExpress), tempol (HY-100561, MedChemExpress), and trolox (HY-101445, MedChemExpress). All inhibitors were dissolved according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were treated with each inhibitor at the indicated concentrations immediately upon the initiation of OGD or control conditions. The concentrations used were as follows: chloroquine (25 μM), Necrostatin-1 (5 or 10 μM), Z-VAD-FMK (5 or 10 μM), Ferrostatin-1 (10 μM), deferoxamine (100 μM), tempol (25 mM), and trolox (100 μM). These concentrations were selected based on preliminary experiments and previous literature to ensure efficacy without significant cytotoxicity under control conditions.

Following the designated treatments, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by a 2-hour incubation at 37 °C in the dark. The absorbance (optical density, OD) at 450 nm was measured using a SpectraMax Plus384 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). Cell viability was expressed as a percentage relative to the untreated control group, which was set as 100%. Each experiment was performed in triplicate with at least three independent biological replicates to ensure reproducibility and statistical reliability.

Animals and experimental protocol

Eight-week-old male Wistar rats (200–220 g) were obtained from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Benxi, China). The rats were housed in a controlled environment at 22–24 °C with 45–55% humidity, a 12 h light/dark cycle, and provided ad libitum access to standard laboratory chow and water. After 1 week of acclimatization, they were randomly assigned to two experimental groups: (1) the sham group and (2) the MI group (n = 18 per group).

Animal studies were conducted in accordance with the guidelines outlined in Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament. The experimental protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Pharmaceutical Sciences, Jilin University (approval code 20240073). Rats showing signs of severe distress or excessive weight loss ( > 20% of initial body weight) were humanely euthanized and excluded from further analysis.

Establishment of the MI injury model

Following one week of acclimatization, rats were randomly assigned to either the sham-operated group or the myocardial infarction (MI) group. Anesthesia was induced with 3–5% isoflurane in an induction chamber and maintained with 1–2% isoflurane delivered via a face mask throughout the procedure. Heart rate and respiratory rate were continuously monitored to ensure physiological stability, and the depth of anesthesia was assessed by the absence of the pedal withdrawal reflex. The rats were then intubated with a tracheal tube connected to a rodent ventilator (BME-ZH, ZH-DW-3000A/B, China) with the following parameters: tidal volume 2–3 mL, respiratory rate 70–80 breaths/min, and inspiration/expiration ratio 1:1.5.

The surgical area was shaved and disinfected with povidone-iodine. A left thoracotomy was performed at the fourth intercostal space, and the pericardium was gently opened to expose the heart. The left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery was identified and permanently ligated ~2–3 mm below the left atrial appendage using a 6-0 polypropylene suture. Successful ligation was confirmed by immediate blanching (pallor) of the anterior wall of the left ventricle. In the sham group, rats underwent identical surgical procedures except that the LAD suture was passed through but not tied. Following the procedure, the chest cavity was promptly closed.

After surgery, the rats were carefully disconnected from the ventilator and placed under a nose cone with 100% oxygen supply until spontaneous breathing recovered. To maintain body temperature, the animals were kept on a 38 °C heating pad throughout the surgical procedure and during the recovery period.

Echocardiography measurement

Twenty-four hours after LAD ligation or sham surgery, transthoracic echocardiography was performed to evaluate cardiac function. Rats were anesthetized with 3–5% isoflurane in an induction chamber, and anesthesia was maintained with 1–2% isoflurane delivered via a face mask throughout the procedure. The rats were placed in a supine position on a heated platform (38 °C) to maintain body temperature, and the chest area was shaved and depilated to minimize ultrasound attenuation. Heart rate and respiratory rate were continuously monitored to ensure physiological stability, and the depth of anesthesia was assessed by the absence of the pedal withdrawal reflex.

Echocardiographic images were acquired using a Vivid i ultrasound system (GE Healthcare, Piscataway, NJ, USA) equipped with a 10S linear array transducer. Two-dimensional guided M-mode images were obtained from the parasternal short-axis view at the level of the papillary muscles. Key indicators of left ventricular performance, such as the left ventricular internal dimension during diastole (LVIDd), systole (LVIDs), fractional shortening (LVFS), and ejection fraction (LVEF), were automatically measured. All measurements were performed by a single experienced operator who was blinded to the experimental groups to avoid observer bias.

Euthanasia and tissue collection

Following the echocardiography measurement, blood samples were collected from the rats via the abdominal aorta under deep anesthesia. After blood collection, the rats were euthanized by an overdose of isoflurane inhalation (3–5%). Death was confirmed by respiratory arrest and the absence of a heartbeat.

Immediately after euthanasia, the hearts were rapidly excised and perfused with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) to remove residual blood. Subsequently, myocardial tissues from the infarcted area were collected, and for the sham-operation group, myocardial tissues from the same anatomical locations as those in the MI model group were retained as controls.

Cystine assay

H9c2 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well and incubated for 24 h under normoxic conditions. After incubation, the cells were divided into two groups: (1) subjected to OGD for 6 h as described in Section 4.3, and (2) maintained under normoxic conditions as untreated controls.

Following the designated treatments, the culture medium was carefully aspirated, and the cells were gently washed once with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) to remove residual medium components. The cells were then detached by incubation with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, #25200056) at 37 °C for 2–3 min. Trypsinization was terminated by adding culture medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum. The cell suspensions were collected into centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellets were washed twice with ice-cold PBS by gentle resuspension and centrifugation (300 × g, 5 min, 4 °C). After the final wash, the supernatant was carefully removed, and the cell pellets were resuspended in ice-cold PBS to achieve a final concentration of 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. An aliquot of each cell suspension was used for cell counting using a hemocytometer to confirm the cell density.

The cell suspensions were then transferred to pre-chilled microcentrifuge tubes and subjected to ultrasonic disruption on ice using an ultrasonic cell disruptor (Ningbo Xinzhi) with the following settings: 50 HZ, 5 s pulse on, 10 s pulse off, for a total of 3 min.

Cystine concentration in the supernatants was measured using a rat-specific enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (MEIMIAN, China, catalog #MM-927202O1). The assay was performed strictly following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 50 μL of each sample or standard was added to pre-coated wells, followed by addition of horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated detection antibody. After incubation at 37 °C for 60 min, the wells were washed five times with wash buffer, and 50 μL of substrate solution (TMB) was added. The plate was incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 15 min, and the reaction was terminated by adding 50 μL of stop solution. Absorbance was measured immediately at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, Multiskan FC). A standard curve was generated using the provided cystine standards. Cystine concentrations in the samples were calculated by interpolating their absorbance values onto the standard curve. Each experiment was performed with at least three independent biological replicates.

Measurement of NADPH/NADP⁺ ratio

Cellular NADPH and NADP⁺ levels were assessed using an NADPH assay kit (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, China, #S0179) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. For sample preparation, H9c2 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of ~5 × 104 cells per well (yielding ~2 × 105 cells at full confluence). After the designated treatments, the culture medium was completely aspirated, and cells were immediately lysed by adding 50 μL of NADP⁺/NADPH extraction solution (provided in the kit) directly to each well. The cells were gently scraped and repeatedly pipetted to ensure complete lysis. The lysates were collected into pre-chilled microcentrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were transferred to fresh tubes and kept on ice for subsequent analysis.

A standard curve was prepared using NADPH standards at concentrations ranging from 0 to 4 μM. For total NADP (NADP⁺ + NADPH) measurement, diluted samples or standards were added to a 96-well plate. For NADPH measurement alone, separate sample aliquots were incubated at 60 °C for 30 min to degrade NADP⁺ prior to assay. G6PDH working solution was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 10 min. After adding color development solution, the plate was incubated at 37 °C for 10–20 min, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm. NADPH and total NADP concentrations were determined by interpolating from the standard curve. NADP⁺ concentration was calculated as the difference between total NADP and NADPH, and the NADPH/NADP⁺ ratio was calculated.

Measurement of GSH/GSSG ratio

GSH and GSSG levels were measured using commercial kits (Beyotime, #S0053) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were collected as described in Section 4.9. Protein removal reagent was added to the cell pellet, followed by two rapid freeze-thaw cycles. The samples were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected for total glutathione determination.

For GSSG determination, a separate aliquot of the prepared supernatant was incubated with GSH scavenging reagent at 25 °C for 60 min to remove reduced GSH. A series of GSSG standards was prepared using the provided standard and protein removal reagent M solution. Diluted samples or standards were added to a 96-well plate, followed by total glutathione detection working solution. After incubation at 25 °C for 5 min, NADPH solution was added, and absorbance was measured at 412 nm using a microplate reader. Measurements were taken at 25 min post-NADPH addition. Total glutathione and GSSG concentrations were determined by interpolating from standard curves. GSH concentration was calculated by subtracting GSSG (expressed as GSH equivalents) from total glutathione, and the GSH/GSSG ratio was calculated.

Measurement of G6P levels

G6P levels were quantified using a WST-8 based G6P assay kit (Beyotime, #S0185) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In this assay, G6P is converted by G6PD to generate NADPH, which then reduces WST-8 to formazan, detectable by absorbance at 450 nm.

Cells were collected as described in Section 4.9. The cell pellet was resuspended in 200 μL of ice-cold G6P extraction solution, gently pipetted to ensure lysis, and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected for analysis. A series of G6P standards was prepared by diluting the provided 10 mM G6P standard with G6P extraction solution to concentrations ranging from 0 to 500 μM. A 50 μL aliquot of each sample or standard was added to a 96-well plate, followed by 50 μL of G6P detection working solution. The plate was incubated at 37 °C for 10 min in the dark. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. G6P concentrations were determined by interpolating from the standard curve.

Measurement of G6PDH activity

G6PDH activity was assessed using a commercial kit (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, China, #S0189) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were collected as described in Section 4.9. After centrifugation, the supernatant was carefully aspirated, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 200 μL of ice-cold G6PDH extraction solution. The suspension was gently pipetted to ensure complete lysis and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were collected and kept on ice for subsequent analysis. A 50 μL aliquot of each sample or standard was added to a 96-well plate in duplicate. Then, 50 μL of G6PDH detection working solution was added to each well and mixed gently. Background control wells were prepared by adding 50 μL of G6PDH detection working solution without G6PDH substrate to separate wells containing the same samples. The plate was incubated at 37 °C for 10 min in the dark. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. G6PDH activity in the samples was determined by interpolating from the standard curve.

Fluorescent staining of actin filaments and the cell membrane

A total of 2 × 10^4 cells per well were seeded onto confocal dishes (Biosharp Life Science, China, # BS-15-GJM) one day before treatment. After treatment, cells were washed with PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. The cells were then permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5 min at room temperature. After permeabilization, cells were washed again three times with PBS. Following permeabilization, cells were incubated in the dark for 30 min at room temperature with 100 nM phalloidin-iFluor 555 reagent (Abcam, #176756) in PBS. After incubation, the cells were washed twice and mounted with an antifade medium containing DAPI (Biosharp Life Science, China, BL739A).

For co-staining of F-actin and the plasma membrane: To preserve membrane integrity, fixed cells were not permeabilized. Instead, they were incubated directly with Deep Red Actin Tracking Stain and CellMask Green Plasma Membrane Stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A57245 and #C37608, respectively) diluted in PBS according to the manufacturer’s recommended concentrations. Incubation was carried out for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. After staining, cells were washed three times with PBS and mounted with antifade medium as described above.

Images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (OLYMPUS, Japan, FV4000) equipped with a 100× oil-immersion objective. The following excitation/emission wavelengths were used: DAPI (405 nm/450–500 nm), phalloidin-iFluor 555 (555 nm/570–620 nm), Deep Red Actin Tracking Stain (640 nm/660–700 nm), and CellMask Green (488 nm/500–550 nm).

Phalloidin staining of myocardial tissue was performed on frozen sections of the infarcted region in rat myocardial tissue. After tissue collection, it was first dehydrated in a 15% sucrose solution for 1–2 days, followed by transfer to a 30% sucrose solution for another 1–2 days. Subsequently, the target tissue area was removed from the 30% sucrose solution, and its surface moisture was wiped dry. The temperature of the cryostat was set to −18 °C to –22 °C. An appropriate amount of OCT embedding compound was dispensed onto the circular base in the cryostat. Before the compound solidified, the tissue was submerged in it. Once the embedding compound solidified, the circular base with the OCT block was placed on the sample holder. The section thickness was initially set to 30 μm for trimming. After tissue exposure, the thickness was adjusted to 8 μm for sectioning. The subsequent phalloidin staining steps were identical to those used for cells.

Reverse transcription quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated from rat heart tissue (~20–30 mg from the infarcted region) using TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #15596026) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Tissue samples were homogenized in 1 mL of TRIzol, followed by addition of 0.2 mL of chloroform. After centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, the aqueous phase was collected. RNA was precipitated with 0.5 mL of isopropanol, centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, washed with 75% ethanol, and dissolved in DEPC-treated water. RNA concentration and purity were determined by measuring absorbance at 260 nm and 280 nm using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Samples with an OD260/OD280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 were used for subsequent analysis.

For each sample, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a PrimeScript RT reagent kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, #RR047A) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The reaction conditions were: 37 °C for 15 min and 85 °C for 5 s. The resulting cDNA was stored at –20 °C until use. qPCR was performed using SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Takara, #RR820A) on a PCR System. Each reaction (20 μL) contained 10 μL SYBR Premix Ex Taq II, 0.8 μL each of forward and reverse primers (10 μM), 2 μL cDNA, and 6.4 μL RNase-free water. Thermal cycling conditions were: 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 34 s. A melt curve analysis was performed to verify amplification specificity.

All reactions were performed in triplicate. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method with GAPDH as the internal reference gene. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 16.

SiRNA and plasmid transfection procedures

To manipulate gene expression in H9C2 cells, we first seeded the cells into six - well plates and allowed them to grow until they reached ~50% confluence. For gene knockdown experiments, we transfected the cells with either targeted Nckap1 or nonspecific small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) at a final concentration of 40 nM. The transfection was carried out using the RNATransMate reagent (Sangon Biotech, China, #E607402) strictly in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol, and the cells were incubated for 24 h. Following the transfection period, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium to ensure optimal cell growth conditions. The siRNAs used in this study were obtained from Sangon Biotech, and the specific sequences of the siRNAs targeting Nckap1 are provided in Supplementary Table 17.

For gene overexpression studies, we transfected H9c2 cells with either the pcDNA3.1(+)-Nckap1 plasmid (for Nckap1 overexpression) or the empty pcDNA3.1(+) vector as a control. The transfection was performed using Lipo8000 transfection reagent (Beyotime, China, C0533) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Specifically, 2.5 μg of plasmid DNA per well was used, and the cells were incubated for 24 h. After transfection, the medium was changed, and the cells were further cultured for an additional 48 h to allow sufficient time for gene expression. The same transfection protocol was employed for the overexpression of Rac1 - Q61L, Wave2-WT, and Wave2-ΔVCA (420-497aa)31,42, ensuring consistency and reproducibility across different gene manipulation experiments. The plasmids were all purchased from the Public Protein/Plasmid Library (China).

Western blot analysis

Protein extraction and quantification: Protein concentrations in H9c2 cell lysates and rat myocardial tissue homogenates were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (#P0010, Beyotime Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For tissue samples, approximately 50 mg of myocardial tissue from the infarcted region or corresponding area from sham-operated rats was homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, #P0013B) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail using a tissue homogenizer at 25 Hz for 2 min. For cell samples, H9c2 cells were lysed directly in RIPA buffer on ice for 30 min with intermittent vortexing. All lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to remove insoluble debris, and the supernatants were collected. Protein concentrations were measured using a microplate reader at 562 nm, with bovine serum albumin (BSA) as the standard.

Gel electrophoresis and transfer: Equal amounts of protein samples (20–30 μg per lane) were mixed with 5× SDS-PAGE loading buffer (Beyotime, #P0015) and heated at 95 °C for 5 min to denature proteins. Proteins were separated by 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) at 80 V for 30 min through the stacking gel, followed by 100 V for 60–90 min until the bromophenol blue dye front reached the bottom of the gel. Subsequently, proteins were transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, #IPVH00010) using a wet transfer system (Bio-Rad, Mini Trans-Blot Cell) at 300 mA constant current for 90–120 min at 4 °C.

Immunoblotting: The membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA) in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 for 2 h at room temperature on a rocking platform to minimize non-specific binding. Following blocking, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. The following primary antibodies were used: SLC7A11 (1:1000; HUABIO, #HA721868), NCKAP1 (1:1000; Proteintech, #12140-1-AP), WAVE2 (1:1000; Selleck, #F0665), Rac1 (1:1000; HUABIO, #ET1703-80), Arp3 (1:1000; Selleck, #F2365), NRF2 (1:1000; Affinity, #BF8017), and GAPDH (1:5000; Proteintech, #10494-1-AP).

After overnight incubation, membranes were washed three times with TBST (10 min each) at room temperature. The membranes were then incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies diluted in TBST containing 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. The secondary antibodies used were: goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:5000; DINGGUO CHANGSHENG, #IH-0011) and anti-mouse IgG (1:5000; HUABIO, #HA1006). After secondary antibody incubation, membranes were washed again four times with TBST (10 min each) to remove unbound antibodies.

Detection and analysis: Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (YEASEN, #36208ES60) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Chemiluminescent signals were detected using a chemiluminescence imaging system (Jiapeng, JP600). GAPDH served as the internal loading control to normalize protein expression levels. Densitometric analysis of the immunoreactive bands was performed using ImageJ software (version 1.5.0.26; National Institutes of Health, USA). The relative protein expression levels were calculated as the ratio of the target protein band intensity to that of GAPDH.

For non-reducing WB, the sample preparation was modified by mixing protein extracts with LDS sample buffer (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, China, #P0731) without adding reducing agents13. The samples were split into two portions: one was analyzed under non-reducing conditions, and the other was treated with 2% 2-mercaptoethanol (2ME) (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, #P0733) for reducing analysis. All samples were heated at 70 °C for 10 min before protein separation by SDS-PAGE. The subsequent electrophoresis, transfer, and immunoblotting procedures were identical to those described above. The primary antibodies used for WB were MYH9 (1:1000; ZEN-BIOSCIENCE, #R27092), FLNA (1:1000; ZEN-BIOSCIENCE, #R380781), FLNB (1:500; Proteintech, #20685-1-AP), TLN1 (1:500; HUABIO, #ER65629), and ACTB (1:5000; Proteintech, #66009-1-Ig).

Analysis of protein disulfide bonds based on LC-MS/MS

Initially, 200 mg of the apical tissue was isolated and rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen. Subsequently, 300 µL of radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis and extraction buffer was added to the sample using a pipette. The mixture was sonicated on ice with alternating 3-second intervals on and off for a total 10 min. The lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was transferred to a pre-cooled 1.5 mL EP tube for subsequent use. Subsequently, 10 µL of 10 mM IAM solution was added to the sample to achieve a final concentration of 1 mmol/L, followed by incubation at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. The protein solution for SP3 bead enrichment was placed in a 96-well plate, with 86 µL of each well. Subsequently, 4 µL of 1 M DTT was added to each well, and the plate was shaken at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, 8 µL of 1 M IAM was added to each well, and the solution was agitated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. An additional 8 µL of 1 M DTT was added to each well, and the mixture was shaken at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. Magnetic beads (10 µL per well) were added, mixed thoroughly by pipetting, and incubated on a shaker at room temperature for 15 min. The plate was positioned on a magnetic stand for 10 min to separate the beads, and the supernatant was carefully removed using an elevated magnetic bar. Following the lowering of the magnetic bar, 220 µL 80% ethanol was added to each well. The plate was agitated at room temperature for 3 min and then placed on the magnetic stand for 4 min before removing the supernatant. This ethanol washing step was repeated three times. For enzymatic digestion, 16 µL of T enzyme (0.25 µg/µL) was added to each well, followed by shaking of the plate at room temperature for 2 min. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 700 rpm for 9 h, followed by shaking at 300 rpm for an additional 6 h at 37 °C. Afterward, the plate was shaken for 2 min at room temperature and left to rest for 5 min before transferring the supernatant to a new plate. A 100 µL sample solution was combined with 5 µL of Glu-C enzyme (0.5 µg/µL) and incubated at 37 °C for 16 h. For SP2 desalting, acetonitrile (Fisher Chemical) was added to the digested peptide supernatant to adjust the mixture to 95% acetonitrile. A volume of 10 µL of magnetic beads was pipetted into the sample, which was subsequently positioned on a magnetic rack for a duration of 5 min to facilitate the removal of the supernatant. Then, 200 µL of acetonitrile was added, and the sample was shaken thoroughly for 5 min before being placed on the magnetic rack for another 5 min to remove the supernatant, with this wash step being repeated three times. The dried beads were reconstituted with 150 µL of 2% acetonitrile, shaken for 15 min, and the supernatant was then transferred to a new centrifuge tube. This extraction step was repeated once, and the supernatants were combined for a final volume of 280 µL, which was then dried for detection.

The experimental procedure for determining protein disulfide bonds using LC-MS/MS involves several key steps. First, for liquid chromatography conditions setup, begin by selecting an analytical column with an internal diameter of 100 μm and a length of 180 mm was used, using Reprosil-Pur 120 C18-AQ as the packing material with a particle size of 3 μm. Prepare the mobile phases as follows: Mobile Phase A is a 0.1% formic acid solution, whereas Mobile Phase B is a mixture of 0.1% formic acid and 80% acetonitrile (ACN) from Sigma-Aldrich. Set the flow rate to 600 nL/min to ensure adequate sample separation within the analytical column and use a gradient program for an analysis time of 131 min, starting with a high aqueous phase (Mobile Phase A) and gradually increasing to a high organic phase (Mobile Phase B) for stepwise elution of proteins or peptides. Access the mass spectrometer control software and navigate to the MS1 parameter setup interface. Configure the resolution to 120,000, set the AGC target to 400,000, and adjust the maximum ion injection time to ‘custom’ based on instrument and sample conditions. Define the scan range from 300 to 1800 m/z. In the MS2 parameter setup, set the resolution to 15,000, the AGC target to 50,000, and the maximum ion injection time to 22 ms, and configure the collision energy as NCE (Normalized Collision Energy) to 30 or use stepped NCE if necessary. The model of the liquid chromatography instrument is the Vanquish Neo, manufactured by Thermo Fisher Scientific, whereas the mass spectrometer model is the Orbitrap Fusion Lumos, likewise produced by Thermo Fisher Scientific.

Raw MS files were analyzed using PLink to search the target protein database with specified parameters. The variable modifications included carbamidomethyl (C), while the cross-linking form was specified as SS. The enzymes used were Trypsin and Glu-C, and the database employed was Rattus norvegicus. The maximum number of missed cleavage sites allowed was set to three. The peptide mass tolerance for the first stage of mass spectrometry was set to 20 ppm, and the fragment mass tolerance for the second stage was set to 0.02 ppm. Additionally, the significance threshold was set to ≤5% at the PSM level.

The abundance values of disulfide bond sites between the two groups were compared using an unpaired t-test, with a p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Subsequently, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted on differently expressed proteins. The selected differential peptides were further analyzed using the ggseqlogo package to generate position weight matrix (PWM) plots, and motif analysis was performed to display the distribution of different amino acids before and after crosslinking sites within the protein sequences.

Inhibitor administration

Erastin2, a specific inhibitor targeting SLC7A11, was procured from Selleck (#E1874, USA). For subsequent experimental use, the compound was formulated into a solution with the following composition: 5% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), 40% polyethylene glycol 300 (PEG300), 5% Tween 80, and 50% double − distilled water (ddH₂O). This formulation was carefully prepared to ensure the stability and solubility of Erastin2 for in vivo administration. In the MI group, either erastin2 or vehicle was administered via a single intraperitoneal injection (n = 10 per group). The sham group received a single intraperitoneal injection of vehicle as a control. The dose of Erastin2 used was 10 mg/kg body weight. The administration was performed precisely 1 h prior to the induction of MI43.

Measurement of serum troponin I concentration level and AST, LDH activity via assay kits

Blood sample collection and processing: After echocardiographic examination, blood samples were collected from the abdominal aorta of rats under anesthesia. Blood was allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min and then centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to separate the serum. The resulting serum was collected and stored at –80 °C until further analysis.

Measurement of cardiac troponin I (cTnI): Serum cTnI levels were measured using a rat-specific ELISA kit (Jiangsu Meibiao Biotechnology, #MB1967A) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, serum samples or standards were added to pre-coated plates, incubated with HRP-conjugated detection antibody, and developed with chromogen solution. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and cTnI concentrations were calculated by interpolating from a standard curve. Results were expressed as ng/mL.

Measurement of AST and LDH activity: Serum AST and LDH activities were determined using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, #C010-2 for AST, #A020-2 for LDH) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, serum samples were mixed with the respective substrate solutions, incubated at 37 °C, and chromogenic reagents were added. Absorbance was measured at 510 nm for AST and 450 nm for LDH using a microplate reader. Enzyme activities were calculated by comparing sample absorbance to standard curves.

Histopathological examination

Twenty-four hours after ligating the LAD, the left ventricles of the hearts in the infarcted area were carefully harvested and immediately rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Following this, they were fixed in 4% polyformaldehyde for a period of 24 h. Subsequently, the specimens were embedded in paraffin and meticulously sectioned into 4-micrometer-thick slices, which were then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). These stained sections were meticulously examined under a Nikon light microscope.

Immunohistochemistry

Myocardial tissues were collected from rats after 24 h of ischemia, immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and subsequently embedded in paraffin. The tissues were then sliced into 3–4 μm sections. For immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis, tissue sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, including anti-SLC7A11 (1:100; Servicebio, #GB115276), anti-NCKAP1 (1:100; Proteintech, #12140-1-AP), and anti-ARP3 (1:100; Selleck, #F2365), followed by secondary incubation with HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (1:200; Servicebio, #GB23303). DAB chromogenic agent (Servicebio, G1212) was used to initiate chromogenic reactions. After washing, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin and observed under an optical microscope at ×400 magnification. Image analysis was conducted using ImageJ software.

Measurement of intracellular ROS levels

H9c2 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5.0 × 10⁴ cells per well and incubated for 24 h. Following incubation, cells were subjected to OGD for 6 h, while control cells were left untreated. To assess the effects of antioxidant interventions, cells were treated with Tempol (HY-100561, MedChemExpress), Trolox (HY-101445, MedChemExpress), or DMSO as a vehicle control. After treatment, cells were collected, centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, and washed twice with serum-free DMEM. The cells were then resuspended in serum-free DMEM containing 10 μM DCFH-DA at a concentration of 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL and incubated at 37 °C in a cell culture incubator for 20 min, with gentle inversion every 3–5 min to ensure adequate probe loading. Subsequently, cells were washed three times with serum-free culture medium to remove extracellular DCFH-DA. Finally, ROS levels were measured using a flow cytometer (FACS Calibur, BD Biosciences).

Downloading and analyzing human cardiac snRNA-seq data

We downloaded snRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics data from the cellxgene website (https://cellxgene.cziscience.com/collections/8191c283-0816-424b-9b61-c3e1d6258a77), selecting samples based on specific criteria: three IZ samples from patients with pMI ≤2 days and two BZ samples from patients with MI ≤ 5 days post-infarction, two IZ samples >10 days post-infarction, and four normal control samples32. Using the R package Seurat (v5.0.3), we filtered out low-quality cells (nFeature_RNA: 300–7000, percent.mt < 5%), yielding a dataset of 83,582 cells. Data normalization was performed with NormalizeData, followed by identification of 2000 highly variable genes using FindVariableFeatures and data scaling with ScaleData. Linear dimensionality reduction was conducted using RunPCA, and batch effects were mitigated with RunHarmony. UMAP dimensionality reduction was applied on the top 30 dimensions with RunUMAP. Cells were clustered using FindNeighbors (dims = 1:30) and FindClusters (resolution = 0.3), identifying eleven primary cell types based on classical marker genes. This single-cell dataset served as a reference for identifying cellular distribution within the spatial transcriptomics data.

Analyzing spatial transcriptomic data

Spatial transcriptomics data were processed using the R package Seurat, with individual samples undergoing normalization, scaling, and identification of 3000 highly variable features via the SCTransform function. Pathway scores were computed using the AddModuleScore function, and correlation coefficients with p-values for key genes were determined within each sample. Subsequently, cell types within the spatial transcriptomics data were annotated using the R package spacexr (v2.2.1), which employs the Robust Cell Type Decomposition (RCTD) algorithm. This deconvolution-based approach leverages snRNA-seq data as a reference to resolve the cellular composition within each spatial spot. An RCTD object linking the snRNA-seq reference to the spatial data was created using the CreateRCTD function, and the RCTD algorithm was executed with the parameter doublet_mode set to “full”. The R package STdeconvolve (v1.9.0) was then utilized to visualize the cell type proportions identified by the RCTD algorithm in the form of scatter pie charts.

Analysis of cardiomyocyte cell subsets

Cardiomyocyte subpopulations were analyzed through a standardized computational pipeline: Highly variable genes were identified using FindVariableFeatures, followed by principal component analysis (RunPCA) and batch correction (RunHarmony). Nonlinear dimensionality reduction was performed via UMAP (RunUMAP, dims=1:20), with initial clustering (FindNeighbors/FindClusters, resolution=0.25) refined to five distinct subpopulations (CM1-CM5) at resolution 0.3. Differential expression analysis (FindAllMarkers, parameters: only.pos=T, min.pct=0.2, min.diff.pct=0.2) identified subpopulation-specific markers, with the top five genes (ranked by differential expression percentage) visualized in bubble plots

Data visualization was performed using ggplot2 (v3.5.0) to generate bar and violin plots, while statistical comparisons (IZ vs CTRL and BZ vs CTRL) were conducted via t-tests implemented in ggpubr (v0.6.0) with geom_signif. Significance thresholds were denoted as: *p < 0.05, ‌**p < 0.01, ***‌p < 0.001, ns = p > 0.05.

Statistical analysis

The figures display the number of samples (n) used in each experiment, which represent biological replicates. Results are present as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.5.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Student’s t-test was used for comparisons between two groups, whereas for comparisons involving multiple groups, ordinary one-way ANOVA was conducted, followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test or Tukey’s post hoc test. Asterisks in the figures indicate the levels of statistical significance (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001), as detailed in the figure legends.
