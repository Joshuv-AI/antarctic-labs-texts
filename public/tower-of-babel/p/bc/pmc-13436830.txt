TANGENT study design: a phase III, randomized study of emactuzumab for the treatment of tenosynovial giant cell tumors
Authors: Hans Gelderblom, Emanuela Palmerini, Michiel van de Sande, Javier Martin-Broto, Gabriel Tinoco, Elyse Seltzer, Madhu Davies, Ruth Coll, Sander Veltkamp, Jean-Yves Blay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTenosynovial giant cell tumors (TGCT) are rare, locally aggressive neoplasms causing pain, stiffness, swelling, limited range of motion, and joint degeneration, which can be debilitating. Therapeutic options include surgery or systemic therapy with colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor (CSF-1R) pathway inhibitors. However, many are not amenable to surgery or have high postsurgical recurrence rates. Available systemic therapies require long-term administration and can have burdensome side effects. Emactuzumab is a novel, potent, CSF-1R inhibiting monoclonal antibody with a unique mechanism of action targeting the receptor dimerization interface of the CSF-1R to reduce tumor-associated macrophages and inflammation within the TGCT microenvironment. It is the only short-course, intravenous therapy in development for TGCT. In a phase I study, emactuzumab resulted in robust and durable responses, and a manageable safety profile in patients with TGCT, supporting further research as a treatment option to address unmet need and improve quality of life in patients with TGCT. TANGENT is a randomized, double-blind, global, phase III study (NCT05417789) to investigate the safety and efficacy of intravenous emactuzumab versus placebo in patients with TGCT not amenable to surgery.Clinical trial registration: www.clinicaltrials.gov identifier is NCT05417789 initially registered on 1 June 2022.

Introduction

Background and rationale

Tenosynovial giant cell tumors (TGCT), formerly known as pigmented villonodular tenosynovitis, are rare mesenchymal neoplasms that most often arise in the synovium of joints, bursae, or tendon sheaths [1–4]. They are locally aggressive and destructive and can cause joint erosion, joint space loss and effusion, and bony mineralization, ultimately resulting in pain, limited range of motion, stiffness, joint degeneration, and swelling [2,3,5–7]. The prevalence of TGCTs was estimated to be between 12–44 per 100,000 persons at the end of 2012, and they typically present in younger people between 35–50 years old, but can occur at any age [1,8,9]. In the United States, TGCT is associated with high healthcare use, disability, and work loss, reflecting substantial, underrecognized burden [10].

TGCTs have two radiologically distinct subtypes, localized (L-TGCT, also referred to as nodular N-TGCT) and diffuse (D-TGCT), based on tumor location and growth pattern [1,7,11,12]. L-TGCT commonly presents as a unique or single limited lesion, whereas D-TGCT has more extensive and infiltrative involvement [1]. L-TGCT and D-TGCT share common mechanisms of pathogenesis, most of which involve translocations of the colony-stimulating factor 1 (CSF-1) gene, ultimately resulting in the overexpression of CSF-1 [13,14]. CSF-1 regulates the survival, proliferation, and differentiation of mononuclear phagocytes, and its overexpression results in tumor expansion through the recruitment of inflammatory cells to the tumor microenvironment [15–17].

Treatment for TGCT may involve surgical resection of the tumor [1]. However, not all tumors are amenable to surgery, and for those that are, postsurgical recurrence can occur (up to 36% recurrence reported for L-TGCT and 72% for D-TGCT), with rate of recurrence varying by affected joint [1,7,12,18]. Surgery may also lead to complications and morbidity, including, but not limited to, infection and wound healing problems, hemorrhage (including hemarthrosis, requiring drainage), nerve damage, joint stiffness and dysfunction, and the potential need for joint replacement [1,19–22]. Patients may require additional surgeries throughout their lifetime, and the risk of morbidity increases with subsequent procedures [1,7,19].

In cases where tumors are not amenable to surgery, systemic therapies such as oral CSF-1 receptor (CSF-1R) pathway inhibitors are available [1], but these therapies have limitations, such as chronic administration burden (including impact on family planning), drug-drug interactions, chronic side effects, food effect, and adherence challenges [23,24]. Currently, three oral CSF-1R inhibitors are approved for the treatment of TGCT, pexidartinib (approved in the US, Taiwan, and South Korea), vimseltinib (approved in the US and European Union), and recently, pimicotinib (approved in China) [23–27]. Pexidartinib resulted in a 39% overall response in a phase III trial of patients with TGCT, but has a black box warning and is only available to patients through a risk evaluation and mitigation strategy (REMS) program due to its association with hepatotoxicity [23,28]. In phase III trials of patients with TGCT, vimseltinib and pimicotinib demonstrated objective response rates of 40% and 54% at 25 weeks, respectively [29,30]. However, oral CSF-1R inhibitors require long-term administration and can result in side effects such as periorbital edema, fatigue, pruritus, itching, headache, asthenia, and nausea, among others [23,24,28,29], which may affect treatment adherence. Therefore, there remains an unmet medical need for alternative therapies for TGCT.

Emactuzumab (formerly RG7155 and RO5509554) is a novel, potent, highly selective, monoclonal immunoglobulin G1 (IgG1) antibody directed against CSF-1R [31,32]. Emactuzumab has a distinct mechanism of action targeting the CSF-1R dimerization interface with high affinity to block ligand-induced- and ligand-independent receptor activation at the surface of cells expressing this receptor, such as macrophages and monocytes in the TGCT microenvironment [31]. By inhibiting CSF-1R and downstream signaling pathways, emactuzumab induces apoptosis of tumor-associated macrophages and reduces recruitment of inflammatory cells to the tumor microenvironment [31]. Emactuzumab selectively binds to human CSF-1R but not to similar tyrosine kinase receptors such as c-Kit and PDGFR, thus enabling highly specific targeting of CSF-1R [31].

Emactuzumab offers a new differentiated treatment option delivered as a short-course intravenous (IV) infusion, with a treatment course of approximately 8 weeks (5 visits). While IV administration requires repeat visits to an infusion center for treatment it has the advantage of ensuring compliance with treatment, enables patient healthcare provider interactions during infusions, and avoids issues of adherence that have been observed with oral anti-cancer medications, including tyrosine kinase inhibitors [33,34]. In addition, because CSF-1R inhibitors are contraindicated in pregnancy for both male and female patients, from a family planning standpoint, short course therapy allows patients to complete a course of treatment and move forward with their lives. The overall treatment duration is also shorter than that of oral TKIs.

In early phase clinical trials, emactuzumab has been explored as a single agent and a combination treatment in patients with advanced solid tumors, and safety data are available in over 450 patients [31,32,35–38]. From the early phase clinical data of emactuzumab in patients with D-TGCT or advanced solid tumors, the optimal biological dose was determined to be 1000 mg once every 2 weeks (Q2W), leading to target saturation and optimal reduction in tumor-associated macrophages in tissue biopsies [39]. In a phase Ia/b, open-label dose-escalation and extension study of 63 adult patients with D-TGCT, the best overall objective response rate over all dose levels was 71% (45/63) in patients treated with emactuzumab, with 3% (2/63) of patients having a complete response and 68% (43/63) a partial response [35]. The disease control rate was 98%, and clinical responses were durable, with objective response rates of 70% and 64% observed at 1- and 2-year follow-up, respectively [35]. Across all patients, the median (min, max) number of treatment cycles administered was 4 (1, 14) [35]. Adverse events reported with emactuzumab were comparable to other CSF-1R agents, with common events including pruritus, asthenia, and various types of edema [35]. Therefore, based on these data and the unmet medical need for therapeutic options for TGCT, the phase III TANGENT study was designed.

Objectives

The phase III TANGENT study will assess the safety and efficacy of 5 cycles of emactuzumab once every 2 weeks, with the possibility of retreatment in the case of progression, in patients with TGCT.

Trial design

TANGENT is a randomized, double-blind, global, phase III study (NCT05417789) to investigate the efficacy and safety of emactuzumab versus placebo in patients with TGCT. During the 8-week treatment period, patients enrolled in this study will receive five doses of either emactuzumab or placebo.

The TANGENT study is being conducted at multiple centers in North America, Europe, and Asia. This study includes an option for open-label treatment in adolescent patients (Figure 1). Additionally, adults may receive open-label emactuzumab, if indicated, after the initial six months of the study. The approximate total duration of study participation is 2 years. Methods

Participants, interventions, and outcomes

This study allows for the enrollment of both adult and adolescent patients (≥12 years of age), per local legislation requirements. Eligible patients must have measurable disease, biopsy-confirmed local or diffuse TGCT, and be determined as not amenable for surgery by the investigator and one other qualified physician (an orthopedic surgeon or medical oncologist) not involved in this study. Adult patients will be randomized (2:1) to receive either 1000 mg of emactuzumab or placebo administered by IV infusion over 90 minutes on day 1 of each 2 week cycle (Q2W) for 5 cycles, or treatment for approximately 8 weeks over a 10-week treatment phase (Figure 2). By comparing to a placebo control, the current study intends to demonstrate that emactuzumab offers a safe and effective treatment in patients with TGCT not amenable to improvement with surgery, including those who have been unsuccessfully treated with other systemic agents. The total duration of the adult double-blind phase of the study is 2 years, which consists of screening, treatment, and follow-up periods. Patients who complete the first 6 months of the double-blind trial and have disease progression (either objective progressive disease as measured locally by noncontrast magnetic resonance imaging [MRI] or symptomatic progression) will be eligible for open-label emactuzumab between visit 10 (day 181) and visit 13 (day 541). Adult patients eligible for open-label emactuzumab will receive the treatment regimen described above, and will be followed for up to 2 years from initial randomization. The primary objective is to assess the efficacy of emactuzumab compared to placebo 6 months from the initiation of treatment by objective response rate (ORR) according to RECIST v1.1 (primary endpoint) and tumor volume score (TVS, key secondary endpoint) based on an independent, blinded central review. Tumor volume score is an estimation of tumor volume relative to the total size of the affected joint or tendon that can provide a more comprehensive measure of disease burden and treatment effects in diffuse TGCT [40]. Additional key secondary endpoints are change in healthcare professional (HCP)-reported joint mobility score by goniometry from baseline to 6 months, and change in patient-reported outcomes (PROs) from baseline to 6 months. Assignment of interventions

After screening for eligibility, adult patients will be centrally randomized using an Interactive Web Response System in a 2:1 ratio to receive an IV infusion of emactuzumab or placebo Q2W for 5 cycles. Randomization will be stratified by prior tyrosine kinase inhibitor use. Study participants and all persons involved in the conduct of the study will be blinded to treatment (which are indistinguishable from each other) during the primary analysis period. Unblinding prior to the primary analysis data lock can only occur in emergencies on a case-by-case basis if specific emergency treatment would be dictated by knowing the study medication status of the participant.

Discontinuation or modification of study interventions can occur based on investigator discretion, disease progression, or clinically relevant adverse events or lab toxicities that indicate continuation in the study is not in the best interest of the patient.

Data collection, management, and analysis

Study assessments will be performed prior to study drug administration, unless otherwise specified. The primary endpoint of ORR at 6 months by RECIST 1.1 will be measured by noncontrast MRI scans with contiguous slices (<5 mm) performed at approximately 6 weeks (visit 5) and 13 weeks (visit 7) or at early treatment discontinuation. Subsequently, during the follow-up period, MRI scans will be performed every 6 weeks for up to 6 months, then every 8 weeks for up to 1 year, and then every 12 weeks until study completion.

Disease will be measured by following a target lesion, with its longest diameter ≥20 mm at the diseased joint or tendon, from baseline. Assessments will be made on the longest diameter for the target lesion, and if the lesion becomes too small to measure or disappears completely, it will be recorded as either 5 mm or 0 mm, respectively. Response to treatment will be determined using serial radiologic longest diameter measurements for the target lesion, and RECIST v1.1 will be used for classification of the primary efficacy endpoint. Observed disease response (eg, partial response [PR], complete response [CR]) will be determined by the centrally adjudicated tumor assessment. As a secondary, clinically meaningful measure of response to treatment, the relative volume of the lesion at the diseased joint or tendon will be followed from baseline using TVS by blinded central independent radiology review, which will estimate the tumor volume relative to that of the synovial cavity of the diseased joint or tendon in 10% increments. Antitumor activity will be further assessed by RECIST v1.1 for duration of response, disease control rate, time to response, and time to progression.

Functional outcomes will be measured by the PROMIS-PF scale, which has been validated in patients with TGCT [41]. Additional secondary endpoints will be measured by additional clinical outcome assessments, and based on the HCP-reported joint mobility score, worst pain and worst stiffness numerical rating score (NRS), Short Form 12-Item Survey version 2 (SF-12v2), Patient Global Impressions (PGI) of Severity and Change for Physical Functioning, Euro-Quality of Life 5-Dimension 5-Level Questionnaire (EQ-5D-5L), and patient interviews.

Safety assessments will include general physical examination, vital signs, electrocardiogram measurements, clinical safety laboratory tests, pregnancy tests, and antidrug antibody testing. Adverse and serious adverse events, as well as adverse events of special interest (AE, SAE, and AESI, respectively) will be monitored throughout the study. AEs will be coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA), and the investigator will assess severity using the 1–5 scale of the Cancer Therapy Evaluation Program (CTCAE) version 5.0.

Participant retention will be achieved by several strategies. The sponsor has actively focused on a patient-centered approach applied to all trial activities and interactions with trial participants. The study staff at each site will be accessible to participants to answer their questions and respond to any concerns. To minimize the burden on a participant’s time, where possible assessments conducted by different departments will be scheduled for the same visit day. Travel expenses will be reimbursed for visits that are study-specific. Practical guidance and suggestions for participant retention will be discussed at the site initiation visits. For participants who withdraw from the trial, no further information will be collected from the date of withdrawal.

Analysis populations and missing data

Efficacy analyses will be performed in the full analysis set, which will include all randomized patients who receive at least one dose of study drug or placebo. The safety analyses set will include all adults who sign informed consent and receive at least one dose of study drug or placebo. The primary analysis will occur when patients have completed their month 6 visit or withdrawn from the study. For secondary analyses that include data from the whole of the double-blind phase (ie, to month 24), patients that enter the open-label phase will have subsequent data set to missing and assumed to be and a penalized imputation will be used. Data collected after the first dose of study drug in the open-label phase will be summarized under the open-label phase only. Patients who received at least 1 dose of open-label study drug will be included in the open-label full analysis set and open-label safety analysis set.

For ORR, if a participant has a missing or nonevaluable scan at month 6, but has been classified as CR or PR in adjacent scans prior to and after month 6, they will be classified as a responder. Otherwise, they will be classified as a nonresponder. Participants who withdraw from the study or die before the 6-month visit will be classified as nonresponders. For other endpoints, a penalized imputation will be used for participants who discontinue treatment and withdraw from the study for reasons related to TGCT, with the assumption that subsequent data would be associated with a moderately worse change from baseline. For participants who withdraw from the study due to a treatment-related AE, it will be assumed that subsequent data will follow a similar pattern to placebo and therefore a placebo trajectory imputation will be used. For all other missing data, a missing at random (MAR) approach will be used.

Statistical analyses

Statistical comparisons for ORR, the primary endpoint, and key secondary endpoints will occur in a hierarchical order. If the ORR is met at the ≤0.025 1-sided level, then PROMIS-PF T-scores will be tested sequentially at the ≤0.025 1-sided level. The order of hierarchical testing for the additional key secondary endpoints will be: TVS ORR, HCP-reported joint mobility score, worst stiffness NRS, worst pain NRS, and EuroQol visual analogue scale (EQ-VAS). The ORR is expected to be 10% in the placebo arm and 42% in the emactuzumab arm; therefore, a total of 96 patients (64 emactuzumab and 32 placebo) randomized on a 2:1 basis will provide 90% power based on Fischer’s exact test at the 1-sided 2.5% alpha level.

A 1-sided Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel (CMH) test, stratified by prior tyrosine kinase inhibitor use (yes/no), at the 2.5% level of significance, will be used to compare month 6 ORR between the two treatment arms for the full analysis. A Fisher’s exact test with corresponding exact 95% confidence interval (CI) for the difference in proportions will be provided as a sensitivity analysis. A logistic regression analysis for the FAS will be performed as a sensitivity analysis. The model will include treatment and prior tyrosine kinase inhibitor use as categorical factors. The estimated odds ratio and difference in proportions, along with their associated 2-sided 95% CIs, and the P value will be presented.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses may also be conducted for ORR, the primary endpoint, and key secondary endpoints and will be analyzed in the same way as the primary analysis. Pharmacokinetic analyses will include geometric mean, geometric coefficient of variation, and number of patients with serum concentration of emactuzumab above the limit of quantification. The other secondary endpoints in this study will be analyzed as change from baseline to six months, and change from baseline over time. Safety data will also be summarized and descriptive statistics will be calculated for quantitative safety data.

Monitoring

The data safety monitoring board (DSMB) consists of three experts at minimum, including a medically qualified chair (with expertise in the field of TGCT, where possible), an expert medical statistician, and a second physician with pertinent clinical and/or DSMB experience. The DMSB will meet approximately every 6 months after the first patient is dosed, and will review the study safety listings and determine if any safety measures or protocol modifications are required.

Ethics and dissemination

The study was approved by the institutional review board (First (central) IRB approval, WCG IRB; IRB tracking number, 20221064) and independent ethics committee and was conducted according to the recommendations of Good Clinical Practice, Declaration of Helsinki, the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences, and any applicable laws and regulations. All patients or their legally authorized representatives provided written informed consent to participate in the study.

Clinical information relating to study participants is strictly held in trust by the site principal investigator, participating investigators, research staff, and the sponsor and their agents. To preserve confidentiality and reduce the risk of identification during collection, analysis and storage of data and information, the following will be undertaken: (1) The number of private/confidential variables collected for each individual has been minimized. The data collected will be limited to that required to address the primary and secondary objectives. (2) Participant data and samples will be identified through use of a unique participant study number assigned to the study participant (“reidentifiable”). The site principal investigator is responsible for the storage of a master file of names and other identifiable data with the participant ID; access to this document will be restricted to members of the research team and authorized persons as listed previously. The master file will be stored securely, and separately, from study data in locked/password-protected databases with passwords kept separately. (3) Separation of the roles responsible for management of identifiers and those responsible for analyzing content. The data will be analyzed by members of the research team, who will be provided with anonymized data identified only by the unique participant study ID. To ensure confidentiality, any data dispersed to investigators will be blinded of any identifying participant information.

Participants will receive ancillary care beyond the scope of the trial as required. Any adverse events or complications arising from trial participation will be promptly addressed, and necessary medical interventions will be provided at no cost to the participants. Ancillary care will extend to managing conditions unrelated to the trial that may arise during the study period, ensuring the overall well-being of the participants. Posttrial care will be provided to participants for any ongoing effects or complications related to the trial interventions. Participants will have access to their routine medical care and follow-up visits, ensuring continuity of care beyond the trial’s conclusion. Provisions for compensation will be in place for participants who may suffer harm from trial participation. In the event of an adverse event directly attributable to the study interventions, compensation will cover medical expenses, additional treatments, and any other related costs. The compensation process will be transparent, fair, and in compliance with local regulations and ethical guidelines.

The results from the TANGENT trial will be submitted for presentation at relevant congresses and published in peer-reviewed journals. Patients are still being followed, and long-term follow-up results will be presented in subsequent publications.

Conclusion

TGCTs are rare mesenchymal neoplasms affecting joints, bursae, or tendon sheaths that can result in pain, functional impairment, and a reduced quality of life [1–3]. Surgical resection is the primary treatment for TGCT and local resection can be curative; however, additional treatment options are needed for patients with TGCT who are not amenable to surgery [1]. Research on the mechanisms underlying TGCT has led to the development of therapeutic agents targeting CSF-1R signaling as a treatment option for patients with TGCT that cannot be managed with surgery [1,20,21]. Pexidartinib, an oral tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) inhibitor that blocks CSF-1R signaling, was the first systemic targeted therapy approved for TGCT; however, it is only available through a risk mitigation strategy (REMS program) in the US due to potential for hepatotoxicity, which precluded approval in the EU [23,27,28]. Subsequently, additional oral TKI inhibitors have been approved for the treatment of TGCT in some countries, offering additional treatment options for TGCT [24,26,29,30]. Future TGCT treatments, including novel targeted CSF-1R therapies, have the potential to further shift the treatment landscape from dependence on surgery or chronic treatments to systemic approaches that offer clinically meaningful tumor reduction, pain relief, and functional gains, with manageable toxicity, shortened treatment duration, and reduced tumor recurrence in patients with TGCT.

Emactuzumab is a novel, IV-administered, monoclonal immunoglobulin G1 antibody against the CSF-1R, with a unique mechanism of action (blocking the receptor dimerization interface) and a short treatment duration, with the potential to offer durable benefit without the challenges associated with chronic oral therapies [31,32,35].

The TANGENT trial will evaluate the safety and efficacy of emactuzumab in adult and adolescent patients with TGCT. The trial design is consistent with prior trials for targeted CSF-1R therapies in patients with TGCT (ie, ENLIVEN, MOTION, and MANEUVER), including similar design, study population, and primary and secondary endpoints. TANGENT is a double-blind placebo-controlled phase III study with a study population comprised of patients with TGCT not amenable to surgery. The primary objective of TANGENT was ORR at six months, as measured by RECIST v1.1 (primary endpoint) and tumor volume score (TVS) (key secondary). Additional key secondary endpoints included clinically functional endpoints to evaluate the effect of emactuzumab on range of motion, pain, stiffness, and functional outcomes (PROMIS PF) [28–30,42]. Differences between TANGENT and prior trials for targeted CSF-1R therapies in patients with TGCT include earlier MRI assessment in TANGENT (first postbaseline assessment at 6 weeks vs 13 weeks) and exclusion of patients with prior TKI treatment in TANGENT.

The TANGENT study introduces a distinctive treatment paradigm for TGCT, and is the only study evaluating a short-course, intravenous CSF-1R antibody, rather than continuous oral inhibition. PROs will also be prospectively assessed after treatment, which are relevant for treatment decisions [43]. If sustained tumor control, functional improvement, and meaningful patient-reported benefit can be demonstrated after completing a treatment course, emactuzumab could represent a shift toward a time-limited, disease-modifying therapeutic option to help reduce hospital visits, lower economic costs, and potentially improve patients’ quality of life.
