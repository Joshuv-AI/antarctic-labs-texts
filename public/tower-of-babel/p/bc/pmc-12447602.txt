Neuroimaging in traumatic brain injury: a bibliometric analysis
Authors: Sidhartha R Ramlatchan, Benjamin Colaco Jamal, Latha Ganti, Samiya Natesan-Torres, Sreeja Natesan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMild traumatic brain injury (mTBI), commonly referred to as concussion, accounts for the vast majority of TBI cases globally, yet remains challenging to define and manage due to its heterogeneous presentation and often subtle clinical and radiographic findings. This bibliometric review aims to characterize the global research landscape on TBI, identifying trends, prolific contributors, and key thematic areas in the literature.MethodsA bibliometric analysis was conducted using a structured search of the Web of Science Core Collection to identify publications focused on neuroimaging and traumatic brain injury. Data were analyzed using VOSviewer to map co-authorship networks, keyword co-occurrence, and citation patterns over the past decade.ResultsThe analysis revealed a steady increase in publications related to TBI, with prominent contributions from institutions in the United States, Canada, and Australia. Common research themes included sports-related concussion, military blast injuries, pediatric TBI, neurocognitive outcomes, and return-to-play protocols. Despite the high volume of research, heterogeneity in diagnostic terminology and outcome measures remains prevalent.ConclusionsThis bibliometric review highlights the growing scholarly attention to TBI while emphasizing the need for standardization in diagnostic criteria and outcome assessment. Future research should prioritize consensus-building and longitudinal cohort studies to address persistent gaps in knowledge and improve patient care.Keywords: Neuroimaging, TBI, Bibliometric

Introduction

Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI) is a significant cause of death and disability [1, 2]. It is caused by an external force such as a blow, jolt, or penetrating injury, resulting in bleeding, swelling, and damage to brain tissue. Globally, an estimated 69 million people sustain a TBI each year [3, 4]. This disparity reflects a heavier burden in low- and middle-income countries, where road traffic collisions and falls are more prevalent [5]. In the United States, there are over 25 million injury-related emergency department visits annually. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), TBIs are a major cause of death and disability nationwide with approximately 3 to 4 million new cases annually [2, 6].

Young adults aged 15 to 24 account for the highest number of emergency medicine visits related to TBI. While most TBIs are classified as mild, severe injuries can lead to prolonged unconsciousness, memory loss, seizures, and sleep disturbances [2]. The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) is routinely used in the initial evaluation to assess levels of consciousness (LOC) but does not provide detailed information about the injury itself. Beyond the clinical implications, the global economic impact of TBI is estimated to exceed $400 billion annually, placing a significant burden not only on the individual and family, but the healthcare systems [3, 4, 7].

Depending on the force and its effect, TBIs can range widely in severity and lasting symptoms as noted in Table 1 [2, 8]. Specifically, the GCS, duration of LOC, presence and length of post-traumatic amnesia (PTA), and imaging findings help define the classification of TBI. The majority of cases (70–90%) of TBI are considered mild and are typically limited to headaches, dizziness, confusion, and other symptoms that do not suggest permanent or long-term damage [2].

Neuroimaging techniques identify the locations and qualities of the TBI, as well as prognoses [6, 9–13]. Noncontrast CT is the neuroimaging scan of choice for effectively and quickly locating hemorrhages, providing context when determining severity of the injury or the need for surgical intervention. However, MRI scans, less commonly used, have proved to be more effective at locating non-hemorrhagic or micro-hemorrhagic injuries that may not be as severe at the moment.

The heterogeneity of TBI cases makes it difficult to standardize treatments at an individual level, and there is no consensus on guidelines to determine when neuroimaging is necessary for a TBI patient [14]. Neuroimaging use for TBI management is a highly debated and researched field, with rules that have been developed, such as a Canadian head CT rule, with the purpose of limiting neuroimaging for head injuries [15]. At the same, there is evidence to suggest that neuroimaging use should be increased for acute care of mild TBIs, due to its value in predicting the occurrence of post-concussion syndrome, ED readmission, and length of hospital admission [16]. This bibliometric analysis study aims to provide an overview of the research done on neuroimaging use for TBI patients from the past four decades, looking at where research is originating from, what categories comprise it, and where trends are pointing.

Methods

Bibliometric methods were applied to examine the medical literature [17]. The Web of Science database was selected for this analysis due to its extensive coverage of high-impact journals and strong citation tracking capabilities [18–21]. These features are essential for evaluating research trends and impact within the field of neuroimaging and TBI and have been utilized in previous bibliometric studies on this topic.

A structured search strategy was developed using the keywords “traumatic brain injury” OR “TBI” AND “neuroimaging” OR “computed tomography” OR “CT” OR “MRI” OR “magnetic resonance imaging.” Boolean operators were used to refine the search and ensure inclusion of relevant publications. The search was limited to articles published between 1983 and 2024, generating a list of 9,981 publications that met the inclusion criteria.

Data extraction included downloading bibliometric details such as author names, article titles, journal names, publication years, keywords, citation counts, and country of origin from the Web of Science. Duplicate entries were removed to ensure accuracy. Key indicators analyzed included total publications, citation counts, h-index, and co-authorship networks to assess productivity, research impact, and collaboration patterns within the field. To enhance the reliability of the findings, data were cross-referenced with PubMed and Scopus. Manual verification was also performed on key data points for added accuracy.

Advanced analyses included co-citation analysis, keyword co-occurrence analysis, and thematic clustering using VOSviewer version 1.6.20. A bar graph illustrating publication counts by year was created using Google Sheets. Network maps were used to visualize relationships among different entities, with larger circles representing greater publication volume and closer proximity indicating stronger co-authorship links. VOSviewer played a central role in the visualization and interpretation of bibliometric data from Web of Science. This comprehensive approach provided a detailed overview of research output, collaboration, and thematic development in the study of neuroimaging for TBI.

Results

The Web of Science database was used to identify 9,981 research articles that included prominent keywords related to both traumatic brain injury and neuroimaging. An analysis of publication volume by year shows that the field has grown steadily since 1983 [Fig. 1]. A noticeable strong upward trend occurred between 2005 and 2006, when the number of publications rose from 56 to a higher level. The peak year was 2020, with 764 publications—the highest annual total and the largest year-over-year increase of 80 articles. This surge was followed by a gradual decline in the number of publications in the years that followed.

Among the top contributing institutions, eight of the ten with the highest publication counts are based in the United States [Fig. 4]. Harvard Medical School leads with 253 publications, followed by the University of Pittsburgh with 236 and the University of Pennsylvania with 230. The University of Cambridge and the University of Toronto are the only non-U.S. institutions in the top ten, ranking seventh and eighth with 205 and 204 publications, respectively. The most cited publication in the dataset, with 2,088 citations, is a 2017 article titled “Efficient multi-scale 3D CNN with fully connected CRF for accurate brain lesion segmentation,” co-authored by Imperial College London and the University of Cambridge.

Discussion

The peak in publication volume observed in 2020, followed by a gradual decline, may reflect the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic. During early lockdowns, researchers may have had increased time to write and submit manuscripts, while subsequent restrictions, including laboratory closures, likely contributed to reduced output [22]. The overall growth in publications from 1983 to 2020 corresponds with a broader global expansion in scientific activity, including a 66% increase in health science publications between 2010 and 2022 [23, 24]. The relationship between traumatic brain injury (TBI) incidence and publication trends remains unclear due to conflicting data. Some studies report a decline in global TBI incidence from 1990 to 2019, attributed to improved road safety measures [25], while others note a rise between 1990 and 2016, linked to increased falls and road injuries [26, 27]. In 2019, falls accounted for 74% of TBI cases. Geographic and socioeconomic disparities also shape TBI patterns; in parts of Africa, underdeveloped infrastructure contributes to vehicle-related TBIs, whereas in certain Middle Eastern regions, trauma from conflict and terrorism plays a more prominent role [27].

Although global incidence rates of TBI vary widely, they may not fully explain the consistent rise in publications focused on neuroimaging treatments. It is worth considering whether the increasing academic interest in this field, independent of incidence trends, plays a larger role in driving research output. Despite significant progress in neuroimaging technologies, the field continues to hold substantial potential—particularly in improving its ability to predict long-term functional outcomes [28].

The United States, a high-income nation with a well-established research infrastructure, leads neuroimaging research in both publication volume and citation count. Given its substantial funding and emphasis on scientific advancement, it is not surprising that the U.S. holds the top position in this field. Its prominent role suggests that investment in research significantly contributes to national influence, productivity, and scholarly output. The European Union also plays a major role in scientific research, with countries like France, Germany, Italy, and Spain appearing prominently on the network map of nations active in TBI neuroimaging studies. Their close clustering on the map indicates a higher likelihood of collaboration. At the same time, several Asian countries have significantly expanded their research output between 2020 and 2022—China and Iran by 250%, and India by 180% [23]. These countries now stand out as newer contributors in the field, as shown on the network map displaying year of entry. In contrast, many low-income countries remain limited in participation due to the high cost of neuroimaging technology and the need for specialized training and computing capacity [29]. The strong presence of American universities among the top contributing institutions is likely tied to their high levels of research and development investment [30].

The broad medical categorization of the publication dataset reflects the extent to which neuroimaging is integrated into various areas of medicine. While the categories “Radiology Nuclear Medicine” and “Neuroimaging” have fewer publications compared to “Clinical Neurology” or “Surgery,” neuroimaging has long been regarded as a standard component of medical practice. In 1975, the American Academy of Neurology issued a committee statement describing neuroimaging as “an integral part of the practice of neurology, that requires broad knowledge of neuroanatomy, neuropathology, pathophysiology, and clinical neurology” [31]. This longstanding recognition of neuroimaging’s clinical value has likely contributed to the field’s continued growth and strong presence across multiple specialties.

Limitations

This bibliometric analysis of traumatic brain injury (TBI) and neuroimaging has several important limitations. Using Web of Science as the sole data source may limit the completeness of the analysis. While the database offers wide coverage and includes historical publications, it updates more slowly than platforms like PubMed. As a result, recent studies or emerging trends may not be fully captured [18, 19, 32]. Additionally, the analysis relied on specific keywords related to TBI and neuroimaging. Relevant studies that did not use terms like “traumatic brain injury,” “CT,” “MRI,” or “neuroimaging” may have been excluded. For example, work on advanced modalities such as diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) or functional MRI (fMRI) might not have been retrieved if these terms were absent or infrequently used [13, 28]. Broader search strategies may help improve the inclusiveness of future analyses. Neuroimaging also continues to evolve quickly, with new technologies, terminology, and applications emerging regularly. Research priorities may shift soon after the publication of this analysis, which could limit its relevance over time [13, 28]. Ongoing updates and periodic reviews are necessary to keep pace with developments in the field. In addition to these methodological limitations, there are broader global disparities in research output and access.

Neuroimaging plays a complex and evolving role in TBI care, yet not all countries have the same capacity to contribute to this body of work. Nations with fewer resources tend to produce less research and have limited visibility in the field. However, every country faces distinct health and safety challenges that shape their TBI burden and could inform globally relevant solutions. Strengthening collaboration between leading research institutions and underrepresented regions is essential. Applying findings to diverse populations can help guide the development of more inclusive, evidence-based standards for the use of neuroimaging in TBI diagnosis and treatment.

Conclusion

Neuroimaging’s nuanced and often debated role in traumatic brain injury (TBI) treatment places it at the forefront of biomedical research. Whether evaluating functional prognosis, radiation exposure, or hospital costs, the field’s extensive research has led to significant technological advances and ongoing refinement. However, disparities in research output and influence across countries must be addressed to fully realize neuroimaging’s potential in TBI care. Each nation faces distinct health and safety challenges that contribute to its TBI burden, offering important context for globally applicable treatment strategies. Countries with limited research funding and reduced access to advanced imaging technologies naturally have lower output and influence in this area. As such, increased collaboration and outreach between established leaders and emerging research communities are essential to foster the exchange of knowledge and innovation. Applying research findings across diverse TBI populations will help build more inclusive, evidence-based guidelines for the use of neuroimaging worldwide.
