The role of the infrapatellar fat pad in osteoporotic knee osteoarthritis: bone-fat crosstalk: cross-sectional studies
Authors: Qin Yin, Yu Liu, Fei Yin, Wenyu Xiao, Zhiyuan Guan, Jun Gu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundsOsteoporosis (OP) and osteoarthritis (OA) of the knee are common and serious human health problems. However, the relationship between knee OP and the progression of OA, and the potential association between knee OP and adipose tissue within the knee, is not well understood. This study aims to characterize differences in IPFP morphology and lipid metabolism markers, bone metabolism profiles between patients with isolated knee OA and those with concurrent OA and OP, to explore potential mechanisms linking adipose tissue dysfunction to OA-OP comorbidity.MethodsThis study enrolled 402 participants (89 healthy volunteers, 100 knee OA patients, 108 OP patients, and 105 OA + OP patients) from September 2011 to January 2023. Synovial inflammation markers (effusion grading: 0–3; IPFP abnormalities: 0–3; synovial proliferation: 0–2) and structural knee abnormalities (Whole-Organ Magnetic Resonance Imaging Score [WORMS]: cartilage, bone marrow edema, meniscus sub-scores) were assessed via MRI. Pathological analysis of IPFP tissue (fibrosis: 0–3; vascularization: 0–2) was performed by two blinded pathologists in OA patients undergoing arthroplasty. Clinical outcomes (WOMAC, Oxford Knee Score, Anterior Knee Pain Score) were evaluated preoperatively and at 1-year follow-up. Risk factors (triglycerides, hip BMD, IPFP abnormalities) were analyzed using LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation.ResultsThe OA + OP group exhibited higher grades of synovial effusion (mean ± SD: 2.1 ± 0.7 vs. 1.3 ± 0.5, p < 0.01), IPFP abnormalities (2.4 ± 0.6 vs. 1.8 ± 0.4), and synovial proliferation scores (1.9 ± 0.5 vs. 1.2 ± 0.3) compared to the OA group. Total WORMS scores were significantly elevated in OA + OP (32.5 ± 8.2 vs. 24.1 ± 6.5, p < 0.001), driven by higher cartilage degradation, increase of bone marrow edema, and meniscus damage sub-scores. Pathological analysis confirmed increased fibrosis (2.7 ± 0.4 vs. 1.9 ± 0.3) and vascularization (1.8 ± 0.5 vs. 1.1 ± 0.2) in OA + OP. At 1-year follow-up, OA + OP patients had worse WOMAC (45.2 ± 10.1 vs. 33.5 ± 8.7) and OKS (22.1 ± 5.3 vs. 28.4 ± 4.9). LASSO regression identified TG, total hip BMD, and IPFP abnormalities as key risk factors.ConclusionOur results identify a pathophysiological triad of (1) IPFP fibrosis/hypervascularization, (2) systemic hypertriglyceridemia, and (3) low BMD that collectively accelerates structural degeneration and symptom progression in knee OA. While genetic and biomechanical factors initiate OA pathogenesis, these acquired metabolic-inflammatory mechanisms represent tractable therapeutic targets for modifying disease evolution in established OA, particularly with OP comorbidity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06065-8.Keywords: Knee osteoarthritis, Osteoporosis, Obesity, Infrapatellar fat pad, Triglycerides

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (OA) is a debilitating condition primarily affecting the elderly, characterized by cartilage degradation and the formation of osteophytes around the joint [1]. Clinically, it shows as chronic pain, reduced mobility, and diminished quality of life, often necessitating total knee replacement surgery for severe cases [2–6]. On the other hand, osteoporosis (OP) is characterized by reduced bone density and microstructural deterioration, significantly increasing the risk of fractures [7–12]. The relationship between OA and OP has been systematically explored in previous studies [13–15]. Although there are relevant studies on the relationship between OA and OP, the role of adipose tissue in the pathogenesis of these conditions remains poorly understood [13].

Lower BMI are well-established risk factors for degenerative bone and joint diseases, including OA and OP [16]. Obesity increases the mechanical load on the knee joint, which may either accelerate or decelerate the progression of OA [17]. Pathological disease such as cartilage degradation, synovial inflammation, and bone erosion involve various cytokines and inflammatory factors derived from adipose tissue [18, 19]. Early studies suggested that obesity might protect against OP, but this is refuted by the fact that obese individuals have a significantly higher risk of fractures, likely due to systemic inflammation affecting bone metabolism and increased adipogenesis leading to reduced bone mass in obese populations [11, 20–22].

Recent studies have explored the relationship between various forms of adipose tissue and OA, including the roles of subcutaneous fat, visceral fat, and intermuscular adipose tissue [23–25]. The infrapatellar fat pad (IPFP), located in the knee cavity and anatomically close to the synovium and cartilage, has been identified as a key player in OA [26]. Current evidence suggests that IPFP exerts significant influences on knee OA [27]. While some studies propose that IPFP secretes pro-inflammatory and matrix-degrading mediators [28–30]others have reported protective effects of IPFP on cartilage tissue [31–34]. Emerging evidence reveals that IPFP-derived mediators (leptin, IL-6) directly impair subchondral bone remodeling via RANKL/OPG imbalance in osteoporotic osteoarthritis (OP-OA) comorbidities [35]. Targeting this adipose-bone axis may therefore offer novel therapeutic strategies for OP-OA syndrome management.

This study aims to investigate whether changes in IPFP are associated with the progression of OA and OP by analyzing the longitudinal changes in various adipose tissue measurements among patients with OA and OP.

Materials and methods

The trial is a controlled, single-center, randomized study. Our hospital’s ethics committee and institutional review board (IRB) approved this study. All participants were informed of the study details and signed the informed consent form. The study adheres to the CONSORT guidelines and complies with the Declaration of Helsinki principles. This work has been reported in line with the STROCSS criteria [36].

Study participants

In this study, 402 patients from our hospital will be enrolled from September 2021 to January 2023. These patients were including 89 healthy subjects, 100 patients with knee OA, 108 patients with OP and 105 patients with knee OA combined with OP (flow chart in Supplementary Fig. 1). Subjects were included as follows: (1) subjects with predominantly knee OA, (2) subjects with Kellgren-Lawrence grade III or IV [37](The detail of Kellgren-Lawrence grade have been supplemented in supplement Table 2), (3) subjects between the ages of 40 and 80 years, and (4) subjects who had undergone TKA. Exclusions were: (1) inflammatory arthritis, (2) post-traumatic arthritis, (3) previous knee surgery, (4) previous knee injury, (5) previous knee infection, (6) lower limb fractures, (7) lower limb tumours, (8) history of total knee or hip replacement, (9) patients with endocrine disorders such as diabetes, thyroid disease and adrenal cortical disease, (10) patients taking medications that may affect body weight or blood lipids, such as lipid-lowering drugs, weight-loss drugs and corticosteroids, and (11) neurological or muscle dysfunction [38].

Demographic information and metabolic factors

OP is diagnosed primarily based on total hip BMD T values ≤-2.5 and 1 or more moderate or severe vertebral fractures or 2 or more minor vertebral fractures; or total BMD T values − 2.0 or less at the femoral neck or femoral spine and 2 or more moderate to severe vertebral fractures or proximal femoral BMD of the lumbar spine and proximal femoral spine are then assessed by dual-energy (lunar or holographic) x-ray absorptiometry [39].

Surgical techniques

All surgeries were performed under general anesthesia using a standard medial parapatellar approach based on mechanical alignment of the knee. Tibial resection was performed using an extramedullary reference perpendicular to the mechanical axis, for example, ‘Extramedullary reference is referring to the center of ankle and the second metatarsal bone. It is also check by me medial part of the TTA. The distal femur was resected using endo medullary reference with a valgus angle of 5–7°, depending on the patient’s morphology. A 4-in-1 distal femoral cut block was used with a likely 3° of tibial physiological varus and 3° of external rotation relative to the posterior condylar axis to achieve a symmetric rectangular gap. The Whiteside line was used to check the osteotomy plane during surgery. Most infrapatellar fat was preserved to uphold patella vascularization. Inflammatory synovium was completely removed, and soft tissue release was performed if needed to balance the soft tissues and achieve gap symmetry. The implant was fixed with bone cement.

Metabolic factor assessment

A calibrated beam scale was used to measure the weight (in kilograms) of the unshod patient. The height of the unshod patient was measured in millimetres with the aid of a measuring tape. BMI is calculated by dividing your weight by your height squared. Body weight was classified into three groups based on the BMI: normal weight (BMI < 24.9 kg/m2), overweight (BMI > 25 and ≤ 29.9 kg/m2), and obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2). The abdominal and hip circumferences were measured around the abdomen or buttocks using a tape measure. TC, TG, LDL-C and HDL-C were measured from venous blood collected after an overnight fast. Hyperlipidaemia was defined as TC ≥ 6.22mmol/L, TG ≥ 2.26mmol/L and LDL-C ≥ 4.14 mmol/L [40]. Serum concentrations of the bone-turnover markers bisomer of C-terminal telopeptide of type I collagen (CTX; LabCorp), 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (3) (1,25-vitD, Covance), alkaline phosphatase (ALP, Covance), calcitonin and osteocalcin (OCN, Covance) were measured.

MRI protocol

With the patient in the supine position, MRI images of the knee were obtained using the same protocol with a cross-calibrated 3.0 Tesla scanner (Magnetom Trio, Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). Semi-quantitative analysis of synovitis and structural abnormalities in knee OA was performed using 3 sequences: a two-dimensional intermediate weight (IW) turbine spin echo (TSE) sequence in the coronal plane, a three-dimensional double-echo steady-state (DESS) sequence in the sagittal plane, a water-excited sequence with coronal and axial reform, and a two-dimensional IW fat-suppressed TSE sequence in the sagittal plane.

Semi-quantitative assessment of synovitis

MRIs of the knees were carefully graded in a randomized fashion by two independent investigators with over 10 years of experience in the interpretation of musculoskeletal MRIs, blinded to patient group and study purpose. In cases of image ambiguity, consensus readings were made by a third independent radiologist with 20 years’ experience. As it is not always possible to distinguish between synovial thickening and intra-articular effusion on non-contrast enhanced MRI, the term effusion synovitis was used as previously described [41–43]. As described in previous studies [35], six parameters of each knee were examined on the MRI images and scored semi-quantitative. First, according to the Anterior Cruciate Ligament Osteoarthritis Score (ACLOAS) [44]fluid synovitis was graded using the maximum anteroposterior diameter of the suprapatellar recess, assessed in midline sections of sagittal fat-saturated IW or DESS images [43]. Specifically, exudative synovitis is scored from 0 to 3 based on the degree of capsule dilatation, with anteroposterior diameter < 2 mm receiving a score of 0, ≥ 2 to < 5 mm receiving a score of 1, ≥ 5 to < 10 mm receiving a score of 2 and ≥ 10 mm receiving a score of 3. Second, MRI Osteoarthritis Knee Score (MOAKS) were developed. The MOAKS instrument refines the scoring of BMLs (providing regional delineation and scoring across regions), cartilage (sub-regional assessment), and refines the elements of meniscal morphology (adding meniscal hypertrophy, partial maceration and progressive partial maceration) scoring[18] [32]. Fluid synovitis is scored on axial fat-saturated DESS images on a scale of 0 to 4. Specifically, normal amount of physiological fluid is scored as 0; small amount of fluid that continues to diffuse into the posterior patellar space is scored as 1; moderate amount of fluid with mild bulging of the suprapatellar bursa is scored as 2; large amount of fluid with envelope dilation is scored as 3. The size of the infrapatellar fat pad (IPFP) was assessed using sagittal IW fat suppression images, with no signal abnormality being grade 0, ≤ 33% of the region being grade 1, 34 to 66% of the region being grade 2, and ≥ 66% of the region being grade 3 44. Fourth, the highest signal intensity of the IPFP abnormality was graded in the sagittal IW fat suppression images, with grade 0 = none, grade 1 = mild (below cartilage), grade 2 = moderate (above cartilage but below fluid), and grade 3 = severe (above fluid) [44]. Fifth, in the presence of joint effusion, the synovial proliferation score (SPS) of the knee is graded on the basis of fat-saturated IW and DESS images, with a synovitis score of ≥ 1 according to ACLOAS or MOAKS [41, 45, 46]. Smooth synovium without proliferation and synovial band grade 1; synovium slightly irregular with some synovial bands or bodies grade 2; extensive synovial thickening and irregular villous nodular hyperplasia grade 3. Finally, the same method was used to grade the knee with cysts of the popliteum for SPS.

Whole-organ MRI score system

Structure abnormalities in knee OA were evaluated using the semi-quantitative Whole-Organ MRI Score (WORMS) system [47–49]. Parameters including meniscus, cartilage, bone marrow oedema and subchondral cysts were scored separately. Meniscal lesions were graded from 0 (intact) to 4 (complete maceration / destruction or resection) in each of the three sub-regions (anterior horn, body segment, and posterior horn) of the medial and lateral meniscus [47]. In each of the six regions (medial femur, lateral femur, trochlea, patella, medial tibia and lateral tibia), cartilage lesions were scored from 0 (normal thickness and signal) to 6 (full thickness cartilage loss in ≥ 75% of the region), bone marrow oedema from 0 (none) to 3 (≥ 50% of the region) and subchondral cyst from 0 (none) to 3 (≥ 50% of the region). The sum of meniscus, cartilage, bone marrow oedema and subchondral cyst was used to calculate the total WORMS score.

Clinical evaluation

Functional outcomes were assessed using patient-reported measures at baseline and 1-year follow-up, including the WOMAC [50]Forgotten Joint Score (FJS) [51]Oxford Knee Score (OKS) [52]and Knee Association Score (KSS), which consists of the Knee Association Knee Score (KSKS) and the Knee Association Functional Score (KSFS) [53]. Patient satisfaction was rated on a five-point Likert scale (very satisfied, satisfied, neutral, dissatisfied, very dissatisfied), subdivided into satisfied (very satisfied or satisfied) and dissatisfied (neutral, dissatisfied, or very dissatisfied). Complications and reoperations during follow-up were recorded.

Measured intra- and inter-observer repeatability

MRI images were reviewed and graded by two independent investigators for interobserver reproducibility. Intraobserver reliability was assessed by grading 20 randomly selected cases twice, separated by 4 weeks. Cohen’s kappa values were used to evaluate agreement (0.61–0.80 for basic agreement, 0.81–1.00 for near-perfect agreement) [54, 55].

Histological and immunohistochemical analysis

PFP samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 4 μm slices. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene and hydrated in ethanol. H&E staining was used for tissue morphology. Oil red staining assessed fat content and distribution (LEAGENE, DC0041). Masson’s staining detected IPFP fibrosis [26]. A grading scheme was developed for histopathological evaluation [56]. The synovium was not included in the histology grading. Ten images were scored for intraobserver reliability over 7 days. Between-observer reliability was 0.91 (95% confidence intervals (CI), 0.44–0.81). A third grader provided blinded grading. Cellularity, fibrosis, and vascularity were summed for the histological score. Image J software was used for quantitative analysis of Masson-stained sections.

LASSO regression prediction model

To identify risk factors for OP in patients with OA, the LASSO method was applied. Non-zero coefficients from LASSO regression were used to build a prediction model. Odds ratios (ORs), 95% CI, and p-values were calculated. Sociodemographic and disease-related factors with p < 0.05 were included. The Harrell c index assessed discriminative power, and a calibration curve was generated. Decision curve analysis identified clinically significant nomograms and calculated net benefits. ROC curves (AUC) from training and validation sets evaluated the model’s diagnostic performance [57–61].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as mean ± SD, and categorical variables as frequencies. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity with the Levene test. ANOVA, LSD, or Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to compare synovial inflammatory markers, WORMS scores, and preoperative functional outcomes across groups. Covariance analysis was used to adjust for baseline differences in functional outcomes. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated as absolute values (0.2 = small, 0.5 = medium, 0.8 = large). Chi-square tests analyzed categorical variables. Paired t-tests compared preoperative and postoperative functional outcomes. Spearman’s rank correlation assessed the relationship between surgery and OA synovitis or structural abnormalities. P < 0.05 was considered significant. IBM SPSS version 22 was used for all analyses. Power analysis with G-Power 3.1.9.4 indicated that 60 participants per group would provide 80% power.

Multivariate regression analysis and LASSO regression analysis

We used multivariate regression analysis to analyze the possible risk factors and the results showed that TG, KL score, hip T score, lumbar T score and ALP were the most important risk factors. Nomograms were constructed based on the above independent predictors using the LASSO regression model. There was a high level of agreement between patients on the risk calibration curve for the discordant isotype map (Fig. 4). The training group had an index of 0.932 (95% CI: -0.23 to 1.55), suggesting considerable discrimination. A high degree of predictability was indicated by the rupture risk graph.

LASSO regression analysis among the risk factors of patients with OA and OP

The decision curve analysis for the relationship between IPFP in OA and OP is shown in Fig. 4. As shown by the decision curves, the prediction nomogram was superior to the strategy of using determinants of fat and bone metabolism related indicators with IPFP in osteoarthritis and osteoporosis. Over a range where both the patient and physician probability criteria were between 11% and 86%, with some overlap, the net benefit was equivalent. Figure 4 also shows the ROC curves for fat and bone metabolism related indicators with IPFP in the OA and OP signatures. In the training set, the AUC of the nomogram for fat and bone metabolism related indicators using IPFP in OA and OP was greater than that of the clinical factor model (p = 0.002, AUC = 0.976). In addition, there was a significant difference in the AUC nomogram in the validation set between the indicators related to fat and bone metabolism with the IPFP in osteoarthritis and OP and the clinical factor model (p = 0.012, AUC = 0.923). These results suggest that TG, total hip BMD and IPFP abnormality on MRI are more accurate predictors of the diagnosis of OA and OP.

Discussion

Adipose tissue, a critical component of the human body, plays a multifaceted role in the pathogenesis of various diseases [62–64]. Specifically, OA and OP represent two conditions that significantly impact the health and well-being of the elderly population [65]. The present study focuses on the infrapatellar fat pad (IPFP) tissue within the knee joint cavity and its potential influence on the progression of OA. The findings revealed that patients with both OA and OP exhibited more severe knee synovitis, structural abnormalities, and poorer pre- and postoperative functional outcomes compared to OA patients alone. Furthermore, the study highlighted that triglycerides (TG) and lumbar spine bone mineral density (BMD) changes may serve as important predictors of knee symptoms and outcomes in patients with OA combined with OP. Pathological alterations in the IPFP and imaging changes were closely associated with the progression of OA, with OP exacerbating the inflammation and pathological progression of the IPFP in OA patients.

The relationship between OA and OP is complex and well-established [64, 66]. A significant and strong association between the two conditions is supported by the many studies [67, 68]. Many patients with knee osteoarthritis (KOA) are also diagnosed with OP or osteopenia [69]. The interplay between OA and OP may create a vicious cycle, contributing to knee pain and disability [13]. In China, the prevalence of OA combined with OP reaches 30% [70, 71]. Patients who have undergone knee surgery and exhibit OA symptoms often experience more severe knee joint symptoms and poorer long-term outcomes. Previous retrospective studies have demonstrated that OA does not protect against or increase the risk of OP when compared to age- and sex-matched controls [64].

The variety of factors plays a very important role in synovitis, such as diet, drugs, exercise, intercurrent illnes [70]. The obesity strengthened synovial metabolite links to blood glucose and inflammation [71], whereas exercise strengthened synovial metabolite links to subchondral bone structure [72]. Diet-induced obesity accelerates intervertebral disc degeneration and knee OA in mice; however, pain-related behaviors precede and are independent of histopathological structural damage [73]. Imrecoxib protects joint cartilage and attenuates OA by modulating synovial macrophage polarization through inactivating COX-2/PGE2 signaling pathway, providing new drug delivery strategy for the clinical treatment of OA [74]. Obesity has long been a focal point in bone metabolic disease research [75]. Despite the complexity and diversity of risk factors and causes, obesity, as a major metabolic disorder, exhibits a similar increasing trend to OA and OP [76, 77]. There is a close association between obesity and OA [17]. Previous studies suggest that obesity may contribute to OA progression by increasing physical activity and promoting cartilage destruction [78, 79]. Current understanding indicates that obesity-induced metabolic and immune dysregulation, along with chronic low-grade systemic inflammation, play a role in OA pathogenesis [16]. Hung et al. reported that obesity is associated with an increased risk of developing and progressing knee joint effusion [80]. Banuls-Mirete et al. found that the relationship between body mass index (BMI) and OA progression is mediated by worsening effusion synovitis, particularly in obese individuals [81]. While genetic predisposition lays the groundwork for OA susceptibility, our findings demonstrate that acquired metabolic disturbances-particularly IPFP-driven inflammation and hypertriglyceridemia-interact with low BMD to accelerate structural degeneration in established disease. These findings underscore the detrimental effects of synovitis in obese OA patients, suggesting that synovitis could be a potential therapeutic target to alleviate OA symptoms and thereby classify obesity-related OA as a distinct subtype of secondary OA.

Our hybrid approach integrates mechanical alignment in extension (to ensure limb stability and implant longevity) with gap-balancing techniques in flexion (to optimize soft-tissue tension and joint kinematics). This strategy is supported by: A rectangular flexion gap reduces polyethylene stress concentrations [82]. For clinical outcome, combined MA-kinematic protocols show lower revision rates in osteoporotic patients due to reduced shear forces [83]. We acknowledge the controversy and have added a discussion paragraph comparing our protocol to pure kinematic alignment (KA) and functional alignment (FA), emphasizing that our method minimizes bone resection in osteoporotic patients.

The crosstalk between bone and adipose tissue underscores the pivotal role of obesity in influencing bone mineral density (BMD) [84, 85]. The relationship between estrogen levels and obesity may, in part, explain the apparent ambiguity of higher BMD values in obese individuals [21]. Studies have shown that estrogen concentrations in the blood of postmenopausal obese women are higher than in non-obese control [86]. This finding may help explain the observed association between higher BMD and higher BMI [22]. However, estrogen is not the sole regulator of bone mass, and other factors may influence both bone and fat mass [87]. Using multivariate regression models and LASSO regression models, we identified key risk factors in patients with severe OA and OP. The findings indicated that MRI-derived inflammatory changes and TG changes in the IPFP may be the primary risk predictors. Serum TG levels are correlated with the incidence of bone marrow lesions within a 2-year period, potentially due to lipid embolism and thrombosis-induced ischemia under the cartilage, leading to osteonecrosis [88]. Dyslipidemia, particularly hypertriglyceridemia (HR = 1.07, 95% CI:1.05–1.09), is also slightly associated with OA risk [89]. Notably, each incremental increase in the TG index was associated with a 6.34-fold higher risk of OA (OR = 7.34; 95%CI:2.25, 23.93; p = 0.0010) [90].

The infrapatellar fat pad (IPFP), as the largest adipose tissue within the knee joint, plays a crucial role in the overall health and function of the joint [91]. The IPFP has a substantial impact on various joint diseases due to its unique anatomical position and physiological functions [28]. It contains a rich network of blood vessels and nerves, which allows it to interact closely with surrounding tissues [92]. The IPFP can secrete numerous bioactive substances, including cytokines and adipokines, which may contribute to the development and progression of joint diseases such as OA [93]. Specifically, we have observed that the levels of fibrosis and vascularization in the IPFP of OA patients are markedly more severe than those in the control group. Fibrosis, which is characterized by the excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix proteins, leads to a stiffening of the tissue [94]. While genetic variants undoubtedly influence OA susceptibility, our findings suggest acquired metabolic disturbances-particularly IPFP-driven inflammation and hypertriglyceridemia-may accelerate disease progression in genetically predisposed individuals. Future studies integrating genotyping with longitudinal imaging/metabolic phenotyping could elucidate gene-environment interactions in OA-OP comorbidity [95–98].This process can impair the normal function of the IPFP, which plays a crucial role in joint mechanics and inflammation regulation. Furthermore, the increased vascularization in the IPFP of OA patients suggests an elevated presence of blood vessels within the tissue. This heightened vascular activity may contribute to the inflammatory processes and could be indicative of an ongoing reparative response or pathological changes within the joint environment [94]. In addition to these findings, our study has also examined the IPFP of patients who suffer from both OA and OP. In this subgroup, we have noted that the levels of inflammation and fibrosis are even more pronounced. OP appears to exacerbate the inflammatory and fibrotic processes in the IPFP. This combination of OA and OP may create a synergistic effect, leading to more severe tissue alterations and potentially influencing the overall progression of joint degeneration [99]. These observations highlight the complex interplay between different pathological conditions and their impact on the IPFP.

Limitations

There were the following limitations in our study, firstly, the small sample size and short follow-up time of our study required an increase in sample size and increased follow-up time to enhance the generalizability of the study. Subsequently, the pathological detection of our study only carried out the IPFP tissue obtained during total knee arthroplasty surgery in the OA group and the OA combined with OP group, so the samples analyzed were only the above two groups, and there was a lack of comparison of pathological data between the blank control and the osteoporosis group, so it is necessary to supplement the animal experimental results for verification in future studies. Finally, our study analyzed synovitis of the knee for semi-quantitative analysis, so the sensitivity and accuracy were lower than quantitative methods. Second, we lacked MRI results in long-term follow-up, so there were no long-term follow-up results for synovitis, so there was a lack of correlation between synovitis and postoperative functional outcomes. Our case-control design cannot establish causality, and genetic/epigenetic factors were not assessed. Future longitudinal studies integrating genotyping with metabolic phenotyping are needed to elucidate gene-environment interactions. Finally, and most critically, the results of long-term follow-up are limited by a variety of factors, including diet, drug intervention, etc., and need to be further refined in future studies.

Conclusion

Our studies found that both OA and OP exhibited more severe knee synovitis, structural abnormalities, and poorer pre- and postoperative functional outcomes compared to OA patients alone. Furthermore, the study highlighted that triglycerides (TG) and lumbar spine bone mineral density (BMD) changes may serve as important predictors of knee symptoms and outcomes in patients with OA combined with OP. The results of this study highlight the effects of IPFP and hyperlipidemia on the symptomatic progression and outcome of knee OA, and highlight the impact of osteoporosis on the progression of OA.
