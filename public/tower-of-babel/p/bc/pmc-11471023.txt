Spatial multiomics atlas reveals smooth muscle phenotypic transformation and metabolic reprogramming in diabetic macroangiopathy
Authors: Yongjiang Qian, Shizheng Xiong, Lihua Li, Zhen Sun, Lili Zhang, Wei Yuan, Honghua Cai, Guoquan Feng, Xiaoguang Wang, Haipeng Yao, Yun Gao, Li Guo, Zhongqun Wang

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetic macroangiopathy has been the main cause of death and disability in diabetic patients. The mechanisms underlying smooth muscle cell transformation and metabolic reprogramming other than abnormal glucose and lipid metabolism remain to be further explored.MethodSingle-cell transcriptome, spatial transcriptome and spatial metabolome sequencing were performed on anterior tibial artery from 11 diabetic patients with amputation. Multi-omics integration, cell communication analysis, time series analysis, network analysis, enrichment analysis, and gene expression analysis were performed to elucidate the potential molecular features.ResultWe constructed a spatial multiomics map of diabetic blood vessels based on multiomics integration, indicating single-cell and spatial landscape of transcriptome and spatial landscape of metabolome. On this basis, our study provides favorable evidence for the cellular fate of smooth muscle cells, which can be transformed into pro-inflammatory chemotactic smooth muscle cells, macrophage-like smooth muscle cells/foam-like smooth muscle cells, and fibroblast/chondroblast smooth muscle cells in the anterior tibial artery of diabetic patients. Background

The main manifestation of diabetic macrovascular disease is atherosclerosis of the aorta, coronary arteries, renal arteries, basilar arteries, and peripheral arterial arteries, which can lead to vascular obstruction or vascular calcification [1]. At the same time, diabetic macroangiopathy can lead to stroke, myocardial infarction, heart failure and amputation, and is the main cause of death and paralysis in diabetic patients. The role of inflammation, hyperglycemia, dysglycemia, smooth muscle cell (SMC) phenotypic transformation, and other factors in diabetic macroangiopathy has been extensively documented in existing studies [2–4]. With the continuous development of spatial omics technology and single-cell technology, it is now possible to conduct gene expression examination with even nanoscale accuracy based on the two-dimensional spatial structure of tissue slices, thus leading to further understanding of disease pathology and physiology [5].

The transformation of smooth muscle cell phenotype was considered to be an important mechanism of diabetic macrovascular disease and the initiation mechanism of vascular calcification [6–10]. SMCs are a very plastic cell type, which can change from physiological contraction to mesenchymal, fibroblast-like, macrophage-like, osteoblast-like or adipocyte like phenotype [11, 12]. However, the phenotypic transformation of smooth muscle in diabetic macroangiopathy remains controversial. Glucose and fatty acid metabolism, neural metabolism, and exosome regulation have recently attracted attention simultaneously [13–15]. The trend of smooth muscle cell transformation in diabetics and metabolic reprogramming other than abnormal glucose and lipid metabolism remain to be further explored.

To gain a more detailed and comprehensive understanding of the specific mechanisms of diabetic macrovascular disease, we performed multiomics methods of spatial transcriptome, spatial metabolome and single-cell sequencing on the lower limb blood vessels of diabetic patients, revealing the spatial characteristics of gene and metabolite expression in diabetic blood vessels from the perspective of cell resolution, and thus constructing a multi-omics map of diabetic blood vessels. By analyzing the gene and metabolite expression levels of different vascular substructures and different cells, the metabolic activation and metabolic reprogramming of diabetic great vessels were revealed. Through further definition and transcriptional drive analysis of SMCs subsets, we identified three phenotypic transformation trends of SMCs, including macrophage-like SMCs, inflammatory chemotactic SMCs, and fibroblast-like SMCs. In addition, we identified the potential transcription factor of driving smooth muscle cells phenotypic transformation and constructed transcriptional regulatory network. Based on the public database and quasi-time series analysis, the driving transcription factors corresponding to different phenotypic transformations were determined. Our study can provide a basis for further research on vascular smooth muscle lineage tracing and vascular metabolic reprogramming. The construction of multiomics atlas of diabetic great vascular diseases also provides a new direction for clinical treatment and detection.

Methods

Clinical samples and ethics

The clinical samples and related information obtained in this study were all approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Affiliated Hospital of Jiangsu University, and all patients gave written informed consent. Included diabetic amputation patients met the following criteria: (1) Diagnosed with diabetes according to the diagnostic criteria of China’s Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Type 2 Diabetes (2020); (2) Received diabetic foot major amputation according to the Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Diabetic Foot in China (2019); (3) Complete relevant clinical data; (4) Computed Tomography (CT) of lower limbs showed significant calcification; (5) Sign informed consent to participate in this study. Patients meeting the following criteria were excluded: (1) Patients with chronic kidney disease, dialysis patients and other diseases that significantly affect vascular calcification; (2) Vulnerable groups such as mental cognitive-behavioral disorders; (3) With lower extremity arterial CT deficiency; (4) With severe hepatic and renal insufficiency and other multiple organ failure; (5) With Malignant tumor patient. From June 2021 to June 2023, a total of 11 patients amputated anterior tibial artery specimens were sequenced for quality control, and Table S1 summarized clinical characteristics of this patient cluster. Included car accident amputation group met the following criteria: (1) Complete relevant clinical data; (2) CT of lower limbs showed no calcification; (3) Sign informed consent to participate in this study. Patients meeting the following criteria were excluded: (1) Patients with diabetes, chronic kidney disease, dialysis patients and other diseases that significantly lead vascular calcification; (2) Vulnerable groups such as mental cognitive-behavioral disorders; (3) With lower extremity arterial CT deficiency; (4) With severe hepatic and renal insufficiency and other multiple organ failure; (5) With Malignant tumor patient.

Single-cell transcriptome

A single-cell suspension was prepared from the tibial anterior vessels isolated from 4 diabetic patients. The samples were labeled CA1, CA2, CA3, NC1, NC2 and NC3. CA samples were the corresponding blood vessel specimens where the anterior tibial artery calcification can be seen in the lower limb CT as samples of the calcification group. NC samples were the corresponding blood vessel specimen in the lower extremity CT without calcification of anterior tibial artery as the control sample. CA1, NC1 came from the same patient and CA3, NC3 from the same patient. After the dead cells were removed, the samples were sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 platform. Sequencing reads were processed with the Cell Ranger pipeline (version 3.0.2; 10 × Genomics) and were compared with the 10 × Genomics mm10 reference transcriptome package (version 3.0.0). Cell expression matrix was further analyzed based on seurat package (v5.0) [16]. We filtered cells that have unique feature counts less than 200 or have > 5% mitochondrial counts. Use scDblFinder to evaluate the doublet identification cells [17], then perform normalizing and Scaling on the data. We chose 15 Principal Component Analysis (PCA) dimensionality to find neighbors and clustered data with 0.8 resolution. Two-dimensional Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was used to represent cell clusters. The annotation of cell types and subtypes was conducted using the Blueprint and Encode databases as single references, in conjunction with marker genes based on published literature. Raw data files and processed data files had been uploaded in Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GSE248609).

Spatial transcriptome

Spatial transcriptome was prepared from the tibial anterior vessels isolated from 4 diabetic patients. The samples were labeled CA1, CA2, CA3, NC1, NC2 and NC3. CA1, NC1 came from the same patient and CA3, NC3 from the same patient. The frozen sections were embedded and sequentially sequenced on the NovaSeq analysis platform after sample quality control. The displantable data were processed by Space Ranger pipeline. Further data analysis was performed using the seurat package. Seurat (v5.0) was used to process Space Ranger output files. We used SCTransform to normalize the data, ScaleData to scale the data, RunPCA to perform dimensionality reduction, FindNeighbors and FindClusters to cluster ST points, and used RunUMAP to visualize the data. Loupe browser was used to annotate the tissue. For tissue structure annotation, the spot corresponding to dark blue HE staining was annotated as calcification, the spot on the inner side of the inner elastic lamina was annotated as plaque, the spot on the outer side of the inner elastic lamina where smooth muscle cell structures were visible was annotated as media, and the spot on the outer side of the media was annotated as adventitia. We performed function enrichment analysis of the vascular tissue structure. Spatial deconvolution using Robust Cell Type Decomposition (RCTD) to connect scRNA-seq. Raw data files and processed data files were available in GSE248608.

Spatial metabolome

Frozen sections of 15 blood vessels from 7 diabetic amputations were prepared and divided into calcified cluster (n = 7) and non-calcified cluster (n = 8) according to HE staining. The samples were labeled CA1-7 and NC1-8. AFAI-MSI imaging platform was used to collect data in positive (POS) and negative (NEG) ion scanning modes respectively. After the sample was ionized, ions with positive charges and negative charges would appear simultaneously. According to the ion charged charge was scanned separately. Cardinal2 package was used for data preprocessing of the raw data. The matrix was based on seurat package for further data analysis. We used ScaleData to scale the data, RunPCA to perform dimensionality reduction, FindNeighbors and FindClusters to cluster spatial metabolome spots, and use RunUMAP to visualize the data. Ggplot2 was used to data visualization. MSIreader was used to annotate the tissue structure, and annotation was done as described in Sect. “Spatial transcriptome”. Raw data files and after processing the data files can be searched in group id (e274b50a-899c-11ee-adab-636868f00bce) from METASPACE (https://metaspace2020.eu/).

Polygenic score based genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and classical calcifying genes

Referred to four clinical studies including GWAS and Mendelian randomization about vascular calcification [18–21], we screened out 21 related genes that may be highly involved in calcification as follows: PHACTR1, CDKN2B-AS1, CDKN2B, APOE, APOB, ENPP3, ENPP1, IGFBP3, CXCL12, ARID5B, ADK, FGF23, COL4A1, COL4A2, ADAMTS7, MORF4L1, HDAC9, RAP1GAP, KL, FGF23, PSRC1, PIK3CG and CELSR2. Then, we used AddModuleScore to score the single cell data, and screened the cell types with scores greater than 0 as the cells highly involved in calcification. We then performed polygenic scores for classical calcifying genes including RUNX2, SPP1, SP7, IBSP and BGLAP to identify the major calcifying cells.

Phenotypic transformation analysis of smooth muscle cells

Subset function was used to extract the corresponding cell populations of SMCs and fibroblasts. The extracted seurat object was converted into h5ad format by SeuratDisk [16], and the cell velocity was predicted by referring to the TFvelo process [22]. The root cell and transformation trend were visually identified on the processed anndata. Monocle3 was used to process the matrix and metadata of smooth muscle cells and fibroblasts, and the spatial coordinate correspondence of UMAP was imported into the monocle3 object, and the root was determined according to the root cell of TFvelo [23]. In addition, CytoTRACE2 was used to evaluate the differentiation potential of the cells. We identified marker genes for each smooth muscle subcluster by running the FindAllMarkers function in the Seurat package (Table S2), setting logfc.threshold = 1 and min.pct = 0.8. The transcription factors (Table S3) shared by these subcluster marker genes (log2FC ≥ 1) were inferred in Transcription Regulatory Relationships Unraveled Sentence-based Text mining (TRRUST) [24]. We also screened 55 highly active transcription factors based on DoRothEA and their trend in pseudotime series was observed. By adjusting the root of monocle3 object, the expression abundance of transcription factors with different transformation trends was analyzed.

Metabolic pathway network analysis and identify metabolic reprogramming

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis was performed on genes with spatial transcriptome structure characteristics and metabolites with spatial metabolome structure characteristics, respectively. Metabolites, tissue structure, genes and metabolic pathways were used as nodes to construct a network connecting nodes. The network uses cytoscape v3 for visualization. The frequency histogram of vascular structure and metabolic pathway connection counts was made. We used limma for differential expression gene analysis of GSE250283 [25] to obtain the differential genes in blood vessels of diabetic patients and normal population, and intersected them with the aforementioned characteristic metabolic genes to obtain metabolic reprogramming genes.

Expression validation

Metabolic genes with both spatial characteristics and cell cluster expression characteristics were selected, and ALDH1B1 antibody (Boster, PB1092),FBP1 antibody (abcam, ab109732), NDUFA4L2 antibody (Boster, NDUFA4L2), PLA2G2A antibody (affinity, DF6366), SCD antibody (Proteintech, M02273), ɑ-SMA antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, F3777), Vimentin antibody(Boster, BM0135), CD68 antibody (abcam, ab283654) and SDS antibody (Boster, BM0135) were used. To stain with immunofluorescence on the frozen sections remaining from the previous sequencing. CYP27A1 antibody (67045-1-lg) and HNMT antibody (11874-1-AP) were used for immumnohistochemical staining.

Immunohistochemistry

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue sections were subjected to deparaffinization and rehydration. To recover the antigen epitopes, samples were placed in an ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA)-based antigen retrieval solution and boiled for 110 s in a pressure cooker, followed by natural cooling to room temperature. Subsequently, tissues were surrounded with a tissue pen, and 3% hydrogen peroxide was applied to inhibit endogenous enzyme activity, while goat serum was used to block specific antibodies. Monoclonal primary antibodies were then applied to the slices and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Following this incubation, secondary antibodies were applied for 10 min. Subsequently, a streptavidin–biotin-peroxidase complex was applied for another 10 min. The reaction was visualized using a diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen substrate for 5 min, counterstained with hematoxylin for 1 min, and subsequently washed with 1% hydrochloric acid in ethanol for 3 s. Finally, the slides were mounted with a neutral gum and observed under a light microscope. Five non-adjacent random images were captured as replicates for each slice, and then image analysis was performed using ImageJ. The positive coloring area and AverageOptical Density (AOD) were calculated for each image. To ensure blind experimentation, the pathologist responsible for performing the immunohistochemical staining was not informed about the grouping of the sections.

Immunofluorescence

Frozen sections were rewarmed for 20–30 min and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. The sections were then placed in an EDTA-based antigen repair buffer (pH 6.0) in a pressure cooker for antigen retrieval. Using a tissue pen, a circular border was drawn around the tissue. Next, 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) was added and the sections were sealed for 30 min. Primary monoclonal antibodies were applied to the slices and the samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C. Following this, the sections were removed from the refrigerator and warmed for an additional 20 min. Then, the secondary antibody was added and the samples were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Diluted 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) was subsequently applied to the slices and incubated for 10 min. An appropriate amount of anti-fluorescence quenching agent was then added to each section, and the cover glass was secured. The slides were finally observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Statistical analysis

A sample size calculation was conducted using the PASS11 software. The mean optical density values were determined from five randomly selected non-contiguous 40 × immunohistochemistry section images. Statistical significance tests were performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction to assess functional enrichment analysis and the CytoTRACE2_Score of SMC subpopulations of cells. Box plots and two-sided t-tests were employed to analyse differences between diabetic amputee vessels and those from car accident amputations, given that the data were normally distributed with Shapiro–Wilk test. It is important to note that the patients who exhibited differential expression and were validated in this study were not the same as those who underwent multi-omics sequencing. The following definitions were used to determine statistical significance: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Spatial metabolic landscape of diabetic macroangiopathy

NEG and POS scanning patterns were performed on the adjacent sections of the tissue sections respectively, and clustering was performed on them respectively. This resulted in 7 subclusters for NEG and 25 subclusters for POS (Fig. 2G and H). Certain spatial characteristics could be visually seen. However, the confusion among different subclusters were serious, and the characteristics of marker metabolites were highly nonspecific. There was great metabolic heterogeneity among all blood vessels, among which Neg-identified subclusters were distributed in all blood vessels, CA1, CA2, CA3, CA4, N1, N2, N3 and N7 had relatively similar clustering proportions, and CA5, CA6, N5, N6 and N8 had similar clustering proportions. The multiple subclusters based on pos identification were only characterized in one blood vessel. Blood vessels from the same patient were heterogeneous, with CA6 and CA7 having similar clusters, CA3 and N3 having similar clusters, and CA4 and N4 having similar clusters (Figure S7).

Similarly, the spatial metabolome was divided into calcification, plaque, media, and adventitia based on HE sections. Through differential expression analysis, most of the heterocyclic compounds identified in the neg were enriched in the calcified region (Table S5). However, metabolites were not significantly enriched in the adventitia and the media (Table S6). Some organic acids and lipids were mainly enriched in the media, while lipids were mainly enriched in the plaque, which was consistent with previous findings [30] (Table S7–S8).

Characteristics of cell communication and adjacent areas of calcification in diabetic macroangiopathy

Markers were identified in adjacent and non-adjacent regions, and then functional enrichment and pathway enrichment of marker genes were performed. For specific calcification adjacent regions, chemokine function activation and erk1/2 function activation were induced, in addition, there was activation of phenylalanine pathway (Fig. 3D and E). This was consistent with the perception of inflammation in calcifies and may lead to the production of phenylalanine. The changes in cell composition of this annotation were explored by RCTD, and the main changes in cell composition were reduced SMCs in the calcified area, increased T cells in the calcified area, and increased macrophages in the adjacent area.

Definition of calcified smooth muscle cells and construction of transcription factor regulatory networks that drive smooth muscle cell phenotypic transformation

Vascular calcification, an important pathophysiological change of diabetic vascular disease, was closely related to the terminal events of diabetes. In order to evaluate the contribution of various cell types in the progression of vascular calcification, we conducted a polygenic score for all cells based on the genes associated with calcification events obtained by GWAS (Fig. 4A and B). It was found that SMCs and fibroblasts had higher gene scores (Fig. 4D). Therefore, we conclude that SMCs and fibroblasts were the main cells involved in vascular calcification. Vascular calcification was currently thought to be mainly caused by the phenotypic transformation of SMCs into osteoblastic SMCs as the core process of calcification [31].

Definition of smooth muscle cells subclusters

To further understand the detailed cells subclusters of SMCs, we defined the SMCs subsets and named SMCs based on marker genes (Table S2). Clusters 4, 6 and 8 were contractile SMCs because MYH11 was highly expressed in these clusters. Cluster 6 was CXCL3 + SMC mediating chemotaxis, and Cluster 9 APOE + SMC was foam SMCs. Cluster 3 was IGFBP2 + SMC, which was the synthetic SMCs, and was the intermediate form of phenotypic transformation of SMCs [32] (Fig. 4). Based on the identification of classical calcification genes and polygenic scores of classical osteoblastation-related genes (RUNX2, SPP1, SP7, IBSP and BGLAP), osteoblast-like SMCs were identified as a small part of IGFBP2 + SMC (Fig. 4J). The expression of classical calcification genes was verified on the spatial transcriptome (Fig. 4E–I), and it was found that RUNX2, SP7, IBSP and BGLAP were also highly expressed at the edge of calcified regions in some blood vessels (Fig. 4K–P).

Definition of smooth muscle cells phenotypic transformation trend and root

According to TFvelo, rootcell was identified in 4 clusters, and the transformation of contractile SMCs like foam cells could be seen according to the rate curve. The conversion of synthetic SMCs to CXCL3 + SMC and the conversion of synthetic SMCs to osteoblast-like SMCs and fibroblasts (Fig. 5A). Monocle3 pseudotime analysis based on graph clustering showed that SMCs had a complex gradient relationship between clusters 4, 8 and 3, and had three trends: fibroblasts, APOE + SMC and CXCL3 + SMC. In addition, we used CytoTRACE2 to Identify the differentiation potential of cells potential of the cells, and the results obtained were roughly consistent with the aforementioned cell trans-differentiation. It was concluded that the calcified SMCs as defined above were also highly differentiated cells. Our study provided favorable evidence for the cellular fate of SMCs, which can be transformed into pro-inflammatory chemotactic SMCs, macrophage-like SMC / foamlike SMCs, and fibroblast/chondroblast SMCs in the anterior tibial artery of diabetic patients (Fig. 5B). Moreover, when synthetic SMCs became inflammatory chemotactic SMCs, certain contractile phenotype was restored.

In order to further identify the transcription factors driving the change of smooth muscle cell phenotype and follow the TFvelo, the common transcription factors of marker genes in clusters 3, 14, 6, and 9 were inferred through TRRUST database [24]. MYC and ETS1 were driven in the direction of osteogenes and fibroblasts (Fig. 5B).

TRRUST, as a highly manual annotated database, can avoid false positives of the data, whereas the high confidence brings about the limitation of the exploration of most of the omics data. To identify transcription factors potentially involved in the regulation of smooth muscle cell phenotypes, we evaluated the activity of transcription factors based on DoRothEA [33]. The transcriptional factors from DoRothEA were analyzed in pseudo-time series, and 55 transcriptional factors related to pseudo-time series were selected manually, and the transcription factor network was mapped (Table S9, Fig. 5C and Figures S9–S12).

Based on the characteristic expression genes of each vascular tissue in the above spatial transcriptome, the spatial characteristic genes enriched the metabolic pathways of each blood vessel. By comparing the metabolic enrichment of different blood vessels, it was found that some metabolic pathways were tissue-specific. α-linolenic acid metabolism and linoleic acid metabolism were enriched in the adventitia, folic acid biosynthesis was characteristically expressed in calcification, O-glycan biosynthesis, and primary bile acid biosynthesis was characteristically expressed in plaques. Interestingly, some pathways were co-expressed in plaques and adventitially, such as arachidonic acid metabolism. Biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, biosynthesis of terpenoid main chains (Fig. 6A). Discussion

In summary, we revealed the metabolite and gene characterization of diabetic macroangiopathy through spatial multi-omics technology, and constructed a network map of tissue-metabolic gene-metabolite-metabolic pathway, which provided a solid foundation for subsequent research and exploration of diabetic macroangiopathy. Although there were some NMR-based studies of vascular metabolism [34], this was the first metabolome study of isolated blood vessels, and our study provides a metabolic landscape with higher identificationd accuracy and spatial characteristics than other vascular metabolome studies.

According to HE staining, blood vessels were divided into four regions in the membranous membrane of plaque calcification. In the calcified region, heterocyclic compounds and their metabolites were mainly enriched, which may be caused by local abnormal physical and chemical properties. Meanwhile, the calcified region showed activation of the Folate biosynthesis metabolic pathway. This may provide more substrates for biosynthesis (purines and thymidine), amino acid homeostasis (glycine, serine, and methionine), epigenetic maintenance, and redox defense [35, 36]. Based on spatial transcriptomic data from plaque regions, we identified genes potentially capable of recognizing lesions, among which PLD3, TMSB10, SAT1, and CSTB have not been reported to be associated with atherosclerosis or calcification. We found that nucleus-dense areas tend to appear on the calcified region edge (Fig. 3A, B). Interestingly, compared with normal nuclei, this part of the media showed activation of phenylalanine pathway, chemokine function activation and erk1/2 function activation, and the correlation between these areas remains to be further studied.

Arachidonic acid metabolism was believed to be closely related to platelet activation and thrombosis [37]. We provided evidence of transcriptionalbolism activated in plaques and adventitias. This provides new insights into the pharmacological mechanisms of low-dose aspirin. Biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids were also activated in adventitia, which may lead to the dual regulation of plaque inflammation, plaque stability and vasoconstriction.

In addition, alpha-Linolenic acid metabolism, the effect of Linoleic acid metabolism on blood vessels in addition to circulation, may also come from the adventitia. This was possible because the PLA2G2A gene was characteristically expressed in the adventitia and was also characteristically expressed in fibroblasts. PLA2G2A has both tissue specificity and cell specificity. In addition, the spatial metabolome also supports the related functions of PLA2G2A, which hydrolyze the ester bonds of fatty acyl clusters attached to the phospholipid sn-2 position (phospholipase A2 activity), preferentially over phosphatidyl ethanolamine and phosphatidylglycerol rather than phosphatidylcholine [38, 39], thus corresponding to the enrichment of phosphatidylcholine in the vascular adventitia. The effect of these features-enriched phosphatidylcholine on blood vessels remains to be further studied. Folic acid was considered to be a factor to reduce cardiovascular risk, and folic acid metabolism with enriched features in calcified regions has not been studied. At the same time, O-glycan biosynthesis and Primary bile acid biosynthesis in plaques have not been studied.

Combined with the public datasets, we found that the major metabolic reprogramming genes were CYP27A1 and HNMT. Selective deletion of cyp27a1 in macrophages resulted in a reduction in the severity of atherosclerotic lesions, and Cyp27a1/ApoE double-knockout mice fed a Western diet did not develop atherosclerosis [40, 41]. Diabetes induced CYP27A1 may play a role in plaque progression. Hnmt inactivates histamine through n-methylation and was known to play an important role in degrading histamine and regulating airway response to histamine [42]. Histamine was involved in the development of atherosclerosis in apoE-KO mice by regulating the expression of genes that regulate inflammation regulators, and this effect appears to be independent of serum cholesterol levels [43]. In addition to acute inflammatory responses, histamine was also involved in chronic inflammation, such as hyperlipidemia-induced atherosclerosis, and may be a new therapeutic target for the treatment of atherosclerosis [44]. The relationship between Hnmt and vascular disease remains to be further studied.

Our study also serves as a strong evidence to prove the trend of smooth muscle cell transdifferentiation, and may well explain why smooth muscle cell transdifferentiation was so controversial. Based on cell trajectories and existing studies, we believe that contractile smooth muscle cells and synthetic smooth muscle cells transform into contractile smooth muscle cells with inflammatory chemotaxis, but some synthetic smooth muscle cells will transform into contractile smooth muscle cells. At the same time, synthetic smooth muscle also diverges to the direction of chondrogenesis and fibrogenesis. There was a trend of macrophage conversion in foam smooth muscle cells/macrophage smooth muscle cells, but there was no direct curve from contractile smooth muscle cells to APOE + SMC in the cell rate graph. According to single-cell transcriptional sequencing combined with lineage tracing, if smooth muscle transdifferentiates into other cell types, these cells should cluster together with cells of non-smooth muscle origin to appear fully differentiated [45]. Therefore, there were two intermediate states (APOE + SMC and IGFBP2 + SMC) during the transformation of synthetic smooth muscle into fibroblasts and macrophages. When synthetic smooth muscle cells transform into contractile smooth muscle cells, some cells will release inflammatory chemokines to induce chronic inflammation.

We identified transcription factors that potentially drive phenotypic changes in SMCs based on two approaches. For most of these transcription factors, no one has reported the specific relationship between them and smooth muscle cell phenotypic transformation, such as KDM5B, DDIT3, etc. Studies have shown that Ets-1 overexpression reduces the transcriptional activity of SMC-labeled promoters in SMCs and may promote phenotypic transformation of SMCs [46]. IRF3 is a pro-inflammatory transcription factor activated by cytoplasmic DNA and is believed to be a key driver of the transition of aortic SMCs from contractile phenotype to inflammatory phenotype [47]. A study based on disease-associated methylation suggests that NR2F2 may be associated with atherosclerosis and may help reduce or reverse changes in the contraction characteristics of SMCs [48]. AHR promotes maintenance of the integrity of the lesion cap and reduces disease-associated SMC-to-chondrocyte transformation in atherosclerotic tissue [49]. Our study suggests that AHR may promote the conversion of SMCs into foam cells, which is consistent with previous studies. Another study involving ATF4 focused on the effect of ATF4 on the extracellular matrix during acute vascular events and suggested that ATF4 plays an important role in SMC migration and negative vascular remodeling after injury [50]. This study can also prove that ATF4 can regulate the smooth muscle cell phenotype and regulate the extracellular matrix through the transformation of SMCs into fibroblasts. Some scholars have proposed the effect of neuroendocrine-STAT5-BCL6 hypothesis on the smooth muscle of pulmonary artery vessels [51, 52]. Furthermore, YY1, TWIST1, HIF1A, SOX9, SIRT1 and other relevant studies have reported their association with vascular smooth muscle phenotype changes[53–64].

We used TRRUST and DoRothEA to identify transcription factors potentially driving smooth muscle phenotypic transformation, respectively. 16 transcription factors in the TRRUST library included NCOR1, IRF1, and NFIL3, three of which have not yet been reported to be associated with phenotypic transformation of smooth muscle cells. 56 transcription factors were identified in the relatively loose DoRothEA library. transcription factors were ARID3A, BACH2, BHLHE40, ELF1, ELK4, IRF1, KLF9, NR2F2, NR3C1, ZBTB11, ZBTB33, ZEB2, ZNF217, ERG, NR1H2, ATF3, EBF1, CREM, NFKB2, KDM5B, NFE2, and NFKB2. KDM5B, NFE2L1, NR2F1, TFDP1, ZKSCAN1, EPAS1, FOSL1, MAFF and MXI1 totalling 29 have not yet been reported to be associated with smooth muscle cell phenotypic transformation. NCOR1 controls positive and negative thymocyte selection during T cell development [65], but its regulation of vascular smooth muscle is unclear. Some studies have reported that IRF1 promotes the development of atherosclerotic lesions [66], and IRF1 can mediate endothelial cell dysfunction [67] as well as regulate myeloid differentiation [68] through the regulation of Retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I), and the relationship between IRF1 and the phenotypic transformation of smooth muscle has not yet been explored by the studies. Several other studies have reported a strong association between transcription factors such as NFIL3, BHLHE40, ARID3A, ZEB2 and cell differentiation [69–72], whereas their regulation of phenotypic transformation in response to vascular smooth muscle cells remains to be further explored. Our study reveals the transformational driving network behind smooth muscle cell phenotypic transformation. Most of these transcription factors still need to be targeted smooth muscle cell lineage tracing.

It was noteworthy that the expression of IGFBP2 was not emphasized in previously identified synthesized SMCs based on single cell sequencing of aorta and carotid artery, suggesting that IGFBP2 may be influenced by diabetic factors and lead to overexpression in vascular synthesized smooth muscle of diabetic patients. In addition to the high expression of IGFBP2, extracellular matrix related MGP, FN1, VCAN, COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1, LUM, LTBP2, AEBP1, ELN, BGN, ANTXR1 and SULF1 were also highly expressed, suggesting that IGFBP2 + SMC was highly involved in the regulation of extracellular matrix. Of these, high expression of POSTN was also closely related to calcification formation [73]. FN1 was involved in the regulation of type I collagen deposition by osteoblasts, and participates in osteoblast compaction through the matrix assembly process mediated by fibulin fibroblast, which was crucial for osteoblast mineralization [74]. S100A4 was a calcium-binding protein that plays a role in various cellular processes. Including movement, angiogenesis, cell differentiation, apoptosis and autophagy and other calcification related links [75–77], In addition, it was noteworthy that IGFBP2 + smc also highly expresses DKK3 and SULF1, DKK3 locally inhibits the regulation of Wnt [78, 79], and SULF1 extracellular sulfatase regulates Wnt signaling through the desulfurization of heparan sulfate proteoglycan on cell surface [80]. Therefore, the changes of WNT pathway and cell differentiation in IGFBP2 + smc were also worthy of further exploration. In CXCL3 + smc, the contractile phenotype of SMCs remained. A previous study of single-cell sequencing at high hydrostatic pressure also suggested the presence of this subgroup, which may be caused by direct hydrostatic pressure [81].

This study has potential limitations. Firstly, due to the difficulty in obtaining samples and the quality of the samples to meet the sequencing quality control requirements, the sample size of the study was small and we could not conduct the most appropriate bulk-RNAseq for lower limb vascular from the diabetic amputation group and the automobile accident amputation group to external validate of the metabolic reprogramming. All we explored more at the level of cells and spots in single-cell and spatial genomics to reduce the bias introduced by the samples, and the data can be seen to be consistent based on the clustering results. To circumvent the problem of not being able to match the most appropriate bulk-RNAseq, we explored metabolic reprogramming in diabetes after integrating external datasets and further validated the expression on paraffin sections of lower limb vessels in diabetic amputation and car accident amputation groups, thus supporting our conclusions. In addition, the driving cybernetics of smooth muscle cell phenotypic transformation remains to be further validated. Nearly half of our proposed transcription factors involved in smooth muscle phenotypic transformation have been validated to be associated with smooth muscle cell phenotypic transformation, and the trend of transcription factor rate speculation is consistent with the trend of the proposed temporal sequence speculation, which can illustrate the reliability of our putative transcription factors from two perspectives. Finally, due to the sample requirements of the single-cell sequencing data itself, even though we selected calcified and noncalcified vessels based on CTA at the time of sampling, we could not prove that vessels coded as noncalcified did not undergo microcalcifications that could not be recognized by the CTA itself, or that there was a transformation of the vessel in the direction of osteogenesis as well as resorption. Functional enrichment of differentially expressed genes on single-cell data from both subgroups also showed predominantly extracellular matrix changes rather than calcification and osteogenesis. Therefore, we based our exploration of calcification on classical calcification genes as well as spatial histology with clear correspondence on HE sections.

Conclusion

Taken together, this study reveals the characteristics of vascular calcification, the phenotypic transformation trend of SMCs, and the transcriptional driving network of SMCs phenotypic transformation by constructing a multiomics map of diabetic macrovascular disease. In the perspective of combining the transcriptome and metabolome, the study demonstrates the activated metabolic pathways in diabetic blood vessels and the key genes involved in diabetic metabolic reprogramming. The value of this study lies in providing more refined guidance for metabolism-related drug applications, and providing a basis for further research on smooth muscle cell phenotypic transformation and metabolic reprogramming.
