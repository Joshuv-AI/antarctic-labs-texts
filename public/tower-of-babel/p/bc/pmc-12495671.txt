Risk factors and predictive modeling of intraoperative hypothermia in laparoscopic surgery patients
Authors: Yan Cheng, Huasong Sheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInadvertent intra-operative hypothermia (< 36 °C) is frequent during laparoscopic surgery and worsens postoperative outcomes. Reliable risk-prediction tools for this setting are still lacking.MethodsWe retrospectively analysed 207 adults who underwent laparoscopic procedures at a single hospital (June 2021 – June 2024). Core temperature was recorded nasopharyngeally every 15 min. Hypothermia was defined as any intra-operative value < 36 °C. Univariable tests and multivariable logistic regression identified independent predictors; coefficients were converted into a weighted risk score. Model discrimination (AUC) and calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow test, calibration plot) were assessed with 1 000-bootstrap internal validation.ResultsHypothermia occurred in 110 patients (53%). Five variables remained independently associated with risk: body-mass index ≤ 23 kg m⁻², baseline core temperature ≤ 36.1 °C, operating-room temperature ≤ 22 °C, surgical duration ≥ 120 min, and pre-operative systolic blood pressure > 130 mmHg (all P < 0.05). The resulting five-item score (0–12 points) showed good discrimination (optimism-corrected AUC 0.80, 95% CI 0.74–0.87) and adequate calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow P = 0.10; Brier = 0.18). A cut-off ≥ 3 points identified high-risk patients with 68% sensitivity and 90% specificity.ConclusionsLow BMI, low baseline temperature, cool theatre environment, prolonged surgery and hypertension jointly heighten hypothermia risk during laparoscopy. The simple five-item score enables rapid pre-operative stratification and may guide targeted warming strategies. External multicentre validation is warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03186-z.Keywords: Laparoscopic surgery, Intraoperative hypothermia, Risk factors, Logistic regression, Prediction model, Thermoregulation

Introduction

Minimally invasive surgical techniques have advanced significantly, with laparoscopic procedures now commonplace in many surgical disciplines [1]. While laparoscopy generally enhances operative safety and recovery, it carries inherent risks such as intraoperative hypothermia due to anesthesia-induced thermoregulatory impairment and insufflation of cold CO₂ gas into the abdomen. Intraoperative hypothermia (commonly defined as core temperature < 36.0 °C) can lead to serious complications. Clinical evidence shows that even mild perioperative hypothermia elevates the risk of cardiovascular events, increases the incidence of surgical site infections, and prolongs postoperative recovery time. For example, inadvertent hypothermia has been associated with ~ 20% higher odds of cardiac complications and a 2–3 fold increase in wound infection rates, along with an average prolongation of post-anesthesia care unit stay by 40–60 min [2, 3]. Maintaining normothermia (36.0–37.5 °C) is therefore a critical component of patient management during surgery.

Human thermoregulation maintains metabolic homeostasis through complex neurovascular mechanisms that balance heat production and loss [4]. General anesthesia impairs these thermoregulatory defenses by redistributing heat from the core to the periphery and reducing metabolic heat generation [5]. Consequently, unwarmed surgical patients can experience a core temperature drop of 1–2 °C within the first hour of anesthesia. Laparoscopic surgery poses additional challenges for temperature homeostasis; the insufflation of CO₂ for pneumoperitoneum can induce heat loss and further contribute to core temperature reductions [6]. Despite awareness of these issues, current literature lacks predictive tools specific to intraoperative hypothermia in laparoscopic surgery populations [7]. Most prior studies have focused on identifying individual risk factors or evaluating warming interventions, but few have provided clinicians with a practical risk scoring model.

Our study addresses this gap by identifying key risk factors for intraoperative hypothermia in a general laparoscopic surgery cohort and developing a simple predictive model. Several known risk factors (e.g., low BMI, low preoperative temperature, cold ambient operating room) have been reported in previous research [8]. We also explore the role of hypertension as a potential novel risk factor in this context. By comparing our findings with recent studies and incorporating contemporary data, we aim to contextualize the results and enhance generalizability. Compared to existing models developed for specific procedures such as colorectal or gynecologic laparoscopy [7–10],our model is applicable across general laparoscopic surgeries. It incorporates hypertension as a novel predictor and uses a five-variable additive score for simple bedside use, without the need for laboratory or imaging inputs.Ultimately, the goal is to enable early identification of high-risk patients so that targeted preventive measures (such as active warming protocols) can be implemented to improve patient outcomes. This manuscript is organized according to the TRIPOD guidelines for reporting predictive modeling studies [11].

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective observational study included 207 surgical patients (112 men and 95 women) aged 60 to 91 years who underwent elective laparoscopic procedures at our hospital between June 29, 2021 and June 29, 2024. All cases were performed under general anesthesia. We obtained ethics approval from the hospital’s Ethics Committee (Approval No. 2018LLW035) and written informed consent from all participants (or their legal surrogates). Patients were divided into two cohorts based on the occurrence of intraoperative hypothermia (yes/no).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Adult patients (age ≥ 18 years) scheduled for laparoscopic surgery were eligible. We required a documented normal preoperative core temperature (36.5–37.5 °C) prior to anesthesia. Patients were excluded if they had any preoperative fever or hypothermia (body temperature < 36.5 °C or > 37.5 °C before induction), severe systemic illness (e.g., uncontrolled cardiovascular disease or renal insufficiency), or conditions interfering with temperature measurement (e.g., significant nasopharyngeal pathology precluding probe placement). We also excluded patients if critical intraoperative data were missing from the records. Note: Although the inclusion criteria allowed ages up to 100, the actual enrolled patients ranged from 60 to 91 years old (reflecting the older demographic of our surgical population during the study period).

Intraoperative temperature monitoring

Intraoperative core temperature was measured using calibrated nasopharyngeal temperature probes (Mindray® PN TC‑T1). Probes were inserted 10–12 cm into the nasopharynx and connected to an anaesthesia monitoring system (Mindray WATO EX‑65). Temperature was automatically recorded every 5 min throughout surgery. Probe calibration was performed monthly using a certified laboratory thermometer, and weekly functional tests were conducted to ensure measurement consistency.

Core body temperature was continuously monitored in all patients from anesthesia induction until the end of surgery. At induction of anesthesia (immediately after endotracheal intubation), a nasopharyngeal temperature probe was inserted to measure core temperature; this initial reading under anesthesia was recorded as the baseline intraoperative temperature. Temperatures were then recorded at specific time points: at skin incision (start of surgery), at 15-minute intervals throughout the operation, and at the end of surgery. The nasopharyngeal probes were calibrated according to manufacturer specifications, and anesthesia machines’ temperature monitoring systems were routinely checked for accuracy. Intraoperative hypothermia was defined as any occurrence of core temperature < 36.0 °C during the surgery. This definition (any single reading below 36.0 °C) was chosen to ensure high sensitivity in detecting hypothermic events. If a patient’s temperature dropped below 36.0 °C at any recorded time point, they were classified in the hypothermia group.Nasopharyngeal core temperature was recorded every 5 min from anesthesia induction until the end of surgery using a standardized nasopharyngeal probe, inserted 10–20 cm past the nares to ensure core temperature accuracy. Baseline temperature was defined as the first measurement following induction.

Data collection

We collected comprehensive perioperative data from the electronic medical records. Baseline patient characteristics included age, sex, height, weight, and body mass index (BMI). Preexisting medical conditions (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, etc.) and prior surgical history were noted. “Basal” body temperature in our analysis refers to the patient’s core temperature at anesthesia induction (baseline intraoperative temperature), since all patients had normal temperatures immediately before surgery by inclusion criteria. Intraoperative variables recorded were: ambient operating room (OR) temperature (in °C, as per OR thermostat setting during the case), type of anesthesia (general vs. general + regional combined), duration of anesthesia, duration of surgery, total volume of intravenous fluids infused, and volume of irrigation fluid used (if applicable). We also recorded any active warming measures applied (e.g., forced-air warming blankets), although in our institution during the study period, active warming protocols were not standardized and were applied at the anesthesiologist’s discretion.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (version 4.2.3). Continuous variables were first tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed variables, we present means with standard deviations (mean ± SD) and compared groups using the independent-samples Student’s t-test. For non-normal data, we present medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) and used the Mann–Whitney U test (Wilcoxon rank-sum). Categorical variables are summarized as counts and percentages, and comparisons between groups were made with the chi-square (χ²) test (or Fisher’s exact test if an expected cell count was < 5). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

Variables that showed a significant difference between the hypothermia and normothermia groups in univariate analysis were entered into a multivariate logistic regression model to identify independent risk factors for intraoperative hypothermia. We used a backward stepwise selection approach based on likelihood ratios. Collinearity among predictors was checked (variance inflation factor < 5 as acceptable). The logistic regression results are reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and associated P values. We assessed model fit using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (with P > 0.05 indicating good fit). Model discrimination was evaluated via the area under the ROC curve (AUC). We constructed an ROC curve for the predicted probability of hypothermia and calculated AUC with 95% CI.

To create a practical risk score from the logistic model, we converted each independent predictor’s regression coefficient (β) to an integer score. The conversion was done by taking each β coefficient relative to the smallest absolute β, then multiplying by a constant factor (≈ 2) and rounding to the nearest whole number. Each risk factor was assigned a point value (e.g., a larger β yields more points). These points were summed to give each patient a total risk score. We evaluated the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) of various score thresholds for predicting hypothermia. We chose an optimal cutoff score based on maximizing Youden’s index (sensitivity + specificity – 1) and considering clinical consequences of false negatives vs. false positives. Internal validation of the model was performed using a bootstrap resampling method (1000 resamples) to estimate optimism in the AUC. We also calculated the Brier score for overall model accuracy in probability predictions. All analysis steps followed recommended practices for clinical prediction model development (TRIPOD guidelines).

To assess the risk of model overfitting and adequacy of sample size, we conducted a post-hoc events-per-variable (EPV) calculation and power analysis. A total of 110 patients experienced intraoperative hypothermia (“events”) and five variables were retained in the final logistic regression model, yielding an EPV of 22. This exceeds the minimum EPV threshold of 20 recommended for prediction models with modest anticipated R² values [12]. We assumed an R² = 0.20 for the final model and performed post-hoc power calculations using G*Power (v3.1), which showed > 85% power to detect an odds ratio ≥ 2.0 at α = 0.05. To further assess model robustness, we performed bootstrap shrinkage with 1,000 resamples and report an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.789.

In the final analytic dataset, the proportion of missing values for any individual variable was < 2%. Because of this low level of missingness, we used complete-case analysis. No imputation was required. We observed no systematic pattern of missingness, and key demographic and clinical characteristics were balanced between patients with complete vs. incomplete records. The overall sample size after complete-case restriction remained sufficient for all planned analyses.

Results

Clinical characteristics of patients undergoing laparoscopic surgery

A total of 207 patients were analyzed, of whom 110 (53.14%) developed intraoperative hypothermia and 97 (46.86%) maintained normothermia. Table 1 summarizes the baseline clinical characteristics and intraoperative variables in the two groups. The hypothermia and non-hypothermia groups were similar in terms of age (mean 58.1 vs. 57.6 years, P = 0.720) and sex distribution (males 53.6% vs. 53.6%, P = 0.997). The incidence of diabetes was also similar between groups (26.4% vs. 28.9%, P = 0.688). However, several factors differed significantly. Patients who became hypothermic had a lower mean preoperative core temperature (36.03 ± 1.34 °C) compared to those who remained normothermic (36.95 ± 1.02 °C, P < 0.001). Hypothermic patients also had a lower average BMI (21.26 ± 2.35 kg/m²) than normothermic patients (24.55 ± 3.05 kg/m², P < 0.001). In addition, operations in the hypothermia group were significantly longer on average (154.13 ± 20.38 min) than in the normothermia group (112.89 ± 34.26 min, P < 0.001). The ambient operating room temperature during cases was slightly lower for the hypothermia group (mean 21.7 ± 3.13 °C) vs. normothermia group (22.8 ± 2.03 °C, P = 0.003). A higher proportion of hypothermic patients had a history of hypertension (53.6% vs. 40.2%), although this difference did not quite reach conventional significance in univariate analysis (P = 0.054). There was no statistically significant difference in incidence of ASA physical status classification, surgical category (gastrointestinal vs. hepatobiliary vs. etc.), type of anesthesia (general vs. general + regional), duration of anesthesia, intraoperative fluid infusion volume, or irrigation volume between the groups (P > 0.05 for all). We note that significance testing for baseline characteristics should be interpreted cautiously, as our primary aim was to identify potential predictors rather than to draw causal conclusions from these unadjusted comparisons.

Multivariate predictors of intraoperative hypothermia and model development

The five variables with significant univariate associations (baseline core temperature, BMI, OR temperature, surgery duration, and hypertension) were entered into a multivariate logistic regression. All five factors remained independently associated with hypothermia risk after adjustment (Table 2). Specifically, patients with lower BMI (≤ 23 kg/m²) exhibited an adjusted OR of ~ 2.66 for hypothermia (95% CI 1.73–4.75, P = 0.024), and those with a baseline core temperature ≤ 36.1 °C had the highest relative risk (OR ~ 3.49, 95% CI 1.80–6.85, P = 0.016). Environmental and procedural factors also contributed: an operating room ambient temperature ≤ 22 °C was associated with OR ~ 2.25 (95% CI 1.20–4.20, P = 0.020), and prolonged surgery duration ≥ 120 min had OR ~ 2.87 (95% CI 1.68–5.35, P = 0.039). Notably, a history of hypertension (systolic BP > 130 mmHg) emerged as a significant predictor as well (OR ~ 2.25, 95% CI 1.10–4.55, P = 0.037). Hypertension has not been widely reported in prior studies of intraoperative hypothermia, making this an interesting finding of our study. We speculate on possible mechanisms in the Discussion.

Using the above logistic model, we constructed a risk scoring system (Table 3). Each risk factor was assigned points proportional to its regression coefficient magnitude. The smallest coefficient was for OR temperature (β ≈ 0.81) which we set as 2 points; accordingly, BMI ≤ 23 kg/m² (β ≈ 0.97) was also assigned 2 points, baseline temp ≤ 36.1 °C (β ≈ 1.25) 3 points, surgery ≥ 120 min (β ≈ 1.06) 2 points, and hypertension (β ≈ 0.81) 2 points. A patient’s total risk score is the sum of points for all risk factors present (range 0 to 11 points). Higher scores indicate higher predicted risk of hypothermia.

Model performance and validation

We evaluated how well the risk score predicted hypothermia using ROC curve analysis (Fig. 1). The AUC was 0.806 (95% CI 0.746–0.869), indicating good discriminative ability. We examined various cutoff values for the total score to classify patients as high-risk for hypothermia (Table 4). For example, a threshold of ≥ 4 points yields high sensitivity (86.6%) but somewhat lower specificity (92.3%), whereas a threshold of ≥ 5 points yields very high specificity but at the cost of sensitivity. To balance sensitivity and specificity, we considered the range of 3–4 points. Using the Youden’s index criterion on our dataset, the optimal cutoff was around 3.5 points (which is not an integer and thus not directly usable). We chose 3 points as a clinically convenient cutoff to categorize high-risk patients. At ≥ 3 points, the model achieved sensitivity ~ 79.3% and specificity ~ 95.7% in our sample (Youden’s index = 0.75). However, this was based on the retrospective data; using the more pragmatic cutoff of ≥ 3, our observed sensitivity was slightly lower (68.2%) with specificity 89.6%. We decided on 3 points as the threshold to err on the side of including more patients in the “at-risk” group (favoring sensitivity) so that preventive measures could be applied more broadly, accepting a moderate number of false positives. In practice, patients scoring 3 or higher should be considered at elevated risk for hypothermia.

Although PPV and NPV depend on cohort prevalence, we calculated and reported them for the cutoff score of ≥ 3, which was determined to be clinically optimal. These values, along with likelihood ratios, are provided as a footnote to Table 4.

We also assessed model calibration. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test result was χ² (8) = 12.8, P = 0.098, suggesting no significant lack of fit (i.e., predicted probabilities aligned well with observed outcomes in deciles of risk). We plotted a calibration curve (Fig. 2) which indicated that the model’s predicted risk was close to the actual observed risk across most of the range, with slight overestimation at higher predicted risk levels. The Brier score for the model (mean squared error of predictions) was 0.138, which is reasonably low and comparable to other clinical prediction models of this type. Internal validation via bootstrap resampling yielded an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.789, indicating only a slight overfitting in the original model. The distribution of total risk scores across the cohort is shown in Fig. 3. Most patients scored between 3 and 6 points, with fewer at the extremes. This supports the clinical utility of a cutoff of ≥ 3. These results support that the model has acceptable accuracy for predicting intraoperative hypothermia in our setting. We emphasize, however, that external validation is needed before generalizing the score to other hospitals or patient populations.

Discussion

In this retrospective study, we identified five independent predictors of intraoperative hypothermia in patients undergoing laparoscopic surgery: low body mass index (BMI ≤ 23 kg/m²), low baseline core temperature (≤ 36.1 °C), operating room (OR) ambient temperature ≤ 22 °C, surgical duration ≥ 120 min, and hypertension (systolic blood pressure > 130 mmHg). Based on these variables, we developed a five-item risk score that demonstrated good predictive accuracy (AUC = 0.806, Fig. 1) and acceptable calibration (Fig. 2). Most patients scored between 3 and 6 points (Fig. 3), suggesting that this tool may effectively stratify risk and guide preventive strategies.

Our observed hypothermia incidence of 53.1% is higher than previously reported rates (30–40%) in similar surgical populations. For comparison, Cao et al. reported a 30.8% incidence in gynecologic laparoscopic tumor resections [9], while Penjore et al. observed 34.9% in a broader laparoscopic cohort [10]. These differences may be attributable to patient population, perioperative warming protocols, and the definition of hypothermia used. Our broader inclusion and definition (any core temperature < 36 °C) likely contributed to the higher observed rate.

Possible explanations include our cohort’s older age distribution, lack of standardized warming protocols, and inclusion of any intraoperative temperature < 36 °C as hypothermia, which increases sensitivity. Previous studies that defined hypothermia based on end-of-procedure temperature or sustained temperature drops may report lower incidence [13].

Our findings reaffirm several well-established risk factors. Low BMI increases surface-area-to-mass ratio and reduces endogenous insulation, predisposing patients to faster heat loss under anesthesia [7, 8]. Similarly, a lower core temperature at induction offers less thermal buffer before hypothermic thresholds are crossed, highlighting the value of prewarming even normothermic patients with initial temperatures near 36.0 °C. Ambient OR temperature has long been recognized as a modifiable environmental risk factor. While comfort and infection control must be balanced, maintaining OR temperature above 22–23 °C is recommended in high-risk cases [5].

Prolonged surgery increases cumulative heat loss unless effective warming measures are maintained. In our cohort, surgeries ≥ 2 h were associated with nearly threefold increased risk of hypothermia, even though total anesthesia time showed smaller differences. This underscores the importance of continuous intraoperative thermal management throughout long procedures [14, 15].

A notable and novel finding was the identification of hypertension as a significant predictor of hypothermia. While not widely reported in prior hypothermia research, one plausible explanation is impaired vasoconstrictive response in patients with long-standing hypertension, potentially due to altered autonomic tone or vascular stiffness. Experimental data suggest that such patients may experience enhanced heat redistribution or less effective thermoregulation [16]. It is also possible that hypertensive patients receive different anesthetic or vasoactive drug regimens that influence thermal stability. Given the novelty of this association, further mechanistic and prospective validation is warranted.

Hypertension emerged as an independent risk factor for intraoperative hypothermia in our model. One possible explanation involves impaired thermoregulatory vasoconstriction. Chronic hypertension has been associated with altered baroreflex sensitivity and increased sympathetic tone, which may blunt peripheral vasoconstriction during cold stress [16]. Additionally, operating room conditions such as low ambient temperature and anaesthetic-induced vasodilation may exacerbate this heat-loss tendency [17]. These mechanisms are consistent with findings in both animal models and clinical observations [16, 17], although further physiological studies are warranted to confirm these effects in surgical patients.

When compared to other predictive models for intraoperative hypothermia [7–10, 18], our scoring tool offers several strengths. First, it is derived from routinely collected clinical data, enhancing feasibility. Second, it requires no specialized laboratory or imaging variables. Third, it offers a simple additive scoring approach, enabling real-time preoperative risk stratification. For example, a patient with low BMI, low baseline temperature, and hypertension would already accumulate 7 points, triggering early intervention. Other studies have proposed complex nomograms or machine learning models [18], which may provide superior accuracy but pose barriers to bedside application.

A suggested warming framework based on this score includes pre-induction forced-air warming, warmed intravenous fluids, and maintaining OR temperature above 23 °C. Prior randomized trials have demonstrated the effectiveness of prewarming and fluid warming in reducing perioperative hypothermia [11, 18].

From a clinical standpoint, our model enables targeted warming interventions for high-risk patients, such as pre-induction forced-air warming, warmed intravenous fluids, and maintaining higher ambient OR temperature during initial anesthesia. While our study did not directly assess the impact of these interventions, previous research supports their efficacy in reducing hypothermia-related complications [11, 19]. Additionally, recent meta-analyses suggest that thermal insulation during the post-anesthesia recovery period further enhances temperature stability and patient comfort [17].Integrating risk scores into perioperative protocols may improve patient safety, especially among older adults or in facilities without universal warming practices.

There are several limitations to consider. Our single-center design limits generalizability, and the modest sample size may reduce the precision of some estimates. We did not include certain potentially relevant variables, such as thyroid status, intraoperative medication types, or patient anxiety level. Additionally, our model has only been internally validated via bootstrap resampling; external validation in larger, multi-institutional cohorts is essential before broad implementation. We also acknowledge that our cutoff of 3 points, while clinically practical, was selected based on internal Youden index optimization and may require refinement in future studies.

Conclusion

We developed a simple scoring model to predict intraoperative hypothermia in laparoscopic surgery based on five factors: low BMI, low baseline core temperature, cold operating room environment, prolonged surgery, and hypertension. The score demonstrated acceptable predictive accuracy and internal calibration. Patients with a score ≥ 3 may warrant preventive warming strategies.

Intraoperative hypothermia remains a common and multifactorial complication. Early identification of high-risk patients allows for targeted interventions, potentially improving safety and recovery outcomes. Our findings support the integration of risk-based warming protocols into perioperative care.

However, this model was derived from a single-center retrospective cohort and requires external validation. We have initiated collaboration with other centers to prospectively test its generalizability. Challenges such as standardizing temperature monitoring and resource availability must be addressed before broader adoption.
