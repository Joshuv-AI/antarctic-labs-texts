A costing analysis of an intermittent biolarviciding intervention with limited epidemiological effect in Tanga Region, Tanzania
Authors: Sophie Diarra, Denis Kailembo, Yahya Derua, Tegemeo Gavana, Best Yoram, Stella Kajange, Jubilate Bernard, Charles Dismas, Samwel Lazaro, Elizabeth Kasagama, Noela Kisoka, Prosper Chaki, Christian Lengeler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria vector control strategies in sub-Saharan Africa have largely relied on the use of Insecticide Treated-mosquito Nets and Indoor Residual Spraying. However, after substantial reductions in malaria burden associated with the scale-up of these interventions, little progress has been achieved over the last decade. With the increase in insecticide resistance and outdoor biting of mosquitoes, larviciding offers the possibility to be an additional malaria control tool. This study is part of a comprehensive evaluation and aimed to assess prospectively the financial cost of a large-scale biolarviciding intervention in North-Eastern Tanzania.MethodsBiolarviciding was carried out from June 2022 to April 2024 in three councils in Tanga Region, Tanzania: Handeni District Council (DC), Tanga City Council (CC) and Lushoto DC. The intervention area covered 10,396 km2, with a population of 1,059,384 living in 70 wards. Implementation followed a community-based approach, whereby Community-Owned Resource Persons (CORPs) identified breeding habitats and applied biolarvicides for three rounds per year, based on the prevailing rainfall patterns, whereby each round comprised of 8 consecutive weeks of application. Costing data were obtained from budgets, receipts of actual expenditure and discussions with finance administrators of the project. A micro-costing approach was used to calculate the total cost of the intervention, cost per person protected per year (cost pppy), and cost of projected scale-up.ResultsThe total cost of this intermittent biolarviciding intervention in the three councils over the two years was 3,354,672,133 TZS (1,397,198 USD), with an average cost pppy of 1,583 TZS (0.66 USD). One-way sensitivity analysis showed that the cost of the procurement of biolarvicide had the greatest impact on the total cost. Continuous application of biolarviciding for six rounds per year would result in a cost pppy of 2,716 TZS (1.13 USD). Scale-up to all councils in the country would cost between 97,689,865,621 TZS (40,687,158 USD) and 167,582,345,528 TZS (69,796,895 USD) for three and six annual spray rounds, respectively.ConclusionsWith the intermittent mode of implementation, yearly per capita costs of biolarviciding with Bacillus thuringiensis var israelensis and Bacillus sphaericus are likely to be financially feasible for scaling of the intervention in Tanzania. However, in separate publications the entomological and epidemiological impact of the intervention were found to vary according to settings, and hence the case for upscaling will crucially depend on determining impact in a given setting. Under this condition, biolarviciding could provide an additional complementary vector control tool for further reductions in malaria transmission and addressing the rise in outdoor malaria transmission.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-025-05553-w.Keywords: Malaria, Bacillus thuringiensis var israelensis, Bacillus sphaericus, Vector control, Cost analysis, Larviciding, Tanzania

Background

Malaria remains a large contributor to morbidity and child mortality in sub-Saharan Africa, despite global efforts to prevent and treat it. Total malaria cases and incidence have increased since 2020, with 94% of cases occurring in sub-Saharan Africa [1]. Tanzania is classified as a high burden malaria country, accounting for about 8.5 million (3.3%) of global cases in 2023 [1]. Globally, malaria vector control efforts have included mainly Insecticide-Treated mosquito Nets (ITNs) and Indoor Residual Spraying (IRS), in line with World Health Organization (WHO) guidance. Whilst effective [2, 3], malaria burden has remained relatively stable since 2015 suggesting new strategies are required [1]. The WHO has noted stagnation in malaria cases and deaths both globally and in the African region within this time-frame [1]. Moreover, over the past five years, many countries in the African region including Cameroon, Democratic Republic of the Congo, Ethiopia, Madagascar, Mali, Nigeria, Tanzania, and Uganda have reported increases in malaria cases [1]. Additionally, there are challenges with ongoing core malaria vector control interventions, including insecticide resistance and outdoor mosquito biting [4–6].

Larval source management including larviciding, has high potential to be included as a key strategy in the vector control toolbox, particularly for addressing malaria transmission linked to outdoor biting mosquitoes [7]. Specifically, biolarvicides based on mosquito-specific toxins that are produced by Bacillus sphaericus (Bs) and Bacillus thuringiensis var israelensis (Bti) are used widely and offer a promising tool due to their efficacy and high safety profile [7, 8]. However, whilst larviciding is a tool that has been used extensively for mosquito control both historically and to-date, the WHO currently recommends its use only as a supplementary intervention in settings in which mosquito larval habitats are few, fixed and findable [7]. This recommendation is due firstly to the limited evidence of epidemiological impact, and secondly to the lack of guidance in which settings it might be effective [7, 9, 10]. As a result, larviciding is not currently funded by major malaria donors such as the Global Fund to fight AIDS, Tuberculosis and Malaria (GFATM) and the United States President's Malaria Initiative (PMI), thus greatly restricting its uptake and use. In addition to the need for more evidence on epidemiological impact of larviciding, there is a clear need for more evidence on financial and operational feasibility through different modalities of larvicide application.

To date, several studies have investigated the cost of larviciding when used as a malaria control intervention [11–15]. However, these studies are difficult to compare as they vary in scale, type of larvicide used, human population density relative to larval habitats, larvicide targeting strategy (temporal or spatial) and their costing method. Costing data from previous studies have been adjusted for inflation to 2024 USD costs using annual inflation rates from World Bank data to facilitate comparison with the current study. Additional file 1 provides the original and adjusted 2024 USD prices for the following studies [16]. In Tanzania, costing studies have only been carried out on small-scale larviciding implementation projects [11–13]. For example, in a two-year community-based larviciding study in Mvomero District covering an estimated population of 37,083 people, and using an ingredients approach to costing, the financial cost per person protected per year (cost pppy) was 1.52 USD in 2024 prices [11]. In a costing analysis comparing heterogeneous areas including the metropolitan area of Dar es Salaam (592,338 people) to two settings in Kenya—a highland area (609,324 people) and a rural setting (55,558 people), the financial costs were reported to be 1.76 USD, 2.30 USD and 4.19 USD pppy in 2024 prices, respectively [12]. In a costing study conducted in Zanzibar comparing the costs of implementing larviciding between the conventional community-led approach and an alternative approach which incorporated drones for the mapping of breeding habitats, the financial costs pppy were 4.27 USD and 4.56 USD in 2024 prices respectively [13].

In a study based in Burkina Faso comprising of 127 rural villages with a total population of 156,000, the financial costs of larviciding were estimated to be 1.02 USD pppy in 2024 prices for treatment of only the 50% most productive larval sources and 1.39 USD pppy in 2024 prices, for treatment of all larval sources with Bti every 10 days [14]. In another study in São Tomé and Príncipe including three districts with a total population of 166,500 people, digitized mapping technology was used for more accurately identifying breeding habitats [15]. The financial cost pppy in rural and urban sites was 1.45 USD and 0.45 USD in 2024 prices respectively [15]. The per capita cost of implementing a larviciding intervention varies greatly in different settings which is driven by ecological and epidemiological settings, larvicide product used and the size of population to be protected.

In recent years, there has been a renewed interest, advocacy and commitment in deploying larviciding for malaria control [8, 17, 18]. In line with this, the government of Tanzania has shown a strong commitment to implement larviciding in the country. Larviciding with biological larvicides is included as one of the additional measures to existing vector control interventions within the National Malaria Strategic Plan (NMSP) 2021–2025 [19]. Moreover in 2014, the Tanzania Biotech Products Limited (TBPL) manufacturing plant was built in Kibaha District Council in Pwani Region of Tanzania to produce biological larvicides for use in malaria control [20]. Currently, the Ministry of Health (MoH) and the National Malaria Control Programme (NMCP) are planning to introduce and scale-up larviciding using biological larvicides produced in-country by TBPL.

To support the government’s commitment, the Swiss Tropical and Public Health Institute –Towards Elimination of Malaria in Tanzania (Swiss TPH –TEMT) project, which is funded by the Swiss government, collaborated with the NMCP and launched a large-scale pilot biolarviciding programme in three heterogeneous councils in Tanga Region, North-Eastern Tanzania. The whole project aimed to evaluate comprehensively large-scale biolarviciding for malaria vector control. In this study, the main interest was to report on the financial cost of implementing the biolarviciding intervention at scale and entirely through the local government system, using real-world data collected prospectively by the TEMT project while the related studies report the results for community awareness and acceptance (Kihwele et al. submitted manuscript), operational feasibility (Gavana et al. submitted manuscript), entomological impact (Gavana et al. submitted manuscript) and epidemiological impact [21]. No attempt was made to calculate the cost-effectiveness of the intervention because of the heterogeneity of entomological and epidemiological impact results [24].

Methods

Study setting

The larviciding intervention was carried out in three councils in the Tanga Region in North-Eastern Tanzania, namely Handeni District Council (DC), Tanga City Council (CC) and Lushoto DC. The intervention councils were selected from one region to facilitate the coordination and supervision of the intervention. Tanga Region was selected because of its heterogeneity in malaria risk across its councils and having both rural and urban settings. Within the region, the three councils were programmatically selected to represent both rural and urban areas as well as settings with different malaria risk strata as defined in the NMSP 2021–2025: high, moderate and low malaria risk strata, respectively [19]. This enabled the evaluation to be conducted per malaria risk strata and to gather costing data per stratum to inform the decision for scale-up of the intervention from a financial perspective. Council specific information on geographic size, malaria risk, estimated population, number of villages/wards and number of larval habitats is presented in Table 1. Average population figures for 2022 to 2024 were used, incorporating the projected population growth rate [22]. In these three councils biolarviciding had never been implemented before. However, the three councils had other simultaneously ongoing preventive malaria interventions including: ITNs through campaigns (latest campaign was conducted in 2023), school nets and reproductive and child health programs; social behavior change and advocacy activities; and intermittent preventive treatment among school children in Handeni DC conducted between March 2020 and June 2021.

Biolarviciding intervention

The intervention was planned in collaboration with the NMCP, the President’s Office Regional Administration and Local Government (PO-RALG), and the Swiss TPH–TEMT project, in collaboration with two other partners: the Ifakara Health Institute (IHI) and the National Institute for Medical Research (NIMR). Implementation of biolarviciding followed a community-based approach with coordination and supervision conducted through the existing local government structure. Community volunteers, known as Community-Owned Resource Persons (CORPs), were selected from within the communities. They were trained using standard operating procedures (SOPs) on how to conduct identification of breeding habitats and on how to apply the biolarvicides based on manufacturer’s instructions. Village Executive Officers (VEOs) directly supervised CORPs, and they were in turn supervised by Ward Executive Officers (WEOs). The Regional and Council Health Management Teams (R/CHMTs) supervised the intervention in their respective areas, while the national team provided overall guidance, coordination and supervision. Both the local government officers and the national team provided on-site assistance through supportive supervision to ensure the activities were implemented as per the SOPs.

In all three councils, biolarvicide was applied intermittently between June 2022 and April 2024, based on rainfall patterns. Tanga Region has a bimodal rainfall pattern, with heavy rainfall from April to May and light rainfall from November to December. Thus, the application of biolarvicide was conducted before and after the heavy rainfall season and before the light rainfall season. Each round consisted of eight consecutive weeks of biolarvicide application. Biolarviciding was initiated in June 2022 commencing after the heavy rainfall season and was subsequently applied for 3 rounds or 24 weeks each year. Hence biolarvicide was applied for roughly 46% of the year (24/52 weeks) in each of the two intervention years. In total, six rounds of biolarvicide application were conducted from June 2022 to April 2024. This intermittent implementation was selected to avoid biolarvicide application at the height of the rainy season for operational reasons and washout of the product by the rain.

The project used two biolarvicide products from TBPL, a subsidiary company of the National Development Corporation that manufactures biological larvicides: Bacillus thuringiensis var israelensis (Bti, Bactivec™) and Bacillus sphaericus (Bs, Griselesf™). Moreover, the project used both Bactivec™ and Griselesf™ as these are the products the country is planning to use during the scale-up of the intervention. Both products have been tested by two independent institution laboratories, and their efficacy was found to be in line with the label recommendation [23, 24].

Costing data

Costing data were obtained from budgets, receipts of actual expenditures and discussions with the financial administrators of the project. Planning activities for the larviciding intervention commenced in March 2021, thus costing data from March 2021 to April 2024 was included. A micro-costing approach was used in which cost categories were defined by key activities [25]. Data included all expenses for human resources, transportation of people and commodities, conference packages for meetings and training, procurement of biolarvicide, equipment and stationery/printing costs at all stages of programme implementation. Costing data for the implementation of larviciding were incurred over the two years in which the spraying operations were carried out. Certain costs were excluded as they were related specifically to the detailed impact evaluation of the project, without being directly required for a successful intervention outside of the specific context of the study, e.g. the cost of electronic data digitisation, entomological monitoring activities, and meetings and travel of international staff members. The perspective taken in the costing analysis was that of the government as the intervention provider.

The costs included in the analysis consisted of only financial costs incurred during the project, excluding a broader economic perspective. Costs such as rental values, salaries of employed government staff, or any other structures or resources already existing before the start of the project were not included. This also applied to any volunteer time or time of any other persons not directly paid. Hence, this analysis accounts for financial costs directly related to the larviciding programme, as it would be incurred by the existing government structure. All costs were calculated in Tanzanian Shillings, reflecting inflation rates of the year in which the cost was incurred. Estimates of costs in USD were calculated using the average formal exchange rate for the period 2022 to 2024, which was 1 USD to 2,401 TZS.

Key cost components of the current study are shown in Table 2. After all cost components, activities and resources were defined for the intervention, ingredient cost inputs were determined at a low level of aggregation to make use of the detailed structure of the cost data: frequencies, number of units and costs per unit. Costs for each component were then determined by a linear combination of prices and quantities as expressed by costing functions (Table 2), and then summed up for each council, as in Eq. (1). Some costs were incurred at the national or regional level only, namely planning and data review, and in these cases the cost for that component was equally shared amongst the three councils. Cost per person protected per year (cost pppy) was calculated by dividing the average one-year cost per council by the council population. The components in Eq. (1) were considered either as a start-up cost or as a recurrent cost.

Start-up costs

All costs incurred under start-up costs were assumed to be incurred in the first year of implementation. Planning costs included all cost items incurred from the planning phase to the implementation phase. This included: initial planning meetings between TEMT, NMCP, PO-RALG, and other stakeholders; meetings to develop the SOPs, costs to conduct internal and external review of SOPs, and translation of SOPs to Kiswahili; meetings for the development and review of training and advocacy materials; and meetings to plan supervision of the intervention. General planning for all activities was carried out by TEMT project members, who were salaried members of staff and carried out such planning as part of their routine employment. Therefore, it was assumed that they fulfilled a role similar to high-level government employees. The costs for planning (Cplan) were calculated as a summation of all planning and review meetings, which in turn were calculated as a function of per diems or allowances for all participants, transport and conference package costs. Communication costs (Ccom) included costs incurred in both advocacy and social behavioural change meetings. Advocacy included national, regional and council level meetings and was a summation of per diems, transport and conference package costs. Social Behavioural Change (SBC) costs were also a summation of per diems, transport and conference package costs in developing SBC materials. Training costs included training at the national, regional, council and sub-council levels. The cost of training (Ctrain) was a function of per diems or allowances for different categories of attendees, transportation costs, conference package costs and stationery for training at each administrative level. Mapping (Cmap) included all costs incurred by CORPs during baseline mapping and data collection. This included 182 CORPs for Handeni DC, 131 for Tanga CC and 180 for Lushoto DC. It also included the supervision costs incurred during the baseline mapping activities. Equipment costs (Cproc_eq) included a one-time cost for the procurement of equipment and spare parts, such as knapsack sprayers, gumboots, dippers, larval trays based on the number CORPs for each council.

Recurrent costs

Recurrent costs were incurred during all six rounds of biolarviciding. The cost of larvicide procurement (Cproc_larv) was calculated as a function of the area of larval habitats identified during each round, the application rate of larvicide (5mls per m2) over eight weekly cycles of application in each round and the commodity cost of both Bti and Bs. Distribution costs (Cdist) included the cost of transporting biolarvicide to the councils and within the councils. Operational costs (Cops) consisted of the monthly allowances paid to the CORPs for larvicide application, on the basis that each application round required 2 months of activity. Lump sum printing costs of Monitoring and Evaluation (M&E) materials for each CORP, village, ward and council level were also included in operational costs. Supervision costs (Csup) included transport and allowances payable to supervisors from the national, regional, council and sub-council levels. Data review costs (Creview) included the allowances of all personnel attending data review meetings, including representatives from the three councils, and additionally conference package and transport costs.

Sensitivity analysis

The effect of varying key parameters on the total financial cost was evaluated in a one-way sensitivity analysis. Key cost drivers were increased and decreased by 20% to assess their overall impact on total cost. The sensitivity analysis evaluated the responsiveness of the programme’s total costs to changes in key cost component variables, namely the cost of larvicide procurement, operations, supervision and training at different administrative levels. A component’s effect on total programme costs was assessed while keeping all other cost components constant.

Extensions of the costing work

The costing work was extended to provide the government and the NMCP with estimated biolarviciding implementation costs under the following scenarios: (i) increasing the number of spray rounds per year, and (ii) scaling-up of the intervention to other councils within the country, to inform the decision on scale-up of the intervention. To calculate the cost of a programme implementing biolarviciding with a varying number of spray rounds compared to the baseline calculations, average recurrent costs based on actual implementation costs across the three intervention councils were multiplied by the number of extrapolated spraying rounds and then added to start-up costs. Calculations were made for four, five and six spray rounds per year. Cost pppy was calculated for each scenario. The resulting cost pppy from the projections were then used to estimate an indicative total cost of extending the larviciding intervention (i) to all councils stratified as high (n = 57) or moderate (n = 57) for malaria transmission in the country, and (ii) to all 184 councils in the country, again for four, five and six spray rounds per year.

Results

The total cost of the intervention in the three councils over the two-year period was 3,354,672,133 TZS (1,397,198 USD). Cost in year one included start-up costs and accounted for 66% of total project cost, with 34% being incurred in year 2 (Table 3). Procurement of biolarvicide accounted for 25% of the total cost, while supervision accounted for 21% and training accounted for 17%. The total implementation cost in Handeni DC was 1,074,500,537 TZS (447,522 USD), 704,450,081 TZS (293,399 USD) in Tanga CC and 935,315,070 TZS (389,552 USD) in Lushoto DC. Additional file 2 (Table S1) highlights the costs in TZS. As noted in Table 1, Handeni DC had the largest geographical area and the highest number of breeding habitats, while Tanga CC had the smallest geographical area and the lowest number of breeding habitats. These differences were reflected in the implementation costs.

Sensitivity analysis

The cost of procurement of larvicide had the greatest impact on total cost. Holding all other inputs constant, a 20% increase in the cost of larvicide increased the total cost by 5%. Operations, which was essentially payment to CORPs, had the second largest impact with a 20% increase resulting in a 4% increase in the total implementation cost. Increasing sub-council training costs impacted total cost by an increase in total cost of 3%. Increases in national, regional and council training impacted total cost positively by between 0.25 and 0.5%.

Costing of different implementation scenarios

The total biolarviciding cost of 3,354,672,133 TZS (1,397,198 USD) was based on three rounds of biolarvicide application per year, or six rounds in a two-year implementation program. Discussion

The study found that the financial cost of implementing a large-scale community-based larviciding intervention across three heterogenous councils of North-Eastern Tanzania amounted to 3,354,672,133 TZS (1,397,198 USD) for two years of intermittent application. Key activities included: planning, training, social behavioural change and advocacy, procurement and distribution of biolarvicide, operational and supervision activities. The start-up year contributed to the largest proportion of total cost (66%). This compared well to another larviciding intervention in Tanzania, in which the start-up cost amounted to 74% of the total cost [11].

The average cost pppy across the three councils was under 1 USD with the cost pppy ranging from 0.47 USD to 0.91 USD between councils. Tanga CC, representing an urban setting and moderate risk stratum, had the lowest cost pppy at 0.47 USD as it had the lowest number of breeding habitats, relatively fewer CORPs deployed, the least amount of biolarvicide used while human population density was the highest. On the other hand, Lushoto DC, a rural setting representing a low-risk stratum, had the highest cost pppy at 0.91 USD. This was mainly due to the low population density in Lushoto DC compared to the other councils, that is, 66% of Handeni DC and 65% of Tanga CC [22]. Additional file 2 (Table S2) shows the variation in population dynamics, number of breeding habitats and CORPs, and liters of biolarvicide used per council.

The average cost pppy across the three intervention councils in Tanga Region given the application modality was slightly lower compared to previous larviciding studies. In Tanzania, a larviciding study in the rural setting of Mvomero using VectoBac™ (an alternative brand of Bti) reported a cost pppy of 1.52 USD in 2024 prices [11]. Evaluation of the Urban Malaria Control Programme (UMCP) in Dar es Salaam reported that the cost pppy of implementing larviciding in three wards using VectoBac™ and VectoLex™ was 1.76 USD in 2024 prices [12], while it was 1.19 USD in 2024 prices [26] when the costs were extrapolated for the entire study area (15 wards).

Similarly, the cost pppy across the intervention councils in Tanga Region was comparable to other settings across sub-Saharan Africa. A larviciding study conducted in Kenya using VectoBac™ reported a cost pppy of 2.30 USD in 2024 prices in a highly populated rural areas, while it was 4.19 USD in 2024 prices in a rural lakeside low population density area [12]. In another study in Burkina Faso, also using VectoBac™, the reported cost pppy ranged from 1.02 USD in 2024 prices when treating 50% of the breeding habitats to 1.39 USD in 2024 prices when treating all breeding habitats [14]. A systematic literature review of costing of vector control interventions showed that the financial cost pppy from a larviciding intervention ranged from 1.04 USD to 2.31 USD in 2024 prices, with a median cost of 1.47 USD in 2024 prices [27]. These differences in cost pppy were expected considering variations in the scale of implementation, the type of larvicide used, environmental conditions, population dynamics, and the frequency of application.

The same systematic review also summarized the key economic cost pppy for different malaria interventions including vector control [27]. While the median cost pppy for larviciding was 1.47 USD in 2024 prices, it was 1.74 USD in 2024 prices for ITNs and 7.12 USD in 2024 prices for IRS. Hence, larviciding seemed to be comparable in terms of costs to ITNs, but it was notably cheaper than IRS. Considering that the above review considered economic rather than financial costs, the findings from this study align well with other studies.

Unfortunately, the impact results for larviciding programme are currently too few and heterogenous for conducting an overall cost-effectiveness of biolarviciding compared to other malaria interventions. The 2019 Cochrane review on larviciding intervention for prevention of malaria transmission reported mixed impact results and highlighted that the effectiveness of the intervention was influenced by differences in the settings and operational implementation [10]. The experience in Tanga Region confirmed this, as the study identified heterogeneous entomological and epidemiological impact in the three intervention councils. The results showed that with the chosen intermittent larviciding strategy, the impact on larvae and adult mosquitoes varied by malaria risk strata and mosquito species. This could be explained in part by the intermittent implementation of larviciding, but also because of the difficulty of finding all breeding habitats, particularly for Anopheles funestus (Gavana et al. submitted manuscript). Moreover, the intervention showed a tendency towards a reduction in malaria epidemiological parameters in the moderate and low malaria risk settings, but not in the high-risk strata, although the results were mostly not statistically significant [21].

Although no cost-effectiveness assessment was conducted in this study, the detailed costing work performed remains useful for malaria vector control. The presented financial costs can be disaggregated into key intervention activities and adapted by malaria programmes and relevant stakeholders to estimate the implementation costs for other modalities of implementation. Since the entomological evaluation showed rebounds in both larvae and adult mosquitoes in between the spray rounds, the costing analysis also extrapolated the costs for increasing the number of spray rounds per year (Gavana et al. submitted manuscript). When comparing the cost of the intermittent application of three spray rounds (24 weeks) per year to a more continuous spraying pattern throughout the year (6 rounds/48 weeks per year), the difference in unit cost is non-linear, indicating that there are potentially benefits of economies of scale to be made. Specifically, the cost pppy increased from 1,583 TZS (0.66 USD) for three spray rounds per year to 2,716 TZS (1.13 USD) for six spray rounds per year. However, while considering an increase in the number of spray rounds per year, larviciding should not be conducted in weeks with heavy rains to avoid the risk of wash-out of the larvicides.

Finally, the costing work included estimating the implementation costs for scaling-up biolarviciding to other councils within the country. This was conducted to inform the MoH and the NMCP on the budgetary requirements for scaling-up the intervention under different scenarios. When considering scaling up to the entire country, the cost of scale-up with three spray rounds per year was 29 times greater than the cost of the current three-council intervention, while scale-up with six rounds per year resulted in a 50-fold increment in the total cost.

The current study has several strengths and limitations. The study utilized prospective costing data collected before and during the intervention and was based on full financial records. This allowed for a high level of completeness and accuracy. Additionally, this was the largest larviciding study area for which a costing analysis has been performed in sub-Saharan Africa, and the implementation model was entirely through the local government system. A limitation was that the study did not attempt to calculate the economic cost of larviciding, which would have then included opportunity costs, discounting rates and any indirect or hidden costs. Presumably, the economic cost calculation would have been higher than the financial cost. However, the financial cost was more intuitive to government agencies such as the NMCP, and more relevant for planning and financing malaria control at national and council level. Another limitation was that the cost projections for upscaling were made using data from only three councils, whereas other councils in Tanzania vary substantially in terms of area, topography, breeding habitats and rainfall patterns. Therefore, these projections must be considered as indicative.

Conclusion

The present costing analysis provides insights into the financial cost of a large-scale larviciding intervention across three heterogenous councils in North-Eastern Tanzania. Assessing the cost of large-scale implementation shows that biolarviciding is similar in implementation cost to other vector control interventions. If effective, sustained biolarviciding operations could contribute to the efforts of further reducing the malaria burden in Tanzania and other settings.
