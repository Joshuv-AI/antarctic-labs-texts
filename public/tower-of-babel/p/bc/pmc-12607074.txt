Sex-Specific heart rate acceleration as predictors of positive head-up tilt test outcomes in syncope patients
Authors: Carlos Eduardo Batista de Lima, Patryck Araújo Dantas da Silva, Sheila Raquel Alves de Sá Nascimento, Marina Vilarinho Alves de Freitas, Frankeline Gonçalves de Arêa Leão Moreira, Valmir Oliveira Silvino, Ricardo Martins Ramos, Marcos Antonio Pereira dos Santos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHead-up tilt test (HUTT) is widely used to evaluate patients with unexplained syncope, but data on clinical predictors of HUTT results are limited. This study aimed to evaluate heart rate (HR) acceleration and blood pressure (BP) changes associated with the HUTT results in syncope patients and the differences according to sex.MethodsA cross-sectional study was performed with patients who presented with unexplained syncope and were subjected to HUTT (70-degree angle) from January 2011 to April 2015. BP changes and Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves for the maximum interval in HR acceleration from the first to the tenth minute (delta-HR 10) after tilting were analyzed. The significance level was set at 5%.ResultsOf 149 patients (mean age: 28.5 ± 29 years), 40 (26.85%) had a positive HUTT. The delta-HR 10 acceleration was higher in the positive group (14.0 ± 9.25 bpm vs. 9.0 ± 11 bpm, p = 0.002). When considering sex, a cut-off point of 12 bpm was found, with an area under the curve of 0.805 for females, showing a sensitivity of 80% and a negative predictive value of 91.2%, and an area under the curve of 0.580 for males, with a sensitivity of 52% and a negative predictive value of 73.9%.ConclusionsThe HR acceleration of 12 bpm up to the tenth minute after tilting was an independent predictor of a positive HUTT in female syncope patients, demonstrating good sensitivity, accuracy, and negative predictive value.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT06496074.

Background

Syncope is defined as an intermittent loss of consciousness and postural tone caused by cerebral hypoperfusion. The episodes have a rapid onset, short duration, and complete spontaneous recovery, accounting for about 3% of admissions to emergency services in the USA [1]. n the age group between 10 and 30 years, the first syncope event has a high prevalence, with an estimated peak of 47% in women and 31% in men around 15 years of age [2]. This condition can be explained by several mechanisms, with vasovagal reflex syncope being responsible for most events [3, 4].

For several decades, head-up tilt test (HUTT) has been used in the investigation of unexplained syncope [5]. However, there are still controversies regarding its usefulness in clinical practice. In the latest international guidelines, its class of recommendation for this purpose was reduced from I to IIa with a level of evidence B [6, 7]. The main criticisms of HUTT are based on the prolonged examination time, the considerable presence of false positives, and its lack of influence on the therapeutic management of patients [8].

In this context, the development of new protocols that enhance patient comfort and reduce examination time is desirable. Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity of clinical and hemodynamic variables associated with a positive result on the HUTT in syncope patients.

Methods

Patients

An observational, cross-sectional study was conducted on patients with a history of syncope who underwent HUTT between January 2011 and April 2015 at the Federal University of Piauí, Brazil. The study was carried out in collaboration with the private sector, including Clinicardio and ITACOR Hospital, specialized centers for the diagnosis and treatment of heart disease. The study flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. All principles of good clinical practice were followed, including those set out in Resolution No. 466/2012 of the National Health Council. This study was approved by the Ethics and Research Committee of the Federal University of Piauí on 06/26/2015 under the number 1.132.024.Fig. 1Study flowchart. Abbreviation: HUTT, Head-up tilt test

Protocols

Two HUTT protocols were used at the discretion of the attending physician: (1) passive or prolonged inclination protocol (40 min); and (2) mixed protocol: passive phase (first 20 min) plus nitrate-provoked phase (last 20 min), using 1.25 mg of sublingual isosorbide dinitrate. Blood pressure (BP) measurements were obtained manually with the Welch Allyn® DS44-11BR Durashock sphygmomanometer, and heart rate (HR) data were continuously monitored using a conventional 12-lead ECG-PC TEB® surface electrocardiogram, with automatic measurements certified by NBR IEC 60601-2-51 and registered with ANVISA (registration number: 10265690026). All patients were evaluated at rest for 10 min in the horizontal position before the tilt phase. During this period, HR, systolic (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) measurements were obtained every 2 min. After this phase, the table was tilted to an angle of 70°, with measurements of SBP, DBP, and HR recorded at the end of each minute. Patients older than 60 years were investigated for carotid sinus hypersensitivity by carotid sinus massage during the passive phase of the HUTT. The exam was considered positive according to the criteria defined in the guidelines [6, 7].

Statistical analysis

The variables analyzed were age, sex, and maximum HR acceleration from the first (delta-HR 1) to the tenth minute (delta-HR 10) in the tilt phase. Changes in SBP and DBP measurements were obtained from the last measurement of the rest phase to the first minute after tilting. Variables were also analyzed considering the combination of Delta-DBP and Delta-HR. Pearson’s Chi-square test was applied to verify the association between qualitative variables. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to check whether the numerical variables followed a Gaussian (Normal) distribution. The Mann-Whitney test was used to compare two groups of numerical variables when the distribution did not follow normality. Receiver operating chacteristic (ROC) curves were constructed to define cut-off points in HR accelerations for delta-HR 10, evaluating performance and analyzing sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and accuracy for association with positivity in the HUTT. The cut-off points were obtained using the Youden method, which maximizes the sum of sensitivity and specificity. A logistic regression model was also applied when appropriate. The significance level adopted was 5%. Statistical analysis was performed using R Core Team Statistical Software, version 3.6.3.

The positive rate was 26.8% for all HUTTs performed. According to the protocol, the positive rate was 37.3% for the nitrate-provoked protocol and 16.2% for the passive protocol (P = 0.004). The HUTT results

showed a mixed response in 50.0% of cases, vasodepressor response in 47.5%, and cardioinhibitory response in 2.5%.

In the comparative analysis between the groups regarding the presence of a positive neurocardiogenic response to HUTT, there was a significant difference in age (22.0 ± 17.75 years vs. 33.0 ± 31.7 years, p = 0.023), but no significant difference according to sex (27.8% of males vs. 25.4% of females, p = 0.898).

Among the hemodynamic parameters, there was no difference in the levels of SBP variation, but there was lower variation in DBP levels among patients with negative HUTT results. Additionally, there was a statistically significant difference in HR levels among patients with positive HUTT results compared to those with negative results. Specifically, there was an acceleration in HR levels during the first minute (12.5 ± 9.75 beats per minute (bpm) vs. 8.0 ± 11.0 bpm; P = 0.019) and during the interval from the first to the tenth minute of the examination (14.0 ± 9.25 bpm vs. 9.0 ± 11.0 bpm; P = 0.002).

The area under the curve (AUC) from the ROC curve analysis demonstrated the global predictive ability of heart rate acceleration as a predictor of positive outcomes in the HUTT. For delta-HR 10, the AUC was 0.667 (Fig. 2), indicating moderate predictive power. The analysis identified an optimum cut-off point for HR variation of 9.0 bpm. When stratified by sex, the predictive performance improved for females, with an AUC of 0.805 (95% CI: 0.679%–0.931%), a cut-off point of 12 bpm, a sensitivity of 80% (95% CI: 0.52%–0.957%), and a negative predictive value of 91.2% (95% CI: 0.736%–0.956%). For males, the AUC was 0.58 (95% CI: 0.45%–0.71%), with a sensitivity of 92% (95% CI: 0.74%–0.99%) and a negative predictive value of 89% (95% CI: 0.664%–0.935%) at the corresponding cut-off point. Discussion

The main finding of this study was that HR acceleration during the early stages of HUTT could predict the test result in female patients. The ROC curve analysis identified a cut-off point of 12.0 bpm in HR acceleration as a predictor of a positive HUTT result in females. This strategy showed better performance compared to males, with an area under the ROC curve of 0.805 (p = 0.010). This hemodynamic variable demonstrated a sensitivity of 80.0%, specificity of 70.5%, NPV of 91.2%, and accuracy of 72.9%. For the entire study population, the ROC curve revealed a cut-off point of 9 bpm, with an area under the curve of 0.667. When HR variation was below this cut-off, the NPV of the HUTT was high, indicating that the absence of significant HR acceleration is a strong predictor of a negative HUTT result. In selected cases, observing an insufficient HR acceleration could guide the early interruption and shortening of the examination time. Conversely, the predictive value of hemodynamic parameters may be more limited in patients with a higher probability of a positive response, for whom a complete HUTT should be performed to identify the type of response.

The findings of this study suggest that when the HR acceleration reaches a cut-off point of 12 bpm in female patients, the probability of a positive HUTT result is high, observable at an early stage of the exam. Efremov et al. [9] evaluated 64 patients by performing heart rate variability (HRV) analysis within the first 20 min of passive tilting. They demonstrated that patients with nitroglycerine-induced syncope exhibit a progressive decrease in parasympathetic activity, which does not occur in patients with a negative response to nitroglycerine. This suggests that HRV analysis could potentially replace nitroglycerine administration and shorten the duration of the HUTT.

Ghariq et al. [10] analyzed the results of 766 HUTTs and observed differences between males and females in patients with suspected spontaneous vasovagal syncope. They found that males were more susceptible to vasovagal syncope induction without nitroglycerin, while females were more susceptible after its administration. These findings suggest the presence of sex-specific pathophysiological differences that are not yet fully understood. The effect of sex on HUTT results was closely associated with differences in blood pressure changes during inclination, but not with HRV. There was no difference in the overall positivity rate of syncope between males and females.

In this context, Robertson et al. [11] studied the differences between sexes in the regulation of autonomic and cerebrovascular responses during vertical inclination compared to the supine position. They found that males exhibited a greater increase in sympathetic indices and cerebrovascular resistance index during vertical inclination, increasing the low frequency to high frequency ratio of HRV. In contrast, females showed higher parasympathetic activity and decreased sensitivity of the cardiovagal baroreflex.

Other studies have sought to assess hemodynamic parameters to predict the outcome on the results of the HUTT. Mallat et al. [12] evaluated patients with unexplained syncope, finding that an early and sustained HR increase of ≤ 18 bpm in the first 6 min at a 60° tilt predicted a negative response with 100% specificity, 100% positive predictive value, and 88.6% sensitivity. Sumiyoshi et al. [13] investigated the predictive value of early HR increase during the initial 5 min of an 80° tilt for 30 min. They found that the early HR increase was greater in the positive group (P = 0.0008). An increase of ≥ 18 bpm had modest sensitivity (76%) and low specificity (62%) for predicting a positive response during passive tilt for 30 min. However, when predicting a positive response within 15 min, an early HR increase of ≥ 18 bpm had 100% sensitivity and 61% specificity. Likewise, Movahed [14] found that in the absence of an HR increase greater than 19 bpm during the first 10 min of the HUTT, the test had a sensitivity of 93.3% and specificity of 66.7% for predicting a negative result.

According to the data published so far, heart rate acceleration appears to be positively associated with dysautonomia. However, there is still no consensus on which parameter could be used with greater precision in clinical practice. The findings of our study reinforce this pre-existing association by introducing new parameters that could be used for this purpose.

As for BP changes during the HUTT, the systolic component showed a slight decrease compared to resting parameters with no difference between the studied groups. However, the diastolic component exhibited different behavior. According to a logistic regression model, when there was no acceleration in DBP, the probability of a negative HUTT result was 72.1%, and with an increase of 10 mmHg, this probability rose to 75.7%. Asymptomatic reductions in SBP during the initial phase of tilt may reflect the vasomotor instability that precedes reflex syncope. In a study involving healthy patients with unexplained syncope, reductions of more than 14 mmHg in SBP during the first 15 min of tilt predicted a positive test result after 15 min with 93% sensitivity, 58% specificity, and positive and negative predictive values of 28% and 98%, respectively. This suggests that continuing the test beyond this point offers no advantage, as the likelihood of later syncope-induced test interruption would be very low [15].

Some authors have attempted to use HR and SBP values together to predict the occurrence of syncope during HUTT, employing algorithms that consider the acceleration of these data over time and the HR/SBP ratio, achieving good sensitivity and specificity [16, 17]. In our study, however, variations in BP did not influence the outcome of the HUTT.

To reduce the testing time, the absence of HR acceleration above the established cut-off points, combined with a DBP change of less than 10 mmHg, can be particularly useful in identifying patients with a higher probability of a negative HUTT response. In such cases, the HUTT could potentially be stopped earlier. In clinical practice, when the HUTT result is negative, it is generally acceptable to assume the pre-test diagnosis, whether vasovagal syncope, hypotensive orthostatic syncope, or syncope of undetermined origin, as the probable cause of the syncopal episode [18].

Study limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the study population consisted predominantly of young individuals, limiting the generalizability of the results to older populations where syncope and related conditions may present differently. Additionally, patients were not stratified or analyzed based on the number of syncopal events in their medical history, the presence of concomitant diseases, or prior traumatic injuries sustained during syncope, all of which may influence the outcomes of HUTT. It is also noteworthy that patients who had a negative response during the passive phase of HUTT might have exhibited a positive response during the provocation phase, which was not systematically analyzed in this study. From a methodological standpoint, we did not employ automated methods for blood pressure and heart rate variability measurements, which might have introduced some variability. However, all HUTTs were conducted by experienced cardiologists, which likely reduced procedural inconsistencies. Additionally, the study design and analyzed population may not fully address potential confounders, such as unmeasured demographic or clinical characteristics, which could affect the interpretation of the results.

Conclusion

In this cohort of young patients evaluated for reflex syncope, an early HR acceleration of ≥ 12.0 bpm within the first ten minutes of the tilt phase was identified as a predictor of a positive HUTT in female patients. Additionally, the absence of DBP change or a slight increase of less than 10 mmHg in the first minute after tilting, regardless of sex, was associated with a high probability of a negative response HUTT result.
