We need to understand “when” not “if” generative AI can enhance personalized persuasion
Authors: Jacob D. Teeny, Sandra C. Matz
Published: 2024

Recent advances in generative AI (gen-AI) have sparked a lively debate about its ability to design personalized, persuasive messages and the consequences of this for society. In a recent article, Hackenburg and Margetts (1) empirically investigated this question by using ChatGPT-4 to dynamically personalize political messages to one or more sociodemographic characteristics, concluding that personalization is an ineffective strategy and that gen-AI is incapable of dynamically enhancing it (1, p. 5). As the authors acknowledge, their findings stand in contrast to decades of research demonstrating the effectiveness of personalized persuasion (2–5). In addition, they also contradict several studies developed in parallel, which have supported the effectiveness of gen-AI in this domain (6–8). Instead of dismissing gen-AI’s potential for personalized persuasion, we argue that the findings by Hackenburg and Margetts call for a much-needed discussion on when personalized persuasion using gen-AI is effective and a theory-informed agenda for future research.

We start this discussion by providing two potential explanations for the observed null effects. First, personalization needs to be perceptible. This does not mean it should be obvious, but it should “feel right” or relevant to the message recipient (9). Because personalization that is too obvious can feel intrusive and become ineffective (6), Hackenburg and Margetts reasonably prompted ChatGPT to “keep participants from realizing they are being microtargeted.” This restriction, however, might have led to overcorrection, preventing the creation of messages that were, in fact, personalized. In a reanalysis of their data (Fig. 1), we tested how the accurately personalized and falsely personalized conditions—using between one and nine targeted sociodemographic traits—compared to one another (and the control group) with regard to participants’ self-reported perception of personalization (i.e., a personalization manipulation check). Two-thirds of these comparisons were nonsignificant, and all yielded small effect sizes, suggesting any personalization in their messages was minimally perceptible.

Second, personalization needs to be psychologically meaningful. Most existing research on personalized persuasion has employed rich psychological characteristics for personalization, such as personality traits or moral foundations. In contrast, Hackenburg and Margetts employed simple sociodemographics (e.g., age, education), which offer a shallower range for meaningfully personalizing the appeal to the target (6). Additionally, a personalized message’s effectiveness hinges on the targeted trait’s importance to the message recipient. If a person does not place much psychological weight, for example, on their geographic location or even their political orientation, personalization on this trait should minimally if at all enhance persuasion. Because the authors primarily relied on simple sociodemographics, which might have been irrelevant to the participant—let alone lack any kind of connection to the political issues that the messages discussed—we would expect personalized messages to be largely ineffective, even if they were perceived as personalized.

In sum, we commend Hackenburg and Margetts for their valuable contribution, but caution against considering their results as evidence against the general effectiveness of personalized persuasion or gen-AI’s inability to enhance it. Instead, we advocate for a systematic investigation of the factors limiting or amplifying gen-AI’s potential for personalized persuasion.
