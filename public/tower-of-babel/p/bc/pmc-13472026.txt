The widespread adoption of electronic medical records (EMRs) transformed clinical documentation by enabling large-scale clinical research and analysis of patient outcomes. By 2015, >80% of US hospitals had adopted an EMR system.1 However, extracting meaningful data from these records remains labor-intensive, especially in specialties such as ophthalmology, which rely on multimodal documentation, including structured metrics (e.g., visual acuity), imaging reports, and narrative procedural notes.2 Manual chart review is time-consuming and prone to errors, making it inefficient in daily clinical workflows.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI), particularly large language models (LLMs) such as OpenAI’s GPT-4 and Google’s (Alphabet) Gemini 1.5, have opened new avenues for automating clinical data retrieval. Artificial intelligence‑based chatbot systems that use LLMs as their foundation exhibit strong performance in medical natural language understanding and have been successfully deployed, such as Google’s Med-PaLM 2 for medical question answering and Glass AI for clinical documentation.3,4 While most current AI chatbot tools in medicine focus on patient education, triage, or clinical decision-making support, there is growing recognition of using AI to accelerate the efficiency and productivity of clinical and research workflows by training and implementing AI to perform chart abstraction and cohort identification.5

We previously presented OphthoACR, our custom AI pipeline designed to perform highly accurate, efficient cohort-wide analysis and to extract in-depth patient-level insights.6 In the study, we demonstrated OphthoACR’s ability to accurately and efficiently conduct qualitative and interpretative analysis on a cohort of nearly 100 patients who underwent complicated secondary intraocular lens (IOL) surgery at Columbia University Irving Medical Center (CUIMC) from 2020 to 2024.6 However, a key limitation of the study was its static nature. It did not support real-time interaction or incremental querying, so users cannot intercept or refine responses as they are generated. Another major limitation of OphthoACR is that it requires technical proficiency in coding to set up the tool and perform cohort analyses. This limits its accessibility to the typical clinical ophthalmologist without a computational background. To address the need for a powerful yet user-friendly AI tool for clinicians and trainees to interface with, we developed OphthoChat, a dialogue-based, conversational AI chart review system. OphthoChat integrates GPT-4, an LLM with advanced natural language processing capabilities, into a custom pipeline that enables real-time, natural-language querying of comprehensive patient data histories. GPT-4 is pretrained on a broad knowledge base derived from publicly available online information and licensed data, including substantial medically oriented content and ophthalmology-related knowledge drawn from textbooks, clinical literature, academic publications, and reputable web sources. This pretraining provides the OphthoChat with a strong baseline understanding of ophthalmic terminology, disease mechanisms, diagnostic patterns, and treatment principles. OphthoChat was further enhanced with a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) framework to optimize chart-based responses and was integrated into a custom pipeline that manages and automates patient chart review and cohort analyses.

OphthoChat enables clinicians and researchers without technical expertise or understanding of coding to conduct sophisticated queries across comprehensive patient chart histories. This overcomes a major barrier to using AI-based medical tools, which often require technical expertise. Ophthalmology heavily relies on narrative documentation, including visual outcomes, procedural methods, and intraoperative events. These are often misinterpreted or difficult to capture with rule-based keyword searches. However, modern language models such as OphthoChat and advanced LLMs can accurately interpret these unstructured data. Automating access to this information will significantly enhance outcomes research, quality improvement, and clinical evidence ascertainment collection.

In this study, we present OphthoChat and evaluate its accuracy and performance by validating the outputs of 1000 diverse clinical queries of varying complexity involving patients with retinal and anterior segment diseases seen at Columbia University Harkness Eye Institute from 2020 to 2025. Query tasks included tracking temporal changes in visual acuity among patients with wet age-related macular degeneration (AMD) treated with intravitreal injections and assessing changes in astigmatism after cataract surgery. Age-related macular degeneration and cataract surgery patients were specifically chosen to evaluate OphthoChat’s robustness in processing and interpreting ophthalmic data due to the inherent complexity of the procedural interventions and extensive documentation involved in the treatment of AMD and cataracts. Additionally, patients with AMD and cataracts typically have pre-existing ocular histories, which often include multiple comorbid eye diseases, thereby ensuring that our patient cohort evaluated by OphthoChat encompassed a wide breadth of ophthalmic conditions.

Methods

Study Design and Evaluation Framework

We evaluated OphthoChat, our conversational, LLM-powered chart review tool, on a set of 1000 natural language queries of varying clinical complexity. OphthoChat was applied to query the entire EMR chart histories of patients who had either undergone intravitreal injections for wet AMD or cataract surgery at CUIMC from 2020 to 2025, and the responses were evaluated for accuracy. Figure 1 outlines the ease of use of OphthoChat’s interface with an example given of a simulated patient chart.

OphthoChat’s interface enables intuitive interaction in which users simply select a patient and submit natural-language queries. This example shows a query requesting a simulated patient’s current medications, with the OphthoChat system output displaying medications and dose, in addition to the reason for being prescribed the medication.

Access, Governance, and Data Security

The study was approved by the CUIMC Institutional Review Board with a waiver of informed consent for a retrospective chart review and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the US Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) Privacy Rule. All components of the OphthoChat pipeline run entirely on HIPAA-compliant, institution-managed infrastructure. Source clinical documents are stored on encrypted CUIMC servers with restricted access, and all preprocessing steps, including OpenCV-based denoising and contrast enhancement, are performed locally on HIPAA-compliant institutional endpoints.

OphthoChat is hosted on a protected CUIMC portal, allowing authorized users to log in and interact with the system via natural language without additional technical setup. The system operates exclusively within CUIMC’s HIPAA-aligned environment under an OpenAI–Columbia Business Associate Agreement.7 Access requires single sign-on authentication, encrypted virtual private network-based connectivity to obtain chart data from EMRs, and role-based Application Program Interface keys issued by CUIMC IT. All intermediate and final outputs are stored on encrypted institutional servers, and comprehensive audit logs capture each query, retrieval step, and LLM response.

Data Preparation and Preprocessing

We queried Epic for adults seen at CUIMC between January 2020 and January 2025 who met ≥1 of 2 inclusion criteria: (1) received ≥1 intravitreal injections for wet AMD (Current Procedural Terminology code 67028 and International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision code H35.32∗) or (2) cataract extraction with IOL implantation (Current Procedural Terminology codes 66984 or 66982). From this search, we identified 250 AMD and 250 cataract patients. Their charts contained >5000 EMR objects, including progress notes, operative reports, imaging summaries, scanned PDFs (e.g., handwritten notes, imaging), and OCT images. Scanned documents are rendered as images, then locally denoised and contrast-enhanced using OpenCV to improve legibility. GPT-4 Vision optical character recognition (OCR) then converts images to text. GPT-4 Vision OCR was selected for its robustness across variable-quality and formatted clinical scans.8,9 From a sample of 50 randomly selected images analyzed using GPT-4 Vision, average OCR accuracy was 86% (range = 72%‑100%). The resulting text is then normalized (whitespace cleanup, terminology normalization, and tabular standardization) before downstream extraction.

Each cleaned document was embedded for retrieval in a RAG pipeline with the text-embedding-ada-002 model and stored, together with metadata (patient identifier, date, and file type), in a Pinecone vector index using cosine similarity. This structure enables rapid, semantically driven retrieval while preserving traceability to the original source document.

System Architecture: Query-To-Answer Pipeline

OphthoChat enables users to interact with ophthalmology patient records using free-text, natural-language queries through a dialogue-based interface. Unlike traditional chart analysis tools that require structured inputs and coding knowledge, OphthoChat accepts clinician-initiated questions (e.g., “How has this patient’s visual acuity changed since their first intravitreal injection?”) and returns precise, context-aware responses that synthesize information from the patient’s entire chart history.

LLM Configuration

All queries were served by GPT-4o (March 2025 release) via the CUIMC HIPAA endpoint. Model parameters identified via prior parameter optimization were fixed at temperature = 0.1, top_P = 0.95, frequency penalty = 0.2, and presence_penalty = 0.10 Twenty high-quality few-shot examples spanning retinal, cataract, and glaucoma scenarios were supplied to guide in-context learning; the model was otherwise used “out of the box,” with no additional fine-tuning.

Although the study cohort consisted of patients with AMD and cataracts, glaucoma cases were intentionally included among the few-shot examples to support robust in-context learning. Glaucoma accounts for a significant proportion of ophthalmic disease burden, and many patients undergoing intravitreal injections for AMD or cataract surgery may have coexisting glaucomatous optic nerve damage that affects best-corrected visual acuity outcomes.

Performance Evaluation and Statistical Analysis

The system performance of OphthoChat was tested on 1000 clinician-authored queries, intentionally stratified by complexity: 40% simple (single-field extraction, e.g., latest logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution acuity), 40% intermediate (temporal trends across visits, e.g., visual acuity change at 1, 3, and 6 months after intravitreal injection), and 20% advanced (narrative inference, e.g., evidence of cystoid macular edema persisting >3 months) (Table 1).

For each query, OphthoChat’s output was compared with a “gold standard” or ground truth reference standard established by 2 trained independent clinician graders (V.D., K.C.); disagreements were resolved through joint adjudication. During the manual grading process, clinical variables and patient chart information were independently evaluated by 2 independent human clinician graders to respond to each query. This involved a thorough manual examination of all relevant data, including electronic health record notes and imaging reports from patient charts. Human manual grading responses were logged and compared in Excel 2024 (Microsoft) to create one “gold standard” correct answer for each query. Then, OphthoChat responses to queries were placed in a column. Responses included visual acuity pressure trajectories, intraocular pressure trends, surgical dates, postoperative complications, and medication usage, along with the corresponding source text excerpts OphthoChat used to reach its conclusions.

The following performance metrics for OphthoChat’s binary classification tasks (e.g., identification of complications, medication use, presence of imaging findings) were recorded: accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and wall-clock time-to-answer. Additionally, Cohen κ was calculated to quantify inter-rater agreement between automated OphthoChat outputs and the human clinician “gold standard” or ground truth correct answer outputs. Because Cohen κ accounts for chance agreement, it provides a more reliable measure of performance instead of accuracy when data are imbalanced in one direction. All analyses were performed in Python 3.11 (Python Software Foundation) using pandas 2.2, scikit-learn 1.4, and statsmodels 0.14.

Code for the clinician-facing OphthoChat interface has been made available for research purposes on GitHub (https://github.com/advaitapatil/OphthoChat). Several simulated patients have been loaded onto the OphthoChat mockup on GitHub. The GitHub mockup is a skeleton without an input institutional proprietary chart string or RAG-enhanced model.

OphthoChat Validation

Query Set and Reference Standard

We evaluated 1000 clinician-authored questions: 400 simple, 400 intermediate, and 200 advanced. Two independent graders reviewed every OphthoChat answer for accuracy, coadjudicating disagreements (n = 62) to establish the final reference standard.

Overall Performance Results

OphthoChat achieved an overall accuracy of 96% in correctly answering clinician-authored queries, with an aggregate sensitivity of 93% and specificity of 97%. Table 2 shows the performance statistics of OphthoChat across different query task difficulties (Table 2). Cohen κ of 0.82 indicates substantial agreement and robust performance on complex, binary variable‑based query tasks. This shows that the OphthoChat model consistently matches the classifications assigned by human clinician graders. Mean reciprocal rank of cited evidence was 0.92 ± 0.07, indicating that the correct supporting evidence was almost always returned at the top of the ranked list. Of note, there was no difference in OphthoChat’s performance across disease categories other than AMD and cataracts.

Median wall-clock time-to-answer latency was 35 seconds, an 11-fold speed-up compared with manual chart review, which is 6.6 minutes. Profiling showed that vector search accounted for ∼25% of runtime and LLM generation for ∼70%, while interpretation and consolidation consumed ∼5%.

Every response included a plain-language answer and a citation to the date and location of the evidence. Reviewers confirmed that among correctly answered responses, 100% of citations were correct; among correct answers, the cited line contained the ground-truth data in 97% of cases.

Error Analysis

Of the 40 responses generated by OphthoChat that differed from clinician ground-truth responses, 3 root causes of poor responses were identified: 42% of errors stemmed from typographical or OCR artifacts that obscured critical information (misspelled or truncated words, ophthalmic abbreviations); 35% of errors stemmed from ambiguous clinical phrasing that prevented clear yes/no determinations (“successful procedural outcome”); and 23% of errors stemmed from conflicting chart entries, with different notes listing conflicting visual acuity values or procedure dates (visual acuity logged as 20/40, while the ophthalmic tech logged 20/50).

By requerying with tighter instructions, such as asking the model to ignore illegible scans, requiring explicit affirmative language, or cross-checking multiple sources, we resolved 63% of erroneous responses. This indicates that the majority of OphthoChat mistakes stem from data quality or query wording rather than fundamental model limitations.

The remaining 37% of erroneous OphthoChat responses were due to: (1) truly incomplete portions of the patient chart (e.g., incomplete ophthalmology examination, missing intraocular pressure values, absent visual field or OCT interpretations, missed postoperative assessment visits) or (2) continued poor prompt engineering by the human user, such as querying OphthoChat with short, ambiguous sentence fragments.

Regarding erroneous responses driven by incomplete chart data, we further incorporated a structured abstention mechanism with a predefined threshold (<65% certainty score) that prompts OphthoChat to withhold an answer when supporting evidence is insufficient, thereby reducing the risk of incorrect or poorly substantiated responses, as well as “hallucinations,” confident but false, nonsensical, or fabricated responses not supported in the electronic health record text. A representative AMD case illustrates why this safeguard is necessary. When a user asked, “Is the patient’s neovascular AMD improving based on OCT over the last 3 months?”, the most recent retina note stated “AMD stable” and “s/p Eylea today,” but the OCT report itself (e.g., fluid status or interpretation) was not present. OphthoChat initially answered “stable/improving” and cited “AMD stable,” a citation that was accurate to the note and therefore not a hallucination, but nonetheless incorrect relative to the query constraint (“based on OCT”) because no OCT interpretation was available. With the abstention mechanism enabled, OphthoChat instead returned: “Unable to determine OCT-based AMD activity from the available record; OCT is referenced/reviewed but no OCT interpretation or fluid assessment is documented,” with citation to the same clinic note.

Additionally, the following is an example of poor prompt engineering by the human user that led to an erroneous OphthoChat response: “What is the IOL power?” OphthoChat returned the IOL power the surgeon had already implanted in the right eye, although the user wanted OphthoChat to use the Barrett Universal II formula to calculate the ideal IOL power from the patient’s ocular bio parameters to plan cataract surgery on the left eye. Although these instances were still deemed “erroneous responses,” the error stemmed from the short, ambiguous query initially posed by the human user. Thus, thoughtful and detailed prompt engineering is essential for operating and using OphthoChat, and clinicians and researchers should be taught and trained to build queries and prompts for LLMs to execute tasks accurately. Once prompt queries were further developed and elaborated on, OphthoChat was able to accurately return the intended answer the human user requested (e.g., stating explicitly, “using the Barrett Universal II calculation to determine the correct IOL power”).

Overall, when challenged with 1000 clinical ophthalmology questions, OphthoChat delivered fully traceable answers in an average of 35 seconds, with 96% accuracy, consistently outperforming manual review in both speed and reliability, even for complex narrative-inference tasks. Additionally, OphthoChat has a function that allows nonophthalmic clinicians to simplify ophthalmic charts and medical language.

Discussion

At the intersection of patient-centered clinical research and advanced AI data processing, OphthoChat and related LLM-based systems introduce transformative capabilities for patient chart review and analysis. Pretrained on extensive medical knowledge, these chatbots employ natural language processing to interpret, synthesize, and generate clinical language, enabling seamless, human-like interaction while executing complex information-retrieval tasks. They can accurately address clinical inquiries, analyze vignettes with contextual reasoning, simulate empathetic physician–patient dialogue, tailor explanations to diverse literacy levels, and facilitate documentation from unstructured free-text inputs.11 Models such as GPT-4 and Med-PaLM 2 have demonstrated performance on par with medical trainees on standardized assessments, including United States Medical Licensing Examination Step 1 and 2, and long-form board-style questions.12,13 Yet, these prevailing tools often face restrictions in token capacity, context-window length, and cross-source synthesis, precluding a comprehensive review of a patient’s full longitudinal record, especially when extensive unstructured notes or scanned documents are involved.

OphthoChat overcomes these constraints by aggregating a patient’s entire chart to respond to high-level clinical questions in real time, with each answer grounded by in-line citations. In our evaluation, OphthoChat was benchmarked against 1000 clinician-authored queries, encompassing simple data extraction through advanced narrative inference. The system achieved 96% overall accuracy, 93% sensitivity, and 97% specificity across varying query difficulties. Even for the most complex queries requiring longitudinal reasoning or interpretation of ambiguous documentation, accuracy remained at 91%. Median response times ranged from 31 to 41 seconds (mean: 35 seconds per query), representing an approximately 11-fold speed improvement over manual review (6.6 minutes per query). OphthoChat’s automated approach substantially lowers the cost of chart review compared with manual chart review processes, which typically exceed USD $50 per chart.14 Even accounting for infrastructure and licensing fees, our internal estimates suggest OphthoChat can process a patient’s entire chart for less than $5 per review, an approximate 90% reduction. Applied to a batch of 1000 charts, this efficiency could yield savings of around tens of thousands of dollars, allowing existing resources and clinician effort to be redirected toward higher-value activities.

Although AI-driven chatbot tools for patient chart review have been explored in specialties such as radiology and pathology, most prior systems rely on structured data fields or rule-based keyword matching and report lower throughput or a narrower scope. For example, PathChat is a generative AI system used in pathology that identifies diseases from biopsy slides with high accuracy while supporting interactive, multiturn conversations.15 Radiology AI assistants have also demonstrated high performance on specific report-extraction tasks, but remain tied to predefined templates.16 In contrast, OphthoChat’s 96% accuracy across heterogeneous query types and its ability to synthesize free-text narratives represent a substantial advancement over earlier efforts.

Key use cases for OphthoChat span the full spectrum of clinical and research workflows. During patient visits, OphthoChat can deliver in-clinic decision support by surfacing high-risk features, summarizing recent imaging findings, and offering evidence-based management suggestions in real time. As a clinical research aid, it can automatically compile each patient’s longitudinal history, capturing visual acuity trends, prior interventions, and medication adherence, so that clinicians can begin encounters with a comprehensive, up-to-date overview. For clinical trials, OphthoChat can significantly reduce the burden of cohort identification, helping researchers seamlessly verify whether an individual patient meets specific cohort criteria via simple, code-free queries against EMR data, significantly streamlining study enrollment.

Although optimized for ophthalmology, OphthoChat's underlying RAG architecture is inherently adaptable. With domain-specific retraining on specialty-relevant corpora, similar conversational assistants could be deployed in cardiology, oncology, or primary care to accelerate chart review across medicine.

Study Limitations

Despite significant advancements in natural language processing-powered AI chatbots, limitations persist. A well-documented concern with LLMs and AI chatbots is the potential to generate “hallucinations” or overly confident responses, particularly in rare or ambiguous clinical scenarios.17 To mitigate model safety risks, particularly the risk of hallucinations, we implemented safeguards designed to minimize unsupported fabrications. OphthoChat uses a RAG pipeline that grounds responses in retrieved patient-chart text and requires source-linked citations, reducing reliance on pretrained knowledge. We also used a low temperature setting to reduce output variability and discourage speculative generations.10 In addition, OphthoChat applies an agreement-derived certainty threshold with an explicit abstention mechanism, prompting the system to withhold an answer when available chart evidence is insufficient rather than generate an unsupported response. To facilitate oversight and auditability, each output is returned with the corresponding source text and its location in the chart for rapid clinician verification. Across our evaluation, we did not observe unsupported, citation-misaligned assertions (“substantive hallucinations”) under our operational definition. We did, however, identify errors made by the model, as previously described in the Results section, which were attributable to contextual misinterpretations, such as typographical artefacts (misspelled, truncated, and abbreviated words), ambiguous clinical phrasing, and conflicting chart entries. Errors stemmed from data quality or ambiguous user query wording and were not due to fundamental OphthoChat model limitations. Model drift and performance variability are important considerations for future applications, as LLM behavior may change over time with updates to the underlying OpenAI models, potentially affecting reproducibility and reliability. As such, we have documented the version and model date of the LLM we used, but acknowledge that access to it may become unavailable over time. Future implementation must address computational demands, data privacy, and security mandates and evolve with clinical standards. This study was confined to a single institution’s patient population; external validation across diverse patient populations and care settings is necessary.

Ethical Implications

Automation of EMR chart review with tools like OphthoChat offers substantial efficiency gains but introduces ethical challenges. Key concerns include data privacy vulnerabilities, the risk of adversarial attacks, and the amplification of algorithmic biases, especially across race, gender, and health literacy strata.18 Models trained on historical EMRs may inadvertently perpetuate disparities in diagnostic accuracy or risk prediction for under-represented groups who experience disparate care patterns or incomplete documentation.19 For example, patients with fewer documented visits may be more likely to have disease-progression signals missed by the AI model. Additionally, under-represented demographic groups, who often have fewer OCT scans for AMD monitoring, may lead AI systems to underestimate progression or stability. Regular bias audits across demographic subgroups, coupled with corrective measures, are recommended to detect and rectify performance disparities.20 Transparency regarding AI use in both research and practice is essential for accountability. Moreover, even after data sets are deidentified under HIPAA’s Safe Harbor Law, emerging data show that machine learning models are susceptible to cybersecurity threats, such as the exploitation of Application Program Interface endpoints and adversarial manipulation of RAG models, underscoring the necessity of rigorous security and ethical governance before clinical integration.21,22

Beyond technical vulnerabilities, ethical implications of incorporating AI-powered EMR chart review tools into the daily clinical workflow risk include over-reliance on model outputs during high-volume clinics, potential erosion of clinician documentation quality if AI-generated summaries become the default record, and unintended propagation of erroneous AI output in patient charts. From a regulatory standpoint, LLMs like GPT-4 and OphthoChat are purely tools as they stand. They are not yet approved or authorized by the US Food and Drug Administration and should only be used with human-in-the-loop systems. Automated chart-review tools fall within an evolving framework of US Food and Drug Administration oversight for clinical decision support, and institutions must establish clear governance structures that outline audit trails, human-in-the-loop requirements, and criteria for safe model updates. Ensuring alignment with HIPAA, the Office of the National Coordinator for Health Information Technology information-blocking rules, and US Food and Drug Administration guidance for learning algorithms is essential to prevent unintended clinical, legal, and organizational consequences as these systems scale.23

Future Directions

As previously noted, our previously published paper introduced OphthoACR, a powerful chart-review analytics tool that currently requires coding experience to use.6 We are currently embedding OphthoACR’s multistep, advanced cohort review functions directly into OphthoChat’s easily navigable interface. We also aim to validate OphthoChat’s performance across multiple institutions with diverse charting practices and user populations while assessing its practical impact on workflow efficiency and adoption.
