The cascade of care for latent tuberculosis infection in Malaysia: a cohort analysis
Authors: Aslene Siu Tjing Yeoh, Mohd Ihsani Mahmood, Mugilan Ganason, Swee Hung Ang, Patrick Wee Yao Peng, Jinsoo Min, Siti Hafsah Abdul Halim, Asmah Razali, Sheamini Sivasampu, Peter Seah Keng Tok
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to examine the cascade of care for individuals with latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI) in Malaysia and explore the determinants of non-initiation and non-completion of tuberculosis preventive treatment (TPT).MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted using the national LTBI surveillance database, including all individuals diagnosed with LTBI from 2021 to 2022. LTBI was diagnosed based on a positive tuberculin skin test (TST) or interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA). Logistic regression analyses were performed to examine the determinants of TPT non-initiation and non-completion.ResultsOf the 10,820 individuals diagnosed with LTBI, 7,516 (69.5%) initiated TPT; of these, 5,169 (68.8%) completed TPT. Healthcare workers had higher odds of TPT non-initiation (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 1.27; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.10–1.47). Conversely, those aged < 18 years (aOR, 0.78; 95% CI, 0.68–0.88), aged ≥ 65 years (aOR, 0.71; 95% CI, 0.57–0.87), history of TB contact (aOR, 0.49; 95% CI, 0.43–0.56), and diabetes mellitus (aOR, 0.56; 95% CI, 0.39–0.78) had lower odds of non-initiation. Non-Malaysians had increased odds of non-initiation (aOR, 2.89; 95% CI, 2.38–3.52) and non-completion (aOR, 1.48; 95% CI, 1.04–2.08). Individuals who tested only TST-positive also had increased odds of non-initiation (aOR, 2.29; 95% CI, 2.08–2.51) and non-completion (aOR, 1.42; 95% CI, 1.24–1.61) compared to those who underwent IGRA testing. Finally, individuals in congregate settings had lower odds of non-initiation (aOR, 0.10; 95% CI, 0.03–0.23) and non-completion (aOR, 0.30; 95% CI, 0.14–0.56).ConclusionsThis study estimated that two-thirds of individuals with LTBI initiated TPT, and that two-thirds of those who initiated TPT completed it. The identified determinants offer evidence to inform future efforts to improve LTBI programmatic management.Keywords: Latent tuberculosis infection, Tuberculosis, Care cascade, Tuberculosis preventive treatment, Malaysia

Background

Latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI) is a state in which Mycobacterium tuberculosis remains viable without clinical manifestation of active tuberculosis (TB) [1]. Approximately one-quarter of the global population is estimated to be infected [2]. If left untreated, individuals with LTBI have a 5–10% lifetime risk of progressing to active TB disease [3, 4]. As LTBI represents a large reservoir for TB, treating active TB alone is insufficient to end the TB epidemic [5]. The World Health Organization (WHO) End TB Strategy emphasises tuberculosis preventive treatment (TPT) in its first pillar [6], as TPT is 60–90% effective in preventing TB disease among individuals with LTBI [7–9].

Malaysia has an upper moderate burden of TB [10, 11], with an estimated TB incidence of 96 per 100,000 by the WHO in 2024 [12]. Between 1990 and 2019, the LTBI prevalence in Malaysia was modelled to be around 26% [13], consistent with the global estimates of one-quarter of the population being infected [2]. In alignment with the WHO End TB strategy, the Ministry of Health (MOH) Malaysia developed the National Strategic Plan (NSP) to End TB (2021–2030) [10]. Accordingly, the programmatic management of LTBI is among the strategies prioritised in the NSP. Additionally, the Clinical Practice Guidelines (CPG) for the Management of Tuberculosis in Malaysia provide evidence-based recommendations for LTBI management, including LTBI testing and the provision of TPT [14].

Malaysia adopts targeted LTBI screening among at-risk individuals, including contacts of pulmonary TB cases; people living with human immunodeficiency virus (PLHIV); healthcare workers (HCWs); institutionalised inmates; diabetic patients; and patients receiving immunosuppressants, renal replacement therapy, or preparing for organ or haematological transplantation. Individuals diagnosed with LTBI are offered TPT free of charge at MOH healthcare facilities.

Understanding the LTBI cascade of care—from targeted screening to the uptake and completion of TPT—is crucial for identifying determinants of attrition and areas for improvement, to strengthen the programmatic management [15, 16]. However, most of the existing evidence from published studies on the LTBI cascade of care originates from high-income countries (HICs) with a low TB burden, while real-world evidence from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) with moderate and high TB burden remains limited [15, 16]. Existing studies from LMICs primarily focus on subnational settings or specific risk groups, with few providing more comprehensive country-level analyses [17, 18].

To address this evidence gap, we conducted a national-level study to examine the LTBI cascade of care and the determinants of TPT non-initiation and non-completion, to generate real-world evidence that will inform the national TB programme while also being relevant to other LMICs with similar resource settings. To our knowledge, no study has examined the programmatic management of LTBI at the national level in Malaysia, which is an upper-middle-income country [19] with an upper moderate TB burden [10, 11].

Methods

Study design and data source

This was a retrospective cohort study conducted using secondary data from the national LTBI surveillance database, maintained by the National TB Control Programme Malaysia. LTBI notification to the National TB Control Programme is recommended by the national CPG for Management of TB, although it is not legally mandated [14]. The national LTBI surveillance database consolidates reporting of cases from all states in Malaysia, with routine data collection including sociodemographic characteristics, tuberculin skin test (TST) or interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA) results, LTBI risk factors, and TPT initiation and completion status.

LTBI diagnostic criteria

LTBI was diagnosed according to the diagnostic criteria outlined in the national CPG [14]. Individuals were classified as having LTBI if they were asymptomatic, had a normal chest radiograph, and had a positive LTBI test result. For the TST, an induration of ≥ 10 mm was considered positive for most high-risk groups, whereas a threshold of ≥ 5 mm applied to PLHIV, organ transplant recipients, or individuals receiving immunosuppressive therapy [14]. A positive IGRA was defined as reactivity in either of the TB antigen tubes of the QuantiFERON-TB Gold Plus assay [20]. In routine clinical practice, a positive result from either TST or IGRA was considered diagnostic for LTBI once active TB had been excluded [14].

Study population and cohort selection

The study included all individuals meeting the LTBI diagnostic criteria between 1 January 2021 and 31 December 2022. All TPT outcomes data were extracted from the LTBI database at a single time point, 31 December 2023, to allow time for reporting and recording of follow-up outcomes for study individuals. Individuals were excluded if they had abnormal chest radiographs, were diagnosed with active TB at the time of LTBI screening, or had previously received treatment for TB.

TPT regimens and follow-up

Individuals diagnosed with LTBI were offered one of three regimens: three months of combined isoniazid and rifampicin (3 HR), four months of rifampicin monotherapy (4 R), or six months of isoniazid monotherapy (6 H). Child-friendly fixed-dose formulations were not available during the study period. As outlined in the CPG, recommended follow-up visits for individuals on TPT are scheduled at weeks 2–4 after initiation, at treatment completion, and at 3, 9, and 18 months post-TPT to monitor adherence, adverse drug reactions, and signs of active TB [14].

Outcomes of interest

The study examined the LTBI cascade of care in Malaysia, assessing the total number of individuals diagnosed with LTBI and the subsequent rates of (i) TPT initiation (among those diagnosed) and (ii) TPT completion (among those who initiated TPT). TPT initiation was defined as starting the first dose, and TPT completion as taking at least 80% of the prescribed doses within the recommended duration [21].

Covariates of interest

Demographic variables included age at diagnosis (< 18, 18–64, ≥ 65 years), sex, and nationality (Malaysian or non-Malaysian). LTBI risk factors were based on self-reported information. Contacts of TB disease were defined as individuals with close exposure to active TB index cases. HCWs were those diagnosed with LTBI through TB screening protocols (including pre-employment, post-exposure screening, where applicable) and periodic medical examinations (depending on the respective facility risk classification) [22]. Congregate settings included prisons or drug dependency rehabilitation centres, encompassing detainees, inmates, and staff. Comorbidities included human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, dialysis, and diabetes mellitus. Testing modality was classified as “TST-positive only” or “IGRA-positive (with or without a recorded TST)”. The TPT regimen offered (3 HR, 4 R, or 6 H) was also included as a covariate for the analysis of non-completion.

Statistical analysis

Demographic and clinical characteristics were summarised using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and means with standard deviations for continuous variables. The LTBI cascade was examined, starting with all individuals diagnosed with LTBI, followed by the proportions who initiated TPT (among those diagnosed) and completed TPT (among those who initiated TPT).

Univariable and multivariable binary logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify the determinants of TPT non-initiation and non-completion. The non-initiation model included all individuals diagnosed with LTBI. In contrast, the non-completion model included only individuals initiated on TPT who were eligible for outcomes assessment, excluding individuals who developed TB disease while on TPT and those still on treatment at the time of analysis.

All covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and prior literature [15, 23] and entered into the multivariable regression to avoid univariate screening biases [24, 25]. Given the low proportion of missing data, complete-case analysis was performed for multivariable analysis [26]. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). Results were reported as adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals, and statistical significance was set at a two-sided p-value of < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.1) [27].

Results

Study cohort selection

Of 11,273 individuals with a positive TST or IGRA result, 130 with abnormal chest radiographs, 306 with a history of prior TB treatment, and 17 with concurrent active TB disease were excluded. The final study cohort comprised 10,820 individuals diagnosed with LTBI (Fig. 1).

Flow chart of individuals enrolled in the study cohort throughout the cascade of care for LTBI and the reasons for TPT non-initiation and non-completion

Demographic and clinical characteristics of the study cohort

A total of 10,820 individuals met the LTBI diagnostic criteria during the study period (Table 1). The mean age was 36 years (SD = ± 18.0), with females representing slightly more than half of the cohort (54.3%). Most participants were Malaysians (95.3%) and had a history of contact with TB cases (80.2%). HCWs comprised 12.1% of the cohort, and 1.2% resided or worked in congregate settings. A small percentage of individuals had comorbidities, including HIV-positive status (0.2%), undergoing regular dialysis for end-stage renal failure (0.2%), and diabetes mellitus (2.2%). Around two-thirds of individuals (65.8%) had IGRA-positive results. Among those who initiated TPT and were eligible for outcomes assessment, the majority (83.4%) received 6 H, followed by 3 HR (15.3%) and 4 R (1.3%) (Table 2).

Cascade of care for LTBI

There were 7,516 (69.5%) of 10,820 individuals with LTBI initiated TPT. Among those who initiated TPT, 5,169 (68.8%) completed it (Fig. 2).

The LTBI cascade of care for TPT initiation and completion among all individuals with LTBI (N = 10,820). † Percentage calculated among individuals who initiated TPT. ‡ Percentage calculated among all individuals diagnosed with LTBI

Among the 3,304 individuals who did not initiate TPT, more than half had undocumented reasons (55.1%). Documented reasons included refusal of TPT (39.2%), medical reasons (2.9%), loss to follow-up (1.5%), transfer out (0.8%; defined as transfer to another recording or reporting unit and for whom the TPT initiation was not known [14]), and pregnancy (0.5%) (Fig. 1).

Among the 1,600 individuals who did not complete TPT, the majority had undocumented reasons (78.8%), and the documented reasons included side effects of the TPT (17.7%), loss to follow-up (2.1%), refusal to continue TPT (0.8%) and all-cause mortality (0.6%) (Fig. 1).

In multivariable analysis, several determinants were significantly associated with a higher likelihood of not initiating TPT. These included being HCWs (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 1.27; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.10–1.47), non-Malaysians (aOR, 2.89; 95% CI, 2.38–3.52), and having TST-positive only (aOR, 2.29; 95% CI, 2.08–2.51). Compared with individuals aged 18–64 years, those < 18 years (aOR, 0.78; 95% CI, 0.68–0.88), and ≥ 65 years (aOR, 0.71; 95% CI, 0.57–0.87) had lower odds of non-initiation. Other determinants associated with decreased odds of TPT non-initiation included having a history of TB contact (aOR, 0.49; 95% CI, 0.43–0.56), residing or working in congregate settings (aOR, 0.10; 95% CI, 0.03–0.23), and diabetes mellitus (aOR, 0.56; 95% CI, 0.39–0.78). The analysis excluded 667 (6.2%) individuals with incomplete data.

Meanwhile, we identified three variables as significant in the multivariable analysis for TPT non-completion. Non-Malaysians (aOR, 1.48; 95% CI, 1.04–2.08) and those who tested TST-positive only (aOR, 1.42; 95% CI, 1.24–1.61) showed a higher likelihood of not completing TPT. In contrast, being in congregate settings (aOR, 0.30; 95% CI, 0.14–0.56) was associated with lower odds of TPT non-completion. This analysis excluded 646 (9.5%) individuals with incomplete data.

Discussion

This study presents the largest analysis utilising the national LTBI surveillance database, describing the LTBI cascade of care—from diagnosis to TPT initiation and completion—in Malaysia between 2021 and 2022. Most individuals diagnosed with LTBI were contacts of active TB patients (80.2%), with a sizable minority being HCWs (12.1%) and very few from other high-risk groups.

A key finding in our cohort was a TPT initiation rate of 69.5% among all individuals diagnosed with LTBI and a completion rate of 68.8% among those who initiated TPT. These rates were comparable to those reported among LMICs (72% initiation; 52% completion) in a meta-analysis [15]. Although the initiation rate was slightly below the LMIC average, the higher completion rate reflects a relative strength in Malaysia’s system for supporting adherence once TPT is started, likely supported by the structured, regular follow-up schedule implemented in the provision of LTBI care and monitoring [14].

The completion rate observed in our study appears lower than the 90.8% recently reported in one of the states in Malaysia [28]. However, the study in the state of Sabah, Malaysia, focused and reported on TPT completion, whereas our study assessed the cascade from LTBI diagnosis, including both TPT initiation and completion. Although the LTBI surveillance is largely standardised, variations in recording and reporting practices in the database may have contributed to differences in completion rates. Our cascade-based approach provides a more nationally representative estimate, which may benefit policy-level decision-making and guide resource allocation to ensure equitable LTBI care.

Despite our best efforts, most reasons for non-initiation (55.1%) and non-completion (78.8%) were undocumented, contributed in part by the limited healthcare resources for follow-up and record-keeping during the study period, which coincided with the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic. This resonates with observations from other high TB burden settings, where limitations in recording and reporting systems for LTBI have been highlighted as programmatic challenges [29].

Among individuals who did not initiate TPT, 39.2% had “TPT refusal” recorded as the reason, without specification of the exact underlying reason. Themes from a previous locally conducted qualitative study highlighted fear of TPT side effects and low perceived risk of LTBI as barriers to TPT uptake among individuals with LTBI in Malaysia [30]. These findings highlight the need to strengthen health promotion initiatives to improve awareness and knowledge of TPT, in line with strategic interventions outlined in the latest NSP [10].

A total of 283 (17.7%) individuals with documented reasons for TPT non-completion reported experiencing side effects. This finding is consistent with previous evidence that identified side effects as one of the main reasons for stopping TPT once initiated [31]. This observation also reinforces the need for a programmatic, targeted management approach for LTBI, rather than a population-wide LTBI screening strategy [3, 32, 33]. To maximise uptake and adherence, all eligible individuals should be adequately informed about potential side effects and provided with counselling and sufficient support throughout the treatment monitoring phase [3, 14, 31, 32].

In the study’s further analysis, we assessed determinants significantly associated with TPT non-initiation and non-completion and identified several key findings. Individuals in the productive age group (18–64 years) had a higher likelihood of TPT non-initiation compared to younger and older groups (< 18 or ≥ 65 years); however, once TPT was initiated, no significant differences in completion rates were observed across age groups. This contrasts with studies from the Republic of Korea and the United States, which reported lower initiation among individuals aged ≥ 65 years but better completion once treatment was started [34, 35]. The reasons underlying the higher non-initiation observed among the productive age group in our study may be multifactorial, including perceptions of being healthy and at low risk of progressing to TB disease (thus lowering the motivation to initiate TPT) [30, 36], as well as work-related barriers and challenges to adhere to the treatment once initiated [36]. Digital health interventions, such as tele-consultations and mobile reminders, can play a part in reducing in-person visits and help working-age adults to adhere to their TPT without disrupting their daily routines [37].

Another observation is that HCWs in our study were less likely to initiate TPT. This hesitancy is also likely to be multifactorial, possibly influenced by knowledge gaps, physicians not being convinced of the need and benefits of TPT, concerns about side effects, and the busy schedules of HCWs [38]. Addressing HCWs’ hesitancy through educational sessions and dedicated LTBI clinics with trained personnel may help improve TPT uptake among this group [38].

Conversely, our study revealed that contacts of active TB patients had a higher likelihood of TPT initiation. This finding was reassuring, given that this was one of the central target populations recommended for LTBI testing and TPT [14]. The finding also aligns with evidence from other studies, suggesting that greater awareness and perceived threat among this group motivated treatment-seeking behaviour [39–41].

Non-Malaysians in our study cohort had higher odds of TPT non-initiation and non-completion. This finding aligns with evidence from other settings; a meta-analysis of global migrants identified language, socioeconomic, and cultural barriers as significant challenges to TPT adherence [42]. Locally, this pattern was not unique to LTBI, as non-Malaysian active TB patients have been observed to have higher odds of unsuccessful TB treatment outcomes in Malaysia [43]. Although they comprised a small minority (< 5% of the study cohort), efforts to better understand the issues and needs of non-Malaysians with LTBI are warranted to enable responsive strategies.

Individuals diagnosed with LTBI solely based on TST had a higher likelihood of TPT non-initiation and non-completion compared with those who tested IGRA-positive regardless of TST. This may reflect lower confidence in TST results, as IGRA is often perceived to be more reliable. This aligns with a study from the United States that similarly reported a preference for IGRA [35]. In Malaysia, a local qualitative study also identified challenges in evaluating and interpreting TST results as barriers to TPT uptake among individuals with LTBI [30]. Supporting the case for IGRA, a modelling study from Japan demonstrated that IGRA-based screening for TB contacts is more cost-effective than TST, particularly in populations vaccinated with Bacille Calmette–Guérin (BCG), where TST’s higher false-positive rate can result in unnecessary TPT [44]. Consistent with this evidence, Malaysia’s latest NSP outlines efforts to expand IGRA testing capacity and coverage as a strategic intervention [10].

Finally, our study found that individuals in congregate settings were more likely to initiate and complete TPT. This is consistent with findings from a study in Thailand, which reported high TPT initiation (82%) and completion (93%) rates among prisoners [45]. Congregate settings provide a structured environment that enables supervision and monitoring of TPT provision, thereby improving adherence.

In our study, we did not observe a significant association between TPT regimen type and completion rate, which differs from the existing evidence elsewhere favouring shorter, rifamycin-based regimens [46]. However, this finding is highly contextual within the study period, where the older 6 H were still predominantly utilised (83% among those on TPT), while the newly adopted 3 HR were still gaining familiarity following the updated national CPG that adopted 3 HR as the preferred first-line option following the 2020 WHO recommendations on TPT [3, 14, 32]. It could be possible that healthcare providers may have selectively prescribed shorter 3 HR/4 R regimens to patients perceived as having a higher risk of non-adherence, but this requires further investigation beyond the present study’s scope and data availability. Going forward, this observation merits continuing monitoring and assessment as the shorter regimens become more widely prescribed.

Our study draws strength from utilising the national-level LTBI surveillance database to provide, to the best of our knowledge, the largest assessment of the LTBI cascade of care in Malaysia to date. This study covers a two-year LTBI programmatic period (2021–2022), enabling a national-level assessment of TPT outcomes in an upper-middle-income country with an upper moderate TB burden. Study findings also provide insights into determinants of TPT non-initiation and non-completion, which may guide future strategies to improve these outcomes. Considering the lack of similar studies in LMICs, findings from this study offer a benchmark for comparison and insights on the monitoring of LTBI programmatic implementation that may be more relevant to other LMICs.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, because LTBI notification and reporting are not mandated by law, underreporting in the LTBI database utilised is likely, and findings should be interpreted in the context of non-mandatory reporting. Given non-mandatory reporting, cases from public healthcare facilities, and those who are actively monitored, such as contacts of active TB cases and HCWs, may be overrepresented in the present study cohort, which may affect the TPT initiation and completion rates. Future assessments are recommended to address these limitations and ascertain their impacts when more comprehensive data sources are available.

Second, we acknowledge the challenges of working with real-world secondary data sources, which led to limitations in data availability and analysis. This was further compounded by the study period (2021–2022) coinciding with the COVID-19 pandemic, during which diversion of resources to pandemic control was inevitable. As a result, study findings were limited by constraints such as missing information and the unavailability of additional contextual variables, such as socioeconomic factors, that may influence treatment adherence. The high proportion of undocumented reasons for TPT non-initiation (55.1%) and non-completion (78.8%) also illustrated existing operational limitations in routine data collection for surveillance.

Additionally, we acknowledge the limitations of the diagnostic test results for LTBI being used in this study, which were recorded according to local clinical practice, where a positive test for either TST or IGRA would be sufficient. Not all individuals underwent both TST and IGRA, and in many instances, only the positive test that led to the LTBI diagnosis was reported in the database. As a result, the diagnostic categories used in this analysis were based on pragmatic real-world documentation of testing, and we were unable to further explore the extent, and the potential impact of possible discordant test results. Overall, these limitations identify the gaps and underscore the need to strengthen and refine the national LTBI surveillance database reporting system in the future.

Conclusion

Overall, the current study assessed the cascade of care for individuals with LTBI in Malaysia from 2021 to 2022, focusing on TPT initiation, TPT completion, and their respective determinants of attrition. Findings from this national-level LTBI cascade analysis establish a baseline for Malaysia in monitoring progress towards improving LTBI programmatic management, in line with the End TB strategy. Determinants of TPT non-initiation and non-completion, as well as issues and gaps identified in the reporting and documentation for surveillance, may also be relevant to other countries, particularly LMICs with at least a moderate burden of TB, to strengthen their programmatic management of LTBI.
