The cannabinoid receptor 1 suppresses neuropeptide Y transcription in the peripheral nervous system to mediate analgesia
Authors: YongMin Liu, Xue Gong, Yindi Wang, Zongqi Huang, Jiayi Li, Kexing Wan, Wen Liu, Li Kou, Fangfang Bi, Man Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cannabinoid receptor 1 (CB1) mediate cannabinoid-induced analgesia, yet CB1-targeted therapies show poor clinical efficacy against neuropathic pain . The present study aimed to elucidate the molecular mechanism of CB1-mediated analgesia in sensory neurons and to identify potential therapeutic targets. We established a chronic constriction injury (CCI) model in male mice and evaluated pain-related behaviors using von Frey filament and hot plate tests. Transcriptome sequencing was performed to screen differentially expressed genes in the dorsal root ganglia (DRG) following CB1 conditional KO and nerve injury, with key differentially expressed geness validated by quantitative polymerase chain reaction and immunofluorescence. The transcriptional interaction between activating transcription factor 3 (ATF3) and neuropeptide Y (NPY) was further verified using chromatin immunoprecipitation analysis-quantitative polymerase chain reaction and luciferase reporter assays. We demonstrated that sensory neuronal CB1 plays a vital analgesic role under both physiological and neuropathic conditions. CCI-induced CB1 downregulation in the DRG impairs the analgesic efficacy of CB1 agonists. Genetic deletion of CB1 in sensory neuron activates ATF3 and NPY transcription, causing persistent hyperalgesia. After nerve injury, the expression of CB1 is suppressed in DRG, while the transcription of ATF3 and NPY is activated. ATF3 translocates into the nucleus and subsequently drives the transcription of NPY. Consistently, silencing ATF3 or NPY effectively alleviated hyperalgesia induced by CCI or CB1 conditional KO. Collectively, our results indicate that CB1 may exert analgesic effects by indirectly inhibiting the ATF3/NPY signaling cascade. Targeting ATF3/NPY signaling provides a promising alternative therapeutic strategy for neuropathic pain, overcoming the clinical limitations of conventional CB1 agonists.

Chronic neuropathic pain (NP) is a major factor contributing to the global burden of disease (1). Due to limited understanding of the complex neural and immunopathophysiological mechanisms underlying NP, specific treatments for NP are still lacking (2). The endocannabinoid system is a key regulator of nociceptive transmission in the nervous system (3). Substantial evidence indicates that cannabinoids are effective for the treatment of chronic pain in adults (4, 5).

Cannabinoid receptor 1 (CB1) are Gi/o protein-coupled receptors predominantly expressed on neuronal presynaptic membranes and mediate endocannabinoid-induced analgesia (2). Previous studies have shown that CB1 KO in peripheral sensory neurons leads to physiological hyperalgesia and exacerbates NP caused by chronic constriction injury (CCI). These findings suggest that sensory neuronal CB1 plays a critical role in both physiological and pathological pain conditions (6). However, CB1 agonists exert limited analgesic efficacy in NP patients and also trigger severe central nervous system adverse effects.

Activating transcription factor 3 (ATF3) serves as a marker of nerve injury or overactivation (7). It is rapidly induced under stress conditions and modulates the transcription of downstream genes. Neuropeptide Y (NPY) is a highly conserved endogenous peptide in the central and peripheral nervous systems of mammals (8). Accumulating evidence indicates that NPY participates in the modulation of NP following peripheral nerve injury (9, 10). After nerve injury, ATF3 and NPY are robustly produced in the dorsal root ganglia (DRG) and contribute to the onset and progression of NP (11, 12). However, it remains unclear whether ATF3 and NPY interact with CB1, and whether they can act as alternative analgesic targets to CB1.

This study elucidated the important role of sensory neuron CB1 in analgesia, and explored the reasons for the poor analgesic effect of CB1 agonists on NP; Further screen and identify the downstream molecules ATF3 and NPY in the CB1 analgesic pathway, reveal their expression and regulatory relationship in DRG after nerve injury. Finally, it is determined whether intervening in both after nerve injury can exert analgesic effects, so as to overcoming the drawback that CB1 agonists are not ideal targets.

Results

CB1 in sensory neurons modulates basal pain threshold and nerve injury-induced NP

To evaluate whether CB1 plays a role in regulating physiological and NP states, we first established a CCI-induced NP mouse model and examined CB1 expression in the DRG at day 14 post-CCI. Compared with the sham group, CB1 mRNA and protein expression was significantly downregulated in the ipsilateral L4–L6 DRGs of CCI model mice (Fig. 1, A and B, p < 0.05). Beginning on day 8 post-CCI surgery, CCI mice received i.p. injections with CB1 antagonist AM251 or CB1 agonist ACEA once a day for 7 days. The results showed that compared with CCI + vehicle group, AM251 further aggravated NP induced by CCI (Fig. 1C, p < 0.05), whereas ACEA exerted no significant analgesic effect at this therapeutic paradigm (Fig. 1D, p > 0.05). To clarify the role of CB1 in pain regulation in sensory neurons, we generated the mice with CB1 conditional knockout (CB1 cKO) on sensory neurons by crossing the CB1flox/flox mice with AdvillinCreERT mice (sensory neuron specific Cre line) (6). Pain behavioral results revealed that compared with WT + CCI group, the mice of CB1 cKO + CCI group developed more severe mechanical hyperalgesia (Fig. 1, E and F, p < 0.05). Collectively, these results indicate that pharmacological inhibition or genetic ablation of CB1 leads to further aggravation of NP after CCI, supporting a crucial analgesic role for CB1 in sensory neurons following nerve injury. Intriguingly, however, we observed that administration of CB1 agonists exhibited limited analgesic effect at 7 to 14 days following CCI induction.

In addition, we found that even without CCI induction, mice with sensory neuron-specific CB1 KO also exhibited sustained mechanical hyperalgesia (Fig. 1G, p < 0.001; Fig. 1H, p > 0.05). Consistently, naive mice treated with the AM251 also exhibited persistent mechanical hyperalgesia (Fig. 1, I and J, p < 0.05). Overall, these findings suggest that CB1 in sensory neurons participates in regulating both basal pain thresholds and neuropathic pain.

ATF3 and NPY are key molecules downstream of the CB1 signaling pathway

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying CB1-mediated pain regulation, we performed mRNA sequencing on DRG tissues from WT mice and CB1 cKO mice at 14 and 28 days after tamoxifen (TM) induction and CCI surgery. Sequencing data demonstrated no significant difference in ATF3 and NPY mRNA levels between the KO14 and WT14 groups (Fig. 2, A and B, p > 0.05). In contrast, both ATF3 and NPY were significantly upregulated at the mRNA level in the KO28 group relative to the WT28 group (Fig. 2, A and B, p < 0.01). Subsequently, real-time polymerase chain reaction was applied to examine ATF3 and NPY mRNA levels in DRG at different time points after TM induction. It was found that only on the 28th day after TM induction, the ATF3 and NPY mRNA levels in CB1 cKO mice were significantly higher than those in WT mice (Fig. 2, E and F, p < 0.05). Consistent with this finding, we also observed upregulated ATF3 and NPY mRNA in DRG of naive mice treated with the AM251 (Fig. 2G, p < 0.05). These data indicate that ATF3 and NPY are regulated by CB1, and they may be key molecules downstream of the CB1 analgesic signaling pathway. Furthermore, reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis confirmed that CCI surgery further increased ATF3 and NPY mRNA levels compared with sham group in CB1 cKO mice (Fig. 2H, p < 0.05). Collectively, these results demonstrate that ATF3 and NPY expression is regulated both by CB1 signaling and by nerve injury.

Immunofluorescence staining also revealed some interesting phenomena. Under physiological conditions, ATF3 and NPY were almost not expressed in DRG of WT mice. After CB1 cKO, ATF3 was mainly expressed in the cytoplasm rather than in the nucleus (Fig. 2I, indicated by yellow arrows), while NPY was mainly expressed in nerve fibers (Fig. 2I, indicated by white arrows). After CCI modeling, the expressions of NPY and ATF3 in the DRG of CB1 cKO mice increased significantly, and the positions of their expressions also changed. The upregulated ATF3 was mainly expressed in the nucleus (Fig. 2I, indicated by the yellow arrow), while the upregulated NPY was mainly found in the cytoplasm of neurons (Fig. 2I, indicated by the white arrow).

To elucidate the types of neurons with high ATF3 expression after nerve injury, we costained ATF3 with different types of neuronal markers by immunofluorescence (Fig. 3D). Quantitative analysis revealed that approximately 22.2% of CGRP+ (a marker of peptidergic C-fibers and some Aδ-fibers) neuron expressed ATF3, 22% IB4+ (a marker of nonpeptidergic C-fibers) neuron expressed ATF3 respectively, 10.2% of NF200+ (a marker of large-diameter neurons) neuron expressed ATF3 and 5.3% TH+ (a marker of low-threshold C-fibers) neuron expressed ATF3 (Fig. 3D). Within the ATF3+ population, 27% of neurons coexpressed CGRP, 6.3% coexpressed NF200, 5.7% coexpressed IB4+, whereas only 2.8% coexpressed TH (Fig. 3E). Our results show that ATF3 is highly expressed in peptidergic and nonpeptidergic neurons after nerve injury.

To elucidate the types of neurons with high ATF3 expression after nerve injury, we costained NPY with different types of neuronal molecular markers by IF (Fig. 4D). Quantitative analysis revealed that approximately 50.5% of CGRP+ neurons expressed NPY, 26% of NF200+ neurons expressed NPY and 7.3% TH+ neuron expressed NPY, and was almost undetectable in IB4+ neurons. ATF3 is a transcription factor that is induced rapidly after injury (7) and necessary for transcriptional reprogramming of DRG (13). As a transcription factor, whether ATF3 regulates NPY expression is not known. There are abundant colocalization of ATF3 and NPY in DRG neurons after CCI (Fig. 5A). To further elucidate the regulatory relationship between ATF3 and NPY, we infected N2a cells with ATF3 shRNA lentivirus. The results showed that compared with vector group, all lentivirus of ATF3 shRNA could down-regulated ATF3 at mRNA level (Fig. 5B, p < 0.05), but only the No.95 and No.96 ATF3 shRNA significantly down-regulated NPY at mRNA level (Fig. 5C, p < 0.05). However, silencing NPY with NPY shRNA lentivirus significantly down-regulated NPY mRNA in N2a cells (Fig. 5D, p < 0.01), but had no effect on ATF3 mRNA levels (Fig. 5E, p > 0.05). These results indicate that ATF3 may regulate the transcription of NPY, but NPY has no effect on the transcription of ATF3. Moreover, in the luciferase reporter assays, we first knocked down ATF3 in 293T cells, and then transfected them with plasmids carrying luciferase reporter genes with NPY promoters. Compared with vector group, the luciferase activity in the ATF3si group (including ATF3si-94, -95, and -96) was significantly reduced (Fig. 5, F–H, p < 0.05), which further confirms that downregulating ATF3 affects the expression of NPY.

To determine whether NPY is regulated by ATF3 at the animal level, CCI mice were intrathecal injected with lentiviral ATF3 shRNA. Immunofluorescence showed that ATF3 was significantly down-regulated in DRG of CCI + ATF3 shRNA group compared with CCI + vector group (Fig. 5I), indicating that the knockdown effect of ATF3 shRNA was ideal. Meanwhile, NPY in DRG of CCI + ATF3 shRNA group was also significantly lower than that of CCI + vector group (Fig. 5I), showed that silencing of ATF3 could reduce the expression of NPY in DRG. Together, these results indicate that ATF3 may be necessary for NPY synthesis in DRG after nerve injury.

ATF3 cannot independently regulate NPY transcription

Further clarify whether ATF3 regulates the transcription of NPY via chromatin immunoprecipitation analysis (ChIP)-qPCR. The ChIP-qPCR results showed that the NPY promoter region was significantly enriched in the Anti-ATF3 group compared with the Anti-IgG group (Fig. 6A, p < 0.001), confirming that ATF3 directly binds to the NPY promoter and participate in its transcriptional regulation. Subsequently, luciferase reporter assays were carried out to detect the regulatory effect of ATF3 on NPY promoter activity. No obvious change in luciferase activity was observed between the vector group and the ATF3 overexpression group (Fig. 6B, p > 0.05). Discussion

This study clarified that CB1 in DRG sensory neurons exerts endogenous analgesic effects under physiological conditions and after nerve injury. However, the reduced expression of CB1 in DRG after nerve injury may lead to limited efficacy of its agonists in this therapeutic paradigm. We further revealed that ATF3 and NPY may be downstream targets of the CB1 analgesic pathway. Then, elucidated their expression and their regulatory relationship in DRG after nerve injury. Finally, we found that intervening in ATF3 and NPY in DRG can improve NP caused by nerve injury. This strategy effectively solves the problem that CB1 cannot be used as an ideal analgesic target.

The endocannabinoid system plays an important role in regulating the transmission of pain information in the nervous system. Nitin Agarwal et al. also observed sustained tactile pain in mice after selectively knocking out CB1 in nociceptive neurons (expressing the Nav1.8 promoter) (14). This study also observed sustained mechanical hyperalgesia after CB1 KO in sensory neurons, which is consistent with Nitin Agarwal's report. In addition, antagonizing CB1 under physiological conditions also cause sustained hyperalgesia in WT mice. The previous results indicate that CB1 in sensory neurons regulate basal pain threshold under physiological conditions, and antagonizing or knocking out CB1 leads to the loss of their regulatory effect, resulting in physiological hyperalgesia.

Luo et al. found in spinal nerve ligation (SNL) model that peripheral nerve injury induced sustained downregulation of the expression of CB1 in DRG, and CB1 agonists did not exert analgesic effects (15). This study also found that 2 weeks after CCI modeling, the expression of CB1 in the DRG of the affected side of CCI mice was down-regulated, which is consistent with the results of Luo et al. Both CCI and SNL surgeries involve mechanical damage to the peripheral end of DRG neurons, which may lead to sensory neuron damage and dysfunction, resulting in down-regulation of CB1 expression in neuronal bodies. The reduction of CB1 further leads to the weakening of endogenous cannabinoids-mediated analgesic effects (16), causing chronic NP. Angelo Jdayamanne et al. find that the fatty acid amide hydrolase (an endocannabinoids hydrolase) inhibitor URB597 can increase the concentration of anandamide in the body, but it has no analgesic effect on the chronic sciatic nerve partial ligation model (17). This study found that the CB1 agonist ACEA had limited analgesic effect in this CCI model (consistent with the results of Luo et al.), while the CB1 antagonist AM251 could further exacerbate NP. The possible reason is the remaining CB1 may have been fully activated by endogenous cannabinoids after peripheral nerve injury (receptor saturation), so ACEA has no analgesic effect; while AM251 antagonizes CB1, leading to further loss of residual CB1-mediated analgesic effects and exacerbating NP. Furthermore, this was also observed in mice with CB1 KO of sensory neurons, that is, CB1 cKO mice exhibited more severe NP than WT mice after CCI. These results indicate that the remaining CB1 in sensory neurons still exert analgesic effects after nerve injury. However, due to the down-regulation of CB1 expression, further activation of the CB1 may not exert good analgesic effect in this therapeutic paradigm.

Then, we aim to identify the downstream targets of the CB1 analgesic pathway and achieve analgesia by intervening in these targets, thereby addressing the issue that CB1 cannot serve as an analgesic target. In the pain model induced by the cisplatin, activating peripheral CB1 can temporarily relieve hyperalgesia and reduce the upregulation of ATF3 and TRPV1 expression induced by cisplatin in DRG neurons (18). AM4113 (CB1 antagonist) treatment increased the binding rate between H3K9/14ac and the NPY promoter region, resulting in increased mRNA and protein levels of NPY in the amygdala (19). Transcriptome sequencing of CB1 cKO mice revealed that ATF3 and NPY may be regulated by CB1. Subsequent experiments also found that knocking out or antagonizing CB1 can upregulate the expression of ATF3 and NPY in DRG, indicating that ATF3 and NPY may be downstream targets of the CB1 analgesic pathway. The specific reason may be that antagonizing or knocking out CB1 may weaken the retrograde inhibitory effect of the endogenous cannabinoid system, leading to deinhibition of ATF3 and an increase in its synthesis, which in turn leads to an increase in the synthesis of pain inducing substances (such as NPY), ultimately resulting in hyperalgesia.

In addition, we also discovered some interesting phenomena. After CCI modeling, ATF3 entered the nucleus in DRG and was highly expressed in the nucleus. Meanwhile, NPY was mainly expressed in the neurons of DRG. However, after CB1 KO, although the expression of ATF3 in DRG was higher than that in the WT group, most of them did not enter the nucleus. Meanwhile, NPY was mainly expressed in nerve fibers. Therefore, although the expression of ATF3 and NPY is elevated in both pain models, their manifestations and the underlying regulatory mechanisms may be different. In single-cell sequencing of various pain models including axonectomy, chemotherapy drugs, and complete Frejner adjuvant modeling, William Renthal et al. found that the transcriptome data of DRG in different pain models vary greatly (13). These findings confirm that the mechanisms driving the sensitization of nosreceptors in neurogenic and other pain models may be different.

ATF3 and NPY are highly expressed in DRG in various neural injury models (12, 20, 21), but there are few reports on whether they interact with each other. Tetsuo Fukuoka et al. found in a rat SNL model that NPY was expressed in ATF3+ large neurons of the affected DRG (22). After KO of ATF3, the increase in NPY transcription levels mediated by polyphenol A also decrease (23). Our results indicate that ATF3 is specifically induced in DRG after nerve injury, then entering the nucleus and exerting a transcriptional activation effect, promoting the expression of NPY. Although ATF3 is involved in the transcriptional activation of NPY, but it does not directly bind to the NPY promoter region to initiate transcription. The transcription factor of NPY may be someone else. Zhou Jun's team found that ATF3 may assist transcription factor JunB, mediate the release of inflammatory factors by macrophages in DRG, and ultimately promote NP (24). Therefore, ATF3 merely acts as a cofactor in the transcriptional activation of NPY after peripheral nerve injury.

Studies have shown that in the model of diabetes peripheral neuropathy, ATF3 KO mice treated with streptozotocin developed hyperglycemia and skin denervation, but do not develop thermal hyperalgesia and mechanical hyperalgesia (25). Our results show that silencing ATF3 in DRG can improve hyperalgesia caused by CCI and CB1 KO, indicating that ATF3 mediates the occurrence and development of NP after nerve injury. However, some studies have shown that ATF3 can promote axonal regeneration (26, 27), has neuroprotective functions (28). Mice with ATF3 KO exhibit poorer neurological function and larger injury areas after spinal cord injury or ischemic stroke (29, 30). Although intervention in ATF3 can exert analgesic effects, it remains to be further confirmed whether silencing ATF3 will delay the repair of nerve damage. Therefore, whether ATF3 can become an ideal analgesic target remains to be verified.

For many years, there has been controversy over whether NPY plays a pronociceptive (31) or antinociceptive role (8, 32) after nerve injury. After peripheral nerve injury, NPY is released from DRG and acts as a chemical mediator for intercellular signal transduction (33). It promotes axonal excitability, causing abnormal spontaneous discharge of injury nerves, leading to NP (34, 35). In SNI model, NPY enhanced the excitability of nociceptors, and local injection of NPY at the sole of the foot could reverse the analgesic effect caused by mTOR ablation (31). We also observed that silencing NPY in DRG can alleviate persistent mechanical hypersensitivity caused by CCI or CB1 cKO, indicating that NPY has a pronociceptive effect after nerve injury. Therefore, intervention with NPY may be a potential therapeutic strategy for NP.

In summary, this study elucidates that inhibiting the activation of ATF3 and the expression of NPY may be one of the key mechanisms by which CB1 exerts analgesic effects in sensory neurons. Targeting ATF3 or NPY to alleviate NP is expected to replace CB1 agonists. Therefore, intervening in downstream targets of the CB1 analgesic pathway may be a new strategy for treating NP.

Experimental procedures

Experimental animals

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of The Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Ethics Approval No. 00553). All mice were housed at 22 to 24 °C and a 12 h light/dark cycle and had free access to food and water. Advillin-CreERT2 (AvCreERT2) mice (Jax Stock No. 026516) express a TM-inducible iCre recombinase directed by mouse Avil (Advillin) promoter elements. When induced by TM, iCre recombinase activity is observed in DRG neurons. Cnr1tm1.2Ltz/J mice (Jax Stock No.036107) CB1foxl/flox mice have loxP sites flanking the entire coding region of the Cnr1 gene. After crossing Advillin-CreerT2 (AvCreerT2) mice and CNR1TM1.2LTZ/J mice for two generations, tissue/cell-specific KO homozygous mice were obtained in the F3 generation after TM induction (the treatment details are shown in drug treatment), which referred to as CB1cKO mice. Adult male C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old, 20–25 g) were purchased from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. WT mice served as controls. All mice underwent baseline pain threshold tests for 3 days before experiments, and received i.p. injection of TM at the same dosage and duration.

Experimental cells

Neuro-2a cells (Procell system) were cultured in minimal essential medium (C12571500BT, GIBCO) with 10% fetal bovine serum (S711-001S, Lonsera) and 1% Penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C, 5% CO2. 293T/17 cells (iCell) were cultured in High-Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (C11995500BT, GIBCO) with 10% fetal bovine serum (S711-001S, Lonsera) and 1% Penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C, 5% CO2.

Identification of transgenic mice

For specific experimental details, please refer to this literature (6).

Drug treatment

TM (Sigma-Aldrich T5648) was dissolved into corn oil, and was injected intraperitoneally once every other day, a total of 5 times, 2 mg per day (10 mg total dose). Subsequent verification and CCI modeling were performed after 14 days after the final injection.

ACEA (Sigma-Aldrich A9719) is CB1 agonists, and AM251 (Sigma-Aldrich A6226) is a highly specific CB1 antagonist. The drug was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide, Tween80, and normal saline (the ratio is 1:2:7).

RNAi lentivirus

The preparation, packaging, and quality testing of RNAi lentivirus clones were all completed by Shanghai Genechem Co.,Ltd. The target lentiviral vector is GV493, and the element sequence is hU6-MCS-CBh-gcGFP-IRES puromycin. The sequence of shRNA was inserted into the MCS polyclonal site of the vector, and finally ATF3 and NPY RNA shRNA lentivirus were synthesized.

Experimental groups

To determine the role of CB1 in NP induced by CCI, 18 WT male mice (WT mice) were randomly divided into CCI + vehicle (dimethyl sulfoxide: Tween80: normal saline = 1:2:7), CCI + 3 mg/kg ACEA groups, CCI + 1 mg/kg AM251 groups (n = 6 at each group).

To clarify the role of CB1 in sensory neurons after nerve injury, 14 CB1 cKO male mice were randomly divided into CB1 cKO + sham group, CB1 cKO + CCI group (n = 7 at each group), and 14 WT mice were randomly divided into WT + sham group, WT + CCI group (n = 7 at each group). CB1 cKO mice have completed TM injection and a 2-weeks waiting period before CCI modeling.

In order to clarify the role of CB1 of sensory neurons in the physiological state, the experiments were divided into WT group and CB1 cKO group (n = 7 at each group). Both groups of mice were given 5 injections of TM, and finally underwent 28 days pain behavior testing.

To observe the effect of antagonizing the CB1 on Naive mice, ten WT male mice were randomly divided into Naïve + vehicle group and Naïve + 1.5 mg/kg AM251 group (n = 5 at each group).

To observe the expression changes of ATF3 and NPY in DRG at different time points after nerve injury, 18 WT male mice were divided into 6 groups (n = 3 at each group). One group was directly sampled without surgery, while the remaining 5 groups underwent CCI surgery for modeling, and samples were collected on the first, third, seventh, 10th, and 14th days after the surgery, respectively.

To determine the role of ATF3 or NPY in DRG after nerve injury, 28 WT male mice were randomly divided into four groups, including Naïve + vector group, Naïve + ATF3/NPY shRNA group, CCI + vector group and CCI + ATF3/NPY shRNA group (n = 7 at each group).

To determine the role of ATF3 or NPY in DRG after CB1 cKO, 12 CB1 cKO male mice divided into CB1 cKO + vector group, CB1 cKO + ATF3/NPY shRNA group (n = 6 at each group).

Behavioral testing

The tactile withdrawal threshold of mice was measured by using the “up-down” method (36). After an acclimation period of 30 min, we stimulated the plantar surface of the left hind paw vertically with a series of von Frey filaments (Stoelting) with logarithmically increasing stiffness. The filament was bent for 5 s to the central plantar surface with sufficient force, and brisk withdrawal or paw flinching was considered as a positive response. The test of tactile withdrawal threshold was repeated two times in each mouse, and the mean value was calculated. The thermal latency of mice was tested by using a hot plate. The surface temperature of the hot plate was maintained at 53 °C. The withdrawal latency started from putting the mouse on the plate and terminated when a quick withdrawal or flick of the paw was observed. Twenty seconds was set as a cut-off time to prevent tissue damage (37). Thermal tests were repeated three times at 10 min intervals, and the mean value was calculated.

CCI model establishment

CB1 cKO mice have received TM induction. Fourteen days after the final TM injection, CB1 cKO mice underwent CCI surgery (38). 0.5% pentobarbital sodium was injected intraperitoneally at a dose of 0.2 ml/10 g body weight. Corneal reflex, muscular tension, respiratory and pain indicators were examined after i.p. injection to ensure the anesthetic effect and the safety of animals. Experiments were conducted after the injection, and the body temperature was maintained by heating pad during anesthesia. The left sciatic nerve was exposed at the midthigh level. Proximal to the sciatic trifurcation, two ligature knots (4–0 chromic gut) were loosely tied with a spacing of approximately 1 mm. However, in the sham-CCI group, muscles were separated and the sciatic nerve was exposed, but not ligated with the chromic gut. The mechanical withdrawal threshold, and thermal withdrawal latency of every mouse were measured as an assessment of nociception on days 0, 3, 5, 7, 10 and 13 after the CCI operation.

Intrathecal injection in mice

The skin over the lower one-third of the mouse back was shaved and sterilized with iodine. Mice were restrained by covering the upper torso with a sterile towel and fixing the iliac crest. Gentle compression on the buttocks arched the L4–L6 vertebral column to expose the lumbar intervertebral space. A syringe was inserted into the L5–L6 interspace at 30 to 45°. Successful spinal canal penetration was confirmed by a distinct breakthrough sensation and instantaneous tail or hindlimb twitching. The syringe was further advanced 2 to 3 mm, held for 30 s, and then slowly withdrawn.

For intrathecal lentivirus transduction, 3 to 5 μl of virus solution was injected per mouse, with the volume adjusted based on viral titer. Injections were administered every other day for a total of two times (39). The ATF3-targeting shRNA (shRNA-96) sequence was 5′-ACCTCCTGGGTCACTGGT, NPY shRNA-57 “CACTGATTTCAGACCTCTTAA”, NPY shRNA-58 “ACACTGATTTCAGACC TCTTA”, and the negative control plasmid sequence was 5′-TTTCCGAACGTGTCACGT-3′.

Real-time polymerase chain reaction

L4-L6 DRGs were harvested and immediately frozen on dry ice and stored at − 80 °C. Total RNA extracted from rapidly frozen DRG tissues was isolated using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen,#15596026CN). To analyze the expression level of CB1R in each group, real-time polymerase chain reaction was performed by using Vazyme SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Perfect Real Time). Reactions were carried out in a total volume of 10 μl, including 1 μl cDNA, 5 μl SYBR Premix Ex Taq TM (2 × ), and 0.2 μl of each specific primer (10 μM), 3.6 μl ddH2Oand run in QuantStudio (TM) Real-Time PCR. The cycling conditions were set 95 °C for 3 min at hold stage; cycles (40 × ) 95 °C for 8 s, 60 °C for 20 s at PCR stage; the last stage is melted curve stage. GAPDH was used for normalization. Data were calculated relative to GAPDH using the 2−▵▵Ct method. The qPCR primer sequences are as follows: GAPDH-forward, GGTTGTCTCCTGCGACTTCA; GAPDH-reverse, TGGTCCAGGGTTTCTTACTCC; Cnr1(CB1)-forward, GCTT GATGTGGACTAT TGCAAT; Cnr1-reverse, TCCAGAACATCAGGTAGGTTTC; ATF3-forward, ACCTCTGCCATCG GATGTCCTC; ATF3-reverse, CGCCTCCTT TTCCTCTCATCTTCTTC; NPY-forward, TGTGTTT GGGCATTCTGGCTGAG; NPY-reverse, TGTAGTGTCGCAGAGCGGAGTAG.

mRNA library constructs and sequencing

In this experiment, 12 CB1cKO mice and 6 WT control mice were randomly divided into six groups (n = 3 per group), namely WT14, WT28, CB1cKO14, CB1cKO28, CB1cKO sham28, and CB1cKO CCI28. All mice received consistent TM induction treatment. For transcriptome analysis, L4-L6 DRG tissues were isolated from CB1cKO and WT mice on days 14 and 28 after the last TM injection. In addition, L4-L6 DRG samples were collected from CB1cKO mice on day 14 post CCI modeling for subsequent RNA sequencing. We compared the sequencing data between WT and CB1 cKO mice under three conditions: TM treatment for 14 days, TM treatment for 28 days, and 28-days TM combined with CCI modeling. Differentially expressed gene sets were identified for each group. Detailed experimental procedures were described in the previous literature (6).

Western blotting

All proteins were extracted from the tissues using a lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The protein samples were separated by electrophoresis on 7.5% to 12.5% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gels, and subsequently transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Millipore Corp.). The membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed milk for 1 h at RT and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody ATF3 (1:1000, abcam, #ab254268), NPY (1:500, CST, #11976), GAPDH (1:20,000, Proteintech, #60004-1). After incubation with the secondary antibody for 1 h, signals were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence western blotting detection system. Images were then acquired with a chemiluminescence imaging system (Bio-Rad).

Immunohistochemistry

Mice were deeply anesthetized with 4% chloralhydrate (10 ml/kg, i.p.). The DRGs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) for 4 to 8 h, and dehydrated in 0.1 M PBS with 20 and 30% sucrose. The fixed DRG were then embedded in OCT (Miles Inc.) and vertically sectioned at 15 μM thickness on a freezing microtome. The sections were collected and mounted on chrome-alum-gelatin-coated slides. After rinsing with 0.1 M PBS, the sections were blocked with 5% donkey serum plus 1.2% Triton X-100 for 2 h at RT. Then the sections were incubated with primary antibodies of rabbit anti-CB1R (1:200, Abcam, #ab137410), ATF3 (1:100, abcam, #ab254268), NPY (1:500, CST, #11976), neurofilament-H (NF200) (1:200, CST, #30564T), calcitonin Gene-Related Peptide (CGRP) (1:200, CST, #14959), tyrosine Hydroxylase Antibody (1:400, NOVUS, #NB300-109), and FITC-conjugated IB4 (10 μg/ml, Sigma-Aldrich) overnight at 4 °C. Binding sites of the primary antibodies were visualized by incubating with AffiniPure donkey anti-rabbit IgG (1:400 dilution, Jackson). Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (BX51, Olympus). Images were analyzed by using NIH Image J software.

ChIP

ChIP was performed using the Pierce Magnetic ChIP Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, # 26157). Cross link cell samples with formaldehyde and lyse cells on ice. Use MNase for enzymatic hydrolysis at 37 °C and sonicate to break up chromatin. Take 10% of the ultrasound treated samples as input samples, add primary antibodies to the remaining samples, and incubate overnight at 4 °C. Add magnetic beads for incubation the next day, then collect the magnetic beads using a magnetic scaffold and wash them three times. Wash the magnetic beads and digest the protein with proteinase K. Finally, perform DNA purification.

Double luciferase reporter gene experiment

293T cells were seeded in 12-well plates and divided into 4 groups, with 3 duplicate Wells in each group. The plasmids used in the luciferase reporter gene experiment were all synthesized and amplified by the Shanghai Genechem Co.,Ltd. The first group was simultaneously transfected with three plasmids, namely renilla luciferase, control-firefly luciferase, and vector (ATF3 control). The second group was simultaneously transfected with three plasmids, namely renilla luciferase, control-firefly luciferase, and ATF3 overexpression. The third group was simultaneously transfected with three plasmids, namely renilla luciferase, NPY promoter-firefly luciferase and the vector (ATF3 control). The fourth group was simultaneously transfected with three plasmids, namely renilla luciferase, NPY promoter-firefly luciferase and ATF3 overexpressed. After transfection for 4 to 6 h, the medium was changed and the cells were collected after 36 h. The samples were processed according to the dual luciferase reporter gene detection kit (Beyotime, RG027), and the bioluminescence was detected by microplate reader. Each sample will detect two results, namely the activity of marine luciferase and firefly luciferase. Standardizing firefly luciferase activity with sea kidney luciferase activity yields the true numerical value.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted with GraphPad Prism 8 software. All data are expressed as means ± SD. ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test was used for pain behavioral data, as well as data shown in Figures 2, A, B, E, F and 6, B–D. One-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test (versus the control group) was applied to data in Figures 3C, 4C and 5, C–H. One-way ANOVA coupled with Bonferroni’s post hoc test was adopted for Figures 2, G, H, 5, F–G and 6, E, F. Two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests were used for comparisons between two groups, including datasets in Figures 2, C, D, 6A, 7C and 8B. Differences with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
