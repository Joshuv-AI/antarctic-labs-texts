Antibacterial activity of promising nanostructured cesium oxide
Authors: Wedam Alghazzawi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs bacterial strains become increasingly resistant to antibiotics, many researchers are seeking novel, effective, and inexpensive antibiotics. Some nanomaterials have been suggested as potential candidates for use as novel antibacterial agents. In this study, cesium oxide nanoparticles were synthesized and preliminarily investigated for their ability to inhibit the growth of bacteria. Although there are few studies on the applications of cesium oxide, it is possible that this oxide could have a substantial impact on biological activities. Cesium oxide nanoparticles were synthesized via fast calcination at 500 °C for 3 h. The composition and morphology of the prepared samples were characterized via X-ray diffraction, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy, and scanning electron microscope analysis, which verified the synthesis of cesium oxide nanoparticles. Agar well diffusion and minimum inhibitory concentration assays were used to study the antibacterial activity of cesium oxide nanoparticles against gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria. The experimental results provide preliminary support for developing cesium oxide nanostructures as antibacterial agents against a wide range of infections and diseases caused by bacteria.Graphical abstract Keywords: Cesium oxide, Nanoparticles, Calcination, Antibacterial activity, Agar well diffusion, Minimum inhibitory concentration

Introduction

Pathogenic bacteria are microorganisms capable of causing several severe diseases in humans [1]. Globally, resistant bacteria are emerging at a rapid pace, posing a threat to the effectiveness of antibiotics, which have revolutionized medicine and prevented countless deaths [2–4]. A report from the World Health Organization in 2021 revealed antimicrobial resistance as one of the top ten worldwide risks to public health [5]. The rise of multidrug-resistant bacteria has rendered existing antibiotics less effective, resulting in treatment failure [6]. Therefore, the development of new antibiotics poses significant challenges. Prompt treatment of the illness induced by these bacteria is therefore imperative. Nanoparticles can be used against antibiotic-resistant bacteria and help to treat fatal bacterial diseases. It is possible to synthesize nanoparticles that eliminate resistant forms of bacteria by disrupting their cell walls or membranes [4]. Nanoparticles have several attributes that render them viable substitutes for conventional antibiotics. The initial factor is the high surface-area-to-volume ratio of nanoparticles, which enhances the contact area with target organisms. In addition, nanoparticles can interact with bacterial cells to regulate the penetration of the cell membrane and disrupt molecular pathways [4, 7].

Inorganic metal oxides have recently been used to control organisms in a range of fields. Reducing inorganic metal oxides to their nanoscale form affects their physical, chemical, and biological properties, providing advantages in various fields [8–10]. Metal oxide nanoparticles have been demonstrated to exhibit a wide range of antibacterial properties because of the ions produced [1, 11, 12]. The emergence of antimicrobial resistance in recent years has drawn increasing attention to metal ions, which have a long history of antibacterial action. These ions contribute to the generation of reactive oxygen species, which can permeate bacterial membranes and damage their DNA, and proteins [1, 13].

A number of metal oxide nanoparticles, including Fe2O3, SnO2, ZnO, NiO, and CuO, have also been investigated for antibacterial activity [14–18]. Nanostructured metal oxides have been extensively investigated because of their wide range of possible applications across various scientific disciplines. Although such nanoparticles are in use, some metal oxide nanoparticles have not yet been well studied. Cesium oxide is an alkali metal oxide semiconductor with the potential to offer a sustainable and viable solution for the management of drug resistance.

Cesium, represented by the symbol Cs, is the chemical element with atomic number 55. It is an alkali metal that is soft and silver-white [19]. Cesium and its compounds can be utilized in catalysts and solar cells [20, 21]. Oxides of cesium include cesium oxide (Cs2O), cesium suboxides (Cs7O and Cs4O), cesium superoxide (Cs2O2), and cesium sesquioxide (Cs2O3). Cesium oxides are considered hygroscopic and unstable when subjected to air [22]. However, studies have nevertheless been conducted on the applications of cesium oxides. For example, cesium oxide is of interest in the photodetection industry because it has a relatively low work function (1 eV) and plays a role in the oxidation of semiconductor surfaces [22]. Furthermore, a study demonstrated the possible utility of the cesium oxide-based nanocomposite Cs2O − MgO/MPC as a potent catalyst for the production of biodiesel from olive oil with remarkable durability, which was successfully accomplished through the use of biogenic synthesis [23]. Moreover, cesium oxide nanoparticles may lead to the development of effective drugs for the treatment of cancer and diabetes. This material may serve as an effective electrochemical sensor for biological molecules. Furthermore, cesium oxide is highly basic and, so localized pH fluctuations and ion exchange reactions can damage microbial membranes, creating adverse microenvironments for bacteria [24]. Additionally, metal ions produced from metal oxides may disrupt bacterial ionic homeostasis and subsequently inhibit bacterial growth, providing a distinct mechanism of action from that of traditional metal oxide nanoparticles, although additional mechanistic studies are needed [1, 7, 25]. The physicochemical properties of cesium oxides make them promising candidates for antibacterial applications. Although substantial research has been conducted on the antimicrobial characteristics of several metal oxide nanoparticles, alkali metal oxides such as cesium oxide nanoparticles have not been sufficiently investigated. In addition, previous studies have assessed the antibacterial efficacy of cesium-based nanomaterials [26, 27], but limited research has been conducted on cesium derivatives [23]. The aim of this preliminary study was to explore the feasibility of synthesizing cesium oxide nanoparticles via calcination, a simple and effective method with readily managed reaction conditions. Cesium oxide nanoparticles were characterized using XRD, FTIR, EDX, and SEM, and evaluated as a potential new candidate for the development of antimicrobial agents against E. coli and S. aureus. To the best of our knowledge, no prior studies have conducted a zone inhibition test and resazurin-based MIC assay to investigate the antibacterial activity of nanostructured cesium oxide synthesized via the calcination technique against pathogenic bacteria. This study could provide new insights into the development of unconventional antimicrobial nanomaterials.

Experimental

Chemicals

The cesium precursor salt utilized was cesium acetate, a 99.9% trace metal base purchased from Aldrich in St. Louis. Muller–Hinton broth and Muller–Hinton agar were obtained from Scharlab S.L., Spain.

Synthesis of cesium oxide nanoparticles

A gram of cesium acetate salt was ground in an agate mortar until it became completely soft. Cesium oxide nanoparticles were subsequently synthesized by subjecting the precursor powders to calcination in a furnace at 500 °C for 3 h. Following calcination, the powder was ground finely for use in characterization.

Characterization of synthesized cesium oxide nanoparticles

The structural properties of the synthesized cesium oxide nanoparticles were studied via X-ray diffraction (XRD) and was obtained using a Rigaku Ultima IV model. Cu was used as the anode of the tube and was monochromatized with a graphite crystal. The Kα value was 0.154 nm. A voltage of 40 kV and a current of 40 mA were used to collect the pattern. A Bruker Alpha II Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer with ATR was used to identify the functional groups in the range of 4000–400 cm− 1. Elemental analysis of the sample was performed via an energy‒dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDX) and scanning electron microscopy (JSM-7600 F; JEOL). The sample was coated with platinum metal for analysis by scanning electron microscopy (SEM).

Antibacterial activity of cesium oxide nanoparticles

The antibacterial activity of the synthesized cesium oxide nanoparticles was evaluated against pathogenic gram-negative and gram-positive bacteria, namely, Escherichia coli ATTC 11775 and Staphylococcus aureus ATTC 12600, respectively. The antibacterial effect of the cesium oxide nanoparticles was assessed through agar well diffusion and minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) methods.

Agar well diffusion method

The agar well diffusion method was used to test the antibacterial activity on a Mueller‒Hinton agar plate. Sterile Petri dishes were each filled with approximately 20 ml of sterile molten Mueller–Hinton agar. One milliliter of 30% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was mixed with cesium oxide nanoparticles to conduct the antibacterial activity test. The effectiveness of different concentrations (100, 200, and 300 mg/ml) of cesium oxide nanoparticles was assessed. A sterile steel cork borer measuring 6 mm was placed on Mueller‒Hinton agar plates. As a negative control, 100 µL of standard DMSO (30%) was used. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for a full day. After the incubation period, the zone of inhibition in each well was measured in millimeters. The experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the average values were used to evaluate the antibacterial activity of the cesium oxide nanoparticles.

Minimum inhibitory concentration method

The MIC assay was used to investigate the inhibitory effect of synthetic cesium oxide nanoparticles on the growth of pathogenic bacteria. The MIC was estimated via the resazurin (0.015%) test. The experiments were run on disposable microtitration 96-well plates. MICs were measured by twofold serial dilutions from standard concentrations of synthesized cesium oxide nanoparticles. For Column 1, 100 µl of cesium oxide nanoparticles (100 mg/ml) was added. For Columns 2–12, 50 µl of Muler–Hinton broth was added via a multichannel pipette. Columns 1–10 were subsequently serially diluted (each well had half the sample concentration of the previous well). After that, all the wells except for those in Column 12 (negative control) were treated with 50 µl of the bacterial suspension. Next, the 96-well microtiter plates were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. After incubation, resazurin was added to each well (20 µl per well) and incubated for an additional 2–4 h. The wells were then visually examined for color changes in comparison to the positive control (Column 11) and negative control. A purple color indicated the absence of bacterial growth, whereas a pink color indicated bacterial growth.

Results and discussion

The typical XRD pattern of the cesium oxide nanoparticles is shown in Fig. 1. The synthesized cesium oxide nanoparticles show multiple characteristic peaks, indicating the presence of a crystalline phase. The highly intense peak at 2θ = 21.46° is the potential assigned to the (301) plane. Other small peaks are also observed at 32.1° and 34.18°, which can be attributed to the (330) and (421) planes of the Cs7O hexagonal structure (PDF no. 28–0328). In addition, other prominent peaks are observed at 2θ values of 13.16°, 24.38°, 27.36, 31.46°, and 33.2°, corresponding to the (003), (012), (006), (104), and (015) planes of the Cs2O rhombohedral phase (PDF no. 09-0104), respectively. There are additional small peaks in the XRD pattern, suggesting the presence of impurities that may have been introduced during the synthesis of cesium oxide nanoparticles or occurred because of their hygroscopic nature [22, 28]. The sensitivity of cesium oxide nanoparticles to ambient conditions, such as moisture and atmospheric CO2, may result in the formation of hydroxide or carbonate phases. This could alter the peak positions and intensity or incorporate additional phases into the XRD pattern.

The XRD pattern of cesium oxide nanoparticles

These findings imply that the cesium oxide nanoparticles synthesized by the calcination process may exist in multiple crystalline forms, possibly including Cs2O and Cs7O, depending on the thermal conditions and atmospheric environment during the synthesis process. The prominent peaks in the XRD pattern validated the crystallinity of the synthesized nanoparticles. The reactivity of the material surface and the production of reactive oxygen species, which are essential components of the antibacterial mechanism of metal oxides, can be influenced by phase heterogeneity [29]. The average crystallite size of the cesium oxide nanoparticles is approximately 28 nm. The nanocrystalline nature is indicated by the presence of distinct peaks in the XRD pattern. The size was determined from the XRD data via the Debye–Scherrer equation (D = 0.9λ/βcos), where D is the cesium oxide nanoparticle crystalline size, λ is the wavelength of Cu Kα radiation at 1.54 Å, θ denotes the Bragg diffraction angle for the (h k l), and β represents the full width at half maximum intensity of the sample’s diffraction peak [30].

The FTIR spectrum of the cesium oxide nanoparticles is shown in Fig. 2. It displays a broad peak at approximately 3110 cm− 1, indicating the stretching of the O-H bond in the hydroxyl group that is attached to the metal oxide surface [31]. The spectrum indicates that water molecules are adsorbed onto the metal surface during production: The band at approximately 1634 cm− 1 corresponds to the bending vibration (H-O-H) of water molecules in hydrated cesium oxide [32, 33]. Furthermore, the presence of atmospheric CO2 molecules on the surface of the cesium oxide nanoparticles may be responsible for the band observed at 2344 cm− 1 [34]. Furthermore, the peaks at 1045 cm− 1 and 1342 cm− 1 correspond to carbonate ion [35, 36], possibly resulting from the hygroscopic nature of cesium oxide nanoparticles, which are susceptible to the adsorption of atmospheric carbon dioxide on their surface following calcination. Moreover, metal‒oxygen interactions may occur in the lower wavenumber bands ranging from 400 to 800 cm− 1 [37, 38]. Additionally, the metal oxide nanoparticles’ stretching and bending vibrations could be related to the peaks at 833 cm− 1, 878 cm− 1, 898 cm− 1, and 989 cm− 1 [39–41].

The FTIR spectra of cesium oxide nanoparticles

The results confirmed the synthesis of cesium oxide nanoparticles and suggested a propensity to absorb H2O and CO2 owing to their hygroscopic nature. This could affect their physicochemical stability and surface reactivity.

EDX was used to confirm the elemental composition of the cesium oxide nanoparticles [Fig. 3]. This analysis revealed the presence of the elements Cs (85.04%) and O (14.96%).

EDX spectrum of cesium oxide nanoparticles

Figure 4 shows SEM images of the cesium oxide nanoparticles at different magnifications. The images show variation in particle sizes and irregular shapes. The average particle size of the cesium oxide nanoparticles is approximately 137 nm. The presence of certain larger particles can be attributed to the aggregation or subsequent overlap of smaller particles. SEM investigations revealed irregular particle morphologies caused by the hygroscopic properties of cesium oxide nanoparticles. A histogram of the particle size distribution obtained from the SEM images also indicated significant variability in the particle size. This broad size distribution is consistent with the observed aggregation and irregular shape [Fig. 4D].

A-C SEM images of cesium oxide nanoparticles at different magnifications and D histogram of the particle size distribution for cesium oxide nanoparticles

Figure 5A and B show the antibacterial activity, expressed as the zone of inhibition in millimeters, of the cesium oxide nanoparticles. Antibacterial activity was evaluated against both gram-positive (S. aureus) and gram-negative (E. coli) bacteria. Compared with that of E. coli, S. aureus exhibited a slightly larger zone of inhibition when treated with cesium oxide nanoparticles at all the tested concentrations. Stronger antibacterial activity is shown by a larger zone of inhibition, which also shows that the tested bacteria are strongly inhibited by cesium oxide nanoparticles. The diameter of the zone of inhibition varies depending on the type of strain, the properties of the nanoparticles, and other factors. The consistently higher inhibition against S. aureus across all doses suggests that gram-positive bacteria may be more vulnerable to cesium oxide nanomaterials, as shown in Fig. 5C. Higher concentrations release more ions, probably increasing the amounts of reactive oxygen species and nanoparticle–cell interactions, thus enhancing bactericidal effects [42].

This differential antibacterial response may be attributed to the structural differences between the cell walls of the two types of bacteria. The gram-positive bacterium S. aureus has a thick peptidoglycan layer and no outer membrane [43–45]. This structure allows metal oxide nanoparticles and their released ions to penetrate the bacteria, exposing them to oxidative damage, protein dysfunction, and structural disturbance. Gram-positive bacteria often show greater inhibitory zones, especially when exposed to reactive nanomaterials [45–48]. On the other hand, gram-negative bacteria have a thin peptidoglycan layer and a protective outer lipid membrane comprising lipopolysaccharides. This outer membrane acts as a permeability barrier that hinders nanoparticles and ROS from entering the bacterial cytoplasm and subsequently interacting with intercellular components [43–45]. As a result, E. coli frequently exhibits reduced susceptibility and smaller inhibition zones in antimicrobial assays [47, 48].

Mechanisms have been suggested for the antibacterial activity of cesium oxide nanoparticles against E. coli and S. aureus. These nanoparticles could exhibit similar behaviors to those of other metal oxide nanoparticles because of their shared characteristics, including small size, high surface area, and antibacterial activity. However, each type of metal oxide nanoparticle has unique properties, making metal oxide nanoparticles a versatile category of materials with diverse applications across various fields. The primary mechanism is the generation of ROS on the nanoparticle surface via surface redox reactions. ROS can induce oxidative stress in bacterial cells, which leads to membrane lipid peroxidation, protein denaturation, and DNA damage, ultimately resulting in cell death [4, 42, 49, 50]. The second proposed mechanism is electrostatic interaction between cesium oxide nanoparticles and the negative charge of the bacterial cell surface, which causes increased membrane permeability and leakage of the intracellular contents [4, 42]. Moreover, in aqueous environments, the dissolution of cesium oxide nanoparticles may release cesium (Cs+) ion [25]. Owing to its similarity to potassium ion, Cs⁺ may compete with K⁺-dependent enzymes and disrupt the osmotic balance, which is essential for bacterial survival [51–54]. Additionally, cesium oxide nanoparticles may interact with DNA and ribosomes, leading to changes in cellular protein deactivation and gene expression [55]. Finally, the presence of these nanoparticles may alter the pH and ionic strength of the bacterial environment [24].

Additionally, the lowest antibacterial concentration at which bacterial growth is inhibited is the MIC value [56]. The MIC values of the synthesized cesium oxide nanoparticles ranged from 100 to 0.195 mg/ml, indicating high antibacterial activity against these two harmful microorganisms [Fig. 6]. Two bacterial strains showed marked sensitivity to cesium oxide nanoparticles, with MIC values ranging from 100 mg/mL to 3.125 mg/mL. Thus, the MIC data confirmed significant antibacterial activity against the two microorganisms tested.

Conclusions

This study examined the antibacterial properties of cesium oxide nanoparticles. An economical and uncomplicated technique was used for the synthesis of cesium oxide nanoplates. The synthesized cesium oxide nanoparticles were verified via XRD, FTIR, EDX, and SEM. The XRD diffractogram indicated the potential synthesis of two forms of cesium oxide nanoparticles. The average size of the nanomaterial crystallites was approximately 28 nm. Additionally, the FTIR analysis revealed Cs–O vibrational characteristics, indicating the adsorption of moisture. This observation may reflect the hygroscopic nature of the cesium oxide nanoparticles. The EDX results also confirmed the presence of cesium oxide nanoparticles. Furthermore, SEM analysis revealed irregular shapes and different sizes of the cesium oxide nanoparticles. The average particle size of the cesium oxide nanoparticles is approximately 137 nm, which illustrates the tendency of the cesium oxide nanoparticles to form aggregates, possibly also because of their hygroscopic properties. These nanoparticles produced a strong zone of inhibition against both E. coli and S. aureus in antibacterial experiments. The antibacterial applications of cesium oxide nanoparticles are significantly affected by their properties. The particles have the potential to be modified for application in the treatment of cancer and diabetes, as well as infectious diseases. However, further investigations are necessary to examine the toxicity of these nanoparticles prior to their approval and use as new antibacterial treatments.
