Proxied therapeutic inhibition on sclerostin and atrial fibrillation risk
Authors: Yu Qian, Peng-Lin Guan, Jingyan Hu, Ming-Yu Han, Yi-Hu Fang, Wei-Lin Wang, Saber Khederzadeh, Yun-Piao Hou, Mo-Chang Qiu, Jian-Hua Gao, Cheng-Da Yuan, Fei Huang, David Karasik, Ching-Lung Cheung, Liu Yang, Yong Wang, Chao Zheng, Lin Bo, Hou-Feng Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPharmacological inhibition of sclerostin (SOST) is clinically applied to treat osteoporosis. However, large-scale randomized controlled trials have reported conflicting findings regarding the cardiovascular effects of SOST inhibition. This study aimed to evaluate whether sustained SOST inhibition, mimicked by instrumental genetic variants, is associated with the altered risk of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs).MethodsThe individual-level genomic data were obtained from the UK Biobank, including 377,585 participants in the genome wide association study (GWAS) for estimated heel bone mineral density (eBMD). Summary-level genetic data for CVDs were retrieved from publicly available GWASs, with sample sizes ranging from 332,477 to 1,030,836 participants. The conditional quantile-quantile (QQ) plot was used to visualize the genetic pleiotropy between circulating SOST, eBMD and CVDs. By integrating genetic, transcriptomic, and proteomic data, Mendelian randomization was performed to assess the relationship between exposures and CVDs.ResultsWe observed polygenic overlap between circulating SOST and eBMD as well as atrial fibrillation (AF). Trans-protein quantitative trait loci (pQTLs) around the B4GALNT3 gene, instead of the genetic variants across the genome, could be instrumental variables to proxy the therapeutic effect of SOST inhibition. Further, increasing level of B4GALNT3 gene expression in several tissues was associated with a decreased level of circulating SOST. Consistently, in vitro evidence validated that B4GALNT3 overexpression significantly decreased SOST protein secretion. Notably, increasing level of B4GALNT3 gene expression in the same tissues was also associated with increased eBMD and higher risk of AF. The proxied SOST inhibition (instrumented by B4GALNT3 genetic variants) had a significant causal effect on increased eBMD (β-coefficient=0.034, SE = 0.007, P = 2.81 × 10−7) and increased risk of AF (OR = 1.353, 95%CI = 1.077–1.701, P = 0.009). The results were replicated in the MR analyses with different instrumental variables based on different linkage disequilibrium (LD) thresholds.ConclusionsThis multi-omics study suggested that reduced circulating SOST was associated with an increased risk of AF, which warrants attention in patients undergoing SOST inhibition treatment.Subject terms: Genetic association study, Atrial fibrillation, Osteoporosis

Introduction

Osteoporosis is a condition characterized by decreased bone mass and a disruption of bone microstructure, resulting in increased susceptibility to fracture and mortality1. In the European Union, the estimated number of people diagnosed with osteoporosis and new osteoporotic fracture cases were 27.5 million and 3.5 million in 2010, respectively2. The high prevalence and incidence of this disease warrant novel therapies for preventing and treating osteoporosis-associated fractures3.

Genome-wide association studies (GWASs) identified the association of genetic variants on WNT pathway members (e.g., SOST and WNT16) with bone mineral density (BMD) in humans4,5. Experimental evidence also shown that the activation of canonical Wnt signaling could increase bone mass and strength6,7. Therefore, therapeutical inhibition on Wnt signaling antagonists, such as sclerostin (SOST), has been developed for the treatment of osteoporosis7,8. Sclerostin, a Dan family glycoprotein encoded by the SOST gene, can inhibit the Wnt pathway signaling in osteoblasts, thus reducing bone formation9,10. Several humanized monoclonal sclerostin antibodies, such as Romosozumab and blosozumab, have been clinically tested11,12. Phase 2 and phase 3 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have consistently shown that romosozumab potentially increased bone mineral density in both men and postmenopausal women13–19. However, the potential cardiovascular adverse effects of romosozumab were observed in some (not all) of the RCTs. The largest-scale phase 3 RCT, FRAME (Fracture Study in Postmenopausal Women with Osteoporosis) study, enrolled 7180 postmenopausal women and found that cardiovascular events were balanced between the participants treated with romosozumab and placebo14. But another large-scale phase 3 RCT, ARCH (Active-Controlled Fracture Study in Postmenopausal Women with Osteoporosis at High Risk) trial, included 4093 postmenopausal women and suggested the potential cardiovascular adverse effect of romosozumab17. Previously, atrial fibrillation (AF) has also been reported in the romosozumab group16. Besides these large-scale trials, other RCTs were conducted under much smaller sample sizes, and the findings were also inconsistent13,15,16,18,19. A meta-analysis of several RCTs suggested that romosozumab therapy might increase the risk of cardiovascular outcomes when the four major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) (cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, stroke, and heart failure) were grouped together20. In fact, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) rejected the Biologics License Application of this drug in June 2019 due to the concern about “an increased risk of serious effects on the heart or circulatory system”21, but in December 2019, EMA approved romosozumab with a warning to exclude patients with a high risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD)22.

Although large-scale and well-designed RCT is still needed to illustrate the adverse effect of romosozumab, an alternative cost-efficient approach is to investigate the therapeutic targets through human genetics by integrating omic data23. Under the hypothesis of “experiments of nature” in human genetics, the naturally occurring mutations in genes that affect the activity of a particular protein target (e.g., SOST) can be used as instrumental variables to evaluate the efficacy and potential toxicity of the corresponding drug23. Under this hypothesis, Bovijn et al. examined the effect of SOST variants on cardiovascular phenotypes and tried to model the cardiovascular effect of the drug24. However, as pointed out by a review25, the genetic evidence provided by Bovijn et al. was not sufficient to illustrate the cardiovascular effect of SOST inhibition. Recently, a GWAS identified 719 genome-wide significant (P < 5 × 10−8) biallelic variants to be associated with circulating SOST protein level, however, none of the single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) reached genome-wide significance within the region of 250 kb upstream or downstream of the SOST gene (the lowest Pvalue was 2.85 × 10−6 for rs374672798)26.

Therefore, in this study, we first examined the genetic pleiotropy between circulating sclerostin and eBMD as well as CVDs to assess the shared genetic architecture of circulating sclerostin protein with eBMD and CVDs. We then integrated genetic, transcriptomic, and proteomic data to explore the causality of SOST protein with eBMD/CVDs to estimate the proxied cardiovascular safety of therapeutical inhibition of this Wnt signaling antagonist.

Materials and methods

Data sources of GWASs

Genotypic and phenotypic data for BMD

The individual-level genomic data were obtained from the UK Biobank (Application 41376), the same dataset as we used in the previous studies27,28. Briefly, the UK Biobank is a large cohort that involved half a million participants. Information on more than 2000 traits, including eBMD, was documented by touch-screen questionnaires, physical measurements, and hospital admission data (Supplementary Table 1). Further, participants were genome-wide genotyped by the UK Biobank Axiom Array. Genotype imputation was conducted by using a combined panel of 1000 Genomes Project (Phase 3) and UK10K project29,30. In this study, we excluded non-European participants (Field ID 21000) and related participants, leaving 379,259 participants who have genotyping data. Additionally, participants without information on main covariates (i.e., sex, age, smoking, alcohol, and body mass index) were excluded (N = 1674) (Supplementary Table 1). The eBMD phenotype in the present study was heel BMD estimated from quantitative ultrasounds.

To estimate the association between genetic variants and eBMD, we conducted the GWAS, based on the UK Biobank dataset. Specifically, we excluded participants with more than 10% missing genotypes, leaving 377,585 participants in the final GWAS analysis. For genotype quality control, we excluded variants with a minor allele frequency <1% and variants with Pvalue for Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium ≤1.0 × 10−6 in all samples. Sex, age, smoking, alcohol, body mass index, and the first 5 principal components were included as covariates in this study. PLINK v2.0 was used to perform this analysis.

GWAS summary statistics for CVDs

The summary-level genetic data for CVDs, including atrial fibrillation (AF), coronary artery disease (CAD), heart failure (HF) and stroke were obtained from publicly available GWASs (Supplementary Table 2)31–34. Detailed information on the summary-level genetic data for these CVDs has been described elsewhere31–34. Briefly, the participants of these GWASs were of European ancestry and the sample size ranged from 332,477 (CAD) to 1,030,836 participants (AF) (Supplementary Table 2).

Expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) and Proteomic dataset

To derive the genetic determinants of expression for genes, we obtained genetic summary data from Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) consortium35. Due to the absence of direct osteoblast or trabecular bone tissue data in GTEx dataset, we analyzed expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) data from three tissue types (Sun-exposed Lower Leg Skin, Tibial Nerve, and Esophagus Mucosa) in the GTEx version 8 dataset, as the expression of these tissues is consistently associated with eBMD. Additionally, to obtain the genetic determinants of circulating SOST, we used the genotype–protein association (protein quantitative trait locus, pQTL) summary statistics released recently by deCODE Genetics26. In this large-scale GWAS of plasma protein, a total of 4907 proteins measured in 35,559 Icelanders using SOMAScan aptamers were included26.

Pleiotropy of SOST protein with eBMD and CVDs

We constructed conditional quantile-quantile (QQ) plots to visualize cross-trait enrichment of SNP associations between circulating SOST and eBMD as well as CVDs. This conditional QQ plot revealed the distribution of Pvalues for the primary phenotype (i.e., eBMD and CVDs) conditioning on the significance of the association with the secondary trait (i.e., circulating SOST) at the level of P < 0.1, P < 0.01, and P < 0.00136. Cross-trait enrichment is reflected by stepwise leftwards deflections away from the null hypothesis line.

Selection and validation of instrumental variables

The selection of genetic variants that were instrumental variables (IVs) of SOST inhibition involved the strategies as the following: (1) Based on the pQTL dataset for circulating SOST26, we selected the biallelic genetic variants associated with circulating SOST at a genome-wide significant level (P < 5 × 10−8) (Supplementary Table 3); (2) Among these genetic variants, we calculated the Wald ratio (i.e., the ratio of genetic association with outcome to the genetic association with exposure) to estimate the association of each pQTL for circulating SOST (i.e., exposure) with eBMD (i.e., outcome). A False discovery rate (FDR)-corrected Pvalue cut-off of 0.01 was used to select genetic variants with an effect on eBMD (Supplementary Table 3); (3) Keep the genetic variants that were inversely associated with eBMD, that is, the allele lowering the level of circulating SOST would increase eBMD, which might proxy the SOST inhibition.

Colocalization analysis

For each SOST-associated (P < 5 × 10−8) independent lead SNP (r2 < 0.1), we validated whether circulating SOST and eBMD share a genetic association signal by conducting colocalization analysis37. Specifically, for each colocalization analysis, we extracted all SNPs available in both circulating SOST and eBMD summary statistics from a region of ±100 kb from the lead SNP for circulating SOST-associated signaling. Based on the coloc R package, we then used the coloc.abf function to colocalize pQTL summary statistics with eBMD summary statistics. The colocalization probability of a shared genetic variant > 90% was considered to be the evidence of colocalization.

Summary-based Mendelian randomization analysis

The genetic variants associated with B4GALNT3 gene expression were used as instrumental variables to assess the effect of exposure (i.e., B4GALNT3 gene expression) on outcomes. The eQTL and GWAS summary-level data were integrated using the summary-based Mendelian randomization (SMR) method38. The cis-eQTLs showing associations of P < 5 × 10−8 in the original tissue were selected as instrumental variables. Linkage clumping was also conducted according to default SMR protocols (--ld-upper-limit 0.9 --ld-lower-limit 0.05 --peqtl-heidi 1.57e-3 --heidi-min-m 3 --heidi-max-m 20 --cis-wind 2000)38.

Mendelian randomization analysis

After the selection and validation of IVs, we pruned the genetic variants with high correlation (r2 > 0.8) within a 500 kb window using the PLINK1.90 clumping method (--clump-r2 0.8, --clump-kb 500). We randomly selected 10,000 unrelated white British individuals from the UK Biobank to calculate the linkage disequilibrium (LD). The genetic variants with the lowest Pvalue were retained for further analyses. We then used these genetic variants as instrumental variables (i.e, primary instrumental variables) to estimate the effect of SOST inhibition on eBMD and CVDs risk, based on the generalized inverse variance-weighted (IVW) method. Since the extra variants explained the additional variation for exposure, the generalized IVW method would provide a result with higher numerical efficiency and stability39. In this method, the linkage disequilibrium matrix of these genetic variants was generated using PLINK1.90 software with the command of --r square. These correlations were flipped by aligning their signs with the effect allele of the genetic association.

Additionally, to assess the robustness of our findings, we re-run the MR analyses with different LD thresholds (R2 < 0.4, R2 < 0.1, and R2 < 0.001) to include the instrumental variables. Specifically, the generalized IVW and weighted median as well as MR-Egger methods were used to analyze the instrumental variables which were clumped using relatively high LD thresholds (i.e., 0.4), while the IVW method was applied for low LD thresholds (i.e., 0.1 and 0.001).

We then performed the weighted median analyses as sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of IVW results40. We further applied the MR-Egger regression to evaluate the presence of horizontal pleiotropy (Pvalue for intercept term <0.05)41. All these two-sample MR analyses were conducted based on the “MendelianRandomization” package in R software42.

In vitro experimental validation

The human osteosarcoma cell line Saos-2 was used for western blot and qPCR assays. Saos-2 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (Sigma-Aldrich), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco) at 37 °C and 5% of CO2. We transfected Saos-2 cells with siRNAs (GenePharma) or lenti-viruses (HanBio) for 36 h and 72 h for qPCR and western blot assays, respectively. Western blot and qPCR assays were performed as previously reported43. Proteins in the culture medium were concentrated using 10 K Amicon Ultra filters (Merck-Millipore). Blots were probed with primary antibodies, including anti-SOST antibody (Proteintech, 21933-1-AP, 1:1000), anti-B4GALNT3 antibody (Proteintech, 24675-1-AP, 1:1000) and anti-GAPDH antibody (Proteintech, 60004-1-Ig, 1:10000). Primers used for qPCR were as follows: B4GALNT3, 5′-CGA GGA CCA CGA CAT TGA CC-3′ (forward) and 5’- CCC GGA ACT CTG AGA GCC A-3′ (reverse); GAPDH, 5′- GGA GCG AGA TCC CTC CAA AAT-3′ (forward) and 5’- GGC TGT TGT CAT ACT TCT CAT GG-3′ (reverse).

Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism software, and the results were given as mean ± S.D. Differences between the groups were analyzed with unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett’s post hoc test when appropriate and as indicated in figure legends. p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Ethics approval and waiver of consent

This study was performed under generic ethical approval obtained by UK Biobank from the National Health Service National Research Ethics Service (approval letter ref 11/NW/0382, 17 June 2011). Additional IRB approval and written informed consent were waived due to the secondary nature of this study.

Results

Polygenic overlaps of circulating SOST with eBMD and atrial fibrillation

We conducted GWAS analysis for eBMD in UK Biobank dataset. After quality control, a total of 5,672,305 genetic variants and 377,585 participants were included in the GWAS of eBMD. We identified 874 independently genome-wide significant genetic variants, which explained 16.6% variance of eBMD (Supplementary Fig. 1). Integrating the GWAS summary data for CVDs and circulating SOST (Supplementary Fig. 2), we estimated the genetic pleiotropy of the five trait pairs (i.e., SOST-eBMD, SOST-AF, SOST-CAD, SOST-HF, and SOST-stroke), respectively. The stratified conditional QQ plot was utilized to visualize the enrichment of association with eBMD and CVDs across varying significance thresholds for circulating SOST. We observed leftward deflected from the expected null line in QQ plot (Fig. 1A), and the reverse conditional QQ plots revealed consistent enrichment in circulating SOST given association with BMD (Fig. 1B), which suggested the existence of polygenic overlap between circulating SOST and eBMD. More importantly, we observed cross-trait enrichment between circulating SOST and AF (Fig. 1C and D), but not with any of the other three CVDs (i.e., HF, CAD, and stroke) (Supplementary Fig. 3), indicating the polygenic overlaps between circulating SOST and AF.

Trans-pQTLs around the B4GALNT3 gene could proxy the therapeutic effect of SOST inhibition

Considering the overlapping genetic architecture between circulating SOST and eBMD, we next tried to select reliable instrumental variables that proxy the treatment with Wnt signaling inhibition for osteoporosis. Based on the pQTL dataset for circulating SOST, a total of 719 genetic variants were significantly (P < 5 × 10−8) associated with circulating SOST, including 8 genomic loci (Supplementary Fig. 2E). By clumping the SNPs at r2 < 0.001 as the instrumental variables (5 SNPs), the MR analysis yielded no evidence of a significant association between genetically predicted circulating SOST and eBMD (β-coefficient = −0.001, SE = 0.011, Pvalue = 0.894 with IVW method), suggesting the circulating SOST level determined by all significant SNPs could not mimic the treatment effect of SOST inhibition.

Of the 719 SNPs, 109 of them were associated with eBMD (FDR-corrected Pvalue for Wald test <0.01) (Supplementary Table 3), with the top SNP at rs215226 (Pvalue = 1.50 × 10−17) (Fig. 2A). Among them, 29 pQTLs were inversely associated with eBMD, that is, the allele lowering the level of circulating SOST would increase eBMD, which might proxy the SOST inhibition. Interestingly, all these 29 pQTLs were located around the B4GALNT3 gene region (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Table 3), with the top pQTLs at rs7294354 (Fig. 2B). In addition, we conducted colocalization analysis to identify whether circulating SOST and eBMD share the same genetic association signal. The results suggested that two regions around the B4GALNT3 gene (with lead SNPs rs11613357 and rs7294354) might possess the same causal variant between circulating SOST and eBMD (colocalization probability of a shared genetic variant = 0.987) (Supplementary Table 4, Fig. 2B, C). These results revealed that trans-pQTLs around the B4GALNT3 gene, instead of the SNPs across the genome, could be instrumental variables to proxy the therapeutic effects of inhibition on SOST.

In vitro validation of the relationship between B4GALNT3 expression and SOST protein

In addition, we found that the SNPs around the B4GALNT3 region were significantly associated with the gene expression of B4GALNT3 in sun-exposed lower leg skin tissue (lead SNP: rs11830324, Pvalue = 1.97 × 10−9) (Fig. 2D) and tibial nerve tissue (lead SNP: rs7306778, Pvalue = 9.21 × 10−25) (Supplementary Fig. 4) as well as esophagus mucosa tissue (lead SNP: rs35393359, Pvalue = 7.66 × 10−16) (Supplementary Fig. 5). By conducting the Summary-based Mendelian randomization, we found that elevated B4GALNT3 gene expression in these tissues was associated with a reduced of circulating SOST level (Esophagus Mucosa: beta = −0.212, P = 2.75 × 10−6; Nerve Tibial: beta = −0.198, P = 5.5 × 10−7; Skin Sun Exposed Lower leg: beta = −0.398, P = 3.35 × 10−5) (Fig. 3A).

To experimentally validate B4GALNT3 as a causal gene for circulating SOST levels, we determined secreted SOST levels in cell culture medium following B4GALNT3 knockdown and overexpression (Fig. 3B–H and Supplementary Fig. 6). Transfection of Saos-2 cells with three designed siRNAs targeting B4GALNT3 identified two effective siRNAs that reduced B4GALNT3 expression at both mRNA and protein levels; these were selected for subsequent assays (Fig. 3C–F and Supplementary Fig. 6). Consistent with our in silico findings, B4GALNT3 knockdown significantly elevated SOST protein secretion from Saos-2 cells, as demonstrated by western blot analysis (Fig. 3D, F, and Supplementary Fig. 6). Furthermore, forced overexpression achieved through lenti-B4GALNT3 infection of Saos-2 cells revealed that B4GALNT3 overexpression significantly decreased SOST protein secretion into the culture medium (Fig. 3G, H and Supplementary Fig. 6).

Proxied therapeutic effect of SOST inhibition and atrial fibrillation risk

Further, the SNPs around the B4GALNT3 region were associated with atrial fibrillation (lead SNP: rs215225, Pvalue = 4.11 × 10–4) (Fig. 2E). Summary-based Mendelian randomization analysis suggested that elevated B4GALNT3 expression in the three tissues was associated with increased eBMD (Esophagus Mucosa: beta = 0.008, P = 1.53 × 10–5; Nerve Tibial: beta = 0.008, P = 5.38 × 10–6; Skin Sun Exposed Lower leg: beta = 0.016, P = 1.00 × 10–4) (Fig. 4A). Additionally, increasing level of B4GALNT3 expression in these tissues was associated with was associated with increased risk of atrial fibrillation (Esophagus Mucosa: OR = 1.203, P = 0.018; Nerve Tibial: OR = 1.185, P = 0.019; Skin Sun Exposed Lower leg: OR = 1.418, P = 0.021). Accordingly, after the clumping process (r2 < 0.8), 13 instrumental variables (IVs) were left in the follow-up MR analyses (Supplementary Table 5). We found that the genetically determined decreasing level of circulating SOST was associated with increased eBMD (β-coefficient=0.034, SE = 0.007, Pvalue = 2.81 × 10–7) and increased risk of AF (OR = 1.353, 95%CI = 1.077–1.701, Pvalue = 0.009) with generalized inverse-variance–weighted (IVW) MR approach (Fig. 4). Consistently, the weighted-median MR analyses also revealed significant associations, (β-coefficient = 0.043, SE = 0.004, P = 4.71 × 10–34 for eBMD; OR = 1.418, 95% CI = 1.270–1.584, P = 6.05 × 10–10 for AF) (Fig. 4), suggesting the robustness of our findings. The pleiotropy tests using the MR-Egger intercept term (P = 0.473 for eBMD; P = 0.128 for AF) were not significant, which implied no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy. Similarly, when strict LD was applied (2 IVs for r2 < 0.1), the genetically determined level of circulating SOST was associated with both eBMD (β-coefficient=0.039, SE = 0.009, P = 2.06 × 10–5) and AF (OR = 1.313, 95% CI = 1.106-1.560, P = 0.002) (Fig. 4A, B and Supplementary Table 6). The results were consistent when setting LD thresholds at 0.4 (5 IVs) and 0.001 (one IV) (Supplementary Table 5 and Supplementary Table 6). The association estimates observed for other CVDs were with confidence intervals across 1 (Supplementary Table 6).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, inhibition of SOST is a new strategy to treat severe osteoporosis in postmenopausal women with high fracture risk. While the RCTs have consistently shown the therapeutic effect on osteoporosis, the potential cardiovascular adverse effect of romosozumab was not consistent in the RCTs14,17. Although well-designed RCT is still needed to illustrate the adverse effect of romosozumab, an alternative cost-efficient way is to evaluate the efficacy and potential toxicity by using human genetic data23. Benefiting from the recent GWAS of circulating SOST26, we first assessed the polygenic overlap between circulating SOST and five outcomes (i.e., eBMD, AF, CAD, HF and stroke), and the results suggested the shared genetic architecture between circulating SOST and eBMD as well as AF. We then identified that trans-pQTLs around the B4GALNT3 gene could be reliable instrumental variables that proxy the treatment of SOST inhibition. By applying the MR approach, we found that increasing level of B4GALNT3 gene expression (instrumented by cis-eQTL) was associated with a decreased level of circulating SOST, similarly, in vitro, the B4GALNT3 overexpression significantly decreased SOST protein secretion. Additionally, elevated B4GALNT3 gene expression was associated with increased eBMD and a higher risk of AF. Finally, the reduced circulating SOST (instrumented by B4GALNT3 variants) was associated with increased BMD and increased risk of AF.

Leveraging the GWAS summary datasets, the genetic pleiotropy analyses could help us to understand the genetic overlap between two traits, and such a pleiotropy would suggest an alteration of one trait in the individuals with another trait44,45. Tanha et al. conducted the genetic pleiotropy analyses for 4625 blood proteins with migraine and suggested that the proteins within the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway could represent therapeutic targets against migraine44. In this study, we demonstrated widespread evidence of genetic overlap with circulating SOST across atrial fibrillation and BMD, suggesting an alteration of the SOST protein level in individuals with atrial fibrillation.

Previously, Bovijn et al. reported that BMD-associated genetic variants near SOST gene were associated with a higher risk of myocardial infarction and/or coronary revascularization and major adverse cardiovascular events, commensurate with the therapeutic effect of romosozumab24. However, only two cis-SNPs (rs7209826 and rs188810925) around the SOST gene region were used, these two genetic variants were associated with the SOST expression, but were not directly associated with cardiovascular phenotypes25. Furthermore, none of the SNPs around SOST gene (250 kb upstream or downstream) reached genome-wide significance in the association with circulating SOST, while there were 719 genome-wide significant biallelic trans-pQLTs26. In our study, independent SNPs were selected as instrumental variables from the 719 biallelic trans-pQTLs for circulating SOST, nevertheless, no evidence was found to suggest an association between circulating SOST and BMD. Among these biallelic trans-pQTLs, 109 of them were determined to have a statistically significant association with BMD, displaying mixed effect directions. Specifically, 80 of 109 SNPs showed a positive association between circulating SOST and BMD, that is, the allele lowering the level of circulating SOST would not increase BMD, which might not proxy the SOST inhibition. And the remaining 29 pQTLs were inversely associated with BMD, interestingly, all these 29 pQTLs were located around the B4GALNT3 gene region. Additionally, among all circulating SOST-associated signals, only the B4GALNT3 gene region had colocalization evidence for sharing the same variant. Consistently, we found that the increasing expression of B4GALNT3 gene (instrumented by cis-eQTLs) was associated with lower circulating sclerostin levels, higher BMD and higher risk of atrial fibrillation. All these findings suggested that trans-pQTLs around the B4GALNT3 gene, instead of the SNPs across the genome, could be instrumental variables to proxy the therapeutic effects of inhibition on SOST.

In our study, we found that after aggressive pruning (R2 < 0.001), only a single variant (rs7294354) was included as the instrumental variable in the drug target MR analysis. Including too few variants as the instrumental variable may result in inefficiency46. By increasing the pruning threshold, additional variants were included as instrumental variables, which can increase the power to detect a causal effect46 and provide more efficient MR estimates by including all such genetic variants39. However, considering that using high-correlated variants could lead to spurious estimates and inflated Type 1 error rates39, we further incorporated a correlation matrix and performed the generalized IVW method to estimate the association between SOST inhibition and BMD as well as AF. Accordingly, in this study, to obtain a robustness and efficient result, the trans-pQTLs around the B4GALNT3 gene were pruned at different LD thresholds (R2 < 0.8, R2 < 0.4, R2 < 0.1 and R2 < 0.001), then the MR analyses were conducted using multiple instrumental variables. Results from all MR analyses consistently supported that SOST inhibition (instrumented by trans-pQTLs around the B4GALNT3 gene) was associated with increased BMD, suggesting the robustness of our findings. This inverse association is also consistent with the therapeutic effect of SOST inhibition reported in previous RCTs13–19. Furthermore, we found that genetically determined SOST inhibition was associated with an increased risk of atrial fibrillation, which was also replicated in MR analyses using other instrumental variable sets. In fact, atrial fibrillation was reported in the romosozumab group in previous RCT, but further details were not available16. The first post-marketing case demonstrating atrial fibrillation was reported in a 78-year-old female after 3 months of romosozumab treatment47. Another case was observed in a 70-year-old woman, who developed new onset atrial fibrillation after 6 months of romosozumab48.

The Wnt signaling pathway is critically involved in cardiac development, cardiomyocyte differentiation, and the maintenance of adult cardiac function49. As a natural inhibitor of Wnt pathway, SOST may indirectly modulate cardiac conduction properties by regulating intercellular junction proteins, ion channel expression, and myocardial fibrosis processes. Notably, excessive Wnt signaling activation has been linked to adverse cardiovascular pathologies50, and SOST, acting as a key negative regulator of canonical Wnt signaling, likely influences vascular calcification progression51. Given that vascular calcification is associated with an elevated risk of incident atrial fibrillation (AF)52,53, and vascular calcium deposition plays a fundamental role in both the induction and maintenance of AF54, disruptions in Ca²⁺homeostasis—such as mitochondrial dysfunction, Ca²⁺ leakage, and cardiomyocyte apoptosis—may further drive AF progression and atrial remodeling55.

There were several limitations in this study. First, due to the absence of direct osteoblast or trabecular bone eQTL data in GTEx datasets, we used other tissues as proxy tissue, which may not fully capture bone-specific regulatory mechanisms. Second, although other CVDs did not show significant results in MR analyses, we could not rule out their association with SOST inhibition, since the patients with atrial fibrillation were more likely to develop CAD56, stroke57, and heart failure58. This null result may also reflect limited power in stroke/CHD GWAS (explaining only 0.6-1.8% and 8.5% heritability, respectively) compared to AF (11.2%).

In summary, our study uncovered shared genetic architecture between circulating SOST and atrial fibrillation. It is suggested that the B4GALNT3 gene might play an important role in regulating the circulating SOST and atrial fibrillation. The multi-omics study provided evidence to support the causal effect of SOST inhibition on atrial fibrillation, which warrants attention in patients undergoing SOST inhibition treatment.
