Efficacy of clonidine and melatonin for treating insomnia in anxious children: a randomized comparison
Authors: Ali Jalali Firouzkohi, Helen Behmanesh, Mona Doostizadeh, Alireza Soltanian, Tahere Etesamifard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClonidine and melatonin are two medications that are used off-label in the management of sleep disorders in children. However, there is currently no well-established and effective treatment for sleep disturbances among pediatric patients with anxiety disorders.Materials and methodsIn this study, 72 children aged 6 to 12 years diagnosed with anxiety disorders and suffering from insomnia who attended the Farshchian Hospital Clinic in Hamadan between October 2022 and July 2023 were enrolled. Participants were randomly assigned, using simple randomization, into three groups of 24 each to receive clonidine, melatonin, or placebo. Sleep quality was assessed using the Children’s Sleep Habits Questionnaire (CSHQ) at baseline, week 4, and week 6. Both participants and investigators were blinded to the treatment allocation. This study registered with the Iranian Clinical Trials Registry (IRCT20220820055756N1, registered October 16, 2022).ResultsAll 72 participants completed the study. Children receiving clonidine or melatonin showed significant improvement in sleep habits compared with the placebo group at the end of six weeks (P < 0.001). However, there was no significant difference between clonidine and melatonin in terms of sleep quality improvement at week six (P = 0.267). Notably, the incidence of adverse effects did not differ significantly among the three groups.ConclusionThe findings of the present study indicate that both clonidine and melatonin are effective treatments for sleep disturbances in children with anxiety disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06611-1.

Introduction

Anxiety disorders are among the most common psychiatric problems in children and adolescents and can profoundly affect multiple aspects of an individual’s life. These disorders are characterized by symptoms such as excessive anxiety, persistent worry, irrational fears, and physical manifestations that can lead to reduced quality of life, impaired academic and social functioning, and familial difficulties [1]. Anxiety disorders rank as the sixth leading cause of disability in high-, middle-, and low-income countries [2]. The most prevalent types of anxiety disorders in children include social anxiety disorder (SoAD), separation anxiety disorder (SeAD), generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), and specific phobias [3].

In Iranian children and adolescents, the lifetime prevalence of generalized anxiety disorder has been reported to range between 2.4% and 2.8%, with higher rates observed among females and adolescents aged 15–18 years. Notably, children whose mothers have only primary-level education tend to experience higher levels of anxiety compared to others [4]. The prevalence of separation anxiety disorder among Iranian children and adolescents is approximately 5.3%, with the highest incidence observed among children aged 6–9 years [5]. According to a 2014 systematic review, the prevalence of anxiety disorders among Iranian children and adolescents varies widely—from 6.8% in Saravan to as high as 85% in Bandar Abbas [1]. The burden of anxiety disorders has been estimated at 390 cases per 100,000 population, with the greatest impact observed in individuals aged 15–34 years [2].

One of the most common problems in children with anxiety disorders is sleep disturbance, particularly insomnia, which plays a significant role in exacerbating anxiety symptoms. Insomnia can lead to decreased concentration, increased irritability, poor academic performance, and behavioral problems; thus, timely and appropriate management is crucial [6]. Improving sleep quality in anxious children can substantially reduce the severity of anxiety symptoms and enhance their daily functioning. Although cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) and psychotherapy are considered the first-line treatments for childhood anxiety disorders, pharmacological interventions may be required in cases involving severe sleep disturbances [7]. Such pharmacologic treatments must be prescribed carefully under specialist supervision to minimize potential adverse effects.

Clonidine and melatonin are two medications frequently used “off-label” for managing insomnia in children, especially those with anxiety. Clonidine, an α2-adrenergic agonist, acts by reducing sympathetic activity and exerting sedative effects on the central nervous system, thereby improving sleep quality without the need for benzodiazepines [8]. Melatonin, a neurohormone involved in regulating the sleep–wake cycle, shortens sleep onset latency and enhances sleep quality, particularly in children with neurodevelopmental and sleep disorders [9, 10].

Previous studies have shown that melatonin improves sleep quality with minimal adverse effects, making it a promising therapeutic option [11]. Clonidine, due to its sedative and anxiolytic properties, may also be an effective treatment; however, careful monitoring is required to prevent potential side effects such as hypotension [12]. Nevertheless, comparative studies directly evaluating the efficacy and safety of clonidine and melatonin in children with anxiety-related insomnia remain limited.

Given the importance of managing insomnia in children with anxiety disorders and the lack of direct comparative evaluations between clonidine and melatonin, the present study was designed to compare the therapeutic effects of these two agents in children aged 6 to 12 years with anxiety disorders and sleep disturbances. The findings of this research may provide valuable clinical insights for selecting optimal therapeutic approaches, minimizing side effects, and improving the quality of life of these patients.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial was conducted at the Child Psychiatry Department of Sina (Farshchian) Hospital in Hamadan, Iran, from October 2022 to July 2023. The study was designed to compare the efficacy and safety of clonidine and melatonin versus placebo for the treatment of insomnia in children diagnosed with anxiety disorders.

Participants and eligibility criteria

Children aged 6 to 12 years presenting to the clinic with parent-reported anxiety symptoms were screened for eligibility. The diagnostic process was conducted exclusively by a board-certified child and adolescent psychiatry subspecialist who had received formal training in the administration of the Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia-Present and Lifetime Version (K-SADS-PL). This specialist performed a comprehensive evaluation consisting of: (1) a clinical interview based on DSM-5 criteria, followed by (2) a structured K-SADS-PL interview to verify the primary diagnosis and screen for comorbid psychiatric conditions. The Persian version of the K-SADS-PL has established validity and reliability [12]. Insomnia severity was objectively quantified using the parent-reported Children’s Sleep Habits Questionnaire (CSHQ); a score of ≥ 41, representing the validated clinical cutoff, was required for study entry [13].

Inclusion criteria required participants to meet all of the following: (1) age 6–12 years; (2) a confirmed primary diagnosis of one of four specific anxiety disorders—Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD), Social Anxiety Disorder (SoAD), Separation Anxiety Disorder (SeAD), or Specific Phobia (SP)—as established by both the clinical DSM-5 assessment and the K-SADS-PL verification; and (3) clinically significant insomnia, defined by a baseline CSHQ total score ≥ 41.

Participants were excluded from the study if they met any of the following criteria: (1) comorbid major psychiatric disorders, a current or lifetime diagnosis on the K-SADS-PL of any major psychiatric disorder other than the four eligible anxiety disorders (e.g. major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, psychotic disorders such as schizophrenia, or autism spectrum disorder); (2) significant medical or neurodevelopmental conditions, the presence of neurological, significant neurodevelopmental, or chronic medical conditions known to directly cause or exacerbate sleep disturbances (e.g. epilepsy, significant traumatic brain injury, cerebral palsy, or uncontrolled endocrine disorders like hyperthyroidism); (3) other primary sleep disorders, a suspected or diagnosed primary sleep disorder other than insomnia, based on clinical history (e.g. obstructive sleep apnea (evidenced by reported snoring, gasping, or daytime hyper somnolence), restless legs syndrome, or narcolepsy); (4) confounding medications/substances, concurrent use of any psychotropic medications (other than the protocol-mandated fluoxetine), hypnotics, or supplements known to affect sleep or anxiety (e.g. antidepressants (other than fluoxetine), antipsychotics, benzodiazepines, over-the-counter sleep aids (e.g., diphenhydramine), melatonin (if taken regularly), or stimulants).

Interventions and dosing rationale

Following diagnosis, foundational pharmacotherapy for anxiety was initiated with fluoxetine for all participants, starting at 10 mg/day and titrated to 20 mg/day after one week, to standardize background treatment.

Participants were then randomized to receive one of three adjunctive nightly sleep interventions for six weeks: Clonidine group, received 0.1 mg (100 µg) of clonidine orally at bedtime; Melatonin group, received 3 mg of melatonin orally at bedtime; Placebo group, Received an identically appearing placebo at bedtime.

The clonidine dose of 0.1 mg was selected as it represents a common and effective starting dose for pediatric insomnia in the context of neuropsychiatric comorbidities, consistent with doses used in prior studies [14]. The 3 mg dose of melatonin is the standard pediatric dose for sleep initiation. All interventions were prepared and dispensed by the hospital pharmacy to ensure blinding.

Randomization and blinding

After confirming eligibility and obtaining informed consent, participants were randomly allocated in a 1:1:1 ratio to one of three groups: clonidine, melatonin, or placebo. A computer-generated random number sequence was created by a statistician not involved in recruitment or assessment using block randomization (block size of 6). The allocation sequence was concealed using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes (SNOSE) prepared and kept by the hospital pharmacy. Upon enrolling a participant, the research assistant would contact the pharmacy to receive the corresponding pre-packed, identically appearing medication kit (coded to maintain blinding) according to the next number in the sequence.

This was a double-blind study. The participants, their parents, the clinicians administering the interventions, and the outcome assessors were all unaware of treatment assignments until the completion of the final statistical analysis. The pharmacy prepared identical-looking medication kits for all three groups, labeled only with the participant’s study ID.

Outcome measures and assessments

The primary outcome was the change in sleep habits, measured by the total score of the Children’s Sleep Habits Questionnaire (CSHQ). The CSHQ is a 33-item parent-reported instrument with established validity and reliability for Iranian children [13, 15]. It assesses multiple sleep domains, including bedtime resistance, sleep onset delay, and night waking. Lower scores indicate better sleep habits. The CSHQ was administered at baseline (Week 0), Week 4, and Week 6.

The safety and tolerability of the interventions were assessed through active, systematic monitoring at each scheduled follow-up visit (Week 4 and Week 6). During these visits, the research clinician conducted a structured review of systems with the parent or guardian. This review utilized a pre-specified checklist of potential adverse events (AEs) common to the study medications, including drowsiness, dry mouth, dizziness, headache, fatigue, changes in appetite, and nightmares. The checklist was supplemented with an open-ended inquiry to capture any unreported concerns. Furthermore, vital signs (blood pressure and heart rate) were measured and recorded at every clinical visit to monitor for potential cardiovascular effects, particularly in children receiving clonidine. All reported events were documented regardless of suspected relationship to the study drug.

Sample size calculation

Based on an assumed effect size of 0.7, a 95% confidence level, and 80% statistical power, a minimum of 24 participants per group was required (total N = 72). This calculation accounted for three-group comparisons using analysis of variance (ANOVA).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 20 (IBM Corp.). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were compared across groups using one-way ANOVA for continuous variables and Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables.

The primary analysis employed a mixed-model analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Time (Week 0, Week 4, Week 6) as the within-subjects factor and Intervention Group (Clonidine, Melatonin, Placebo) as the between-subjects factor. The primary test was the significance of the Group × Time interaction effect on the total CSHQ score. If this interaction was significant, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted with Bonferroni correction to control for multiple comparisons. These included between-group differences at the primary endpoint (Week 6) and within-group changes from baseline to Week 6 for each active treatment. Adverse event frequencies were compared across groups using Chi-square tests. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for the primary analysis.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hamadan University of Medical Sciences (ethical code: IR.UMSHA.REC.1401.460) and registered with the Iranian Clinical Trials Registry (IRCT20220820055756N1, registered October 16, 2022). Written informed consent was obtained from all parents, who were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without penalty.

Primary outcome analysis

The primary analysis, a mixed-model ANOVA, revealed a significant Group × Time interaction for the total Children’s Sleep Habits Questionnaire (CSHQ) score (F([df], [df]) = [Value], p < 0.001). This indicates that the pattern of change in sleep habits over the 6-week study period differed significantly between the interventions groups.

Post-hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction were conducted following the significant interaction. At the primary endpoint (Week 6), both active treatment groups demonstrated significantly greater improvement in CSHQ scores compared to the placebo group. The mean difference (MD) in CSHQ score at Week 6 was − 13.42 (95% CI: -16.88, -9.96; p < 0.001) for the clonidine group versus placebo, and − 11.83 (95% CI: -15.29, -8.37; p < 0.001) for the melatonin group versus placebo. Within-group comparisons confirmed significant reductions in CSHQ scores from baseline to Week 6 for both the clonidine (p < 0.001) and melatonin (p < 0.001) groups, but not for the placebo group (p = 0.12). Exploratory subgroup analyses

Exploratory analyses were conducted to examine potential differential treatment effects across demographic and clinical subgroups, including gender, age group (6–9 vs. 9–12 years), and anxiety disorder subtype (Generalized Anxiety Disorder, Social Anxiety Disorder, Separation Anxiety Disorder, Specific Phobia). No significant moderating effects were found for any of these variables (all interaction p-values > 0.05). Detailed results of these exploratory subgroup analyses are provided in Supplementary Tables S1-S3.

Safety and tolerability

Regarding safety and tolerability, the most frequently reported adverse event was drowsiness, observed in 20.8% of the clonidine group, 25.0% of the melatonin group, and 16.7% of the placebo group. Other reported effects included dry mouth and fatigue. The incidence of adverse events did not differ significantly among the three groups (all p > 0.05), and no serious adverse events, such as hypotension or bradycardia, were reported (Table 3).

Discussion

Since the meta-analysis conducted by Haugland et al. (2021) confirmed a significant association between anxiety disorders, insomnia, and prolonged sleep onset latency [16], in the present study, the primary analysis using a mixed-model ANOVA demonstrated a significant Group × Time interaction, indicating that both clonidine and melatonin were superior to placebo in improving the sleep quality of children with anxiety disorders over six weeks. Post-hoc comparisons confirmed that both active treatments led to significant improvements compared to placebo at the study endpoint.

The findings of this study are consistent with previous research indicating that melatonin is an effective medication for improving sleep disorders in children [9, 11]. As a natural hormone involved in the regulation of circadian rhythm, melatonin not only reduces sleep onset latency but also enhances sleep continuity and decreases sudden awakenings during the night. Schrader et al. (2021) also reported the positive effects of melatonin on sleep duration and stability in children and adolescents with neurodevelopmental disorders [11]. Other studies have similarly demonstrated that the prolonged-release form of melatonin is effective in improving sleep disorders among various patient populations, including children with neurodevelopmental abnormalities [10].

In contrast, clonidine, through its mechanism as an alpha-2 adrenergic receptor agonist, can improve both sleep disturbances and anxiety symptoms by reducing noradrenergic activity and exerting sedative effects [8]. Jang et al. (2022) demonstrated that clonidine can effectively enhance both the quality and quantity of sleep in children and may be used off-label in pediatric populations [14]. Consistent with the findings of the present study, Bamgbade et al. (2022) also reported that clonidine had a positive effect in reducing sleep onset latency and improving sleep quality among patients with chronic pain [17].

The results of the present study indicated that there was no statistically significant difference between clonidine and melatonin in reducing sleep disorder indices. In other words, both treatments were similarly effective in improving sleep quality and alleviating insomnia-related symptoms in children with anxiety disorders.

This finding holds considerable clinical significance, as it can provide valuable guidance in clinical decision-making regarding the selection of an appropriate medication based on individual patient characteristics, potential side effects, and family preferences. For instance, children or families concerned about the side effects associated with alpha-2 adrenergic agents may prefer melatonin as a safer option with fewer adverse effects, whereas clonidine may be more effective in cases involving a combination of more severe anxiety and sleep disturbances.

From the perspective of side effects, clonidine may cause drowsiness, dizziness, dry mouth, and hypotension, and therefore should be administered under medical supervision. In contrast, melatonin is generally well tolerated, has minimal adverse effects, and is non-habit forming, making it a preferred option [18, 19]. The present study provided reliable data on the side effect profile of these medications; despite reports of drowsiness, dry mouth, and fatigue in some children, no significant differences were observed between the two treatment groups, and no serious or life-threatening adverse effects—such as hypotension or bradycardia—were reported. This aligns with previous studies, supporting the notion that both medications have an acceptable benefit-to-risk ratio in this population [14, 20].

Despite the positive findings, certain limitations of the present study should be considered when interpreting the results. The sample size and the six-week follow-up period may limit the generalizability of the findings to longer durations and broader populations. Additionally, the data collected from parent reports may be subject to subjective bias. Future studies with larger sample sizes, longer-term designs, and more comprehensive assessments encompassing behavioral, cognitive, and psychological aspects of pharmacological interventions could contribute to a deeper understanding of this area. Furthermore, a significant limitation of the present study’s design is the uniform administration of fluoxetine to all participants alongside the investigated sleep aid (clonidine, melatonin, or placebo). While this approach ensured homogeneity in the foundational anxiety treatment across groups, it introduces a major confounder, as fluoxetine itself is known to influence sleep architecture (sometimes improving and occasionally causing or exacerbating insomnia) and anxiety levels. This makes it challenging to precisely isolate and attribute the observed improvements in sleep specifically to clonidine or melatonin. The potential modulating effects of fluoxetine on the response to adjunctive hypnotic treatments represent a significant confounding variable in this trial. To delineate the pure effects of these agents on sleep disturbance in anxious children, future studies might consider designs involving either a population not concurrently on stable SSRI treatment or incorporating a longer stabilization period on fluoxetine prior to initiating the sleep aid intervention.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the findings of this study indicate that both clonidine and melatonin are suitable pharmacological options for the treatment of insomnia in children with anxiety disorders. The choice between these two medications may be guided by individual differences among children, medical conditions, family preferences, and the child’s clinical status. This study can serve as a useful reference for clinicians, child psychiatrists, and families to support more accurate and evidence-based decision-making, ultimately aiming to achieve the best possible sleep quality and overall well-being for children.
