miR-196b inhibits tumor proliferation and metastasis by targeting GATA6 in endometrial cancer
Authors: Zhenzhen Guo, Yangyou Liao, Yijing Yang, Xinrui Li, Ning Wang, Yuhong Shang, Hongxiu Liu, Ying Kong, Linlin Sui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEndometrial cancer (EC) is a common gynecological malignancy with increasing incidence, yet its precise pathogenesis remains unclear. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) have been widely implicated in tumorigenesis. Although studies suggest that miR-196b is closely associated with the progression of various malignancies, its specific biological functions and mechanisms in EC remain to be elucidated.ResultsIn this study, analysis of tissue and serum samples from EC patients revealed that miR-196b is downregulated in EC, whereas GATA6 is upregulated, with a notable negative correlation between their expression levels. In Ishikawa and HEC-1 A EC cell lines, functional assays including CCK-8, colony formation, wound healing, and Transwell experiments revealed that downregulation of miR-196b significantly enhanced cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. A nude mouse xenograft model further confirmed that high miR-196b expression weakened the tumorigenic ability of EC cells. Mechanistically, dual-luciferase reporter assays identified GATA6 as a direct target of miR-196b. Downregulation of miR-196b facilitated the proliferation, metastasis, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) of EC cells by up-regulating GATA6. The miR-196b/GATA6 axis is involved in regulating the activity of the AKT/ERK signaling pathway. Furthermore, through a series of bioinformatics analyses, we found that miR-196b and GATA6 have potential as diagnostic and prognostic markers for EC.ConclusionsThis study elucidates the molecular mechanism by which miR-196b suppresses EC progression by targeting GATA6. These findings provide novel insights into the pathogenesis of EC and highlight the potential of miR-196b/GATA6 as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13062-026-00749-9.Keywords: Endometrial cancer, Tumor proliferation, Metastasis, miR-196b, GATA6

Background

Endometrial cancer (EC) is a common malignancy of the female reproductive system, with its incidence steadily increasing each year [1]. EC typically affects postmenopausal women but can also occur in younger women. Prognostic factors for EC include the FIGO classification, tumor differentiation grade, and estrogen receptor expression [2, 3]. While current treatments, such as hormone therapy, hysterectomy, and a combination of chemotherapy and radiation, are effective for early-stage EC patients. But those patients who with advanced, recurrent, or metastatic EC often face poor treatment outcomes and prognosis [4]. Therefore, understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying EC progression and tumorigenesis, identifying novel diagnostic and prognostic markers, and exploration of novel therapeutic strategies such as targeted drug delivery systems are crucial for improving patient outcomes [5].

MicroRNAs (miRNAs), small non-coding RNAs approximately 22 nucleotides in length, regulate gene expression by binding to the 3’-untranslated regions (3’-UTR) of target mRNAs [6]. Several miRNAs, including miR-145 [7], miR-29b [8], miR-200a, miR-200b [9], and miR-152 [10], have been studied for their roles in EC, yet their specific mechanisms remain largely unclear. The miR-196 family, consisting of three members, has been strongly associated with various malignancies and plays key roles in embryonic development and other processes [11, 12]. In addition to its function in normal cell differentiation, miR-196b has been implicated in cancer proliferation and tumorigenesis. In liver cancer, miR-196b inhibits cell proliferation and induces apoptosis by targeting IGF2BP1 [13]. Furthermore, miR-196b has been identified as a prognostic factor for human laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma, and it promotes tumor progression by targeting SOCS2 [14]. In leukemia, miR-196b directly targets CDKN1B. The suppression of CDKN1B leads to an embryonic stem cell-like signature, reduced leukemia latency, and an increased population of leukemia stem cells in vivo [15]. Our previous study demonstrated that miR-196b inhibits hydatidiform mole proliferation, migration, and invasion through MAP3K1 [16]. However, the function of miR-196b in EC remains to be fully elucidated.

The GATA binding protein family, which consists of zinc finger-containing transcription factors, is essential for organ proliferation, differentiation, and development [17, 18]. The family includes six members (GATA1–6) that share a conserved DNA sequence, (A/T) GATA (A/G). Recent studies have emphasized the involvement of the GATA family in tumorigenesis. For instance, phosphorylation of GATA1 has been associated with breast cancer metastasis and the induction of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [19]. Overexpression of GATA2 promotes tumorigenesis and resistance to therapy in prostate cancer [20]. Mutations of GATA3 have been linked to a growth advantage in breast cancer [21]. GATA4 and GATA5 are potential biomarkers for colorectal [22], gastric [23], and pancreatic cancers [24]. Moreover, GATA6 plays a critical role in the proliferation and migration of various cancers, including cholangiocarcinoma [25], pancreatic [26], gastric [27], and lung cancers [28]. Despite these findings, the role of the GATA family in EC has been less thoroughly explored. Only two studies have reported that GATA4 is associated with EC prognosis [29] and that GATA3 stimulates the proliferation of endometriotic cells [30]. Our research on GATA6 aims to provide new perspectives on the role of the GATA family in EC.

In this study, we discovered that the expression of miR-196b was significantly reduced in EC tissues and patient serum. In both in vivo and in vitro models, ectopic expression of miR-196b inhibited the proliferative, migratory, and invasive abilities of EC cells. Moreover, miR-196b suppressed proliferation, metastasis, and angiogenesis by directly targeting GATA6. There was an inverse relationship between miR-196b and GATA6 expression, and GATA6 expression could rescue the effect of miR-196b. Additional experiments demonstrated that miR-196b could modulate the AKT/ERK signaling pathway, which are key pathways frequently dysregulated in EC progression [31]. Taken together, these results suggest that the miR-196b/GATA6 axis is critical for endometrial tumorigenesis.

Methods

Clinical samples

Patients with histopathologically confirmed endometrial cancer and age-matched healthy controls (no history of malignancy, uterine disease, or hormonal therapy) were enrolled with informed consent. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University (PJ-KS-KY-2022-436). Serum was separated from peripheral blood within 2 h of collection (clotted for 1 h at RT, centrifuged at 1,500 g for 15 min at 4 °C). Total RNA was isolated using the miRNeasy Serum/Plasma Kit (Qiagen). For reproducibility, each serum sample was analyzed in triplicate (independent RNA extractions and qPCR), and the mean value was used for expression analysis.

Human tissue microarray

The EC tissue microarray was purchased from Shanghai Oudu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The immunostaining score was determined by multiplying the staining intensity (0–3) and the extent of staining (0–100) for each tissue point.

Western blotting (WB)

Total protein was extracted using lysates (KeyGen, China) containing a protease and phosphatase inhibitor mix. Protein concentration was measured using the BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE, transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (PALL, UK), and blocked with 5% skim milk for 2 h. Membranes were then incubated with a specific primary antibody overnight at 4℃. After incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at 25℃ for 1 h, the blots were visualized using ECL detection reagents.

Cell culture and plasmid transfection

Ishikawa and HEC-1 A cells (ATCC, USA) were cultured in RPMI-1640 or McCoy’s 5 A (Invitrogen, USA) medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; GIBCO, USA). MiR-196b mimics, anti-miR-196b, GATA6 overexpression plasmids (contains only the GATA6 CDS region and does not include the 3’-UTR) and GATA6 interfering small RNA were purchased from RiboBio (Guangzhou, China) and Gene Pharma (Suzhou, China). Cells at 70% confluence were transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent, and cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (Takara). qPCR was performed on an ABI Step One Plus system, with miR-196b and GATA6 expression normalized to U6 and GAPDH, respectively, and analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Cell proliferation and colony formation assays

For cell proliferation, 5 × 103 cells were seeded in 96-well plates, and CCK-8 reagent (APExBIO, USA) was used to measure cell viability. Colony formation assays were conducted by seeding 3 × 103 cells in 6-well plates and culturing for two weeks. Colonies were fixed with methanol and stained with 0.2% crystal violet.

Cell migration and invasion assays

For the invasion assay, 6 × 10⁴ cells were seeded in the top chamber of a transwell coated with Matrigel (Corning, USA), and the lower chamber contained a 20% FBS medium. After 36 h, cells in the top chamber were fixed in methanol, stained with crystal violet, and photographed. The migration assay was performed similarly, except without Matrigel, using 5 × 10⁴ cells and a 10% FBS medium for 24 h.

Wound healing assay

Cells were cultured to 80% confluence, and a 200 µL sterile pipette tip was used to create a scratch in the cell layer. Images were taken at 0 h and after 48 h of serum-free medium incubation, and cell migration was quantified by comparing the scratch width.

Cell-cycle analysis

Cell-cycle distribution was analyzed using a FACS Calibur flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson). Cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with ethanol, and stained with propidium iodide. Data were analyzed using ModFit software.

Tumorigenicity assays in nude mice

All animal experiments were approved by the Dalian Medical University Ethics Committee (AEE21041). Ten 1-month-old female BALB/c-nude mice were randomly assigned to control (saline) or agomir-miR-196b treatment groups. Mice received subcutaneous injections of 1 × 107 HEC-1 A cells in 100 µL saline. After one week, saline or agomir-miR-196b was injected bi-daily for 20 days. Tumor volume was measured every three days using the formula: (Rmax × Rmin2)/2. On day 28, tumors were harvested, and their weight and volume were recorded.

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were fixed at 60℃ for 3 h, deparaffinized with xylene, and rehydrated in graded alcohol. Antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer at 95 °C for 20 min. After blocking with 3% H2O2 and goat serum, sections were incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4℃. A biotin-streptavidin reaction system was used for detection, and nuclei were stained with hematoxylin. The immunostaining intensity was quantified using the formula: H-score = ∑Pi (i + 1). Where i is the staining intensity (0 to 3), and Pi is the percentage of stained cells.

Double-luciferase reporter assay

HeLa cells (5 × 104) were transfected with 20nM miR-196b mimics or NC and a 10ng firefly luciferase reporter construct containing either wild-type or mutant GATA6 3′-UTR. After 48 h, luciferase activity was measured using a luciferase assay kit (Promega, USA) and an M200 microplate reader (Tecan, Switzerland).

Tubule formation assays

1 × 104 HUVEC cells were seeded on a 96-well plate covered with Matrigel and incubated for 8 h. Tube formation was evaluated using Image-Pro Plus software by measuring tubular length and counting tube and node intersections in five random fields.

5-Ethynyl-2´-deoxyuridine (EdU) assays

Transfected cells were incubated with EdU reagent (Beyotime, China) for 2 h, then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton-X 100. Cells were incubated with Azide-488 for 30 min, and images were captured using an inverted microscope (Olympus Corporation).

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100. After blocking with goat serum, cells were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4℃, followed by FITC-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at 37℃. Nuclei were stained with DAPI, and images were acquired using an Olympus IF microscope.

Bioinformatics analysis

Data on gene expression and clinical characteristics of patients were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival analysis was conducted using the R packages “survival” and “survminer”. Differential analysis was used by the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for two-group comparisons. Correlation analysis was performed by Spearman correlation analysis. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated using the R package “timeROC” to evaluate the diagnostic potential, with higher area under curve (AUC) values indicating better diagnostic accuracy.

Results

miR-196b correlates negatively with GATA6 in human EC tissues and serum

Our previous study identified a connection between miR-196b expression and the proliferative and metastatic capacities of hydatidiform mole [16]. Based on this finding, we hypothesized that miR-196b expression might be associated with the progression of EC. To test this hypothesis, we collected tissue and serum samples from EC patients. The characteristics of which are summarized in Table 1. Through database analysis, we identified GATA6 as a potential target of miR-196b (Fig. 5A). Subsequently, we examined the expression levels of miR-196b and GATA6 in these samples and found that miR-196b was significantly downregulated in both serum and tissue samples from EC patients, while GATA6 was significantly overexpressed in EC tissues compared to paracancerous tissues. Correlation analysis confirmed a negative relationship between GATA6 and miR-196b expression (Fig. 1A-B). Additionally, WB and tissue microarray analyses revealed increased GATA6 expression in EC tissues (Fig. 1Bd, C). Notably, as the pathological stages of EC advanced, GATA6 expression increased (Fig. 1C). These findings suggest that the suppression of miR-196b and subsequent overexpression of GATA6 may contribute to the progression of EC, positioning them as potential biomarkers for diagnosis and therapeutic evaluation.

miR-196b inhibits Ishikawa and HEC-1A cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro

To explore the role of miR-196b in EC, we transiently upregulated miR-196b by using miR-196b mimics and downregulated it by using anti-miR-196b in Ishikawa and HEC-1 A cell lines. The transfection efficiency was confirmed by qPCR (Fig. 2A-B). CCK-8 assays demonstrated that miR-196b mimics significantly reduced the proliferation of EC cells, whereas transfection with anti-miR-196b led to enhanced proliferation (Fig. 2C-D). Similarly, colony formation assays revealed a reduced colony number in the miR-196b mimics group and an increased colony count in the anti-miR-196b group (Fig. 2E-F). Wound healing and Transwell assays demonstrated that miR-196b mimics impaired the migration and invasion of EC cells, while anti-miR-196b enhanced these abilities (Fig. 2G-J). Overall, overexpression of miR-196b suppressed the proliferation, migration, and invasion of EC cells in vitro.

To investigate the mechanism underlying the inhibition of EC proliferation by miR-196b, we analyzed the cell cycle distribution using flow cytometry. MiR-196b mimics slightly reduced the percentage of cells in the G0/G1 phase and significantly increased the percentage in the S phase in both Ishikawa (Fig. 3Aa) and HEC-1 A cells Fig. 3Ca). Conversely, anti-miR-196b decreased the percentage of cells in the S phase, with no significant changes observed in the G2 phase (Fig. 3Ab, Cb). These results indicate that miR-196b mimics induce cell cycle arrest at the S phase.

WB analysis of cell cycle regulators revealed that miR-196b mimics resulted in a downregulation of cyclin A1 and CDK2 and an upregulation of CDKN1A in Ishikawa (Fig. 3Ba) and HEC-1 A cells (Fig. 3Da). The opposite effects were observed after transfection with anti-miR-196b (Fig. 3Bb, Db). Notably, alterations in miR-196b levels did not affect the expression of the DNA damage marker γ-H2AX in EC cells (Supplementary Fig. 1). Collectively, these results suggest that miR-196b induced S-phase arrest in EC cells primarily mediated through direct regulation of the cell cycle, rather than triggering a DNA damage response.

miR-196b suppresses tumor progression and GATA6 expression in vivo

To validate our findings in vivo, we employed xenograft tumor models to assess the influence of miR-196b on EC progression. As depicted in Fig. 4A, compared with the injection of normal HEC-1 A cells, the body weight of mice injected with agomir-196b-treated cells was significantly increased. In comparison to the control groups, the tumor volume and weight in the agomir-196b group were markedly decreased (Fig. 4Ba-c). In line with the results from clinical samples, the expression of GATA6 was substantially lower in the tumors of the agomir-196b group than in the control group (Fig. 4Bd-e). H&E staining demonstrated that the tumor morphology in control mice was disorganized, characterized by an elevated nucleus-to-cytoplasm ratio and prominent nuclear pleomorphism (Fig. 4Bf). Immunohistochemical staining indicated lower levels of GATA6, VEGF, p-AKT, and p-ERK in the agomir-196b group (Fig. 4C). Notably, xenograft models formed by Ishikawa cells treated with agomir-196b injection also demonstrated identical results to those described above (Supplementary Fig. 2).

miR-196b targets and suppresses GATA6 expression in EC cells

Bioinformatics tools (miRanda, TargetScan, PicTar) predicted that GATA6 is a target of miR-196b, since a complementary sequence for miR-196b was identified in the 3’-UTR of GATA6 mRNA (Fig. 5A). qPCR analysis confirmed that miR-196b mimics significantly downregulated GATA6 expression, whereas transfection with anti-miR-196b promoted GATA6 expression (Fig. 5C-D), and this was further confirmed by WB (Fig. 5E-F).

To further validate GATA6 as a direct target of miR-196b, we performed luciferase reporter assays. Reporter plasmids containing the wild-type or mutant 3′-UTR of GATA6 were co-transfected with miR-196b mimics or an empty vector. The luciferase assay showed that when miR-196b was overexpressed, it significantly decreased the luciferase activity in the WT GATA6 3′-UTR reporter plasmids. However, it had no effect on the luciferase activity of the mutant GATA6 3′-UTR reporter plasmids (Fig. 5B). This result provides strong evidence that GATA6 is indeed a direct target of miR-196b.

The miR-196b/GATA6 axis regulates cell proliferation, invasion, and migration in EC

We next investigated whether miR-196b regulates EC proliferation, invasion, and migration by directly targeting GATA6. In order to explore the role of the miR-196b/GATA6 axis, we performed CCK8, colony formation, and EdU assays in Ishikawa and HEC-1 A cells. The results indicated that transfection with anti-miR-196b remarkably promoted the proliferation of both cell lines. On the contrary, when GATA6 was knocked down alone, it resulted in a significant decrease in the proliferation of cells. Moreover, the proliferation enhancement induced by anti-miR-196b was reversed following the knockdown of GATA6 (Fig. 6A-F). Notably, wound healing and Transwell assays revealed that migration and invasion had similar trends as in proliferation. Anti-miR-196b treatment enhanced migration and invasion, while GATA6 knockdown reduced them (Fig. 6G-J). Also, we found that miR-196b inhibited the tube formation capability of HUVEC cells via GATA6 (Supplementary Fig. 3). These findings indicate that the miR-196b/GATA6 axis plays a crucial role in regulating various aspects of tumor development.

The miR-196b/GATA6 axis regulates the EMT process and the activity of the AKT/ERK signaling pathway in EC cells

EMT is a key process in epithelial cancer invasion. To assess the role of the miR-196b/GATA6 axis in regulating EMT, we first evaluated the expression of key EMT-related molecules, such as E-cadherin and Vimentin, using IF (Fig. 7A-B). Both IF and WB analysis revealed that the expression levels of MMP2/9, Snail2, EpCAM, vimentin, and N-cadherin were significantly elevated in cells transfected with anti-miR-196b. In contrast, si-GATA6 transfection resulted in a decrease in the expression of these markers in both Ishikawa and HEC-1 A cells. Interestingly, co-transfection with anti-miR-196b and si-GATA6 reversed these effects. Moreover, E-cadherin expression showed an inverse pattern compared to N-cadherin and other markers (Fig. 7A-C).

C-D: WB analysis of EMT-related markers (C) and AKT and ERK pathway-related proteins (D) in Ishikawa (a) and HEC-1 A (b) cells. (Scale bar: 100 μm, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001)

Several signaling pathways play a crucial role in cancer progression. Our previous results indicated that miR-196b could influence p-AKT and p-ERK in animal tumor xenografts (Fig. 4C). To further investigate this, we examined the activation of the AKT/ERK signaling pathway in Ishikawa and HEC-1 A cells through WB. The data indicated that phosphorylation of AKT and ERK was significantly elevated in cells treated with anti-miR-196b, while the total protein levels of AKT and ERK remained unchanged. Conversely, transfection with si-GATA6 resulted in a significant reduction in the phosphorylation levels of both AKT and ERK. Notably, the enhanced phosphorylation observed with anti-miR-196b was reversed when co-transfected with si-GATA6 (Fig. 7D). Taken together, these findings imply that the miR-196b/GATA6 axis modulates AKT/ERK signaling in EC cells.

Prognostic and diagnostic value of miR-196b and GATA6 in EC

We downloaded transcriptomic data and corresponding clinical information for patients with uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC) from the TCGA database. Patients were initially stratified into high- and low-expression groups based on the median expression levels of miR-196b and GATA6. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that patients with low miR-196b expression exhibited significantly shorter overall survival (OS) (Fig. 8A), whereas those with high GATA6 expression had significantly poorer progression-free survival (PFS) and disease-specific survival (DSS) (Fig. 8B). We further analyzed the relationship between GATA6 expression and clinicopathological characteristics of UCEC patients. As illustrated, GATA6 expression was significantly elevated with increasing tumor grade and clinical stage (Fig. 8C). Within the molecular subtypes of UCEC (POLE-mutant, MSI-H, and p53-mutant), we found that miR-196b expression was significantly higher in the POLE-mutant subtype (Fig. 8D). Correlation analysis indicated that miR-196b expression was negatively correlated with the expression of the MSI-related molecule MLH1 (Fig. 8E), whereas GATA6 expression was positively correlated with the expression of MLH1, MSH6, and PMS2 (Fig. 8F). Subsequently, we evaluated the diagnostic potential of miR-196b (AUC: 0.762) and GATA6 (AUC: 0.684) in our own serum samples using ROC curve analysis (Fig. 8G). Furthermore, we assessed the potential of miR-196b (AUC: 0.910) and GATA6 (AUC: 0.625) as diagnostic and prognostic markers using TCGA data (Fig. 8H).

Discussion

EC is the second most common gynecological malignancy worldwide and ranks as the fourth most common cancer in women. The number of EC cases is anticipated to increase in the upcoming years, according to projections [32]. Histologically, EC is classified into Type I, or endometrioid carcinomas (EEC), and Type II, or non-EEC [33]. EECs constitute nearly 80% of all EC cases [34]. In the present study, the majority of the analyzed samples were of Type I, while the number of Type II samples was relatively small. Myometrial invasion is recognized as a critical event in the spread of EC and an important prognostic factor in determining the recurrence risk. According to the FIGO staging system, EC is classified into stages I–IV, and patients in stages III and IV show a poor 5-year survival rate [35]. Women with locally recurrent or metastatic advanced EC have a median survival time of only 7–12 months [36]. Understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying proliferation and metastasis is critical for developing more effective therapeutic strategies for EC.

The expression of miR-196b in different cancers has exhibited inconsistency. For instance, miR-196b functions as a tumor suppressor in CDX2-driven acute myeloid leukemia [37], whereas its knockdown suppresses breast cancer proliferation and metastasis [38]. MiR-196b inhibits cell proliferation and induces apoptosis in HepG2 cells by targeting IGF2BP1 [13], and in MLL-rearranged leukemia, miR-196b targets HOXA9/MEIS1 oncogenes as well as FAS tumor suppressor [39]. On the other hand, miR-196b has been reported to promote the progression of cancers such as lung cancer [40] and hepatocellular carcinoma [41]. However, the interaction between miR-196b and EC has not been thoroughly explored. In this study, we found that miR-196b is downregulated in EC, and its overexpression suppresses cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and angiogenesis. GATA6, which has been identified as an oncogenic in several cancers and precancerous conditions, including Barrett’s esophagus, gastric, pancreatic, and colon cancers [42], is also involved in EC. However, the role of GATA6 in gynecological diseases remains understudied. Our data show that GATA6 is highly expressed in EC and promotes cell proliferation and metastasis, although its precise role—whether it is causative, correlative, or protective—remains to be clarified.

Several studies have confirmed that miRNAs can suppress tumorigenesis by targeting GATA6. For instance, miR-124 targets GATA6 to inhibit invasion and metastasis of cholangiocarcinoma [25]; suppresses the progression of laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma by targeting GATA6 [43], and miR-143 represses gastric cancer proliferation by targeting GATA6 [44]. Taken together, these findings suggest that miR-196b might act as a tumor suppressor in EC by targeting GATA6.

Regulation of the cell cycle is crucial for cell division and proliferation [45]. In this study, we observed a higher proportion of Ishikawa and HEC-1 A cells arrested at the S phase after transfected with miR-196b mimics compared to the control. The cyclin A/CDK2 complex, a key regulator of the S-phase, controls DNA synthesis and cell-cycle progression [46]. Following miR-196b mimic transfection, the expression of cyclin A1, an essential regulator of S-phase progression [47], significantly reduced. Moreover, CDK2, a well-known modulator of cell proliferation [48], exhibited varying effects under different conditions. Knockdown of CDK2 induces S-phase arrest [49], whereas its overexpression can lead to S-phase arrest in HeLa cells [50]. Our findings demonstrate that the transfection with miR-196b mimics resulted in a significant increase in S-phase arrest in EC cells, accompanied by downregulation of the cyclin A-CDK2 complex. This suggests that the cyclin A-CDK2 complex plays a critical role in S-phase arrest in EC. Furthermore, we observed that miR-196b upregulated CDKN1A/P21, a known cell-cycle inhibitor involved in regulating cell proliferation [51]. The KHSRP complex facilitates the release of CDKN1A/P21 mRNAs, which is subsequently translated into the p21 protein that negatively regulates CDK2 expression [48]. Taken together, our data imply that miR-196b acts as a tumor suppressor in EC by impairing DNA replication and regulating cell-cycle-related gene expression.

Given the significant downregulation of miR-196b in EC, we conducted further mechanistic investigations. Our results showed that miR-196b directly targets the 3’-UTR of GATA6, thereby inhibiting cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in Ishikawa and HEC-1 A cells. In breast cancer, GATA6 overexpression promotes EMT and metastasis [52]. EMT is a process through which epithelial cells lose their polarity and acquire mesenchymal traits, thus enhancing their migratory and invasive properties [53]. During EMT, epithelial cells typically lose the cell-cell adhesion molecule E-cadherin, while the mesenchymal marker vimentin is upregulated [54]. MiRNAs can modulate EMT in EC, as shown by miR-214, which inhibits EMT and metastasis by targeting TWIST1 [55]. Additionally, estrogen signaling and miRNAs are involved in EC progression and metastasis [56]. In accordance with these findings, we hypothesized that miR-196b could influence EMT-mediated EC metastasis. In our study, the expression of vimentin was reduced, and the expression of E-cadherin was increased in EC cells transfected with miR-196b mimics. Moreover, siGATA6 treatment rescued this effect, suggesting that miR-196b targets GATA6 to induce EMT and metastatic phenotypes. These findings provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying EC metastasis.

The AKT/ERK signaling pathway plays a crucial role in regulating various biological processes, including EMT, in cancer [57]. In EC, several genes regulate tumor growth via the AKT/ERK pathway [55, 58], and this pathway has been identified as a druggable target for EC and ovarian cancer [59]. In our xenograft model of EC, we observed that miR-196b overexpression led to GATA6 downregulation, thereby blocking the AKT/ERK pathway and suppressing tumor growth. Furthermore, we demonstrated that the miR-196b/GATA6 axis mediates the activation of the AKT/ERK pathway in EC cells, significantly influencing the phosphorylation of AKT and ERK. Previous studies have investigated the role of miR-196b and GATA6 in AKT and ERK signaling. For instance, miR-196b inhibited both ERK and AKT signaling pathway activity by downregulating NRAS and BRAF in colorectal cancer [60]. Separately, GATA6 both activates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in endometrial stromal cells [61] and participates in regulating ERK signaling in endodermal cells [62]. These reports are consistent with our finding that the miR-196b/GATA6 axis functionally converges on the phosphorylation status of these key kinases.

Given that EC pathogenesis is closely associated with estrogen signaling, we explored their potential interactions with estrogen pathways. Previous studies have demonstrated that elevated estrogen levels promote GATA6 expression [63], while GATA6 in turn plays a key role in activating estrogen synthesis. Specifically, GATA6 cooperates with NR5A1 to induce expression of key enzymes involved in the conversion of cholesterol to estradiol, driving endometrial stromal cells toward a high estrogen-producing phenotype [64]. In endometriotic lesions, GATA6 overexpression resulting from CpG methylation defects leads to excessive local estrogen production [65]. These findings suggest a potential feedback loop involving the miR-196b/GATA6 axis and estrogen signaling that may contribute to EC pathogenesis, warranting further investigation.

In this study, we found that miR-196b and GATA6 were significantly associated with patient survival, tumor grade, and clinical stage, highlighting their prognostic value and relevance to disease progression. Further analysis of molecular subtypes revealed that miR-196b was highly expressed in POLE-mutant tumors and negatively correlated with MSI, whereas GATA6 showed positive correlations. ROC curve analysis based on serum samples and TCGA data further demonstrated the diagnostic potential of miR-196b (serum AUC: 0.762; TCGA AUC: 0.910) and GATA6 (serum AUC: 0.684; TCGA AUC: 0.625). In addition, we conducted a preliminary exploration of the therapeutic relevance of miR-196b in the context of chemotherapy resistance. Upregulation of miR-196b partially restored the sensitivity of cisplatin-resistant Ishikawa cells to cisplatin treatment, attenuating their proliferative and migratory capacities (Supplementary Fig. 4). This finding suggests that miR-196b may be involved in regulating chemoresistance in EC, potentially broadening its therapeutic value. Although our findings establish the miR-196b/GATA6 axis as a critical regulator of EC progression, its translational potential remains to be fully explored. Future studies investigating delivery strategies, combination with standard therapies, and efficacy in advanced models will be essential to assess its therapeutic applicability.

Conclusions

In conclusion, miR-196b is downregulated in EC tissues and correlates with disease progression. MiR-196b suppresses EC proliferation, migration, invasion, and EMT by directly targeting GATA6. Moreover, miR-196b inhibits tube formation in HUVEC cells through GATA6 expression. The miR-196b/GATA6 axis plays a crucial role in the regulation of the AKT/ERK signaling pathway. Collectively, these findings highlight miR-196b/GATA6 as a potential biomarker and a key molecular node in EC, laying a foundation for future studies.
