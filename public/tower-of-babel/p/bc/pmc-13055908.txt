Introduction

Pulpal and periapical diseases are primarily caused by microorganisms. In order to promote the healing of periapical lesions, the main goal of an endodontic therapy is to remove these microbes from the root canal system.1 However, one of the therapeutic challenges is getting rid of all the microbes from the affected root canal system and making these areas bacteria-free. Thus, failure of endodontic therapy is typically a result of persistence or recontamination of the root canal system by microbes.2 Aerobic and facultative anaerobic bacteria are most commonly found in failed root canal cases. Among those, Enterococcus faecalis (E. faecalis) is the commonest (in 90% of cases) and the most resistant intracanal microbe.3 E. faecalis can penetrate deep inside dentinal tubules (up to 300 µm). Hence, the antibacterial effectiveness of various intracanal medicaments (ICMs) is primarily tested against E. faecalis.4 To eliminate such resistant bacteria from the entire root canal system, various contemporary intracanal medicaments (ICMs) with an effective antibacterial action are evaluated.5 In nanoemulsions (droplet sizes between 50 and 500 nm), a water-immiscible oil phase is mixed with an aqueous phase under high shear forces.6 The key advantages of nanoemulsions are sustained release and kinetic stability, both desirable properties of an ideal ICM.

Literature has shown that the Triple Antibiotic Paste (TAP) ICM is considered the most effective against E. faecalis.7 However, use of Triple Antibiotic Paste (TAP) has a few disadvantages such as staining, surface demineralization, increase in dentinal roughness, reduction in dentine wettability, inorganic content, micro-hardness, and fracture resistance.8–10 So, to overcome these shortcomings, various natural products that have anti-bacterial properties, like Phytosphingosine (PS) or Chitosan (CS), can be explored. The chitin found in the exoskeleton of marine crustacean shells is deacetylated to produce chitosan (CS), a naturally occurring biopolymer polysaccharide.11 Chitosan (CS) can be used as an ICM due to its properties, including antibacterial activity, biodegradability, biocompatibility, and nontoxicity.5,12 Therefore, even against the biofilm of E. faecalis, chitosan nanoemulsion (CSN) was found to be efficient.13 The stratum corneum of the skin contains phytosphingosine (PS), a naturally occurring antibacterial component. Phytosphingosine (PS) has been extensively evaluated in dermatology due to its antimicrobial, safety, and anti-inflammatory properties.14 A careful search of the literature found no studies evaluating the antimicrobial properties of PS nanoemulsion (PHSN) against E. faecalis. Hence, it was decided to test the antimicrobial properties of PHSN.

The use of phytosphingosine in endodontics remains poorly studied, despite growing interest in biologically derived antimicrobial agents. Although phytosphingosine has shown antimicrobial and bioactive properties in other biomedical settings, little is known about its effectiveness as an intracanal medication, especially when used as a nanoemulsion, against persistent endodontic pathogens such as E. faecalis. In the quest for safer and more efficient substitutes for traditional intracanal agents, this knowledge gap is significant. Additionally, while nano-emulsion systems may improve antimicrobial activity and drug delivery in lab settings, their behaviour in the intricate root canal environment, which is impacted by anatomical variability, microbial biofilms, and dentin interactions, may deviate from in vitro results. Determining the translational relevance of new endodontic medications requires filling in these gaps.

It was hypothesized that using PHSN as an ICM would be more effective against E. faecalis than the traditionally used TAP and CHN. Thus, the objective of this in vitro study was to determine the Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) and Zone of Inhibition by the Kirby-Bauer Disk diffusion method for Phytosphingosine nano-emulsion (PHSN), Chitosan nano-emulsion (CHN), and Triple Antibiotic Paste (TAP) against E. faecalis using the microbroth dilution method. The working hypothesis is that Phytosphingosine and chitosan nano-emulsions will demonstrate antimicrobial activity against E. faecalis that is comparable to or greater than that of Triple Antibiotic Paste.

Materials and Methods

The research protocol was certified by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) and Institutional Ethical Committee (IEC) - All India Institute of Medical Sciences, Nagpur (IEC/Pharmac/2025/1165). This study was conducted following CRIS Guidelines.15

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size was determined considering the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) as the primary outcome measure. The assumed MIC value (15.93) and corresponding standard deviation (1.53) were derived from preliminary pilot experiments conducted under conditions identical to those planned for the main study. These pilot observations provided an empirical estimate of the expected effect size and variability. Using these parameters, with an alpha error of 5%, statistical power of 80%, and an allowable error of 1, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 9 per group.

Preparation of Intracanal Medicaments (Figure 1)

Showing prepared solutions of Phytosphingosine nano-emulsion (A), Chitosan nano-emulsion (B), and Triple Antibiotic Paste (C).

All three intracanal medicaments (ICMs) were prepared according to the below mentioned protocols.

Phytosphingosine Nanoemulsion (PHSN) Figure 1(A)

The PHS solution was prepared by dissolving 500 mg of PHS powder (Evolution Life Science, Chennai, India) in 100 mL of ethanol (Merck, Mumbai, India). To prepare the PHSN, a high-speed stirrer was used. Separate preparations were made for the oil and water phases. One percent PS and two percent lecithin were dissolved in oleic acid (w/w) to produce the oil phase (40%) of the PS-NE. Two percent Tween 80 was dissolved in distilled water (w/w) to create the aqueous phase (60%) in this way. Using a high-speed stirrer (Ultra-Turrax T8, IKA, China) set at 8000 rpm for three minutes at room temperature, the water phase was introduced to the oil phase and stirred.

Chitosan Nanoemulsion (CHN) Figure 1(B)

The chitosan (CS) (Suvidhinath Laboratories, Vadodara, India) powder was obtained. The oil phase was prepared by mixing olive oil (29.5% w/w), span 20 (1% w/w), and chitosan (1% w/w) using a magnetic stirrer for 15 min. The aqueous phase was prepared by mixing distilled water 78.5% w/w, chitosan (1% w/w), and Tween 80 (1% w/w). The oil phase was homogenised with the aqueous phase using a high-speed mixer at 10,000 rpm for 7 min.

Triple Antibiotic Paste (TAP) Figure 1(C)

Minocycline tablets (100 mg; Minocin®, Wyeth Pharmaceuticals, USA), metronidazole tablets (400 mg; Flagyl®, Sanofi Aventis, France), and ciprofloxacin tablets (250 mg; Ciplox®, Cipla Ltd., India) were combined in equal amounts (1:1:1) to create Triple Antibiotic Paste (TAP). To create a homogenous mixture, the tablets were aseptically ground into a fine powder and then thoroughly mixed. To get a consistent paste-like consistency, a vehicle made of propylene glycol and macrogol ointment was added. To ensure uniformity and reproducibility, the entire preparation process was carried out in the College of Pharmacy under carefully monitored laboratory conditions, following the Hoshino et al protocol.16 These ICMs were categorised into 3 groups as follows:

Group I: Phytosphingosine nanoemulsion (PHSN)

Group II: Chitosan Nanoemulsion (CHN)

Group III: Triple Antibiotic Paste (TAP)

Evaluation of Antibacterial Efficacy

The antibacterial property of these intra-canal medicaments was evaluated using the following method:

Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) Testing (Figure 2)

Showing microtitre plates before (A) and after (B) completing micro broth dilution method to find out the Minimum Inhibitory Concentration of Phytosphingosine nano-emulsion, Chitosan nano-emulsion, and Triple Antibiotic Paste.

The micro broth dilution method was used to find out the Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) of PHSN, CHN, and TAP. The Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 29212 strain was used for testing. The stock solutions of PHSN and CHN were prepared at a concentration of 3200 μg/mL by diluting them with sterile distilled water and Dimethyl Sulphoxide (DMSO), respectively. A 3200 μg/mL stock solution of TAP (metronidazole, ciprofloxacin, and minocycline in the ratio 2.5:4:1) was prepared by dissolving them in distilled water. These were further diluted 100 times, and the final concentration in the microtitre plates was adjusted to be 32 μg/mL to 0.06 μg/mL in doubling dilutions. The final inoculum concentration of Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 29212 was taken as 5 × 105 CFU/mL for MIC testing by microbroth dilution method. The medium used for testing was cation-adjusted Mueller–Hinton broth. The microtitre plates were incubated at 37°C with the lid closed for 18 hours, and the MIC reading was noted. The lowest concentration at which the growth of microorganisms was inhibited, considered as MIC value. Thus, the MIC was determined when turbidity was not visible to the naked eyes in the microtitre plates.

Disk Diffusion Test (Figure 3)

Showing plates before (A) and after (B) microbial growth. The diameters of zone of inhibition disc diffusion are highest for Phytosphingosine nano-emulsion (PHSN), Chitosan nano-emulsion (CHN), and Triple Antibiotic Paste (TAP).

An inoculum of 0.5 McFarland Standard of the test microorganism E. faecalis (ATCC 29212) suspensions were obtained from freshly grown culture and inoculated on Mueller–Hinton Agar culture plates. Using a sterile cotton swab, lawn culture of the microorganism was done by uniformly spreading the inoculum on the culture plates. For disc diffusion assay, 10 microliters of each of the intracanal medicament was added to the Whatman paper no. 1 (6 mm disks) using a pipette. It was dried at room temperature for 3 hours and then placed on the top of the agar plates. It was then followed by incubation of the plates for 24 hours at 37°C in an incubator. The plates were then examined for zones of inhibition of microbial growth after incubation period. The diameters of the zones were evaluated and measured in millimetre. The results obtained were analysed so as to determine the efficacy of the natural products as an intracanal medicament in comparison with conventional intracanal medicaments.

Statistical Analysis

Data was analysed in the statistical software STATA version 10.1 (2011). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the quantitative parameter with mean and standard deviation. There was the fulfilment of the normality by the Skewness/Kurtosis test; therefore, Parametric test was used. Comparison of means between all groups was done by analysis of variance test (ANOVA). Overall F-test from ANOVA revealed significant differences between the groups. Pair-wise comparisons from the post hoc Tukey’s test revealed significant differences in all possible pairs. p value <0.05 was considered to be statistically significant for all comparisons.

Results

The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were 1 μg/mL for PHSN, 32 μg/mL for CHN, and 4.0 μg/mL for TAP against E. faecalis (Table 1). PHSN showed the largest zone of inhibition (38.3 ± 1.48), followed by CHN (8.16 ± 0.966), and TAP (6.30 ± 1.71) (Table 2). Thus, PHSN showed significantly higher antimicrobial efficiency compared to CHN and TAP (p < 0.05) (Table 2). Although CHN showed a larger zone of inhibition than TAP, the difference between them was not statistically significant (p < 0.05) (Table 3).

These results suggest that PHSN has superior antimicrobial efficacy against E. faecalis, likely due to enhanced membrane disruption and nanoparticle penetration, which may increase microorganism susceptibility. The relatively low activity seen with TAP and CHN points to variations in antimicrobial mechanisms and diffusion properties under the tested circumstances. Using standardised MIC and diffusion assays, this study offers one of the first direct comparative assessments of phytosphingosine and chitosan nano-emulsions against a traditional Triple Antibiotic Paste from a novelty standpoint. A viable alternative intracanal antimicrobial strategy is supported by PHSN’s significantly higher antimicrobial potential, which also provides strong preclinical evidence in favour of nanoparticle-based therapeutics for endodontic disinfection.

Discussion

To the best of the author’s knowledge, this was the first in-vitro study that aimed at comparing the antimicrobial efficacy of Phytosphingosine nano-emulsion (PHSN), Chitosan nano-emulsion (CHN), and Triple Antibiotic Paste (TAP) against E. faecalis. The null hypothesis (H0) assumed no difference in antimicrobial efficacy between PHSN, CHN, and TAP against E. faecalis. However, results of the current study revealed that PHSN showed significantly higher antimicrobial efficiency compared to CHN and TAP (p < 0.05). Hence, the null hypothesis (H0) was refuted. All variables, like laboratory setting, bacterial strain, assessor’s standardization, etc., were carefully controlled. Even with the use of conventional intra-canal medications (ICMs) and several irrigants, some bacteria species do persist and lead to reinfection.17,18 The predominant bacteria responsible for these chronic root canal infections have long been identified as E. faecalis. It is the most often found microbial species in root canals with periapical infections that occur after therapy. Different kinds of contemporary ICMs are being studied to address such diverse microbial flora since root canal infections are complex and multi-microbial.19,20 Hence, antibacterial properties of a novel, natural Sphingoid, PHSN, were tested against E. faecalis in this study.

TAP is the most commonly used ICM in root canal treatment. Hoshino et al investigated TAP’s antibacterial effectiveness.21 For the 3MIX formulation, he suggested a 1:1:1 combination of metronidazole (500 mg), minocycline (100 mg), and ciprofloxacin (200 mg) with carrier propylene glycol and macrogol ointment. According to Adl et al, TAP dramatically decreased the number of E. faecalis colony-forming units.22 Hence, TAP was taken as the gold standard against E. faecalis in this study. Chitosan (CH) is a natural polysaccharide derived from the deacetylation of chitin in crustacean shells. In this study, the antimicrobial potential of Chitosan nano-emulsion (CHN) was comparable to that of TAP against E. faecalis (p < 0.05). There is various mechanism of actions suggested for the CHN. It interacts with microbial cell membrane and cytoplasmic constituents that inhibit the bacterial biofilm formation.23 Moreover, it binds efficiently to the cell membrane as well as DNA of bacteria because of low molecular weight of CHN and its ionic interaction with negatively charged bacterial structures.24 Therefore, CHN exhibits antibacterial activity against microbes that are both Gram-positive and Gram-negative.25,26 This was consistent with the Supotngarmkul et al investigation.27

Ceramides, sphingomyelins, cerebrosides, and other complex sphingolipids are all derived from sphingoid bases.28 One of these sphingoids, phytosphingosine (PHS), is found naturally in the stratum corneum and aids in the skin’s innate immunological defence. Saliva and the oral mucosal surface also contain PHS.29 In dermatology, the antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory properties of PHS were investigated thoroughly. However, the role of PHS against bacteria responsible for failure in endodontic infection has not been evaluated to date. Hence, PHSN was compared in this study. Here, the mean zone of inhibition against E. faecalis was found to be greatest with PHNE as compared to other CHN and TAP. CHN exhibits a slightly larger zone of inhibition compared to TAP, but this difference is not statistically significant. The exact antibacterial mechanism of PHSN remains unclear. Various mechanisms were proposed for the antibacterial actions of PHNE. It was demonstrated that PHSN led to the rupture of the mitochondrial membrane by producing reactive oxygen species.30 It disrupted ATP production and caused Ca2+ overload. Moreover, apoptosis-related proteins Bax, Bcl-2, cytochrome-c, and cleaved caspase-3 were expressed when the mitochondrial membrane ruptured, causing the permeability transition pore to open and proapoptotic substances to be released into the cytoplasm.31 The intrinsic apoptotic pathway mediated by the mitochondria was then triggered by these events. This further accelerated apoptosis by blocking PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, a mitochondrial repair mechanism.

The disc diffusion method, also known as the Kirby Bauer Method, was employed in this investigation to investigate E. faecalis’s susceptibility to different ICMs. Paper discs that had been impregnated with ICMS were used. This technique for figuring out how susceptible microbes are to various antimicrobial drugs is easy, quick, efficient, and precise.32 The technique’s fundamental idea is that antimicrobial drugs diffuse into an agar medium that has been infected with the test bacterium from a paper disc. The disc’s antimicrobial chemicals flow outward, forming a concentration gradient that prevents the microbe from growing nearby. The degree to which the microbe is susceptible to the antimicrobial agent is shown by the zone of inhibition that envelops the disc. The kind and age of the microbe, the inoculum’s density, the agar medium’s composition, and other variables can all have an impact on the development of microorganisms in the Kirby Bauer Disc Diffusion Method. Therefore, in this study, all of these characteristics were standardised and meticulously controlled.

Here, the lowest concentration of an ICM required to prevent the observable growth of an E. faecalis utilising the Broth dilution method was found using the Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) test. A standardized bacterial suspension was added after ICMs had been serially diluted in a liquid media using the broth dilution procedure. By identifying the exact ICM concentration required to inhibit bacteria, MIC encourages appropriate ICM use, lowers the danger of overuse and resistance, and boosts its effectiveness. The likelihood of underdosing, which can result in resistant strains, is decreased with MIC data. It improves the therapeutic result by enabling medical personnel to modify medication dosages according to each patient’s unique demands. With moisture and osmolarity taken into account, nutrition, aeration, and oxygen levels can be regulated for precise MIC measurements. The MIC test is simple to administer and set up, which improves the test’s results. The amount of antimicrobial agent used can be decreased by using the MIC test on a modest scale. Nevertheless, the application of the MIC test has several restrictions. Results can be greatly impacted by even minor adjustments to the MIC test parameters. The MIC test does not verify total eradication of bacteria, but it does demonstrate that the antimicrobial agent may impede growth at specific concentrations.

Conclusion

Within the scope of this study, phytosphingosine nano-emulsion (PHSN) demonstrated the highest antimicrobial activity against E. faecalis, inhibiting bacterial growth at a minimum inhibitory concentration of 1 μg/mL. This superior activity was also reflected in a significantly larger zone of inhibition than that of chitosan nano-emulsion (CHN) and Triple Antibiotic Paste (TAP). Although CHN produced a greater inhibitory effect than TAP, the difference between these agents was not statistically significant. These findings suggest that PHSN may represent a promising alternative intracanal medicament with potential clinical relevance.

However, in-vitro conditions cannot fully replicate the complexity of the clinical root canal environment. Factors such as polymicrobial interactions, infected dentin structure, and bacterial by-products may influence antimicrobial performance in vivo. Therefore, further well-designed clinical investigations are necessary to validate the translational applicability of these findings.
