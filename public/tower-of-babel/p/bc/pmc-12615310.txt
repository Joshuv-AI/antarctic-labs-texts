Investigation on the protective effects of the color stability of phycocyanin in vitro
Authors: Xiyu Zhang, Xinran Li, Wenjing Zhang, Yali Li, Boxiong Wu, Haozhe Cheng, Baoguo Sun, Shuai Hao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhycocyanin (PC), which is commonly extracted from marine Spirulina platensis, has been explored as a natural blue pigment for application in the food industry. As a pigment-protein complex, the color stability of phycocyanin could be easily affected by the variation of temperature and light intensity in environment, which limits its further utilization. Increasing the thermal- and photo-stability of phycocyanin are key challenges in its industrial production, especially in food area. In this work, the thermal- and photo-stability of phycocyanin under different pH conditions were investigated, and the underlying mechanism was studied by molecular dynamics simulation. The results showed that bingding of bovine serum albumin (2:1), sugar alcohols (10:1) and oligosaccharides (0.5:1) with phycocyanin could significantly enhance the color stability of phycocyanin under neutral, alkaline and acidic environments, respectively. The molecular dynamics simulation and molecular docking showed that sugar alcohols and oligosaccharides could establish hydrogen bonds and form an encapsulating structure with the hydrophobic regions of phycocyanin, which stabilized it in extreme environment. The current results could lay a theoretical foundation for the in-depth development and utilization of phycocyanin, and also provide guidance for the application of marine-derived natural blue pigment in the food industry.Keywords: Phycocyanin, Molecular dynamics simulation, Thermal-stability, Photo-stability

Introduction

Phycobiliproteins are light-harvesting pigment proteins found in cyanobacteria and cryptophyte (Usai et al., 2024). Phycocyanin, a member of the phycobiliprotein family, functions as an accessory photosynthetic pigment. Phycocyanin comprises oligomeric proteins, with the fundamental structural units being the α and β subunits (An et al., 2016). Each subunit consists of an apoprotein and an open-chain tetrapyrrole-structured chromophore, which is covalently linked to the cysteine residues of the proteins via a thioether bond (Pez Jaeschke et al., 2021).

The fluorescent properties and diverse physiological activities of phycocyanin have led to its extensive application in the food, cosmetic, and medical industries. Owing to its distinctive blue hue, there is an increasing trend among food companies, such as Nestlé, Hershey's, and Rexall's coffee, to utilize phycocyanin as a natural alternative to synthetic pigments (Maddiboyina et al., 2023). Furthermore, given the significant side effects associated with synthetic drugs, there is a growing interest in exploring the pharmacological potential of phycocyanin, attributed to its high safety profile. For example, Saini et al. investigated the combination of piroxicam and phycocyanin in preventing DMH-induced colon carcinogenesis in rats (Saini et al., 2012). Nishanth R. P. et al. have demonstrated that phycocyanin can inhibit the activities of human hepatocellular carcinoma cells via the reactive oxygen species and cyclooxygenase-2 pathways (Nishanth et al., 2010). Furthermore, phycocyanin has been confirmed to possess various pharmacological properties, including anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective and hepatoprotective effects (An et al., 2016).

The denaturation of protein significantly impacts the color stability of phycocyanin, thereby restricting its advanced development and application within the food industry. To address this limitation, researchers have employed various strategies to enhance the stability of phycocyanin, such as encapsulation, protein modification, nanoparticle delivery systems, and the incorporation of stabilizers. For example, Schmatz et al. (Lafarga et al., 2020) utilized the electrospray method for the encapsulation of phycocyanin, producing ultrafine particles of polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and polycarbonate (PC) with an average particle size of 395 ± 71 nm. This approach resulted in a heat resistance of phycocyanin up to 216 °C while preserving its antioxidant activity. Additionally, N. Nogueira (De Moraes Nogueira et al., 2021) et al. employed liposome embedding to form phycocyanin liposomes using hydration techniques with soybean lecithin, which improved the stability and specificity of phycocyanin. Furthermore, Wu et al. (Wu et al., 2024) employed pepsin enzymatic modification of phycocyanin at an enzymatic pH of 2.0, inactivation through boiling for 6 min and an enzymatic reaction time of 2 h. The modified phycocyanin demonstrated excellent coloration and structural stability under high temperatures and acidic conditions.

Apart from above methods, alternative approaches are being developed to protect phycocyanin in foods, such as controlling food matrix composition and encapsulation. The stability of phycocyanin can be enhanced by physically complexing or chemically conjugating them to polysaccharides or other carbohydrates (Faieta et al., 2019). Dithiothreitol and sodium azide have been shown to protect phycocyanin from degradation, however, these are toxic substances that are not suitable for food applications (Mishra et al., 2008). Citric acid has been shown to protect phycocyanin from degradation in aqueous solutions when stored at 35 ± 5 °C for 45 days (Fabre et al., 2022; Mishra et al., 2008). In addition, studies have shown that the stability of phycocyanin in some food products can be improved by incorporating oils. For instance, adding sunflower oil or extra virgin olive oil (10 g oil/100 g bakery dough) protected phycocyanin from thermal degradation during cooking (Niccolai et al., 2021).

These current modification methods, including encapsulation, acid or oil addition, have significantly enhanced the stability of phycocyanin through various methods, however, its application in the food and beverage industry remains limited. Here, the thermal- and photo-stability of phycocyanin under different pH conditions were investigated by protein-protein (including Bovine Serum Albumin, whey protein and soy protein et al.), protein-sugar alcohol (including (sorbitol, lactitol and xylitol et al.), and protein-oligosaccharides (including maltose, soy sugar oligosaccharides et al.) bindings, and the mechanism was studied by molecular dynamics simulation, molecular docking analysis. The current study was conducted in both neutral and inverse (alkaline and acidic) environments, resulting in an effective method for improving the color stability of phycocyanin.

Materials and methods

Experimental reagents

Phycocyanin was purchased from Zhejiang Binmei Biological Co., Ltd. (Zhejiang, China). Bovine serum protein, whey protein, fish protein, and egg white protein were sourced from Shanghai Biyuntian Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Wheat gluten, soy protein, lactitol, erythritol, xylitol, sorbitol, mannitol, oligosaccharides xylose, oligofructose, oligogalactose, oligomaltose, oligoisomaltose, oligosoyaose were all sourced from Beijing Jinlis Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Primary reagents: KBr powder was procured from Hubei Zixin Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Hubei, China).

Ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy scan of phycocyanin

A certain mass of phycocyanin was accurately weighed and prepared into a suitable concentration of phycocyanin solution. A UV-1800 UV spectrophotometer from Shimadzu Laboratory Equipment Co., Ltd. was used for baseline calibration in the wavelength range of 200–800 nm using a blank solvent to ensure that the zero point of the instrument was set correctly. The sample solution was then loaded into a UV absorption cuvette and placed into the UV spectrophotometer for scanning measurement. According to the measured absorbance data, the absorption spectrum curve of phycocyanin under the UV spectrum was plotted to determine the position of its absorption peak. The phycocyanin was prepared as 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, 1.2 mg/mL solution at a certain concentration, and then at the wavelength of the maximum absorption peak, a linear regression standard curve was plotted according to the absorbance value versus the mass concentration with water as the blank control.

The effects of pH, light and temperature on the preservation rate of phycocyanin

1 mg/mL phycocyanin solution was prepared and aliquoted into 9 test tubes. The pH of each aliquot was adjusted incrementally from 2 to 9. Asorbance measurements at 620 nm were recorded every 2 h. The pigment preservation rate was determined by calculating the mean of the three parallel measurements (Jespersen et al., 2005).

1 mg/mL phycocyanin solution was prepared and placed under light intensity of 0, 250, and 500 Lux, respectively, and treated with incubator irradiation. The absorbance value at 620 nm was measured every 1 day, and the pigment retention rate was calculated by taking parallel measurements for 3 times.

A 1 mg/mL solution of phycocyanin was prepared and placed in 6 temperature gradients (30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80 °C), and the absorbance value at 620 nm was measured every 1 h and the pigment retention rate was calculated, and the measurements were carried out in parallel for 3 times.

Preparation of protein-phycocyanin complex

A certain mass of phycocyanin was weighed and dissolved in ultrapure water at room temperature under complete protection from light and stirred on a constant temperature magnetic stirrer to make a phycocyanin solution with a concentration of 1 mg/mL and a homogeneous color, and then left for a period of time to be used. At the same time, 10 mg/mL, 5 mg/mL, 2 mg/mL, 1 mg/mL, 0.5 mg/mL and 0.2 mg/mL Bovine Serum Albumin solutions were prepared, and placed on a shaker to make the different ratios of the protein solutions mix well and make the protein complex. 10 mL of different proportions of protein solutions were stirred at 300 rpm away from light, and 10 mL of phycocyanin solution was weighed and added to the stirring protein solutions, and placed in a constant temperature magnetic stirrer to make them mix well. The other five protein- phycocyanin complexes (fish protein, egg white protein, whey protein, soy protein and wheat protein) were prepared as described above, and stirred overnight. When the above preparation process was completed, a part of the self-assembled complex solution was taken and frozen in a refrigerator at −80 °C for 24 h. It was put into a vacuum freeze-dryer and operated in main drying mode for 48 h to obtain the lyophilized powder of the protein- phycocyanin complexes.

Preparation of sugar alcohol-phycocyanin and oligosaccharide-phycocyanin complexes

Five sugar alcohols (sorbitol, lactitol, xylitol, erythritol and mannito) and six oligosaccharides (xylo-oligosaccharides, fructooligosaccharides, galactooligosaccharides, maltose oligosaccharides, isomaltooligosaccharides and soy sugar oligosaccharides) were respectively combined with phycocyanin. Briefly, phycocyanin solution (10 mL) was thoroughly mixed with five types of sugar alcohols (10 mL for each sugar alcohols) at different ratios (10:1, 5:1, 2:1, 1:1, 0.5:1, and 0.2:1), respectively. The pH of the mixed solution was adjusted to 9.00 ± 0.02 using hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide solutions with a concentration of 0.1 moL/L, followed by lefting overnight to promote the combination of phycocyanin and sugar alcohol molecules. The binding process of ologosaccharides and phycocyanin is similar to that of sugar alcohols, except that the pH of the mixed solution is adjusted to an acidic condition (4.00 ± 0.02).

Determination of colorimetric values, characteristic peak positions and absorbance values

Referring to Zhong et al. (Zhong et al., 2020), the potentials of the mixtures were determined using a ZS90 potentiometer at a temperature of 25 °C and an angle of 90°, the dispersed phase, dispersing medium and the measurement mode were set up individually for the samples' characteristics, and three measurements were made for each sample, and the results are presented as the “mean potential ± standard deviation” (mV).

The colorimetric b-value was analyzed by reference to Moula et al. (Moula et al., 2022), Lab colorimetry was performed using a benchtop colorimeter, and after calibration with a white plate and deionized water, 16 mL of the sample was injected into a glass cuvette and sealed with a sealing film, which was placed in the measurement port and the colorimetry was measured. The experiment was repeated three times for each set of samples.

The method of Nunes et al. (Nunes et al., 2023) was used to analyze the position of the characteristic peaks. The absorbance values of the composite solutions were measured in the range of 200–800 nm using a UV-1800 UV–visible spectrophotometer, and the degree of shift of the UV peaks at 620 nm was analyzed by UV–visible spectroscopy, and the experiments were repeated three times for each group of samples, using water as the baseline.

Absorbance values were determined by reference to the method of Torres et al. (Espinoza-Torres et al., 2023) and the absorbance values of the composite solutions at 620 nm were determined using a UV-1800 UV–visible spectrophotometer. The instrument was calibrated using water as the baseline, after which the samples were placed in a transparent cuvette, inserted into the sample slot, the wavelength was set and the absorbance values were recorded. The experiment was repeated three times for each set of samples.

Thermal stability analysis

The samples were placed in a dark environment at 70 °C controlled by an electrically heated thermostatic water bath, about 20 mL of freshly prepared samples were taken and sealed in 50 mL centrifuge tubes, wrapped in tinfoil and then fixed vertically in a shelf and immersed in the water bath, heated continuously for 3 h. The samples were sampled at intervals of 30 min, and immediately placed in an ice box to cool down, protected from light. The visual appearance was photographed and analyzed by UV–visible spectroscopy and colorimetry.

Light stability analysis

The samples were placed in a light incubator and the ambient temperature was set at 25 °C with 500 Lux of light intensity. About 20 mL of freshly prepared samples were sealed in 50 mL centrifuge tubes, placed flat in the incubator, and exposed to light for 4 consecutive days, with sampling every day. The visual appearance was photographed and subjected to UV–visible spectral analysis and colorimetry.

Fluorescence spectral analysis

The fluorescence intensity of the algal blue protein solution heated in a 70 °C water bath for 30 min was compared with that of the freshly prepared sample of the protein complex solution which was left to stand for 1 day in a light incubator with a light intensity of 100 %. The fluorescence intensity of the samples was measured using an SC-50 fluorescence spectrometer with the excitation wavelengths of 280 nm and 580 nm, the emission wavelengths of 290–450 nm and 600–800 nm, the excitation slit and the emission slit both of 5 nm, and the scanning rate of 1200 nm/min, and the fluorescence intensity of the aqueous solution was measured as the baseline. The experiment was repeated three times for each group of samples.

FTIR analysis

The method of Sarabandi et al. (Sarabandi & Jafari, 2020) was referred and slightly modified. The lyophilized sample was mixed with dried KBr powder in the ratio of 1: 200 by mass and ground until fine and grain free. The mixture was pressed into transparent, crack-free flakes on a tablet press, and the flakes were fixed on a sample holder and placed into the sample cell. Turn on the FTIR instrument, initialize and calibrate the instrument. Set up the experimental parameters, the scanning range was 4000–400 cm−1, the number of scans was 64, the scanning resolution was 4 cm−1, and the scanning speed was 0.2 cm/s. The infrared spectra were scanned with the pure potassium bromide flake as the background in the measurement chamber at a constant temperature of 25 °C, and the infrared absorption spectra of the samples were recorded. The infrared spectra were scanned in the measuring chamber at a constant temperature of 25 °C, and the infrared absorption spectra of the samples were recorded, including the positions, intensities and shapes of the absorption peaks.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Structures of oligosaccharides, sugar alcohols, and phycocyanin (pdb_00008xf1) from PDB bank and pubchem, using the GROMACS 2023 (CUDA) software package and the Amber ff14sb force field for MD simulations. First, the system of phycocyanin and the desired sugar or sugar alcohol was constructed, and the entire system was solvated in TIP3P water, to which neutralizing ions were added to make the entire system electrically neutral. The steepest descent and conjugate gradient methods were used to minimize the energy of the system until convergence was reached. The system was then pre-equilibrated in the NVT system by gradual heating from 0 to 298.15 K over a period of 1 ns, followed by isothermal and isobaric simulations in the NPT system over a period of 1 ns. Finally, a 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation was performed at 298.15 K temperature and 1.0 bar pressure with a time interval of 2 fs, during which the H atoms of the system were weakly constrained to prevent structural errors. The resultant trajectories were PBS-processed to prevent periodic problems. The processed trajectories were then subjected to SASA trajectory simulations.

Molecular docking

To investigate the binding affinities and interaction modes between PC and molecular compounds such as sugar alcohols or oligosaccharides, Autodock Vina 1.2.2, an in silico protein–ligand docking software, was employed. Sorbitol and xylose were selected as representative sugar alcohols and oligosaccharides, respectively, for molecular docking analysis. The molecular structures of these small molecules were retrieved from the PubChem Compound database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The 3D coordinates of PC (PDB ID: 8XF1) were downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (http://www.rcsb.org/pdb/home/home.do). For docking analysis, all protein and molecular files were converted into PDBQT format, with all water molecules excluded and polar hydrogen atoms added. The grid box was centered to cover the domain of each protein and to accommodate free molecular movement. The grid box was set to 30 Å × 30 Å × 30 Å, and grid point distance was 0.05 nm. Molecular docking studies were performed by Autodock Vina 1.2.2 (http://autodock.scripps.edu/).

Isothermal Titration Calorimetry (ITC) analysis

ITC was employed to determine the thermodynamic characteristics of the interactions between phycocyanin and different ligands. Measurements were carried out using a Nano ITC microcalorimeter (TA Instruments, USA) at 25 °C with a stirring speed of 600 rpm. The titration consisted of 20 sequential injections of 2 μL each, with an interval of 120 s to ensure thermal equilibrium between injections.

All samples were dissolved in the same phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) and degassed under vacuum prior to analysis to eliminate air bubbles. Phycocyanin (0.05 mg/mL) was loaded into the sample cell (300 μL), while the titrant solutions of galactose (0.5 mg/mL), whey protein (2 mg/mL), and lactitol (10 mg/mL) were placed in the syringe (50 μL). The system was allowed to equilibrate for approximately 10 min before titration.

Control titrations of each ligand into buffer were performed under identical conditions to correct for dilution heat. The heat flow (μJ/s) generated upon each injection was recorded and integrated using NanoAnalyze software after baseline correction. The integrated heats were plotted against the mass ratio and fitted with the Independent binding site model to obtain thermodynamic parameters including the dissociation constant (Kd), binding stoichiometry (n), enthalpy change (ΔH), and entropy change (ΔS). The association constant (Ka = 1/Kd) and Gibbs free energy (ΔG = ΔH - TΔS = −RT ln Ka) were subsequently calculated.

Particle size analysis

The Malvern Zetasizer Nano-ZS90 was employed to assess the size distribution of colloidal particles within complexes formed by phycocyanin and various molecules. Under dark conditions, equal volumes of a 1 mg/mL phycocyanin solution were introduced into stirring solutions containing 2 mg/mL protein, 10 mg/mL sugar alcohol, and 0.5 mg/mL oligosaccharide. These mixtures were thoroughly homogenized and maintained on a constant-temperature magnetic stirrer at 300 rpm overnight to ensure complete dissolution and facilitate between phycocyanin and the other molecules. The mixture was then transferred using a syringe into a cuvette (DTS0012). Each sample was measured three times at 25 °C without interruption.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analysis

The freeze-dried samples were immediately placed in sample boxes to ensure isolation from reference materials. The instrument was powered on, and initialization and calibration procedures were performed. Experimental parameters were set to a nitrogen atmosphere with a flow rate of 50 mL/min, a scanning rate of 5 °C/min, and a scanning temperature range of 30–140 °C. Subsequently, 5 mg of the sample was weighed into a specialized aluminum crucible, which was then sealed with its lid and allowed to stand overnight. Conduct differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) on the sample within an insulated chamber. Utilize a sealed aluminum crucible as a blank control and perform the DSC under a nitrogen atmosphere. Continuously heat the sample chamber, recording the sample's heat capacity curve across various temperatures while observing its thermal properties and reactions. Analyze the sample's thermal characteristics, phase transition temperatures, and thermal reactions based on the features of the DSC curve.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism was used to assess significant differences using a Student's t-test or an analysis of variance (ANOVA). The results are presented as the means ± SD from three or more independent experiments. *, p < 0.05 was considered significant; **, p < 0.01 was considered extremely significant.

Results

Protein interaction affects the color stability of phycocyanin

Light and temperature are two main factors affecting the color stability of phycocyanin. In this work, the preservation rate of phycocyanin was first measured under different light intensity (0, 250, and 500 Lux) and temperature (30, 40, 50, 60, 70, and 80 °C) conditions. As depicted in Fig. 1A, the preservation rate of phycocyanin significantly declined under 100 % light intensity, dropping to 58.75 % on the second day and further to only 20.2 % after five days. In contrast, when protected from light, the preservation of phycocyanin was substantially improved, with a preservation rate of 86 % after five days. The temperature stability showed a similar trend to the results of light stability (Fig. 1B). The results have verified that enhancement of light intensity and temperature could significantly destroy the stability of phycocyanin and affect its color.

Bovine serum albumin-phycocyanin binding enhances the color stability of phycocyanin in neutral environment

Fig. 2A presents the thermal stability detection results of phycocyanin after interaction with six proteins, respectively. The visual observation images show that only bovine serum and whey protein exhibit a pronounced color-stabilizing effect on phycocyanin after 3 h treatment. In addition, Fig. 2B reveals that with increased heating time, the b-value results of the protein-phycocyanin complexes approximate those of the single phycocyanin, with the exception of bovine serum albumin, which decreases to 9.28, a value significantly higher than that of phycocyanin alone. Furthermore, for both individual phycocyanin and its complexes, the characteristic peaks exhibit minimal blue-shifting, with the exception of the wheat gluten protein. These results have indicated that the blue coloration, when combined with bovine serum albumin, is significantly more intense than that of the phycocyanin solution alone under continuous heating conditions.

Additionally, the structure of phycocyanin was also compromised in the presence of light (500 Lux), while whey protein provides substantial protection, preserving the blue coloration (Fig. 2C). As shown in Fig. 2D, shows that all composite proteins, except for soy protein, maintain high absolute chromaticity values, thereby exerting a strong color-stabilizing effect on phycocyanin. It's worth noting that egg white protein seems to be a better binding protein because its complex with phycocyanin shows a delaying blue-shifting phenomenon with light treatment. While the characteristic absorption peak of the complex formed by bovine serum protein still remained stable under the exposure of light. The above results have indicated that compared with plant-derived protein, the interaction of animal-derived proteins have better effects on the thermal and light stability of phycocyanin, of which, Bovine Serum Albumin shows the best protective effect.

Meanwhile, the infrared absorption spectra of phycocyanin and its associated complex were also analyzed (Fig. 3). In the spectral range of 3500–3100 cm−1, the spectra of phycocyanin when bound to other proteins exhibiting varying degrees of shifts, indicating an increase in the polarity of the microenvironment post-binding, which resulted in strong interactions. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the spectrum of egg white protein displayed the most significant shift, followed by that of bovine serum albumin. In the wave number range of 1550–1450 cm−1, Bovine Serum Albumin and egg albumin caused more pronounced shifts, suggesting that these proteins interact with the phycocyanin surface through more complex intermolecular interactions. Overall, the optimal stabilization of phycocyanin was achieved with bovine serum albumin at a mass ratio of 2:1 in a natural environment.

Oligosaccharide protects the color stability of phycocyanin under acidic conditions

Phycocyanin is unstable under acidic conditions. In this study, six different types of oligosaccharides were employed to binding with phycocyanin. Molecular dynamics simulation analyzes the interaction mechanism of phycocyanin with oligosaccharides and sugar alcohols

At the molecular level, we employed molecular dynamics simulations to model the binding interactions between sugar alcohol-phycocyanin and oligosaccharides-phycocyanin, conducting comprehensive analyses of the binding forces and mechanisms involved. The solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) was utilized as a metric to evaluate the stability of the protein system throughout the simulations, as it quantifies the extent of the protein's surface exposed to the solvent (Da Fonseca et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2023).

During the progression of the simulation, phycocyanin aggregation was observed, with sugar alcohols consistently adsorbing onto the phycocyanin surface, ultimately leading to the formation of an aggregation core (Fig. 8, Fig. 9). Simulated aggregation propensity scores were derived from the initial and final conformations, with higher scores indicating a greater likelihood of amino acid aggregation in specific regions. All sugar alcohol-phycocyanin complex scores exceeded 1, with sorbitol and mannitol exhibiting the highest scores. It shows that the addition of each substance enhanced the stability of phycocyanin, with sorbitol and mannitol demonstrating the most pronounced stabilizing effects. Previous studies have indicated that hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bond formation contribute to protein aggregation (Antony et al., 2003). It is hypothesized that sugar alcohols establish hydrogen bonds with polar residues in proteins and form an encapsulating structure with the hydrophobic regions, thereby stabilizing phycocyanin in alkaline environment.

Molecular docking suggests that sugar alcohols and oligosaccharides have a relatively stable binding with phycocyanin

To evaluate the affinity of the small molecules (sugar alcohols and oligosaccharides) for phycocyanin, we performed molecular docking analysis. Sorbitol and xylose were selected as representatives of sugar alcohols and oligosaccharides for molecular docking analysis with phycocyanin, respectively. The binding poses and interactions of molecules with phycocyanin were obtained with Autodock Vina v.1.2.2 and binding energy for each interaction was generated. Results showed that both sorbitol (Fig. 12A-D) and xylose (Fig. 12E-H) bound to phycocyanin through visible hydrogen bonds and strong electrostatic interactions. Among them, the binding energies of sorbitol and phycocyanin are A (−6.96 kcal/mol), B (−6.45 kcal/mol), C (−5.64 kcal/mol), and D (−5.11 kcal/mol). The binding energies of xylose and phycocyanin are E (−6.12 kcal/mol), F (−5.829 kcal/mol), G (−4.98 kcal/mol), and H (−4.617 kcal/mol). Moreover, the hydrophobic pockets of each targets were occupied successfully by sorbitol and xylose.

Sugar alcohols and oligosaccharides promote the binding and aggregation of PC and enhance its stability

The results of molecular docking and dynamics simulation indicate that sugar alcohols and oligosaccharides could respectively combine with phycocyanin through binding effect under alkaline and acidic conditions, which might be an important factor for enhancing the stability of phycocyanin. Therefore, the particle sizes of the phycocyanin and its complexes were employed to verify the results of molecular dynamics simulation. As shown in Table 4, in alkaline conditions, no particle size indicators were detected in the phycocyanin solution. However, as time increased, the particle sizes of the phycocyanin-complexes with sorbitol (473.9 ± 22.8) or mannitol (649.6 ± 49.2) showed a significant increase, suggesting that sorbitol and mannitol could undergo self-assembly effects with phycocyanin. Similarly, in an acidic environment, the addition of xylooligosaccharides (835.7 ± 51.8) and fructooligosaccharides (848.2 ± 65) could also significantly increase the particle size of their complexes with PC. The above results have indicated that sugar alcohols (sorbitol and mannitol) and oligosaccharides (xylooligosaccharides and fructooligosaccharides) could induce phycocyanin aggregation and self-assembly, which further verified the molecular dynamics results.

Discussion

Our finding indicates that phycocyanin can enhance its stability through binding with various proteins. The experimental results demonstrate that different proteins, when interacted with phycocyanin, confer varying degrees of stability enhancement. This variability is likely due to the differing strengths of non-covalent interactions and the distinct structural densities of the resulting complexes. A previous study by Zhang et al. reported that low concentrations of whey proteins could promote the transformation of the secondary structure of phycocyanin from an α-helix to a β-folding, which kept the secondary structure from being destroyed by light, and thus had the effect of improving the stability of phycocyanin. Similarly, by employing a colorimeter to determine the statistical color difference and utilizing Fourier infrared analysis and fluorescence analysis for quantitative description, (S. Zhang et al., 2021a)) concluded that whey protein effectively protects phycocyanin from degradation at a pH of 3.0. Our research shares several similarities with this study; however, it broadens the scope by examining a diverse range of proteins rather than focusing solely on the enhancement of phycocyanin by whey proteins. We conduct a comparative analysis of the effects of various protein species on the stabilization of phycocyanin. This approach not only seeks to establish a universal application framework but also suggests that different proteins can be strategically combined with phycocyanin based on specific environmental conditions, thereby offering a more versatile reference for its application. Furthermore, we employed additional methodologies to quantitatively assess phycocyanin stability, identifying optimal assembly results at a protein-to-phycocyanin volume ratio of 2:1.

In this work, bovine serum albumin and whey protein both showed significant protective effect on the thermal stability and light stability of PC. Actually, it has been reported that phycocyanin-whey protein complexes can encapsulate the chromophore and decrease the damage caused by oxidizing radicals (S. Zhang et al., 2021b). Another investigation reported by Zhang et al. represented that the whey protein blocked the aggregation of phycocyanin and stabilized it at pH 3.0 (Z. Zhang et al., 2020). This effect may be due to that whey proteins protected the phycocyanin structure from being disrupted. In addition, Minic et al. studied the stability of phycocyanobilin (PCB) through binding to bovine serum albumin. They found out that although the PCB protein complex complex partly masked the antioxidant properties of PCB and BSA, a mutually protective effect against free radical-induced oxidation was found (Minic et al., 2018), which has been further verified by Radomirovic et al. in their research (Radomirovic et al., 2024). Based on this, the present study investigated the photostability and thermal stability of the PC-protein complex. The results further indicated that certain types of proteins, such as bovine serum albumin and whey protein, can serve as important carriers for stabilizing PC activity.

As a pigment protein, phycocyanin is distinguished by its unique blue hue, as well as its structural and nutritional attributes. It is noteworthy for its lack of odor and adverse reactions, alongside its non-toxic effects on the human body, and its alignment with principles of green and sustainable development. Consequently, there has been significant scholarly interest in its applications. Numerous studies have explored the incorporation of phycocyanin into ice cream (Campos Assumpção de Amarante et al., 2020), alcoholic beverages, and dairy products (Roy et al., 2024), with a particular focus on neutral or weakly acidic conditions, as these are common characteristics of many food and beverage products. However, our research extends beyond these conditions to investigate the stability of phycocyanin in alkaline environments. Through the interaction of numerous samples containing phycocyanin under varying conditions, our study identified sugar alcohols as the most effective stabilizing agents for phycocyanin in alkaline settings. These findings have the potential to broaden the application of phycocyanin in alkaline food products, such as baked goods, by enhancing its stability in such environments, which has good prospects for further utilization in the food industry. Hou et al. have demonstrated the potential of alkaline peptides derived from soybean globulin as natural food additives and preservatives (Roy et al., 2024). Building on these findings, our research explores the potential of phycocyanin as a food additive specifically for alkaline foods, thereby broadening its application in this domain.

Our study highlights the significance of phycocyanin's binding with other molecules, which is crucial for understanding the stability indicators of phycocyanin. Through molecular dynamics simulations conducted at specific temperatures and pressures, we observed that the movement of the complexes, phycocyanin gradually undergoes gradual aggregation over time. Concurrently, small molecules are adsorbed onto the surface of the aggregated phycocyanin, eventually forming clustered aggregates at the center. This phenomenon is likely due to the aggregation of small molecules within the hydrophobic regions of phycocyanin polypeptides, where they form hydrogen bonds with the polar residues of the polypeptides. Notably, there is a limited number of molecular dynamics studies focusing on proteins. D. Jeya Sundara Sharmila et al. investigated the interaction forces between the aphid transmission auxiliary component proteins of the cauliflower mosaic virus and the epidermal proteins of the peach aphid (Sharmila, n.d.). Hossain Shadman et al. conducted molecular dynamics simulations to elucidate the specifics of disulfide bonds between fish sperm proteins and DNA (Shadman et al., 2022). Juliette Martin et al. performed all-atom molecular dynamics simulations on protein-protein complexes across eight distinct functional classes, characterized by varying interface sizes in explicit solvents (Martin & Frezza, n.d.).

The present research could lay an important scientific foundation for the application of phycocyanin in the food industry. For instance, Algae from the genus Spirulina have been put to use in the pasta-manufacturing industry in order to produce enriched pasta with enhanced nutritional, sensory, and therapeutic benefits (Ghaeni, 2020). During the heating and maturation process of the dough products, the phycocyanin-protein complex can maximize the enhancement of its stability under high-temperature conditions, thereby maintaining the color stability of the foods. In addition, functional yogurt was made using dried spirulina at different concentrations (0.1 %,0.2 %, 0.3 %, and 0.5 %), with the concentration of 0.3 % giving the best positive effect in enhancing the nutritional value and improving the sensory characteristics. It showed the highest survivability of S. thermophilus and L. bulgaricus compared with the control throughout the storage period (Malik, 2011). The present study deviates from previous research on protein intermolecular interaction forces. Our findings demonstrated that phycocyanin interacts with small molecules under aggregation-based conditions, leading to a aggregation phenomenon. This novel dioscovery has not been previously reported. This observation is particularly significant as it offers a new perspective on the study of phycocyanin stability.
