The Effects of Distraction and Reappraisal on the Late Positive Potential Across Discrete Emotions: A Study With Latinx Children
Authors: Laura E Quiñones‐Camacho, Yelim Hong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study examined the effects of two emotion regulation (ER) strategies: reappraisal and distraction on the late positive potential (LPP) in a sample of Latinx children (n = 78, ages 8–11, 50% girls) across sadness, fear, and happiness contexts. We aimed to expand our understanding of ER strategies by (1) examining distraction as a regulatory strategy, (2) assessing discrete emotional contexts rather than aggregating negative emotions, and (3) including Latinx children, an underrepresented demographic in neuroscience and developmental work. Results showed that sadness elicited larger LPP amplitudes than other emotions, highlighting sadness as particularly salient emotion. ER strategies effectively reduced LPP amplitudes for sadness in the early processing window (300–700 ms) but failed to sustain this effect over time (700–3500 ms) and even appeared to intensify neural responses to sadness in later windows, suggesting developmental limitations in children's ability to maintain ER strategies across extended periods. Our findings add to existing work and offer novel and needed evidence of the discrete emotion‐dependent effects of ER strategies on the LPP in children.Keywords: childhood, discrete emotions, emotion regulation strategy, late positive potential (LPP), Latinx

Plain Language Summary

This study looked at how two different ways of managing emotions—reappraisal (rethinking the situation to change how you feel) and distraction (thinking about something else)—affect brain activity in Latinx children aged 8–11. We wanted to understand how these strategies work when children try to regulate specific emotions like sadness, fear, and happiness. We also aimed to include Latinx children, who are often underrepresented in neuroscience and developmental research. Our results showed that sadness resulted in the strongest brain response, suggesting that it is a particularly powerful emotion for children at this age. When children tried to manage their emotions, they were able to lower their brain's response to sadness at first. However, this effect did not last over time, and their brain's response to sadness actually grew stronger, suggesting that it may be hard for children to keep using these strategies for long periods. Our findings add to our understanding of children's emotion regulation by showing how two different strategies impact brain activity for sad, fear, and happy pictures in children.

Introduction

The ability to accurately and efficiently process emotional information is essential for adaptive functioning (Calkins 1994; Cole et al. 1994). A substantial body of work has shown that emotion recognition, knowledge, and regulation greatly develop throughout the childhood years (Broeren et al. 2011; Durand et al. 2007; Peltola et al. 2011). Impairments in these skills have been linked to poorer academic achievement, social functioning, and mental health problems (Denham et al. 2003; Ensor et al. 2011; Fine et al. 2003; Izard et al. 2001; Kujawa et al. 2014; Trentacosta and Fine 2010; Trentacosta et al. 2006), further supporting the importance of adaptive emotional processing and regulation for broader developmental outcomes. Research suggests that neural systems in the human brain have evolved to support rapid emotional processing by directing attention to information that would functionally increase the chances of survival (Bradley et al. 2003; Friedman and Förster 2010; Eimer et al. 2003; Lang et al. 1997; Öhman et al. 2000; Schupp et al. 2012). While most of this work has focused on adults, research with children suggests that the neural systems that facilitate the processing of emotional information are already established at birth and that even in infancy, the ability to discriminate across emotional expressions is developing (Leppänen and Nelson 2009; Tottenham 2017).

Electroencephalogram (EEG) is a particularly useful tool for studying the temporal dynamics of emotional processing as it provides precise temporal information at the millisecond scale. The late positive potential (LPP) is a slow positive deflection in the EEG waveform that appears approximately 400 ms after an affective stimulus is presented. The LPP is considered a neural marker of motivated attention to emotional information (Brown et al. 2012; Hajcak et al. 2010; Weinberg and Park 2024), with several studies showing that LPP amplitudes are larger in response to emotional compared to neutral stimuli (Dunning and Hajcak 2009; Hajcak and Dennis 2009; Hajcak and Nieuwenhuis 2006). Research on the LPP in childhood samples has shown that the LPP can be reliably measured in children (Bondy et al. 2018; Dennis and Hajcak 2009; Dennis et al. 2009; Hajcak and Dennis 2009; Hua et al. 2014; Kujawa, Klein, et al. 2012). For example, Hajcak and Dennis (2009) demonstrated that presenting affective stimuli to 5‐ to 8‐year‐olds elicits a positivity that is maximal at occipital–parietal recording sites, consistent with adult work with the LPP.

Importantly, research over the past decade has shown that the amplitude of the LPP can be modulated by the use of emotion regulation (ER) strategies (e.g., DeCicco et al. 2014; Dunning and Hajcak 2009; Foti and Hajcak 2008; Hajcak and Nieuwenhuis 2006). Research on how ER strategies modulate the LPP in children has exclusively focused on reappraisal—a cognitive ER strategy involving the reframing of negative events to alter their emotional impact—finding that older children show greater reductions in LPP amplitude after being instructed to use reappraisal (DeCicco et al. 2014, 2012; Van Cauwenberge et al. 2017). For example, Dennis and Hajcak (2009) showed that presenting 5‐ to 10‐year‐olds with neutral auditory interpretations while viewing unpleasant pictures (for the second time) resulted in decreases in the LPP. This approach has been replicated in several studies, with varying degrees of success (e.g., Babkirk et al. 2015; DeCicco et al. 2014, 2012; Van Cauwenberge et al. 2017). In another study, DeCicco and colleagues (2014) used the same directed reappraisal approach (i.e., presenting reappraisal stories prior to stimuli presentation) with a new and larger sample of 7‐ to 9‐years‐old but found inconsistent modulation effects of reappraisal on the LPP. Thus, while several studies have investigated how ER strategies modulate the LPP in children, all these studies have focused exclusively on a single ER strategy (i.e., reappraisal) and reported mixed findings regarding children's ability to modulate the LPP by effectively implementing the strategy (see Kennedy and Montreuil [2021] for a review).

Importantly, while most work on reappraisal in adults provides instructions on how to reappraise, allowing participants to choose how they modify their interpretation of the event or stimuli, research on the LPP in children has mostly used tasks that require children to remember or listen to a predefined reappraised version of the stimuli (e.g., audio‐recorded reappraisal scripts). This approach limits what can be inferred about children's ability to implement the strategy independently. In contrast, our approach emphasizes children's use of self‐generated reappraisal‐like strategies—where they are given broad instructions and encouraged to regulate their emotional response without being told exactly how to do so. For example, some children spontaneously reframed negative images by thinking “It is just a picture,” or “This did not really happen.” Others minimized the emotional impact by thinking, “It is not that bad,” or added a positive twist, such as imagining a happy ending. These self‐directed responses differ from previous methods that guide children through specific reappraisal narratives and suggest that children are capable of drawing on their own cognitive resources to reinterpret emotional stimuli. Supporting this, research using other physiological measures suggests that children younger than 12 can independently implement strategies, including reappraisal and distraction, when given broad instructions (e.g., “think about something happy” or “think about how it is not real”).(Davis et al. 2010, 2016). Thus, it is crucial to investigate how children implement reappraisal in tasks that allow them to independently choose their approach to reframing stimuli. Such research can bridge the gap between research on ER strategies and the LPP, and the broader body of literature on these associations. This approach will not only help clarify the developmental timeline of when children become able to effectively use ER strategies to modulate the LPP but also provide deeper insights into the LPP's role as a marker of children's developing ER abilities. Some evidence suggests that being able to effectively implement an adaptive strategy such as reappraisal early in childhood (participants were five to seven at Time 1) is associated with the concurrent and longitudinal use of other adaptive strategies across various emotional contexts (Babkirk et al. 2015). Although participants in this study varied in how effectively they implemented this strategy, the finding that the successful use of an adaptive strategy when instructed was related to the spontaneous use of another adaptive strategy 2 years later highlights the importance of understanding early ER strategies and their potential long‐term developmental impact.

While work on reappraisal and the LPP has significantly advanced our understanding of the temporal dynamics of ER in childhood, reappraisal is not the only strategy that children use to manage their emotions after negative events (Davis et al. 2016; Quiñones‐Camacho and Davis 2018). Distraction—shifting attention away from emotional stimuli by thinking about something else—is a particularly common strategy children use to regulate during emotional situations (Davis et al. 2010, 2016). As an ER strategy, distraction operates primarily through attentional mechanisms, redirecting cognitive resources away from emotionally salient stimuli toward neutral or task‐irrelevant information. This attentional shift is particularly relevant in the context of the LPP, an event‐related potential (ERP) that reflects sustained attention to emotionally stimuli (Hajcak et al. 2010). Because the LPP is sensitive to changes in attentional allocation, distraction is a theoretically meaningful strategy to examine within LPP paradigms. Despite its relevance, distraction has received comparatively less attention than reappraisal in developmental LPP research.

A few studies that examined children's use of distraction during experimental procedures have demonstrated that children are able to deploy distraction strategies in a way comparable to their use of reappraisal (Davis et al. 2016; Parsafar et al. 2019; Braet et al. 2024). For example, a study with adolescents by Braet and colleagues (2024) found that distraction resulted in larger increases in positive affect during the initial minutes of stress recovery, although both distraction and reappraisal were similarly effective in reducing negative affect and promoting respiratory sinus arrhythmia recovery. While these findings are with adolescents, they serve as one of a handful of studies demonstrating that youth are capable of implementing distraction during experimental studies. Furthermore, studies with adults demonstrated that distraction can successfully modulate the LPP, underscoring its utility in tasks that require rapid regulation of emotional responses (Dunning and Hajcak 2009; Schindler et al. 2022; Thiruchselvam et al. 2011).

However, despite the effectiveness of distraction in the short term—particularly in lab‐based tasks that require immediate regulation—it differs from reappraisal in both cognitive demands and long‐term implications for psychological outcomes. Distraction may be easier for children to implement than reappraisal because it requires less advanced cognitive processing. Empirical research with adults suggests that distraction tends to be more effective in the moment, particularly because it places fewer demands on working memory and executive function (Sheppes and Meiran 2008; Strauss et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2022). While distraction is generally effective in reducing negative affect in the short term (Webb et al. 2012; Gross 2015), it may become maladaptive when habitually used to avoid emotional experiences. Overreliance on distraction may hinder the processing of meaningful or recurring emotional information and has been linked to risk for psychopathology, particularly anxiety (Sheppes et al. 2014; Wilson and Gilbert 2008). In contrast, reappraisal—a more sophisticated cognitive strategy—has been associated with greater emotional resilience and long‐term emotional psychological adjustment.

Notably, some studies suggest that distraction may be more adaptive than reappraisal when coping with stressful or traumatic experiences. Reappraisal demands substantial self‐regulatory effort and is associated with increased sympathetic nervous system activation, whereas distraction limits emotional processing of information and facilitates more effective regulation of high‐intensity stimuli (Sheppes and Meiran 2008; Sheppes et al. 2009). Crucially, the effectiveness of any given strategy depends on the context; individuals’ ability to flexibly select the most appropriate strategy plays a central role in successful ER (Aldao et al. 2015; Zhang et al. 2022). Thus, although both strategies can modulate the LPP, their distinct cognitive mechanisms and psychological consequences underscore the importance of examining their unique neural correlates. The current study aims to directly compare LPP modulation in response to reappraisal versus distraction to enhance our understanding of how these commonly used strategies operate at the neural level in children. While we acknowledge children's emerging capacity to implement cognitively demanding strategies like reappraisal, we predicted that distraction would be more effective due to its relative ease of use and lower cognitive demands.

Additionally, a growing body of research on children's developing ER suggests that children use information about the specific emotional context (e.g., sadness vs. fear) to guide their regulatory choices (Quiñones‐Camacho and Davis 2020), but LPP tasks often use broad “negative” conditions, lumping different negative emotion contexts into a single category. While this approach maximizes statistical power and minimizes the complexity of comparing multiple negative contexts, it obscures potentially significant developmental differences in how children implement reappraisal and distraction across distinct emotional contexts. This distinction is important, as children may find it easier to regulate certain emotions over others (Davis et al. 2010). For example, reappraisal is considered more effective for managing sadness, a lower arousal, socially oriented emotion (Davis et al. 2010; Waters and Thompson 2014), while distraction may be more useful for regulating high‐arousal emotions like fear, especially in younger children who may have limited cognitive resources (Sheppes et al. 2014). Happiness was included as a positively valenced emotional context to assess whether children regulate positively arousing stimuli differently than negatively valenced ones. Including both positive and negative emotional contexts provides a more comprehensive view of how children deploy regulation strategies across emotions that vary in arousal, salience, and familiarity. Despite these meaningful differences, most previous LPP work has primarily focused on the use of reappraisal for the regulation of broad negative emotions, leaving open the question of how distraction functions across emotion types. To address this gap, the current study not only compares reappraisal and distraction but also incorporates emotional context (sadness, fear, happiness) as an important within‐person factor that could influence the implementation of ER strategies.

The Role of Sociocultural Context in the Neural Underpinnings of Emotional Processing

While most research on the LPP has focused on primarily White and middle‐class samples, there is a growing body of evidence on how the sociocultural context influences emotional processing and its neural underpinnings (Kitayama and Salvador 2017; Matsumoto et al. 2008; Mesquita 2007). Evidence suggests that norms for what constitutes an optimal reaction to an emotional event and the desirability of certain emotions vary across cultures (Kitayama et al. 2006). Although little work on the LPP has looked at this, existing studies suggest that the sociocultural context can influence LPP amplitudes (Myruski et al. 2019; Murata et al. 2013). For example, in a study with European and Asian Americans, LPP amplitudes following unpleasant images were similar across cultural groups. However, only the Asian American group reduced LPP amplitudes when using emotional suppression (a strategy that is more commonly used in Asian cultures; Murata et al. 2013). To our knowledge, only one study has explicitly examined cultural differences in LPP responses in children, comparing samples of 5‐ to 7‐year‐old children from the United States and Japan (Myruski et al. 2019). This study found that Japanese children had significantly greater LPP amplitudes to neutral pictures compared to children from the United States, while US children showed greater reappraisal‐induced reductions in the LPP (Myruski et al. 2019). While a small body of work, these studies suggest that cultural expectations and experiences with emotion and ER significantly influence neurocognitive processes. Given this, it is reasonable to expect that LPP amplitudes and the successful modulation of the LPP by ER strategies may look different across ethnic groups.

Historically, research on cultural differences in psychology has focused on comparisons between European Americans and East Asian, even within US samples (e.g., Murata et al. 2013). This focus has greatly limited our understanding of how other cultural groups common in the United States process and regulate emotions. Notably, Latinx individuals represent the fastest‐growing demographic group in the United States, with an estimated one in four children identifying as Latinx (Ramirez et al. 2024); yet, these children are often underrepresented in this type of work. There is evidence suggesting that while Latinx culture shares some interdependent values with East Asian cultures, the ways in which Latinx individuals process emotional information are distinct (Kitayama and Salvador 2017). For example, Latinx individuals are more likely to value high‐arousal emotions, positive ones in particular, as a way of strengthening social ties and report experiencing significantly more positive and negative emotions than other cultures (Soto et al. 2005, 2011).

Differences in the regulation of emotions have also been found. For example, cross‐cultural behavioral work on ER has found that Latinx adults (specifically, Mexican Americans) report lower well‐being and life satisfaction when they suppress negative emotions compared to Chinese adults (Su et al. 2015). However, there is only one study, to our knowledge, that has explored the neural processing of emotions using the LPP in a Latinx sample (Hampton et al. 2021). In this study, European American, Chinese and Chinese American, and Mexican and Mexican American undergraduates completed an ER task where they were asked to either up‐ or downregulate their emotional response to positive and negative images. They found that Mexican and Mexican American participants were able to upregulate both positive and negative emotions in a similar way as European American participants, while Chinese and Chinese American participants were unable to do so (Hampton et al. 2021). They also found that only the Mexican and Mexican American participants were able to downregulate positive emotions, an effect that was absent for both the European American and the Chinese and Chinese American groups (Hampton et al. 2021).

Importantly, the ER task in Hampton et al.’s study involved instructions to suppress, diminish, or hide visible emotional reactions. These suppression instructions, while framed as emotion‐hiding strategies, likely engaged attentional disengagement mechanisms, suggesting functional overlap with distraction. That is, although labeled as suppression, the regulatory strategy may have operated in part through attentional shifts away from emotionally salient content—akin to distraction. Thus, while these findings highlight the effective use of suppression in Mexican and Mexican American adults, this does not imply that these are the only or most accessible strategies across development. In childhood, cognitively demanding strategies like reappraisal may be harder to implement due to the ongoing development of executive function. In contrast, distraction may be a cognitively accessible strategy, involving a more automatic or implicit shift of attention away from emotional stimuli. Thus, the current study examines whether distraction may be more easily implemented and effective for children in emotionally challenging situations. Finally, while cultural groups, on average, varied in their ability to modulate the LPP to both positive and negative stimuli, there was within‐cultural group variability in the implementation of these strategies. This finding suggests that an approach that focuses on variability within an ethnic group (i.e., Latinx) may better help us understand emotional processing and regulation within these cultural groups.

Importantly, while much of the work on differences in emotional processing in Latinx individuals comes from work in adults, a growing body of work suggests that Latinx children are socialized to express and regulate emotions differently from other ethnic groups (Ispa et al. 2004; Perez Rivera and Dunsmore 2011). For example, in studies with Mexican American mothers and other ethnic groups, Mexican American mothers did not differ in the frequency with which they discussed emotions with their children, but they did differ in the nature of these conversations (Eisenberg 1999; Flannagan and Perese 1998). Mexican American mothers were more likely to discuss emotions in relation to interpersonal relationships than African American mothers (Flannagan and Perese 1998) and less likely than European American mothers to discuss the child's emotions, instead focusing more on their own emotions (Eisenberg 1999). This suggests that Latinx children may not only think about and perceive emotional information differently but may also exhibit differences in more automatic measures of emotional processing, such as the LPP. Our study is consistent with a growing body of work that calls to examine the experience of Latinx (and other racial and ethnic groups) on its own, rather than as a comparison to other ethnic/racial groups (Dotson and Duarte 2020; Garcini et al. 2022). This approach allows for a more nuanced understanding of the unique factors shaping ER and neural processing within this group.

The Current Study

The current study explored Latinx children's implementation of reappraisal and distraction during sad, fear, and happy emotional stimuli among children aged 8–11. The LPP can be modulated by ER strategies such as reappraisal beginning in middle childhood, though more consistent modulation effects tend to emerge in late childhood and adolescence (Deng et al. 2019). This pattern is consistent with developmental models of ER, which propose that between ages 8 and 11, children transition from primarily autonomic, caregiver‐supported regulation to more deliberate, self‐initiated strategies (Elsayed et al. 2023). This age range represents a critical window during which children develop the cognitive and neural capacities required to effectively engage in regulatory strategies, such as reappraisal and distraction. Thus, children in this age range were thus selected because they demonstrate increasing autonomy in regulating emotions and greater reliance on goal‐directed strategies, which can be reliably assessed using both behavioral and neural measures such as the LPP.

This study expands upon previous research on the LPP in children in three important ways. To our knowledge, this is the first study to (1) examine the effects of distraction on the LPP in a sample of children, (2) explore differences in LPP modulation across discrete emotions (e.g., sadness, fear, and happiness) using ER strategies, and (3) assess LPP amplitudes in a sample of Latinx children. First, we expected that children instructed to use distraction would find this strategy easier to implement, resulting in greater modulation of the LPP compared to children implementing reappraisal. Second, we expected that children in our sample would show similar patterns of LPP amplitudes—specifically, greater LPP amplitudes to emotional stimuli compared to neutral—as reported in previous work with similarly aged children. Lastly, based on a functionalist view of emotion that posits that emotions vary in their motivational significance, we expected to observe differences in how effectively children were able to implement each strategy across discrete emotions. However, given the limited prior research directly comparing regulation strategy use across distinct emotions in children, this hypothesis was considered exploratory.

Method

Participants

A total of 78 (50% girls) children, identified by their parents as Latinx or part Latinx and aged between 8.00 and 11.92 years, were recruited for this study (M age = 9.91; SD age = 1.14). Caregivers were asked only whether they identified the child as Hispanic, Latino/a/x/e, or Chicano(a), without specifying their nationality. Participants were recruited from a database of families interested in research studies and from community events in the Southern California area. Caregivers identified 74% of children as solely Latinx and 26% as both Latinx and another ethnic group. Families were primarily low‐ to middle‐SES, with 16% reporting a household income below $15,000, 28% between $15,000 and $30,000, 19% between $30,000 and $50,000, and 37% above $50,000. Only one caregiver per child was asked to participate, resulting in 71 female and seven male caregivers taking part in the study. Among male caregivers, 24% had not completed high school, 24% held a high school diploma, 37% had some college education, 4% had completed college, and 7% had some graduate training or a graduate degree, and 4% did not provide education information. For female caregivers, 22% had not completed high school, 12% had a high school diploma, 31% had some college education, 9% had completed college, and 15% had some graduate training or a graduate degree, and 11% did not provide educational information.

Procedure

Study procedures were approved by the institutional review board (HRRB code: HS 17‐124). Written consent was obtained from the participating caregiver, and both written and verbal assent were obtained from children before study procedures took place. Families came to the laboratory for a 3‐h visit. Children were randomly assigned to one of two ER conditions: distraction or reappraisal. At the end of the study, families were debriefed, thanked, and given a $40 compensation for their participation, and children received a small toy of their choosing.

Materials

Stimuli Selection

A total of 160 images were chosen from the International Affective Picture System (IAPS; Lang et al. 2008) and the Nencki Affective Picture System (NAPS; Marchewka et al. 2014) to create four emotional categories of stimuli (fear, sad, neutral, and happy). A list of identification numbers for the images is shown in Supporting Information S1. Each category consisted of 40 images, allowing for 20 images of each emotion to be used in each task (passive viewing and regulation) without repeating any image. A similar approach has previously been used to assess LPP amplitudes for discrete emotions in adults (Quiñones‐Camacho et al. 2018). Images were selected from two different picture systems due to the limited availability of age‐appropriate images for each discrete emotional context in the more traditionally used IAPS. Information on valence, arousal, and discrete emotion ratings for pictures on both stimulus sets was used to derive a set of discrete emotion images that were then checked by undergraduate RAs in the lab for appropriateness. These RAs also rated a subset of images to ensure images selected primarily belonged to a single emotion category. To further ensure the stimuli was appropriate for our target sample, two Latinx 10‐year‐olds, who did not take part in the actual study, piloted the task (and other tasks in the larger study) and reported on the emotional type and intensity of the images using the same scale used by the participants in the study; ratings from these children and follow‐up questions regarding their thoughts about the pictures confirmed that the included images were appropriate for children and elicited the intended emotions. In addition, the caregivers of these two children were asked to view the images and report on their appropriateness to confirm that caregivers thought the pictures were appropriate for the target age range.

Emotion Self‐Report

Before beginning any study procedures, children were trained to self‐report their emotions. They were introduced to a face scale for each emotion (sadness, fear, and happiness), with each face representing a different intensity level (e.g., not at all sad). Each emotion face was accompanied by a corresponding label at the bottom (e.g., “not at all”) and a numerical value (e.g., a 1 for not at all). The experimenter explained each emotion scale in detail and provided the child with an opportunity to practice their emotion self‐report by prompting the child to indicate which face they would choose if they felt different levels of an emotion. The emotion self‐report training was initially conducted before the EEG procedures and was repeated right before the task to ensure children knew which button to press when asked to indicate how they felt. During both EEG tasks, children were asked to indicate the intensity of their feelings of sadness, fear, and happiness on a 4‐point scale (4 = very; 1 = not at all) after each block.

Passive Picture‐Viewing Task

The picture‐viewing task (Figure 1) was designed based on similar LPP tasks and was presented using E‐Prime software. Children were told that they would be playing a game and were instructed to pay close attention to the screen. A total of 80 trials were presented, 20 for each emotion (neutral, sad, fear, happy). This number of trials was selected based on prior research showing that for large ERP components, such as the LPP, as few as eight trials are sufficient to produce reliable ERPs, with the ability to detect significant effects increasing with more trials (Boudewyn et al. 2018; Huffmeijer et al. 2014). Each trial began with a white fixation cross at the center of a black screen for 2000 ms, followed by an instruction cue (“view”) for 2500 ms. The sequence of pictures within each block was randomized, along with the order of blocks (except for the neutral block, which was always presented first). Children were instructed to simply “pay attention to the pictures.” These trials were intended to elicit natural, uncontrolled emotional responses. Following the instruction cue, a black screen was presented for a 750‐ms pretrial interval. Each image then appeared on the full screen and remained on screen for 3500 ms. After each picture, a black screen was presented again for a 1000‐ms posttrial interval before the next trial began. Children were given a resting period between blocks to allow time for affective recovery and to blink and relax.

Trial structure for the passive and ER tasks (all pictures within a block belonged to the same emotion condition).

ER Strategy Training

After a short break following the passive viewing task, the experimenter explained the ER instructions corresponding to each child's assigned condition (reappraise or distract) (Supporting Information S2). Children only completed one regulation task (reappraise or distract). To ensure their understanding of the ER instructions, children were guided through six practice trials using images that were not included in the main tasks. The experimenter gave clear instructions and examples of how to implement the strategy, asked children to explain how they would apply the strategy during each practice trial, and provided feedback after each regulatory attempt. All children completed the six practice trials, even if they got the instructions right on the first attempt. Children were told that they would see instructions to “change” (i.e., reappraise or distract) their thoughts about each image and that they should wait to implement the ER strategy on change trials until the picture appeared on the screen. The task did not start until children correctly described using the instructed strategy on at least two consecutive practice trials. If needed, up to three additional practice trials were given. All children ultimately demonstrated correct use of their assigned strategy.

ER Picture‐Viewing Task

This task followed the same format as the passive viewing task. Each of the 80 trials began with a white fixation cross displayed at the center of a black screen for 2000 ms, followed by an instruction cue (“change”) displayed for 2500 ms. After the instruction offset, each image appeared on 75% of the screen for 3500 ms. At the end of each block, children were asked to indicate the intensity of feelings they experienced during this task (using the same age‐appropriate scale introduced earlier). Each of the four picture types (fear, sad, neutral, happy) was presented in separate blocks. Each block consisted of 20 “change” (distraction or reappraisal) trials. The sequence of pictures within each block was randomized, along with the order of blocks (except for the neutral block, which was always presented first). Children were given a resting period between blocks to allow time for affective recovery and to blink and relax. During the change trials, half of the participants were instructed to “reappraise” and half to “distract” based on the strategy‐specific training they had received (all children saw the word “change”; Supporting Information S2). For the reappraise group, participants were instructed to feel neutral toward the images by thinking about the image in a different way, such as “Think how the image is not real, how it is not a big deal because it is just a picture. For example, you can think about how it is from a movie, how it is posed so it is not a big deal, or how it is just a picture and nothing will happen to you.” These distancing‐based instruction are consistent with prior research on developmentally appropriate reappraisal strategies for children (Davis et al. 2017). In contrast, for the distraction group, participants were instructed to redirect their attention entirely away from the image: “Thinking of something boring instead of the picture. For example, you can think about a circle, the color white, or the number three. Anything that you can think of that doesn't make you feel any kind of way is ok to think about instead of the pictures, ok?” (Davis et al. 2017). To address how effectively distraction and reappraisal modulated the LPP, we used a mixed‐design ANOVA comparing view and regulate conditions across each emotion and time segment as within‐person factors and strategy as a between‐person factor.

EEG Recording, Data Reduction, and Analysis

Children were capped with a Brain Products Acti‐CHamp 32 active electrode system using the 10–20 system. Children were told to ask any questions about the electrodes and the cap and were given instructions to minimize movement and blinking during this part of the study. After this, children were given a couple of minutes to acclimate to wearing the electrodes and the EEG chamber before the picture‐viewing task began. Continuous EEG recordings were made using the Brain Vision system, sampled at 500 Hz. The left earlobe was used as the reference during recording. Offline processing of EEG data was carried out using Brain Vision Analyzer 2. All data were later re‐referenced to the average of both earlobes and band‐pass‐filtered using 0.1 Hz (high pass) and 30 Hz (low pass) cutoffs with a 60‐Hz notch filter. Eye movements and blinks were corrected using the Gratton et al. (1983) method, commonly used in LPP research (Hajcak et al. 2013; Thiruchselvam et al. 2011). Event‐locked EEG epochs were extracted, starting 750 ms before stimulus onset (standard 20% of the trial duration) and continuing for the entire duration of the trial (3500 ms). Segments were baseline corrected using the first 750 ms before stimulus. Artifact rejection was performed semiautomatically for all EEG data by using the following criteria: a voltage step of more than 50 µV between sample points, a voltage difference within a trial greater than 300 µV, a maximal voltage difference smaller than 50 µV within a 100‐ms interval, and an amplitude +100 µV within a 100‐ms interval (Hajcak et al. 2013; Quiñones‐Camacho et al. 2018; Weinberg and Hajcak 2011). Any trials with excessive physiological artifacts (e.g., excessive movement) identified with the previously described criteria were rejected before averaging the segments for each block. A minimum of nine trials per each block was required for inclusion in analyses. After artifact rejection, the remaining segments were averaged to create the ERPs of interest for each emotion type. Based on previous research with children, the LPP was defined as the average activity of the PO7, PO8, O1, O2, and Oz electrodes (DeCicco et al. 2014; Van Cauwenberge et al. 2017). LPP was defined as the maximum electrical activity in the 300–3500 ms time window following picture onset (similar approaches have been used in other studies with children; DeCicco et al.2012). Following the approach of previous studies (DeCicco et al. 2012; van den Heuvel et al. 2018), we divided the 300–3500 window into four segments for analyses (300–700, 700–1200, 1200–2000, and 2000–3500 ms).

Results

Missing Data

Missing data were due primarily to the child not having usable EEG data. Three children refused to do the EEG portion of the study, nine children completed both tasks but had excessive artifacts that resulted in insufficient number of trials for inclusion in the analyses, one child refused to do the regulate task, and one child completed both tasks but refused to see the fear pictures. This resulted in a total of 64 children with full data, 33 in the distraction group and 31 in the reappraisal group. Children with usable EEG data did not differ from children without usable data in demographic variables (i.e., age, gender, income).

Primary Analyses

The results are divided into two major sections. The first section describes analyses of the emotion self‐reports as a manipulation check to confirm that the intended discrete emotions were elicited. The second section describes analyses of the LPP data to examine the effects of task (i.e., view vs. regulate), discrete emotion (i.e., neutral, sad, fear, happy), and strategy (i.e., reappraisal vs. distraction) on the LPP. We then conducted simple effects contrasts for each time window, as follow‐up analyses by time window are common in LPP studies (e.g., Van Cauwenberge et al. 2017; DeCicco et al. 2014). This approach allowed us to fully disentangle the effects of emotion (i.e., neutral, sad, fear, happy), task (i.e., view vs. regulate), and strategy (i.e., reappraisal vs. distraction) for each time window separately.

Behavioral Results

A mixed‐design ANOVA was conducted to compare intensity ratings of the emotions felt (sadness, fear, or happiness; a within‐subject factor) after the view‐neutral block, after the target emotion in the view task, and after the target emotion in the regulate task (another within‐subject factor). This ANOVA also included a between‐subject factor of strategy to assess the effect of each ER instruction on self‐reported emotion. The main effect of emotion (i.e., picture type; sad, fear, or happy) was significant, F(2, 132) = 69.337, p < 0.001, η 2 = 0.512, indicating discrete emotional differences in self‐reported emotional intensity across emotion blocks. The main effect of task was also significant F(2, 132) = 20.091, p < 0.001, η 2 = 0.233, indicating differences in emotional intensity across the neutral, view, and regulate trials. These main effects, however, were qualified by a significant task‐by‐emotion interaction, F(4, 264) = 6.820, p < 0.001, η 2 = 0.094, suggesting that changes in emotional intensity varied depending on the specific emotion. Interactions with the ER strategy condition (distraction vs. reappraisal) were all nonsignificant, Fs < 1.743, ps > 0.179, η 2s = 0.026.

Summary of Results

To summarize our results, we successfully elicited sadness, happiness, and, to a lesser extent, fear in our sample of Latinx children, as reflected in both behavioral and neural measures. Consistent with previous studies, we identified an occipital LPP in children across all emotion conditions (Figures 2 and 3). We only found significant effects of task (view vs. regulate) in the expected direction (i.e., a reduction of LPP amplitudes) during the 300–700 ms window. Specifically, LPP amplitudes were significantly smaller for sad pictures during the regulate task compared to the view task in the 300–700 ms window only. Contrary to our hypotheses, we found significant hindering effects for neutral pictures (700–1200, 1200–2000, and 2000–3500 ms segments) and to a lesser extent for sad pictures (1200–2000 and 2000–3500 ms segments). To further explore these findings, we conducted exploratory follow‐up analyses to assess whether distraction and reappraisal differed on their effect on the LPP during the regulate task (see Supporting Information S3). While no significant task by strategy interactions emerged, patterns of simple effects do point to potential differences in the effect of distraction and reappraisal. Specifically, reappraisal was more effective at reducing LPP amplitudes for sadness in the first segment, while distraction resulted in stronger hindering effects across neutral and sad pictures. However, since the overall interactions of task × strategy were not significant, these results should be interpreted with caution. Thus, altogether, we found no significant differences in the effect of reappraisal and distraction on LPP amplitudes across emotions.

Discussion

The current study investigated how Latinx children use reappraisal and distraction by examining differences in LPP modulation during sad, fear, and happy contexts. Our study addressed three major gaps in the literature. First, we examined the effects of distraction on the LPP in children, extending research that has largely centered on reappraisal. Second, we explored discrete emotional contexts (i.e., fear, happy, and sad) rather than grouping various “negative” emotions into a single category, allowing us to assess the modulation of the LPP by two ER strategies across distinct emotions. Third, we assessed LPP amplitudes in a sample of Latinx children. The focus on Latinx children adds an important dimension to this study, as research often underrepresents this rapidly growing demographic in the United States (Zong 2022). We hypothesized that (1) children in our sample would show similar patterns of LPP amplitudes to those observed in previous studies (greater LPP amplitudes to emotional stimuli compared to neutral) with similarly aged children; (2) children instructed to use distraction would find it easier to implement the strategy, resulting in greater modulation of the LPP; and (3) there would be differences in how effectively children would be able to implement each strategy across discrete emotions.

First, we examined self‐reported emotions as a manipulation check to confirm whether the intended discrete emotions were elicited. Each block of emotional stimuli successfully elicited the targeted emotion during the view task. Specifically, fear pictures elicited significantly higher self‐reported fear compared to neutral pictures, and sad pictures elicited significantly higher self‐reported sadness than neutral pictures. However, happy pictures did not elicit significantly higher happiness ratings compared to neutral pictures. To further investigate the implementation of ER strategies, we compared the self‐reported emotions between the view and regulate tasks for the reappraisal and distraction groups. In the reappraisal group, self‐reported ratings of fear, sadness, and happiness remained unchanged between the view and regulate tasks. In contrast, the distraction group showed significant reductions in self‐reported fear and sadness ratings from view to regulate tasks, with happiness ratings showing a marginal decrease. These findings highlight the differential impacts of reappraisal and distraction on emotional modulation, as reflected in self‐reported measures.

Second, we investigated LPP amplitudes during emotion processing across sad, fear, and happy contexts. Consistent with previous studies, emotional pictures successfully elicited an LPP in occipital electrodes as expected. Interestingly, LPP amplitudes were larger for sad pictures compared to neutral pictures across all time windows (300–3500 ms). Specifically, sad pictures elicited larger LPP amplitudes than both fear and happy pictures in the 300–700 ms windows and larger than fear in the 700–1200 and 1200–1200 ms segments, indicating a particularly robust early neural response to sadness. To our knowledge, only one other study has examined LPP differences during discrete emotion processing, focusing on a sample of undergraduate students. That study found that LPP amplitudes were smaller for neutral pictures compared to sad, fear, and happy pictures in early and middle windows. Specifically, fear pictures elicited greater LPP amplitudes compared to sad and happy pictures in the early stages of processing (Quiñones‐Camacho and Wu 2018), contrasting with our findings that sad pictures elicited larger LPP amplitudes than fear and happy pictures.

In contrast, our study consistently found larger LPP amplitudes for sad pictures than for any other emotions, while LPP amplitudes for fear pictures were not consistently larger than for other emotions. These differences may reflect age‐related differences in emotion processing. For example, sadness may be more easily elicited than fear in children of this age group, potentially facilitating more efficient ER and sustained attentional engagement. Research indicates that during this developmental period, children more frequently use and understand the term “sad” compared to “fear,” suggesting a greater familiarity and accessibility of sadness as an emotional experience (Grosse et al. 2021). In contrast, fear may elicit more intense autonomic arousal and be more difficult for children to cognitively modulate, especially when the perceived threat is ambiguous or abstract. In comparison, young adults may be more attuned to detecting threat‐related stimuli and thus process fear stimuli more rapidly and robustly. It is also possible that the fear‐inducing pictures used in our study were less effective in eliciting fear‐related processing in this age group. Notably, children in our study rated the neutral pictures as relatively positive, indicating that these “neutral” images may not have provided a true affective baseline. This discrepancy in valence perception could explain the lack of consistent LPP differences between fear and neutral images. An alternative interpretation is that children's emotional processing may prioritize sadness over fear, contrasting with findings in undergraduate samples where fear often elicits stronger responses (Quiñones‐Camacho et al. 2018). This could reflect developmental differences in the relevance or salience of sadness, which may engage more sustained attention in childhood. Of course, it is possible that a task that more strongly elicited fear may have shown different effects, and future work is needed to clarify this.

Next, we examined the impact of two ER strategies—reappraisal and distraction—on LPP amplitudes across sad, fear, and happy contexts. Because we failed to find a main effect or significant interactions with strategy, we collapsed across both strategies for our follow‐up analyses. We found only one effect that was consistent with our hypotheses, and this was that our ER strategies significantly reduced the LPP for sad pictures in the 300–700 ms segment, suggesting that implementing an ER strategy was effective in dampening neural responses to sadness only early on. Exploratory analyses conducted to assess whether this effect varied between distraction and reappraisal revealed that reappraisal significantly modulated the LPP, while distraction emerged as only marginal. However, due to the lack of significant interaction effects, these results should be interpreted with caution. The finding that the strategies were only effective at dampening the LPP during the earliest segment is consistent with previous studies on reappraisal that have also found reappraisal to modulate only the earliest window of the LPP in children (DeCicco et al. 2014, 2012; Van Cauwenberge et al. 2017). Notably, our study extends this work by using a more ecologically valid approach for assessing children's implementation of ER strategies. Unlike prior research, which often employed directed reappraisal (where children receive structured guidance on how to reinterpret emotional content), we instructed children to apply reappraisal and distraction independently (after training). This self‐directed approach may better reflect real‐world scenarios but might also explain the lack of sustained reappraisal effects across all time windows, as children may still be developing the skills necessary for effective, autonomous reappraisal. The observed limitation of ER strategy effects to the early processing window (300–700 ms) may reflect the developmental trajectory of ER skills in this age group. Younger children might be able to initiate the independent implementation of these strategies but lack the cognitive resources to maintain its effects over extended periods. Alternatively, the absence of prolonged effects might indicate that the strategies employed by children were less sophisticated or varied in effectiveness, resulting in a transient rather than sustained modulation of LPP responses. Interestingly, our findings are consistent with previous work showing that children report that reappraisal is more effective for managing sadness compared to other emotions (e.g., Davis et al. 2010; Waters and Thompson 2014).

Existing research on children has primarily focused on reappraisal to examine its effects on LPP modulation, with findings indicating that the ability to reduce LPP amplitude through reappraisal strengthens with age. Specifically, older children tend to show greater reductions in LPP amplitude following reappraisal instructions (DeCicco et al. 2014, 2012; Van Cauwenberge et al. 2017). However, there is still uncertainty about the precise age at which children can effectively use ER strategies, such as reappraisal, to consistently modulate the LPP. For example, while one study with 8‐ to 9‐year‐olds reported the expected reduction in LPP amplitude after a reappraisal condition (DeCicco et al. 2014), another study with a broader age range (8–15 years) found that only children aged 12 or older showed significant reductions in LPP following reappraisal (Van Cauwenberge et al. 2017). Given this developmental trajectory, it is perhaps unsurprising that we did not observe consistent LPP modulation across all time windows in our sample of 8‐ to 11‐year‐olds, as children in this age range may still be developing the ability to effectively implement cognitive strategies in a prolonged and effective manner. For instance, maintaining the reappraisal narrative in working memory long enough to influence the LPP over extended periods might be particularly demanding at younger ages. This age‐related variability underscores the need for further research to precisely identify when children can reliably apply reappraisal in ER tasks and sustain its effects.

Interestingly, we found that our strategies not only failed to reduce the LPP but also appeared to elicit larger amplitudes in later segments for neutral and sad pictures. For neutral pictures, this hindering effect appeared consistent across strategies. However, exploratory analyses of the later segments for sad pictures revealed that distraction had a greater hindering effect than reappraisal. This finding suggests that distraction did not effectively mitigate the emotional salience of sadness and may even have enhanced engagement with the emotion over prolonged periods. As mentioned above, these exploratory analyses should be interpreted with caution due to the lack of significant task by strategy interactions. This pattern aligns, to some extent, with findings from DeCicco et al. (2014), who reported that directed reappraisal in children generated larger LPP amplitudes than a neutral baseline. Our results suggest that sustained attempts at distraction and reappraisal, particularly without external guidance, did not effectively reduce neural engagement with sad and neutral pictures but rather increased attentional processing. Moreover, the open‐ended nature of the instruction may have introduced additional cognitive demand, ultimately increasing attentional engagement rather than diminishing it. Overall, these findings highlight developmental limitations in children's ability to use distraction and reappraisal independently as effective regulatory strategies for quick emotional modulation. While these strategies may be behaviorally feasible, the observed neural data suggest that children may struggle to effectively sustain independent regulatory attempts in the context of an LPP task. The discrepancy between behavioral self‐reports and neural LPP findings highlights the complexity of ER processes in children. While distraction appeared effective in reducing self‐reported fear and sadness, the lack of corresponding reductions in LPP amplitudes suggests that the neural processing of emotional stimuli persisted. This divergence may reflect the differential sensitivity of these measures: self‐reports capture conscious emotional experiences, whereas the LPP reflects sustained neural engagement with emotional salience at an implicit level. Additionally, developmental differences may play a role, as children may not yet possess the cognitive maturity to fully implement these strategies effectively across both behavioral and neural levels. Together, these findings underscore the importance of integrating behavioral and neural measures to comprehensively assess ER strategies.

A contribution of this study was our examination of the effects of distraction as a regulatory strategy on LPP amplitudes across sad, fear, happy, and neutral contexts. As reported above, distraction did not emerge as a strong ER strategy for modulating the LPP in our sample. To our knowledge, no studies have specifically examined the impact of distraction on LPP amplitudes in children, despite evidence that children can use strategies like distraction similarly to reappraisal (Davis et al. 2016; Parsafar et al. 2019; Braet et al. 2024). Although previous research has demonstrated that children are capable of deploying distraction as an ER strategy in behavioral paradigms, the neurophysiological effects of distraction on LPP modulation during emotional processing remain unexplored in this age group. Studies with adults have shown that effective distraction strategies can modulate LPP amplitudes in the expected direction (Dunning and Hajcak 2009; Schindler et al. 2022; Thiruchselvam et al. 2011), indicating successful ER. However, our current findings suggest that children aged 8–11 may struggle to deploy distraction effectively in the context of our LPP task. It is possible that in the context of our task, where children were asked to look directly at the images, engaging in distraction may have been too demanding. Another possible explanation for the lack of distraction effects is that fear, as a discrete emotion, may be less amenable to regulation through distraction. Fear is evolutionarily tied to survival and is associated with heightened autonomic arousal and increased attentional vigilance toward threat‐related cues. These characteristics may make fear more resistant to top‐down regulatory strategies, particularly distraction, which requires shifting attention away from emotionally salient stimuli (Davis et al. 2016). In contrast, sadness may be more modulated through attentional strategies, given its lower physiological intensity and potentially more familiar nature in childhood. Supporting this, Davis et al. (2016) found that while both distraction and reappraisal led to greater RSA augmentation compared to a control condition when children regulated sadness, reappraisal produced greater parasympathetic benefits when children regulated fear.

While there are several strengths, including the use of a Latinx sample, the inclusion of multiple discrete emotions, and the inclusion of two ER strategies, there are also several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, our relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of our results, as it restricts our ability to detect subtle effects and limits the statistical power of our analyses. However, we still had over 30 participants per condition, which is standard and even larger than similar studies. Second, we did not collect data on cultural values or specific ethnic subgroups within the Latinx community, which limits our ability to talk about the development of emotional processing in our sample in the context of culture‐specific values. Cultural values and experiences within diverse Latinx subgroups may shape children's approaches to reappraisal and distraction as well as their emotional responses; this should be thoroughly and systematically assessed in future studies. Third, the self‐directed ER tasks may have been too challenging for children in this age range, potentially affecting their ability to consistently and correctly apply the instructed strategies. However, despite using an approach that allowed children to independently choose their methods for reframing stimuli—arguably more challenging than approaches in previous studies—our findings were still consistent with previous work. Moreover, using this approach gets us closer to understanding how children independently implement these strategies across emotional contexts. Future studies should explore various levels of difficulty (e.g., first an externally guided reappraisal task followed by a self‐reappraisal task) to better assess developmental differences in children's ability to implement these strategies. Fourth, because the images were only piloted with two children prior to the study, the selected stimuli may not fully reflect age‐appropriate perceptions of discrete emotions. In our sample, neutral images were often rated as more positive, which may have reduced the emotional contrast necessary to elicit differential LPP responses. While our piloting of the stimuli with two children is an advancement over previous LPP work that has failed to test the validity of their chosen stimuli in their samples, future studies should incorporate more extensive, developmentally appropriate validation of emotional stimuli and account for age‐related differences in emotion perception and regulation. Fifth, the absence of a comparison group from another ethnic background limits our ability to draw conclusions about cultural differences in ER. However, our focus on Latinx children was intentional and aligns with calls for more within‐group research in developmental and cultural neuroscience (Nielsen et al. 2017; Roberts et al. 2020). Given the underrepresentation of Latinx population in this field, centering this group allows for a more nuanced understanding of variability and regulatory processes without relying on the majority group as a default reference. However, future studies should include diverse comparison groups to explore potential cultural similarities and differences more fully. Lastly, while our study was in line with most LPP studies where jitters between images are not typically used, it is possible that the lack of a jitter between trials could have resulted in anticipatory effects that may have contributed to minimizing the condition effects we were exploring. Future work should aim to replicate this and other studies by including jitters between their trials.

Conclusion

Our study adds to a growing number of studies investigating neural markers of ER in children. Specifically, our study sheds light on how different strategies across discrete emotions differentially impact the LPP, a well‐established marker of emotion processing and regulation. While we failed to find significant modulation effects (in the expected direction) in most windows for both reappraisal and distraction, our study still adds novel information to this field by showing how both strategies had similar effects on the LPP and how reappraisal appeared to be slightly more effective at modulating the LPP for sadness compared to other emotions. Our paradigm was intentionally designed to increase ecological validity by allowing children to self‐direct their regulatory strategies. LPP modulation in the expected direction was primarily observed during early time windows, likely reflecting initial attentional and regulatory engagement. These early effects are developmentally meaningful and suggest that children may begin to regulate emotional responses rapidly, though sustaining this regulation may be more challenging. The absence of modulation and the presence of hindering effects in later time windows may also reflect the increased variability inherent in more naturalistic tasks, as children may engage with emotional content in more flexible or inconsistent ways. Future research should further investigate how task structure and ecological validity influence the temporal dynamics of neural responses during ER.

Moreover, our study advances previous work by showing that children experience comparable difficulties in implementing distraction in an LPP task despite the fact that distraction is considered an easier strategy to implement, suggesting that it may be the timing in which these strategies need to be implemented in an LPP task that may be obscuring the measurement of developmental changes in ER development as measured by the LPP. Future research should investigate how the developmental maturity of ER skills is directly influenced by task complexity, in order to better understand how children's ability to autonomously regulate emotions evolves over time.
