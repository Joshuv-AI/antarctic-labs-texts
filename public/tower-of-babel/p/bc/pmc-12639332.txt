Systematic Review of Immune and Symptomatic Treatments for Stiff‐Person Syndrome
Authors: Timothée Lenglet, Jérôme Honnorat, Shahram Attarian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and PurposeThis systematic review evaluated the effectiveness and safety of immune and symptomatic treatments in patients with stiff‐person syndrome (SPS).MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library was conducted up to September 30, 2024 in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines. All studies that involve SPS patients treated with symptomatic pharmacotherapy or immunotherapy and where the outcome is reported were included in the initial screening. Case reports were excluded. Two independent reviewers carried out study selection, bias assessment with the Murad et al. tool for case series, ROBINS‐I for observational studies or RoB‐2 for randomised trials, and data extraction into a predefined template. All available efficacy and safety data were recorded. A narrative synthesis was conducted to address the heterogeneity of the studies.ResultsThirty‐six studies were included. Most of the included studies consisted of small‐scale studies, with 21 case series, 10 cohort studies and only five randomised controlled trials. Outcome assessments varied across the reviewed studies. Most studies assessed measures with subjective self‐report and/or through clinical observations. Spasms and stiffness were assessed in around 60% of studies. Walking ability and pain were evaluated in less than half of studies. Only 10 (28%) studies assessed functional improvement using a valid and reliable tool. The present review highlights the paucity of data and the heterogeneity in SPS treatment responses and underscores the need for individualised therapeutic strategies.ConclusionsStandardised assessment methods in clinical practice are necessary to benefit understanding and approval of future therapy strategies.Keywords: immunotherapy, pharmacotherapy, spasms, stiffness, stiff‐person syndrome

Introduction

Stiff‐Person Syndrome (SPS) is a rare, progressive autoimmune neurological disorder marked by muscle stiffness, episodic spasms, and heightened sensitivity to external stimuli, such as sudden noises or tactile stimulation [1]. These symptoms primarily affect the axial and proximal limb muscles, often resulting in severe disability, gait disturbances, postural instability, and frequent falls [1]. The underlying pathophysiology is largely attributed to impaired gamma‐aminobutyric acidergic (GABAergic) neurotransmission [2]. While most patients exhibit high titres of anti‐glutamic acid decarboxylase 65‐kilodalton (GAD65) autoantibodies, not all present these markers. Besides, other immune markers, including anti‐amphiphysin and anti‐glycine receptor antibodies, have also been implicated in some cases [3].

SPS is a clinically heterogeneous disorder, with recognised subtypes including classical SPS, partial or focal SPS, and progressive encephalomyelitis with rigidity and myoclonus (PERM) [4]. Due to its rarity and varied presentation, SPS is frequently misdiagnosed, delaying appropriate treatment initiation and increasing patient morbidity [5]. As a result, there is an urgent need to clarify optimal management strategies to improve clinical outcomes and patients' quality of life.

Over the decades, various treatments have been explored, beginning with diazepam in 1963, followed by corticosteroids in 1988 and plasma exchange in 1989 [6]. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) was introduced as a therapeutic option in 1994 [6]. Management of SPS frequently involves a combination of symptomatic therapy (e.g., benzodiazepines, baclofen, and other GABAergic agents) to enhance GABAergic neurotransmission relieving muscle stiffness and spasms, and immunomodulatory treatments (e.g., IVIg, plasmapheresis, rituximab) aimed at modifying the underlying autoimmune response [2]. Recent advances in targeted immunotherapies, such as B‐cell depletion therapy and autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), have shown promise, but their role in routine SPS management remains unclear [4].

Although these therapies are widely used in clinical practice, their effectiveness remains uncertain, as much of the available data is derived from case series, small observational studies, and non‐randomised trials [4, 7]. The heterogeneity in study methodologies, inclusion criteria, outcome measures, and treatment protocols complicates the interpretation of treatment efficacy, making it challenging to establish evidence‐based guidelines for SPS management.

Despite the increasing recognition of SPS and its variants, there is no consensus on the most effective treatment strategy, and clinical decision‐making is often based on limited evidence and expert opinion [3]. While various symptomatic and immunomodulatory therapies are used in clinical practice, their efficacy varies significantly between patients, and no standardised treatment algorithm has been established.

This systematic review aims to provide a comprehensive analysis of clinical studies of immune and symptomatic treatments in SPS.

Methods

Search Strategy

The protocol was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (CRD42024611543). The conduct and reporting of this systematic review was guided by the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses) guidelines [8].

A systematic literature search was carried out on PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library, from inception to September 30, 2024. The search strategy was limited to full‐text papers published in English. Furthermore, a manual reference review of included articles and identified reviews was conducted. The full search strategy is provided in Tables S1–S3.

Eligibility Criteria

Randomised controlled trials (RCTs), cohort studies, case–control studies, cross‐sectional studies, case series, and literature reviews containing original data were considered for inclusion. Studies that involve paediatric or adult patients diagnosed with SPS were included if efficacy data of symptomatic pharmacotherapy or immunotherapy were reported on the pre‐planned outcomes of interest. The primary outcome was a composite measure of treatment effectiveness, including spasms, stiffness, gait disturbances, and balance impairment. Secondary outcomes included pain, speech difficulties, phobias and treatment‐related adverse events. Case reports and studies not conducted on humans (animal, in vitro/ex vivo studies) were excluded. Studies were excluded if they did not explicitly focus on SPS, failed to report whether treatment was administered, or lacked defined outcome measures.

Study Selection and Data Extraction

Two independent reviewers unaware of the authors, institutions, journal titles and study results, conducted an initial screening of the title and abstract of all articles. Full texts of potentially relevant articles were then retrieved and assessed for inclusion by the two reviewers, based on the pre‐established inclusion criteria. Any disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion to ensure a rigorous and consistent selection process.

Two reviewers independently extracted data from the included articles using a data extraction form developed for the present review. No investigator was contacted in case of missing or obscure data. The following study details were collected to characterise the included studies: country, design, study period, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and for each treatment group (for at least two patients): patient characteristics (number, age, gender, diagnostic criteria, time from SPS symptoms to SPS diagnosis, comorbidities, concomitant treatments, follow‐up), treatment details (type, dose, duration, time from SPS symptoms to first treatment, time from treatment start to outcomes evaluation) and outcome data. Any discrepancy or uncertainty was resolved by discussion among reviewers to reach a consensus.

Risk of Bias Assessment

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed by two independent reviewers depending on the study design using Murad et al. tool [9] for case series, ROBINS‐I [10] for observational studies or RoB‐2 [11] for RCTs. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion.

Data Synthesis

Evidence was presented via narrative synthesis and tabulation. Studies were tabulated according to treatment type.

Results

The studies examined 18 therapeutic interventions. The most frequently evaluated treatments were immunotherapies, including IVIg [14, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25], subcutaneous immunoglobulin (SCIg) [26, 27], Rituximab [12, 13], therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) [28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34], HSCT [35], Efgartigimod [36], Eculizumab [37], Tacrolimus [38] and intravenous steroids [24]. Additionally, several symptomatic treatments were explored, including benzodiazepines (mainly Diazepam) [24, 39, 40], Baclofen [15, 24, 41, 42], Milacemide [43], Gabapentin [24], Levetiracetam [16, 44] and Botulinum toxin A (BTA) [45].

The sample sizes ranged from 2 to 36 patients, with 21 (58%) studies involving 2 or 3 patients. All studies included classical SPS, and only two studies also included a small number of focal SPS [18] and PERM [18, 31]. In 25 (69%) studies, the diagnosis of SPS was supported by biological testing (GAD65‐IgG in 24 studies and GlyRα1‐IgG in 1 study). However, in these studies, antibody positivity was not used as a standalone diagnostic criterion but rather as a complementary marker to clinical assessment. Seven studies based the diagnosis primarily on clinical criteria [18, 20, 25, 26, 30, 31, 41] while four relied on electrophysiological findings [21, 34, 42, 43]. In studies where the diagnosis was not based on biological markers, at least 67% of patients tested positive for anti‐GAD65 antibodies, with the exception of one study [42], where only one patient out of four (25%) was anti‐GAD65 positive.

Quality Assessment

Most of the reviewed case series were of low quality according to the Murad tool (Table S1). Using the ROBINS‐I assessment tool, all the cohort studies were classified as having a low‐to‐moderate risk of bias, except one with a serious risk of bias for reported results [25] (Table S2). According to the ROB‐2 tool, two RCTs were judged to have a high risk of randomisation process bias due to a lack of detailed information [15, 16] (Table S3). Regarding deviation from the intervention, two were judged to have some concerns [12, 15]. Regarding the selection of the reported result, three RCTs had some concerns related to missing information about the pre‐specified analysis plan [13, 15, 16].

Spasms

Across the 21 (58%) studies that assessed spasms, various measures were used to evaluate treatment efficacy in reducing muscle spasms. The Heightened‐Sensitivity Scale (HSS) was applied in three studies assessing IVIg [14], Rituximab [12] and Tacrolimus [38]. The Spasm Frequency Scale (SFS) was used in three studies evaluating the impact of IVIg (and switch to SCIg) [19], Baclofen [41] and BTA [45]. Myoclonus was assessed using the acoustic startle response test [37]. The effectiveness of BTA was also measured using the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale [45]. Self‐reported outcomes were also considered in three studies following treatment with IVIg [25], HSCT [46] and Efgartigimod [36]. In 11 (52%) studies, the test performed was not specified.

Almost all studies with less than 10 patients reported a 100% response rate of patients treated with IVIg [19, 20] (except one patient), TPE [29, 33], HSCT [46, 47], Efgartigimod [36], Tacrolimus [38], Diazepam [40], Baclofen [41, 42], Levetiracetam [44] and BTA [45]. However, the two patients of Shariatmadar and Noto, assessed using an unspecified measure, showed no meaningful improvement after TPE [34]. Repeat electrophysiological testing at 1 year revealed normalisation of acoustic startle response in one of the two patients treated with eculizumab [37]. A larger cohort on HSCT demonstrated variable efficacy in managing spasms with 74% responding to treatment including 57% with complete resolution [35].

RCT showed some benefit of IVIg on spasms. While HSS values at each time point over the six‐month follow‐up period were not statistically significantly different between IVIg and placebo, a significant overalltreatment effect was observed (p = 0.03) [14]. Furthermore, long‐term benefits on spasms were observed in 33% of patients, according to patients' subjective report of symptom improvement and physician assessments [25].

RCT on Rituximab failed to demonstrate any improvement on the HSS over 6 months [12].

Stiffness

In 22 (61%) studies, the impact of treatment on muscle stiffness was evaluated, while 11 (31%) studies did not specify the exact measurement. The Ashworth Scale or Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS) was used to assess the effects of Baclofen [15, 41] and IVIg (and switch to SCIg) [19], while the distribution‐of‐stiffness index (DSI) was applied in studies evaluating IVIg [14], Rituximab [12], and Tacrolimus [38]. The impact of IVIg, SCIg and Efgartigimod was also assessed through self‐reported measures [25, 27, 36]. Additional assessments included electromyography (EMG) in HSCT‐treated patients [46] and in Baclofen‐treated patients [15], forward bend test in TPE‐treated patients [29] and chest expansion/waist bending measurements in Rituximab‐treated patients [13].

Except for one case series, using an unspecified measure, that reported no improvement in stiffness in three out of five (60%) patients treated by IVIg [24], evaluation of IVIg's impact on stiffness demonstrated both objective and subjective improvements. Quantitative scales, such as the DSI, confirmed significant reductions, particularly in the trunk, abdomen, and face, with IVIg consistently outperforming placebo [14]. The MAS showed sustained benefits, even after transitioning to SCIg [19]. Functional assessments highlighted notable improvements in mobility, including enhanced hip and knee flexion and, in some cases, complete resolution of stiffness [21]. Long‐term benefits on stiffness were sustained in 39% of patients treated with monthly maintenance IVIg [25]. Spasticity improvement was also reported by patients treated by SCIgPro20 [27].

The efficacy of TPE in managing stiffness was assessed in four case series using an unspecified measure, producing mixed results. In some cases, patients experienced substantial improvement [29, 30], while others reported little to no benefit [32, 34].

Both RCTs on Rituximab showed no significant reduction of stiffness compared to placebo [12, 13].

HSCT demonstrated limited effectiveness. While stiffness persisted in responders, its severity decreased, suggesting a partial but not complete therapeutic effect [35]. Kass‐Iliyya et al. [46] provided EMG‐based evidence of response variability among three patients: one exhibited ongoing abnormal motor unit activity, another showed complete suppression of motor potentials in previously affected muscles, and the third retained some motor potentials despite treatment.

Baclofen demonstrated varying efficacy in reducing stiffness, with outcomes seeming to depend on the assessment method. Using the MAS, all seven patients of a case series experienced a reduction in stiffness within 6 months, with sustained improvements in the five patients available for long‐term follow‐up [41]. Following intrathecal baclofen administration, all three patients of a RCT exhibited improvement in reflex EMG activity (p < 0.0001) and in the Ashworth score (p = 0.08) [15]. However, inconsistencies were noted, as a case series reported no response in all three patients [24], while another reported a significant reduction in stiffness in two out of three (67%) patients [42].

Among studies with small sizes, other therapies such as Efgartigimod [36], Tacrolimus [38], benzodiazepines [24, 40] and BTA [45] were associated with improvement in spasticity for 100% of cases.

Walking

Walking ability was assessed using a variety of objective and subjective measures across 15 (42%) studies. Five studies assessed walking speed, using the time required to walk a fixed distance [13, 14, 17, 35, 41]. While one study evaluated walking through subjective report [25], seven studies did not specify the measurement. Furthermore, three studies did not report the number of evaluated patients [32, 34, 47].

IVIg demonstrated significant efficacy in improving walking ability across multiple assessments [14, 17, 20, 21]. In the Timed 30‐Foot Walk test, 86 to 100% of patients showed a sustained reduction in walking time, with many transitioning from assisted walking (using a cane or walker) to unaided mobility [14, 17]. Long‐term evaluation reinforced these findings, with 12 (36%) patients experiencing improved ambulation, including bedridden individuals regaining independent mobility [25].

The effectiveness of TPE in enhancing walking ability was variable [30, 32, 34]. Among the five patients treated with TPE, three had Medical Research Council (MRC) scores available; all experienced an improvement in the MRC grading. Two achieved independent ambulation at discharge, one required support to walk, and another, initially requiring assistance, was able to walk unassisted at 6 months follow‐up [30].

In contrast, Rituximab failed to produce significant improvements, as assessed through timed activities such as stair climbing, chair rising, and turning, where performance changes were minimal [13].

HSCT led to notable improvements in walking ability, with patients no longer requiring assistance to walk [46, 47]. In a larger cohort, 52% experienced meaningful functional improvements, including patients transitioning from wheelchair use to independent walking or requiring minimal assistance [35]. However, the timed 25‐Foot Walk improved only in responders from a mean of 10.5 s pre‐HSCT to 5.7 s, though this did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.09) [35].

Efgartigimod showed promise in all three patients treated, MRC assessing muscle strength of upper and lower limbs improved in all three patients, and two of the patients achieved independent walking by the end of the second treatment cycle [36].

Among the symptomatic therapies, only two studies on Baclofen reported data relating to walking [41, 42]. Baclofen showed no effect on the Timed 25‐Foot Walk and on MRC in all patients assessed in the study by Abbatemarco et al. [41]. However, another case series reported functional gains in three patients following Baclofen infusion: two progressed from wheelchair use to walking with or without crutches, although one of them continued to experience cramps, while a previously bedridden patient regained the ability to walk with crutches despite persistent hip and back pain [42].

Gait and Balance

Gait was assessed only in three studies on immunotherapy, though data available was limited. IVIg consistently showed benefits, with videotape evaluations of three patients revealing striking improvements [17]. Gait improvements were sustained in 42% of patients in a large cohort after long‐term maintenance treatment with IVIg [25]. SCIgPro20 was evaluated through self‐reports in two patients, both of whom reported improvements in gait [27].

Balance improvement was assessed only in four studies on immunotherapy. Small studies suggested promising effects of TPE [30, 32] and Efgartigimod [36]. In a larger cohort, patients' subjective report indicated that 9% of patients had a balance improvement following long‐term treatment of IVIg [25].

Pain

Twelve (33%) studies assessed the effects of different therapies on pain using various measures, though four did not specify the test performed. Apart from the visual analogue scale (VAS) used in three studies [13, 20, 22], and the numeric rating scale (NRS) used in two studies [19, 41], each study employed a different method for pain evaluation.

IVIG demonstrated promising effects on pain management in some patients. Objective assessments using the NRS showed a moderate reduction in pain intensity in one patient (from 7 to 5), with this benefit sustained after transitioning to SCIg [19]. In another case series, two patients reported a marked pain reduction, with VAS scores decreasing from 8 to 4 or 3 [22]. A cohort study involving six patients also showed a significant improvement in the VAS scores 2 weeks after the IVIg course [20]. In a larger cohort of 36 patients, subjective reports on pain indicated limited response (15%) following long‐term maintenance of IVIg [25].

TPE produced variable results in pain relief, with limited data available. In one documented case, lumbar pain resolved completely [29], while another report indicated pain relief comparable to that achieved with oral medication [34]. Additionally, one study reported pain reduction in one of two patients [30].

In contrast, Rituximab and HSCT did not show significant pain relief when assessed using the VAS and a chronic pain acceptance questionnaire, respectively [13, 35].

Meanwhile, Efgartigimod, evaluated through the SPS activity of daily living score, showed pain reduction in 67% of patients, with two experiencing notable improvement and one reporting worsening symptoms [36].

Among the symptomatic therapies, only two studies on Baclofen examined the impact on pain. Baclofen demonstrated notable pain reduction, particularly over time [41, 42]. In a case series of seven patients with chronic Baclofen infusion, 83% of patients experienced pain relief within 6 months, as assessed by NRS [41]. Furthermore, among the five patients available for long‐term follow‐up, four (80%) reported sustained pain reduction beyond 6 months.

Overall Functional Outcomes

Several studies reported treatment responses using composite outcomes, that is, a combination of several outcomes such as spasms, stiffness, walking, mobility, gait, balance, pain and speech [15, 16, 28, 32, 33, 43]. These results need to be interpreted with caution because the construction of composite outcomes is susceptible to post hoc choosing of a favourable combination of component outcomes. Furthermore, except for one case series [32], all studies reported no individual component outcomes.

Ten (28%) studies assessed functional improvement using a valid and reliable tool: the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) [18, 23, 25, 26, 31, 35, 36, 39, 40], the Clinical Global Impression Severity scale (CGI‐S) [23], the Barthel index [35], and the Patients Global Impressions Scale (PGIS) [16].

IVIg demonstrated significant benefits, with three cohort studies showing mRS improvement [18, 23, 25]. The mRS scores were significantly improved following treatment with IVIg within 4 to 10 weeks (p < 0.0001) and sustained after 5 years of treatment (p = 0.0003) [18]. The mRS scores of these patients were significantly better (p = 0.02) compared with patients who did not receive immunomodulation [18]. Similarly, another large cohort study demonstrated that long‐term maintenance of IVIg is effective to maintain the short‐term IVIg benefits on mRS. Of the 24 IVIg responders, eight (33%) patients responded with an mRS score improved by 1 or 2 points, and the other 16 responders maintained the same mRS score after long‐term IVIg treatments, demonstrating a stabilising effect [25].

SCIg treatment led to mRS improvement in three out of five cases, with the remaining two patients experiencing symptom relief despite unchanged scores [26].

TPE yielded limited nonsignificant functional benefits, with only three out of 21 (14%) patients showing mRS improvement at 3 months, while 17 (81%) remained unchanged and one patient's score (5%) worsened [31].

HSCT and Efgartigimod treatment demonstrated notable improvements with mRS scores decreasing from 3.13 to 2.10 (p = 0.004) in 23 patients [35] and from 4 ± 1.0 to 3.3 ± 0.6 [36], respectively. However, while the Barthel Index scores of the responders and of the responders who relapsed were significantly better following HSCT from 85.9 to 95.9 (p = 0.04) and from 75 to 83 (p = 0.05) respectively, the Barthel Index scores of the whole cohort did not improve significantly from 83.4 to 88.8 [35].

Diazepam demonstrated various efficacies across studies, with mRS score improvements from 3.7 to 1.3 in all three evaluated patients of a cohort [39], whereas a case series of four patients treated with Diazepam reported symptom improvement only in one patient (25%) [40].

Quality of Life

Five studies reported significant improvement in quality of life scores, assessed via a survey questionnaire, following treatment with IVIg [18, 20], Rituximab [12, 13] and HSCT [35]. Interestingly, a placebo‐controlled randomised trial of rituximab showed that the quality of life scores improved significantly (p < 0.01) at 3 months in both groups, but not at 6 months, denoting a strong early placebo effect [12].

No data on quality study is available for symptomatic therapies.

Concomitant Treatments

Marked reduction in the required dosage of concomitant therapies, including corticosteroids, diazepam and baclofen, was reported through several studies following regimes of IVIg [21], HSCT [35, 46, 47] and BTA [45].

Anti‐GAD65 Antibody Levels

Anti‐GAD65 antibody titre reduction has been observed in patients treated with IVIg [14], TPE [29], Rituximab [12], HSCT [46], Efgartigimod [36] and Tacrolimus [38]. RCT showed that the decline in anti‐GAD antibody levels was statistically significant following IVIg therapy (p = 0.05), but not after placebo administration [14], nor after treatment with Rituximab [12]. There was no correlation between anti‐GAD65 antibody titre changes and clinical improvement [12, 14].

Adverse Events Related to Treatment

Overall, 22 (61%) studies reported information on adverse events potentially related to treatment. Immunomodulatory treatments such as IVIg, SCIg and Rituximab frequently led to infusion site reactions, headaches and nausea [12, 25, 26, 27]. More severe reactions, including fever, blistering rashes, and infections, were also reported [14]. TPE was associated with hypotension, catheter‐related infections, and mild citrate toxicity but was otherwise well tolerated [31, 32, 33]. HSCT was associated with the highest burden of adverse events, including metabolic disturbances, infections, and hematologic complications [35, 46, 47].

Efgartigimod, Eculizumab, Milacemide and BTA had relatively mild side effect profiles, such as flu‐like symptoms, headaches or sedation [36, 37, 43, 45]. Baclofen therapy was associated with the risk of adverse effects due to the procedure of intrathecal infusion [41, 42].

Discussion

This systematic review synthesised the best available evidence on the effectiveness and safety of various therapeutic interventions for SPS.

The results reveal that IVIg improved spasms, muscle stiffness, walking ability, gait disturbances, pain and overall functional ability. Furthermore, the long‐term benefits of maintenance IVIg treatment were sustained in some patients. TPE and HSCT yielded variable results, with some patients experiencing complete resolution of spasms and stiffness, while others showed only limited benefits. Walking and mobility outcomes were similarly inconsistent, and overall effectiveness in pain management was limited. In contrast, Rituximab consistently failed to demonstrate meaningful improvements in spasms, stiffness, walking performance and pain. Meanwhile, Efgartigimod and Tacrolimus showed promise in reducing muscle stiffness, improving spasticity and walking ability, and alleviating pain severity in a subset of patients. Few data are available on symptomatic pharmacotherapy. Baclofen was effective in short‐ and long‐term reductions of spasms, stiffness and pain. However, its impact on walking varied, with some patients regaining mobility while others experienced minimal changes.

In summary, this review highlights the heterogeneity in SPS treatment responses and underscores the need for individualised therapeutic approaches. IVIg remains the most studied and widely used therapy, with documented benefits across multiple outcome measures, though its effectiveness is not universal. At present, it stands as the most consistently effective treatment (Table 2), supported by the strongest level of clinical evidence [14]. In clinical settings, IVIg should indeed be considered advantageous, particularly as a first‐line immunotherapy for classic SPS, especially for patients with severe or refractory symptoms who do not respond adequately to symptomatic treatments. Its favourable safety profile, evidence‐based efficacy, and rapid action make it a cornerstone of treatment. However, clinicians must weigh these benefits against its high cost, logistical complexity, and variable long‐term effects. Careful patient selection and monitoring, as well as integration with symptomatic therapies, remain key to optimising its use. Baclofen, HSCT, and emerging treatments like Efgartigimod appear promising but require further validation through controlled studies. The variable efficacy of TPE and Rituximab suggests that these treatments should be reserved for selected cases.

Beyond efficacy, treatment safety is crucial for clinical decision‐making. Symptomatic therapies are generally well tolerated with dose‐dependent, reversible side effects. Baclofen may lead to pump‐related complications. Immunomodulatory treatments have a moderate risk profile, mostly infusion reactions and rare thromboses. More intensive therapies like TPE and HSCT carry higher risks, including serious infections and hematologic complications, necessitating careful patient selection. These considerations highlight the importance of individualised treatment strategies that balance benefits and risks in SPS management.

Interpreting these findings requires caution due to several limitations that need to be considered. First, given the scarcity of RCTs evaluating the efficacy of treatments in SPS patients [12, 13, 14, 15, 16], we conducted a systematic review of randomised and non‐randomised studies, including cohort studies and case series. This approach inherits the potential limitations associated with observational studies, such as different treatment regimens, patient selection bias (including varying degrees of SPS severity, refractory SPS cases) and confounding factors. The heterogeneity of SPS variants was largely overlooked, with most studies failing to assess whether treatment responses differed based on disease subtypes. Second, most of the included studies consisted of small‐scale studies, reducing statistical power and increasing the risk of bias. Third, outcome assessments varied widely, with most studies relying on subjective self‐reported measures rather than standardised, objective evaluations. This inconsistency complicates direct comparisons between treatments and limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions about efficacy. Additionally, selective reporting was evident in some studies, as results were sometimes presented only for patients who showed improvement, potentially exaggerating treatment efficacy [25, 27, 30, 32, 40]. Missing data were a common issue, and in many cases, not all enrolled patients were tested for the reported outcomes, further limiting the reliability and generalisability of the findings. Fourth, long‐term follow‐up data were rarely available, preventing any robust assessment of sustained treatment effects, relapse rates, or the durability of clinical benefit.

In contrast to narrative reviews, which may be influenced by expert opinion, selective citation, or variable inclusion criteria, systematic reviews provide a transparent, reproducible, and unbiased summary of available evidence. Our systematic review adds value by implementing a comprehensive literature search across multiple databases, minimising the risk of missing relevant studies. Furthermore, by using standardised tools such as the Cochrane Risk of Bias assessment, we offer an objective evaluation of study quality, an essential step that has often been lacking in previous reviews.

Ongoing research worldwide is evaluating clinical outcomes of future therapeutic strategies on SPS patients. A recent case report showed that chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR‐T) cell therapy could be a promising strategy for severe treatment‐refractory SPS patients, by reducing stiffness and improving walking and gait disturbances [48].

Standardised evaluation of outcomes is needed in clinical practice to further understand the treatment efficacy on SPS patients. The use of validated tools, ideally incorporating both clinician‐assessed and patient‐reported outcomes (e.g., mRS and SF‐36), would not only enhance the quality of evidence but also support more informed therapeutic decisions and long‐term monitoring of disease progression. Furthermore, the findings underscore the need for larger well‐controlled RCTs and longitudinal studies to better define the optimal therapeutic approach for SPS patients at short‐ and long‐term.
