Effect of Novel Etching System on Etch Pattern in Primary Teeth: An In Vitro Study
Authors: S Sri Sudev, VR Pooja
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate and compare the effects of different etching systems on etch patterns and mineral retention in primary tooth enamel.Materials and methodsForty extracted, noncarious primary teeth were randomly divided into four groups, each containing 10 teeth. The groups were as follows: group I (Control)—no intervention, group II—etched with 37% phosphoric acid, group III—etched with 37% phosphoric acid combined with tricalcium phosphate (TCP), and group IV—etched with 10% citric acid combined with TCP. Introduction

The success of adhesive dentistry largely depends on the quality of the etched enamel surface, which creates the necessary micromechanical retention for bonding agents.1 Traditionally, 37% phosphoric acid has been the gold standard for enamel etching, first introduced in the mid-20th century by Buonocore as a means to enhance bonding by creating microretentive structures on the enamel surface.2 The acid dissolves hydroxyapatite crystals, effectively removing calcium and phosphate ions, thus roughening the enamel and increasing the surface area for adhesive bonding.3 This process has been extensively studied and applied in both primary and permanent dentition. However, its application in primary teeth poses unique challenges.

The enamel of primary teeth is thinner, more porous, and less mineralized compared to permanent teeth, leading to more rapid and deeper demineralization during the etching process.4 Studies by Gwinnett and Silverstone classified the etch patterns seen in primary teeth as type I and II, but they also highlighted the increased susceptibility of primary enamel to over-etching.5 Over-etching results in excessive enamel loss, potentially leading to weakened tooth structure, increased sensitivity, and compromised bonding longevity.6

Given these concerns, researchers have been investigating alternative etching agents and modifications to reduce the adverse effects of aggressive etching while maintaining adequate bonding performance. One promising approach is the incorporation of tricalcium phosphate (TCP) into etching solutions. TCP is a biocompatible, bioactive compound known for its ability to promote remineralization by releasing calcium and phosphate ions into the etched surface, potentially counteracting some of the demineralization caused by traditional phosphoric acid.7 Studies by Paik et al. demonstrated that TCP-enriched etching systems not only reduce the harsh demineralization of enamel but also aid in preserving the enamel structure while promoting remineralization, especially in weaker, more porous enamel like that of primary teeth.8

Another proposed alternative is the use of citric acid, an organic acid with milder etching properties compared to phosphoric acid. Citric acid is known for its chelating ability, meaning it can bind calcium ions and remove them from the enamel surface without the excessive demineralization typically seen with stronger acids.9 When combined with TCP, citric acid could provide a controlled etching effect that minimizes enamel loss while maintaining sufficient surface roughness for bonding.9,11 Studies emphasized the importance of a conservative approach in etching primary teeth, noting that less aggressive agents could offer the dual benefits of effective bonding with minimal damage to the enamel structure.8–12

While the efficacy of TCP and citric acid in etching permanent teeth has been explored, there is a paucity of research specifically focusing on their effects on primary teeth. This gap in the literature underscores the need for further investigation into these alternative etching systems. Primary teeth, due to their unique composition, require a careful balance between effective enamel conditioning and preservation of tooth structure, which novel etching systems may be able to achieve.

Objective

The objective of this study is to evaluate the etch patterns and mineral content changes in primary teeth when etched with novel etchants such as phosphoric acid combined with tricalcium phosphate and citric acid combined with tricalcium phosphate, compared to the conventional 37% phosphoric acid. This in vitro study aims to assess whether these newer etching systems offer comparable bonding efficiency while mitigating the risks associated with overetching in primary enamel.

Materials and Methods

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size was determined using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.2). The parameters for the calculation included an effect size (d) of 0.6, an α error probability of 0.20, and a power of 0.90. Based on these parameters, a total of 40 primary teeth was required for the study, with 10 samples per group. Institutional Scientific and Ethical Review Board approval was obtained before initiating the research.

Sample Preparation

Forty extracted, noncarious primary teeth were used in this study. The teeth were stored in saline at room temperature before being prepared for the experimental procedure. Each tooth was sectioned mesiodistally at the cementoenamel junction using a microtome under water irrigation to ensure a clean and precise cut. The buccal surfaces of the teeth were retained for the study.

The teeth were randomly divided into four experimental groups, each containing 10 teeth:

Group III: 37% Phosphoric acid + Tricalcium phosphate (TCP)

Group III: 37% Phosphoric acid + Tricalcium phosphate (TCP)

Group IV: 10% Citric acid + Tricalcium phosphate (TCP)

Group IV: 10% Citric acid + Tricalcium phosphate (TCP)

Etching Procedure

For groups III and IV, etching pastes containing β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP) powders were prepared by mixing the powder with either 37% phosphoric acid or 10% citric acid. The powder-to-liquid ratios for both groups were carefully controlled at 0.8:1 and 3:1, respectively, to achieve a consistent and homogeneous paste, using a stainless-steel spatula to mix the components for 30 seconds on a glass slab. The final paste's pH was measured using a digital pH meter to ensure standardization.13

The etching agents were applied to the prepared enamel surfaces using a 00 paintbrush, ensuring uniform coverage of the entire buccal surface (Fig. 1). The etchants were left on the surface for 15 seconds, after which they were rinsed thoroughly with water for 15 seconds and air-dried for 30 seconds using an air syringe.

Scanning Electron Microscope and Energy-dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy Analysis

After the etching process, the teeth were mounted on aluminum stubs and sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold to ensure optimal surface conductivity for SEM analysis. The etched surfaces were then examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) at magnifications ranging from 6,000× to 25,000× (Fig. 2).

Samples mounted for SEM analysis

To assess the mineral content, the samples were subjected to energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX). The EDX analysis provided quantitative data on the weight percentages (wt%) of calcium (Ca) and phosphorus (P) in the etched enamel surfaces. The goal was to compare the mineral retention between the control and experimental groups.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows (version 17.0). The data were first checked for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. An ANOVA test was applied to determine the significance of differences between groups, followed by Tukey's post hoc test for multiple comparisons. A significance level of p < 0.05 was set for all statistical tests.

Results

Analysis of Etch Pattern

The control group shows a relatively smooth surface and lacks significant microstructural detail, which is characteristic of intact enamel that has not been etched or demineralized. The absence of significant surface roughness or porosity indicates that this is likely a natural, unaltered enamel surface. primary enamel has a more uniform mineral density, fewer mineral inclusions, and thinner prism boundaries, which explains the relatively homogeneous appearance in this image (Fig. 3A). For phosphoric acid group the scanning electron photomicrographs showed type I or type II etching pattern with dissolution primarily of the enamel prism cores and dissolution of the prism peripheries, leaving the prism cores intact. The image shows a network of rough, irregularly shaped peaks and valleys, consistent with the dissolution of hydroxyapatite crystals in enamel (Fig. 3B).

Scanning electron microscope images of (A) Group I—control; (B) Group II—phosphoric acid; (C) Group III—phosphoric acid + tricalcium phosphate; (D) Group IV—citric acid + tricalcium phosphate

For the phosphoric acid + tricalcium phosphate group, the surface appears less rough and more granular than typical phosphoric acid etching alone, with small pits or depressions scattered across the etched area. The presence of granular deposits may indicate the remnants or precipitates of tricalcium phosphate (TCP), which could have reacted with the phosphoric acid during the etching process. The partially smoother areas could be regions where TCP precipitates formed, reducing the etching in those specific spots. The image shows smaller pores and microstructures, which could be due to the modification by TCP (Fig. 3C).

The SEM image of a tooth etched using citric acid combined with tricalcium phosphate (TCP) reveals a surface with a distinct pattern compared to other acid etching processes. The surface appears smoother overall with linear or streak-like features, suggesting that citric acid may have produced less aggressive demineralization compared to phosphoric acid. The surface does not exhibit significant porosity or roughness, which indicates a milder etching process. Citric acid is a weaker acid compared to phosphoric acid, leading to more subtle enamel etching. The limited presence of pits or pores suggests that the citric acid etched less deeply, and the surface retains much of its pre-existing morphology (Fig. 3D).

Mineral content across each group

Group I (control) showed calcium and phosphorus levels consistent with the natural composition of primary enamel, serving as a baseline for comparison. Group II (phosphoric acid) exhibited a slight reduction in calcium content, though phosphorus levels remained unchanged, indicating that while phosphoric acid initiated some demineralization, the impact on phosphorus retention was minimal.

Group III (TCP + phosphoric acid) displayed the highest calcium percentage among all groups, suggesting that the addition of tricalcium phosphate (TCP) significantly enhances calcium retention. This increased calcium content implies that TCP promotes remineralization, likely by supplying additional calcium ions during the etching process. Phosphorus levels in this group showed a slight decrease compared to the control, but this reduction was not as pronounced as in group IV, indicating that TCP moderates the demineralization process more effectively than phosphoric acid alone.

In contrast, group IV (TCP + citric acid) showed the lowest calcium and phosphorus levels. This significant reduction, particularly in phosphorus, suggests that citric acid, known for its chelating properties, may have a stronger tendency to bind and remove calcium and phosphate ions from the enamel surface. The more aggressive etching effect observed with citric acid could explain the lower mineral retention, as citric acid likely impacts phosphate ion retention more than phosphoric acid.

The results suggest that TCP + phosphoric acid (group III) enhances calcium retention and mitigates demineralization, promoting a protective remineralizing effect on the enamel. In contrast, TCP + citric acid (group IV), while creating a smoother etching pattern, may lead to more aggressive demineralization due to citric acid's chelating action, resulting in lower levels of both calcium and phosphorus.

Discussion

This study was conducted to address the need for more effective and conservative etching systems for primary teeth, given their unique enamel characteristics. Primary teeth have thinner, more porous enamel compared to permanent teeth, making them more susceptible to over-etching when using traditional etchants such as 37% phosphoric acid.14 While phosphoric acid is effective in creating a microretentive surface for bonding, there is a risk of excessive enamel loss, which can compromise both bond strength and the long-term durability of restorations.15 Given these concerns, this study aimed to evaluate whether adding tricalcium phosphate (TCP) to phosphoric acid or using citric acid + TCP could provide a more balanced etching system that minimizes demineralization while still facilitating effective bonding.

The SEM images of group II (phosphoric acid) showed a type I and type II etch pattern, characterized by pronounced dissolution of the enamel prisms, creating a highly porous surface. This aligns with the findings of Buonocore2 and Pashley et al.12, who noted that 37% phosphoric acid is highly effective in creating micro-retentive structures by removing calcium and phosphate ions from the enamel surface. These micro-porosities enhance bonding by allowing dental materials to mechanically interlock with the etched enamel. However, in primary teeth, the risk of over-etching is elevated due to the enamel's reduced thickness and mineralization, which can weaken the tooth structure and potentially reduce bond longevity.16 This highlights the need for etching systems that strike a balance between creating adequate roughness for bonding and preserving enamel integrity.

Group III (phosphoric acid + TCP) demonstrated a more controlled etching pattern compared to group II. The SEM images showed smaller pits and granular deposits on the enamel surface, suggesting that TCP acted as a buffer, reducing the aggressive demineralization typically seen with phosphoric acid. This finding is consistent with research by Cochrane et al., which showed that TCP can release calcium and phosphate ions during the etching process, promoting remineralization and helping to maintain the structural integrity of the enamel.17

The EDX analysis supported these observations, as group III exhibited the highest calcium retention (41.47 ± 0.56%), significantly higher than group II (35.83 ± 0.39%). The increased calcium levels suggest that TCP may enhance remineralization by depositing calcium phosphate precipitates onto the enamel surface. This remineralizing effect could improve long-term bond strength by preventing excessive enamel loss, which is especially important in primary teeth, where enamel conservation is crucial. The ability of TCP to modulate the etching process makes it a valuable addition to phosphoric acid etchants, offering a more balanced approach that reduces demineralization while still creating a surface rough enough for bonding.

In group IV (citric acid + TCP), the etching pattern observed was the smoothest among all the experimental groups, with minimal porosity and linear streaks. The milder etching effect of citric acid is consistent with its status as a weaker organic acid compared to phosphoric acid. Citric acid, due to its chelating properties, removes calcium ions in a less aggressive manner, which may help to preserve the enamel surface.18 However, the EDX analysis for group IV revealed the lowest calcium and phosphorus retention (29.49 ± 0.34% calcium and 9.39 ± 0.13% phosphorus), suggesting that citric acid's chelating action may also result in the removal of critical minerals from the enamel surface.

This reduced mineral retention, particularly for phosphorus, raises concerns about the long-term durability of restorations using citric acid + TCP etchants. While the smoother surface created by citric acid may still provide adequate bonding, the loss of calcium and phosphate ions could potentially compromise the strength of the bond over time, especially in cases where strong mechanical interlocking is required. Despite this, the mild etching effect of citric acid + TCP could be advantageous in situations where conservative enamel preservation is prioritized, such as in young children with thinner primary enamel.

Previous studies have demonstrated that traditional phosphoric acid etching can effectively create a retentive surface, but at the cost of increased demineralization in primary teeth.19,20 The findings of this study align with those results, showing that 37% phosphoric acid creates a highly porous enamel surface, but also highlight the potential risks of overetching. In contrast, the use of tricalcium phosphate (TCP) mitigates the demineralization process, promoting remineralization while still creating sufficient surface roughness for bonding.21

The novel use of citric acid + TCP in this study aligns with previous research, which found that citric acid provides a more conservative etch.19 However, the significantly lower mineral retention observed in group IV suggests that the chelating nature of citric acid may reduce the effectiveness of bonding by depleting essential minerals from the enamel surface.

Clinical Implications

The results of this study suggest that the combination of phosphoric acid + TCP offers a promising alternative to traditional etching methods, particularly in primary teeth. This system provides the necessary surface roughness for effective bonding without the risk of over-etching seen with phosphoric acid alone. By promoting calcium and phosphate ion retention, TCP may enhance the long-term durability of dental restorations, making this etching system particularly beneficial in pediatric dentistry, where enamel preservation is critical.

On the other hand, citric acid + TCP could serve as a viable option for cases requiring minimal enamel loss, such as in patients with highly fragile enamel or those undergoing conservative restorative procedures. However, its reduced mineral retention may limit its use in situations requiring high bond strength. Further clinical studies are needed to confirm the long-term efficacy of these novel etching systems and their effects on bond durability in primary teeth.

Conclusion

This study highlights the potential benefits of incorporating tricalcium phosphate (TCP) into etching systems for primary teeth. Phosphoric acid + TCP demonstrated the best balance between effective etching and enamel preservation, making it an ideal candidate for pediatric restorative procedures where long-term bond strength is a priority. Although citric acid + TCP offers a milder etch, its reduced mineral retention may affect the durability of restorations. Continued research and in vivo studies are necessary to fully understand the clinical implications of these findings and to explore how these etching systems perform over time.
