Low prevalence of sub-microscopic malaria among rural communities of an endemic area in Kapit Division, Malaysian Borneo
Authors: Siti Farah Norasyikeen Sidi Omar, Romano Ngui, Sam Froze Jiee, Siong Hee Lim, Timothy Jinam, Melvin Hsien Liang Chung, Micheal Pelitini Ugak, Drend Jores, Paul Cliff Simon Divis, Yvonne Ai Lian Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalaria remains a major public health concern, especially with zoonotic Plasmodium knowlesi presenting specific challenges in Southeast Asia. In Malaysia, Sarawak ranks second in P. knowlesi cases after Sabah. Rural populations in endemic areas experience increased exposure to forest and forest-edge zones where vector mosquitoes breed, thereby raising infection risk and complicating routine surveillance. These groups may carry asymptomatic or submicroscopic infections that act as hidden reservoirs, potentially hindering elimination efforts. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of Plasmodium infections, including submicroscopic cases, among rural communities in Kapit Division, a malaria-endemic area.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted from March 2024 to August 2025, including 376 participants aged 2 to 90 years. Venous blood samples were examined under a light microscope with Giemsa-stained thick and thin smears. Additionally, all samples underwent species-specific nested PCR to verify microscopy results and identify sub-microscopic infections. Hemoglobin levels were measured using a HemoCue® Hb 201 + analyzer. Face-to-face interviews were conducted using a standardized questionnaire. The data analysis was performed with IBM SPSS version 27.0.ResultsThe overall malaria prevalence was 5.32% (20/376; 95% CI 3.47–8.07%). Of these, 18 (90%; 18/20) infected individuals were febrile and microscopy-positive. Nested PCR detected two additional afebrile, submicroscopic infections (10%; 2/20). Molecular speciation identified 16 P. knowlesi, one P. falciparum, and one P. vivax among febrile participants. Both submicroscopic cases were P. knowlesi. All infections occurred in the Kapit District. Anemia was found in 17.6% (66/376) of participants. Of these, 4 were positive for P. knowlesi, with no significant association with the infection. Infection was significantly associated with age over 40 years (p = 0.02), non-field employment (p = 0.003), and higher household income (p < 0.001).ConclusionThis study shows low Plasmodium infection rates in Kapit, with some submicroscopic infections. This emphasizes the importance of molecular surveillance in eliminating malaria, as traditional diagnostics may overlook hidden reservoirs. To monitor ongoing transmission, prevent re-establishment of local human malaria, and control zoonotic P. knowlesi, routine surveillance should include both PCR and microscopy in endemic areas.

Background

Malaria continues to pose a formidable global public health challenge, with an estimated 282 million cases and 610,000 deaths reported in 2024 [1]. In response, the World Health Organization (WHO) launched the Global Technical Strategy for Malaria 2016–2030, aiming for a malaria-free world by 2030. A key milestone toward this goal requires countries to achieve three consecutive years of zero indigenous transmission before being certified malaria-free [2]. Malaysia made remarkable progress toward elimination, notably recording zero indigenous cases of human malaria (i.e., Plasmodium falciparum, Plasmodium vivax, and Plasmodium malariae) in 2018 through its Malaria Eradication Program [1].

However, this achievement has been challenged by the persistent and rising incidence of zoonotic malaria, primarily caused by Plasmodium knowlesi. Environmental changes, such as deforestation and agricultural expansion, have increased human encroachment into forested areas, thereby elevating contact between humans, macaque reservoirs, and competent mosquito vectors [3]. Between 2018 and 2022, P. knowlesi remained the sole cause of indigenous malaria transmission in Malaysia [4], with annual case numbers fluctuating between 2000 and 3000 [1]. According to the WHO Malaria Report 2025, P. knowlesi now makes up 89% of all reported cases in Malaysia [1]. This pattern is particularly evident in Malaysian Borneo states such as Sarawak, where extensive forests and local occupations support continuous zoonotic transmission [5].

A critical complication in elimination efforts is the presence of sub-microscopic and asymptomatic malaria infections. These low-parasitemia infections frequently evade detection by routine light microscopy and require more sensitive molecular diagnostics, such as PCR [6]. Asymptomatic individuals do not seek treatment, allowing parasites to persist undetected. While asymptomatic and submicroscopic infections have been implicated in sustaining transmission of human malaria species, their contribution to P. knowlesi transmission remains incompletely understood [6]. This challenge is particularly pronounced in rural communities, such as those in Sarawak, where livelihoods often depend on activities near forests, including farming, hunting, and logging [7–9]. In these settings, housing conditions often provide limited protection against vectors, and healthcare access is restricted, further establishing these communities as potential reservoirs for ongoing transmission [10].

Together, these factors indicate that traditional surveillance methods might underestimate the true burden of malaria in endemic regions. While asymptomatic and sub-microscopic infections are increasingly reported in Peninsular Malaysia [6, 11–17], information from rural communities in Sarawak, particularly in endemic areas, remains limited and poorly understood [18, 19]. This surveillance gap hinders evidence-based interventions and risks undermining Malaysia’s progress toward elimination. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the prevalence of Plasmodium infections, including submicroscopic cases, among rural communities in the Kapit Division. The findings are expected to provide contemporary insights into the local malaria burden and inform targeted surveillance and control strategies to support Malaysia’s malaria elimination goals.

Methods

Study design and areas

A cross-sectional study was conducted in the Kapit Division, the largest administrative division in Sarawak, Malaysia. Kapit Division is further divided into three districts: Kapit, Song, and Belaga. The division covers approximately 38,934 km2 and had an estimated population of 134,800 in the 2020 national census. The region has become increasingly accessible following the recent completion of road infrastructure connecting Kapit with Sibu, the nearest major urban center. According to official health surveillance data, Kapit Division is classified as an endemic area for malaria, with P. knowlesi as the predominant species [20].

The study sites comprised traditional longhouses in rural areas across the Kapit Division. Longhouses are communal dwellings that house multiple families or entire villages and are characteristic of indigenous communities in Sarawak. Using a convenience sampling approach, longhouses were selected based on recommendations from the Kapit Divisional Health Office and accessibility via the road network. Sampling sites were situated in two districts: Kapit District (i.e., Nanga Banyau) and Belaga District (i.e., Lusong Laku, Sungai Asap, and Sungai Koyan). These areas are predominantly located near forest fringes and along the banks of major rivers, including the Rajang and Baleh, which influence local vector ecology and human movement patterns. Sample size determination

The minimum sample size was calculated using the single population proportion formula [21]. Based on the recent reported submicroscopic malaria prevalence of 15.3% among indigenous populations in Malaysia [11], and assuming a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error, a minimum of 199 participants were required. To account for potential nonresponse or incomplete data, the sample size was increased by 20%, resulting in a final target of 239 participants. Participants were eligible if they were aged 1 year or older, regardless of gender, were registered residents of Kapit Division, had no recent travel history outside the study area, and had not received antimalarial treatment in the 4 weeks preceding enrolment. Individuals who declined informed consent or those currently under treatment for malaria were excluded from the study. In addition, pregnant women were excluded from this community-based surveillance to ensure a consistent non-pregnant reference population for our prevalence, hematological analyses, and related factors in the general population.

Sociodemographic data collection

A pretested questionnaire was administered face-to-face to the head of each participating household, who served as the representative respondent. The questionnaire was provided in both English and Bahasa Malaysia. The Bahasa Malaysia version, a national language of Malaysia, was used to collect sociodemographic, socioeconomic, and clinical information. Variables included age, gender, ethnicity, education level, occupation, household income, recent history of malaria or febrile illness, and self-reported symptoms at or before sample collection.

Blood collection

Approximately 5 mL of peripheral venous blood was drawn by trained nurses or medical assistants into EDTA-anticoagulated vacuum tubes. A small aliquot of fresh blood was immediately used to measure hemoglobin levels to screen for anemia. EDTA samples were placed in a cooler box with ice packs and transported to the Parasitology Laboratory, Faculty of Medicine and Health Sciences, UNIMAS, where they were stored at − 20 °C until further analysis.

Hemoglobin (Hb) measurement

Finger-prick blood samples were obtained from participants to assess Hb levels. Hemoglobin was measured in the field using a portable HemoCue® Hb 201 + analyzer (HemoCue AB, Sweden). The analyzer was calibrated against the international reference method for Hb concentration, in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The cut-off value for anemia was determined according to the WHO guidelines. Anemia was classified according to WHO age- and sex-specific hemoglobin thresholds criteria for children aged 2–14 years, non-pregnant women and men: mild (Hb 10.0–10.9 g/dL [children 6–59 months], 11.0–11.9 g/dL [non-pregnant women/children 5–14 years] or 11.0–12.9 g/dL [men]), moderate (Hb 7.0–9.9 g/dL [children 6–59 months] or 8.0–10.9 g/dL [children aged 5–14 years, non-pregnant women, men]) and for severe anemia (Hb < 7.0 g/dL [children 6–59 months] < 8.0 g/dL [children aged 5–14 years, non-pregnant women, men]) [22].

Microscopic examination

Both thin and thick smears were prepared immediately after blood collection in the field by a trained medical team. For the thin smear, a small blood drop (3–5 µL) was placed near one end of a slide, spread at 30–45° with a spreader slide to form a thin layer, air-dried, and fixed with methanol for 30 s. The thick smear involved placing a larger drop (10–15 µL) on a slide, spreading it in a circle, and drying for 30 min to lyse red blood cells and concentrate parasites. Both smears were transported to the laboratory for staining. Then, the smears were stained with 3% Giemsa for 30 min, rinsed, air-dried, and examined at 100× oil immersion for malaria parasite identification. Each slide was independently examined by two microscopists, with results validated by an experienced parasitologist. Slides were systematically examined using a zig-zag method. The entire smear was examined before it was deemed positive or negative.

Molecular detection of Plasmodium spp. by nested PCR

Genomic DNA was extracted from EDTA-anticoagulated blood using the PrimeWay Genomic II DNA extraction kit (1st BASE, Malaysia) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA concentration and quality were measured using NanoDrop Spectrophotometers (NanoDrop Technologies, USA), and the extracted DNA was stored at −20 °C until further use. Briefly, the extracted DNA was subjected to nested PCR targeting the 18S ribosomal RNA gene of Plasmodium spp. [23]. The first (genus-specific) amplification used PLU1 and rPLU5 primers (nest 1) [24], followed by a nested reaction using rPLU3 and rPLU4 (nest 2) [19] to confirm the presence of Plasmodium DNA. Positive sample for Plasmodium was further tested with species-specific primers to identify human malaria (i.e., P. falciparum, P. vivax, P. malariae, Plasmodium ovale) [23] and simian malaria (i.e., Plasmodium knowlesi) [25] using the PCR product of genus-specific nested 1. DNA-free water and DNA from a confirmed malaria-positive sample were included as negative and positive controls, respectively, in each PCR run. Amplification was performed in a Veriti™ Thermal Cycler (Applied Biosystem, USA). The amplified products were visualized on a 2% agarose gel stained with SYBR Safe DNA Gel Stain (Invitrogen™, USA) at 100 V for 30 min.

Statistical analysis

Data was entered and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., 2021). Data completeness was verified before analysis, and any errors or missing data were excluded from the relevant analyses. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. Bivariate analysis using the Pearson chi-square or Fisher’s exact test was applied where appropriate to assess for associations of Plasmodium infections and demographic or sociodemographic factors. Variables showing an association with a p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

A total of 376 participants were enrolled between March 2024 and August 2025. Demographic characteristics of the study population are presented in Table 1. Participants ranged in age from 2 to 90 years, with a median age of 30 years. The largest age group comprised young adults aged 18–40 years (40.2%), and most participants were female (59.3%). Most (79.5%) were not formally employed, including 41.8% who were housewives, 23.9% who were dependent, and 5.6% who were unemployed men. Formal education was reported by 68.1% of participants, and 81.0% resided in low-income households with a monthly net income below RM 500. Among enrolled participants, 4.8% (18/376) reported a history of malaria infection. At the time of sampling, 4.8% (18/376) presented with self-reported fever, and 17.6% (66/376) had anemia. Among anemic individuals, severity was reported as mild (8.2%; 31/376), moderate (8.2%; 31/376), and severe (1.1%; 4/376), as defined by WHO hemoglobin thresholds [22]. Prevalence of malaria infections and their associated factors

Overall, 5.32% (20/376) of participants were positive for Plasmodium infection by nested PCR (Table 2). All infections were mono-infections, with P. knowlesi as the predominant species (4.78%, 18/376), followed by P. falciparum (0.27%, 1/376) and P. vivax (0.27%, 1/376). Out of these 20 positive cases, 18 (90%) were febrile and detected by microscopy. Among these febrile cases, 88.9% (16/18) were P. knowlesi cases and detectable by microscopy, while 11.1% (2/18) were asymptomatic and submicroscopic, identified only by nested PCR. The remaining two positive cases were febrile P. falciparum (5%, 1/20) and P. vivax (5%, 1/20). All positive individuals were referred to the Kapit district clinic for treatment. Infection prevalence was significantly associated with age (P = 0.02), with the highest proportion among participants aged ≥ 40 years (11/126; 8.73%), followed by those aged 18–40 years (9/151; 5.96%). No infections were detected in individuals under 18. While prevalence did not differ significantly by gender (P = 0.34), it was marginally higher in males (10/151; 6.62%) than in females (9/223; 4.04%). Geographically, all 20 cases were identified in the Kapit District (i.e., Nanga Bayau), with none in the sampled areas of the Belaga District (i.e., Lusung Laku, Sungai Koyan, and Sungai Asap).

Malaria prevalence also showed significant associations with occupation (P = 0.003) and household income (P = 0.001). By occupation, the highest proportion of cases was among non-field workers (6/29; 20.69%), followed by field workers (5/67; 7.46%), housewives (7/157; 4.46%), and dependents (i.e., a tertiary student aged 21 years old) (1/90; 1.11%). Infection was more prevalent in higher-earning households (9/50; 18.0%) than low-earning households (11/305; 3.61%). No significant association was found with prior malaria history, anemia status, or education level.

Discussion

The study examines the prevalence of Plasmodium infection among 376 participants in rural communities within Kapit Division, Sarawak, from March 2024 to August 2025. The overall prevalence of Plasmodium infection was 5.32%. P. knowlesi was the predominant species, which aligns with the current trends in malaria epidemiology across Malaysian Borneo. The zoonotic P. knowlesi malaria now accounts for most reported cases, while indigenous human malaria has become rare [5, 7, 26]. The detection of a single case of P. falciparum and P. vivax underscores that low-level transmission of these human-specific parasites persists in rural communities.

The predominance of P. knowlesi observed in this study is linked to Sarawak’s ecological landscape, characterized by hilly terrain, coastal plains, and mountainous interiors, which shape malaria transmission dynamics [27]. These forested areas are habitat for the macaque reservoir, the long-tailed macaque (Macaca fascicularis) and the pig-tailed macaque (Macaca nemestrina), and the Anopheles vector [28, 29]. Deforestation and land-use change have increased human exposure to these natural reservoirs, facilitating the spread of P. knowlesi infections [30, 31]. Furthermore, most home settlements in rural areas of Sarawak, in proximity to forests and their forest-dependent livelihoods, such as farming and logging, increase the risk of infection [7–9].

A notable finding of this study is the low prevalence of submicroscopic P. knowlesi infections, confirming the existence of a low-density reservoir in rural settings that is undetectable by routine microscopy. Previous studies in Malaysia have documented a variable range of low-density malaria prevalence, from 0.36% to 36.9% across different settings and time periods [6, 11–19]. These studies span more than a decade, during which Malaysia experienced a substantial shift in malaria epidemiology from predominantly human malaria species to zoonotic P. knowlesi infections. Consequently, differences in the prevalence and species composition reported across studies should be interpreted within their respective epidemiological contexts. Consistent with our findings, several studies have identified P. knowlesi as the predominant species among submicroscopic infections [6, 14, 19]. Nevertheless, submicroscopic infections caused by other malaria species, including P. vivax, continue to be reported in certain populations and settings. For example, Jiram et al. [18] identified P. vivax as the most common submicroscopic species in a study conducted between 2013 and 2014 among the longhouse community in Belaga, Sarawak. These observations reflect the transition of Malaysia’s malaria landscape from predominantly human malaria species to one dominated by zoonotic P. knowlesi, with occasional reports of P. vivax infections often linked to specific outbreak periods or localized transmission contexts.

Despite the low detection rate observed, submicroscopic infections pose a challenge to elimination efforts. While the epidemiological significance of submicroscopic P. knowlesi infections remains incompletely understood, low-density infections may represent a hidden source of parasite persistence. However, unlike human malaria species [32], the extent to which asymptomatic and submicroscopic P. knowlesi infections contribute to mosquito infection and onward transmission remains unclear and requires further investigation. These infections are routinely missed by standard microscopy, particularly in low-transmission settings and asymptomatic individuals. This underscores the necessity of sensitive molecular tools, such as nested PCR, in surveillance programs for active case detection in community-based screening, outbreak detection, and routine epidemiological monitoring in endemic and post-elimination settings.

This study observed a pronounced geographic disparity in infection rates, with all positive cases identified in Kapit District and none in Belaga District. This finding aligns with spatial–temporal analyses that consistently identify the Kapit district as a high-risk area for malaria in Sarawak, with transmission often clustering in the northern and central regions [5, 33]. The heterogeneity between these two rural, forested districts suggests highly localized variations in transmission intensity. Kapit's status as a persistent hotspot can be attributed to a confluence of ecological and anthropogenic factors. A key factor is the significant overlap between human activity and the reservoir host's habitat. The distribution of long-tailed macaques (M. fascicularis) harboring P. knowlesi was predominant in districts such as the Kapit Division [33]. This macaque species is known to inhabit areas near humans due to deforestation and easier access to food [31, 34]. Furthermore, the presence of competent vectors such as Anopheles latens, previously incriminated in Kapit, facilitates efficient transmission [35].

In contrast, the absence of cases in the Belaga District warrants careful interpretation. This finding contrasts with a prior study conducted 12 years earlier in Belaga that reported a low prevalence (1.7%) of submicroscopic P. vivax malaria during a local outbreak [18]. The temporal gap between our study and this study is important, as Malaysia has since achieved and sustained zero indigenous human malaria with substantial changes in malaria epidemiology [1]. While zoonotic P. knowlesi infections increasingly dominate the current malaria trend in Malaysia, the earlier study was conducted during P. vivax outbreak, which may have influenced detection rates. The discrepancy may also be due to our sampling, which may have occurred during a period of low seasonal transmission or missed focal transmission hotspots. Another possible reason is that genuine differences in macaque density, vector ecology, or local behavior may exist between the two districts, warranting further investigation.

Analysis of demographic data reveals a clear association between malaria infection and older age groups, with most cases occurring in individuals over 40 years old and a complete absence of cases in children. This age distribution pattern is consistent with previous findings on P. knowlesi [9, 12, 13] and is associated with occupational exposure. Adults are more likely to engage in high-risk, forest-based livelihoods, such as farming, logging, or hunting, thereby increasing contact with reservoir hosts and vectors [7–9]. Whereas children may typically remain within or near the longhouse, limiting exposure.

Occupation was also significantly associated with infection, although the observed pattern deviates from conventional expectations. A higher risk was observed among non-field workers, such as teachers and office staff, compared to field workers, including farmers, loggers, and laborers. Additional cases were also found among housewives and dependents, including tertiary students. These findings indicate that malaria transmission is not limited to forest-based occupational exposure but also extends to peridomestic environments. This observation aligns with growing evidence that P. knowlesi transmission can occur in peridomestic environments, particularly in communities near forest fringes, where human settlements overlap with vector habitats and macaque reservoirs [7, 9, 30, 36].

In settlements near forest fringes, occupational categories alone do not fully account for transmission, and incidental exposure during routine activities contributed substantially to infection risk [9]. The high incidence of cases observed among non-forest-related occupations in this study may reflect differences in behavioral exposure and vector-avoidance practices. While individuals in forest-related occupations may adopt protective measures at work, such as wearing protective clothing or using repellents, those not routinely exposed to forest environments may be less vigilant in everyday activities. Furthermore, the primary vector, An. latens, is exophagic and has been documented to initiate biting as early as 18:00 h in Kapit [30, 35]. This early-evening biting behavior overlaps with routine activities such as commuting home from work, social interaction among longhouse communities, childcare, and household tasks [36]. Because these vectors predominantly bite and rest outdoors, conventional indoor-based interventions offer limited protection [37]. In contrast, children are more likely to remain indoors during these times, which may partially explain their lower observed risk.

Household income also showed a paradoxically similar pattern. This study observed a higher risk among participants with household incomes > RM500 per month than among those with incomes < RM500 per month. This contrasts with previous studies that have linked lower socioeconomic status to increased malaria risk through poorer housing conditions and reduced access to preventive measures [10, 38]. However, in a rural setting, higher income does not necessarily indicate affluence; it is a proxy for better housing conditions or the ability to acquire preventive measures such as repellent. These individuals living within a longhouse environment likely share similar baseline exposure risks. While this study did not collect behavioral data to assess these factors directly, higher earners were more likely to hold formal employment, such as non-field work. They may have greater mobility, enabling them to travel more frequently between settlements or forest-fringe districts. Increased mobility has been identified as a significant risk factor for P. knowlesi infection [9].

While this study's findings provide valuable insights into the prevalence of malaria in rural communities in Sarawak, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study uses a cross-sectional design, which provides only a snapshot of the infection at a single time point. Therefore, this study cannot establish a causal relationship between low-density parasitemia and symptom development or the acquisition of partial immunity, and thus requires a longitudinal study design. This study also used convenience sampling to recruit community members. The study was conducted in a limited number of communities or longhouses within Kapit and Belaga districts, which may not be fully representative of Sarawak's entire population. The longhouses in this study were selected based on accessibility and community willingness to participate, which may have introduced selection bias. At the individual level, participation was voluntary, and individuals who were absent during sampling or declined to participate may have different exposure risks, potentially biasing prevalence estimates and risk factor analyses. Additionally, the complete absence of cases in the Belaga district in our study may be due to a lack of transmission or an inadequate sample size to detect very low-level transmission in that specific area. As reported by Jiram et al. [18], a low prevalence (1.7%) of submicroscopic malaria was observed in a large sample of 1744 respondents.

Furthermore, the relatively small sample sizes of non-field workers (n = 29) and of households with income > RM500 (n = 50) limit statistical precision and may inflate prevalence estimates. The unequal distribution between groups may increase the risk of false-positive associations and require verification in a larger, more representative population. This study examines only certain risk factors, primarily demographic and socioeconomic information. It did not examine practice-related factors that could influence infection prevalence, such as housing conditions, personal protective measures, or vector exposure. The inclusion of these variables in future studies is essential to developing a more comprehensive understanding of malaria prevalence, risk, and transmission drivers among rural communities in Sarawak. Similarly, we recognize the exclusion of pregnant women as a limitation of this study. Although this decision was made to ensure a consistent comparator group for our prevalence and hematological analyses, pregnant women constitute a significant population in malaria-endemic regions. Future P. knowlesi surveillance studies should be specifically designed to include pregnant women, as their exclusion creates a significant gap in understanding the true burden and clinical impact of this emerging zoonotic infection across all at-risk groups.

Additionally, while PCR provides high sensitivity for detecting sub-microscopic infections, the findings lack data on infection density to investigate the causal relationship. The detection of only two submicroscopic cases in our study may have underestimated the true burden of asymptomatic carriers. More sensitive molecular approaches, including quantitative PCR (qPCR) for parasite quantification and ultra-sensitive methods such as reverse transcription-based total nucleic acid detection, have demonstrated improved limits of detection for P. knowlesi and other zoonotic malaria species [39]. Additionally, this study did not incorporate serological assessments, which can provide complementary information on historical exposure and cumulative transmission intensity [40]. The integration of serological markers with molecular diagnostics would enable a more comprehensive assessment of current and past malaria exposure in low-transmission settings, thereby enhancing the discovery of hidden reservoirs.

Conclusions

This study provides important findings that strengthen Malaysia's path toward malaria elimination, particularly in Sarawak. The findings confirm that P. knowlesi is the principal cause of infection, underscoring the need to integrate a One Health approach that coordinates human, animal, and environmental health to effectively manage transmission from wildlife reservoirs. Importantly, the research shows that a significant portion of infections in rural communities are submicroscopic and would be undetected by conventional diagnostics. This evidence supports investing in advanced molecular surveillance tools and incorporating serological approaches, as the combined use of molecular and serological methods can enhance the detection of hidden reservoirs and improve the assessment of transmission dynamics. Furthermore, the study identifies clear links between infection risk and specific sociodemographic factors, and transmission is not restricted to forest-based occupations but extends to peridomestic settings and is likely shaped by behavioral and environmental exposures. This enables public health authorities to design precisely tailored interventions that reduce exposure and protect those most at risk. To maintain national elimination, a targeted strategy is recommended, one that combines enhanced detection, timely treatment, focused vector control, and community-specific prevention. Implementing this integrated approach, especially in rural-endemic areas, will be key to achieving a malaria-free Malaysia.
