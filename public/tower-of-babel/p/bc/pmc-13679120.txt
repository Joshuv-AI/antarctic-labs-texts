Phytochemical profile and biological activities of Nerium Oleander L. flowers and leaves
Authors: Amen Bouabidi, Ahlem Zrig, Kaouther ben Yahia, Mohammad K Okla, Ibrahim A Alaraidh, Abd Alhamed Alkhaled, Yousef Alhaj Hamoud, Mehrez Romdhane, Maria Gabriela Maridueña-Zavala, Ezzeddine Saadaoui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the phytochemical composition and biological activities of leaves and flowers from seven Nerium oleander L. cultivars grown in Tunisia to evaluate their potential for industrial and crop-related applications. Primary metabolite analysis showed that flowers had a higher accumulation of metabolites than leaves, with the ‘Roseum Plenum’ (RP) cultivar reaching a peak total soluble sugar concentration of 31.57 µg/ml, dominated by sucrose (19.47 µg/ml). Amino acid profiles were also significantly higher in flowers; glycine peaked at 50.66 µg/ml in ‘Villa Romaine’ (VRS), while ‘RP’ flowers showed elevated levels of asparagine (3.17 µg/ml) and glutamine (1.18 µg/ml), suggesting elevated amino acid production and stress resilience. Regarding the secondary metabolome, catechin emerged as the predominant compound, reaching 13.67 µg/ml in ‘Petit Salmon’ (PS) flowers compared to 6.64 µg/ml in ‘R’ leaves. Other abundant polyphenols identified across the profiles included caffeic and ferulic acids. In addition, quercetin and quercitrin were identified as the main flavonoids, with particularly high concentrations observed in the ‘PS’ cultivar. Biological assays demonstrated that floral extracts generally possess superior bioactivity. ‘RP’ flower extracts exhibited exceptional antimicrobial potency, specifically against Escherichia coli and Salmonella typhimurium, significantly outperforming leaf extracts. Antioxidant tests (DPPH, FRAP, and lipid peroxidation) further confirmed that ‘PS’ and ‘Mont Blanc’ (MB) cultivars are the most active. These findings indicate that N. oleander flowers, particularly from the ‘Roseum Plenum’ and ‘Petit Salmon’ cultivars, serve as high-value sources of bioactive compounds for the development of natural antioxidants and botanical antimicrobial agents in the industrial and agricultural sectors.Keywords: Antioxidant, Antimicrobial, Nerium oleander, Chemical compositions, Cultivars

Introduction

For centuries, plants have served as a cornerstone of traditional medicine and a major source of therapeutic compounds used to treat a wide range of human diseases. With the advancement of modern phytochemistry and pharmacology, it has become evident that many plant species synthesize a vast array of bioactive metabolites with diverse biological activities and promising pharmaceutical potential. Among these plants is Nerium oleander L., commonly known as “Defla” in Tunisia, a highly toxic yet pharmacologically potent shrub belonging to the Apocynaceae family. This species is native to the Mediterranean basin and widely distributed across subtropical Asia and the southwestern United States [1]. It is the sole representative of the Nerium genus and is extensively cultivated for ornamental purposes due to its hardiness and aesthetic value, with more than 400 cultivars exhibiting diverse flower colors such as red, pink, white, orange, and purple [2]. In Tunisia, N. oleander occurs in both cultivated and wild forms and is commonly found along roadsides and in public gardens, providing an abundant source of non-food biomass.

Despite its toxicity, N. oleander has long been used in traditional medicine systems across different cultures. Several studies have reported its pharmacological properties, including anticancer potential [3], antiviral activity against pathogens such as HIV and herpes viruses [4], and anti-inflammatory effects that may contribute to the management of chronic diseases [5]. Moreover, the growing global concern regarding antibiotic-resistant bacterial strains has stimulated the search for novel antimicrobial agents derived from plants. In this context, N. oleander has attracted scientific attention due to its antibacterial activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, including Staphylococcus aureus, Bacillus subtilis, Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Klebsiella pneumoniae [6].

The biological activities of N. oleander are mainly attributed to its rich phytochemical composition, which includes cardiac glycosides, flavonoids, alkaloids, phenolic compounds, tannins, and terpenoids. These compounds are known to exert multiple biological effects by disrupting microbial cell membranes, inhibiting protein synthesis, and scavenging reactive oxygen species, thereby contributing to the plant’s broad pharmacological potential [7]. Although the phytochemical composition and pharmacological activities of N. oleander leaves have been widely investigated, relatively little attention has been given to the chemical composition and biological properties of its flowers, particularly in region-specific cultivars. In Tunisia, no comprehensive study has yet compared the phytochemical profiles or biological activities, such as antioxidant and antibacterial properties, of different N. oleander flower color varieties.

Therefore, the present study aims to address this knowledge gap by systematically evaluating the phytochemical composition, antioxidant capacity, and antibacterial activity of one spontaneous variety and seven cultivars of N. oleander commonly grown in Tunisia. The novelty of this work lies in its focus on locally available flower and leaf materials from different cultivars, an area that remains largely unexplored. Specifically, the objectives of this study are to: (1) characterize the phytochemical profiles of N. oleander cultivars with distinct flower colors; (2) evaluate their antioxidant potential using established in vitro assays; and (3) assess their antibacterial activity against selected Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacterial strains. The identification of cultivar-specific variations in phytochemical composition and bioactivity may support the selection of promising genotypes for potential applications in pharmaceutical, agricultural, and industrial sectors, while also contributing valuable data to regional ethnobotanical and medicinal plant research.

Materials and methods

Plant material collection and preparation

Leaves and flowers from seven distinct cultivars and one spontaneous variety of Nerium oleander L. (Table 1) were hand-picked from the arid regions of Gabes and Medenine in southern Tunisia and formally identified by Dr. Ezzeddine Saadaoui. These specimens, comprising Villa Romaine (Spontaneous), Villa Romaine (Cultivated), Roseum Plenum, Red Variegata, Petit Salmon, Mont Blanc, Alsace, and Saadaouia, were deposited under voucher numbers NO-217 to NO-223 at the Agricultural Experimentation Unit of Gabes, Laboratory of Management and Valorization of Forest Resources (LGVRF, LR11INRGREF01), National Institute for Research in Rural Engineering, Water, and Forestry (INRGREF), University of Carthage, Tunisia. To ensure biological representativeness, samples for each cultivar were collected from at least three different plants in the same location, representing three independent biological replicates. Due to the high concentration of toxic cardiac glycosides, protective gloves were worn throughout the collection process. To ensure the stability of thermolabile compounds, the plant material was thoroughly washed with deionized water and air-dried in a controlled, shaded environment at (25 ± 2 °C) for 15–20 days. The dried biomass was then finely ground into a homogenous powder and stored in airtight containers at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) until multi-platform phytochemical analysis was performed. Extraction yields were calculated as a percentage of the initial dry weight (w/w) and ranged from 10% to 15% across the cultivars. All subsequent phytochemical and biological assays were performed in triplicate (resulting in three technical replicates per biological replicate) to ensure statistical precision and determine within-run technical variance versus true biological diversity.

Sugar extraction and measurements

Soluble sugars were extracted from the dried matrix using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) -grade methanol [8]. Sugars were extracted and quantified by homogenizing 100 mg of the dried, homogenized plant tissue powder (achieving a 1:10 w/v sample-to-solvent mass ratio) in 1.0 mL of 80% (v/v) methanol warmed to boiling in a water bath for 30 s, with extraction maintained at 80 °C under continuous agitation. The mixture was allowed to cool to room temperature (25 ± 2 °C), and was subsequently centrifuged at 2000 x g for 10 min at 4 °C. The pellet was washed twice with additional 1.0 mL volumes of 80% methanol, and the resulting supernatants were pooled to achieve final volume alignment. The aqueous phase was then filtered through a 0.2 μm Millipore membrane and spiked with norvaline or aminobutyric acid as internal standards to ensure recovery accuracy. Quantitative determination was conducted using HPLC system coupled with a Refractive Index Detector (RID). The analytical separation utilized a specialized carbohydrate column maintained at 35 °C with an injection volume of 25 µL. The mobile phase was an isocratic mixture of acetonitrile and water (75:25 v/v) at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. Individual sugars (sucrose, glucose, fructose, etc.) were identified by comparison of retention times and quantified through external standard calibration curves (1–12 mg/100 mL acetonitrile/water 1:1, v/v). The method showed excellent linearity (R² > 0.999) over the concentration range of 0.05–10 mg/mL. Recoveries ranged from 95% to 105%, and the precision was good, with RSD values below 2.0%. The limit of detection (LOD) was 10–40 µg/mL based on a signal-to-noise ratio of 3:1, while the limit of quantification (LOQ) was 30–100 µg/mL based on a signal-to-noise ratio of 10:1.

Quantitative profiling of amino acids

Amino acid extraction and quantification followed the optimized protocol described previously [9]. A 100 mg sample of the dried powdered tissue was homogenized in 5 mL of 80% (v/v) ethanol (representing a 1:50 w/v sample-to-solvent mass ratio) at 5000 rpm for 1 min at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C), and the homogenate was subsequently centrifuged at 14,000 x g for 25 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was partitioned against 5 mL of chloroform in a separator funnel at 4 °C to remove lipophilic pigments. The aqueous phase was then filtered through a 0.2 μm Millipore membrane and spiked with norvaline or aminobutyric acid as internal standards to ensure recovery accuracyy. Targeted analysis was performed using a Waters Acquity UPLC-TQD system equipped with a BEH amide column (2.1 mm × 50 mm). The amino acid reference standards consisted of 15 amino acids, which were used for detection of retention times of each amino acid. Meanwhile, the internal standard (α-aminobutyric) was added to the reference sample as well as the plant sample. Separation was achieved via a binary gradient elution: Solvent A (84% ammonium formate, 6% formic acid, and 10% acetonitrile) and Solvent B (acetonitrile with 2% formic acid). Mass detection and data processing were managed using Star Chromatography software (v. 5.51), with concentrations expressed in µg/mL based on authentic standard calibration curves. Method linearity showed R² > 0.995 across all standard curves, with internal standard recoveries between 92% and 104% and RSD below 3.5%. LOD for the targeted amino acids ranged from 0.5 to 5 ng/mL (3:1 signal-to-noise ratio), while LOQ ranged from 1.5 to 15 ng/mL (10:1 signal-to-noise ratio).

HPLC-DAD analysis of phenolic and flavonoid profiles

For the secondary metabolite profile, phenolic and flavonoid fractions were extracted by combining 100 mg of the dried sample powder with 2 mL of an acetone–water solution (4:1, v/v), establishing a 1:20 w/v sample-to-solvent mass ratio. The extraction was carried out under continuous magnetic stirring at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) for 1 h. The crude homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000 x g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove particulate debris, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm Nylon (or PTFE) syringe filter to ensure sample clarity prior to analysis. The filtered extracts were analyzed using a Shimadzu HPLC system (SCL-10 AVP, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with a diode array detector (DAD) and a Lichrosorb Si-60 column (3 × 150 mm, 7 μm) [9]. Separation was performed using a binary gradient system consisting of (A) water–formic acid (90:10, v/v) and (B) acetonitrile–water–formic acid (85:10:5, v/v/v) at a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min at a column temperature maintained at 30 °C. 3,5-Dichloro-4-hydroxybenzoic acid was used as an internal standard to ensure quantitative reliability. Individual polyphenols, including catechin, caffeic acid, and ferulic acid, were identified by comparing their UV–Vis spectra and retention times with those of authentic standards. Method validation confirmed excellent linearity (R² > 0.998), with internal standard recoveries ranging from 94% to 102% and RSD below 2.5%. LOD for the targeted polyphenols were 0.1–0.5 µg/mL (3:1 signal-to-noise ratio), while LOQ ranged from 0.3 to 1.5 µg/mL (10:1 signal-to-noise ratio).

Antimicrobial potential

The antimicrobial efficacy of N. oleander extracts was evaluated against a panel of industrial and clinical pathogens. For extract preparation, dried plant material from each cultivar was ground into a fine powder and subjected to solvent extraction using 10% DMSO (v/v). The mixture was homogenized thoroughly and subjected to agitation at room temperature for 24 h, followed by centrifugation at 14,000 x g for 15 min to remove particulate matter. The supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane filter to obtain a clarified crude extract. The filtrates were then concentrated under reduced pressure where necessary and reconstituted to a defined concentration prior to analysis. The antimicrobial efficacy was determined using the standard agar well diffusion method [10]. Suspensions of actively growing microbial cultures were prepared in sterile saline and adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland turbidity standard (approximately 1.5 × 10⁸ CFU/mL for bacteria and 1.0 × 10⁶ spores/mL for fungi) using a spectrophotometer. A volume of 100 µl of each standardized suspension was uniformly spread across the surface of the respective agar plates (Mueller-Hinton Agar for bacteria and Sabouraud Dextrose Agar for fungi). Wells (6 mm diameter) were bored into the agar and filled with 50 µL of the prepared extracts. The bacterial strains tested included Staphylococcus epidermidis, Enterococcus faecalis, Escherichia coli, Salmonella typhimurium, Proteus vulgaris, and Enterobacter aerogenes, while fungal strains included Candida albicans and Aspergillus flavus. The plates were allowed to pre-diffuse for 1 h at room temperature. After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h (bacteria) or 28 °C for 48 h (fungi), zones of inhibition (ZOI) were measured in millimeters (mm) using a digital Vernier caliper. To provide a precise quantitative measurement beyond diffusion alone, the Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) was determined via the standard broth microdilution method in 96-well microtiter plates using two-fold serial dilutions of the extracts in liquid media. The MIC was expressed in µg/mL, defined as the lowest concentration of extract resulting in no visible microbial growth.

Antioxidant capacity evaluation

For the antioxidant evaluations, 100 mg of plant samples was extract in 1 mL of HPLC-grade ethanol, followed by centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to collect the clarified supernatant as the standardized stock solution.

DPPH analyses

The total antioxidant potential was evaluated through a suite of complementary assays to capture diverse chemical mechanisms. The DPPH assay measured free-radical scavenging activity using 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl, with Trolox utilized as the standard for chain-breaking activity [11]. DPPH· radical has a violet color with a maximum absorbance at 517 nm that becomes colorless in presence of antioxidants. The reaction mixtures consisted of 0.1 mL of the reconstituted samples and 3.9 mL of 200 µM DPPH• (prepared in HPLC-grade ethanol), establishing a strict 1:39 (v/v) sample-to-reagent ratio. The mixtures were briefly vortexed and incubated in the dark at a controlled temperature of 37 °C for exactly 30 min. Afterwards, the absorbance was detected (517 nm). The percentage of inhibition was calculated against a control. All measurements were executed in technical triplicate across the three independent biological replicates.

FRAP analyses

The FRAP assay (Ferric Reducing Antioxidant Power) determined the reduction of Fe³⁺ to Fe²⁺, with absorbance recorded at 532 nm. For the FRAP assay, 100 µL of freshly prepared FRAP reagent (0.3 M acetate buffer (pH 3.6), 0.01 mM 2,4,6-Tris(2-pyridyl)-s-triazine (TPTZ) in 0.04 mM HCl and 0.02 M FeCl₃·6 H₂O) was mixed with 100 µL of the ethanolic extract for 30 min, and the liberated ferrous ions in samples caused increased absorbance measured at wavelength 590 nm. Trolox was used as standard [12]. To assess the antioxidant capacity of the plant extract, four complementary assays were conducted.

Fe chelation, Fe oxidizing activity, chain-breaking activity in the aqueous phase, and chain-breaking activity in the lipid phase

Iron chelating and oxidizing activities were evaluated by incubating the extract with FeCl₂ and ferrozine (562 nm) or FeSO₄ and potassium thiocyanate (480 nm respectively, at 25 °C for 10 min to determine its ability to bind or oxidize iron. Chain-breaking antioxidant activity in the aqueous phase was measured by monitoring the delay in AAPH-induced bleaching of crocin or fluorescein at 37 °C. In the lipid phase, inhibition of lipid peroxidation was assessed at 234 nm using AMVN in a linoleic acid system incubated at 37 °C. Together, these assays characterize the extract’s capacity to neutralize free radicals and modulate transition metal reactivity in both aqueous and lipid environments. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging was assessed by evaluating hydroxyl radical scavenging in a system generating radicals via the interaction of Fe³⁺, ascorbate, and H₂O₂ (1 mM) at 38 °C. Industrial utility testing included specific assays for iron-binding, iron-oxidizing, and lipid peroxidation inhibition to determine the extract’s stability and protective capacity in fatty acid-rich industrial formulations [12].

Statistical analysis

To establish full methodological transparency and reproducibility, data from the three technical replicates were averaged for each biological replicate before executing cross-sample statistical treatments. All experiments were conducted using these three independent biological replicates, and results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Prior to comparative assessments, normality and homoscedasticity of variance were validated using the Shapiro-Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. Data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) via the General Linear Model procedure to determine significant differences among treatments. When ANOVA indicated significance, mean separation was performed using Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test at p ≤ 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (Version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Primary metabolites

Sugar content and carbon partitioning

The metabolic profiling of Nerium oleander revealed that total soluble sugar concentrations were significantly higher in flowers than in leaves, indicating that reproductive tissues serve as the primary carbon sinks (Table 2). In floral tissues, the Roseum Plenum (RP) cultivar reached a peak concentration of 31.57 µg/ml, while the maximum concentration in leaves was recorded in the Mont Blanc (MB) cultivar at 16.81 µg/ml. This suggests that flowers, particularly in the RP cultivar, accumulate higher sugar levels to support energy-intensive processes such as pollinator attraction and reproductive metabolism. Sucrose emerged as the dominant sugar across all cultivars, though its concentration varied significantly, peaking at 19.47 µg/ml in RP flowers while falling below 3 µg/ml in MB and Villa Romaine (VRS) flowers. This variation points to cultivar-specific strategies for sugar storage and mobilization; whereas RP invests heavily in energy storage within its flowers, cultivars like VRS and MB appear to utilize a less energy-intensive strategy for their reproductive organs.

Amino acid profiles and nitrogen metabolism

Amino acid levels were significantly higher in the flowers than in the leaves, indicating a greater concentration of nitrogen-based nutrients in floral tissues (Table 3). High levels of amino acids such as glycine, alanine, and lysine were localized in flowers, especially within the VRS and PS cultivars. For instance, glycine levels in VRS flowers reached 50.66 µg/ml, representing a much higher concentration than that found in vegetative tissues. This enrichment suggests that these tissues are actively synthesizing proteins required for development and defense mechanisms. Additionally, the elevated concentrations of asparagine (3.17 µg/ml) and glutamine (1.18 µg/ml) in RP flowers indicate a specialized role in nitrogen metabolism and stress tolerance, potentially providing the resilience necessary for reproduction under varying environmental conditions.

Biological activities

Antimicrobial activity

Both leaf and flower extracts inhibited microbial growth, with floral extracts generally showing higher activity (Fig. 1). The RP flower extract demonstrated the highest efficacy against bacterial pathogens, specifically Escherichia coli and Salmonella typhimurium, with inhibition zones reaching 55.93 ± 0.75 mm and 36.71 ± 0.37 mm, respectively. These results suggest that RP flowers are equipped with potent antimicrobial properties driven by their high concentrations of secondary metabolites. Furthermore, RP flowers showed the strongest antifungal activity against Aspergillus flavus (25.06 ± 2.85 mm), supporting their defensive role during the reproductive phase. Sensitivity trials revealed that E. coli was the most susceptible strain to both extracts, with inhibition zones expanding from 18.24 ± 0.086 mm in leaves to 55.93 ± 0.75 mm in flowers. Additionally, Candida albicans was most sensitive to VRS flowers (17.56 ± 2.3 mm). The flowers consistently showed stronger inhibition at lower concentrations across most tested pathogens. The most notable increases in potency were observed against P. vulgaris (average MIC reduction of 81.07%) and the fungal strain A. flavus (average MIC reduction of 71.81%) (Table 5). In contrast, E. aerogenes deviated from this pattern, with leaf extracts exhibiting a lower MIC than flower extracts by 34.24%. Among all tested strains, S. typhimurium was the most sensitive overall, requiring only 3.67 units for leaf extracts and 2.37 units for flower extracts to inhibit active growth.

Principal component analysis (PCA): varietal and organ-specific metabolomic divergence

The comparative analysis of the distinct Nerium oleander cultivars and organs demonstrates a clear biochemical separation between leaf and flower tissues, with the organ type serving as the primary driver of metabolic diversity (Fig. 3). PC1, explaining 68.4% of the variance, represents the primary metabolic shift between the photosynthetic “source” (leaves) and reproductive “sink” (flowers) tissues, driven largely by the contrast between structural lipids and transport sugars. Meanwhile, PC2 accounts for 21.1% of the variance, primarily capturing the secondary metabolic diversity and varietal differences, such as the unique phenolic and antioxidant concentrations that cluster the flower tissues of MBvar7 (Mont Blanc) and the leaf profiles of RPvar3 (Roseum Plenum) and VRsvar1 (Villa Romaine Spontaneous). The PCA results indicate that leaf profiles are strongly clustered on the negative side of PC1, defined by higher relative abundances of structural fatty acids and organic acids, specifically palmitic, stearic, arachidonic, malic, and citric acids, which support vegetative and photosynthetic functions. Conversely, flower tissues, particularly pronounced in the positive PC1 and negative PC2 quadrant for MBvar7, are enriched with specialized metabolites such as glucose, fructose, sucrose, and potent antioxidants like quercetin, quercitrin, rutin, and chlorogenic acid, highlighting their physiological roles in reproduction, pollination attraction, and environmental signaling. Together, these two components explain 89.5% of the total chemical fluctuations across the sampled cohorts, confirming that the combination of tissue type and genetic background provides a comprehensive profile of the plant’s physiological state.

Discussion

Phytochemical partitioning: a strategy for industrial biomass valorization

Our findings indicate that the floral tissues of N. oleander exhibit distinct metabolic profiles, accumulating higher concentrations of primary and secondary metabolites compared to leaves. This tissue-specific allocation appears consistent with source–sink transition theories in plant physiology, where reproductive organs may act as sinks for mobile carbon and nitrogen derived from vegetative tissues [13]. The observed decline in sucrose levels in MB and VRS flowers could reflect potential hydrolytic conversion into hexoses to support cellular expansion during the blooming phase [14], though definitive flux analysis would be required to confirm this pathway. This metabolic partitioning suggests that tissue selection may be a factor in optimizing the recovery of bioactive compounds. The presence of soluble sugars and a diverse profile of twenty amino acids could reflect substantial resource allocation toward reproductive development. Among the studied varieties, the RP cultivar demonstrated notable levels of sucrose (19.47 µg/mL) and asparagine (3.17 µg/mL), which may indicate high metabolic vigor and potential suitability for further investigation in biotechnological applications.

Furthermore, the secondary metabolome, comprising twenty identified polyphenols and flavonoids, exhibited a clear tissue-specific gradient, with flowers serving as the primary reservoir. This distribution may represent an evolutionary strategy to protect reproductive organs. Catechin showed a twofold increase in PS flowers (13.67 µg/mL) compared to R leaves (6.64 µg/mL), potentially driven by the upregulation of the phenylpropanoid pathway for UV protection [15]. Consequently, floral biomass might represent a candidate raw material for the speculative recovery of phenolic acids, such as caffeic and ferulic acids, which have reported properties in literature with hypothetical applications in the cosmetic and pharmaceutical sectors due to their anti-inflammatory and UV-protective characteristics [16].

Phytochemical diversity in N. oleander

Various extraction methods are used to isolate phytochemicals from N. oleander, with outcomes appearing to depend strongly on solvent polarity. Polar solvents such as ethanol are effective for extracting glycosylated flavonoids and sugars, while non-polar solvents like hexane and cyclohexane are generally preferred for lipophilic compounds such as terpenes and waxes. This behavior is consistent with the “like dissolves like” principle. This behavior follows the “like dissolves like” principle. Different plant parts contain distinct phytochemical profiles. Leaves include carbohydrates, flavonoids, alkaloids, steroids, cardiac glycosides, and tannins [17]. Major cardiac glycosides such as oleandrin, neriin, and digitoxigenin-related compounds act by inhibiting the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump, a key enzyme in nerve impulse transmission [18]. Seeds, bark, and roots also contain various glucosides and steroids, with roots and bark potentially exhibiting higher concentrations as a possible defense mechanism against pathogens and soil organisms [19]. Extraction methodologies heavily influence compound diversity. For example, more alkaloids were detected in broader solvent systems compared to pure methanol, suggesting differences in selectivity and polarity coverage [20]. Hydro-ethanolic extracts further reveal coumarins, terpenes, triterpenes, flavonoids, and cardiac glycosides. Differences in molecular size and structure could influence prospective bioavailability and membrane permeability, which may provide a partial, tentative explanation for the variations in antimicrobial activity observed across extracts.

Improved biological activity

Antioxidant activity

The antioxidant capacity of N. oleander is strongly correlated with its polyphenolic density, making it a hypothetical candidate for future evaluation as a natural food and industrial stabilizer. Cultivars MB and PS demonstrated strong free-radical scavenging profiles against DPPH and hydroxyl radicals, while PS exhibited superior anti-lipid peroxidation activity. Mechanistically, these activities are frequently described as driven by the ability of flavonoids to neutralize ROS through hydrogen atom transfer and single-electron transfer mechanisms. The high density of hydroxyl groups on the B-ring of compounds such as quercetin may enable hydrogen donation to free radicals, forming resonance-stabilized phenoxyl radicals that could terminate oxidative chain reactions [21].

Our PCA (Fig. 3) supports this mathematical relationship, as the vectors for antioxidant markers like DPPH-scavenging activity align closely with the concentrations of quercetin and rutin. In the context of industrial product stability, the chain-breaking activity observed in both lipid and aqueous phases, where PS performed best 65.1 ± 8.9 in the aqueous phase), highlights the potential suitability of these extracts for further exploratory investigation in preventing oxidative degradation in lipid-rich model systems. This bioactivity is strongly correlated with the presence of lipophilic aglycones and specific fatty acids found in our lipidomic profile, such as oleic and linoleic acids, which may work in tandem with polyphenols. This biphasic efficacy suggests that N. oleander extracts contain a blend of hydrophilic (glycosylated flavonoids) and lipophilic (aglycones) antioxidants, enabling hypothetical activity at both the oil–water interface and within lipid membranes [22]. These findings align with previous studies [23, 24], supporting the view that the polyphenolic profile of N. oleander may provide induced redox defense system with potential for future exploration as an alternative to synthetic antioxidants. It has also been reported that N. oleander flowers can serve as a source of natural antioxidants [15]. Both leaves and flowers have been shown to contain phenolic compounds, such as rutin, catechin, epicatechin, quercetin, and quinic acid [25], which exhibit high levels of antioxidant activity [19]. Mouhcine et al. [26] reported the high antioxidant capacity of N. oleander extracts; while the tested concentration of the aqueous extract had results comparable to BHT, the ethanol extract’s IC50 was similarly aligned with potent baseline performance.

Antimicrobial activity

The antimicrobial efficacy of N. oleander demonstrated marked potency against Gram-negative pathogens, which are typically resistant to conventional treatments due to their complex cell wall structure. RP flower extracts showed high antimicrobial activity against E. coli and 36.71 mm for S. typhimurium. This potency significantly exceeded that of leaf extracts under our assay conditions, suggesting that floral biomass synthesizes specific membrane-disrupting metabolites that could be evaluated as potential botanical biocides. Gram-negative bacteria possess an outer membrane rich in lipopolysaccharides (LPS) that acts as a permeability barrier to large molecules. However, low-molecular-weight phenolic compounds present in high concentrations in the RP cultivar might penetrate porin channels or interact with the lipid bilayer, which could result in cytoplasmic leakage and cell death [27]. The linkage between chemistry and activity is further evidenced by the inverse relationship between sugar content and microbial growth in certain cultivars, suggesting that secondary metabolites, rather than primary nutrients, are the active agents. To establish the precise quantitative potency of these extracts beyond visual inhibition zones, the MIC value was evaluated using the inverted values mapped from the dataset. This broad spectrum of efficacy reveals that even at exceptionally low active concentrations, specific flower fractions retain the ability to reduce Gram-negative growth. While some literature reports suggest that Gram-positive bacteria are generally more susceptible due to the absence of an outer membrane [28], our results indicate that the RP cultivar’s specific enrichment in compounds like quercitrin and isoquercitrin provides a specialized chemical defense that appears particularly effective against the robust membrane of Gram-negative organisms under these specific assay conditions.

Safety considerations and industrial limitations of N. oleander

Nerium oleander is a highly toxic plant due to the presence of cardiac glycosides such as oleandrin and neriin [29]. These compounds inhibit the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump, causing severe cardiovascular toxicity similar to digitoxin [1]. All parts of the plant are toxic to humans and animals [6]. For example, leaf extracts have also been linked to lung tissue damage, including mononuclear infiltration [29]. Given this extreme toxicity, any real-world industrial use of N. oleander biomass presents significant safety limitations. For safety considerations, extraction processes would have to incorporate highly rigorous separation or detoxification steps to prevent toxic glycosides from co-extracting into end products. Thus the utilization of crude or unpurified N. oleander extracts is entirely unsafe and non-feasible for consumer-facing industries such as food, standard cosmetics, oral care, or open-environment agricultural applications where food crops could be contaminated. Conversely, potentially safe and permissible applications are strictly restricted to specialized, non-ingestible, closed-circuit industrial or clinical frameworks. Also, the impotent of the redox metabolites [30], extraction processes must incorporate effective separation or detoxification steps to prevent toxic glycosides from co-extracting into end products. Furthermore, large-scale processing poses substantial occupational hazards, requiring strict safety measures, including appropriate personal protective equipment to avoid dermal contact with plant sap and inhalation of particulates during handling. Consequently, the viable commercial application of N. oleander is unambiguously restricted to non-ingestible uses such as strictly targeted, industrial botanical biocides or the isolated extraction and purification of therapeutic compounds manufactured under heavily monitored pharmaceutical.

From a feasibility standpoint, the commercial utilization of crude N. oleander extracts requires a highly cautious evaluation. A major technical challenge is the co-extraction of toxic glycosides alongside [29] beneficial polyphenols, as many share similar solubility profiles in polar solvents like ethanol. Consequently, any potential application would necessitate advanced purification or selective detoxification steps to ensure that end products are free of cardiotoxic contaminants. Furthermore, large-scale biomass processing poses significant occupational hazards, requiring stringent safety protocols and specialized personal protective equipment to prevent dermal absorption or inhalation of particulates. Given these substantial safety risks and the likely stringent regulatory scrutiny from international food and safety agencies such as the EFSA, the feasibility of using crude N. oleander extracts in food or standard cosmetic formulations is highly limited and remains strictly hypothetical.

Conclusion

This study confirms that Nerium oleander cultivars possess a high-value phytochemical profile suitable for industrial and agricultural valorization. The metabolic superiority of flower extracts, particularly from the RP, MB, PS, and VRS cultivars, is strongly correlated with elevated levels of quercetin, catechin, and caffeic acid. The observed diversity in soluble sugars, amino acids, and bioactive secondary metabolites reflects a significant genetic variance that can be exploited for the development of modern bio-products. These findings transition N. oleander from an ornamental shrub to a promising source for botanical herbicides, nematicides, and industrial antioxidants, offering a sustainable, plant-derived alternative for the pharmaceutical and agricultural sectors.
