General and transgender-specific quality of life in trans persons assigned female at birth: validation of the Essen Transgender Quality of Life-Inventory
Authors: Sophia Rücker, Jochen Heß, Jil Beckord, Mirko Fasel, Alexander Bäuerle, Johannes Fuss, Julia Bohr, Cordula Lang, Jens Wallmichrath, Andreas Wolter, Sonia Maria Fertsch, Stefan Langer, Madeleine Fink, Martin Teufel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTransgenderism has always been part of society, with some individuals experiencing gender dysphoria (GD). When present, it is associated with diminished quality of life (QoL), along with minority stress, compared to the cisgender population.AimThe primary objective of this study was the validation of the Essen Transgender Quality of Life-Inventory (ETLI), originally designed for assigned male at birth (AMAB) transgender individuals, in the German population on those assigned female at birth (AFAB).MethodsA multi-center survey study with 139 participants was conducted. The assessment encompassed sociodemographic and medical data, and the following instruments: ETLI (transgender-specific QoL), F-SozU (social support), PHQ-8 (depression), GAD-7 (anxiety), and the Belief in a Just World questionnaire. Visual analogue scales (VAS) assessed physical and mental health, general QoL, and social environment quality. Following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines, descriptive statistics, exploratory factor analysis (EFA), confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), reliability analysis, and intercorrelation analysis of ETLI subscales were performed, along with bivariate correlations with related instruments proximal and distal to the questionnaire to assess construct and criterion validity.OutcomesOutcomes included factor structure, reliability, as well as construct and criterion validity of the ETLI.ResultsThe initial CFA, based on previous work, indicated a suboptimal factor structure, leading to the conduction of an EFA, which suggested a revised factor model. A subsequent CFA confirmed a satisfactory model fit for the proposed structure. All ETLI scales reached high reliability coefficients. Intercorrelation analysis revealed moderate to high associations among subscales. Convergent validity was confirmed by significant correlations of ETLI subscales with the PHQ-8, GAD-7, and F-SozU questionnaires. Discriminant validity was partially supported through the Belief in a Just World questionnaire. Criterion validity was affirmed using the VAS regarding general QoL, mental and physical health, and social environment quality.Clinical ImplicationsDue to its confirmed validity and reliability as well as the lack of viable alternatives, the ETLI provides healthcare professionals with a robust tool to assess QoL issues specific to this population, facilitating more targeted and effective interventions to improve QoL.Strengths and LimitationsStrengths are the inclusion of a heterogeneous age range and the use of advanced statistical analyses. However, recruitment from specific clinics and a limited sample size restrict the generalizability of our study.ConclusionThe study substantiates the ETLI’s construct and criterion validity among the AFAB demographic, demonstrating satisfactory reliability and confirming the revised factor structure through a subsequent CFA.Keywords: transgender, assigned female at birth, assigned male at birth, gender dysphoria, quality of life, validation

Introduction

Transgender individuals and gender-diverse populations have always been an integral part of society, with increasing recognition of the complex challenges they continue to face, including systemic discrimination, limited social acceptance, inadequate access to gender-affirming healthcare, and the persistent need for inclusive legal recognition. The modernized ICD-11 guidelines represent a significant change by emphasizing that transgender identities are not mental disorders, aiming to reduce stigma by depathologizing gender diverse identities and replacing obsolete categories such as “transsexualism” with the term “gender incongruence of adolescence and adulthood”.1 Gender incongruence refers to a misalignment between a person's gender identity and the one assigned at birth,2,3 while gender dysphoria (GD) describes the additional distress resulting from this incongruence.4,5 Although GD is associated with significant distress, it is not classified as a mental disorder.6 It is important to acknowledge that not all individuals with gender incongruence experience GD.7 In the present work, the term gender dysphoria is used exclusively within a clinical and diagnostic framework, as defined by current medical classification systems. The multifactorial etiology includes genetics, hormones, neuroanatomy, and social influences, with manifestation varying across age groups.3 Apprehending the demographics of the transgender community is crucial, as individuals within it vary in gonadal, hormonal, or genital sex characteristics, gender identity, and sexual orientation. Research indicates the complexity in accurately quantifying the transgender population, due to disclosure rates and healthcare-seeking behaviors. Prevalence studies estimate approximately 4.6 transgender individuals per 100 000 citizens in Europe, with a ratio of 2.6 assigned male at birth (AMAB) to 1 assigned female at birth (AFAB) transgender individuals. However, underestimation is likely, due to reliance on data from specialized clinics.8 A recent population-based study from Germany reported a total of 19 632 genital gender-affirming surgeries (GASs) performed between 2006 and 2022, demonstrating an exponential increase in both feminizing and masculinizing procedures over time. Particularly sharp rises were observed after 2018, with a predominance of masculinizing surgeries throughout the study period, and a noticeable trend toward younger individuals opting for surgery.9 Recent diagnostic and therapeutic approaches have increasingly focused on personalized treatment addressing both GD manifestations and broader identity impacts.10 For multidimensional therapy, diagnosis of GD requires persistent dysphoria for at least 6 months, confirmed by an expert.6

A German S3 guideline aims to refine and individualize transgender healthcare by addressing psychosocial factors; diagnostic processes; and treatment of physical, psychological, and social issues while acknowledging the importance of body-modifying treatments in enhancing gender congruence.11

Therapeutic interventions, including GASs, hormonal therapy, and psychotherapy, are internationally part of a multidimensional approach and aim to enhance the quality of life (QoL) for transgender individuals, a multidimensional construct heavily reliant on subjective perceptions. Quality of life encompasses various aspects such as physical functioning, emotional well-being, occupational engagement, and social interactions.12 According to the World Health Organization (WHO), QoL is defined as “individuals' perceptions of their position in life in the context of the culture and value systems in which they live and in relation to their goals, expectations, standards and concerns.”13 However, quantifying QoL objectively poses challenges due to the complexity of the construct and the potential divergences between individuals' self-perceptions and external assessments. These discrepancies complicate the ability to obtain an objective and comprehensive evaluation of a person’s QoL.12 Studies have shown variations in QoL among transgender individuals, with most reporting lower QoL compared to the general population.14,15 Numerous factors such as initial circumstances, transition process, and therapy outcomes impact mental and physical health.16,17 A systematic review by Nobili, Glazebrook, and Arcelus18 confirmed that, regardless of the investigated domain, transgender participants described poorer QoL.18 Contemporary literature underscores the beneficial effects of hormone treatment and GAS on mental health and overall QoL; however, controlled trials are lacking.19-21 Furthermore, social and family support seem to exert a predominantly positive influence on QoL of trans individuals,22-24 whereas factors such as anxiety and depression seem to have a detrimental effect.25 Since there is a significant lack of validated instruments specifically designed to comprehensively assess QoL in transgender individuals, only a few targeted tools have been developed to address distinct aspects of this construct. The Transsexual Voice Questionnaire for Male-to-Female Transsexuals (TVQMtF)26 focuses on voice-related QoL in transfeminine individuals. However, its applicability is limited due to its exclusive focus on AMAB transgender individuals and the relatively limited evidence regarding psychometric properties. The more recently developed iTransQoL27 provides a broader, inclusive assessment of QoL in both AFAB and AMAB transgender individuals, with a particular emphasis on experiences during gender-affirming hormone therapy. Nevertheless, this instrument is still in the early stages of validation, and potential limitations remain concerning its ability to capture the full range of QoL dimensions, particularly those unrelated to hormone therapy. As both questionnaires have not been validated within the German population, these limitations highlight the need for an instrument specifically developed and validated for the German-speaking context. Among these, the German “Essener Transgender Lebensqualität-Inventar” (ETLI)28 represents a recent contribution to the field, aiming to address this gap.

Developed by Tagay et al.,28 the ETLI was originally validated for AMAB transgender individuals but has not yet been evaluated for its applicability to the AFAB transgender demographic. The exploratory factor analysis of the ETLI, conducted in a previous study with AMAB participants by Tagay et al.,28 revealed 4 main factors: psychological, physical, social QoL, and openness to life, captured across 30 items with adequate item discrimination. These factors demonstrated good reliability and construct validity, as evidenced by correlations with generic QoL, resources, and negative associations with psychological distress. These results reveal promising measurement properties of the ETLI as a measure of QoL within the AMAB transgender population,28 indicating its potential applicability to the AFAB transgender group as well.

To validate the ETLI for the AFAB transgender population, we administered several well-established questionnaires closely linked to key dimensions of QoL, including the Social Support Questionnaire (F-SozU),29 Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-8),30 and Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7).31,32 These instruments were selected based on their relevance to critical QoL domains, such as mental health and social integration, which are widely recognized as essential for understanding the unique challenges faced by AFAB transgender individuals. Their use is well established in transgender research, highlighting their relevance in this context.33,34

Given the distinct psychosocial experiences and specific healthcare requirements of AMAB and AFAB transgender individuals, such as gender-affirming hormone therapy, gender-specific surgical procedures, and mental health support,7,35 with notable differences observed particularly during adolescence and in the context of social affirmation at school and work,36,37 separate validation is crucial to ensure accuracy and relevance. This validation is essential to ensure that the measure accurately reflects the nuanced experiences of both groups, whose challenges and stressors are similar yet not identical, while maintaining its applicability across different transgender populations. The study’s objectives were to assess the factor structure, reliability, and validity of the ETLI within the German AFAB transgender community to determine its applicability within this population. This study aims to address the necessity for a specialized tool for the AFAB transgender community to substantially enhance clinical evaluations and facilitate effective, targeted interventions in clinical settings.

Methods

The study follows the “Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology” (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies.38 The reporting language aligns with current recommendations for inclusive and respectful terminology regarding gender diversity, following the Sexual Orientation and Gender Diversity Research Manuscript Writing Guide by Veldhuis et al.,39 published in 2024. Ethical approval was granted by the ethical committee of the Medical Faculty of the University of Duisburg-Essen (22-10494-BO).

Design, setting, and study population

Transgender-specific QoL was assessed among German AFAB transgender participants in a multi-center study based on an anonymous online survey. Recruitment was executed via flyers distributed by medical professionals at 10 centers nationwide (LVR-University Hospital Essen; Department of Urology, University Hospital Essen; Evangelical Clinics Essen-Mitte; Department of Urology, University Medical Center Hamburg-Eppendorf; Dr. Lubos Clinics Munich; Urological Clinic Munich-Planegg; Evangelical Elisabeth Clinic Berlin; Sana Hospital Düsseldorf-Gerresheim; Agaplesion Markus Hospital Frankfurt am Main; Department of Plastical, Aesthetic, and Specialized Hand Surgery, University Hospital Leipzig). Participation was voluntary, without compensation or financial incentive, and had no influence on the medical treatment.

Eligibility criteria comprised AFAB transgender identity and age 18 or older. The participants’ history or intention of undergoing GAS was no prerequisite. Thus, AFAB transgender individuals in different stages of the gender-affirming process were targeted. Additional requirements included proficiency in German language, internet access, and providing digital informed consent at outset. Data were collected between December 2022 and January 2024 via the Unipark platform.40

Out of N = 195 initially engaged, 81.5 % (N = 159) successfully completed the survey. Subsequently, 8 participants were excluded from the data analysis due to inadequate prerequisites regarding age or biological gender. Additionally, 12 participants were excluded for incomplete responses to required questions. Consequently, N = 139 participants were ultimately included in the final data analysis, after the complete data sets of 20 participants were excluded. At this stage, no missing data remained, and only complete data sets were utilized for the analysis.

Essen Transgender Quality of Life-Inventory28

The ETLI was developed to quantify transgender-specific QoL, incorporating 30 items covering physical and psychological dimensions of QoL, with particular attention to fear, depression, and psychological resilience as well as partnership, sexuality, and social aspects. Based on the validation by Tagay et al.,28 the ETLI was subdivided into 4 distinct subscales: mental QoL (14 items), physical QoL (6 items), social QoL (5 items), and QoL through openness (5 items). Cut-off points distinguishing between moderate to high QoL and low QoL were established in advance for each subscale, with a cut-off value of 2.11 for mental QoL, 1.13 for physical QoL, 1.65 for social QoL, 1.71 for QoL through openness, and 1.85 for the total scale. Response options range from 0 = “never” to 3 = “always.”28

In the present study, an item analysis was conducted to assess item-scale correlations and individual item contributions to reliability, specifically regarding the change in Cronbach’s ⍺ when each respective item is omitted. The item analysis identified 1 item out of 30 with critical item statistics: Item 9 (“I openly express my gender identity”) has a low discrimination index, rit = .15, and would improve the reliability of the scale upon exclusion from Cronbach’s ⍺ = .914 to ⍺ = .915. However, the improvement is only marginal and the omission of said item would raise concerns regarding content relevance and data comparability, particularly considering its prior administration to AMAB transgender participants. Consequently, retaining item 9 in the ETLI questionnaire is deemed prudent. Detailed item statistics are provided in Table 1.

Social Support Questionnaire (F-SozU)29: The F-SozU gauges social support perception. In this study, the K-22 version was used, with 22 items being dissected into 5 subdomains, measuring emotional/practical support (8/4 items), social cohesion (6 items), confidant relationships (2 items), and satisfaction with social support (2 items). Responses range from 1 = “does not apply” to 5 = "applies exactly." Normative data exist for clinical and non-clinical groups, with extensive validation, internationally and within the German population, confirming its reliability and validity. In the present study, the internal consistencies were high with α = .950 for the total scale (ordinal α = .969), α = .914 for emotional support (ordinal α = .950), α = .821 for practical support (ordinal α = .898), α = .882 for social cohesion (ordinal α = .918), α = .789 for confidant relationships (ordinal α = .882), and α = .612 for satisfaction with social support (ordinal α = .673).

Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-D)30: The PHQ-D assesses mental disorders, psychosocial functioning, stressors, and significant life events and is a validated instrument both internationally and within the German population. This study used the specific module for depression screening (PHQ-8). The PHQ-8 consists of 8 items offering response options from 0 = “not at all” to 3 = “almost every day,” assessing depressive symptoms. Internal consistency in this study was α = .887, ordinal α = .920.

Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7)31,32: The GAD-7 is a well-established tool used to measure symptoms of anxiety and has been validated for use within the German population. It consists of 7 items, each rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 = “not at all” to 3 = “nearly every day.” The internal consistency was α = .891, ordinal α = .920.

Belief in a Just World 41 , 42 : This 20-item questionnaire, a validated tool for measuring individuals' belief in a just world across various scales, has been confirmed for use within a German sample.42 The Belief in a Just World questionnaire is divided into general (6 items) and domain-specific (8 items) belief scales, along with a justice centrality scale (6 items), inquiring about the perceived importance of justice in personal experiences. Response options range from 1 = "absolutely true" to 6 = “not true at all.” The internal consistency for the general belief subscale was α = .694 (ordinal α = .756), while for the domain-specific subscale, it was α = .831 (ordinal α = .881). In terms of justice centrality, the internal consistency was α = .700 (ordinal α = .7451).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using the Statistical Program for Social Sciences SPSS version 29.043 and the R version 4.2.2.44 Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, minimum, and maximum values, were computed for the individual scales of the ETLI. The four-factor model developed by Tagay et al.28 underwent validation within our sample through confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Despite the table presenting the factor loadings missing item 29 in the original paper, the plot of the factor model included 30 items, with 1 item added to the factor Physical QoL.28 Therefore, item 29 was included in this factor in the present CFA, carried out using the R package lavaan, developed by Rosseel.45 The maximum likelihood robust (MLR) estimator was employed to estimate factor loadings and model fit indices. Evaluation of model fit adhered to the criteria set forth by Hu and Bentler46: a Comparative Fit Index (CFI) and Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI) greater than .90, a Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) below .06, and a Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) less than .08 were deemed indicative of a favorable model fit. Reliability of the total ETLI and its 4 subscales was evaluated using the internal consistency measures Cronbach’s ⍺, McDonald’s ω, and ordinal ⍺. To verify convergent validity, bivariate Spearman correlations between the total scale, as well as the subscales of the ETLI and the total scales of the PHQ-8, GAD-7, and F-SozU, including its 5 subscales, were computed, with coefficients ρ interpreted as small (ρ = .10), moderate (ρ = .30), or large (ρ = .50) according to customary procedures.47 As QoL is a complex construct with numerous subscales,48 identifying a construct that does not correlate with it presents a difficulty. Therefore, the Belief in a Just World questionnaire was employed exploratively to assess discriminant validity, given the lack of existing literature explicitly examining the relationship between belief in a just world (BJW) and QoL. Correlations were assessed between the ETLI, its subscales, and the 3 subscales of the Belief in a Just World questionnaire. Intercorrelation analyses between subscales and the total scale of the ETLI were performed using Spearman. Criterion validity was appraised through bivariate correlations of the ETLI with the VAS regarding general QoL and physical and mental health, as well as the quality of the social environment.

Results

Sample characteristics

The sample consists of 139 AFAB transgender persons in total with an average age of 29.32 years (SD = 8.13; range 18-54 years). All respondents have had their gender identity disclosure before participation in our study and were surveyed on average 7.59 years (SD = 5.88; range 0.167-26.92) after. For further sociodemographic key data, see Table 2. Medical key data are shown in Table 3.

Attributes of the ETLI scales

Based on the validation by Tagay et al.,28 the ETLI was subdivided into 4 distinct subscales: mental QoL (14 items), physical QoL (6 items), social QoL (5 items), and QoL through openness (5 items). A total scale combining all 30 items was created. The respective descriptive statistics of the ETLI scales are elaborated in Table 4.

Confirmatory factor analysis

The KMO test indicated satisfactory overall sampling adequacy with a value of .856.49 Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity revealed a significant result, chi-square = 2045.583, P < .001.50 The CFA yielded the following fit indices: CFI = .758, TLI = .737, RMSEA = .088, and SRMR = .102, thereby revealing an insufficient fit. A reason for the insufficient fit could be that the items of the ETLI were handled as metric values, in accordance with the original paper. This is indicated by the rotation method varimax that was used and the Pearson correlations that were calculated.28 As the items only have 4 response levels, it could be more adequate to handle them as ordinal values. We explored this approach in an additional CFA.

Ordinal confirmatory factor analysis

For this CFA, the items of the ETLI were handled as ordinal values. The CFA was therefore calculated based on polychoric correlations. As a robust estimator appropriate for ordinal data, the weighted least square mean and variance adjusted (WLSMV) estimator was applied. The ordinal CFA now yielded the following improved scaled fit indices: CFI = .904, TLI = .895, RMSEA = .077, SRMR = .112. This indicates a slightly better but still not favorable fit.46 Therefore, an additional EFA and successive CFA were calculated.

Ordinal exploratory factor analysis

The EFA was calculated based on polychoric correlations and using the promax rotation method, which is suitable for ordinal data and allows the factors to be correlated. The parallel analysis suggested a factor number of 5, while the Velicer minimum average partial (MAP) criterion and Empirical Bayesian information criterion (BIC) achieved their minimum with 4 factors. The factor loadings of the 4-factor EFA and 5-factor EFA are displayed in Appendix A. The item allocations and loadings on the factors of the 4-factor model differed from the original model. If, as in the original paper,28 only factor loadings >.40 are considered, items 16, 22, 27, and 30 would have to be excluded due to low factor loadings according to the EFA. Additionally, items 6, 17, and 28 of originally the first factor mental QoL were now proposed to be allocated to the second factor, which reflects physical QoL, while it was vice versa for item 29. Item 11 had the highest loading on the factor mental QoL, while item 19 had its highest loading on QoL through openness. The cumulative variance explained by this model was 57%. The CFA based on the proposed model of the EFA revealed the following scaled fit: CFI = .912, TLI = .903, RMSEA = .080, SRMR = .109. As this fit was still deemed insufficient,46 additional CFAs with different item allocations were tested. It was tested whether it would be favorable to exclude said items with low factor loadings and whether items with cross-loadings would be better allocated to the other factor. That said, the best model fit could be achieved with items 11 and 19 allocated to the factor social QoL, item 16 allocated to the factor physical QoL, item 22 excluded, and items 27 and 30 allocated to the factor mental QoL. This model achieved the following scaled fit indices: CFI = .934, TLI = .928, RMSEA = .065, SRMR = .099. Finally, also a 5-factor model was tested that revealed a similar fit: CFI = .932, TLI = .925, RMSEA = .065, SRMR = .099. Both models yielded a sufficient fit with CFI and TLI > .90, RMSEA close to .06, and SRMR < .10. To maintain consistency with the original model, it is recommended to retain the final 4-factor model, which now comprises 29 items. Reliability analysis

The reliability analysis indicated a high level of reliability for the total scale of the ETLI, with Cronbach’s ⍺ = .914 and McDonald’s ω = .773. After handling the items of the ETLI as ordinal values, ordinal ⍺ was additionally calculated and revealed high reliability, as well, ordinal ⍺ = .937. Among the subscales, mental QoL showed the highest reliability (⍺ = .879 and ω = .893, ordinal ⍺ = .915), followed by social QoL (⍺ = .831 and ω = .874, ordinal ⍺ = .886), physical QoL (⍺ = .838 and ω = .856, ordinal ⍺ = .886), and QoL through openness (⍺ = .745 and ω = .728, ordinal ⍺ = .802).

Validity analyses

Intercorrelation analyses revealed statistically significant moderate to high positive correlations among the subscales (Table 5). The strongest positive correlation was found between mental QoL and physical QOL, whereas the correlation between social QoL and QoL through openness exhibited the lowest correlation. All subscales highly correlated with the overall ETLI scale.

To assess construct validity, the total scale and the subscales of the ETLI were correlated with construct-related features (convergent validity) and with construct-distant features (discriminant validity).

For convergent validity, the total scales of the questionnaires PHQ-8, GAD-7, and F-SozU, as well as the 5 subscales of the latter, were used. Particularly, high positive correlations were found between the total scale of the ETLI, the subscale mental QoL and the subscale social QoL and the F-SozU, as well as its subscales. The subscales physical QoL and QoL through openness correlated significantly positive with the F-SozU and its subscales, as well, but less strong. Conversely, a strong negative correlation emerged between the ETLI total scale and the PHQ-8, with similar high negative correlations between the subscales of the ETLI and the PHQ-8. Correlations between the GAD-7 and the ETLI total scale and subscales exhibited similarly strong negative associations.

The 3 subscales “General Belief in a Just World”, “Domain-specific Belief in a Just World,” and “Justice Centrality” of the Belief in a Just World questionnaire were employed to verify discriminant validity. Results of the validity analyses show moderate to strong negative correlations with the total ETLI scale and the mental QoL subscale. The General Belief in a Just World subscale also correlated significantly with the physical and social QoL subscales. The domain-specific and Justice Centrality subscales showed a low but significant correlation with the social QoL subscale but no correlations with the physical QoL subscale. There were no correlations with the QoL through openness subscale.

To evaluate criterion validity, the correlations between the total scale and subscales of the ETLI and the VAS pertaining to general QoL, physical and mental health, and the quality of the social environment were examined. The VAS exhibited strong significant positive correlations with the total scale of the ETLI, as well as with the mental, physical, and social QoL subscales. The QoL through openness subscale correlated low but significantly with the QoL VAS and moderately with the mental health and social environment VAS. There was no correlation between the QoL through openness subscale and the physical health VAS. All correlation results are displayed in Table 5.

Discussion

The study aimed to critically assess factor structure, validity, and reliability of the previously developed ETLI questionnaire for the AFAB transgender community in Germany, originally designed for AMAB transgender individuals.28 To the best of our knowledge, our study serves as a primary evaluation to pursue these objectives in this context, achieved through CFA, reliability analysis, intercorrelation analysis of ETLI subscales, and validation analyses.

Unexpectedly, the CFA did not confirm satisfactory factor structure, as the measured values did not align with the reference values according to Hu and Bentler.46 This discrepancy may arise from item retention decisions. Tagay et al.28 identified that excluding items 1, 2, and 26 could marginally enhance the questionnaire’s reliability. However, they chose to retain said items based on conceptual consideration, given the minimal difference in reliability.28 In line with this approach, we also opted to retain said items for the original CFA to ensure consistency across study populations, which potentially affected our factor structure. However, research indicates that it is not atypical for models to exhibit poor factor structure alongside strong psychometric properties. The term "reliability paradox” refers to the phenomenon where models with suboptimal measurement quality often demonstrate better fit than those with excellent measurement attributes.51 Another possible explanation for the poor fit of the original model could be that the items were handled as metric values. Subsequently, CFA was based on Pearson correlations and the MLE estimator. To test this theory, an additional CFA was calculated where the items of the ETLI were handled as ordinal values and the robust estimator for ordinal data WLSMV was used. Indeed, this CFA executed a better fit although it was still not satisfactory.46 Consequently, additional EFA and CFA were calculated. As the additional EFA proposed to exclude and differently allocate some items, we tested different versions of the model with CFA. Despite scientific literature prioritizing maintaining items for theoretical integrity,51 item 22 was excluded as it notably enhanced model fit. Additionally, items 11 and 19 were finally allocated to the factor social QoL, item 16 to the factor physical QoL, and items 27 and 30 to the factor mental QoL. These proposed allocations revealed the best model fit. The final model showed satisfactory model fit in 3 of 4 fit indices. RMSEA was close to satisfactory. As the fit is best with this model, the new allocations and exclusion of item 22 is recommended, which leads to the revised model now comprising 29 items. However, future research should investigate the factor structure further. Especially, it should be thought about representing cross-loadings in the model as the allocations seem to be not as clear-cut. Additionally, it is recommended that future research should, upon testing a different factor structure, stay with the ordinal handling of the items as it significantly improved model fit. Finally, a higher sample size might reveal a better model fit.

Reliability analyses were based on the final model and revealed satisfactory to good internal consistencies for both the individual subscales and the overall scale. In fact, the overall scale demonstrated even higher reliability within our study cohort compared to the AMAB transgender community.28

Validity analyses, interpreted according to Cohen,47 affirmed the construct validity of the ETLI. Elevated intercorrelations among the subscales of the ETLI and in relation to the total scale support the questionnaire’s construct validity and reliability.

Additionally, significant correlations were found between selected construct-proximal measures (PHQ-8, GAD-7, and F-SozU) and the ETLI scales. Social support (F-SozU) demonstrated notably high positive correlations, while depression (PHQ-8) and anxiety (GAD-7) exhibited high negative correlations. These findings align with contemporary literature on the positive association between QoL in the transgender community and social support,23 as well as the negative association with depressiveness and anxiety.52,53 The strong positive correlation between the ETLI and the F-SozU underscores the importance of comprehensive social support and a stable, supportive social environment for QoL. Research has consistently demonstrated that a supportive social environment serves as a crucial protective factor against mental health challenges, and is positively correlated with enhanced QoL, particularly in underrepresented populations.54 This underlines the importance of integrating social support into assessments of well-being and QoL in transgender populations. Based on these results, convergent validity of the questionnaire can be confirmed.

In contrast, the findings regarding discriminant validity were less definitive. Minor correlations were observed with the scales of the Belief in a Just World questionnaire, refuting discriminant validity. As highlighted by Silva et al.,55 factors such as socioeconomic status, societal norms, and access to opportunities have been shown to influence both beliefs in a just world and well-being. Additionally, BJW may serve as a coping mechanism,56 particularly for social diversities, including transgender individuals, helping them to process perceived injustices based on their unique experiences. It can also function as a personal resource, providing psychological resilience and a sense of control in the face of adversity.57 However, the relationship between BJW and life satisfaction is complex and reciprocal, with BJW influencing life satisfaction and vice versa.58 While life satisfaction is a subjective evaluation of one’s life, QoL encompasses a broader, multidimensional framework that includes both objective and subjective well-being.59 The lack of a clear distinction between the conceptually and empirically separate constructs QoL and BJW suggests a more integrated relationship than initially hypothesized, with BJW potentially influencing both life satisfaction and broader aspects of QoL and thus undermining their discriminant validity.

Despite the literature suggesting a generally positive correlation between beliefs in a just world and psychological well-being, our findings diverge from this association. Specifically, in 2 out of 3 subscales examined, we observed low to moderate negative correlations between BJW and QoL indicators. This discrepancy might be explained by the fact that AFAB transgender individuals represent an underrepresented group, for whom BJW may carry different implications. BJW is often associated with conservative ideologies, as it tends to correlate with assimilative and nationalist beliefs.60 Individuals with strong BJW are more likely to accept social hierarchies and perceive societal structures as legitimate and fair.61 Hence, for AFAB transgender individuals, this association may not apply. As an underrepresented group, BJW may not foster acceptance of social hierarchies. Instead, it may heighten feelings of injustice, as their experiences of discrimination contradict the belief in a fair and just world. Thus, the typical positive relationship between BJW and QoL may not be relevant for AFAB transgender individuals. Their social reality, often marked by structural disadvantage, may lead to a more critical perception of societal structures. As a result, a low BJW may better reflect their awareness of injustice and higher resilience in coping with adversity. Expectedly, no correlations were observed between the subscale Justice Centrality and the subscales of the ETLI. As evidenced in Table 4, moderate to high QoL values were measured in our sample. This supports the hypothesis that transgender individuals with favorable life conditions, such as social support, access to healthcare, and the availability of a supportive network, as observed within our study cohort, may prioritize protective values like self-actualization or social connections. In such contexts, justice centrality may be less relevant to overall QoL. While the results indicate a potential relationship between BJW and QoL in transgender individuals, further empirical research is required to elucidate the correlation between these constructs. Nevertheless, the absence of a significant correlation with the QoL through openness subscale could suggest its potential as a construct-distant measure. Future research should further explore the discriminant validity of the ETLI using additional construct-distant questionnaires.

The instrument’s criterion validity is substantiated by high positive correlations of the total scale of the ETLI and the subscales mental, physical, and social QoL with the VAS regarding general QoL, mental health, physical health, and the quality of the social environment. These results are indicative of the ETLI successfully capturing QoL by assessing its various domains and reflecting the key components encompassed in its definition, such as individual perception, cultural context, and alignment with personal goals and concerns.13 Additionally, the subscale QoL through openness significantly correlated with the VAS regarding general QoL, mental health, and the quality of the social environment. It did not correlate with the VAS regarding physical QoL, which is in accordance with the original paper.28

The clinical utility of a testing procedure is determined by its economy, objectivity, reliability, and validity.62 With 30 items and a processing time of 5-10 minutes, the ETLI serves as an economical assessment tool. Tagay et al.28 emphasized the importance of identifying individuals with diminished QoL. Our review of the mean values of the individual ETLI scales requires careful interpretation, as the cutoffs established by Tagay et al.28 were specifically designed for AMAB transgender individuals. Notably, all scales, except for mental QoL, exceeded the respective cut-off points, suggesting that the measured QoL domains are less diminished than anticipated.

Strengths of the study were the inclusion of a relatively wide age range, allowing for a thorough comprehension of transgender-specific QoL across different life stages. A further significant strength of the study resides in its implementation of anonymity, facilitating candid and impartial responses from participants. The use of advanced statistical analyses, as well as established instruments and questionnaires, augments the validity and reliability of our conclusions; it also facilitates comparability with prior research endeavors. Transparent methodological procedures were employed, illuminating a detailed account of the validation process to enhance replicability and delineate potential sources of bias. However, limitations need to be considered when interpreting the present results. Recruitment from specific clinics and voluntary participation may introduce selection bias, limiting representation of the broader AFAB transgender population. It should be noted that the instruments utilized in this study were not specifically validated for use with transgender populations, which may limit their applicability to this group. Furthermore, correlation results suggested that the construct selected a priori for assessing the ETLI's discriminant validity was suboptimal, highlighting the need for future research to employ a broader range of construct-distant questionnaires for a more comprehensive validation. We would also like to clarify that AFAB transgender individuals were neither involved in the study design nor in the reviewing process. While we recognize the importance of incorporating the perspectives of those with lived experience, this represents a further limitation of our study that should be addressed in future research to enhance inclusivity. Response bias is possible despite anonymity, as participants may be inclined to provide responses that are consistent with societal expectations, potentially distorting the accuracy of the questionnaire’s psychometric properties. Since the ETLI underwent validation as part of a cross-sectional study, it is important to consider that the results may be subject to bias due to factors such as inaccurate recall or the influence of the current state on retrospective evaluations.63 Future studies should apply a longitudinal study design to test the predictive validity of the ETLI. Further limitation is the limited sample size, complicating the generalizability of our findings. It is to be expected that a larger sample size could yield more comprehensive insights into the factor structure of the ETLI.

In conclusion, our study confirmed the suitability of the ETLI’s application for assessing QoL in AFAB transgender individuals. With the proposed new factor structure exhibiting a satisfactory model fit and the good reliability and validity results, it has the potential to serve as a standardized measurement tool for QoL in transgender individuals. Prospectively, the ETLI could assist medical professionals in identifying transgender individuals experiencing diminished QoL, as well as in implementing tailored healthcare approaches to provide adequate support. The ETLI could also serve as a tool to assess QoL in clinical trials in this population.
