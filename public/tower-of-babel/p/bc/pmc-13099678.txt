UDP-Glucose—P2Y14 Receptor Signaling Enhances the Responsiveness of Hematopoietic Stem/Progenitor Cells to Chemoattractants by Facilitating Membrane Lipid Raft Formation and Nlrp3 Inflammasome Activation
Authors: Mateusz Adamiak, Kannathasan Thetchinamoorthy, Diana Wierzbicka, Michalina Kazek, Emilia Waraksa-Zasada, Agnieszka Lukomska, Katarzyna Brzezniakiewicz-Janus, Janina Ratajczak, Magdalena Kucia, Mariusz Z Ratajczak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurinergic signaling plays an important role in hematopoietic stem/progenitor cell (HSPCs) trafficking, and the role of extracellular alarmin adenosine triphosphate (ATP), a major purinergic signaling mediator, is well established. Another alarmin, uridine diphosphate glucose (UDP-glucose), has also been reported to promote HSPCs trafficking after binding to the P2Y14 purinergic receptor. The molecular basis for this phenomenon, however, remains unclear, especially because UDP-glucose alone does not chemoattract HSPCs. To address this question, we hypothesize that UDP-glucose's role in stem cell trafficking may involve promoting the formation of membrane lipid rafts (MLRs) and enhancing intracellular activation of the Nlrp3 inflammasome, which we have previously identified as essential regulators of HSPCs migration. We found that UDP-glucose promotes HSPCs mobilization not only in response to the pro-mobilizing agent G-CSF but also in response to AMD3100. Furthermore, for the first time, we demonstrate that UDP-glucose also promotes homing and engraftment of HSPCs. We also evaluated the biological effects of a newly synthesized UDP-glucose analog, MRS2690. Our studies included HSPCs in vitro and in vivo trafficking assays, immunostaining and confocal analysis to assess MLRs formation on HSPCs, supported by metabolic analyses to identify metabolites involved in MLRs formation. Our data support our hypothesis that activation of the P2Y14 receptor on HSPCs by its ligands positively modulates cell trafficking, primarily by promoting lipid raft formation and Nlrp3 inflammasome activation. Introduction

UDP-glucose is primarily known as an intracellular precursor in carbohydrate metabolism. It is released from cells as a danger-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) alarmin in response to stress or injury, thereby triggering immune responses [1]. It mediates purinergic signaling by specifically activating the G protein-coupled purine receptor P2Y14 [1–3]. This activation initiates downstream signaling pathways that can lead to mobilization of hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs) into peripheral blood [4] and recruitment of eosinophils and neutrophils to sites of inflammation or injury [5]. Recently, it has been shown that UDP-glucose can enhance inflammatory responses in asthma [5]. MRS2690 is a synthetic analog of UDP-glucose reported to have greater potency [6].

Pharmacological mobilization of HSPCs after administration of mobilizing agents such as granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) or the CXCR4 receptor antagonist AMD3100 is currently an important source of cells for hematopoietic transplantation [7, 8]. It has been demonstrated that UDP-glucose, released into extracellular fluids as an alarmin in response to stress, mediates HSPC mobilization [1, 9]. As reported, HSPCs mobilized by G-CSF in the presence of UDP-glucose retain the capacity for long-term repopulation in lethally irradiated animals and the ability to differentiate into multi-lineage blood cells [4]. Interestingly, the addition of N-acetylcysteine (NAC), a prodrug for L-cysteine, a precursor to the biologic antioxidant glutathione, significantly reduced UDP-glucose–facilitated mobilization [4]. This negative effect of NAC supports the hypothesis that HSPC mobilization, as we postulated, is mediated by the induction of sterile inflammation in the BM microenvironment, involving activation of purinergic signaling and the complement cascade [9, 10]. UDP-glucose-P2Y14 signaling is also implicated in several other biological processes, including successful placentation of fetomaternal extravillous trophoblast cells [3] and vascular remodeling, Rho-mediated signaling, and chemotaxis in human neutrophils [4].

Our laboratory focuses on how purinergic signaling and the complement cascade regulate the trafficking of HSPCs, particularly their egress from the BM into PB during pharmacological mobilization and their homing or engraftment into the BM after transplantation [9, 10]. This process involves activation of the intracellular pattern recognition receptor Nlrp3 inflammasome [11] and the formation of membrane lipid rafts (MLRs) at the leading edge of migrating cells [11, 12].

We report that UDP-glucose facilitates HSPCs mobilization not only after G-CSF administration but also in response to AMD3100. Moreover, UDP-glucose enhances HSPC migration toward three major BM chemoattractants: stromal-derived factor-1 (SDF-1), sphingosine-1 phosphate (S1P), and extracellular adenosine triphosphate (eATP) [13]. These pro-migratory effects of UDP-glucose depend on Nlrp3 inflammasome activation and increased formation of MLRs enriched in cholesterol and sphingolipids. Proteomics data demonstrate that UDP-glucose mediates the upregulation of proteins involved in the pentose-phosphate cycle, which is crucial for lipidogenesis, and increases sphinganine levels, a fundamental building block and intermediate in the de novo biosynthesis of sphingolipids [14]. To support this further, RT-PCR data reveal that UDP-glucose upregulates squalene oxidase (SQLE), a limiting enzyme in cholesterol biosynthesis, and acid sphingomyelinase (ASMase), involved in sphingolipid metabolism.

Material and Methods

Animals

This study was performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Louisville School of Medicine and the Warsaw Medical University (Warsaw, Poland), and with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (Department of Health and Human Services, Publication No. NIH 86–23). We employed C57BL/6 mice for mobilization, homing, and engraftment experiments. Cells from these mice were also used in the assays described below.

Transwell Migration Assay

In the Transwell migration assay, aliquots of murine BM-derived MNCs (1 × 106/100 µl) were added to the upper chamber (5 µm pore filters) of a Costar Transwell 24-well plate (Corning Costar, New York, NY, USA) as described [15]. The lower chamber contained RPMI-1640 assay medium (650 μl) plus 0.5% BSA (control) or the medium supplemented with SDF-1 (100 ng/ml), ATP (10 μM), or S1P (0.1 μM). In other experiments, the lower chamber contained SDF-1 (5–25 ng/ml) alone or with UDP-glucose (1 μM) or MRS2690 (1 μM), or SDF-1 with UDP-glucose or MRS2690. The plate was incubated for 3 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Following incubation, aliquots of migrated cells from the lower chambers were resuspended in a methylcellulose-based medium (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA), supplemented with murine recombinant granulocyte/macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) (25 ng/ml) and recombinant murine interleukin 3 (IL-3) (10 ng/ml) (PeproTech, Rocky Hill, NJ, USA). After 7 days of culture (37 °C and 5% CO2), CFU-GM colonies were counted under an inverted microscope.

In Vivo Mobilization Studies

For mobilization studies, female C57BL/6 mice were injected subcutaneously (SC) with Granulocyte-Colony Stimulating Factor (G-CSF, 100 μg/kg daily; Amgen, Thousand Oaks, CA, USA) for 3 days or with a single dose of AMD3100 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) [5 mg/kg, intraperitoneally (IP)] [15]. 6 h after the last G-CSF injection and 1 h after AMD3100 injection, mice were bled from the retro-orbital plexus for white blood cell (WBC) count analysis, and peripheral blood (PB) was obtained from the vena cava (with a 25-gauge needle and a 1-ml syringe containing EDTA). Total nucleated cells (TNCs) were isolated by hypotonic lysis of red blood cells (RBCs) in 1 × BD Pharm Lyse buffer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) and further analyzed for SKL cells and CFU-GM clonogenic progenitors as described below. In some mobilization experiments, mice were exposed to UDP (200 mg/kg/day SC for 6 days) or MRS2690 (5 mg/kg/day SC for 3 days).

Peripheral Blood WBC Count

To obtain white cell counts, 50 μl of PB was collected from the retro-orbital plexus of mice into microvette EDTA-coated tubes (Sarstedt Inc., Newton, NC, USA) and analyzed on a HemaVet 950FS hematology analyzer (Drew Scientific Inc., Oxford, CT, USA) or an Exigo hematology analyzer (Boule Diagnostics, Spanga, Sweden) within 2 h of collection.

FACS Analysis

For staining Lin −/Sca-1 +/c-Kit + (SKL cells), Lin −/Sca-1 +/CD45 + (HSCs), Sca-1 +/Lin −/CD45 − (VSELs), Lin −/CD45 −/CD31 + (EPCs), and Lin −/CD45 −/CD31 −/CD90 + (MSCs), the following monoclonal antibodies were used: FITC–anti-CD117 (also known as c-Kit, clone 2B8; BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) and PE–Cy5–anti-mouse Ly-6 A/E (also known as Sca-1, clone D7; eBioscience, San Diego, CA, USA). All anti-mouse lineage marker antibodies, including anti-CD45R (also known as B220, clone RA3-6B2), anti-Ter-119 (clone TER-119), anti-CD11b (clone M1/70), anti-T cell receptor β (clone H57–597), anti-Gr-1 (clone RB6-8C5), anti-TCRγδ (clone GL3), and anti-CD45 (clone 30-F11), conjugated with PE; anti-CD31 (clone MEC 13.3), conjugated with APC; and anti-CD90.2 (clone 30-H12), conjugated with BV510, were purchased from BD Biosciences. Staining was performed in RPMI-1640 medium containing 2% FBS. All monoclonal antibodies were added at saturating concentrations, and cells were incubated for 30 min on ice, washed twice, and analyzed on an LSR II or FACS Verse cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) as described [11].

Sorting Strategy for SKLs from Murine Bone Marrow

Sca-1+ c-Kit+ Lin− cells (SKLs) were isolated from the BM of 6-week-old mice. Bone marrow (BM) was flushed from the tibial and femoral cavities. The population of TNCs was obtained after RBC lysis with 1 × BD Pharm Lyse buffer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). TNCs were resuspended in RPMI-1640 medium containing 2% FBS. Staining for SKLs was performed as described above. The cells were washed, resuspended in medium with 2% FBS, and sorted using a MoFlo Astrios EQ cell sorter (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) as reported previously [11, 12].

Clonogenic CFU-GM Assays

BMMNCs and/or peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMNCs) were resuspended in a methylcellulose-based medium (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). The medium for CFU-GM assays was supplemented with 25 ng/ml GM-CSF and 10 ng/ml mIL-3. For burst-forming unit-erythroid (BFU-E) assays, cell samples were suspended in methylcellulose supplemented with erythropoietin (EPO, 5 U/ml) and IL-3 (10 ng/ml; PeproTech, Rocky Hill, NJ, USA). Cells were incubated for 7 days (37 °C, 5% CO2) for CFU-GM and 14 days for BFU-E assays. As reported, CFU-GM and BFU-E colonies were scored using a simple inverted microscope (Olympus, Center Valley, PA, USA) [11, 12].

Evaluation of Hematopoietic Stem/Progenitor Cell Mobilization

In the assessment of cell mobilization, WBCs, SKL cells, and the number of CFU-GM colonies were determined according to the above protocols. For recalculation of SKLs and circulating colony-forming unit-granulocyte/macrophage (CFU-GM) numbers in PB, the following formulas were used: (number of white blood cells [WBCs] × number of CFU-GM colonies)/number of WBCs plated = number of CFU-GM per ml of PB, and (number of WBCs × number of SKL cells)/number of gated WBCs = number of SKL cells per μl of PB, as reported previously [11, 12].

Short-Term Homing Experiments

Experimental mice (WT or exposed to UDP-glucose (200 mg/kg/day SC for 6 days)) were irradiated with a lethal dose of γ-irradiation (10 Gy). After 24 h, the animals were transplanted (by tail-vein injection) with 5 × 106 BM cells from syngeneic animals, with or without a brief incubation with UDP-glucose (0.1 mM). All cells were labeled with PKH67 Green Fluorescent Cell Linker (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. 24 h after transplantation, BM cells from the femurs were isolated by Ficoll–Paque centrifugation and divided into two aliquots. One aliquot was analyzed on a flow cytometer for PKH67-positive cells, and the second aliquot was plated in methylcellulose cultures and stimulated to form CFU-GM colonies with GM-CSF (25 ng/ml) and IL-3 (10 ng/ml). After 7 days of incubation (37 °C and 5% CO2), colony numbers were scored under an inverted microscope as described [11].

Evaluation of Engraftment

On day 12 post-transplant, the femora of transplanted mice were flushed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). BM cells were purified via Ficoll-Paque, plated in methylcellulose cultures, and stimulated to form CFU-GM colonies with G-CSF (25 ng/ml) plus IL-3 (10 ng/ml). The colony number was scored under an inverted microscope after 7 days of incubation (37 °C and 5% CO2). Spleens were also removed and fixed in Fekete's solution for CFU-S assays. Colonies were counted on the surface of the spleen, as reported [11].

Recovery of Leukocytes and Platelets

BM cells, with or without a brief incubation with UDP-glucose (0.1 mM), were transplanted. Mice (WT or exposed to UDP-glucose (200 mg/kg/day SC for 6 days)) were bled from the retro-orbital plexus at various intervals to obtain WBC and platelet (PLT) counts. Briefly, 50 μL of PB was collected into EDTA-coated Microvette tubes (Sarstedt Inc., Newton, NC, USA) and analyzed within 2 h of collection on the Exigo hematology analyzer (Boule Diagnostics, Spanga, Sweden).

Membrane Lipid Rafts (MLRs) Detection

Murine tibias and femurs were flushed, and SKL cells were purified by FACS as described above. Isolated SKL cells were plated on fibronectin-coated plates overnight, incubated with SDF-1 (50 ng/ml) and UDP-glucose (2.5 μg/ml), washed, and fixed in 3.7% paraformaldehyde. BM SKL cells were exposed to 1 µM PGE2, washed, and fixed in 3.7% paraformaldehyde. The cholera toxin B subunit conjugated with FITC (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was used to detect the ganglioside GM1, and the rat monoclonal anti-CXCR4 IgG antibody (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) and Alexa Fluor 594 goat anti-rat IgG antibody (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) were used to detect CXCR4. Stained cells were examined, and images were acquired using a FluoView FV1000 laser-scanning confocal microscope (Olympus America Inc., Center Valley, PA, USA) as described previously [11, 12].

Glow Assay to Measure Nlrp3 Inflammasome Activation

To measure caspase-1 activity in cells, the Caspase-Glo® 1 Inflammasome Assay (Promega, USA) was used, and analyses were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Control samples and cells exposed to UDP-glucose or MRS2690 were collected, and 10 × 105 BM MNCs were plated in a 96-well plate. Caspase-Glo® 1 Reagent or Caspase-Glo® 1 YVAD-CHO Reagent was added (100 μl/well), and luminescence was measured using a GloMax 9301 Multi Detection System after 90 min [12]. For cell stimulation, UDP-glucose (100 µM) or MRS2690 (1 µM) was used.

FT-ICR Mass Spectrometry for Metabolite Analysis

The human HSPCs samples were lysed with 145 µL of ice-cold methanol (−20 °C, hyper LC–MS grade, Merck). The samples were incubated at −20 °C for 2 h, vortexing thoroughly every 20 min. After incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 20 min at 10 °C. A 150 µL aliquot of the supernatant was lyophilized using a concentrator (Eppendorf). The lyophilized material was reconstituted just before injection with 2% methanol, then centrifuged again at 14,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C.

Positive- and negative-ion ESI mass spectra were collected online using a 7 T SolariX FTICR mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics, Billerica, MA, USA). The ion source was operated at 4,500 V, with drying gas (N2) at 4 L/min. The end-plate offset was set to − 200 V. The collision voltage and time-of-flight were − 5 V and 1.2 ms, respectively. The mass spectra were acquired in CASI mode (700–1,000 m/z window) at approximately 0.3 Hz, with mass accuracy better than 3 ppm. Extracted ion chromatograms with a spectral width of 0.01 Da were used for integration. Metabolomics analyses were performed on a mass spectrometry system calibrated before sample acquisition using sodium trifluoroacetate (NaTFA) in both positive and negative ionization modes to ensure accurate mass detection. Quality control (QC) procedures were implemented during data processing to ensure instrument performance and data integrity. The resulting mass spectra (64 accumulated scans per spectrum) were analyzed using the T-Rex 2D algorithm for individual MRMS spectra. Metabolite annotation was performed by matching against the HMDB and Lipid Map databases, and false-positive peaks were removed to enhance data accuracy using MetaboScape 5.0 software (Bruker). Data were exported from MetaboScape 5.0 for further pathway enrichment and statistical analysis using the MetaAnalyste 6.0 online platform.

Real-Time RT-PCR

Bone marrow lineage-negative (Lin⁻) cells (150,000) were isolated from wild-type (WT) mice. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Micro Kit with DNase I treatment (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription was performed with the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed with iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad) and gene-specific primers for acid sphingomyelinase (ASMase: sense, 5′-CTGGTTCTGGCTCTGTTTGA-3′; antisense, 5′-GCGCTATGGCACTGAATTTG-3′), squalene oxidase (SQLE: sense, 5′-TATTGGCTCAGGCCTTGTATG-3′; antisense, 5′-CACACACTCTCCACCAAGTTTA-3′), and 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR: sense, 5′-CAGAGAAGACAGTGCTCGTTAG-3′; antisense, 5′-GCTCCATCATTGGCTCTGTAA-3′) on a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad). The PCR conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing at 60 °C for 30 s. A melting curve analysis was performed to confirm primer specificity and exclude genomic DNA contamination. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method, normalized to β-actin (ACTB) as the endogenous control and relative to a calibrator sample.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). All data are presented as mean ± SD. Multiple unpaired t-tests were used for statistical analysis. In all calculations, p ≤ 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

UDP-Glucose and MRS2690 do not Chemoattract Murine HSPCs but Prime or Enhance their Responsiveness to an SDF-1 Gradient

First, we tested the potential chemotactic effects of UDP-glucose and a more potent P2Y14 receptor agonist, MRS2690, on CFU-GM cell migration in a Transwell chemotaxis assay. We confirmed that both compounds alone did not show chemotactic activity against HSPCs [4]; however, they enhanced or primed chemotaxis of clonogenic progenitors toward a low dose of stromal-derived (SDF-1) gradient (Fig. 1). A similar priming effect was observed with low doses of extracellular ATP (eATP) and sphingosine-1 phosphate (S1P).

The chemotactic responsiveness of cells was assessed in a chemotaxis assay against an SDF-1 gradient alone or after priming with UDP-glucose or MRS2690 by evaluating the number of clonogenic CFU-GM progenitors migrating into the lower Transwell chambers. (*p ≤ 0.05; **p ≤ 0.005)

Next, we tested UDP-glucose and UDP as promoting compounds in G-CSF- and AMD3100-induced HSPC mobilization. UDP-glucose enhanced egress of BM cells when combined with G-CSF (Fig. 2A) and AMD3100 (Fig. 2B). Figure 2C shows that, in contrast to UDP-glucose, UDP does not enhance HSPC mobilization.

Effect of UDP-Glucose and its Analog MRS2690 on Mobilization of BM Cells

Next, we compared the side-by-side effects of UDP-glucose and MRS2690, alone and in combination with G-CSF and AMD3100, on BM cell mobilization (Fig. 3). We observed that UDP-glucose and MRS2690 alone mobilized HSPCs. Moreover, when UDP-glucose or MRS2690 was added to G-CSF or AMD3100, we observed an enhanced effect on mobilization efficacy (Fig. 3). This could be explained by the fact that exposure of mice to G-CSF or AMD3100 increases the concentration of chemoattractants in peripheral blood [10, 13] and, thus, both UDP-glucose and MRS2690 enhance migration of HSPCs toward a gradient of these chemoattractants. A similar positive effect of UDP-glucose and MRS2690 priming was observed on mobilization of other BM-residing stem cells, including very small embryonic-like stem cells (VSELs), endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs), and mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) [16] (Supplementary Fig. 1).

UDP-Glucose Enhances Homing and Engraftment of HSPCs after Transplantation into Mice

Next, we tested the effect of UDP-glucose on the ability of BMMNCs, exposed before transplantation, to home and engraft in the murine BM. Compared with control cells not exposed to these priming agents, the number of fluorochrome PKH67-labeled donor cells primed with UDP-glucose was increased by ~ 50% in the BM of transplanted animals 24 h after BMMNC injection. At the same time, the number of donor-derived CFU-GM colonies in the BM after injection of BMMNCs exposed before transplantation to UDP-glucose was increased by more than 15% relative to control non-primed BMMNCs (Fig. 4 A – upper panel left). Moreover, in short-term engraftment experiments, we observed that 12 days after transplantation of UDP-glucose-exposed cells, the numbers of donor-derived CFU-GM progenitors in the BM were increased by ~ 15%, and CFU-S progenitors in the spleen were increased by ~ 15% (Fig. 4 A – upper panel right). Consistent with these results, we observed that mice transplanted with UDP-glucose-exposed cells displayed faster recovery of leukocytes and platelets (Fig. 4 A – lower panel).

We subsequently observed similar results when UDP-glucose was administered after irradiation conditioning for transplantation into recipient mice. Accordingly, the number of fluorochrome-labeled donor-derived cells in the primed BM increased by ~ 30%, and UDP-glucose CFU-GM progenitors by ~ 25% (Fig. 4B, upper panel, left). UDP-Glucose Promotes MLR Formation and Activates the Nlrp3 Inflammasome

We previously reported that HSPC migration in response to BM chemoattractants requires the incorporation of homing receptors, such as CXCR4, into cell membrane lipid rafts [12]. Accordingly, certain innate immune mediators, including cationic antimicrobial peptides (e.g., LL-37, C3a, or β2-defensin) released from the BM during myeloablative conditioning for transplantation, enhance the incorporation of the CXCR4 receptor into MLRs, and this incorporation is required for optimal migration of HSPCs in response to an SDF-1 homing gradient in the BM [11]. To address the potential effect of MLR formation on the observed phenomena, we evaluated MLRs formation in normal syngeneic SKLs and in cells exposed to UDP-glucose. Pathway Enrichment Analysis in Human purified HSPCs Stimulated with SDF-1, UDP-Glucose, or SDF-1 + UDP-Glucose

To address this data at the molecular level, we performed metabolomics studies and, after analyzing SDF-1, UDP-glucose, or SDF-1 + UDP-glucose-induced stimulation, we identified several significant changes in MLRs formation-related metabolites [17–20]. Therefore, we performed further enrichment analyses to identify and interpret coordinated alterations in the enriched pathway (Fig. 6A). For comparison of differentially abundant metabolites in SDF-1, UDP-glucose, or SDF-1 + UDP-glucose stimulation vs. controls, we used the KEGG database with MetaboAnalyst 6.0, which performs metabolite set enrichment analysis (MSEA) and contains human metabolite sets as well as chemical class metabolite sets. The analysis is based on 15 libraries containing ~ 13,000 biologically meaningful metabolite sets collected primarily from human studies, including > 1500 chemical classes.

KEGG enrichment analyses (Fig. 6B) revealed significant pathways in SDF-1, UDP-glucose, or SDF-1 + UDP-glucose samples compared with non-stimulated cells, including galactose, caffeine, D-amino acid, arachidonic acid, drug-cytochrome P450 metabolism, purine and pyrimidine metabolism, the pentose phosphate pathway, and tyrosine metabolism. We also identified significant changes in steroid hormone biosynthesis and valine, leucine, and isoleucine biosynthesis. Notably, pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant changes in the pentose phosphate pathway in cells stimulated with SDF-1 together with UDP-glucose. The detected changes in the “pentose phosphate pathway” induced by UDP-glucose and the SDF-1 + UDP-glucose stimulation group were matched by a significant increase in several metabolites related to the synthesis of membrane lipid raft components and the level of sphinganine (C20). Sphinganine, also known as dihydrosphingosine, is a saturated 18-carbon amino alcohol that serves as a fundamental building block and intermediate in the de novo biosynthesis of sphingolipids [14], which are crucial components of MLRs [11].

Moreover, we also observed by RT-PCR (Supplementary Fig. 2) upregulation of squalene oxidase (SQLE), a limiting enzyme in cholesterol biosynthesis, and acid sphingomyelinase (ASMase), involved in sphingolipid metabolism.

Discussion

The key observation of this paper is that the UDP-glucose-P2Y14 receptor axis potentiates the mobilization and homing of HSPCs by promoting the synthesis of MLR components and their assembly on the cell surface. This facilitates HSPC responsiveness to chemoattractant gradients (Fig. 7). We previously demonstrated the importance of MLRs in HSPC trafficking [11, 12]. This process also involves activation of the intracellular pattern recognition receptor Nlrp3 inflammasome, which, as shown in previous work, is crucial for optimal HSPC trafficking during pharmacological mobilization and after homing and engraftment into the BM [11]. HSPCs are retained in their BM niches through interactions, such as those between stromal-derived factor-1 (SDF-1) expressed in hematopoietic niches and the CXCR4 receptor present on the surface of these cells.

Effect of P2Y14 activation by UDP-glucose (UDP-G) on HSPC migration. P2Y14 signaling stimulates the pentose phosphate pathway, which provides lipid components for membrane lipid rafts. In response to chemotactic gradients, cells more effectively activate the Nlrp3 inflammasome, which promotes their chemotactic responsiveness

UDP-glucose, one of the alarmins released from stressed cells, has been reported to facilitate mobilization of HSPCs from the BM into PB [4]. As a specific natural agonist of the P2Y14 receptor, UDP-glucose also exhibits pro-mobilizing activity and, when combined with G-CSF, mobilizes cells that preferentially support long-term repopulation [4]. In our hands, UDP-glucose also enhanced AMD3100-induced mobilization, a small-molecule CXCR4 receptor blocker. In parallel, we were testing the optimal dose of the synthetic small-molecule P2Y14 receptor agonist MRS 2690, which, as postulated, displays sevenfold higher potency than UDP-glucose [6]. However, to our surprise, we did not observe a significant advantage of this synthetic compound over natural UDP-glucose in mobilizing HSPCs. This somewhat unexpected result could be related to receptor desensitization kinetics, rapid clearance, or differences in vitro stability and requires further studies. This mobilization mechanism described in this work seems to be relevant for other types of BM-residing stem cells, including very small embryonic-like stem cells (VSELs), endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs), and mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) [16].

Studies have shown that the antioxidant N-acetylcysteine (NAC) significantly reduced UDP-glucose–induced mobilization, coinciding with decreased RANKL and osteoclastogenesis in the BM microenvironment [4]. NAC serves as a prodrug for L-cysteine, a precursor to the antioxidant glutathione. In this context, our previous work demonstrated that pharmacological mobilization of HSPCs from BM into PB is associated with sterile inflammation [9–11], and that antioxidants impair this process. Osteoclasts, specialized bone-resorbing cells, play a key role in HSPC egress from BM into PB by modifying the BM stem cell niche and releasing proteolytic enzymes (e.g., MMP-9, Cathepsin K) that degrade niche-anchoring factors for HSPCs, including SDF-1 [21]. Both sterile inflammation in the BM microenvironment and osteoclast activation are important in this process [22].

In our current work, we elucidate an important mechanism involved in HSPC trafficking in response to P2Y14 receptor activation by UDP-glucose or MRS 2690: MLR formation and activation of the intracellular Nlrp3 inflammasome. MLRs are dynamic, small, and highly ordered microdomains in the cell plasma membrane, enriched in cholesterol, sphingolipids, and specific proteins, including cell-surface receptors involved in HSPC retention in BM niches, such as CXCR4 and the α1β4 integrin receptor (VLA-4) [23]. They are tightly packed and slightly thicker than the surrounding membrane bilayer, helping to better organize signaling molecules to improve cellular communication [24]. Our previous work demonstrated that MLRs are enriched on HSPCs after exposure to mediators associated with inflammation, such as C3a, C5a, and eATP [23, 24]. This response to pro-inflammatory mediators depends on Nlrp3 inflammasome activity, as blocking this pattern recognition receptor with the specific inhibitor MCC950 negatively affected MLR formation. We report that the alarmin UDP-glucose has a similar effect to other pro-inflammatory mediators on MLR formation. In our work, we also tested the role of UDP and its specific receptor P2Y6 in mobilization of HSPCs, but we did not observe a significant effect.

Our data also demonstrate that UDP-glucose-P2Y14 signaling provides components to MLRs. The pentose pathway plays an important role, as shown in our metabolome data, in which components were increased in UDP-glucose and UDP-glucose + SDF-1-stimulated cells. This pathway generates, e.g., Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide Phosphate, reduced (NADPH), a crucial coenzyme and primary electron donor for anabolic processes such as fatty acid and cholesterol synthesis [25]. We also detected a strong upregulation in primed cells of sphinganine, which serves as a fundamental building block and intermediate in the de novo biosynthesis of sphingolipids [14]. These data are also supported by upregulation of mRNA for squalene oxidase (SQLE), a limiting enzyme in cholesterol biosynthesis, and acid sphingomyelinase (ASMase), involved in sphingolipid metabolism.

A novel observation is that UDP-glucose also positively affects the homing and engraftment of HSPCs. In our experiments, pre-transplantation exposure of BM cells to UDP-glucose enhanced their homing and engraftment. A similar effect was observed when the transplant recipient was exposed to UDP-glucose. These data further support a role for MLR formation and Nlrp3 inflammasome activation in the trafficking of HSPCs.

In conclusion, our data show that UDP-glucose is a potential new pro-mobilizing agent and, more importantly, a novel alarmin that facilitates the homing and engraftment of HSPCs after transplantation. We also provide a molecular explanation for this phenomenon, involving MLR formation and Nlrp3 inflammasome activation. Based on this data, experiments in larger animals could address whether UDP-glucose could play a role in optimizing hematopoietic transplantation.
