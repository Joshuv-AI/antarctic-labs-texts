Estimating the contribution to transmission of asymptomatic tuberculosis from population-based genomic epidemiology studies
Authors: Meng Li, Long Cai, Qian Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAsymptomatic tuberculosis (TB) poses a major challenge to global TB control. Quantifying its contribution to transmission remains a critical question. Although modelling studies have attempted to address this issue, empirical evidence from real-world settings is scarce. In this study, we estimated the contribution of asymptomatic TB to transmission in China using genomic epidemiology data from five population-based studies, encompassing 6,387 TB patients and 1,626 genomic-clustered patients. Transmission were classified as originating from either asymptomatic or symptomatic TB by comparing reported symptom onset dates with phybreak-inferred transmission timings. Across the study regions, 25.0%−51.3% of transmission was attributed to asymptomatic TB, indicating its substantial role in sustaining transmission. However, accurate estimation is hindered by the subjectivity of symptom reporting and reliance on passive case-finding. While this study likely underestimates the true contribution, it establishes a genomic epidemiology-based framework for future refinement. From a public health perspective, the priority should not be precise quantification but rather a shift toward active case-finding to detect both asymptomatic patients and symptomatic patients who have not sought care, thereby disrupting hidden transmission chains and advancing the End TB goals.KEYWORDS: Tuberculosis, asymptomatic, transmission contribution, genomic epidemiology, active case-finding

In recent years, as research on the natural history of tuberculosis (TB) has deepened, it has become evident that not all TB patients have symptoms. A recent systematic review analyzing data from TB prevalence surveys found that about half of TB patients did not have symptoms (subclinical TB) [1]. Under the current TB control strategy, which mainly relies on passive case-finding, these individuals are difficult to identify, and thus subclinical TB represents an important obstacle to achieving the End TB goals [2], particularly in resource-limited settings.

To better guide global TB control, the World Health Organization (WHO) convened a dedicated meeting in Geneva in October 2024, replacing the term “subclinical TB” with “asymptomatic TB” and providing a clear definition [3]. The meeting also identified priority research areas related to asymptomatic TB, with one critical scientific question being the contribution of asymptomatic TB to transmission [3]. Although bacterial loads in the sputum of asymptomatic TB patients are usually lower, the high prevalence and long duration of asymptomatic TB may make its contribution to transmission comparable, or even greater than that of symptomatic TB [4]. A modelling study based on the relative prevalence of asymptomatic TB and symptomatic TB across 14 countries in Asia and Africa estimated that approximately 68% (95% prediction interval: 27%−92%) of global transmission was from asymptomatic TB [5]. Another modelling study, using pre-antibiotic era TB survival data from five Asian countries, alongside current incidence, prevalence, and mortality data, suggested that smear-positive asymptomatic TB may contribute 35%−51% of future transmission, exceeding that of smear-positive symptomatic TB (27%−35%) [6]. However, the lack of real-world data to validate these models limits accurate assessment of the true contribution of asymptomatic TB to transmission.

Genomic epidemiology of TB provides an approach to evaluate the real world contribution of asymptomatic TB to transmission. We used prospective, population-based data from our previously published work to estimate the contribution of asymptomatic TB to transmission in China [7–11]. These study sites constitute readily available, high-quality, population-based cohorts for genomic epidemiological study of tuberculosis. They span a wide range of geographic, economic, and TB-burden settings across China, including representative locations from its southeastern, northwestern, northern, southern, and central regions (Figure 1A). Data on the presence or absence of pulmonary TB-related symptoms (cough, fever, weight loss, night sweats) and the date of symptoms onset were obtained from the National TB Management System. Symptomatic TB was defined as disease with reported symptoms consistent with pulmonary TB, and asymptomatic TB as disease without such symptoms.

Whole-genome sequencing data were analyzed with the SAM-TB online platform to identify genomic-clustered cases. In brief, raw sequence reads were trimmed with Sickle and aligned to the reference sequence using BWA-MEM. SAMtools and Varscan were then used to identify single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). Pairwise SNP distances were calculated based on the fixed SNPs with a frequency ≥75%. A genomic cluster was defined as strains differing by ≤12 SNPs, consistent with linkage through recent transmission [12]. Transmission direction and timing within each genomic cluster were inferred using R package phybreak (version 0.5.2) [13]. The prior parameter settings used in the model were as follows [14]: mean generation time (μg) = 257, mean sampling time (μs) = 189, shape parameter generation time (kg) = 4.66, shape parameter sampling time (ks) = 3.05, rate parameter generation time (βg) = 0.0181, rate parameter sampling time (βs) = 0.0161, and mutation rate = 1/4.4 × 106/year. To ensure that most estimated parameters reached an effective sample size greater than 200, we ran 20 independent Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) chains with a burn-in of 10,000 cycles and the sampling of the independent chains at each 50,000 cycles.

Transmission events from an asymptomatic index case were classified as from asymptomatic TB. For transmission events from symptomatic index cases, if the symptom onset date preceded the inferred transmission time, the transmission events were classified as from symptomatic TB; if the inferred transmission time preceded the symptom onset date, the transmission events were classified as from asymptomatic TB; if the symptom onset date was unavailable or fell within the 95% confidence interval of the inferred transmission time, the transmission events were classified as indeterminate.

We included data from five prospective population-based genomic epidemiology studies, encompassing a total of 6,387 pulmonary TB patients from urban and rural areas across different TB incidences and geographic locations in China (Figure 1A): Songjiang District of Shanghai, 2,273 patients between 2011 and 2020; Baoan District of Shenzhen, 1,696 patients between 2014 and 2017; Wusheng County in Sichuan Province, 1,289 patients between 2009 and 2020; Wuchang County in Heilongjiang Province, 699 patients between 2009 and 2020; and Linzhou County in Henan Province, 430 patients between 2018 and 2023.

Of all patients, 247 were identified through routine health physical examination, reported no symptoms at diagnosis, and were classified as asymptomatic TB. The remaining patients, all of whom reported at least one TB-related symptom, had sought medical care and were classified as symptomatic. Using a threshold of 12 SNPs, a total of 1,626 genomic-clustered cases were identified, and the clustering rates in the five regions were 26.5% (603/2,273), 12.2% (207/1,696), 26.9% (347/1,289), 39.6% (277/699), and 44.7% (192/430), respectively (Figure 1B). We then used phybreak to infer the transmission direction and timing among these genomic-clustered cases.

The relationship between the timing of transmission and the symptom onset is shown in Figure 1C. After excluding the initial transmission event leading to the source case for each cluster, a total of 390, 116, 219, 179 and 143 transmission events were captured in Songjiang, Baoan, Wusheng, Wuchang and Linzhou, respectively (Figure 1B). After further excluding the indeterminate transmission events (those with unavailable symptom onset dates or symptom onset dates within the 95% confidence interval of the inferred transmission time), 68.2% (266/390), 51.7% (60/116), 63.9% (140/219), 64.2% (115/179) and 53.1% (76/143) of the remaining transmission events were classified as determinate. In Songjiang, of these determinate transmission events (Figure 1D), 40.6% (108/266) occurred before the symptom onset of the index cases, indicating that the transmission was from asymptomatic TB, and 59.4% (158/266) occurred after symptom onset, indicating that the transmission was from symptomatic TB. Results varied across different regions. In Baoan, Wusheng and Wuchang, the proportion of transmission from asymptomatic TB was 33.3% (20/60), 25.0% (35/140), and 36.5% (42/115), respectively, while the proportion from symptomatic TB was 66.7% (40/60), 75.0% (105/140), and 63.5% (73/115). Notably, in Linzhou, transmission from asymptomatic TB (51.3%, 39/76) was similar to that from symptomatic TB (48.7%, 37/76). Due to the relatively high proportion of indeterminate transmission events across the five study sites, to verify the robustness of the above findings, we directly compared the median time of inferred transmission events with the time of symptom onset, without considering the confidence intervals of the inferred transmission timings. This analysis revealed no changes in the proportion of transmissions from asymptomatic TB and symptomatic TB (Supplementary table S1). These results suggest that asymptomatic TB plays an important role in transmission, and in some areas, its contribution to transmission is comparable to that of symptomatic TB.

Discussion

This study estimated the contribution of asymptomatic TB to transmission in China based on real-world genomic epidemiology data, and the results fall within the confidence intervals of previous model-based estimates [5,6]. However, it should be noted that this study relied on genomic-clustered case analysis, and the identification of genomic-clustered cases is influenced by various factors [8], particularly in high-burden areas where the clustering rate is often underestimated. Additionally, our genomic epidemiology studies were based on passive case-finding that would not include undiagnosed asymptomatic TB patients who do not seek care and are absent from the sampled dataset. Therefore, the results of this study likely underestimate the contribution of asymptomatic TB to transmission. Despite these limitations, this study provides the first empirical lower-bound estimate of the contribution of asymptomatic TB to transmission in China and introduces a genomic epidemiology-based framework for its quantification. Future studies can refine this approach by integrating active case-finding, thereby more accurately characterizing the role of asymptomatic TB in sustaining transmission.

Although the definition of asymptomatic TB is clear, its actual determination is highly subjective. A TB screening study conducted in Vietnam found that 47% of patients initially reported as asymptomatic changed their report to symptomatic after being informed of their diagnosis [15]. Without objective criteria, it is difficult to accurately quantify the contribution of asymptomatic TB to transmission. Similarly, because of the difficulty in accurately determining clustering rates in areas with a high TB incidence, the contribution of recent transmission is likely underestimated. Because many factors can influence estimates of the contribution of asymptomatic TB to transmission, we should not focus solely on the precise quantification of its contribution.

TB poses a serious threat to human health, and asymptomatic TB presents new challenges for TB control. Although it is difficult to precisely assess the contribution of asymptomatic TB to transmission, there is sufficient evidence to conclude that asymptomatic TB is responsible for a significant portion of transmission. However, from a public health perspective, the focus shouldn’t be solely on quantifying the contribution of asymptomatic TB to ongoing transmission, but rather on the importance of active case-finding. To reduce the global TB burden and achieve the End TB goals, it is essential to shift from passive case-finding to active case-finding, with an emphasis on identifying hidden sources of transmission in the community – both asymptomatic TB patients and symptomatic TB patients who have not sought medical care actively. However, active case-finding does not imply universal screening. In countries with low to medium TB incidence, active case-finding requires implementation of cost-effective screening that targets high TB-risk local populations. For instance, in low-incidence urban areas of China, TB screening in pulmonary departments of general hospitals has been proven to be an effective method for case detection [16].

To support the implementation of active case-finding, there is an urgent need to develop economical, point-of-care non-sputum screening tests. The performance of computer-assisted detection of TB from digital chest radiographs is currently close to the WHO target product profile requirements [17], but further optimization of accessibility and cost is needed. Although the sensitivity of most currently available screening tests is suboptimal, increasing accessibility to these tests can significantly enhance the overall effectiveness of screening [18].

In summary, TB control is at a critical juncture for transformative breakthroughs, with active case-finding becoming a decisive factor for the success or failure of global TB control efforts. To accelerate the current slow decline of the TB epidemic, countries must prioritize the implementation of targeted measures, and screening tests must be improved and made more widely accessible.
