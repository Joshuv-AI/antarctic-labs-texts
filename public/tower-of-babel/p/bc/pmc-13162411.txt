Large hydropower reservoirs in Russia can act as net anthropogenic sinks of carbon-based greenhouse gases
Authors: A. A. Romanovskaya, P. D. Polumieva, I. A. Repina, A. A. Trunov, V. М. Stepanenko, V. A. Lomov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study presents a methodology for assessing the anthropogenic component of the carbon-based greenhouse gases (GHG) balance of hydropower reservoirs in accordance with the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) guidelines. The methodology utilizes carbon balance calculations and the difference between the parameters of the reservoir water and the river located upstream. It integrates the results of field measurements in 2021–2023 and process-based modeling. Measurements of methane fluxes from the surface of reservoirs were carried out from a vessel using the floating chamber method without bubble emission shield in the shallow and deep-water parts of eight large reservoirs located in different bioclimatic zones of Russia (Kolymskoye, Bureiskoye, Volgogradskoye, Zeyskoye, Kuibyshevskoye, Rybinskoe, Chirkeyskoe, Sayano-Shushenskoe). Using this combined measurement and modeling data, we compared the obtained methane emission factors against those outlined in the IPCC methodological reports. The revised Tier 1 national factors for the boreal and cool temperate climate zones – 5.6 (± 4.3) and 33.2 (± 16.8) kg CH4 ha− 1 year− 1, respectively – are 59% and 39% lower than the corresponding default IPCC factors of 13.6 ± 6.3 and 54.0 ± 5.5 kg CH4 ha− 1 year− 1. According to the Tier 3 approach, the anthropogenic component of the GHG flux of all investigated reservoirs corresponds to negative net emissions, indicating an anthropogenic carbon sink, although the permanence of that accumulated carbon is uncertain. This suggests that the hydroelectric power of the studied reservoirs has a negative carbon footprint when direct fluxes considered.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13021-026-00429-1.

Introduction

Hydropower represents a significant contributor to renewable energy sources [38, 6, 45, 90]. However, the research community has not yet reached a consensus on the role of large hydropower facilities in terms of their contribution to global climate change [95, 86, 22, 21, 72, 23, 66, 16, 73, 14, 3, 17, 19, 52, 53]. Flooding of terrestrial ecosystems and changes in the hydrological regime of rivers contribute to the growth of methane (CH4) emissions into the atmosphere from reservoirs [98, 46, 35, 39, etc.]. Methane is a product of anaerobic decomposition of organic matter, the latter in turn is either produced directly in the artificial water body ecosystem (autochthonous organic matter) or supplied by the inflow of the main river and other tributaries as a result of leaching and erosion at the natural catchment (allochthonous organic matter). The total global methane emission (diffusive and ebullition) from reservoirs is subject to highly variable estimates (mean CI 95%) from 24 ± 8 [73] to 10 ± 4 [43] and about 2.1 Tg CH4 yr− 1 [97]. Prior global CH4 emissions estimates suggest 3 Tg C of CH4 yr− 1 is released from reservoir surfaces [3]. This estimate increases to 13.3 Tg C as CH4 yr− 1 after accounting for spillways, turbines, river reaches below dams, and periodically inundated drawdown areas around the reservoir [47]. According to estimates, reservoirs release the global methane flux approximately 13 (6–28) Tg CH4 yr− 1, with an additional 12 (7–37) Tg CH4 yr− 1 attributed to dam turbine degassing fluxes, resulting in a total of 25 (13–65) Tg CH4 yr− 1 from reservoirs, as outlined in the latest global methane budget [75]. The largest spread of methane emission estimations from the worlds reservoirs is from 2 [48] to 122 Tg CH4 yr− 1 [23] points to a high degree of uncertainty in the assessment methods. According to [43] the annual average intensity of global CH4 flux from reservoirs is 34.1 ± 9.9 g m− 2 yr− 1, with a higher value (44.8 ± 13.0 g m− 2 yr− 1) in the tropical zone than in boreal (6.1 ± 1.8 g m− 2 yr− 1), which is due to higher average temperatures and a longer (or year-round) growing season.

National inventories of anthropogenic emissions by sources and removals by sinks of greenhouse gases (GHGs) under the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) and the Paris Agreement should include all fluxes of anthropogenic GHGs. There are no issues to identify anthropogenic sources and emissions in energy, industrial, agriculture or waste sectors, whereas the inclusion of new fluxes (emissions or absorption) related to land use, land use change and forestry sector (LULUCF) require sophisticated justification and attribution of fluxes to the human activity. As outlined in the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change Guidelines for National Greenhouse Gas Inventories (hereinafter referred to as IPCC Guidelines) [36, 37] the prevailing approach for GHG inventories is to include only so-called managed ecosystems in national reports. This approach is specifically intended for forest ecosystems, where is no reliable method to disentangle anthropogenic influences from natural variability [36, 37]. In managed forests all fluxes are considered to be anthropogenic and included in national inventories, whereas in the non-managed forests all fluxes are considered natural and not taken into account. A more robust and sophisticated approach to estimate the specific anthropogenic contribution involves the systematic classification and justification of each individual GHG flux as anthropogenic or natural, followed by the determination of the proportion attributable to economic activity. This methodology is applied, for instance, to estimate N2O emissions from arable soils: only human-induced nitrogen emissions are accounted and natural soil emissions N2O are excluded.

The IPCC Guidelines [36, 37] for flooded lands provide a hierarchical system of methods for estimating GHG fluxes according to their complexity (from simple Tier 1 using default emission factors to Tier 3, which involves in-situ measurements and/or dynamic mathematical models), based on the calculation of the total emission of GHGs from the water surface into the atmosphere. The emission factors, i.e., the estimated mean annual values of GHG fluxes from the ecosystem, used are similarly divided into three levels of complexity. It should be noted that the approach to assessing only surface emissions from a reservoir differs from the IPCC recommendations for other managed ecosystems, where the budget of carbon fluxes is considered and the accumulation and storage of organic matter in the ecosystem itself is taken into account, which can ultimately result in the ecosystem being either a net source or a net sink. Insufficient consideration of the accumulation of organic matter in artificial reservoirs, especially during flooding of lowland rivers, apparently leads to an overestimation of the contribution of reservoirs to anthropogenic GHG emissions according to the IPCC methodology.

In addition, many studies show that undisturbed rivers are also a source of СН4 emissions [15, 7, 69, 77, 9]. The reported absolute values of emissions from rivers are comparable with the values for reservoirs. The analysis of 652 methane fluxes from 74 publications, in conjunction with Landsat data and Monte Carlo simulations, has yielded estimates of total river emissions about 30.5 ± 17.1 Tg CH4 yr− 1 in the meta-analysis by [73]. A recent study based on Global River Methane database (GRiMeDB) containing more than 8,000 observations of CH4 fluxes has reported a close estimate of 27.9 (16.7–39.7) Tg CH4 yr− 1 [70]. The mean intensity of CH4 fluxes (diffusive and ebullitive) in rivers is 4.23 ± 8.41 mmol m−² d− 1 [78]. In [5] the annual emission of СН4 varies from 2.5 ± 7.8 g m− 2 yr− 1 at high latitudes (54–66°), to 5.1 ± 15.4 g m− 2 yr− 1 at low latitudes (< 24°). Considering that the construction of a reservoir does not result in emergence of the new water sources, but changes the flow rate conditions and the residence time of water in a given area, for reporting under the UNFCCC and the Paris Agreement one should assess the impact of human activity on the intensity of natural processes related to GHGs and quantify the anthropogenic contribution to the respective fluxes. In this case, the total СН4 emission factors from the surface of reservoir water encompass both natural and anthropogenic fluxes, which must be separated.

Although reservoirs comprise a notable source of GHG emissions, they also act as important carbon sink, particularly oceans [65, 55, 1, 2, 58, 86, 11]. Frequently, their carbon stocks are greater than those found in other inland water bodies [14, 58, 84]. Global estimates suggest that reservoir sediments may store 0.06–0.25 Pg C yr⁻¹ [2, 58].

Carbon dynamics in reservoirs is observed at different timescales: emissions (CO2 and CH4 fluxes at the water-atmosphere interface) and carbon burial in sediments undergo transformations at varying rates. Despite this, over extended periods (years or more), reservoirs can act as net carbon sinks, sequestering atmospheric carbon [55, 58, 76]. When assessing the balance of carbon-based GHGs, both net emissions and net uptake have to be taken into account. In this study, we use the term ’carbon-based GHG uptake‘, acknowledging that GHGs are not directly absorbed by bottom sediments. A negative value of this balance indicates a net removal of carbon from the water-atmosphere system (accounting for methane and carbon dioxide emissions/uptake) and a net deposition in sediments. Consequently, a net GHG uptake occurs when the rate of carbon accumulation in sediments surpasses the rate of GHG emissions to the atmosphere.

In national GHG inventories, the GHG balance is often preferred over carbon balance because it better reflects the climatic impact of carbon exchange between aquatic ecosystems and the atmosphere and includes all major GHGs in one assessment [36, 37]. While the carbon balance of water bodies encompasses all carbon fluxes (such as sediment accumulation and carbon in form of CO2/CH4 emissions), the ‘carbon-based GHG balance‘ explicitly accounts for a water body’s contribution to global warming by considering not just carbon mass but also its radiative forcing.

Methane (CH4) and carbon dioxide (CO2) differ significantly in their climate impact: CH4 has a 100-year Global Warming Potential (GWP) 28 times higher than CO2 [40]. Thus, even minor CH4 emissions can substantially amplify atmospheric greenhouse effect induced by a water body. Conversely, when organic carbon is buried in sediments over the long term, it represents a form of sequestration that may offset GHG emissions from the water surface.

The term ’carbon-based GHG balance‘ aligns with international carbon footprint methodologies for aquatic and forest ecosystems, which emphasize the need to evaluate both net carbon exchange and its climate efficiency. As such, this metric provides a more comprehensive assessment, capturing not only ecosystem carbon dynamics but also their actual contribution to global warming.

Based on the preceding arguments, we propose quantifying the GHG balance of an ecosystem (reservoir in our case) as a net sum of chemical species entering and leaving the ecosystem, which can be expressed in atmospheric CO2 radiative-forcing equivalents. These species include GHGs per se (for which CO2 equivalents are well constrained for climate timescales) and other species which can be tracked back to GHGs in a series of biogeochemical transformations. For instance, particulate organic carbon carried into reservoirs by tributaries is not itself a GHG. However, as it originates from soil organic matter that was ultimately built from atmospheric CO2 (via primary productivity, plant mortality and microbial degradation), particulate organic carbon (POC) inflow is treated as CO2 influx in our framework. Under the same reasoning, POC outflow from a water body is treated as CO2 as well assuming it is oxidized and subsequently emitted to the atmosphere as carbon dioxide downstream. In this work the introduced definition of GHG balance is applied to carbon-based GHGs CO2 and CH4 only.

The objective of our work was to refine the default IPCC СН4 emission factors from reservoirs located in the different climatic zones of the Russian Federation, as well as to perform assessments of emission factors using the Tier 3 approach with the identification of anthropogenic impact in the intensity of total СН4 emissions as a result of land flooding. All studied reservoirs were impounded more than 20 years ago. According to the IPCC Guidelines [37], the significant pulse of CO2 emissions from the decomposition of initially flooded biomass occurs primarily within the first two decades post-flooding. Beyond this period, ongoing CO2 emissions are largely attributed to contemporary carbon inputs from the catchment. To prevent double-counting in GHG inventories, these emissions are reported under other managed land categories rather than the reservoir itself. Consequently, direct CO2 emissions of investigated reservoirs were not considered in our analysis.

Methodology

To develop updated national emission factors and a national methodology for assessing anthropogenic GHG fluxes in the reservoirs operated by RusHydro – the largest Russian power company – the research work was organized in 2021. The project research plan included observations of CH4 emissions and concentrations in water at the specified set of reservoirs, as well as of other characteristics of the carbon cycle of reservoirs over a period of 3 consecutive years (2nd quarter of 2021–1st quarter of 2024). These experimental data were used to validate and calibrate a process-based extended 1D thermohydrodinamical model with biochemical and GHG processes modules included [51]. The joint research was carried out by teams of three institutions: field and experimental studies were conducted by the A.M. Obukhov Institute of Atmospheric Physics of the Russian Academy of Sciences, process-based modeling of the СН4 and carbon cycle in reservoirs was undertaken at the Research Computing Center of Lomonosov Moscow State University, and the development of national emission factors based on field and modelling data, and a national methodology for reporting in the National inventory of GHGs was performed by the Yu.A. Izrael Institute of Global Climate and Ecology. Stages of our investigation are shown on Fig. 1.

Field measurements and modeling of methane fluxes and other characteristics of reservoirs

The measurements of CH4 fluxes were conducted in accordance with the International Hydropower Association’s GHG Measurement Guidelines for Freshwater Reservoirs prepared in cooperation with International Hydrological Programme (IHP) of UNESCO [24]. Measurements were carried out according to calendar seasons of the year (spring, summer, autumn, winter) at least once in each season for the period 2021–2023. The duration of measurement campaigns varied from year to year, but on average was at least 5 days per season. Measurements were carried out in the daytime from a vessel using the floating chamber method [4, 71] without bubble emission shield, i.e., capturing the total methane flux at the water surface.

The chamber method for measuring methane (CH4) fluxes from water surfaces utilizes a floating inert chamber to capture emitted gas [4]. The chamber air is circulated through flasks containing a saturated sodium chloride (NaCl) solution, which traps CH4 via reduced solubility at high ionic strength [54, 57]. Following a 20–30 min exposure, the solution samples are sealed and transported to the laboratory. CH4 is subsequently extracted by vacuum degassing or inert gas (N2) purging and quantified via gas chromatography with flame ionization detection (GC-FID) [32].

This method achieves a sensitivity of ≈ 0.1–1 ppm CH4 with a measurement uncertainty of ± 10–15% [91]. While advantageous for field simplicity and high-throughput sampling, it is less precise for low fluxes (< 0.1 mg CH4 m⁻² h⁻¹) compared to automated systems [4]. Its reliability has made it a standard technique for assessing CH4 emissions from freshwater ecosystems, particularly in remote areas [19].

The ANUDEM algorithm (Hutchinson’s method) [31] was chosen to construct the DEM of the bottom of all reservoirs based on vector data. In all cases, satellite images with different resolutions of at least 30 (Landsat-5, Landsat-7, Landsat-8, Sentinel-2) meters and point depth measurements obtained during expeditions within the framework of the project were used. Due to the peculiarities of the morphology of river valleys and the history of their filling, space images have become the predominant source of data for constructing the DEM of two reservoirs: Bureyskoye and Sayano-Shushenskoye.

According to [24, 37] each reservoir was divided into specific areas. Estimates of average fluxes of methane for each field campaign in the reservoirs were obtained on the basis of measured total fluxes at the measurement stations. СН4 fluxes at each measurement point were used for averaging over the area of the entire reservoir, taking into account the division of the reservoir into quasi-homogeneous regions, identified taking into account the bottom morphometry and the proximity of tributaries. CH4 flux measurements were conducted alongside supporting measurements of the vertical profile of temperature, electrical conductivity, concentrations of dissolved СН4 and oxygen in the water column, along with meteorological variables. Sampling horizons for collecting water samples for subsequent analysis of the concentration of dissolved gases were selected at the surface, at the bottom, and multiple depths relative to the thermocline layer (above, within, and below). The chemical composition of water was analyzed for each reservoir. Water samples for determining the content of total carbon and its components (organic total carbon in dissolved and suspended states) in the water of reservoirs were taken from the surface and bottom horizons on a grid of representative stations at each studied reservoir in dark glass jars with a volume of 125 ml in accordance with the requirements of the national standard documents [26, 28, 29]. The samples were then fixed with 1–2 ml of orthophosphoric acid and stored at 2–8 °C for no more than the standard 30-day storage period to ensure chemical stability and suppress microbial growth. Total organic carbon (TOC) content was determined in both filtered and unfiltered sample aliquots using high-temperature catalytic oxidation with a ’Vario TOC Select‘ analyzer (Elementar, Germany). This method involves sample combustion at 1 200 °C followed by infrared detection of the resulting CO2, enabling precise measurement of both dissolved and particulate organic carbon.

For fractionated analysis, filtration was performed through 0.45 μm membrane filters in accordance with standard TOC analysis methodologies for natural waters [34].

The organic matter content in the bottom sediments of the reservoirs was determined as the weight loss of a sample according to ignition method [27]. Bottom sediment samples were collected using an Ekman-Berge grab sampler, and the analysis was performed for the upper soil layer no more than 10 cm thick.

To determine the rate of sedimentation of suspended organic matter in reservoirs, sediment traps were installed in the bottom layer in order to determine the sediment accumulation rate and the content of TOC. The TOC content in sediment trap samples was determined by high-temperature combustion (680 °C) followed by non-dispersive infrared (NDIR) detection of evolved CO₂ using an elemental analyzer (Elementar vario TOC cube), after pretreatment with acid to remove inorganic carbon. Estimates of sedimentation rates for specific reservoirs based on literature data were also used [44, 74, 92–94].

For the Chirkeyskoe reservoir, measurements of СН4 fluxes were carried out in all seasons of the year. The results of winter expeditions to the remaining 7 reservoirs show that winter СН4 emissions from their surfaces are zero due to the ice during that season.

Taking into account the low temporal resolution of the measurement data for CH4 fluxes and concentrations, the LAKE mathematical model, developed for simulating hydrological, thermodynamic and biogeochemical processes in lakes and reservoirs, was used to determine the annual GHG balance of the studied reservoirs and their absorption capacity in terms of carbon burial, taking into account temporal variability down to subdiurnal scales [79, 13, 25, 82]. LAKE model is a mechanistic simulator, solving one-dimensional partial differential equations describing vertical transport and conversions of heat, radiation, momentum, and biogeochemical species in water column, snow and ice covers, and bottom sediments. Sediments located at different depth zones are treated using 1D approach individually. The inlets and outlets to the water body are included and sources and sinks of the above-mentioned quantities. The model explicitly reproduces vertical profiles of all prognostic variables and temporal variability from minutes to centuries. The model has been extensively applied either to individual lakes and reservoirs [81, 79, 13, 82, 51] and to global simulation problems [25, 89]. The LAKE model code is publicly available (https://mathmod.org/lake/).

The LAKE 3.0 model [80, 82, 51] was optimized in terms of equation parameters to match the results of field measurements at each reservoir, which made it possible to realistically reproduce the temperature and oxygen regime of reservoirs, and the annual course of the CH4 fluxes at the surface of reservoirs; then, the model was used to estimate the total annual CH4 emission, and the sedimentation rate of autochthonous organic matter in carbon equivalent into bottom sediments. The input meteorological data for the model included surface air temperature, atmospheric pressure, air humidity, wind speed, downward fluxes of longwave and shortwave radiation, precipitation. The time series of these values were taken from the ERA5 reanalysis data (https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu). The emission factors presented in Table S2 are based on spatial and temporal variations and were obtained by applying empirical Greenhouse Gas Reservoir (G-Res) model [63] to more than six thousand reservoirs worldwide, and the resulting emission factors were averaged across climate zones [37]. The largest number of reservoir data were collected for the cool temperate and humid temperate climates, accounting for about 4,000 water bodies. The boreal climate zone, which contains the majority of large reservoirs in the Russian Federation, accounts for 96 modeled reservoirs [37] which represents less than 2% of all water objects analyzed by IPCC.

The IPCC guidelines [37] suggest that if the trophic status of a reservoir is determined, then the Tier 1 assessments can be enhanced by multiplying default CH4 emission factors by a factor αj, i, either computed from measured mean annual chlorophyll-a (Chl-a) via a recommended empirical relationship or can be derived from general assessments of reservoir trophic status and default trophic state adjustment factors [37]. It generally provides a more accurate approach to reservoirs where Chl-a concentrations have been measured. At the same time, the aforementioned dependencies indicate that chlorophyll concentration values in excess of 5 µg/l results in a multiple increase in estimated CH4 emissions from the reservoir, which, however, does not take into account the increase in the sedimentation of dead organic matter originated from the reservoir plankton mortality in eutrophic water bodies.

In present research αj, i was set 1, based on the fact that we measured the total CH4 emission from the water surface, and the effect of the trophic status of the reservoir was already taken into account in the measured values.

Methodology for estimating net GHG emissions using the balance method (IPCC Tier 3)

According to the IPCC Guidance [37], mass-balance approaches that track organic carbon inputs and their decay represent a Tier 3 method for estimating emissions from reservoirs via the managed land proxy, caused by conveying freshwaters into reservoirs. However, the IPCC does not provide a specific methodology for such an estimate.

In this paper, a preliminary assessment of GHG emissions and carbon sedimentation in reservoirs are carried out based on the original methodology for assessing the carbon budget of the reservoir ecosystems which identifies the anthropogenic impact on the intensity of carbon fluxes and CH4 emissions over the impounded territory. This methodology corresponds to Tier 3 method for emission factors according to the IPCC Guidelines. In accordance with the general approach of the IPCC Guidelines for the land, land use change and forestry sector (LULUCF) [36], any pre-impoundment GHG balance of the landscape (e.g. [64]) is not accounted for, only factorial net GHG emissions in certain years are estimated. Consequently, our approach is consistent with that of the IPCC, insofar as it takes into account only observed fluxes, as opposed to hypothetical ones.

The annual CH4 flux was estimated by multiplying the specific CH4 flux by the duration of the ice-free season for each reservoir, while emissions during the period when the reservoirs were covered in ice were assessed as zero. In order to estimate the total organic carbon content in the water body of the reservoir, Cwater,2, the sum values of the dissolved and suspended carbon content in the reservoir water were obtained from the observation data, which had been averaged over all seasons and measurement locations.

The natural fraction of the total organic carbon, Cwater,2,allo, was estimated as the ratio of the concentration of organic carbon in the river water and in the reservoir water, conventionally assuming that the concentration of organic carbon in the river corresponds to the share of allochthonous matter in the reservoir; by this, we suggest that only allochthonous organic carbon in the reservoir contributes to natural CH4 emissions from the reservoir. The reservoir also produces autochthonous organic matter, which was previously absent in the river. It is therefore vital that all fluxes of this organic matter are fully accounted for, as they are of anthropogenic origin (Fig. 2). Thus, we adopted a methodological approach to estimate changes in individual carbon and GHG fluxes, which is more comparable to those used by the IPCC Guidelines for other managed land categories [36], in contrast to the approach of isolating the anthropogenic contribution by comparing the area of the river before and after reservoir inundation.

Results and discussion

Estimation of Tier 1 emission factors

As detailed in the previous section, the refined emission factors developed in this work were obtained using in situ data on CH4 surface emission for each reservoir for 2021–2023. Measurement data for CH4 fluxes at reservoirs are given in Fig. 3 and Table S3.

Fig. 3Field measurement data of the average CH4 fluxes in reservoirs for 2021–2023, mg C-CH4 m−2 day−1

Field measurement data of the average CH4 fluxes in reservoirs for 2021–2023, mg C-CH4 m−2 day−1

The obtained data indicate that in a boreal dry climate zone, the average annual CH4 emissions are 2.4 ± 0.2 mg CH4 m⁻2 day⁻1 at deep sites and approximately 3.5 ± 0.8 mg CH4 m⁻2 day⁻1 at shallower sites. Corresponding average values for the cool temperate dry zone are 15.5 ± 9.1 and 31.0 ± 29.7 mg CH4 m⁻2 day⁻1, respectively. Total methane flux from the entire reservoirs was 2.5 ± 1.0 mg CH4 m⁻2 day⁻1 in the boreal and 20.0 ± 20.7 mg CH4 m⁻2 day⁻1 in the cool temperate zone. In general, the estimates obtained in this work are in good agreement with the data available in literature. Despite the numerous studies on estimating CH4 emissions from the surface of reservoirs, the problem of reliable assessment remains relevant, since these estimates are based on a limited number of data and have a fairly large scatter. For instance, in the temperate zone total methane emissions, integrating diffusive, ebullitive, and storage fluxes, were found to be 11.5 ± 4.4 mg CH4 m− 2 day− 1 [48]. According to [88] the average CH4 emissions in the moderately cold (boreal) zone for the studied group of reservoirs were 17 ± 25 mg CH4 m− 2 day− 1, while the average estimate for the temperate zone was 9.0 ± 6.1 mg CH4 m− 2 day− 1. In [16] the global average GHG flux from energy-producing reservoirs estimated to be between 32 and 149 mg CH4 m− 2 day− 1. Additionally, [73] reported median total methane flux of 26.1 (3.9–78.8) mg CH4 m− 2 day− 1 for reservoirs smaller than 1 km² and 46.4 (8.6–153.4) mg CH4 m− 2 day− 1 for those larger than 1 km².

Consistent with other studies [18, 42, 85, 50] our results demonstrate that methane emissions in shallow areas of the reservoir are typically higher than in deep water (see Fig. 3). The phenomenon is attributed to three primary factors. Firstly, the reservoir’s shallow waters exhibit elevated sedimentation and nutrient levels [42]. Secondly, the lower hydrostatic pressure facilitates the formation of methane bubbles [42, 96]. In deep water, the probability of bubble formation is diminished, and methane produced in dissolved form can be oxidized before reaching the water surface. Thirdly, various studies confirm the primary effect of temperature in shallow waters on CH4 flux. For example, in the Russian Zeya reservoir [85] and similarly in the Mozhaysk reservoir [50] low water temperatures and a lower rate of organic matter intake in deep water limited methane output.

Default IPCC emission factors are applied at Tier 1 and cannot take into account the specific characteristics of reservoirs, since they are essentially average values for large and small artificial reservoirs. In present work, observation data were obtained only for large and the largest reservoirs, which have a greater depth and water surface area and therefore are characterized by lower values of emissions from the water surface. Using emission factors averaged on a global scale or in latitudinal zones cause inaccuracies in estimates of GHG emissions and sinks both for individual reservoirs and countries. Such emission factors do not incorporate national and socio-economic patterns, nor distinct characteristics of individual reservoirs, which may include:

The obtained national average emission factor for the boreal zone is 59% lower than the IPCC default [37]. The emission factor for the cool temperate zone is 39% less than the default one. The factor obtained for the warm temperate zone is based on measurements for only one reservoir – Chirkeyskoe, which is located in high-mountainous region and cannot be used at this stage of research directly in the national GHG inventory, since this reservoir is not representative of the entire climate zone.

Figure 4 presents the findings from calculations employing the Tier 1 approach, which are delineated in Eqs. (2)–(4). On average, CH4 emission from the surface of all investigated reservoirs using the IPCC default factors is 62% higher than CH4 emission when estimated using national factors. The closest estimates of CH4 emission from the water surface between two implementations of Tier 1 are noted for the Bureyskoye and Sayano-Shushenskoye reservoirs (the difference is 15% and 16%, respectively).

When literature data are recalculated into consistent units, assuming a 235-day average ice-free period for the temperate zone, the resulting emission factors are comparable with our results and fall below the IPCC default, ranging from 19.4 ± 13.2 kg CH4 ha⁻1 yr⁻1 [88] to 24.8 ± 9.5 kg CH4 ha⁻1 yr⁻1 [48]. In contrast, for the boreal zone, literature estimates tend to be higher than both our results and the values given in the IPCC guidelines. For instance, assuming a 150-day ice-free period for the boreal zone, annual methane emissions for that zone have been estimated as high as 25.5 ± 37.5 kg CH4 ha⁻1 yr⁻1 [88]. Similarly, [3] reported average CH4 emissions for high-latitude water bodies (30–68°N) of approximately 15.0 (0–110) mg CH4 m− 2 day− 1, which translates to 28.9 (0–211.8) kg CH4 ha⁻1 yr⁻1. For the boreal zone specifically (50–70°), the same study reported an average value of 33.3 kg CH4 ha⁻1 yr⁻1.

The lower national emission factors obtained in our study, compared to the IPCC default values may be attributed to the large surface area and greater depth of the reservoirs we examined. As previously discussed, deep waters are characterized by relatively lower CH4 emissions. The maximum observed difference was found in the northernmost reservoirs (e.g., Kolyma reservoir), where low temperatures affected methane flux, as well as in the mountain reservoirs (e.g., Chirkeyskoe reservoir), where the amount of organic sediments in the reservoir and methane production from them were minimal. It is important to recognize that the IPCC default emission factors represent a mean value simulated by G-Res model for artificial reservoirs of varying sizes and depths. The G-Res model is dependent on detailed environmental characteristics for each reservoir. Such characteristics include e.g., the soil organic carbon content in the 0–30 cm layer and the depth category (either less than or more than 3 m). It is evident that most Russian reservoirs do not possess the necessary data to facilitate the collection of such information. As follows from [37] the validation of modeled emission factors with measured ones is not strictly comparable. For example, the IPCC default boreal emission factor was validated only to 5 direct measurements, which were characterized by a considerably higher uncertainty range in smaller values. Conversely, it is important to acknowledge that the national emission factors are derived from a smaller number of studied reservoirs than the default ones. This discrepancy in the number of studied reservoirs may also influence the observed difference. According to [46], the use of reservoir-specific data for estimating GHG emissions from hydropower reservoirs yields results that are 22% lower than estimates based on average gross emissions.

Estimation of net GHG emissions using the balance method (Tier 3)

Based on the initial data presented in Table S4, an assessment of the net GHG fluxes in the reservoir was made using measurement and modeling data following the developed methodology for assessing the carbon balance of the reservoir ecosystem and identifying the effect of anthropogenic impact on the intensity of carbon fluxes and CH4 emissions in this ecosystem in the eight studied reservoirs.

Based on the results of measurements and process-based modelling, we estimated the net GHG balance in reservoirs and rivers, taking into account surface CH4 emissions, the sedimentation of organic matter and carbon storage in water. The results are presented in Fig. 5 and Supplementary information (see fig. S1-S9).

For most of the studied reservoirs, the CH4 emission from the water surface increases due to reservoir construction. At the same time, the sedimentation of organic matter increases as well. A comparison of the upward (emissions into the atmosphere) and downward (sedimentation) fluxes shows that all the studied reservoirs, like rivers, are net absorbers of greenhouse gases. At the same time, the magnitude of GHG sink by reservoirs is higher than by rivers (Fig. 5 and fig. S1-S8 in Supplementary information), which is the direct anthropogenic effect on GHG fluxes.

In the literature data the most studied flux is the CH4 emission into the atmosphere from the water surface, since it is known that reservoirs are a source of additional methane emissions [87] comparing to rivers, and it is easier to measure compared to e.g. sedimentation flux. Our data show that average annual CH4 emissions from upstream rivers range from 1.2 ± 0.2 mg CH4 m⁻2 day⁻1 in the boreal climate zone to 14.1 ± 1.3 mg CH4 m⁻2 day⁻1 in the cool temperate climate zone. Other studies also note that CH4 emissions from the surface of unmanaged streams and rivers may vary depending on their latitudinal location, although higher flux values have also been recorded. Thus, in [5], the annual emission of CH4 from the surface of rivers is assessed as 5.1 ± 7.8 g CH4 m− 2 yr− 1 for latitudes < 24°, 4.8 ± 14.6 g CH4 m− 2 yr− 1 for latitudes 25°–54°, 2.5 ± 7.6 g CH4 m− 2 yr− 1 for latitudes > 54°–66°, and as 2.6 ± 7.8 g CH4 m− 2 yr− 1 for latitudes > 66°. In [10], estimates of CH4 fluxes from the surface of rivers flowing in the boreal zone (Canada) vary from 7.2 to 225.2 mg CH4 m− 2 day− 1. In [8], the CH4 emission from the surface of a temperate zone river was estimated as 4.0 (1.1–59.3) mg CH4 m− 2 day− 1, although the CH4 emission value in the work [56] for the same river was obtained as 2.1 ± 0.6 (0.4–11.9) mg CH4 m− 2 day− 1. The CH4 emission value for rivers on which depleted reservoirs are located ranges from 0.1 to 17.8 mg CH4 m− 2 day− 1.

The total organic carbon content in reservoirs water is higher in all cases than in rivers (see Table S4). The observed effect can be attributed to the interruption of downstream organic matter transport by dam construction, which promotes its accumulation within the reservoir, unlike in flowing river systems. The accumulation of autochthonous organic matter also occurs due to the development of its own biota in the reservoir volume [12].

The total organic carbon content in rivers observed in our study falls within the typical range reported for rivers. According to [61], the total organic carbon content in the Odra River ranges from 3.7 to 29.0 g m− 3. In [60], the similar estimate provided a range from 5.7 to 21.1 g m− 3 for rivers flowing through Poland. According to [61], the total organic carbon content in various European rivers ranges from 5.7 to 8.5 g m− 3. The TOC concentration in Russian rivers discharging into the Arctic Ocean falls within the range of 13.1–13.9 g m− 3 [49]. Total organic matter content in the waters of the Valdaika (4.72 g m− 3), Malaya Istra (4.72 g m− 3), Oka (5.23 g m− 3) and Samara (5.58 g m− 3) rivers of European territory of Russia is reported in [68]. The value of the organic matter content in the Volga River water (6.4 g m− 3) according to [68] corresponds to the estimates obtained in our work (7.3 ± 0.7 g m− 3).

The studies [63, 64] employed the ‘what the atmosphere sees’ principle to quantify the anthropogenic contribution to GHG emissions from reservoirs. It is recommended that annual net GHG emissions of all pre-impoundment ecosystems under the area of reservoir be estimated in order to be incorporated into the annual GHG balance. This approach is not entirely comparable to the IPCC approach. The IPCC methodology is divided into two categories: firstly, Land converted to Flooded Land (less than 20 years after impoundment) and secondly, Flooded Land remaining Flooded Land (more than 20 years after impoundment). In the context of converted land all changes in carbon pools before and after conversions are estimated at the year of conversion. Some emissions such as those resulting from inundated soil and biomass decay persist for a period of years following conversion. Consequently, these emissions are accounted for over the duration of this period. Nevertheless, the loss or gain of carbon as a result of land conversion is only accounted for once at the time that the change occurs. For Flooded Land remaining Flooded Land only factual annual GHG fluxes are reported, with no any emissions attributed to the conversion of land. Conversely, if the reporting in the National GHG inventories was to encompass hypothetical GHG fluxes prior to land conversion for all categories of Land remained Land, e.g., including the majority of arable lands and urban territories, such a reporting system would be incapable of facilitating achievement of carbon neutrality.

The paper [64] indicates that the accumulation of sediment due to the increased residence time of water in the reservoir as a result of dam construction can be considered to offset GHG emissions provided that the same sediment would not have formed elsewhere in the river under natural conditions. Nevertheless, if we consider part of the reservoir sedimentation to be displaced carbon storage, it would be logical to assume that part of the CH4 emissions from this sedimentation is also displaced. One proposed solution to this issue is to calculate emissions over a 10-year period of impoundment, under the assumption that, following this period, emissions will have originated from natural processes, resulting in a net emission of zero [46]. In our view that such an approach would result in an underestimation of the anthropogenic impact on GHG fluxes in hydroelectric reservoirs.

From a carbon dioxide removal perspective, carbon accumulated in natural sinks like reservoir sediments is a high-risk asset. Its storage might be not permanent and such factors as flushing, water level changes, maintenance dredging, or extreme events can lead to GHG release. The subsequent fate of mobilized sediments is uncertain: some may be re-deposited in deltas or seas, but a portion can decompose, emitting carbon dioxide and methane. This long-term storage risk undermines the effectiveness of a such nature-based solution for climate mitigation, highlighting the need for more research.

As the analysis of National GHGs inventories (https://unfccc.int/first-biennial-transparency-reports) of developed countries revealed that the IPCC 2019 methodology is not yet used in their annual reports for Flooded Land category. This might indirectly indicate at deficiencies of the default methodology. For example, Canada provides estimates only for CO2, without indicating CH4 emissions [59]. Finland and Germany report only on GHG emissions from the conversion of land to wetlands [30, 83], while the United States included this category for the first time in their 2024 report [20].

Thus, the findings from our large-scale investigation of the eight largest Russian reservoirs, combined with the advanced methodology developed to assess anthropogenic GHG emissions and absorption in flooded lands, mark a significant scientific advancement applicable at both national and international scales. Our findings are applicable to national GHG inventories of other countries within boreal dry and cool temperate dry climate zones, specifically for reservoir types with comparable operational regimes and management (e.g., large plain hydropower reservoirs). The Tier 1 emission factors developed here can be applied in conjunction with default IPCC emission factors, or can replace them if adequately justified. For Tier 3 implementation, the methodology presented here is directly applicable, though the specific emission factors require further country-level validation.

Conclusions

The estimates of anthropogenic GHG emission factors based on in-situ field measurements and process-based modeling for eight large reservoirs in the Russian Federation located in various geographic and climatic conditions were conducted in accordance with the IPCC methodology at two levels of complexity (Tier 1, Tier 3). According to the IPCC, the carbon dioxide fluxes from reservoirs older than 20 years are fully accounted under other managed land categories in the National GHG inventories, so only CH4 emissions are taken into account in our estimates. The average CH4 emission factors from the surface of the studied reservoirs were 5.6 (± 4.3) kg CH4 ha−1 yr−1 for the boreal zone and 33.2 (± 16.8) kg CH4 ha−1 yr−1 for the cool temperate zone. The emission factor obtained in this work for the boreal zone is 59% lower than the IPCC default factor (13.6 ± 6.3 kg CH4 ha−1) and for the cool temperate zone is 39% lower than the default (54.0 ± 5.5 kg CH4 ha−1).

The values ​​of CH4 emissions from the surface of all investigated reservoirs according to Tier 1 shows that estimates with the national emission factors are, on average, 62% lower than the estimates of CH4 emissions according to default Tier 1 IPCC emission factors. National emission factors, unlike the IPCC 2019 factors, directly take into account the physico-geographical features of the reservoirs’ watersheds, as well as the specific properties of individual reservoirs, such as their size and depth, the relative location of oxic/anoxic zones, their trophic status, and other environmental factors (e.g., weather conditions), which allows for more accurate estimates.

According to the results of the net GHG emissions including methane emission, carbon storage and carbon sedimentation (Tier 3 emission factors in line with IPCC), due to combination of measurement data and numerical process-based modeling, the anthropogenic impact by dam construction leads to negative net annual GHG balance. This indicates that the contemporary carbon accumulation exceeds methane emissions, and impoundment’s impact on the river ecosystem results in a net absorption of greenhouse gases.

If our results are confirmed for other large hydropower reservoirs, it will indicate the anthropogenic net absorption of GHGs in reservoirs of boreal and/or temperate zones globally. This, in turn, suggests the negative carbon footprint of electricity from large hydroelectric power plants, at least when direct GHG fluxes are taken into consideration. At the same time, reliable data on the duration of carbon storage for carbon accumulated in reservoirs, as well as an assessment of the risk of its non-permanence, are not yet available.

The estimates presented in this paper base on 3 years of year-round measurements and can be potentially refined by collecting longer data series and by further developing the process-based LAKE model. This methodology includes measurements of GHG fluxes both at the river section before the reservoir, over the reservoir surface, as well as downstream of the dam. To correctly account for interannual variability, it is necessary to estimate the following variables over a period of about 10 years with at least seasonal resolution:

CH4 emission from the surface area;organic carbon concentration in the water;the rate of organic carbon deposition in reservoirs and in the tributaries.
