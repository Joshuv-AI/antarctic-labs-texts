Tilapia Feeding Behavior Image Dataset: A Benchmark Resource for Automated Feeding Intensity Recognition in Aquaculture
Authors: Shahbaz Gul Hassan, Yin Hang, Murtaza Hasan, Ferdous Sohel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultiparametric video-based aquaculture monitoring systems are becoming essential for observing and quantifying fish behavior in real time. These platforms enable high-frequency, continuous recording of underwater activity, supporting automation in feeding management and behavioral analytics. The Tilapia Feeding Behavior Image Dataset (TFBID-mini) was generated using controlled recirculating aquaculture tanks equipped with high-definition underwater cameras to capture feeding events of Oreochromis niloticus (Nile tilapia). The dataset consists of 4,000 expert-annotated images classified into four feeding-intensity levels-None, Weak, Medium, and Strong-each representing a distinct behavioral state observed across multiple feeding sessions. Images were extracted from 1080p video sequences recorded under varying illumination conditions and quality-checked to ensure visual clarity, color balance, and consistency. The resulting dataset provides a standardized benchmarking resource for evaluating computer vision and artificial intelligence models for automated recognition of feeding behavior and for optimizing intelligent feeding systems in aquaculture.

Background & Summary

Aquaculture now supplies over half of aquatic animal production. It continues to expand as a pillar of global food security, with 2022 marking the first year that farmed output exceeded wild capture; per-capita consumption and total production both reached record highs under FAO’s “Blue Transformation” agenda1–4. Feeding is among the most cost- and impact-intensive operations in intensive systems; optimizing feeding reduces waste, improves growth, and limits water-quality deterioration, making objective, high-frequency monitoring of feeding behavior a practical necessity for precision aquaculture5–7.

Computer vision and machine learning are increasingly used for non-invasive aquatic monitoring. Yet, open datasets still skew toward presence/abundance rather than behavioral intensity. Seminal resources such as Fish4Knowledge (long-term coral-reef video and ecological analytics) and DeepFish (≈40 k images across 20 habitats for classification/segmentation/counting) catalyzed progress in detection, counting, and habitat analysis, but do not label feeding intensity per se8–12. More recently, FathomNet aggregated expertly curated labels to accelerate AI for ocean imagery and to support standardization, but again focuses on taxonomic breadth over behavioral labeling13–15. In coastal observatory contexts, Scientific Data’s OBSEA dataset established a benchmark for automated fish detection and classification under real-world conditions with accompanying metocean data and QA/QC procedures16–18.

Regarding feeding behavior, evidence of feasibility for recognizing feeding intensity at scale in publicly available expert-labeled datasets is scarce19–21. This shortage limits reproducibility, hinders benchmarking of lightweight models for edge deployment, and impedes meta-analyses across farms or species. To help address this need, we present the Tilapia Feeding Behavior Image Dataset (TFBID), a curated collection of images depicting feeding intensity in Oreochromis niloticus. The dataset is derived from fixed-camera video through a transparent data-creation pipeline: frame extraction at fixed intervals; image processing (noise elimination, color correction, and cropping); and manual annotation by aquaculture experts via consensus. We assess inter-class separability using t-SNE and support model explainability with Grad-CAM overlays in the technical validation22,23. Quality control follows QA/QC principles advanced within the community (e.g., QARTOD flags) and is adapted to aquaculture images and metadata24.

For baseline validation, we train a lightweight network (SHCNet) aligned with best practices for edge-efficient networks: Swish activation for smooth nonlinearity, SimAM for parameter-free attention, and CSP modules for lower redundancy - combined with transfer learning from large natural image databases, easing convergence speed on medium aquatic datasets25–29. Transferability of features (and their limits) is well documented, supporting targeted layer freezing and fine-tuning, as validated by our experiments30,31. TFBID is made available within the broader open-science framework for Scientific Data: images and labels, codes and metadata are released in detail under CC BY 4.0, maximizing the potential for reuse through persistent identifiers and formal data citation32,33. Collectively, TFBID will enable: (i) benchmarking of lightweight models of behavior recognition, (ii) explainability analysis (e.g., attention/attribution maps), (iii) multimodal fusion with environmental covariates, and(iv) transfer learning across facilities/species. By supplementing scientific focus species/habitat datasets (Fish4Knowledge, DeepFish, FathomNet) and observatory datasets (OBSEA) with behavior-intensity labels, TFBID fulfills a pressing need for precision feeding research and deployment8–18.

Experimental Site and Setup

The dataset was collected at the Recirculating Aquaculture Facility of the Fisheries Technology Extension Center in Nansha District, Guangzhou City, Guangdong Province, China (latitude 23°11′N, longitude 113°27′E). The experimental system consists of multiple circular tanks, each 2.7 meters in diameter, equipped with independent water recirculation, filtration, and aeration systems to maintain stable environmental conditions. Each tank housed 60–100 Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) with an initial body weight of 100–150 g. Throughout the six-month trial period, fish were fed a high-protein pellet diet twice daily at fixed intervals. Water quality parameters were continuously monitored using digital sensors to ensure consistency: temperature (25–28 °C), dissolved oxygen (>6 mg/L), and pH (7.0–8.0). Environmental data were recorded and synchronized with camera timestamps to allow multimodal analysis of behavior and environmental fluctuations.

Imaging system and data acquisition

Underwater video footage was captured using Hikvision high-definition cameras positioned laterally in each tank. Each camera featured a 2.8 mm wide-angle lens (130° field of view) and supported 1080p resolution at 30 frames per second. The cameras were mounted at fixed positions to provide stable viewing angles and minimize refraction effects. The recording system continuously captured daily feeding events, creating a comprehensive time series of visual observations. During the experimental period, all tanks were illuminated using diffuse white LED lighting to ensure consistent brightness and minimize glare. The setup (Fig. 1) reduced specular reflections from the water surface and tank walls, allowing clearer visualization of fish aggregation, movement, and splashing during feeding. Video recordings were stored in MP4 format and subsequently processed for frame extraction.

Behavioral labeling and classification criteria

Feeding intensity was classified into four discrete levels based on behavioral observations and expert consultation (Fig. 2), adapted from prior studies on feeding dynamics.

Representative underwater images showing the four annotated feeding-intensity levels in TFBID: (a) None – no surface activity; (b) Weak – few fish responding; (c) Medium - moderate aggregation and splashing; (d) Strong – vigorous competition at the surface. The classification criteria are shown in Table 1.

Each image was manually annotated using a custom OpenCV-based annotation tool developed in Python. The labeling process was performed independently by two aquaculture experts, and disagreements were resolved through consensus review. This dual-review approach ensured high labeling consistency across all feeding levels.

Frame extraction and image preprocessing

Given the temporal redundancy between consecutive frames, a frame-difference extraction strategy was employed to select key frames that contain distinct feeding activity. Thirty representative frames per second were initially extracted, followed by manual filtering to remove redundant, blurred, or overexposed images. The retained images were then cropped to regions of interest (1839 × 998 px) to focus on the central feeding zone.

Data Records

The Tilapia Feeding Behavior Image Dataset (TFBID) is publicly available on Zenodo34 and distributed under the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International (CC BY 4.0) license. The dataset contains underwater RGB images of Oreochromis niloticus (Nile tilapia) collected during controlled feeding experiments in a recirculating aquaculture system (RAS).

The dataset is distributed as a compressed archive file named fishdata.zip. After extraction, the dataset follows a hierarchical directory structure organized according to feeding-intensity categories. Four primary folders are included: None, Weak, Medium, Strong. Each folder contains JPEG image files corresponding to a specific feeding-intensity class. Images are sequentially named using the convention < class > < frame number > .jpg, for example, medium00001.jpg. This directory organization is compatible with commonly used deep learning frameworks such as PyTorch and TensorFlow, enabling direct loading via functions like torchvision. datasets.ImageFolder and tf.keras.preprocessing.image_dataset_from_directory.

All images are stored in JPEG (.jpg) format using 24-bit RGB color encoding. Each image has a fixed spatial resolution of 1839 × 998 pixels at 96 dpi. The dataset was captured using an underwater HD camera operating at 1080p resolution under mixed illumination conditions consisting of daylight and LED white light. Images were acquired in an indoor recirculating aquaculture system containing Nile tilapia during feeding trials. The dataset includes four feeding-intensity labels representing visually distinguishable fish feeding conditions:

Data overview

TFBID contains 4000 RGB images, evenly distributed across four feeding-intensity categories: None, Weak, Medium, and Strong detail composition is shown in Table 4. Each category contains 1000 images, resulting in a balanced four-class dataset. The average image file size ranges from approximately 0.75 MB to 0.80 MB, depending on image content and compression settings. Annotation consistency between the two independent annotators was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa statistic, yielding a kappa of 0.91, indicating high inter-annotator agreement across feeding-intensity categories.

Technical Validation

Model validation and evaluation

To validate dataset quality, the SHCNet lightweight architecture(Fig. 4)-an enhanced version of ShuffleNet V2 integrating a SimAM attention module, Swish activation, and Cross-Stage Partial (CSP) mechanism-achieved the best classification results shown in Table 5. The average precision (AP) values for the four classes were: None = 1.00, Weak = 0.998, Medium = 0.993, Strong = 0.996. These results confirm that the dataset supports highly accurate feeding-level recognition and contains sufficient visual distinction for reliable model learning.

Usage Notes

The TFBID dataset provides a standardized benchmark for evaluating AI-driven aquaculture monitoring systems. It can be used in various research and application contexts:

Benchmarking Classification Models The balanced class distribution and expert-labeled ground truth make TFBID suitable for testing CNNs, lightweight architectures (e.g., ShuffleNet, EfficientNet), and transformer-based models.

Benchmarking Classification Models The balanced class distribution and expert-labeled ground truth make TFBID suitable for testing CNNs, lightweight architectures (e.g., ShuffleNet, EfficientNet), and transformer-based models.

Transfer Learning and Lightweight AI Development The dataset’s relatively small image size and well-labeled categories enable efficient fine-tuning of pre-trained models for deployment on embedded or edge devices in aquaculture farms.

Transfer Learning and Lightweight AI Development The dataset’s relatively small image size and well-labeled categories enable efficient fine-tuning of pre-trained models for deployment on embedded or edge devices in aquaculture farms.

Temporal and Multimodal Analysis When combined with recorded environmental metadata (DO, pH, temperature), TFBID can support research on temporal behavior modeling and multimodal fusion that links behavior to environmental conditions.
