Identification of a novel bacteriophage attachment site into ffs, the 4.5S non-coding RNA component of the signal recognition particle
Authors: Roy H. Stevens, Hongming Zhang, Derrick E. Fouts
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bioinformatic analysis of Enterococcus faecalis temperate phage ϕEf11 identified prospective attP and attB core attachment (att) sites consisting of identical 27 nt sequences (ACTAAGCAAGTGCCGCCATGTGTCTGA). The presumptive attPcore site was located 74 nts from the terminus of the ϕEf11 integrase (ORF 31) while the presumptive attB site was located within ffs, encoding the 4.5S RNA component of the signal recognition particle (SRP). After examining 6,028 genomes of 61 Enterococcal species using updated Phage_Finder software, attL and attR sequences disrupting ffs could only be detected in lysogenic strains of E. faecalis. We have found no other example of a prophage inserted into ffs, therefore, the ffs locus for ϕEf11 integration represents a novel phage attachment site. SRP functions in the transport of proteins through the cellular membrane to the periplasmic space. Integration into ffs resulted in alteration of the 3’ end of the 4.5 S RNA, where in E. coli, alterations in the same region cause defects in membrane protein insertion. Lysogens of ϕEf11 are resistant to the ϕEf11 endolysin. Since endolysin activity is dependent upon binding to cell surface receptors, it is conceivable that defective SRP function results in alteration of the endolysin receptor, preventing endolysin function.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20531-7.

Introduction

ϕEf11 is a temperate bacteriophage that was isolated by induction from a lysogenic Enterococcus faecalis strain (TUSoD11)1. It is a Siphoviridae morphotype bacteriophage with a genome composed of 42,822 bp organized into 65 Open Reading Frames2. The identity of several of the genes has been confirmed by mass spectrometry analysis of the virion structural proteins. These include: ORF 8 (scaffold protein), ORF 10 (major head protein), ORF 15 (major tail protein) and ORF 23 (receptor-binding protein)3. Screening a panel of more than 100 E. faecalis strains using PCR primers specific for ϕEf11 DNA sequences disclosed the presence of the ϕEf11 prophage in 27% of the strains, indicating that lysogeny in E. faecalis due to ϕEf11 is a common occurrence4. Comparing the lysogenic and non-lysogenic E. faecalis strains revealed several distinguishing characteristics. These included differences in growth rates, biofilm formation, and sensitivity to the endolysin encoded by ϕEf114,5. For example, non-lysogenic strains are almost invariably sensitive to lysis by the endolysin, whereas lysogenic strains are almost invariably resistant to the endolysin4. The impact of lysogeny on the endolysin sensitivity of E. faecalis strains is further emphasized by the fact that non-sensitive, lysogenic strains are rendered endolysin-sensitive upon curing the strain of the ϕEf11 prophage. Inversely, lysogenizing an endolysin-sensitive, non-lysogenic strain with ϕEf11 renders it endolysin-resistant4.

Such lysogeny-associated phenotypic modifications (i.e., “lysogenic conversions”) have long been known for traits such as toxigenicity and antigenicity6,7. The underlying genetic mechanisms responsible for these (and other) phenotypic modifications include: (1) the import and expression of novel genetic elements8, and (2) the insertional inactivation of host genes6,9. The latter mechanism (insertional inactivation of a host gene) is dependent upon the site of integration of the prophage into the host chromosome. Insertion of the bacteriophage DNA within a specific host gene may result in interruption and inactivation of that gene.

Site-specific integration by homologous recombination occurs within the identity segment between the phage attP and the bacterial attB10. These target attB sites can be within protein-coding genes11,12, non-coding RNA genes (e.g., tRNA/tmRNA13, and csRNA14,15) or in intergenic regions of the bacterial chromosome16,17. In the present study, we identified the site of integration of the ϕEf11 prophage into the ffs gene (encoding the 4.5 S RNA component of the signal recognition particle) within the E. faecalis chromosome, putative attachment sequences, and propose an explanation for how this relates to the endolysin-sensitivity status of the host cell.

Results

The ϕEf11 prophage integrates into the E. faecalis ffs gene via a homologous 27- nucleotide sequence located within the ffs gene (attBcore) in the bacterial genome and between ORFs 30 (lysM) and 31(int) (attPcore) in the ϕEf11 phage genome

Alignment of genome sequences of a pair of E. faecalis strains, one non-lysogenic and the other lysogenic, harboring a ϕEf11 prophage, revealed a localized region of divergence within the ffs gene (Figs. 1A and B). The divergent region, present in the lysogen (E. faecalis strain TUSoD11) and absent in the non-lysogen (strain JH2-2), can be recognized as the 5’ end of a ϕEf11 prophage (minus the terminal 27 nucleotides) by aligning the sequence present in TUSoD11 with that of ϕEf11 (accession No. GQ452243) (Fig. 1B). Immediately adjacent and upstream to this divergent region is a 27-nucleotide (nt) sequence of identity (ACTAAGCAAGTGCCGCCATGTGTCTGA) that is present in both the genome of the non-lysogenic strain (JH2-2) and the lysogenic strain TUSoD11, as well as in that of ϕEf11 (Fig. 1B). This 27 nt sequence can be recognized as the 5’ terminus of the ϕEf11 prophage in the TUSoD11 lysogen by aligning the TUSoD11 sequence with that of the ϕEf11 (Fig. 1B). Immediately adjacent and 5’ to the 5’ terminus of the prophage in the TUSoD11 lysogen genome is the predicted 52–61 nt 5’ terminus of the E. faecalis ffs gene (Fig. 1B). The coordinates of the ffs gene varied depending on the reference (e.g., the Signal Recognition Particle Database/SRPDB18, sRNA_00094 from dRNA-seq transcriptomics19, EF2205_EF2206 from 5’ and 3’ RACE20, EF_RS10545 from the E. faecalis V583 reference genome NC_00466821 and Rfam analysis19,22 indicated ffs gene lengths of 105, 95, 87, 87 and 85 nts, respectively (Fig. 2). Furthermore, extending the ϕEf11 prophage sequence within the TUSoD11 lysogen from its 5’ terminus to its 3’ terminus, one finds the terminal 28–44 nts of the ffs gene immediately adjacent (Fig. 1C). Thus, in the TUSoD11 lysogen, the ϕEf11 prophage lies within the ffs gene, with as much as 61 nts of the ffs gene lying 5’ to the prophage, and as much as 44 ffs nts lying 3’ to the prophage (Fig. 3A).

Presumptive attB and attP attachment sites are composed of a 27 nt (ACTAAGCAAGTGCCGCCATGTGTCTGA) core and flanking sequences containing potential integrase binding sites

The architecture of the bacterial (attB) and phage (attP) attachment sites involved in the site-specific recombination between host and phage DNA have been described as BOB’ and POP’ tripartite structures, respectively, where “O” is a common, homologous “core” sequence, and B, B’ and P, P’ are the sequences (“arms”) surrounding the common cores of the attB and attP sites, respectively23–28. P and P’ arms of attP sites of several bacteriophages have been shown to be long (78–152 bp), A + T enriched sequences containing integrase/recombinase binding sequences, and direct and inverted repeats23–35. As described below, the proposed ϕEf11 attP P and P’ arms are consistent with these characteristics.

AttP site

The above-mentioned 27 nt 5’ prophage terminus sequence is present in the ϕEf11 genome in the intergenic region between ORF30 (lysM) and ORF31 (int), and within the ffs gene in the non-lysogenic JH2-2 (Figs. 1B, 3B and 4). This localized homologous sequence common to both the genomes of ϕEf11 and E. faecalis JH2-2 satisfies one of the minimum requirements for site-specific recombination, namely DNA: DNA homology10,27,36, and defines the presumptive attPcore and attBcore attachment sites, respectively (Figs. 1B, 4). A second requirement for site-specific recombination is the presence of integrase binding sites proximal to the paired homologous DNA sequences that are to be recombined25,37. In this regard, it can be noticed that the 125 nt sequence immediately adjacent and upstream from the presumptive attPcore sequence in the ϕEf11 genome ​​includes one direct 8 nucleotide repeat and one direct 11 nucleotide repeat (AAAGGGGCAAA) which includes within it, the sequence GGGC, which has been identified as a consensus sequence for integrase/recombinase binding38 (Figs. 3B and 4A). Other features commonly found at site-specific recombination sites include the presence of direct and inverted repeats, and a high A + T composition23,33,39,40. All of these features are present in this 125 nt sequence, with an A + T content of 74% (vs. 66% across the ϕEf11 genome). Similarly, there is a 73 nt sequence immediately adjacent to and downstream from the presumptive ϕEf11 attPcore sequence that features an A + T% of 68.5% and includes one pair each of a 9-nucleotide direct and an inverted repeat, the direct repeat containing another potential integrase/recombinase binding site, AAAGGGGCA (Fig. 4A). As nucleotide sequences immediately adjacent to an attPcore, and having the above-described features (i.e., enriched A + T%, direct and inverted repeats, and recombinase/integrase binding sites) are characteristic of attP arms of the attP site10,23,35,39,40, we suggest that the two attPcore-adjacent sequences described above represent the predicted P and P’ arms of the ϕEf11 attP site (Fig. 4A).

AttB site

In contrast to the P and P’ arms of the attP site, attB sites characteristically possess B and B’ arms that are of shorter length (4–12 bp) and lack features such as direct and inverted repeats, and A + T enrichment, although integrase/recombinase binding sites are typically present23,24,27,28,30,34,35. In the case of E. faecalis JH2-2, immediately adjacent and 5’ to the presumptive attBcore is a 37 nt sequence that contains the potential recombinase/integrase binding site (GGGC) (Fig. 4B), making this a reasonable candidate for the B arm of the attB site. On the other hand, the sequence immediately adjacent and 3’ to the presumptive attBcore displays no discernable integrase-binding sites, although there are 3 pairs of inverted repeats (Figs. 4B). Thus, the extent of the B’ arm cannot be defined solely from sequence data.

Presumptive attL and attR sites are located at the 5’ and 3’ phage/host junctions of the ϕEf11 prophage in the TUSoD11 lysogen

Crossing over between homologous attP and attB core sites, and subsequent insertion of the phage DNA into the host chromosome, results in the localization of hybridized attP and attB core sites at the prophage/host junctions at either end of the prophage. In the lysogen, these hybridized attP and attB core sites (along with associated flanking phage and host sequences) comprise the attL and attR sites. The putative attL and attR regions in TUSoD11 (lysogenized by ϕEf11) could be identified by examining the sequences at the junctions between prophage and host DNA. The 5’ and 3’ junctional sites of the ϕEf11 prophage in lysogenic TUSoD11 are shown in Fig. 1B and C. It can be seen that the presumptive hybridized attP and attB core sequences are indeed present at the 5’ prophage terminus and immediately adjacent to the 3’ terminus of the prophage. Furthermore, the presumptive hybridized attB/P core sequence at the prophage 5’ terminus is flanked by two A + T rich (63% and 68%) sequences containing several direct and inverted repeats, as well as potential integrase binding sites (Fig. 5A). These features are characteristic of the att sites found in the lysogens of other Gram-positive, low GC bacteria such as Lactococcus lactis, and L. gasseri33,39,40. Similarly, the presumptive hybrid attB/P core sequence immediately adjacent to the prophage 3’ terminus is surrounded by two sequences that are eminently rich (75% and 71%) in A + T nts and contain numerous direct and inverted repeats as well as potential integrase binding sites (Fig. 5B). For the reasons previously stated, this is consistent with an att site. Consequently, we suggest that these two junctional regions represent the attL and attR sites, as shown in Fig. 5A and B.

Prophage insertion into the ffs gene is a common feature of lysogenic E. faecalis strains harboring a ϕEf11 prophage

To confirm the insertion site of the ϕEf11 prophage and to examine the incidence of ϕEf11 prophage insertion into the ffs gene among E. faecalis strains, we used PCR primers flanking potential ffs/prophage chromosomal junctions to screen a panel of lysogenic E. faecalis strains. If the ffs gene is interrupted by the insertion of a prophage, it should be possible to produce amplicons from lysogen template DNA using primers flanking the junctions of either the 5’ or 3’ end of the ffs gene and the ϕEf11 genes at either end of the prophage (Fig. 6A). A panel of thirteen E. faecalis strains lysogenized by ϕEf11 was previously assembled by screening strains for the presence of ϕEf11-specific genes by PCR, using ϕEf11-specific primers4. PCR analysis, using template DNA from these thirteen lysogenic E. faecalis strains (all positive for ϕEf11 prophage), and primer sets A (flanking the predicted 5’-ffs/ϕEf11 ORF31 junction of attL) and B (flanking the predicted ORF30/3'-ffs junction of attR)(Fig. 6A), all produced amplicons of the predicted size from all strains tested (Fig. 6C and D, respectively). In contrast, template DNA from none of the control (non-lysogenic) E. faecalis strains tested produced amplicons using the same two primer sets (Fig. 6E and F). Template DNA from these (non-lysogenic) strains did, however, produce amplicons of the predicted size, when used in PCR reactions with primer set C (Fig. 6B), which encompassed the intact ffs gene (Fig. 6E and F). Therefore, it appears that ϕEf11 prophage insertion into the ffs gene is a common occurrence in E. faecalis.

Prophage insertion disrupts and partially reconstitutes the ffs gene

Due to the identity between the attPcore and attBcore sequences, the insertion of the ϕEf11 prophage into the ffs gene results in the homologous replacement of ffs nts 62–88 (SRPDB prediction) by the identical sequence in the prophage (Fig. 1B). The remaining 17 (3’) ffs nts (TTGAATAAGGAACGTAT) are replaced by a non-homologous prophage sequence (ATTATAGTTGATATCAA) (Fig. 1B). Therefore, following prophage integration, the first 88 nts of the ffs gene are unchanged, whereas 12 of the final 17 (3’) nts are altered. Thus, prophage integration results in only a partial reconstitution of the wild-type ffs gene. The resulting differences between the original ffs transcription product, and that produced by the prophage-modified ffs gene are shown in Fig. 7.

Discussion

The import of bacteriophage genes through transduction and subsequent lysogenization can result in phenotypic alterations in the host cell by a process known as “lysogenic conversion”, a term first proposed by Joshua Lederberg42. The first observation of lysogenic conversion came from Frobisher and Brown, who, in 1927, reported that the scarlatinal toxigenic phenotype could be transferred to a non-toxigenic strain by the addition of filtered culture medium (containing released phage) from a toxigenic strain43. A second early example of lysogenic conversion was that of the acquisition of toxigenicity by cells of Corynebacterium diphtheriae. Upon lysogenization by bacteriophage β, non-toxigenic, non-virulent C. diphtheriae strains become toxigenic and virulent44. Later studies demonstrated that the gene for the diphtheria toxin resides in the β bacteriophage DNA45. Subsequently, it has been shown that the genetic determinants of the protein exotoxins responsible for many other diseases are acquired by strains of pathogenic bacteria through lysogenic conversion. These include the genes coding for the toxins responsible for: botulism46, cholera47, scarlet fever48,49, and dysentery50, to name a few.

Another aspect of lysogenic conversion relates to the antigenicity of the lysogenized cell. There are numerous examples of lysogenically-imported genes coding for enzymes that alter cell-surface macromolecules, resulting in antigenic modification of the host cell. The somatic O-antigens of many Gram-negative bacteria, such as Shigella flexneri51, Escherichia coli52, Pseudomonas aeruginosa53, Vibrio cholerae54, Salmonella anatum55, S. cholerae-suis56, S. johannesburgh57, and S. typhimurium58, all become modified by enzymes that are specified by prophage genes. In the case of Salmonella spp., the O-antigen backbone is composed of an α-linked polymer of a mannose-rhamnose-galactose trisaccharide, which is polymerized by the enzyme α-polymerase59. Upon lysogenization, prophage genes code for an α-polymerase repressor, and a β-polymerase, resulting in the termination of α-linked polymer synthesis, and its replacement by a β-linked O-antigen polymer59. In the case of S. anatum, the galactose residue of the mannose-rhamnose-galactose trisaccharide O-antigen polymer is acetylated59. However, upon lysogenization by phage ε15 synthesis of the transacetylase enzyme responsible for the acetylgalactose formation is repressed by a prophage-encoded protein, resulting in a deacetylated polymer60. In contrast, lysogenic conversion of S. typhimurium by phages A3 or A4 results in the O-acetylation of the rhamnose in the repeating trisaccharide unit of the O-antigen polysaccharide chain58. Although less commonly reported in Gram-positive bacteria, lysogenic conversion of cell wall polymers (i.e., wall teichoic acids) in Staphylococcus aureus has also been demonstrated61. In uninfected, non-lysogenic S. aureus cells, a host glycosyltransferase (TarS) catalyzes the transfer of N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) from UDP-GlcNAc to the C4 hydroxyl of the ribose-phosphate wall teichoic acid backbone. In lysogenic S. aureus strains harboring a ϕtarP prophage, a prophage-encoded glycosyltransferase (TarP) catalyzes the transfer of GlcNAc to the hydroxyl of the C3 carbon rather than the C4 carbon, resulting in a modification of the wall teichoic acid61. These (and other) lysogenic conversions leading to cell surface polymer modifications can contribute to host cell fitness in several ways: Conversion of cell-surface polymers can lead to lysogens that no longer react with antibody produced against the original, non-lysogenic strain, thereby evading immune reactivity55,61. Secondly, modification of cell-surface polymers that serve as phage receptors can prevent phage adsorption and protect the host cell against subsequent phage infection52,58. Another fitness-enhancing outcome of lysogenic conversion is the production of biofilms. For many bacteria, including E. coli17, Bacillus anthracis62, and E. faecalis5 lysogeny promotes biofilm formation.

In addition to the import of novel genes via transduction, lysogenic conversion can be brought about by the integration of phage DNA within host cell genes, thereby resulting in their disruption. Such a gene disruption (i.e., insertional mutagenesis) may result in a phenotypic alteration in the host cell due to a potential loss of function or absence of the resulting gene product. tRNA genes are the most common sites for prophage insertion (63, Table 1). In most of these cases, following prophage insertion, the tRNA gene function is preserved due to the presence of a duplicate copy of the displaced portion of the host gene in the integrated prophage6,64,65. In other cases, gene function is not preserved following insertional mutagenesis. For example, lysogenization of Lactococcus lactis ssp. cremoris by phage TP901-1 results in the disruption of an ORF predicted to encode fimbrial proteins33. S. aureus phage L54a inserts into lipase gene geh, rendering the lysogenized cell lipase-negative66. A prophage-like element (vfbin) integrated within the Bacillus weihenstephanensis dipicolinic acid synthetase gene (spoVFB) results in a lack of production of dipicolinic acid (DPA)67. Lysogenic L. monocytogenes strains 10403 S and 1118 harbor prophages ϕ10403S and A118 respectively within their genes for the transcriptional regulator of the competence system, comK, resulting in the lack of expression of the disrupted comK gene. Only after prophage excision (thereby reconstituting an intact comK gene) is a functional comK protein produced11,68,69. In several cases, the gene regulation derived from this reversible chromosomal integration/excision of the prophage provides a fitness value to the host cell. One such beneficial outcome for the host cell due to prophage integration/excision is related to the regulation of the dipicolinic acid synthetase gene (spoVFB) in Bacillus weihenstephanensis, mentioned previously. The spoVFB gene product, DPA, is not found in vegetative cells, however it constitutes 5–15% of the dry weight of bacterial spores70,71. DPA binds calcium, which destabilizes DNA, and promotes dehydration by binding free water. These conditions (dehydration and depletion of free calcium) would be detrimental to vegetative cells. The integration of a prophage-like element (vfbin) solves this problem by the insertional inactivation of the gene (spoVFB) responsible for the synthesis of DPA, thereby preventing DPA production. On the other hand, DPA is required for maintaining spore dormancy and survival72–74. Consequently, under appropriate environmental conditions for spore formation, the (incomplete) prophage-like element (vfbin) intervening within the spoVFB gene is induced, and excised, resulting in the reconstitution of the intact spoVFB gene. The spoVFB gene product (dipicolinic acid synthetase) then synthesizes DPA, which can then be incorporated into the developing spore. As vfbin is an incomplete prophage-like element, no productive/lytic infection occurs in the host cell.

A second example of increased fitness afforded to a host cell by prophage-mediated insertional mutagenesis was alluded to previously regarding the regulation of the L. monocytogenes gene for the transcriptional regulator of the competence system, comK. The competence (com) system is a series of genes coding for proteins that facilitates the intracellular uptake of DNA by bacteria69,75,76. Among the com system components are pseudopilus proteins (ComGC, ComGD, ComGE and ComGG gene products) and a membrane translocation channel protein (ComEC gene product). These (and all other com system) proteins are dispensable during L. monocytogenes extracellular growth. Therefore, expression of the genes in this system during extracellular growth would be a needless waste of cellular resources. However, following phagocytosis and infection of mammalian cells, the escape of L. monocytogenes from the phagosome and subsequent intracellular growth is dependent upon the production of the com system pseudopilus and the translocation channel proteins. As previously mentioned, comK codes for the transcriptional regulator (ComK) for all genes in the com system. Under extracellular growth conditions (when the com system proteins are not required), prophages ϕ10403S or A118 are integrated into comK, disrupting the gene and preventing com system gene expression11,68,69. Upon L. monocytogenes internalization and phagocytosis by a mammalian cell, the prophages are induced and excised, resulting in the reconstitution of the intact comK gene. Expression of the intact comK produces the com system transcriptional regulator (ComK), which then stimulates the production of all the com system proteins, including the pseudopilus and translocation channel proteins, thereby facilitating L. monocytogenes escape from the phagosome and permitting intracellular growth. It should be noted that in the cases of ϕ10403S or A118, prophage induction does not lead to productive phage infection or lysis of the host cell, possibly due to abortive infection by the phage during intracellular growth69. Thus, lysogenic conversion due to insertional mutagenesis of a host cell gene by the integration of a prophage can increase the fitness of the host cell. This mechanism of prophage-mediated regulation of bacterial gene expression is becoming more widely appreciated77. Our results suggest that a similar process may occur in the case of lysogenization of E. faecalis by ϕEf11.

Lysogenic infection of all non-episomal temperate bacteriophages depends on their ability to integrate into their host’s chromosome. With few exceptions, such as bacteriophage Mu and other transposable phages78,79, phage genome integration is the result of a homologous, site-specific recombination process. Site-specific recombination is a multifactorial process that involves homologous attachment sites between the phage and host chromosomes, recombinase/integrase enzymes, and binding sites for integrase attachment to host and phage DNA near the site of strand exchange. For most temperate bacteriophages, the homologous attachment sequence to that of the host attachment site is located in a region of the phage DNA adjacent to the integrase gene (int)36. In the present case, the ϕEf11 attPcore is located 74 nts from the termination of the int gene (Figs. 3B and 4A). In comparison, the attPcore of Lactobacillus delbureckii phage mv4 is located 192 nucleotides from the 3’ end of int80 and the attPcore of Leuconostoc oenos phage ϕ10MC is located 164 nts from the 3’ end of int81.

Our sequence analyses of the genomes of E. faecalis TUSoD11 (a lysogen harboring the ϕEf11 prophage), E. faecalis JH2-2 (a non-lysogenic strain lacking a ϕEf11 prophage), and bacteriophage ϕEf11, localized attB (including the attBcore) to a site within the host ffs gene. This attachment site results in the insertion of the ϕEf11 prophage within the ffs gene of the lysogen. Furthermore, every E. faecalis lysogen (harboring a ϕEf11 prophage) examined (n = 13) also displayed an ffs gene interrupted by a ϕEf11 prophage. Therefore, the ffs gene appears to be the preferred target site of the ϕEf11 phage genome for integration into the host cell chromosome of E. faecalis strains. In contrast, the preponderance of prophage insertion sites of temperate bacteriophages has been found to be within tRNA-coding sequences (Table 1). Alternatively, experimentally validated intragenic, non-tRNA prophage insertion sites include the genes for: β-toxin (hlb), elongation factor (tsf), lipase (geh), sigma factor K (sigK), dipicolinic acid synthetase (spoVFB), comK, and isocitrate dehydrogenase (icd) (Table 1); however, to our knowledge, the present case of E. faecalis bacteriophage ϕEf11 is the first and only experimentally-verified example of a functioning prophage integrating into the ffs gene. The ffs gene was bioinformatically predicted, with high support values, to have been the target of insertion by integrases of uncharacterized genomic islands and prophages in some Streptococcus pneumoniae genomes, but not experimentally validated82.

ϕEf11 prophage insertion and interruption of ffs may have a pronounced effect on the E. faecalis cell phenotype. Unlike the prophage insertion of many other temperate bacteriophages occurring within tRNA genes, where the structure and function of the tRNA gene is preserved by the substitution of a duplicate copy of the tRNA sequence residing in the attP site of the recombining phage DNA10, the final (3’) 17 nts of ffs are not conserved in the ϕEf11 attP site, resulting in an altered ffs gene, and an altered 4.5S RNA product, in a lysogen containing the ϕEf11 prophage (Figs. 1 and 7).

Whether or not the altered sequence in the ffs gene product produced by prophage insertion is sufficient to result in altered function is not yet known; however, intriguing clues can be gleaned from the following observations: the ffs gene product, a 4.5S RNA, is the RNA component of the signal recognition particle (SRP) ribonucleoprotein. A single protein, termed Ffh (for homologue to fifty-four kDa protein of mammalian SRP), comprises the protein component of the SRP83,84. In prokaryotes, the SRP targets specific cytoplasmic proteins and facilitates their translocation into and through the plasma membrane85–87. Thus, proteins whose functions are applied externally to the cell plasma membrane would depend upon the activity of a functional SRP. These may include enzymes involved in the synthesis and assembly of cell wall polymers. Thus, a mutated 4.5S RNA could render the SRP inactive, resulting in the paralysis of transport of some enzymes required for synthesis of certain cell surface polymers. For example, in E. coli, mutations of ffs near the 3’ terminus result in a defect in the insertion of membrane proteins87 (Fig. 7C).

We previously described an endolysin encoded by bacteriophage ϕEf1188. Although the endolysin produces rapid and profound lysis of most E. faecalis populations, there is a subset of E. faecalis strains that is intrinsically resistant to the effects of the endolysin4. Moreover, when these E. faecalis populations were further examined, it was discovered that resistance to the endolysin is positively correlated with the presence of a ϕEf11 prophage: Lysogens harboring a ϕEf11 prophage are nearly uniformly endolysin-resistant while non-lysogens are almost invariably sensitive to the endolysin. Furthermore, curing an endolysin-resistant lysogenic E. faecalis strain of the prophage renders it endolysin-sensitive, whereas lysogenization of an endolysin-sensitive strain renders it endolysin-resistant4.

It is well known that the lytic activity of bacteriophage endolysins is dependent on their enzymatic cleavage action on the bacterial cell wall peptidoglycan as well as their ability to bind to endolysin-specific receptors on cell wall polymers89–92. Endolysin proteins contain a cell binding domain (CBD) that recognizes a specific receptor ligand on the bacterial cell wall. Endolysins rely on this recognition of cell wall polymer receptors in order to target cell walls of specific bacteria. Any alteration in the receptor structure could abrogate target recognition and result in insensitivity to the endolysin. For example, the CBD domain of L. monocytogenes phage P35 endolysin PlyP35 specifically binds to the N-acetylglucosamine-decorated wall teichoic acid (WTA) found in the cell walls of serovar 1/2 and 4 L. monocytogenes cells90,93. Inactivation of the genes necessary for WTA N-acetylglucosamine-decoration resulted in a lack of PlyP35 binding and absence of cell wall degradation93. Similar receptor-dependent specificities have been described for other endolysins such as Cpl-1 from pneumococcal phage Cp-1, which binds to choline moieties of the WTA in the cell wall of pneumococcal cells94–96, and Lyb5 from Lactobacillus fermentum phage PYB5, which binds to a peptidoglycan ligand in the wall of L. fermentum cells97. The assembly of these cell wall polymers/endolysin ligands depends upon the activity of various enzymes that must be transported from their site of synthesis in the cytoplasm, through the plasma membrane and finally to the site of polymer synthesis in the cell wall. This transport would require the functioning of an SRP, composed of the Ffh protein and the 4.5 S RNA product of the ffs gene. As we now know, E. faecalis lysogens harboring a ϕEf11 prophage possess altered ffs genes. If the resulting altered 4.5S RNA ffs product forms a defective SRP, faulty transmembrane protein translocation may ensue. Consequently, enzymes required for certain cell wall polymer assembly reactions may not be available, resulting in altered cell surface polymers and loss of recognition by endolysin CBDs. This mechanism may explain the loss of E. faecalis endolysin sensitivity following lysogenization by bacteriophage ϕEf11.

It is known from studies of the 4.5S RNA from E. coli and Bacillus subtilis that both the 5’ and 3’ ends of the transcript are processed by various cellular endo and exonucleases98–102, resulting in multiple bands on Northern blots. The similar observation of broad sizes in Northern blots coupled with multiple 5’ and 3’ ends observed by 5’ and 3’ RACE20,103 and RNA-seq19 suggest that the E. faecalis 4.5S RNA behaves similarly and must be processed into a mature form to be functional. The substitution of the phage sequence for the 3’ end of ffs may result in the replacement of nuclease recognition sites in the 4.5S RNA and thereby prevent nuclease processing. This may provide a potential mechanism for the formation of a defective SRP following lysogeny by ϕEf11. We predict that the changes resulting from prophage insertion will manifest as incorrect maturation of the 4.5S RNA, rendering it dysfunctional. Despite the evidence presented from prior knowledge of work on model organisms, a limitation of this study is that the mechanism of endolysin resistance in E. faecalis in unknown and will require further study.

The foregoing proposed regulatory system for endolysin recognition could teleologically be considered beneficial from the bacteriophage’s perspective. Upon initiating a lysogenic infection, the maintenance of the prophage would be dependent upon the preservation of the host. Modifying the host in such a manner as to make it insensitive to the bacteriophage’s endolysin would serve to protect and preserve it from inappropriate exposure to the endolysin. Upon induction, the intact ffs would be reconstituted upon prophage excision rendering the cell susceptible to lysis by the endolysin thereby promoting exit and dispersion of progeny virions.

Finally, the lysogenic conversion (i.e., resistance to the endolysin) resulting from ϕEf11 prophage insertion into the E. faecalis ffs gene may have practical implications. The phage endolysin that we previously described may have therapeutic potential in treating multidrug resistant E. faecalis infections, as it has been shown that antibiotic resistance is not related to sensitivity to the endolysin4. In this regard, we recently reported that the endolysin was successfully used to treat a case of chronic bacterial prostatitis caused by an E. faecalis infection that could not be controlled by conventional antibiotics104. The fact that lysogenic E. faecalis strains are intrinsically resistant to the lytic action of the endolysin limits the potential clinical usefulness of the endolysin. This limitation might be overcome if the nature of the compositional/structural modification(s) caused by the lysogenic conversion were elucidated. It might then be possible to develop countermeasures that would render all E. faecalis strains susceptible to the lysin.

Alignment of E. faecalis ffs gene predictions

Predicted gene sequences of E. faecalis ffs were obtained from the Signal Recognition Particle Database (SRPDB)18, dRNA-seq transcriptomics19, 5’ and 3’ RACE20, the E. faecalis V583 reference genome (GenBank: NC_004668)21 and Rfam analysis19,22. The DNA sequences for each gene start and stop were obtained from the genome of E. faecalis JH2-2 (GenBank: NZ_KI518257) and aligned using MUSCLE105 version 3.8.31 using default parameters. The resulting aligned FASTA file was viewed using Seqview106 version 5.0.4 and exported as a formatted text file.

Prediction and confirmation of attP and attB

Both attP and attB were computationally predicted by Phage_Finder41 version 2.5 in strict mode using the command “Phage_Finder_v2.5.pl -t ncbi.out -i phage_finder_info.txt -r tRNAscan.out -n tmRNA_aragorn.out -c ncRNA_cmscan.out -A $i.con -S” where “$i” is a variable holding the name of each genome searched. To confirm these predictions, E. faecalis strain JH2-2 was lysogenized by ϕEf11 as described previously107. The updated Phage_Finder software is available at https://github.com/JCVenterInstitute/Phage_Finder/tree/Phage_Finder_v2.5.

To confirm these predictions, 3 sets of primers were designed and tested using PCR. Based upon sequencing data for E. faecalis strains TUSoD11 (lysogen harboring a ϕEf11 prophage) and JH2-2 (nonlysogen, lacking a ϕEf11 prophage), primer sets were designed to flank the predicted attBP’ ffs/ORF31 and attPB’ ORF30/ffs junctions formed by the prophage insertion into the E. faecalis chromosome. Primer set A consisted of forward primer ffs/ORF31F (5’-GGCTAGGACAATGGCGG-3’) and reverse primer ffs/ORF31Ra (5’-ATTTGTTGGCAAAACGGGCAACCCAATTCA-3’) and was predicted to produce a 436 bp PCR amplicon spanning the junction site between the 5’ portion of the ffs gene and the 5’ end of the ϕEf11 prophage (at ORF31). Primer set B consisted of forward primer ffs/ORF30F (5’-ATTCCTTTACTGTAGAGGTG-3’) and reverse primer ffs/ORF30Ra (5’-ATTCAATCAGACACATGGCGGCA-3’) and was predicted to produce a 442 bp PCR amplicon spanning the junction site between the 3’ end of the ϕEf11 prophage (at ORF30) and the 3’ portion of the ffs gene. In addition, a third primer set, primer set C, was designed to include only the ffs gene in its entirety. Primer set C consisted of forward primer ffsF (5’-TTGCCGAAAGGCTAGGACAA-3’) and reverse primer ffsR (5’-CGTTCCTTATTCAATCAGACACAT-3’) and was predicted to produce a 102 bp amplicon encompassing the intact ffs gene. Template DNA was present in extracts prepared from cultures of each of the E. faecalis strains used in this study as described previously5. Briefly, cells from 3 to 4 ml of Brain Heart Infusion broth cultures of each E. faecalis strain were washed and then resuspended in 1–2 ml of lysis buffer (1% Triton X-100 (v/v), 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.5, 2 mM EDTA). The suspensions were then immersed in a boiling water bath for 10 min. The released DNA was recovered in the supernatant following centrifugation.

Prediction of non-coding RNAs

Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) were predicted in Enterococcus genomes using cmscan, which is part of Infernal108 version 1.1.4. Specifically, a subset of the cmscan database was created consisting of 32 Rfam models of bacterial ncRNAs, consisting of rRNA, leader sequences, ribozymes and riboswitches: Bacteria_small_SRP (RF00169), Bacteria_large_SRP (RF01854), Archaea_SRP (RF01857), Bacteroidales_small_SRP (RF04183), RNaseP_nuc (RF00009), RNaseP_bact_a (RF00010), RNaseP_bact_b (RF00011), RNaseP_arch (RF00373), RNaseP-T (RF02357), 6 S (RF00013), 6 S-Flavo (RF01685), Bacteroidales-1 (RF01693), RtT (RF00391), RprA (RF00034), DsrA (RF00014), 5S_rRNA (RF00001), FMN (RF00050), TPP (RF00059), Cobalamin (RF00174), mir-5429 (RF03820), Thr_leader (RF00506), Trp_leader (RF00513), His_leader (RF00514), SAM (RF00162), Purine (RF00167), SSU_rRNA_bacteria (RF00177), T-box (RF00230), Glycine (RF00504), PreQ1 (RF00522), L10_leader (RF00557), L20_leader (RF00558), L21_leader (RF00559). The database was formatted using the command “cmpress Rfam_PhageFinder.cm”. The Phage_Finder wrapper bash script, phage_finder_v2.5.sh was updated to include the following cmscan command: “cmscan -Z $Z --cut_ga --rfam --nohmmonly --tblout ncRNA_cmscan.out --fmt 2 Rfam_PhageFinder.cm $prefix.con > $prefix.cmscan” where the value of variable $Z was computed with the command “Z=`esl-seqstat $prefix.con | perl -ne ‘chomp; if (/^Total # residues: \s+(\d+)/) {$n = $1; $Z=($n*2)/1000000; print “$Z\n”;}’`” and $prefix is the unique genome identifier.

Structure of the E. faecalis ffs gene locus
