The effects of daily carbon market dynamics on urban air quality in China
Authors: Dong-xiao Yang, Wenxiu Jiang, Cuilian Liu, Minxing Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates whether carbon trading improves air quality by using high-frequency daily data from China’s seven pilot carbon markets and real-time air pollution readings from 206 surrounding monitoring stations. We construct alternative indicators of carbon market liquidity and assess their effects on the Air Quality Index (AQI) using a fixed effects model. The findings show that poor liquidity—reflected by higher illiquidity ratios—is significantly associated with higher AQI values, suggesting that illiquid markets weaken incentives for carbon emission reductions and air quality benefits. Spatial heterogeneity suggests that pilot carbon markets with more efficient allocation generate greater air quality benefits. Temporal heterogeneity indicates that the effect is most pronounced in summer, moderate in winter and spring, and weakest in autumn. Furthermore, the observed improvement in air quality is primarily attributable to reductions in PM2.5. This study offers new evidence on the short-term environmental impacts of carbon trading and provide actionable insights for improving market design and regional policy alignment.

Introduction

Carbon emissions have become an important issue in global governance, as the issues of global climate change and environmental pollution have become increasingly serious. Countries around the world have adopted a range of policy instruments to address the challenges posed by carbon emissions1–3. Among these, the carbon emissions trading system is widely considered to be an important way of reducing carbon emissions and promoting sustainable development, as a market-based approach. It is also an important means of ensuring a balance between Chinese development and its efforts to reduce emissions. Since the Kyoto Protocol promoted international carbon trading, the carbon market has gradually become an important part of global efforts to reduce emissions. As the world’s largest carbon emitter, China is not only faced with an urgent need to meet the dual-carbon target, but it is also facing a twin pressure of economic development and environmental protection4,5. In this context, China launched a pilot program for the sale of carbon offsets in 2011. Regional carbon exchanges have been set up in Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou and other regions. Through its research on carbon quota management and innovative market mechanisms, it has gathered experience in building a national carbon market. However, the introduction of the carbon trading system is not only a profound transformation of the economy and energy landscape, but also has a far-reaching impact on the environmental quality of cities. In particular, the management of air pollution is a key issue for the sustainability of the environment and for the health and wellbeing of the population.

While the primary goal of carbon trading is to mitigate greenhouse gas emissions, its potential to improve air quality through co-pollutant reductions is increasingly recognized. However, there are important differences between Chinese cities in terms of levels of economic development, energy efficiency and industrial characteristics, which are being curbed by the carbon-trading mechanism, which encourages high-emission businesses to optimize their production methods and reduce their pollutant emissions. This has led to a clear variation in implementation outcomes of the carbon emissions trading system among regions. Although existing studies have shown that carbon trading has had a positive impact in terms of improving energy consumption patterns, controlling pollutant emissions and increasing economic benefits6–9. Some experts, however, believe that a carbon-trading system may reduce the economic benefits to regions and reduce overall factor productivity10,11. They are therefore not pro-active in promoting the carbon market.

The widespread use of high-frequency daily data provides a unique research opportunity to study in depth the environmental impacts of the carbon tax. Combining the carbon market’s daily trading data with daily information on urban air pollution monitoring, weather conditions and the intensity of economic activity can provide more accurate information on the short-term dynamic effects of carbon trading policies on air quality. However, while the potential of carbon trading to improve environmental quality has been gradually recognized12,13, research into the specific mechanisms by which it affects air quality is still lacking. This is demonstrated, first, by the lack of a thorough analysis of the complex effects of external factors such as weather conditions and economic activity. This deficiency limits the scientific basis for optimizing policies. On the other hand, researchers have paid more attention to the micro-effects of the carbon trading system at business level while there has been relatively little discussion of the macro-effects on the environment. Exploring how the carbon trading system affects air quality through multi-dimensional mechanisms can not only enrich the relevant theoretical framework, but also provide strong support for improving the carbon trading system design and developing effective pollution control policies in China.

Compared with existing literature, this paper makes the following contributions: First, by employing high-frequency daily data from seven pilot carbon markets, it captures short-term dynamic interactions between trading activity and air quality, addressing the limitations of prior studies that relied on monthly or annual data. Second, This study focuses on the illiquidity ratio and a composite liquidity-activity index to quantify the market’s functioning, offering a more nuanced understanding of how trading efficiency affects environmental outcomes. Third, we explore spatial and seasonal heterogeneity across cities and time, identifying where and when carbon markets are most effective, thereby providing actionable insights for adaptive policy design. Finally, the findings support more integrated environmental governance, positioning carbon trading not only as a climate mitigation tool but also as a viable instrument for co-controlling urban air pollution.

On this basis, the present study investigates the extent to which carbon trading enhances air quality, drawing on high-frequency daily data from China’s seven pilot carbon markets and real-time pollution measurements from 206 proximate monitoring stations. To capture market liquidity, we construct alternative indicators and examine their impact on the Air Quality Index (AQI) through a fixed-effect model. The results indicate that lower liquidity—reflected in higher illiquidity ratios— is significantly associated with elevated AQI values, suggesting that insufficient market liquidity weakens incentives for carbon emission reductions and air quality benefits. Spatial analysis further demonstrates that markets with more efficient allocation mechanisms achieve greater improvements in air quality. Regarding seasonal patterns, the effect is most pronounced in summer, moderate in winter and spring, and least discernible in autumn. Moreover, the improvements in air quality attributable to market liquidity are primarily driven by reductions in PM2.5 concentrations. The findings provide new empirical evidence on the short-term environmental impacts of carbon trading and offer policy-relevant guidance for optimizing market structures and strengthening regional coordination.

The reminder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 develops the research hypotheses. Section 3 outlines the empirical strategy, including model specification, data sources and processing procedures, variable construction, and descriptive statistics. Section 4 reports the main empirical results, accompanied by a variety of robustness tests. Section 5 conducts heterogeneity analyses from spatial, temporal, and pollutants perspectives. The final section concludes.

Research hypotheses

Carbon market liquidity and regional air quality

Enhancing the liquidity of the carbon market can effectively facilitate carbon emission reductions by strengthening price discovery and information transmission in allowance trading, thereby reducing transaction costs and price uncertainty, and reinforcing firms’ expectations regarding emission reduction. In particular, greater market liquidity may diminish information asymmetries and improve the price discovery function14–16, thereby rendering carbon prices more reflective of the true marginal mitigation costs faced by firms. This, in turn, enables companies to more accurately evaluate the costs and benefits of reducing emissions, delivers clearer mitigation signals, and facilitates the optimization of investment decisions related to emissions reduction17,18. In parallel, increased trading depth enhances the credibility of price signals for emission allowances, encouraging firms to internalize the cost of carbon and incorporate long-term abatement investments into production strategies19. Furthermore, improved liquidity stabilizes trading during the compliance cycles of China’s pilot carbon markets, alleviating market rigidity stemming from the tendency to prioritize compliance over active trading. This affords firms greater flexibility to adjust emission behaviors through market mechanisms. Empirical evidence from regional pilot programs corroborates this effect, indicating that sectors with higher market liquidity exhibit significantly lower carbon emissions per unit of output20,21.

Second, increased carbon market liquidity can promote industrial structural transformation through a market-driven redistribution mechanism. Specifically, active trading in emission allowances facilitates the reallocation of permits to firms with higher resource efficiency and more advanced technologies, whereas those with high emissions and low efficiency incur higher compliance costs. For example, during periods of heightened liquidity in the EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS), allowance trading enabled firms with lower abatement costs to acquire additional permits, thereby concentrating resources within these more efficient firms and substantially enhancing technological efficiency22,23. This market-based selection process gradually reshapes regional industrial structures by fostering the modernization or phasing-out of high-carbon, high-pollution industries, thus freeing capacity for the growth of low-carbon and green sectors. These structural shifts, in turn, play a significant role in reducing carbon emissions.

Finally, carbon emissions share a substantial common origin with air pollutants24–26, particularly in the context of fossil fuel combustion, during which CO₂ is typically co-emitted with sulfur dioxide (SO₂), nitrogen oxides (NOₓ), and particulate matter (PMx). A highly liquid carbon market is conducive to sustaining a stable, upward-trending carbon price, thereby incentivizing firms to systematically reduce their dependence on fossil fuels. This process not only advances carbon mitigation but also drives the co-reduction of air pollutant emissions27, ultimately contributing to improved air quality.

Based on the analysis above, this study proposes the first hypothesis H1:

H1

Provided that other conditions remain unchanged, improving trading liquidity in pilot carbon markets enhances regional air quality.

Spatial heterogeneity of carbon trading

The air quality improvement effect of carbon market liquidity is fundamentally rooted in the market’s resource allocation function. In carbon markets with high allocation efficiency, sufficient liquidity enables carbon prices to accurately reflect abatement costs and facilitates the optimal distribution of mitigation resources across firms and sectors19,20. Such markets are characterized by effective price discovery mechanisms, transparent information disclosure, and well-structured trading rules. Under these conditions, increased liquidity enhances the accuracy of price signals and strengthens incentives for emissions reductions19,20. Moreover, higher liquidity reduces transaction costs and price volatility, thereby increasing firms’ willingness to invest in abatement through carbon trading19,28, ultimately leading to measurable improvements in air quality.

By contrast, in carbon markets with lower allocation efficiency, institutional weaknesses, such as flawed market design, high information asymmetry, and underdeveloped trading mechanisms, impede the effective transformation of liquidity into environmental benefits16,29,30. Even when liquidity improves, the optimal allocation of mitigation resources remains difficult due to distorted price signals, market fragmentation, and irrational participant behavior. Under such conditions, increased liquidity exerts only a limited influence on firms’ abatement decisions, and its overall impact on air quality is correspondingly marginal.

H2

There is substantial spatial heterogeneity in the air quality benefits of carbon market liquidity, with more pronounced effects in markets exhibiting higher allocation efficiency.

Temporal heterogeneity of carbon trading

China’s seven pilot carbon markets generally set their compliance periods between June and July, which coincide with the peak electricity consumption season (June–August). The pilot carbon markets in Guangdong and Chongqing set their annual compliance deadlines on June 20; those in Shenzhen, Tianjin, and Shanghai, on June 30; Beijing, on June 15; and Hubei, on the last working day of May. On one hand, the compliance pressure forces firms to surrender carbon quotas within a concentrated timeframe, significantly boosting market liquidity and facilitating effective carbon price discovery. This mechanism enhances firms’ emission reduction incentives19,31. On the other hand, summer marks the peak period for electricity demand in China, during which major carbon emitters—such as thermal power plants and energy-intensive industries—exhibit heightened sensitivity to carbon price fluctuations23. When market liquidity is robust, carbon prices can effectively transmit emission reduction signals. Consequently, we hypothesize that air quality improvements driven by enhanced carbon market liquidity will be most evident during energy peak consumption period and the highest compliance pressure in summer.

In winter (December–February), centralized heating systems in northern China and large-scale cogeneration plants fall under carbon market regulation32. These entities tend to respond to active carbon trading by adopting technological upgrades or fuel substitutions in response to carbon price signals, thereby reducing pollution emissions. Additionally, frequent temperature inversions during winter lower the atmospheric boundary layer height and hinder pollutant dispersion33–35, thereby intensifying the marginal air quality gains from emission reductions. Thus, we predict that the marginal improvement in air quality from increased carbon market liquidity will also be considerable during winter’s heating peak, particularly under adverse dispersion conditions. Spring (March–May) features moderate atmospheric pollution dispersion conditions in China36,37. Both heightened trading activity and moderate dispersion contribute to observable marginal emission reduction effects, and enhanced carbon market liquidity is expected to have a meaningful positive impact on air quality.

Conversely, in autumn (September–November), China experiences mild temperatures. Atmospheric dispersion conditions (such as wind speed and boundary layer height) are optimal, facilitating efficient pollutant dilution and dispersion38,39. These favorable conditions inherently reduce air quality sensitivity to carbon emission reductions. Hence, we anticipate that increased carbon market liquidity will exert only a limited impact on air quality improvement during this period.

Based on the above theoretical considerations, we propose the following hypothesis H3:

H3

The air quality improvement effects of liquidity in China’s pilot carbon markets exhibit seasonal heterogeneity, with the most pronounced impact in summer, moderate effects in winter and spring, and the weakest effect in autumn.

Data sources and processing

The sample covers China’s seven pilot carbon markets: Beijing, Tianjin, Shanghai, Hubei, Guangdong, Shenzhen, and Chongqing. Two regional carbon markets, Sichuan and Fujian, are excluded because of their relatively short operational histories and significant differences from the pilot markets in institutional design, market maturity, trading activity, and scale. Additionally, China’s national carbon market is excluded. Unlike the regional pilots, it covers enterprises across all provinces, making it challenging to accurately assess its liquidity effects on air quality at the city level.

The daily data of carbon trading price and volume in this study are sourced from the seven pilot carbon markets located in seven cities (Beijing, Tianjin, Shanghai, Wuhan, Guangzhou, Shenzhen, and Chongqing), covering the period between January 4, 2022 and July 2, 2024. The daily data of air quality and meteorological conditions for the seven cities, including wind speed, maximum temperature, and minimum temperature, are obtained from the China Meteorological Data Service Centre. The daily Amap congestion index for each city is collected from the GaoDe Traffic website, while subway passenger flow data is collected from the operational databases of the respective municipal subway systems. City-level macroeconomic indicators, including GDP, secondary industry share, total population, administrative area, green space area, and pollution treatment capacity, are drawn from official provincial statistical yearbooks.

To mitigate the impact of missing and extreme values on the results, this study adopts the following data processing procedures: (1) Observations corresponding to dates without trading activity were removed. (2) Trading days with zero volume were excluded to ensure data validity. (3) To reduce the influence of extreme values, 1 st and 99th percentile winsorization was applied. Following these procedures, the final dataset comprises 2,616 observations.

Variable selection and definition

Dependent variable

The air quality index (AQI) is chosen as the dependent variable to measure the impact of changes in carbon market liquidity on the level of environmental pollution. As a comprehensive environmental indicator, the AQI reflects the concentration status of various important pollutants such as PM₅, PM₂, O₃, SO₂, etc. and may offer a more holistic view of the overall trend in urban air quality. Given the synergies in emissions between carbon emissions and conventional pollutants, a carbon market should simultaneously improve air quality by reducing emissions. It is therefore reasonable to choose AQI as a proxy variable for the pollutant content. Moreover, the AQI data are from authoritative sources, are updated frequently and have good spatial and temporal coverage and reliability, which contributes to the representativeness and reliability of the results of the empirical analysis in this study.

Independent variable

The illiquidity ratio (illiquidity_ratio) of the carbon market is selected as the primary independent variable to capture the effect of trading activity on air quality. A higher illiquidity ratio reflects lower market liquidity. In theory, elevated illiquidity ratios indicate reduced market activity and weaker price signals, which in turn may diminish the carbon market’s impact on air quality improvement. Control variables

This study controls for a set of city-level variables that may influence air quality to improve causal identification in estimating the effect of carbon market liquidity. Specifically, meteorological variables include daily maximum (high_t) and minimum (low_t) temperatures, which capture the impact of high temperatures on photochemical reactions and the role of nighttime inversion layers in pollution accumulation41, as well as daily wind power (wind_power), which reflects the capacity for horizontal pollutant dispersion42,43. Urban operational variables consist of daily subway passenger flow (PF), which serves as a proxy for residents’ travel intensity and daily economic activity, and the Amap congestion index (GD), which captures short-term traffic-related emissions affecting air quality.

At the macro level, per capita GDP (lngdp) captures both the pollution arising from economic activity and a city’s capacity for environmental mitigation. Population density (lnpd), a primary driver of energy consumption, represents the baseline intensity of emissions. Pollution treatment capacity (lner), proxied by the urban wastewater treatment rate, reflects environmental resource allocation and the strength of pollution control efforts, both of which contribute directly to air quality improvement44. The share of secondary industry output (lnsecond) indicates industrial pollution intensity, while per capita green space area (lngreen) measures the role of vegetation in absorbing pollutants45. Results and analysis

Benchmark regression results

The regression results are shown in Table 3. Column (1) only controls the city fixed effect (City FE) and daily fixed effect (Daily FE), column (2) further controls the temperature and wind factors based on column (1), and column (3) controls all control variables. The regression results show that the key variable illiquidity ratio has a positive and very significant effect (p < 0.01) in all three models. In particular, in a model (column 3) in which all control variables are introduced, the AQI increases by an average of 0.139 points for one-point increase in the illiquidity ratio. The regression results indicate that increased carbon market liquidity contributes to improved air quality in the cities where carbon trading takes place, suggesting that greater liquidity enhances carbon pricing signals and encourages firms to optimize their emissions. This study further argues that strengthening market mechanisms, promoting institutional participation, and increasing transparency in compliance processes can stimulate trading activity and thereby realize the environmental synergies of the carbon market.

As regards control variables, meteorological factors have a significant influence on air quality and the trend is in line with theoretical expectations The daily maximum temperature has a significant positive impact on the AQI (3.717, p < 0.01), which may be related to the generation of secondary pollutants such as ozone caused by high temperatures; the minimum temperature is negatively correlated with the AQI (−1.962, p < 0.1), suggesting that during periods of low temperature, the air mobility is enhanced or the pollution emissions are reduced; and the increase in wind strength significantly improves air quality (the coefficient is −3.717, p < 0.01), indicating that the atmospheric diffusion capacity plays an important role in pollution control. Although the coefficients of the GD and the PF are negative, they are not statistically significant. This may be because their changes have a relatively small impact on the total air pollution level on a daily basis, or it may be that there are more complicated non-linear mechanisms at work. The coefficient for lngdp is significantly positive, indicating a negative association between economic development and air quality at the current stage. From a policy perspective, integrating economic growth trajectories with urban greening targets can facilitate effective synergy between the carbon market and environmental governance.

Overall, the model’s fit quality is steadily improving with the introduction of control variables (Adjusted R² increasing from 0.308 to 0.424), which indicates that the model’s explanatory power is increasing and that the effects of the control variables on air quality are reasonable and efficient.

Alternative independent variable

Analysis of heterogeneity

Spatial heterogeneity

This study further investigates the spatial heterogeneity in the relationship between carbon market liquidity and air quality. The carbon market essentially uses market mechanisms to optimize the distribution of carbon emission rights. Therefore, the market distribution efficiency may have an impact on air quality. Since the China’s seven pilot carbon markets exhibit substantial heterogeneities in market distribution efficiency, such structural differences are likely to result in region-specific impacts of carbon trading on air quality. In addition, as a supplementary indicator, market sensitivity can also characterize market distribution efficiency. This spatial heterogeneity analysis facilitates a more comprehensive understanding of how market distribution mechanisms influence the environmental effectiveness of carbon trading across diverse regional contexts.

Table 6 presents the regression results for the spatial heterogeneity analysis. Columns (1) and (2) report estimations based on the grouping of carbon markets by allocation efficiency, where High-effi. denotes the group with high allocation efficiency and Low-effi. refers to the low efficiency group. Columns (3) and (4) report results based on carbon market sensitivity, where High-sens. indicates the high sensitivity group and Low-sens. the low sensitivity group. Following the existing study46, this study assigns the pilot markets of Hubei, Shenzhen, Guangdong, and Beijing to the High-effi. group, while the remaining markets are classified as the Low-effi. group. Similarly, the pilot markets of Hubei, Shenzhen, and Beijing are assigned as the High-sens. group, with the others as the Low-sens. group.

From the perspective of carbon market allocation efficiency, the coefficient of the illiquidity ratio for the high-efficiency group (Column (1)) is 0.122 and statistically significant at the 5% level. Conversely, the coefficient for the low-efficiency group (Column (2)) is −0.408, though this result is not statistically significant. These findings suggest that in markets characterized by higher allocation efficiency, enhanced trading liquidity—reflected by a decline in the illiquidity ratio—is significantly associated with improvements in air quality, as evidenced by a reduction in the AQI. Specifically, every one-point increase in the Liquidity ratio is associated with an average decrease of 0.122 points in the AQI. By contrast, the absence of a statistically significant relationship in low-efficiency markets may indicate that institutional frameworks and operational mechanisms in these regions remain insufficiently developed, thereby constraining the capacity of carbon trading to contribute meaningfully to atmospheric environmental governance.

In terms of the market sensitivity, the empirical results are similar to those above. The coefficient of the illiquidity ratio for the high-sensitivity group (column (3)) is 0.115 and is significant at the 5% level, while the coefficient for the low-sensitivity group (column (4)) is −0.735 but also insignificant. This finding suggests that in carbon markets with more sensitive trading, sufficient liquidity can more effectively promote carbon emission reduction and air quality improvement. For every one-point increase in the liquidity ratio, the AQI index decreases by an average of 0.115 points.

The above empirical findings on spatial heterogeneity underscore the need for regulatory authorities to strengthen the allocation capacity and trading sensitivity of carbon markets. This can be achieved by improving institutional design, optimizing trading mechanisms, and enhancing the participation incentives of market actors, thereby maximizing the positive impact of trading liquidity on air quality. Particular attention should be given to pilot markets characterized by weaker allocation capacity and lower sensitivity, which require more targeted policy interventions and structural reforms to enhance their effectiveness in environmental governance.

Temporal heterogeneity

This study adopts the four-season classification commonly used in China and partitions the sample into four seasonal subsamples: spring (March to May), summer (June to August), autumn (September to November), and winter (December to February). By examining the impact of carbon market illiquidity on AQI at each season, the analysis aims to provide a more comprehensive assessment of the effectiveness of the carbon market mechanism in different climate conditions. This approach will allow a better understanding of whether and how the environmental regulatory function of carbon trading varies from one season to another, and thus provide insight into the time-resilience and adaptability of market-based environmental governance tools.

The results of the temporal heterogeneity analyses are reported in Table 7. The estimated coefficients of the illiquidity_ratio are statistically significant in spring (Column (1)), summer (Column (2)), and winter (Column (4)), whereas the coefficient in autumn (Column (3)) is not statistically significant. These findings suggest that the effectiveness of carbon market liquidity in contributing to air quality governance varies across seasons, potentially reflecting seasonal differences in emission intensity and atmospheric diffusion conditions.

Summer demonstrates the most pronounced environmental effect of carbon market liquidity, with an illiquidity_ratio coefficient of 0.152, which is statistically significant at the 5% level. This effect is primarily attributable to the sharp increase in industrial electricity demand and the substantial rise in thermal power generation caused by elevated summer temperatures. In addition, the obligation to comply concentrates firms’ surrender of carbon allowances within a compliance period in summer, thereby markedly increasing market liquidity and enabling more effective carbon price formation. In turn, the resulting price signals strengthen firms’ incentives to reduce emissions. Under these conditions, active carbon trading more effectively incentivizes emission reductions in the power sector and energy-intensive industries, thereby producing a more substantial marginal improvement in air quality. In comparison, the illiquidity_ratio coefficient in spring is 0.083, and statistically significant at the 1% level. Although the magnitude is smaller, the effect remains economically meaningful, indicating that carbon market liquidity during spring can effectively guide firms to invest in emission reduction and technological upgrading, ultimately contributing to improved air quality.

The results for autumn and winter show different characteristics and formation mechanisms. The illiquidity_ratio coefficient for autumn is 0.177, but it does not reach statistical significance. This may be related to the relatively mild and stable autumn climate and pollutant diffusion environment. Autumn typically has moderate winds and relatively stable atmospheric stratification, and pollutant generation and diffusion are in a relatively balanced state. Therefore, the immediate impact of changes in carbon market liquidity on air quality is relatively weak. The winter illiquidity_ratio coefficient is 0.085 and is significant at the 1% level, reflecting the important role of carbon market liquidity during heating season. Winter is often the season of most severe air pollution in China due to the demand for centralized heating and unfavorable atmospheric diffusion conditions. Sufficient carbon market liquidity can encourage heating companies to adopt clean energy alternatives and improve energy efficiency, thereby alleviating the deterioration of winter air quality to a certain extent.

Pollutants heterogeneity

This paper further investigates the heterogeneity in the effects of carbon market trading liquidity on various types of air pollutants, with the results presented in Table 8. For comparisons, column (1) adopts the AQI index as the dependent variable, whereas columns (2) through (5) employ the concentrations of PM2.5, NO₂, SO₂, and CO, respectively, as dependent variables. The results indicate that the coefficient for liquidity_ratio in column (2) is 0.029 and statistically significant at the 5% level. In contrast, although the coefficients for the other pollutants are positive—albeit marginal—they are statistically insignificant. These findings suggest that the improvement in air quality driven by carbon market liquidity is primarily attributable to the reduction of PM2.5, while the contribution of other air pollutants is limited.

Possible explanations for these results are summarized below. PM2.5 consists of primary particles emitted directly and secondary particles formed through atmospheric chemical reactions. Major industries regulated by carbon trading, including electricity, steel, and petrochemicals, are the main sources of PM2.5 emissions. In contrast, NO₂ mainly originates from vehicle exhaust and small to medium combustion sources, which fall outside the scope of the carbon market. The insignificant coefficient for SO₂ likely reflects the widespread adoption of desulfurization technologies in coal-fired power plants and the strict enforcement of environmental regulations in China, both of which have substantially reduced SO₂ emissions. As a result, increased carbon market liquidity yields limited marginal improvements. Lastly, CO is primarily generated through incomplete fuel combustion, a process essentially distinct from the CO₂ emissions targeted by carbon trading.

The heterogeneous effects of carbon trading provide empirical support for refining market design. Specifically, its limited impact on SO₂, NO₂, and CO emissions highlights the need to broaden regulatory coverage beyond current sectors. By contrast, given the substantial public health risks of PM2.5 and the demonstrated effectiveness of carbon trading in reducing its emissions, policy efforts should prioritize liquidity enhancement to strengthen co-benefits within air quality governance.

Conclusions

This study assesses the contribution of carbon trading to air quality improvement using high-frequency daily data from China’s seven pilot carbon markets, together with real-time pollution readings from 206 surrounding monitoring stations. The empirical findings show that carbon market illiquidity significantly impedes air quality improvement, with each one-point increase in the illiquidity ratio corresponding to about a 0.13-point deterioration in the Air Quality Index across model specifications. The results remain robust across a variety of empirical tests, including an alternative specification of market liquidity, alternative estimation approaches. Furthermore, the observed improvement in air quality associated with market liquidity is primarily attributable to reductions in PM2.5. The evidence demonstrates that enhanced carbon market liquidity strengthens both carbon emission reductions and air quality co-benefits.

The spatial heterogeneity analysis suggests that pilot carbon markets characterized by more efficient allocation deliver greater air quality benefits. In high-efficiency carbon markets, such as Hubei, Shenzhen, Guangdong, and Beijing, enhanced trading liquidity significantly contributes to air quality improvement. However, this beneficial relationship is weak in low-efficiency markets, such as Shanghai, Tianjin, and Chongqing, in which underdeveloped institutional frameworks and operational mechanisms impede the capacity of carbon trading to effectively support atmospheric governance.

The seasonal heterogeneity analysis demonstrates that carbon market liquidity achieves maximum environmental impact during summer due to peak energy consumption period and the highest compliance pressure, while spring and winter show moderate through emission reduction incentives and clean energy adoption during heating seasons. Autumn exhibits the weakest effects, reflecting stable climatic conditions that minimize the immediate environmental benefits of carbon trading liquidity.

Based on the above research findings, the following policy recommendations are presented in this study. First, liquidity-enhancing reforms—such as refining quota allocation mechanisms, introducing market makers, and expanding financial instruments—should be prioritized in industrial cities like Wuhan, where our findings show that improved trading activity has a significant and measurable impact on air quality. Targeted support in these high-emission regions can maximize the environmental co-benefits of carbon markets.

Second, the integration of carbon markets with local air quality management should be strengthened, especially in areas with overlapping sources of CO₂ and conventional air pollutants. Policymakers should incorporate co-reduction targets into the carbon market framework to amplify synergies.

Third, regional policy differentiation should be preserved as the national carbon market is scaled up. In service-oriented or low-carbon-transition cities such as Beijing and Shanghai—where marginal benefits of trading liquidity are limited—policy efforts may instead focus on price stability, compliance efficiency, or complementary regulatory tools.

Fourth, seasonal heterogeneity should inform market operations. Given that liquidity effects are most pronounced in spring and summer, these seasons may offer a window of opportunity for price-driven emission reductions. Conversely, in winter, rigid administrative controls may be more effective due to structural pollution sources such as coal-based heating. A hybrid approach aligning seasonal governance with market signals is recommended.

Finally, high-frequency transaction and pollution data should be more systematically disclosed. Our research demonstrates the value of such data in evaluating real-time environmental impacts, and greater transparency will enhance both public oversight and academic research.
