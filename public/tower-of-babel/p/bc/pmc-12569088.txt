Comparative study of unbalanced mining disaster risk level prediction based on artificial intelligence algorithms
Authors: Zhang Bin, Feng Qian, Li Moxiao, Zhang Jianhui, Ma Tianjiao, Liang Guanhua, Han Mingdan, Qiu Shaofeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Predicting mining disaster risk levels is a critical component of intelligent mining systems. This study utilizes five common mining disaster datasets to predict various risk levels. By analyzing correlation coefficients and feature importance for each dataset, optimal evaluation indicators are identified. The Shapley Additive Explanations model is then applied to enhance interpretability. To address the presence of outliers and imbalanced data categories, the Mahalanobis Distance Discriminant Method and the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique algorithm based on Tomek Links are used for data preprocessing. Subsequently, Support Vector Machine, Random Forest, Extreme Gradient Boosting, one-dimensional Convolutional Neural Networks, and multi-Grained Cascade Forest algorithms are applied to the five mining disaster datasets. Comparative analysis reveals that the Deep Forest algorithm demonstrates superior performance and generalization in predicting stability levels of goaf, slope stability, rockburst intensity levels, pillar stability, and Hanging Wall stability, with prediction accuracies of 92.31%, 96.77%, 92.50%, 91.67%, and 95.00%, respectively. This research provides a systematic solution for mining disaster classification prediction, offering technical support and a scientific theoretical basis for intelligent mining development and mining safety operations.

Introduction

In the process of underground mining, complex geological conditions pose various threats, including water hazards1, explosions2, dust hazards3, hanging wall instability4, and slope instability5. Deep mining areas also face multiple challenges, such as rock bursts6, unstable pillars7, and instability of goaf8. These disasters not only cause delays and enormous economic losses but also endanger the lives of mining personnel, presenting severe challenges to underground engineering operations and safety9,10. Therefore, predicting mining disasters is an urgent problem. Recent advancements in modern information technologies such as the internet, big data, and artificial intelligence have ushered in a new era of intelligent mining. One of the core modules of intelligent mining is the prediction of mining disasters11,12. To meet the essential needs of mining, scholars have focused on cloud computing and big data technologies, establishing a cloud platform based on mining disaster monitoring. By combining on-site monitoring and numerical simulation, they have developed a mining disaster early warning cloud platform, generating a substantial amount of mining disaster data10,13–15. However, most current cloud platforms are limited to data visualization or basic disaster forecasting, and fail to fully achieve accurate mining disaster prediction. With increasing demands for predictive performance, leveraging the valuable mining disaster data remains an urgent issue.

In recent years, with the continuous development of artificial intelligence, numerous scholars have employed machine learning models to mine mining disaster data and predict individual mining disasters, achieving fruitful results. For instance, Yiguo Xue et al. predicted mining rock bursts using the Extreme Learning Machine (ELM) to forecast and classify rock burst intensity. They also utilized Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) to optimize the input weight matrix and hidden layer bias in the ELM model. The results showed that the PSO-ELM model exhibited favorable performance in predicting rock burst levels16. Chongchong Qi et al. addressed the prediction of hanging wall (HW) stability on open-pit mining benches and applied the Random Forest (RF) algorithm to predict HW stability. The optimized RF model achieved AUC values of 0.884 and 0.873 on the training and test sets, respectively, indicating its good predictive performance for HW stability17. Mingliang Li et al. proposed an improved Particle Swarm Optimization Algorithm and optimized RF algorithm to reduce disasters and accidents caused by slope instability, yielding promising results18. In addition, with the continuous advancement of machine learning in recent years, some researchers have employed gradient boosting algorithms to predict rock bursts in underground engineering structures19, forecast fire hazard levels in underground projects20, and develop intelligent decision-driven models for predicting air quality indices in open-pit mining21.

However, due to the specific nature of mining, a single mine may encounter multiple types of disasters, even compound disasters10. The aforementioned researchers have primarily focused on predicting single types of disasters. Currently, there is a lack of studies that approach the prediction of multiple mining disaster risk levels from a data-driven perspective. Furthermore, there remains significant room for improvement in the algorithms used for disaster risk level prediction. Enhancing the interpretability of evaluation metrics is equally critical, yet few scholars have applied such advancements to the field of mining disasters. Different types of mining disasters are characterized by fundamentally distinct failure mechanisms. However, from a data-structural perspective, the classification tasks associated with mining disasters, such as predicting rock burst intensity, slope stability grades, and pillar stability grades, demonstrate significant structural similarities. With the continuous advancement of machine learning and deep learning, an increasing number of algorithms demonstrate sufficient generalization capabilities to handle disaster data with similar structures. Additionally, as various smart mines are being constructed at an accelerated pace, some mines have already implemented disaster monitoring and prediction modules. In the process of daily disaster monitoring and prediction, if each system corresponds to a specific mining disaster prediction, it could lead to system redundancy and pose significant challenges for both hardware and software. In conclusion, it is necessary to develop a comprehensive approach with high generalization, fast response speed, high accuracy, and encompassing multiple modules such as data processing, indicator analysis, grading prediction, and parameter optimization to predict multiple mining disasters effectively.

This study employs various methods, including literature review and field investigations, to collect data on different types of mining disasters, which are organized into a comprehensive database. Correlation analysis and importance analysis are used to select evaluation indicators for each disaster. The Mahalanobis distance discrimination method and an improved Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) algorithm are applied to preprocess the data in the database. Machine learning and deep learning algorithms are then utilized to perform hierarchical prediction for different types of mining disasters. Through comparative analysis, a comprehensive analysis method for predicting multiple types of mining disasters is established. This method enables rapid and accurate assessment and prediction of hazards related to mined-out area instability, rock burst intensity, slope instability, pillar collapse, and hanging wall instability, among other mining disasters, effectively predicting mining disasters.

The rest of this paper is structured as follows: Sect. 2 provides an overview of the machine learning methods employed in this study, along with a brief introduction to the PSO optimization algorithm. Section 3 offers a detailed explanation of the construction of the mining disaster dataset used in this research. Section 4 focuses on the analysis of dataset indicators, the data processing workflow, and the development of the predictive model. Section 5 presents a comprehensive comparative analysis of the model’s prediction results. Finally, Sect. 6 concludes the paper and discusses prospects for future work.

Methodology

Machine learning algorithms

In this paper, we have selected typical machine learning algorithms, namely Support Vector Machine (SVM)22, RF23, Extremely Randomized Trees (ET)24,25, and several classical deep neural networks such as Backpropagation Neural Network (BP), Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)26, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)27, and the novel Deep Forest algorithm. The rationale for choosing these methods can be summarized in two key points: (a) the selected algorithms and neural networks have been widely applied in the field of engineering and demonstrate good predictive performance25, and (b) mining disaster level prediction involves a multidimensional nonlinear computation problem. The aforementioned machine learning algorithms can leverage multiple input variables and learn nonlinear relationships, making them suitable for mining disaster risk classification prediction.

multi-Grained Cascade forest (GCForest) is a novel tree-based model proposed by Professor Zhi-Hua Zhou and Dr. Ji Feng in their published paper titled “Deep Forest: Towards An Alternative to Deep Neural Networks“28. This model presents a promising alternative to deep neural networks, demonstrating comparable performance. It consists of two main components: Cascade Forest and Multi-scale Scanning, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Cascade Forest. The deep forest algorithm employs a hierarchical processing strategy by constructing a cascade structure composed of multiple random forests. Each layer’s random forests learn feature information from the input feature vectors and pass the processed information to the next layer. To enhance the model’s generalization capability, each cascade layer uses two types of forest algorithms as base learners: the completely random forest and the standard random forest. To prevent overfitting during the layer-by-layer transfer, the cascade layers concatenate the transformed feature vectors with the original feature vectors to form the training data for the next layer. Additionally, the deep forest module utilizes cross-validation to adaptively adjust the number of cascade layers, which also defines the model’s depth.Multi-scale Scanning. Assuming a sliding window size of s, the original M-dimensional feature vector is transformed into an (M−s+1)-dimensional feature vector using a sliding window of size s. Subsequently, both random forest and completely random forest are used for feature transformation to obtain class distribution vectors. Finally, these class distribution vectors are concatenated to form enhanced feature vectors. This process is repeated to generate multiple enhanced feature vectors. Therefore, the overall process of the Deep Forest algorithm, as shown in Fig. 1, can be summarized into the following two steps: (1) Preprocess the input features using multi-scale scanning. (2) Input the obtained feature vectors into the cascade forest for training. Fig. 1Flow chart of deep forest algorithm.

Introduction to SHAP

Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) is an additive explanation model constructed by Lundberg in 2017, inspired by cooperative game theory32, Its core is to calculate the SHAP values for each feature, reflecting the contribution of each feature to the predictive capability of the entire model. The schematic diagram is shown in Fig. 3. In traditional machine learning, Feature Importance can intuitively indicate the importance of features, but its implementation process resembles a “black box,” making it difficult to determine the relationship between features and the final predictions. However, SHAP values can reveal the impact of each feature on the final prediction and can demonstrate the direction of the effect, thus enhancing the model’s interpretability. SHAP interprets the model’s predictions as the sum of the attribution values (SHAP Values) for each input feature, that is:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}\widehat{y}={f}_{0}+{\sum\:}_{i=1}^{M}{f}_{i}\end{array}$$\end{document}

In the formula, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{\text{y}}$$\end{document} represents the model′s predicted value, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{i}$$\end{document} corresponds to the attribution value of each feature, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{0}$$\end{document} is the mean prediction of all training samples.

Establishment of database

Through literature review and on-site investigation, a dataset containing five common mining risk sources, namely goaf, slope, rockburst level, mining pillar, and hanging wall, was collected. The evaluation indicators, classification criteria, and corresponding quantities for each level were introduced and visualized, to provide a reference for other scholars in their research.

Goaf dataset

In this paper, 121 sets of data on the goaf were collected by reviewing the literature8and some mines from China. Among them, the risk level I is 25 groups, II is 40 groups, III is 39 groups, and IV is 17 groups.

The factors affecting the stability of the goaf are mainly rock mechanics factors, environmental factors, and structure of the goaf. Among the quantitative indicators are: rock compressive strength X3, goaf volume X6, the exposed area of goaf roof X7, buried depth X8, and span-height ratio X9, and the qualitative indicators are: rock structure X1, geological structure X2, ground-water X4, goaf layout X5, mining disturbance X10, and condition of adjacent goaf X1133. Figure 4 depicts the structure of goaf in three types. Additionally, Table 1 shows that the stability degree of goaf is separated into four categories.

The distribution of the collected data was presented using the ggplot function34 in R language, as shown in Fig. 5. The two different colors correspond to two distinct categories. The upper part of the figure displays the correlation coefficients between each pair of features, where negative values indicate negative correlation, and larger numbers represent stronger correlation. The middle part of the figure represents the probability density distribution of each feature. The lower part of the figure illustrates the distribution of data between pairs of features. Based on the data distribution, it can be observed that the data has good dispersion and the dataset is relatively ideal.

Slope dataset

This study’s slope dataset comprises 148 sets of slope data from various locations worldwide35,36, including 78 stable slope samples and 70 unstable slope samples. The specific distribution of the datasets used is shown in Figs. 6 and 7. Six indicators were used to predict slope stability: X1 volumetric weight (kN/m3), X2 force of cohesion (kPa), X3 angle of internal friction (°), X4 slope angle (°), X5 slope height (m), and X6 pore pressure ratio. The parameter selection is shown in Fig. 6, where slope height and slope angle are geometric parameters of the slope, and bulk density, cohesion, internal friction angle, and pore pressure ratio are soil parameters. Specifically, bulk density refers to the weight per unit volume of soil or rock; cohesion determines the part of shear strength unrelated to normal effective stress during soil or rock movement; the internal friction angle measures its ability to withstand shear stress; the slope angle is the angle between the inclined slope plane and the slope base; slope height refers to the vertical distance from the slope’s bottom to its top; and the pore pressure ratio represents the ratio of pore water pressure to overlying rock pressure.

Data preprocessing

For mining disasters, the existence of outliers is caused by various situations. On the one hand, it could be due to issues with monitoring equipment, such as sensor aging, malfunction, and internal resistance heating of monitoring instruments. On the other hand, outliers may also result from imminent or occurred mining disasters. Therefore, it is not advisable to remove all data with anomalies. Additionally, the frequency of mining disasters varies, with severe accidents occurring less frequently than minor incidents, leading to imbalanced data. As a result, data preprocessing mainly focuses on outlier detection and handling imbalanced datasets.

Outlier detection and replacement

In recent years, outlier handling has gained increasing attention due to its significant impact on the variance and correlations between variables. The growing data dimensionality has made detecting these outliers more complex.

The Isolation Forest algorithm is a tree-based method used to handle outliers in structured data and has been proven effective in mining disaster data for outlier detection43,44. However, Isolation Forest still cannot avoid the curse of dimensionality, as it randomly selects dimensions during training, which may lead to significant dimension wastage and noise, making it less suitable for high-dimensional data. Additionally, when the sample size is large, some normal samples may be located close to outliers, affecting the isolation process. It may also cause outlier samples to form dense clusters, thereby elongating the required path for isolating the outlier, making it challenging to differentiate them from normal samples. In the practical application of this study, the Isolation Forest algorithm exhibits fluctuating performance in outlier selection, with certain outliers having ambiguous boundaries with normal values, resulting in imprecise identification of outliers. This indicates the need for cautious consideration of the algorithm’s applicability and the potential requirement for other methods to further handle outlier values when processing real-world data.

Mahalanobis Distance, proposed by Indian statistician P. C. Mahalanobis, represents the covariance distance of data. It is an effective method for calculating the similarity between two unknown sets of samples and is suitable for outlier detection45,46. Unlike Euclidean distance, Mahalanobis Distance takes into account the relationships between various features and is scale-invariant, meaning it is independent of measurement scales. The use of Mahalanobis Distance for outlier detection is justified for the following reasons: (1) Mahalanobis Distance is insensitive to measurement units, i.e., the distance between any two points is independent of the original data’s measurement units; (2) In multivariate conditions, Mahalanobis Distance considers the correlations between different indicators, which allows it to more accurately capture outliers compared to traditional Euclidean distance measurements.

For a multivariate vector with mean µ and covariance matrix Σ in an n-dimensional space, the Mahalanobis Distance between each individual sample Y and the mean sample µ is calculated as shown in Eq. 5. By applying the Mahalanobis Distance method, a regression relationship is established between a single indicator and all other indicators, which synthesizes the influence of various indicators on that specific indicator to determine whether outliers exist in that indicator. After calculating the Mahalanobis distance for the mining pillar’s various indicator data, a chi-square test was conducted using SPSS to determine the threshold for outlier Mahalanobis distance for each indicator as 12.84. Imbalanced dataset

The collected mining disaster data are all from actual monitoring, resulting in the issue of imbalanced datasets. Existing studies have shown that imbalanced datasets can affect the effectiveness of classification and prediction tasks47. If not addressed promptly, it may lead to disaster prediction results biased towards the larger class, causing significant prediction errors for the smaller class samples. This necessitates data reconstruction of the sample set before model training to reduce prediction errors caused by sample quantity differences.

There are two methods for data reconstruction. One is the data resampling method, which only duplicates samples with small quantities. The advantage is that it does not introduce noisy samples, but it may lead to overfitting due to repetitive training on the same samples. The other method is data synthesis, which uses linear interpolation between samples to synthesize samples for the minority class, avoiding overfitting caused by excessive repetition. However, this method may introduce noisy data. The SMOTE algorithm synthesizes minority class samples during the data balancing process, but it may generate synthetic outliers. Consider using the Tomek Link algorithm for improvement to reduce the generation of synthetic outliers during dataset reconstruction.

The main idea of the Tomek Links algorithm is as follows: Assuming samples X and Y belong to different classes, if there is no other sample Z such that d(X, Z) < d(X, Y) or d(Y, Z) < d(X, Y) holds, then samples X and Y are referred to as a Tomek Link pair. Here, d represents the Euclidean distance between the two samples. As shown in Fig. 15, the shaded area in red represents the Tomek Link pairs. If two samples of different classes, X and Y, form a Tomek Link pair, it indicates that their distance is minimal, making it highly likely that they are either noisy data or both samples are located near the classification boundary. Therefore, removing synthetic sample data identified as Tomek Links can reduce noise and effectively clean up data near the classification boundary. By setting a threshold based on the data magnitude, if the sampling result exceeds the threshold, resampling is performed until the sample classes are balanced.

To mitigate the impact of dataset partitioning randomness and ensure the reliability of sample importance analysis by the algorithm library, a 10-fold cross-validation was employed for algorithm analysis. The database sample data was divided into 10 sets, and in each iteration, one set was used as the testing set while the remaining nine sets were used as the training set. This process was repeated 10 times, and the final cross-validation accuracy was computed as the mean of the accuracies obtained from the 10 iterations. Results

In the process of predicting and classifying five types of mine disasters using algorithms from the algorithm library, the PSO algorithm was employed to optimize and fine-tune the hyper-parameters of the algorithms, achieving their best performance. The predicted results of goaf stability, slope stability, rock burst intensity level, pillar stability, hanging wall stability are shown in Tables 7, 8, 9, 10 and 11.

Among the three machine learning algorithms, ET outperformed SVM and RF in both accuracy and cross-validation results. In the prediction of goaf stability, ET achieved the highest accuracy of 96.15%, making it the best machine learning algorithm in the algorithm library. Similarly, in the comparison of other types of mine disasters, ET consistently outperformed SVM and RF algorithms.

Regarding deep learning networks, the CNN showed promising results due to its powerful feature extraction capability. It achieved an accuracy of 96.77% in slope stability prediction, tying with ET and GCF for the top position. However, for datasets with high feature dimensions and larger data sizes, its prediction accuracy noticeably decreased. For instance, in hanging wall stability prediction, its accuracy dropped to 84%, significantly below expectations. LSTM networks, primarily applied in time series forecasting, did not perform as well with non-time series data. Its accuracy remained below 80% in mine pillar stability prediction, rock burst intensity level prediction, and hanging wall stability prediction, noticeably lower than other machine learning and deep learning algorithms.

In the domain of mine disaster classification prediction, GCF exhibited distinct advantages. As shown in Fig. 23, GCF achieved accuracy rates of over 90% in predicting all five types of mine disasters. Whether it was slope stability prediction, rock burst intensity level prediction, mine pillar stability prediction, or hanging wall stability prediction, GCF demonstrated superior performance in terms of accuracy and cross-validation accuracy, making it the best algorithm. Although in goaf stability prediction, ET’s accuracy was slightly higher than GCF, the cross-validation accuracy of ET was inferior due to its extreme randomness. This implies that GCF’s stability is stronger than ET’s, and in practical engineering applications, algorithm stability is an important criterion for evaluation. Therefore, in goaf stability prediction, GCF outperformed ET. In conclusion, GCF exhibited the best performance in predicting and classifying the five types of mine disasters.

Discussion

Through the collection of diverse types of underground mine disaster data, it was discovered that the data exhibits high structural similarity. Therefore, the objective was to find a set of algorithms to address the multi-disaster prediction problem. In contrast to previous studies by other researchers focusing on single disaster prediction, this study collected multiple datasets of mine disasters and found that the GCF algorithm demonstrated excellent generalization and accuracy in predicting various types of mine disasters, effectively reducing the burden on intelligent mining systems. The GCF achieved accuracy rates exceeding 90% across all five types of mine disaster predictions. Furthermore, the ET algorithm outperformed other machine learning algorithms, such as SVM and RF, with an average accuracy also surpassing 90% in these predictions. However, the performance of the ET algorithm declined in scenarios involving a higher number of features, such as in the prediction of hanging wall stability, where its accuracy was 5% lower than that of GCF.

Furthermore, due to the varying impact indicators for different types of mine disasters, some disasters have a larger number of evaluation indicators. When using machine learning methods for prediction, it is essential to select the optimal evaluation indicators. In this study, a novel approach was adopted that considers both the correlation coefficients between indicators and their feature importance. By taking into account the actual conditions of the mines, the study identified the optimal evaluation indicators for disaster assessment. Moreover, to address the “black-box” issue often associated with machine learning algorithms, the SHAP model was introduced to explain the selected indicator system. The interpretation of the slope indicator system showed strong alignment with traditional slope safety factor theories, thereby enhancing the interpretability of machine learning methods. Higher values of S1 (volumetric weight) and S3 (angle of internal friction) indicate more stable slopes, whereas larger values of S4 (slope angle) and S6 (pore pressure ratio) suggest increased susceptibility to slope instability.

In the field of mine disaster classification prediction, the Deep Forest algorithm exhibits advantages such as high accuracy and stability. It combines the strengths of machine learning tree models and deep learning algorithms, retaining the powerful feature extraction capability of tree models while incorporating the stability advantages of deep learning. Additionally, unlike other deep models with numerous layers, the Deep Forest algorithm maintains a lightweight structure, balancing accuracy and stability, making it more suitable for multi-disaster classification prediction.

Conclusion

This study collected five common mining disaster datasets, calculated the correlation between indicators using Pearson correlation coefficient, and employed machine learning methods to determine the importance of features. By combining these two approaches to evaluate the indicators, a set of optimal evaluation indicators for five types of disaster prediction was obtained. The SHAP model was used to explain the rationality of these indicators, and the results indicated that the learning process of artificial intelligence algorithms remained consistent with traditional theories, enhancing the interpretability of machine learning.To address issues such as outliers and data imbalance in the datasets, the Mahalanobis distance discriminant analysis was employed to handle the outliers. The results demonstrated that this method effectively identified outliers in individual indicators, facilitating the analysis of abnormal data. Additionally, the improved SMOTE algorithm based on Tomek Links was used to tackle data imbalance, effectively improving the balance of the data and enhancing algorithm learning.Various machine learning and deep learning methods were applied to predict the severity of five types of mining disasters. Comparative analysis revealed that the deep forest model exhibited excellent accuracy and generalization, with accuracy rates of 92.31%, 96.77%, 92.50%, 91.67%, and 95.00% for the five mining disaster datasets, all exceeding 90%. Thus, this model can be applied to predict the severity of all five types of mining disasters.
