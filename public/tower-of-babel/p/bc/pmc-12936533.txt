Can riboflavin offer a novel personalised strategy for maintaining healthy blood pressure in pregnancy in populations globally?
Authors: Bethany Duffy, Anmol Patted, Rupsa C Boelig, Mary Murphy, Kristina Pentieva, Mary Ward, Diane Lees-Murdock, Victor Garcia, Frank Barry, Vanessa Short, Catherine Hughes, Richard Derman, Helene McNulty
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHypertension affects 10%–15% of pregnancies worldwide and can lead to serious adverse fetal and maternal outcomes. In addition, women with hypertension in pregnancy are at greater risk of developing stroke and renal disease later in life, while hypertensive pregnancy complications can also affect the long-term health of the child. The identification of strategies to maintain healthy blood pressure in women before and during pregnancy should therefore be prioritised. Emerging research points to an important role for folate, one-carbon metabolism and the related B vitamin, riboflavin, in blood pressure. In particular, evidence from clinical and genome-wide association studies links the C677T polymorphism in the gene encoding the folate-metabolising enzyme methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR) with blood pressure and an increased risk of hypertension and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy. Riboflavin (in the form of flavin adenine dinucleotide) is required as a cofactor for MTHFR, and notably, randomised trials show that supplemental riboflavin can effectively lower blood pressure specifically in individuals with the variant MTHFR 677TT genotype, independently of antihypertensive medications. The evidence that better riboflavin status modifies the blood pressure phenotype in these genetically at-risk individuals has important public health implications, especially for populations worldwide with the highest frequencies of the variant TT genotype in MTHFR, including Guatemala (up to 66%), Mexico (32%) and Northern China (20%). This novel gene–nutrient interaction warrants particular attention in the context of blood pressure before and during pregnancy. Furthermore, the biological mechanisms require investigation, whereby one-carbon metabolism is linked with blood pressure and how riboflavin, a much-overlooked nutrient in health and research settings, can modulate the excess genetic risk of hypertension in affected individuals. Here, we review the generally unrecognised role of riboflavin as a novel personalised solution to prevent hypertension and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy in genetically at-risk women. This article should stimulate current thinking, with potentially important impacts for public health nutrition strategies to promote better pregnancy outcomes in women.Keywords: Blood pressure lowering, Precision nutrition, Pregnancy, Prenatal Care

Introduction

Chronic hypertension and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) are challenges in high-income, middle-income and low-income countries alike. The WHO recognises HDP as a leading cause of maternal deaths globally, with HDP, haemorrhage and sepsis combined accounting for more than one half of all maternal deaths worldwide.1 Addressing HDP is becoming even more urgent as the global incidence has increased by 11% over the past three decades.2 Safe and cost-effective new strategies to address HDP on a global scale are needed.

There has been much research over the years on the prediction and prevention of HDP. The emergence of genome-wide association studies (GWAS) has enabled specific genes linked with blood pressure (BP) to be investigated and the gene encoding the folate-metabolising enzyme methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR) has been identified among the loci associated with hypertension.3 In broad agreement with the findings of these GWAS, meta-analyses of clinical studies show that homozygosity for the 677C→T polymorphism in MTHFR is associated with marked increases in the risk of hypertension in pregnancy and pre-eclampsia.4 5 Among individuals with the homozygous variant TT genotype, there is reduced activity of MTHFR and thus impaired folate and one-carbon metabolism.6 Riboflavin, the cofactor for MTHFR, may, however, aid in restoring enzyme activity, and in doing so, exert a modulating gene–nutrient effect on BP among individuals affected by this polymorphism. This review explores the largely unrecognised role of riboflavin in BP and considers whether optimising riboflavin status could offer a novel personalised approach to prevent hypertension and HDP in genetically at-risk women.

Global burden of hypertension

Hypertension, the strongest modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD), is a leading cause of global morbidity and mortality. It affects an estimated 1.28 billion adults aged 30–79 years globally, and the prevalence has doubled since 1990.7 Furthermore, hypertension complicates an estimated 15% of all pregnancies, affecting 18.1 million women worldwide and is directly responsible for over 27 000 deaths.2 Maternal death rates owing to HDP are reported to be highest in central, eastern and western sub-Saharan Africa, and lowest in high-income countries.2 Notably, the risk of preterm births, stillbirths, neonatal and perinatal mortality is two to five times higher in women with HDP than in women without these disorders.8 Women with hypertension in pregnancy are also at greater risk of developing CVD, stroke, metabolic diseases and end-stage renal disease later in life.9 Apart from the mother’s health, hypertensive pregnancy complications can affect the health of the fetus before and immediately after delivery and into adulthood.10

In addition to the direct impacts on the health of the mother, fetus and neonate, HDP presents a significant economic burden as the cost of treating a pregnancy complicated by pre-eclampsia is estimated to be three times greater than that of an uncomplicated pregnancy.11 Thus, identifying new strategies to effectively prevent and treat hypertension in pregnancy should be prioritised.

One-carbon metabolism and BP

B vitamins and one-carbon metabolism

An adequate maternal diet is essential to support the rapid rates of DNA replication and cellular proliferation occurring during fetal life. Folate, in particular, is crucial during the preconception and early pregnancy period, where it has an established role in preventing neural tube defects.12 Folate and the related B vitamins, B12, B6 and riboflavin, are required for one-carbon metabolism,13 a network of pathways involved in the transfer and utilisation of one-carbon units for essential biological processes including DNA and RNA biosynthesis, amino acid metabolism and methylation reactions (figure 1). These one-carbon nutrients are thus crucial for fetal and placental growth and development.

On entering the one-carbon cycle, tetrahydrofolate (THF) acquires a carbon unit from serine in a vitamin B6-dependent reaction to form 5,10-methyleneTHF, the folate form required for pyrimidine biosynthesis. MTHFR is the riboflavin-dependent enzyme that catalyses the subsequent reduction of 5,10-methyleneTHF to 5-methylTHF. The latter folate cofactor is required in the remethylation of homocysteine to methionine by the vitamin B12 dependent enzyme methionine synthase. Once formed, methionine is activated by ATP to generate S-adenosylmethionine, the universal methyl donor ensuring cellular methylation potential and systemic gene expression. Thus, folate, together with vitamins B12, B6 and riboflavin, is required for effective one-carbon metabolism and the maintenance of normal homocysteine concentrations.13 Although previously largely overlooked, recent evidence indicates that one-carbon metabolism may play a role in the maintenance of healthy BP.6 Conversely, impaired one-carbon metabolism owing to low status of one or more of these B vitamins, or polymorphisms in related genes, can lead to hypertension and several adverse health outcomes, as reviewed elsewhere.13

The MTHFR C677T polymorphism

Homozygosity for the MTHFR C677T polymorphism (TT genotype) is associated with decreased MTHFR enzyme activity and typically leads to hyperhomocysteinaemia. Much less well recognised is that this common folate polymorphism is associated with elevated BP and an increased risk of hypertension.14 The TT genotype has a global frequency of 10%, but this is much higher in certain populations (figure 2). In Mexico, depending on ethnicity, between 32% and 44% of people have the TT genotype, while in Guatemala, the frequency is up to 66%. In the USA, the frequency is higher among Hispanic (18%) compared with white (11%) or black (2.7%) Americans.15

There is now considerable evidence to show that this folate polymorphism leads to not only higher homocysteine, but also BP, and increases the risk of hypertension by 24%–87% and CVD by up to 40%, although with a large geographical variation in the extent of excess disease risk suggestive of a gene–environment interaction.14 16 17 Emerging evidence indicates that the relevant environmental factor may be riboflavin, the MTHFR cofactor, via a novel and genotype-specific effect on BP.14 Thus, although the geographical differences in extent of CVD risk attributable to this polymorphism are usually explained by variations in homocysteine arising from differences in folic acid fortification policies across countries, elevated BP may be the much more relevant phenotype and riboflavin the more relevant nutrient in affected individuals. Of note, the highest risks attributable to this polymorphism have been observed among Asian populations, where dietary intakes of dairy foods (the richest source of riboflavin) are generally very low, and in China, contribute to only 6% of total riboflavin intakes.18

Pregnant women with the variant MTHFR TT genotype are reported to have up to 33% higher risk of pre-eclampsia and 37% higher risk of hypertension in pregnancy.17 19 These risks also appear to be considerably higher within Asian populations during pregnancy, with reports that this polymorphism is associated with a 220% and a 90% increased risk of pre-eclampsia and hypertension in pregnancy, respectively4 5 (table 1). Given riboflavin’s role as the cofactor for MTHFR, it is plausible that reduced enzyme activity among pregnant and non-pregnant TT women, combined with poor riboflavin status, could substantially exacerbate the risks of hypertension and HDP and that riboflavin intervention would reduce these risks.

Riboflavin as a personalised treatment for hypertension

Riboflavin and hypertension

In a study of over 6000 Irish adults, we found that the variant TT genotype in MTHFR was associated with a 42% increased risk of hypertension.20 Notably, however, riboflavin deficiency exacerbated the genetic risk of hypertension throughout adulthood, with a threefold excess risk when the MTHFR TT genotype occurred in combination with deficient riboflavin status (compared with those without this polymorphism and normal riboflavin status). In support of these observational findings, in three previous randomised controlled trials (RCTs) of hypertensive patients, with and without CVD, we showed that intervention with low-dose riboflavin (1.6 mg/day) for 16 weeks resulted in a lowering of systolic BP by up to 13 mm Hg, specifically in those with the TT genotype.21,23 Together, these studies show that riboflavin plays an important role in modulating BP among individuals affected by this common folate polymorphism.

Notably, a recent population-based study of over 12 000 adults in China showed that individuals with the lowest dietary riboflavin intakes were at the greatest risk of developing new-onset hypertension.24 Although the MTHFR C677T polymorphism was not considered, the variant TT genotype affects a considerable proportion of Chinese adults, with a reported frequency of 20% in Northern China.15

Riboflavin status and deficiency

Riboflavin deficiency may be much more widespread than is generally perceived but typically goes undetected in almost all countries because riboflavin biomarkers are rarely measured, and nationally representative data on status are unavailable in all but a few countries.25 26 The global prevalence of riboflavin deficiency is therefore unknown; however, in many low-income and middle-income countries, clinical riboflavin deficiencies have been reported.25 In high-income countries, while clinical deficiency is rare, suboptimal riboflavin status may be widespread, particularly among women of reproductive age. As reviewed elsewhere,26 limited available reports indicate that riboflavin deficiency affects 90% of adults in China, 76% of adults in Guatemala, 71% of Malaysian women, 40% of Canadian women and 67% of teenage girls (11–18 years) in the UK. Notably, it was recently estimated that more than 4 billion people worldwide (55% of the global population) do not consume enough riboflavin.27

Milk and dairy products are the richest dietary sources of riboflavin and therefore individuals, and countries, that have low intakes of this food group will be at high risk of riboflavin deficiency.25 In China, where dairy foods are not typically consumed and thus dietary riboflavin intakes are found to be extremely low, riboflavin insufficiency is widespread.18 24 Also, mandatory riboflavin enrichment policies, to replace the riboflavin lost during milling of grain, are in place in a number of countries, predominantly in North and South America.25 Although this policy may benefit riboflavin intakes of such populations to an extent, the levels of added riboflavin are typically low; for example, in the USA, the mandatory level of riboflavin enrichment is at 0.40 mg/100 g wheat starch, as regulated by the US Food and Drug Administration. However, in the absence of nationally representative biomarker data in most countries, the adequacy of riboflavin nutrition remains uncertain. This uncertainty is especially relevant during critical life stages, such as pregnancy, where riboflavin requirements are elevated.26 28

During pregnancy, dietary riboflavin recommendations are increased (to between 1.3 and 1.9 mg/day depending on the jurisdiction) to account for—fetal uptake and riboflavin accretion in the placenta.28 Although very few studies have reported riboflavin biomarker measurements during pregnancy, given the generally low status among reproductive aged women and the increased riboflavin requirements for pregnancy,28 it is likely that riboflavin deficiency is common among pregnant women globally. The associated adverse consequences for maternal and offspring health generally go undetected, and riboflavin deficiency is thus largely overlooked in public health and clinical care.

Riboflavin, MTHFR genotype and hypertension

The emergence of a novel interaction of riboflavin and the MTHFR C677T polymorphism with impacts for BP may be important in preventing hypertension and related adverse outcomes, before and during pregnancy, and in later life, among subpopulations of women globally affected by this genetic risk factor. We recently reported the first results from our OptiPREG study of over 2200 pregnancies showing that riboflavin deficiency was associated with an almost threefold greater risk of developing hypertension during pregnancy.29 In broad support of these early findings, a small prospective study previously found that riboflavin deficiency was associated with an increased risk of pre-eclampsia.30 The limited studies investigating riboflavin (intake, status or intervention) in relation to BP, hypertension or pre-eclampsia are summarised in table 2. Overall, this evidence suggests that riboflavin may have cardioprotective benefits, most notably among individuals with the TT genotype. There are potentially important clinical impacts of riboflavin intervention in women before and during pregnancy, and in later life. Notably, in non-pregnant adults with the variant TT genotype, supplemental riboflavin was shown in RCTs to lower systolic BP by up to 13 mm Hg; a BP response of this magnitude could have important clinical impacts in women’s health, given that a reduction in systolic BP of 10 mm Hg is estimated to decrease stroke risk by 40%.31 However, neither the link of this folate polymorphism (affecting 10% of populations worldwide) with hypertension, nor the important role of riboflavin in modulating the BP phenotype, is recognised in research or clinical practice and warrants attention. Further research is also needed to better understand the mechanisms whereby this polymorphism is linked with hypertension, and how riboflavin lowers BP in affected individuals.

Underpinning biological mechanisms

The MTHFR-riboflavin-BP relationship

The precise mechanisms linking the MTHFR C677T polymorphism with hypertension, and functional rescue of the phenotype by riboflavin, remain to be established and therefore are speculative at this time. As reviewed elsewhere, however,14 the totality of evidence points to folate-mediated effects on the potent vasodilator, nitric oxide, the bioavailability of which is directly related to endothelial function. Specifically, 5-methylTHF, the product of the reaction catalysed by MTHFR (figure 1), is important in regulating the vascular redox state and endothelial function.32 Notably, in patients undergoing coronary artery bypass graft surgery, 5-methylTHF concentrations in vascular tissue were associated with nitric oxide bioavailability and improved endothelial function and were found to be lower in patients with the TT genotype in MTHFR.32 Our randomised trials in hypertensive patients show that the biological perturbation leading to higher BP in individuals with the TT genotype can be corrected through enhancing riboflavin intake, even in patients concurrently being treated with different classes of antihypertensive agents,21,23 suggesting that the mechanism of action of riboflavin in BP is independent of such medications.

The modulating effect of riboflavin clearly relates to its role as the flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) cofactor for MTHFR, the activity of which is particularly sensitive to riboflavin status in individuals with the variant TT genotype, while riboflavin supplementation appears to stabilise MTHFR in vivo.6 20 33 Molecular studies show that the loss of MTHFR activity which occurs with the TT genotype is the result of a reduced affinity for its FAD cofactor.34 It could be speculated, therefore, that individuals with the variant TT genotype who have optimal riboflavin status would have a higher capacity to replace inactivated enzyme, or that better riboflavin status may prevent the FAD cofactor from leaving the active site or would allow its quick replacement, thus stabilising the variant form of the enzyme.34 Stabilising the variant MTHFR in vivo with supplemental riboflavin35 could at least partially restore 5-methylTHF concentrations in vascular cells and in turn improve nitric oxide availability and endothelial function, inducing vasodilation, thus lowering BP specifically in individuals with the TT genotype. Whatever the mechanism linking one-carbon metabolism with BP, it is unlikely to directly involve homocysteine per se, as although significant associations of homocysteine with BP are reported in several observational studies, no BP response was found in homocysteine-lowering trials aimed at the secondary prevention of CVD.36

Broadly consistent with our current thinking on mechanism, implicating nitric oxide in the MTHFR-riboflavin relationship with BP, is recent genomics evidence37 which identifies nitric oxide signalling pathways among the major functional groups in network analysis of the human flavoproteome. Nevertheless, the precise biological mechanisms remain speculative in the absence of confirmatory data.

Future research directions

Mechanistic studies are required to elucidate the precise biological perturbation leading to higher BP with the common MTHFR C677T polymorphism and to better understand how riboflavin can rescue this phenotype and thus protect against the development of hypertension and HDP. It could be hypothesised, based on the available evidence, that riboflavin supplementation in TT genotype individuals improves endothelial function through upregulating concentrations of the potent vasodilator, nitric oxide; however, this remains to be investigated. Furthermore, recent advances in epigenetic research have enabled investigation of the potential molecular processes involved that could contribute to hypertension via dynamic changes in DNA methylation.38 These epigenetic studies can provide novel insights to help explain how perturbations in one-carbon metabolism influence DNA methylation of key genes in pathways relevant to BP.

Future directions could involve applying novel regenerative and gene editing methods to further our understanding of underpinning mechanisms. Pregnancy, a period of intense growth and development with increased demands for folate and related B vitamins, coincides with programmed dynamic changes in DNA methylation39 and thus represents a window for gene-nutrient interactions to impact offspring health. We previously reported alterations in DNA methylation in specific classes of neurodevelopmental genes in newborns born to folic acid supplemented mothers during pregnancy, and correspondingly, showed beneficial effects on neurocognitive performance in the children followed for up to 11 years.40 Substantial evidence indicates the involvement of folate in modulating DNA methylation both globally and at specific sites in stem cell transitions; however, the influence of riboflavin, which is critical for folate recycling within one-carbon metabolism, remains unexplored (figure 3). We have begun to investigate this using regenerative medicine approaches and have generated patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cells which can be differentiated into vascular smooth muscle cells, recapitulating formation of the vasculature during development, enabling investigation of the effect of riboflavin on vascular cells during developmental processes. The impact of riboflavin supplementation in pregnancy on offspring methylation remains unexplored and this is also a priority for future research.

Limitations and translation of the evidence

Although this review provides a comprehensive overview of the literature on the potential role of riboflavin as a personalised strategy for maintaining healthy BP, several limitations and gaps in current knowledge should be acknowledged. First, the evidence base remains limited by a lack of pregnancy-specific trials, with much of the current understanding derived from observational studies, which are inherently subjected to bias. Furthermore, while outside the context of pregnancy, the evidence (including from RCTs) supports a specific beneficial effect of riboflavin on BP in individuals with the variant MTHFR 677TT genotype, emerging research also suggests that higher riboflavin intakes are linked with healthier BP in the general population, and may not be confined only to those with this genetic risk factor.24 This is especially likely to be the case where riboflavin requirements are increased, as in pregnancy, and/or where prevailing riboflavin intakes are particularly low, as is the case in China.18 24

As regards the wider translation of the evidence presented here, it should be noted that genotyping for MTHFR is not routine clinical practice in antenatal care and may not always be feasible, particularly within low-resource settings. Thus, population-wide riboflavin intervention may be more appropriate as a public health measure in low-income and middle-income countries, especially given that there is an extremely low risk of any adverse effects of high riboflavin intake (based on Expert opinion from the European Food Safety Authority)28 and considering that riboflavin deficiency is a widespread problem. We, therefore, propose that in resource-limited settings, it would be more feasible and cost-effective to implement population-wide riboflavin intervention rather than specifically targeting only those with the variant MTHFR 677TT genotype.

Finally, the mechanisms underpinning the relationship between riboflavin and BP regulation are still speculative at this time and warrant further research.

Conclusions

This narrative review focused on riboflavin nutrition, which has been greatly underinvestigated compared with other nutrients. In particular, we reviewed the evidence linking riboflavin and one-carbon metabolism with BP and the risk of HDP. The C677T polymorphism in MTHFR, affecting 1 in 10 adults globally (and much higher proportions of certain populations), is associated with higher BP and a greater risk of hypertension and HDP. There is emerging observational and randomised trial evidence showing that riboflavin exerts an important modulating effect on the BP phenotype, with riboflavin supplementation proven to lower BP specifically in individuals with the variant TT genotype in MTHFR. Other relevant genes for BP have been identified in GWAS, but this folate polymorphism is the only genetic factor identified for hypertension that offers a personalised management option, via optimising riboflavin. This novel gene–nutrient interaction has yet to be investigated in relation to pregnancy BP, where the impacts of hypertension are arguably greatest, adversely affecting both mother and offspring in the immediate and long term. If shown to be effective, riboflavin intervention could have important immediate and longer-term clinical and public health impacts. For women worldwide with the variant TT genotype, intervention with riboflavin could offer a personalised option to treat or prevent the development of high BP and HDP. The health impacts arising from such a safe and low-cost intervention would be variable, depending on prevailing dietary riboflavin intakes in a given country, but are anticipated to be greatest in those countries worldwide with the lowest riboflavin intakes, especially if combined with a high frequency of the MTHFR TT genotype, particularly Latin American populations.

The evidence also indicates that riboflavin deficiency is highly prevalent globally, yet goes largely undetected, and its role in BP remains entirely unrecognised in current public health policy and practice. Future research on riboflavin is warranted, including randomised trials, particularly targeting genetically at-risk women to determine the effectiveness of riboflavin intervention as a personalised preventative or treatment strategy for hypertension before and during pregnancy. Further study of the MTHFR C677T polymorphism and role of riboflavin in modifying the BP phenotype has the potential to significantly impact paradigms for preventing hypertension in at-risk populations worldwide.
