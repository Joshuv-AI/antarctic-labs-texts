Post-hurricane related flooding and essential services interruptions, and their impact on the quality of life of women with gynecological cancer in Puerto Rico
Authors: Nancy Cardona-Cordero, Amarilis Bolaños, Liliana Castro-Jiménez, Sandra I. García-Camacho, Brenda Torres, Pablo A. Méndez-Lázaro, Ana P. Ortiz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionExtreme weather poses threats to human health. Island communities are especially at risk of compounding disasters. This study assessed the prevalence of post-hurricane-related flooding and essential services disruptions among women with gynecological cancer in Puerto Rico and how these stressors impacted their quality of life (QoL).MethodsInterview-administered surveys to women with gynecological cancer (n = 271) were conducted between 2019-2020 to gather sociodemographic, clinical, service disruption, and QoL data (physical and mental health) at three time points. Generalized Estimating Equation (GEE) models with Difference-in-Differences (DiD) interaction terms were used to isolate the effect of hurricane-related stressors on changes in each QoL outcome across time among women with gynecological cancer.ResultsParticipants' mean age was 58.9 years (SD±13.2), and 62.8% lived below the poverty level (<$20,000). After the hurricane, most participants reported service disruption of waste (57.1%) and debris (55.6%) collection, telecommunications (76.8%), water (85.9%) and electricity (100%). After adjusting for covariates and considering interaction terms, compared to the pre-hurricane period, individual hurricane-related stressors affected at least one health outcome in each QoL domain. Those who experienced compound stressors were more likely to report frequent mental distress (AOR:1.38, CI:1.07,1.80) and frequent activity limitations (AOR:1.55, CI:1.22, 1.98), compared with those who did not experience compounded stressors.DiscussionAddressing the impact of extreme weather events on health outcomes is vital for island communities. Immediate action and improvements in health services and public infrastructure are needed to support cancer care and mitigate the impact of hurricane-related disruptions on the QoL of cancer survivors.

Introduction

Devastating climate events such as wildfires, tornadoes, heat and cold periods, floods, typhoons, and hurricanes [1,2] pose a threat and have serious repercussions for the built environment and vital infrastructure of nations [3]. Compounding climate stressors, such as the exceedance of +1.5C0 global warming threshold, hotter years recorded, excessive precipitation periods, and increased strength of tropical storm winds have been noted in the last decade [1,4]. Island communities are therefore at risk due to high water levels that threaten low-lying coastal areas, causing coastal flooding, and loss of infrastructure and essential services [1,5,6].

On September 20th, 2017, Puerto Rico, a hurricane-prone archipelago territory of the US in the Caribbean region, was devastated by Hurricane Maria, one of the biggest tropical storms of the decade [7], a category 4 hurricane that accounted for 2975 deaths, including those in its aftermath [8]. This hurricane affected the island’s critical infrastructure, leaving individuals without communication, water, power, and health care services [9,10], which had serious health repercussions among people with preexisting conditions, such as cancer patients [11].

Further aggravating the constant threat of hurricanes, Puerto Ricans are an older population with an increased prevalence of chronic health conditions [12], including cancer as the second leading cause of death [13]. Between 2018 and 2022, 80,473 individuals were diagnosed with cancer, and women represented 47.6%, contributing a yearly average of 7666 diagnoses [13]. Puerto Rican women are family leaders and caregivers who manage households, including efforts in disaster recovery [14]. Consequently, the health status of women has an impact on the well-being of their families and their communities [15]. Socio-demographic factors, as well as women’s additional roles and structural norms in society, play a role in receiving cancer care in a timely manner, especially for minorities [16]. Studies show that Latinas’ cultural beliefs and acculturation act as barriers for preventive care services such as cancer screening or timely follow-up, which are risk factors for some types of gynecological cancers [17]. In Puerto Rico, gynecological cancers represent over 16% of all cancers with corpus uterus, uterine cervix, and ovarian cancers included in the top 10 cancer incidence sites for women [12], most with increasing incidence trends in diagnosis for the 2018–2022 period.

With an increasing rate of extreme climate events, a growing interest has been noted in the global scientific community to evaluate the effect of climate disasters on the physical and mental health domains among individuals in island and coastal communities. Noticeable rises in emotional distress, manifesting as fear (53%), anxiety (41%), and self-pity (40%) were observed in Philippines communities affected by typhoon-induced floods and landslides [18]. In the Solomon Islands, 98% of residents experienced worry due to their exposure to sea-level rise, and 95% acknowledged negative effects on their psychological health regarding climate events [19]. However, limited research exists that evaluates the post-hurricanes impact on the quality of life (QoL) of cancer populations [20].

Given these research gaps, this study aims to (1) determine the prevalence of hurricane-related flooding and service disruptions among women with gynecological cancer in Puerto Rico, and (2) evaluate how these post-hurricane stressors impacted their QoL. Understanding women’s QoL, specifically their physical and mental health, can benefit the entire household preparedness and overcome complex challenges in times of disaster. Findings from this study also may encourage stakeholders to strengthen post-disaster physical and psychological support for cancer patients.

Material and methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study was conducted within the University of Puerto Rico Comprehensive Cancer Center (UPRCCC) [21]. A total of 271 women who received services at gynecology oncology hospitals/clinics across San Juan, Ponce and Mayaguez, Puerto Rico [22], were recruited. Women were eligible to participate in the study if they were 21 years or older and were diagnosed with a gynecological cancer between September 2016 and September 2018. This period was selected considering the possibility of a recall bias in less recent years (≤2015). Telephone-based interviews were conducted in Spanish by research staff between September 2019 and November 2020. Interviews assessed demographic, lifestyle and clinical variables as well as environmental stressors, and QoL variables. All participants provided either written or verbal informed consent before study participation; $20 compensation by mail was sent to study participants for their time. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at UPR Medical Sciences Campus (IRB # A1810320).

Hurricane-related stressors

Hurricane-related flooding was measured by asking participants: “Have you experienced any flooding as a result of the hurricanes?”. Each hurricane-related essential services disruption was measured by asking participants: “Have you been without (water, electricity, waste collection, debris collection, and telecommunications) for a while?” Responses to each hurricane-related stressor were measured dichotomously (yes / no). For each service, participants were asked how long (days and months) service was lost.

Physical and mental health domain outcomes

QoL questions from the 2006–2010 Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) questionnaire were used, which contained six questions. These QoL items were categorized by the BRFSS into two domains: physical and mental health [23].

Following BRFSS methodology, the physical health QoL domain included three elements: frequent physical distress, frequent activity limitations, and fair/poor general health. Frequent physical distress was assessed by asking participants, “Now thinking about your physical health, which includes physical illness and injury, for how many days during the past 30 days was your physical health not good?” Similarly, the query for frequent activity limitations was “During the past 30 days, for about how many days did poor physical or mental health keep you from your usual activities, such as self-care, work, or recreation?” For both questions, participants reporting 14 or more days were identified as having frequent physical distress or a frequent activity limitation. For fair/poor general health, participants were asked, “Would you say that in general your health is excellent, very good, good, fair, or poor?” Responses were dichotomized (excellent or very good / good or fair or poor).

The mental health QoL domain included three items: frequent mental distress, life dissatisfaction, and some, rare, or no socio-emotional support. Frequent mental distress was assessed by asking participants, “Now thinking about your mental health, which includes stress, depression, and problems with emotions, for how many days during the past 30 days was your mental health not good?” Participants reporting 14 or more days were identified as having frequent mental distress. Life dissatisfaction was assessed by asking participants, “In general, how satisfied are you with your life?” Responses were dichotomized (very satisfied or satisfied / dissatisfied or very dissatisfied. Finally, the range of some, rare, or no socio-emotional support was assessed by asking participants: “How often do you get the social and emotional support you need?” Responses were dichotomized (always or usually / sometimes, rarely, never).

At the time of the interview, each participant was asked these six questions. For frequent physical distress, frequent activity limitations, and fair or poor general health, participants were asked to think and answer these questions relative to at 30 days pre-hurricanes, 30 days post-hurricanes, and at the time of the interview. For frequent mental distress, life dissatisfaction, and some, rare or no socio-emotional support, participants were asked to answer these questions at 3 months pre-hurricanes, 3 months post-hurricanes, and at the time of the interview.

Covariates

All covariates were transformed into polytomous variables or dichotomized. Age was categorized into 21–39, 40–49, 50–64 and ≥65 years. Marital status was categorized as currently-, previously-, and never married. Education was dichotomized into less than high school and ≥ high school. Employment status categories included employed/self-worker, homemaker and unemployed, unable to work, or retired. Annual gross family income was categorized into <$10,000, $10,000–$19,999, and ≥$20,000. Gynecological cancer type was subdivided into cervix uteri, corpus uteri, corpus uteri and ovarian, ovarian, uterine, vaginal, vulval, and unspecified female genital organ cancer.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were performed to characterize participants’ demographics, hurricane-related stressors, and QoL physical and mental health domains. Given the longuitudinal structure of the data, a Difference-in-Differences (DiD) framework with Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) was employed to account for repeated measures within subjects. This approach analyzed how changes in hurricane-related stressors over time were associated with changes in QoL domains.

For the GEE models, each physical and mental health domain was treated as a separate binary outcome variable using a logit link function for the binomial distribution and an exchangeable correlation structure. In the GEE analysis, each QoL domain was regressed individually with each hurricane-related stressor, time-point, and DiD interaction term (hurricane-related stressor x time-point) as predictors. The reference category for the different time points was set to the pre-hurricane responses, and for each hurricane-related stressor, the reference was set as the women who did not experience the stressor.

To explore the cumulative burden of hurricane-related stressors, a compound disruption variable was created. This variable was a sum of all binary indicators for each hurricane-related stressor at each time point, providing a quantitative measure of the total number of concurrent stressors (between 1 and 5). Separate GEE models were fitted for each QoL domain as outcomes, with the compound disruption variable, time point, and DiD interaction term (compound disruption x time point) as predictors.

All models accounted for within-subject correlation and were adjusted for demographic variables (age, income, education) and time of cancer diagnosis. If the interaction terms in the GEE models were statistically significant, additional GEE models were created for the specific QoL domain as outcome and the specific hurricane-related stressor as predictor, stratified by the three time points.

Statistical analyses were performed using R, version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and R-Studio (Posit Software, PBC). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Population characteristics

At the time of the interviews, the participants’ mean age was 59.8 ± 13.2 years (Table 1). Most women were married (43.6%) and had an educational level above a high school degree (66.2%). Over half (62.8%) reported an annual family gross income of less than $20,000. Hurricane-related stressors

Most participants reported service disruption were electricity (100.0%), water (85.6%), telecommunications (76.8%), waste (54.9%) and debris (53.1%) collection (Fig. 1). Over half of the study participants experienced post-hurricane flooding (51.6%). DiD interaction results showed that the odds of reporting worsening QoL domains increased significantly pots-hurricane when compared to pre-hurricane and at interview time points for those who experienced some specific hurricane-related stressors (Table 3). In stratified GEE models, participants who experienced water service disruption in the post-hurricane time point showed increased odds of reporting frequent physical distress (AOR: 2.17, 95%CI: 0.95–4.96; p < 0.10) as compared to those who did not have this interruption (Table 3). Similarly, debris collection service disruption was associated with increase odds of reporting fair/poor general health (AOR: 1.65, 95%CI: 0.93–2.95; p < 0.10) in the post-hurricane time point. These effects did not persist at the time of the interview, suggesting a temporary impact. In addition, telecommunication service disruption showed a significant association with experiencing frequent activity limitations (AOR: 2.07, 95%CI: 1.04–4.12) only at the post-hurricane time point. Discussion

This study among women with gynecologic cancer in Puerto Rico evidences how cancer patients are particularly susceptible to physical and mental health challenges when exposed to climate disasters and their related stressors. In our findings, a vast group of participants faced a compound of post hurricane stressors, including flooding and disruption of essential services. Moreover, we provide evidence of how experiencing these environmental stressors worsened the QoL (physical and mental health domains) of these women significantly.

Results from our study are consistent with previous research that has shown that people who experience climate disasters report mental health disasters [18,9,20,24], including two studies performed in Puerto Rico among selected cancer patients [25,26]. This pattern of devastating mental health effects has also been observed in residents of coastal US states affected by climate events [4,20,[27], [28], [29], [30]]. As an example, post-Hurricane Katrina, Louisiana residents who lived through the event showed PTSD, depression, and anxiety [30]. Our study shows similar trends; participants reported higher physical mental distress post Hurricane Maria (at 30 days or 3 months), and 2-3 years (at the time of the interview) post-event as compared to their pre-event distress status, especially those who lost essential services. Like our study, in 2015, Chatterjee and Mozumder found that disruption in water supply, due to Hurricane Wilma, was the major driver behind worsened health outcomes in the general population. Our findings also revealed that post-hurricane flooding events can influence the QoL domains by increasing the risk of physical activity limitations, and physical and mental distress among women cancer patients.

These findings call attention to the urgency of improving the public infrastructure to better protect homes against floods and mitigate the impact of these events on the QoL and the many challenges faced by cancer patients. According to previous research, it is not only important to consider infrastructure-level adaptations action to minimize hardships during natural disasters, but also to preserve mental health, and post-disaster support services and programs [31], [32]. This is of relevance because, if not adequately supported, communities affected by extreme weather events can experience a wide range of negative emotions [27]. Those affected by climate disasters mostly report sadness, fear and anger, which can be attributed to extensive property damage, injuries, and loss of life, or the helplessness of affected communities [33]. However, strong social networks and perception of social support have played a key role influencing constructive actions towards positive health outcomes, especially on the health of Latinas [34]. In our study population, socio-emotional support levels stayed consistent across time periods (pre-hurricane, 3 months post-hurricane, and at the time of the interview) and were not associated with any hurricane-related stressors. This finding shows that socio-emotional support was not affected across time in this Hispanic population of women. This is consistent with cultural patterns of support reported among Hispanic/Latino populations [34]. Thus, family, friends and community support should be further explored, as recent studies show that a significant portion of the people can express post-hurricane joy, possibly reflecting relief, hope, community support, and personal resilience [33].

Strengths of this study include proper data collection from study participants by trained interviewers and the use of standardized QoL scales [23]. In addition, women were recruited from gyn-oncology clinics from different geographic regions in Puerto Rico, increasing the representation of women. A limitation of this study is the potential information and recall bias, as data were self-reported by participants and collected two to three years post-hurricane. In addition, results are not generalizable to all cancer patients in Puerto Rico. Future research should evaluate the longitudinal effects of climate stressors on QoL of island populations prone to compounding climate stressors, including cancer survivors. Additional research should also focus on how social support from household members can impact the QoL of patients, as well as the individual burden and responsibilities within the household for caregivers.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to describe the association between hurricane-related stressors (experiencing flooding and essential services disruptions) and the QoL of women with gynecological cancer across time. In this study, women who experienced individual- and compounded post-hurricane stressors reported worsened physical and mental health outcomes. It is important to note that socio-emotional support did not vary across time despite the stressors experienced. This finding should be further studied to assess whether family and community support has positively safeguarded the health of this population.

Initiatives to reduce difficulties in times of disasters, especially personal and service losses, are essential to preserve physical and psychological health immediately and long after a disaster to overcome complex health challenges. Our results call for proper planning and infrastructure that help guarantee a fast recovery of essential services after extreme weather events. Among the US’s 50 states, the District of Columbia, 7 tribes and 7 territories, Puerto Rico is one of the first territories to incorporate climate stressors as an emerging priority into its Comprehensive Cancer Control Plan [35] which prompts the formulation of strategies into a blueprint for action to prevent and control cancer in this and other island territories.
