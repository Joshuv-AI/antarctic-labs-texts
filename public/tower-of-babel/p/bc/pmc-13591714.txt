Urban residents’ choice preferences for hospitals at different tiers and influencing factors: a study based on latent class analysis and Andersen’s model of health service utilization
Authors: Ke Wang, Hang Chen, Yu Jin, Rui Min
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChina’s health system faces widespread bypassing behavior, where residents prefer high-level hospitals, leading to inefficient primary resource use. This study aimed to identify distinct preference groups in urban residents’ choices of hospitals and to examine how predisposing, enabling, and need factors from Andersen’s Model differentially influence healthcare-seeking intention across groups.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted in Wuhan, China, from July 2023 to August 2024, involving 2,890 participants. Latent Class Analysis (LCA) was employed to segment the population based on seven consideration attributes for choosing hospitals. Multinomial logistic regression was then used to analyze the influence of predisposing, enabling, and need factors. Specifically, it examined how these factors affected healthcare-seeking intention within each identified latent class.ResultsLCA identified four distinct preference groups: the Core Resource Preference Group (29.9%), the Medical Technology Preference Group (35.6%), the High-Quality & Low-Cost Preference Group (11.1%), and the Convenient Transportation Preference Group (23.5%). Healthcare-seeking intention varied significantly: the Medical Technology Group had the highest tertiary hospital selection rate (68.0%), while the Convenient Transportation Group had the highest combined rate for self-medication and primary care (57.6%). Regression analyses revealed heterogeneous influences: in the Core Resource and Convenient Transportation groups, higher confidence in family finances was associated with lower odds of choosing tertiary hospitals. In contrast, in the Medical Technology Group, worse past-year health status increased the likelihood of selecting secondary hospitals. Higher education consistently predicted tertiary hospital use across all groups.ConclusionUrban residents’ hospital-choice preferences are heterogeneous, and the factors influencing their healthcare-seeking intention vary significantly across different preference profiles. These findings suggest that “one-size-fits-all” policies are inadequate, and targeted interventions are needed. This study provides empirical evidence for designing more effective, population-specific strategies to promote a rational hierarchical healthcare system. Moreover, these findings indicate that the mechanisms underlying the healthcare-seeking intention of different preference groups require further exploration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12875-026-03449-5.

Introduction

China has a three-tier hospital system, which is divided according to the level and function of medical services [1, 2]. Level-one medical institutions, such as urban community health service centers and rural township health centers, provide primary care and public health services, while the secondary and tertiary hospitals provide specialized care [3]. This hierarchical system aims to promote the rational allocation of medical resources, facilitate a coordinated referral mechanism, and ensure that patients with different severity of conditions receive appropriate levels of care [4]. However, unlike the UK and Germany, which implement strict gate-keeping systems [5, 6], China does not force patients to diagnose and treat according to the level [7], residents are free to choose any hospital according to their preference [8, 9]. This policy, while emphasizing patient autonomy, has inadvertently contributed to a widespread “bypassing behavior,” where patients directly seek care at higher-level hospitals regardless of the severity of their condition. This phenomenon is particularly pronounced among urban residents, who have easier access to higher-level hospitals due to the concentration of high-quality medical resources in urban areas [10, 11]. They tend to bypass primary care institutions and choose these higher-level hospitals regardless of disease severity [12, 13]. This “bypassing behavior” has led to the underutilization of primary care resources and severe overcrowding in higher-level hospitals, threatening the overall efficiency and effectiveness of China’s health system [14, 15]. The “three longs and one short” phenomenon further illustrates this situation: long waiting times for registration, payment, and consultation, contrasted with short appointment durations [16]. This situation has generated significant patient dissatisfaction and contributed to the deterioration of doctor-patient relationships [17, 18]. This structural imbalance may worsen as rapid urbanization and economic development expand the urban population and corresponding healthcare demand [19].

In response, the Chinese government has made unprecedented investments to strengthen the health system. From 2018 to 2025, the government has invested a total of over 3 trillion yuan in medical security. China's ongoing health system reform aims to strengthen the construction of primary care institutions and guide patients to seek medical treatment at the grassroots level. Examples are the introduction of hierarchical medical insurance reimbursement schemes and the continuously increasing resource investment in the infrastructure of primary hospitals [20, 21]. However, the phenomenon of unbalanced utilization among hospitals still exists. Data from 2024 shows that primary healthcare institutions in China account for 95% of the total number of institutions, but their consultation rate is 63.4%, whereas tertiary hospitals, which account for only 0.38%, have a consultation rate of 67.72% [22].

To address these challenges, it is crucial for policymakers to understand not only where residents seek care but why they make specific choices. Andersen’s Model is a widely recognized theoretical framework for examining factors influencing healthcare-seeking behavior [23]. According to this model, an individual’s utilization of health services is contingent upon predisposing (e.g., demographics, social structure, health beliefs), enabling (e.g., income, insurance, social support), and need factors (e.g., perceived severity, self-rated health). Drawing on this framework, existing studies have identified various determinants of residents’ healthcare-seeking behavior. For instance, higher income and education levels correlate with a preference for tertiary or specialized hospitals [24]; the severity residents perceive in their condition serves as a key determinant—people with severe conditions prioritize clinical capacity, whereas those with minor ailments value accessibility and convenience [25, 26]. Additional factors, such as transportation distance [27], medical staff competence [7], and insurance reimbursement rates [20], also play significant roles. A review categorized these determinants into individual, facility, contextual, and composite dimensions, noting substantial heterogeneity in how different population groups weigh these factors [9].

Although prior studies utilize Andersen’s model have identified key determinants of hospital choice, they have predominantly treated the study population as homogeneous, implicitly assuming that the effects of these determinants operate uniformly across all individuals. This assumption conceals unobserved preference heterogeneity—the fact that different people may prioritize different service attributes when selecting a healthcare facility—and obscures how distinct combinations of selection criteria shape healthcare-seeking intentions. Understanding residents’ healthcare preferences is crucial for bridging the information gap between residents and healthcare policymakers. Accurately capturing these preferences can help reduce unnecessary medical expenditures and enhance the efficiency of health system reforms [28]. When residents’ values, priorities, and trade-offs are made explicit, policymakers can design high-quality, responsive healthcare delivery systems that align with population needs [29]. Indeed, growing evidence confirms that individuals actively make trade-offs when choosing healthcare providers, underscoring the necessity of investigating preference heterogeneity [30]..

To capture this heterogeneity, Latent Class Analysis (LCA) has been increasingly utilized in health services research. For example, Jiang et al. [31]. applied LCA to identify distinct preference groups among community residents based on four service attributes (e.g., care provider, mode of services, cost, travel time). While this approach successfully revealed preference heterogeneity, it remained descriptive. It did not further investigate how actual healthcare-seeking intention or behavior differs across these groups, nor did it examine which predisposing, enabling, or need factors drive their decisions within each preference profile. In other words, previous research has either focused on identifying preference classes or on applying Andersen’s model to the general population, but has rarely integrated the two to explain healthcare-seeking intentions within empirically derived preference subgroups.

To bridge this gap, our study employs a two-stage analytical framework that integrates data-driven segmentation with theory-driven explanation. In the first stage, we utilize Latent Class Analysis (LCA) to identify unobserved heterogeneity within the population [32]. Recognizing that healthcare facility selection is shaped by diverse individual backgrounds and prior experiences, LCA provides a flexible, data-driven approach to classifying respondents into mutually exclusive subgroups based on shared preference patterns, without imposing a priori demographic or theoretical grouping assumptions [33]. Specifically, we applied LCA to empirically derive distinct preference profiles based on respondents’ selection patterns across seven key facility attributes identified in prior literature: convenient transportation [34], low price [35], good medical technology [36], good equipment [29], good service attitude [37], short waiting time [38], and informal social networks [39]—in the Chinese context, informal social networks often serve as a compensatory mechanism to navigate systemic barriers, secure appointments, or mitigate perceived quality uncertainty. By empirically deriving preference profiles, LCA reveals distinct subgroups that share similar configurations of healthcare institution selection attributes. Segmenting the population into such preference-based subgroups can inform health policy design and enable the delivery of tailored services that align with each subgroup’s priorities.

In the second stage, we adopt Andersen’s Model as the theoretical framework to explain the behavioral intention within each identified preference group. Given that behavioral intention serves as a significant behavioral predictor [40], our study focuses on residents’ healthcare-seeking intention. Within each identified preference group, we employ Andersen’s model to examine how predisposing, enabling, and need factors are differentially associated with healthcare-seeking intentions. This integration of data-driven segmentation and theory-driven explanation provides a novel approach to revealing preference-based subgroups and their differential determinants. Ultimately, this integrated approach provides a more nuanced understanding of bypassing behavior and offers targeted policy pathways to guide residents toward more rational healthcare-seeking choices—aligning the level of care sought with clinical severity to optimize system efficiency and equity.

This study is guided by the following research questions: RQ1: What distinct latent preference subgroups exist among urban residents regarding their criteria for selecting healthcare institutions? RQ2: How do predisposing, enabling, and need factors differentially influence healthcare-seeking intentions across the identified latent subgroups?

Methods

Study design and setting

Considering that bypassing behavior is more prevalent in urban areas due to the concentration of high-quality medical resources, this study selected Wuhan, a mega-city in central China, as the study area. The Yangtze River and Han River flow through the city, dividing the central urban area into three major districts: Hankou, Hanyang, and Wuchang. Given the practical constraints of data accessibility, time, and funding, a community-based and enterprise-based convenience sampling strategy was employed. Participants were recruited from established community networks and manufacturing enterprises across the three districts. The data was collected from July 2023 to August 2024. While this sampling approach limits population-level generalizability, it effectively captures the healthcare-seeking intention of urban residents.

Participants

Participants were individuals aged ≥ 18 years, who could understand, read, and write Chinese, and were willing to participate. Individuals who were unable to independently complete the questionnaire were excluded. A total of 3011 responses were collected during the study period. After excluding submissions with incomplete responses, logical inconsistencies, or failure to meet eligibility criteria, 2,890 valid questionnaires were retained for analysis. Recruitment utilized a hybrid online-offline approach. For offline surveys, investigators established temporary survey stations at community health service centers and partner enterprises. Posters with study information were displayed, and trained interviewers verified eligibility before distributing questionnaires. For online surveys, community coordinators and enterprise representatives were invited to distribute QR codes linking to the digital questionnaire via internal communication channels. The poster explicitly instructed recipients to forward the link only to eligible individuals within their networks, which may introduced mild network bias but facilitated rapid recruitment. For the online survey, we used Wen-juan-xing, a widely recognized online survey platform in China. Each IP address and device could only submit the questionnaire once to ensure data quality and avoid duplicate responses. This study received ethical approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of the institution affiliated with the research team (2022-S182), and oral informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to survey administration.

Data collection tool

A self-designed questionnaire based on Andersen’s Model was used in this study. The initial items were generated through research group discussions and a comprehensive review of previous related studies. To ensure content validity, the draft questionnaire was reviewed by public health and clinical experts, as well as academic scholars, whose feedback was incorporated to refine the items. Subsequently, a pilot survey was conducted in Wuhan to further test and improve the questionnaire, leading to the final version (see Supplementary Material 1). For the scale in the questionnaire, internal consistency was confirmed (Cronbach’s α = 0.80). Construct validity was assessed via exploratory factor analysis, yielding a Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) value of 0.771 and a significant Bartlett’s test of sphericity (χ2 = 4492.691, p < 0.001).

Analytical framework and variables

We employed a two-stage analytical framework that integrated data-driven preference segmentation with theory-driven explanation (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Two-stage analytical framework

Two-stage analytical framework

Stage 1: Latent Class Analysis (LCA) for preference segmentation.

To capture unobserved heterogeneity in how residents prioritize healthcare facility attributes, we applied LCA. Based on respondents’ endorsement patterns across seven attributes—convenient transportation, low price, good medical technology, good equipment, good service attitude, short waiting time, and informal social networks—LCA empirically classified individuals into mutually exclusive latent subgroups.

Stage 2: Andersen’s Model for within-subgroup explanation.

Based on Andersen’s Model, we assumed that residents’ healthcare-seeking intention would be influenced by three groups of factors: predisposing, enabling, and need factors [41]. Predisposing factors, such as demographic characteristics, social structure, and other psychological factors, refer to an individual’s propensity to seek care that could predispose to use of health care [42]. Enabling factors denote the favorable conditions that help satisfy individuals’ medical treatment needs, including community resources (availability of healthcare resources, transportation), and personal resources (financial ability and medical insurance). Need factors can trigger individuals’ needs for care services, including health status.

The dependent variable reflects respondents’ healthcare-seeking intention when they experience a health problem, categorized into four mutually exclusive options: (1) self-medication or others (e.g., purchasing medication on one’s own, home remedies, or other informal treatments), (2) primary healthcare institution (community health center/township clinic), (3) secondary hospital, and (4) tertiary hospital. Although self-medication occurs prior to formal system entry, it is integrated into this framework as it represents a critical decision node on the care-seeking continuum, shaped by identical predisposing, enabling, and need determinants. Based on three dimensions of Andersen’s model—predisposing characteristics, enabling resources, and need factors—this study operationalized the independent variables. Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of participants. Quantitative data were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages.

Latent class analysis

Latent Class Analysis (LCA) was performed using Mplus version 8.3 to identify unobserved subgroups based on respondents’ selection of up to seven attributes: convenient transportation, low price, good medical technology, good equipment/facilities, good service attitude, short waiting time, and informal social networks. Model fit was evaluated using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), sample-size-adjusted BIC (aBIC), Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMR-LRT), Bootstrap Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT), and entropy [43]. Smaller values of AIC, BIC, and aBIC indicate a better model fit. The LMR-LRT and BLRT were applied to compare the model fit differences between the (k-1)-class and k-class models, thereby verifying the statistical rationality of increasing the number of latent classes [44]. Entropy, which ranges from 0 to 1, was used to assess the classification accuracy of the model, with values closer to 1 indicating a more precise classification [45]. The resulting latent class memberships were subsequently used as a grouping variable to further investigate the heterogeneous characteristics of different latent classes of residents in their healthcare-seeking intention.

Multinomial logistic regression

A multinomial logit model was employed to identify the determinants of healthcare-seeking intention because we assumed that the alternative options were distinct, mutually exclusive, and have different attributes. Healthcare-seeking intention was modeled as the dependent variable across four distinct categories (self-medication or others, primary healthcare institution, secondary hospital, tertiary hospital), with “tertiary hospital” designated as the reference group. The independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA) assumption was assessed using the Hausman–McFadden test implemented in R version 4.5.2. Non-significant test outcomes (all p > 0.05) indicated no violation of the IIA assumption, thereby validating the appropriateness of the multinomial logit model [46]. Subsequent multinomial logistic regression analyses were conducted using SPSS version 27.0, with results reported as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Latent class analysis of urban residents

Latent Class Analysis was conducted to identify unobserved preference subgroups. Models with two to seven latent classes were estimated using maximum likelihood with robust standard errors (MLR) in Mplus version 8.3. Model selection followed established guidelines [47], integrating multiple fit indices, statistical tests, classification quality, analytical feasibility, and theoretical interpretability. As shown in Table 3, information criteria (AIC, BIC, aBIC) decreased monotonically as the number of classes increased, but the rate of improvement substantially attenuated after the four-class solution. Entropy peaked at the five-class model (0.985); however, the four-class model maintained excellent classification accuracy (0.925). Statistical comparison tests (LMR-LRT and BLRT) were significant for the transition from three to four classes (both p < 0.001), supporting the addition of a fourth class. For models with five or more classes, although BLRT p-values remained statistically significant, the majority of bootstrap replications failed to replicate the optimal log-likelihood for the more complex model, even after substantially increasing random starts and the number of bootstrap draws. This pattern reflects a complex likelihood surface with multiple local maxima, rendering the BLRT p-values for k ≥ 5 statistically unreliable [48]. Furthermore, the relative size of the smallest class declined to 9.59% (k = 5) and 6.71% (k = 6, 7), approaching thresholds where subsequent multinomial regression analyses would face instability due to sparse cells. Balancing statistical fit, classification precision, analytical robustness, and real-world interpretability, the four-class model was selected as the optimal solution. Class 1, the Comprehensive Preference Group, simultaneously endorsed three attributes—convenient transportation, low price, and medical technology—each with a probability of 1.00, while the probabilities of the remaining attributes are at or near zero. By prioritizing the largest number of attributes among all latent groups, this group demonstrated a broad and comprehensive set of preferences for healthcare access.

Class 2, the Medical Technology Preference Group, exhibited an endorsement probability of 1.00 for medical technology, with substantially lower probabilities for all other attributes. This pattern indicates a singular emphasis on the technical quality of care.

Class 3, the High-Quality and Low-Cost Preference Group, was characterized by high endorsement probabilities for medical technology (0.89) and low price (0.82), while the remaining attributes exhibited very low probabilities. This reflects a dual focus on affordability and technical quality.

Class 4, the Convenient Transportation Preference Group, showed a strong preference for convenient transportation (0.76) and zero probability of endorsing medical technology, highlighting the importance of geographic accessibility for this subgroup.

Distribution of healthcare-seeking intention across latent classes

The chi-square test revealed statistically significant differences in healthcare-seeking intention across the four latent class groups (χ2 = 280.648, p < 0.001). Specifically, C1 showed that 51.8% selected tertiary hospitals and 24.1% selected primary healthcare institutions. C2 exhibited the highest tertiary hospital selection rate (68.0%), with comparatively lower proportions for all other options. C3 reported 55.8% selected tertiary hospitals and 19.1% selected primary healthcare institutions. C4 demonstrated the highest combined proportion for self-medication (30.0%) and primary healthcare institution selection (27.6%), totaling 57.6%, while showing the lowest tertiary hospital selection rate (32.4%).

Results of multinomial logistic regression on healthcare-seeking intention among four groups

The findings revealed that the four models for each latent class were statistically significant (P < 0.001), indicating that each model could effectively distinguish healthcare-seeking intention among latent groups and could be used to identify the influencing factors.

The Comprehensive Preference Group

In this group, healthcare-seeking intention was primarily driven by age, education, and financial capability. Younger participants (18–25 years) were significantly more likely to choose primary healthcare institutions (OR = 5.001) and self-medication (OR = 2.899) over tertiary hospitals compared to those aged ≥ 45 years. Similarly, participants with middle school education or below showed increased odds of choosing primary care (OR = 2.302) compared to those with a bachelor’s degree or above, while females were less likely than males to visit primary healthcare institutions (OR = 0.554). Financial capability was a critical enabling factor. Participants with UEBMI were less likely to visit primary healthcare institutions compared to the uninsured (OR = 0.551). Those with low financial resilience were significantly less likely to choose self-medication (OR = 0.343) compared to financially secure individuals. Additionally, low health management willingness predicted a higher likelihood of self-medication (OR = 2.293). Health status in the past year and self-rated health status did not significantly influence choices. The Medical Technology Preference Group

In this group, need factors, health management willingness, and educational background significantly affected residents’ healthcare-seeking intention. Participants with poor health status in the past year were more likely to choose secondary hospitals (OR = 2.363) compared to those with good health status, while better self-rated health status was associated with higher odds of choosing primary care (OR = 1.030). Low health management willingness significantly increased the odds of selecting primary healthcare institutions (OR = 4.024). Lower education (high school or below) was associated with higher odds of choosing primary (OR = 3.222) and secondary hospitals (OR = 2.170) compared to the bachelor’s degree or above. Conversely, enabling resources and other predisposing characteristics did not significantly influence choices (Supplementary Appendix 2).

The high-quality and low-cost preference group

For this latent class group’s healthcare-seeking intention, educational background emerged as the dominant predictor. Relative to the bachelor’s degree or above, participants with high school education or below showed markedly elevated odds of selecting primary (OR = 5.724) and secondary hospitals (OR = 3.489). Low health management willingness also significantly increased the likelihood of choosing primary healthcare institutions (OR = 4.137). In contrast, enabling resources and health status in the past year failed to reach statistical significance. (Supplementary Appendix 3).

The convenient transportation preference group

In this group, participants’ healthcare-seeking intention was primarily driven by age, education, health management willingness, and enabling factors. Younger participants (18–35 years) were significantly more likely to choose self-medication and primary healthcare institutions over tertiary hospitals compared to those aged ≥ 45 years (ORs = 2.627–6.050). Similarly, participants with high school education or below showed increased odds of utilizing primary (OR = 1.939) and secondary hospitals (OR = 2.707). Additionally, low health management willingness predicted a higher likelihood of self-medication (OR = 2.782). In enabling factors, financial confidence was positively associated with convenient care options; participants with lower financial resilience were significantly less likely to choose self-medication (OR = 0.366) and primary care (OR = 0.369) compared to those with high confidence. Residents who agreed with the statement “Good accessibility to local healthcare resources” were more inclined to choose primary hospitals over tertiary hospitals than those who strongly agreed (OR = 2.443). Health status in the past year and self-rated health status did not significantly influence choices (Supplementary Appendix 4).

Sensitivity analyses

To ensure robustness, multicollinearity among covariates was formally assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF) and tolerance statistics. Across all models, VIF values ranged from 1.045 to 2.134, and tolerance values ranged from 0.469 to 0.957, indicating negligible multicollinearity among predictors [50].

We conducted two sensitivity analyses to verify the robustness of our primary findings. First, to address potential sampling bias arising from the overrepresentation of male participants (76.7% in our sample versus 51.4% in the Wuhan population aged 18–45 years, per the 2023 Wuhan Statistical Yearbook), post-stratification weights were applied (weight = 0.66 for males; 2.12 for females). Weighted multinomial logistic regression models yielded effect directions and significance levels highly consistent with the unweighted models (Supplementary Appendix S1-S4). Second, acknowledging the conceptual distinction between self-medication and formal healthcare facility selection, we excluded the “self-medication or others” category and re-estimated the model across the three institutional tiers. The re-specified model produced coefficient estimates and significance patterns that closely aligned with the primary results, confirming that the inclusion of self-medication did not distort the core preference determinants (Supplementary Appendix Y1–Y4). Collectively, these sensitivity analyses demonstrate that our main conclusions remain stable under alternative weighting schemes and conceptual specifications of the dependent variable.

Discussion

Latent class analysis identified four distinct preference groups: the Comprehensive Preference Group (C1) (29.9%), the Medical Technology Preference Group (C2) (35.5%), the High-Quality and Low-Cost Preference Group (C3) (11.1%), and the Convenient Transportation Preference Group (C4) (23.5%). Regression results revealed significant heterogeneity in how Andersen’s model factors influence choices across these groups, underscoring the inadequacy of “one-size-fits-all” perspectives.

Differences in healthcare institution choices across preference groups

C1 balanced technology, cost, and convenience, primarily selecting tertiary and primary hospitals. C2, technology-driven and cost-insensitive, showed the highest tertiary dependence. C3 prioritized medical technology and cost, pursuing cost-effective services. More than half of them also chose to seek treatment at a tertiary hospital. Aligning with Rational Choice Theory, both C1 and C3 resembled utility maximizers balancing technical assurance and cost control [51]. However, constrained by information asymmetry [52], residents struggled to independently assess technical quality, relying instead on hospital level as a proxy signal for quality [53–55]. Their choice was concentrated in tertiary hospitals may reflect a choice strategy under bounded rationality: under information constraints, individuals mitigated perceived technical risks by selecting higher-level facilities, tending to find solutions that are adequate rather than optimal [56, 57]. C4 prioritized convenience, displaying the most balanced distribution with the lowest tertiary selection and high self-medication and primary care use. The overall data indicate that, when medical care is required, the respondents in this study generally tend to seek treatment at tertiary hospitals, which is consistent with the healthcare-seeking behaviour and intention of most urban residents in China. Conversely, secondary hospitals are consistently the least selected, suggesting that such medical institutions are less attractive to the surveyed population, which is consistent with the actual situation. In early 2023, outpatient visits at tertiary and primary hospitals rose by 8.7% and 9.0% respectively, while secondary hospitals saw a 7.6% decline [58]. Secondary hospitals are trapped in an awkward situation: insufficiently accessible for basic care, yet inadequately equipped for inpatient cases, ultimately marginalized from the system’s original intent [59].

Factors influencing the choice of healthcare institutions among preference groups

Predisposing characteristics significantly shaped healthcare choices across all latent classes. Higher educational attainment consistently predicted tertiary hospital utilization in all four groups. This pattern aligns with previous studies, which have reported that individuals with higher education tend to prefer higher-level facilities [60, 61]. Age and gender did not show significant associations with healthcare choices in every subgroup. Their effects varied across groups, suggesting that the influence of these predisposing factors on healthcare-seeking intentions may be contingent on an individual’s preference profile. Future research could further examine how age and gender interact with preference characteristics to shape healthcare-seeking intention. Across the four latent groups, health management willingness was consistently associated with healthcare choices. Higher willingness was associated with a greater probability of choosing tertiary hospitals. This finding is consistent with the positive correlation between health management willingness and health literacy, health awareness, and a preference for high medical quality [62].

Enabling resources emerged as decisive factors influencing healthcare-seeking intention within the Comprehensive Preference Group (C1) and the Convenient Transportation Preference Group (C4), whereas they showed no significant association in the Medical Technology Preference Group (C2) and the High-Quality and Low-Cost Preference Group (C3). Specifically, within the C1 group, individuals with UEBMI were significantly less likely to choose primary healthcare institutions compared to the uninsured, preferring tertiary hospitals instead. This finding diverges from prior research suggesting that gradient reimbursement schemes under the hierarchical medical system effectively guide patients toward primary care [63]. In China, however, UEBMI provides more comprehensive service coverage and financial protection than other insurance types [64, 65], and its beneficiaries typically possess stable employment and higher payment capabilities. Moreover, employees often face conflicts between health management and occupational responsibilities. Concerned about work interruptions or adverse career impacts due to sick leave, they tend to seek tertiary hospitals for definitive solutions to avoid the temporal and experiential costs associated with ineffective primary treatment followed by referrals. Thus, UEBMI may inadvertently facilitate upward-seeking behavior among populations balancing competing healthcare priorities—convenient transportation, affordability, and high-quality technology. Consequently, policy interventions should transcend reliance on economic incentives alone, prioritizing the enhancement of primary care technical competencies to align service quality with residents’ preferences and expectations.

In both C1 and C4 groups, higher confidence in family economic capacity was associated with a lower likelihood of selecting tertiary hospitals. This finding differs from some existing studies, which suggest that high-income individuals prioritize physicians’ qualifications and medical competence, and prefer excellent doctors rather than proximity or less cost [66–68]. We attribute this discrepancy to the lack of fine-grained population stratification in prior research, which typically examined economic factors and healthcare choices from an aggregate perspective without considering heterogeneous preferences. This indicates that preferences moderate the effects of enabling resources. Future research should further investigate the associations and potential underlying mechanisms linking individual or household economic capacity to healthcare-seeking behavior across distinct preference configurations.

The accessibility of healthcare is a multidimensional concept encompassing affordability, acceptability, and spatial accessibility [69, 70]. In this study, we defined it as residents’ subjective perception of the convenience in obtaining local medical care or medications, accounting for both supply volume and proximity. Within the C4 preference profile, participants who selected “agree” (rather than “strongly agree”) regarding the ease of accessing local medical resources demonstrated a higher likelihood of preferring primary care institutions over tertiary hospitals. According to the classical psychometric framework of the Likert scale [71], the distinction between “strongly agree” and “agree” represents a gradient in the intensity of subjective agreement rather than a qualitative difference; the former reflects a strong degree of conviction, whereas the latter indicates endorsement with a relatively lower intensity. This finding indicates that, among individuals who prioritize convenience, the strength of subjective endorsement regarding perceived accessibility is associated with distinct healthcare-seeking intentions. However, two methodological limitations warrant consideration. First, this study assessed only subjective perceived accessibility, without capturing objective spatial metrics. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; it remains unclear whether heightened perceptions of accessibility influence healthcare-seeking behaviors, or whether prior utilization of specific facilities subsequently alters accessibility perceptions. Future research could integrate multi-dimensional accessibility perception scales with objective geographic indicators (e.g., travel time, network distance) to elucidate the synergistic mechanisms through which subjective perceptions and actual spatial accessibility jointly shape healthcare-seeking decisions.

Need factors were significantly associated with healthcare-seeking intention only in the Medical Technology Preference Group (C2). In C2, higher self-rated health scores were associated with a greater probability of choosing primary hospitals over tertiary hospitals, a finding consistent with existing studies. This study comprehensively measured an individual’s physical health status over the past year from three aspects: whether they experienced physical discomfort, whether they self-medicated, and whether they took leave to seek a doctor. “Worse” health status was defined as having experienced all three conditions in the past year, while “good” health status was defined as having none of these conditions. In C2, compared to those with “good” health status, respondents with “worse” health status had a significantly higher probability of choosing secondary hospitals over tertiary hospitals. This finding differs from existing research results, which have generally reported that poorer health status is associated with a higher likelihood of selecting higher-level hospitals [72, 73]. It should be noted that our study did not directly measure respondents’ subjective evaluations of service quality at different tiers of healthcare facilities, nor track their decision-making processes or specific care pathways. Therefore, the current data do not allow an explanation of the mechanisms underlying this association. Future research, for example through qualitative studies, could further investigate the healthcare-seeking intention of technology-preferring individuals with poor health and explore the potential mechanisms underlying their decision-making processes.

Implication

This study found that residents’ latent preferences constitute differentiated decision-making contexts, and the effects of enabling resources and need factors vary across these contexts. This finding suggests that future research may focus on the interaction effects between preferences and influencing factors, and explore the boundary conditions and activation thresholds of each factor under different decision-making frameworks.

The findings provide population segmentation-based empirical evidence for optimizing the tiered healthcare delivery system and guiding residents’ rational healthcare-seeking behavior. Significant differences exist in healthcare-seeking intention among groups with distinct preferences. Meanwhile, within the same preference group, individuals’ predisposing characteristics, enabling resources, and need factors still finely shape their diverse healthcare choices. This implies that policy design should consider both inter-group differences and intra-group heterogeneity.

Strengths and Limitations

This research more precisely revealed the heterogeneity in residents’ healthcare-seeking intention, and further explored how predisposing characteristics, enabling resources, and need factors interact with core preferences within each group to jointly shape their intention. These findings deepen the understanding of residents’ healthcare decision-making processes, and offer valuable implications for promoting the rational allocation of medical resources.

The study also has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design does not allow causal inferences between variables. All associations should be interpreted as correlational rather than causal. Future research can adopt a longitudinal design to track actual behaviors, integrate objective indicators of healthcare utilization, and employ qualitative research to explore the possible mechanisms underlying the associations between healthcare-seeking intention and various factors among different preference groups. Second, convenience sampling was used, and the sample was drawn from a single city (Wuhan). In addition, the sample overrepresented males and manufacturing workers. Given the single-city, non-probability cross-sectional design, the findings should be interpreted with caution and are most applicable to similar urban populations. Multi-site probability surveys in future studies are warranted to test external validity and validate these results across diverse settings. Third, in the latent class analysis, binary coding of multiple-response preference items may limit preference intensity and ranking gradients. Future research can use discrete choice experiments to capture finer decision-making nuances.

Conclusion

This study elucidates the profound heterogeneity underlying urban residents’ healthcare-seeking intention by integrating latent class analysis with Andersen’s model. We identified four distinct preference groups, and demonstrated that predisposing, enabling, and need factors exert different influences depending on individual preference structures. Specifically, among convenience-oriented groups, economic security encourages the selection of accessible primary hospitals, and those who perceive good access to local medical resources are more inclined to choose primary hospitals rather than tertiary hospitals. In the technology-oriented group, residents reporting poorer health status in the past year are more likely to choose secondary hospitals over tertiary hospitals. Moreover, for the group pursuing “high-quality, accessible, and affordable” care, UEBMI may inadvertently induce upward healthcare-seeking behavior. Ultimately, this study offers a novel perspective for analyzing the associations between preferences and healthcare-seeking intention and their influencing factors. It also provides directions for future research in populations with similar characteristics and delivers empirical evidence from these urban residents to inform local healthcare resource allocation and the improvement of the hierarchical medical system.
