Predicting children and adolescents at high risk of poor health‑related quality of life using machine learning methods
Authors: Chang Xiong, Lili Zhang, Zhijuan Li, Jiaqi Chen, Hongdan Qian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExisting research has identified health‑related quality of life (HRQoL) is influenced by a multitude of factors among children and adolescents. However, there has been relatively limited exploration of the multidimensional predictive factors (individual characteristics, health risk behaviors, and negative life events) that contribute to HRQoL. This study aimed to develop a nomogram to predict the HRQoL in children and adolescents.MethodsA total of 12,145 children and adolescents were surveyed using stratified cluster sampling method, randomly divided into a training set (n = 8503) and a validation set (n = 3642). Logistic regression, lasso regression, and random forest models were combined to identify the most significant predictors of HRQoL. A nomogram was constructed using multivariate logistic regression. The receiver operating characteristic curve, k-fold cross-validation, decision curve analysis (DCA), and internal validation were used to assess the accuracy, discrimination, and generalization of the nomogram.ResultsNon-suicidal self-injury, academic burnout, parental abuse, stress, bullying victimization, healthy diet, and sleep were found to be significant predictors of HRQoL. The area under the curve (AUC) of the training set was 0.765, whereas that of the validation data was 0.775. The k-fold cross-validation (k = 10) revealed good discrimination in internal validation (mean AUC = 0.771). The nomogram had good clinical use since the DCA covered a large threshold probability: 5%-89% (in the training set) and 4%-81% (in the validation set).ConclusionsThe nomogram prediction model constructed in this study can provide a reference for predicting HRQoL in children and adolescents.Keywords: Children and adolescents, Health-related quality of life, High risk, Nomogram, Machine learning

Introduction

Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) reflects individuals’ perceived physical and mental health status of individuals during specific time frames. It measures the influence of diseases or other factors on personal health, mental function, social relationships and overall status [1, 2]. Recently, an increasing number of studies have utilized HRQoL to evaluate the health outcomes of children and adolescents [3, 4], which is essential for developing of effective interventions to enhance the health status of this population. Studies in multiple countries have found that young individuals generally have better health-related quality of life [5–7]. Some studies have shown that levels of HRQoL among children are inconsistent in healthy populations and populations with health problems [8, 9].

Previous studies have demonstrated that HRQoL is influenced by many factors among children and adolescents. These include: individual characteristics [10, 11], such as sex, grade level, and overweight and obesity; lifestyle and behavior patterns [10, 12], such as screen time, sleeping time, and physical activity (PA); and negative life events [13, 14], such as internet addiction and bullying victimization. Despite several studies investigating the factors that influence health-related quality of life among children and adolescents, these studies have typically concentrated on a single dimension of influence. There has been relatively limited exploration of the multidimensional predictive factors contributing to HRQoL. Notably, most studies on HRQoL among children and adolescents have been descriptive, with a paucity of research adopting predictive and perspective approaches and exploring the impact of negative life events on HRQoL.

The objective of this study was to construct a predictive model for HRQoL among children and adolescents using multidimensional predictive indicators for the first time. Recently, predictive modelling through machine learning has emerged as a powerful means of mining and analyzing big data on children and young individuals, with the potential to identify valuable outcomes [15]. In this study, individual characteristics, lifestyle and behavior patterns, and negative life events were considered as candidate predictors of HRQoL. The most significant predictors were identified using random forest and Lasso regression models to construct a risk prediction model for poorer HRQoL among children and adolescents.

Method

Participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted in Wuxi, China, from September to October 2023, using a multistage stratified cluster sampling method. Schools were selected from each of the city’s seven administrative districts. Two primary schools, two junior high schools, two senior high schools, and one vocational high school were selected randomly from each district. Within these schools, more than two classes were randomly chosen from each grade to ensure a sample size of over 80 students per grade (primary school begins in the fourth grade). The inclusion criteria were students who (1) provided their own and their guardians’ consent for participating in this study, and (2) could complete the questionnaires. Students with severe physical or mental disabilities were excluded from this analysis. A questionnaire survey and clinical examination were conducted for each participant. A total of 12,365 questionnaires were collected. After excluding cases with missing key information and obvious logical errors, 12,145 questionnaires were deemed valid for analysis. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuxi Center for Disease Control and Prevention, and informed consent was obtained from the participating students and their guardians.

Measurement

This study investigated a range of independent variables across three distinct categories: individual characteristics, health risk behaviors (HRBs), and negative life events. Individual characteristics included location, sex, ethnicity, current level of schooling, highest level of parental education, current stage of living with parents, living in school, disease status, body weight status, and stress symptoms. The HRBs include healthy diet, sleeping time, screen time, PA, smoking, Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI), internet addiction, and academic burnout. Negative life events include bullying victimization, serious injuries, sick leave from school, and parental abuse.

EQ-5D-Y(−3L)

We used the EuroQol five-dimensional questionnaire, youth version (EQ-5D-Y) instrument to measure HRQoL in children and adolescents. The EQ-5D-Y is the youth version of the EQ-5D instrument, designed to serve as a brief questionnaire and an age-appropriate gauge of HRQoL [16]. The EQ-5D-Y has been validated across numerous countries and regions [17]. Although the Chinese version of the EQ-5D-Y is not frequently employed, it has demonstrated satisfactory reliability in evaluating the HRQoL of Chinese children and adolescents [12]. The EQ-5D-Y(−3L) instrument encompasses five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Each dimension was measured on three levels: no difficulty, some difficulty, and extreme difficulty. To calculate the combined health utility, the responses across these five health dimensions were weighted based on Chinese EQ-5D-3L value sets developed in 2018 [18]. In this study, the health utility scores ranged from 0.1702 to 1. A binary outcome was used to analyze the HRQoL. Specifically, the poor HRQoL was defined as a score lower than the 10th percentile for the scores (0.9367 for this scale).

Bullying victimization

The revised Chinese version of the Bullying Victimization Questionnaire developed by Dan Olweus [19] was utilized, with the following six item: being made fun of maliciously in or around school within the previous 30 days; being asked for money; being intentionally excluded from group activities or isolated; being threatened and intimidated; being hit, kicked, pushed, shoved, or locked up in a house; and being insulted because of physical defects or looks. The options consist of “Never,” “Sometimes,” “Often,” with the choices of “often” representing “Bullying victimization.”

Stress

The stress module of the DASS 21 [20] was used to assess stress symptoms among children and adolescents. Higher scores on this subscale indicate a greater severity of stress symptoms. A score greater than 14 indicates the presence of stress symptoms, and vice versa for the absence of stress symptoms. The Cronbach’s alpha was 0.907.

Internet addiction

Internet addiction was assessed using the Internet Addiction Questionnaire from the Chinese Adolescent Health-Related Risk Behavior Questionnaire [21]. The scale consists of ten items. Item 1 is “Average daily non-work-related internet usage time ≥ 4 hours.” The remaining nine items are divided into the following categories: withdrawal symptoms (two items), tolerance symptoms (one item), escapism behaviors (three items), internet control disorder (two items), and denial behaviors (one item). Internet addiction was defined as spending an average of ≥ 4 h per day on the Internet for non-work study purposes and more than four of the remaining nine entries were “yes.”

Academic burnout

Wu et al. developed an academic burnout scale in Chinese [22]. The scale consists of 16 items, including three dimensions (academic exhaustion, academic alienation and personal accomplishment) related to academic burnout. All the students completed the questionnaire using a 5-point Likert-type scale. Academic burnout was defined as a score higher than the 75th percentile for the scores (49 for this scale). The Cronbach’s alpha was 0.848.

Other variables

(a) overweight or obesity: body mass index was calculated based on students’ height and weight. Screening for overweight and obesity among school-age children and adolescents: WS/T 586–2018 assesses whether a student is overweight or obese [23]. (b) disease: severe illness during the preceding year, such as hepatitis, nephritis, heart disease, diabetes, allergic diseases, physical disabilities, etc.; (c) healthy diet: according to the Interpretation on Dietary Guidelines for Chinese School-aged Children (2022) [24], a healthy diet is defined as meeting the following criteria: no consumption of sugary drinks, no consumption of fried food, consumption of fresh fruit at least once a day, consumption of fresh vegetables at least once a day, and consumption of breakfast every day; (d) sleeping time: total sleep duration was categorized as “insufficient” or “sufficient” based on Health Requirements of Daily Study Time of Primary and Secondary School Students (GB/T 17223 − 2012) [25] in China; (e) PA: PA was reported as the frequency of at least 60 min of moderate and vigorous physical activity (MVPA) in the preceding 7 days. Sufficient PA was defined as “at least 60 min per day MVPA plus ≥ 2 times per week”; (f) screen time: screen time is defined as any non-learning activity involving the use of a screen, such as watching television, viewing videos or playing video games. This was divided into response categories: <2 h a day and ≥ 2 h a day, according to the physical activity guidelines for children and adolescents in China [26]; (g) NSSI: this was assessed using the following questions: in the past 12 months, have you ever engaged in deliberate self-harm without the intention of killing yourself (e.g., cigarette burns, self-inflicted cuts with a razor blade, banging one’s head against a wall, etc.)? The answer options for each case were “Yes” and “No.” When the answer to the questions was “yes,” it indicated the presence of NSSI behavior; (h) parental abuse: the question is posed as to whether the participant has been physically disciplined or verbally scolded by a parent within the past 30 days. The answer options for each case were “Yes” and “No.” When the answer to the questions was “yes,” it indicated the presence of parental abuse.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using R statistical software (R4.3.0). The data were randomly divided into two groups at a ratio of 7:3 as a training set and a validation set. The training set was used to train the predictive model and adjust the parameters of the fitted curves; the validation set was used to evaluate the model. Summary statistics are presented as frequency (percentage), and univariate logistic regression analyses were used to compare the associations between different variables and HRQoL, with P < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Significant predictors related to HRQoL were screened using the random forest model and the Lasso regression model. Among the model parameters in the random forest model [27], the mean decrease gini (MDG) of random forest reflected the extent to which the variable affected the outcome events.The number of predicted evaluation indicators (mtry) is the number of randomly selected evaluation indicators used to construct a random tree. The number of random trees (ntree) specifies the number of decision trees in the random forest. The optimal number of features for the random forest model is to be selected based on the error rate of out-of-bag (OOB) samples. The variables that meet the statistical significance requirement (P < 0.05) in univariate analysis and the top 50% of MDG in the random forest will be included in subsequent analyses.

The Lasso regression reduces the correlation between the variables and ensures that the predictive model is not overfitted [28]. The intersection variables of these two methods were selected to establish a nomogram. The model’s discriminative ability was assessed by the area under curve (AUC) of the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC). A k-fold cross-validation method was used to validate the model, with k = 10. AUC value greater than 0.70 indicates a satisfactory model for discrimination [29]. The calibration assessed the model’s goodness of fit, which was internally validated with 1000 bootstrapped resamples. Assessment of the clinical applicability was conducted with decision curve analysis (DCA). Results with P < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Development of the predictive nomogram

The nomogram and formula of the risk model were established based on the seven optimal predictive variables in the above analysis. The formula is as follows: −2.270 + 1.224 * NSSI (Yes) + 0.847 * Academic burnout (Yes) + 0.815 * Parental abuse (Yes) + 1.541 * Stress (Yes) + 0.995 * Bullying victimization (Yes) + (−0.398) * Healthy diet (Yes) + (−0.363) * Sleeping time (Sufficient). In Fig. 2, the corresponding score for each risk factor is determined using the first column of the nomogram. The predicted probability corresponding to the total score after the sum of the individual scores was the probability of poor HRQoL in children and adolescents. The higher the total score, the greater the extent of the poor HRQoL. The nomogram showed that stress corresponded to the highest risk score (100 points).

Proposed nomogram for poor HRQoL

Performance and validation of the nomogram

As shown in Fig. 3, the AUCs of the nomogram were 0.765 (95% CI: 0.750–0.780) and 0.775 (95% CI: 0.752–0.797) for the training and validation sets, respectively. In the validation set, the results of k-fold cross-validation showed the average value of AUC was 0.771 (k = 10). Calibration curves, which were plotted for both the training and the validation sets, indicated a high goodness of fit between the predicted and observed values (Fig. 4). In addition, DCA showed that the nomogram model had a net benefit when the risk threshold was 5%−89% in the training set. And the range of risk threshold in the validation set was 4%−81% (Fig. 5).

Discussion

The main aim of this survey was to examine the association between the HRQoL and various factors. Additionally, the nomogram was constructed to predict the risk of poor HRQoL among children and adolescents based on the best combination of predictors for the first time. As a visual tool, the nomogram model could predict the incidence of individual poor HRQoL through scoring system, which was effectively applied to the general population. In previous studies, logistic regression was the most commonly used prediction model to investigate the factors influencing HRQoL [30, 31]. To avoid the problem of overfitting, this study combine random forest and lasso regression on this basis, and the results were then visualized. In this study, NSSI, academic burnout, parental abuse, stress, bullying victimization, healthy diet, and sleeping time were identified to be independent indicators for HRQoL in children and adolescents.

With the increasing number of studies on HRQoL among the general population of healthy children and adolescents, there is a growing understanding of HRQoL in this population [4, 9, 11, 12, 32, 33]. Most of these studies explore the association between HRBs and HRQoL. Simultaneously, only a few examine the association between negative life events and HRQoL, and most studies were descriptive. Systematic and comprehensive risk prediction models for predicting poor HRQoL are still lacking. The nomogram selected variables from multiple aspects, including individual characteristics, HRBs, and negative life events, making it comprehensive in predicting HRQoL. In the final model, the NSSI was a relatively significant predictor. Children and adolescents who engaged in NSSI reported lower HRQoL than those without NSSI. Previous studies agreed with this conclusion regarding NSSI [9, 34]; however, the association between HRQoL and NSSI could be reciprocal. Furthermore, no findings regarding the direction of causality between HRQoL and NSSI were identified. Children and adolescents who experienced persistent stress levels were more prone to change in their general health status [35]. This investigation and other studies have shown that stress symptoms are associated with poor HRQoL [36, 37]. It has been demonstrated that stress management interventions can enhance quality of life and health outcomes [38]. Furthermore, the impact of varying degrees of stress on individuals is inconsistent [39]. Consequently, further research is required to develop a range of flexible intervention strategies that can cater to individuals experiencing different degrees of stress to enhance health outcomes.

Three predictors of HRQoL in HBRs are diet, sleeping time and academic burnout, which were closely associated with the HRQoL of children and adolescents [4, 40]. These findings are corroborated by similar results in Western and Asian countries [4, 41], indicating that the relationships between lifestyle behavior and HRQoL are pervasive among children and adolescents. Regarding sleep duration, it has been documented that short sleep duration could decrease in the levels of neurotransmitters that regulate mood and cognitive functioning [42]. This could be a pathological factor contributing to poorer HRQoL. Furthermore, the findings indicated that meeting the sleep duration recommendations was associated with more mental health benefits than meeting physical activity recommendations [43]. The results of this study showed that sleep duration, but not the amount of physical exercise, was identified as a significant predictor of HRQoL, further highlighting the importance of improving sleep duration for HRQoL. Many studies have examined the effects of various dietary behaviors, such as sugar-sweetened beverages, breakfast eating, and consumption of fruits and vegetables, on HRQoL and health outcomes among children and youth [4, 12, 33, 44]. In particular, an increase in unhealthy patterns of consumption is associated with many unhealthy outcomes, such as cardiovascular disease, anxiety, and cancer [45, 46]. Although the mechanisms and pathways are not precise, these studies suggest that decision-makers and public health workers can use dietary habits to assess a population’s HRQoL. To the best of our knowledge, only a few studies have explored the relationship between academic burnout and HRQoL in children and adolescents. Our study found that children and adolescents who experienced academic burnout were more likely to have poor HRQoL. Academic burnout has been identified as a global issue with a significant association with a range of negative health consequences, including cardiovascular disease, hypertension and mental health problems [47–49]. This can further lead to a poor quality of life for those affected.

Concerning factors related to negative life events, parental abuse and bullying victimization were found to be significant predictors. Adolescents subjected to bullying and/or abuse have been found to exhibit a decline in their mental health and well-being [50–52]. Neurobiology explains that any form of child abuse (physical, sexual, emotional abuse, or neglect) causes permanent changes in brain development [53]. In addition, as a consequence of bullying, adolescents can experience feelings of shame, which could have a detrimental impact on their self-esteem and self-perception, ultimately leading to a reduction in the quality of their life [54].

The nomogram of this study has good discrimination, with the AUC of the training set and the validation set being 0.765 and 0.775, respectively. The good calibration observed in our study indicates the model’s reliability in a clinical setting. Moreover, the DCA curve shows that the model provides good net benefits across a wide range of thresholds, highlighting the practical utility of our nomogram in clinical settings. Establishing of this research model will help identify high-risk individuals in the general population among children and adolescents. This will enable healthcare institutions, schools, and families to tailor interventions to the specific needs of these individuals, which is of great significance for public health policy and population research, and will help improve the health of children and adolescents.

This study had several strengths. Survey sampling was conducted with great rigor, the inclusion of risk factors was more comprehensive, and the use of the Lasso model for screening variables ensured that the predictive model was not overfitted. Nevertheless, this study has some limitations. It has a cross-sectional design that precludes causal inferences. Secondly, recall bias may have affected participants’ responses.Thirdly, the binary division of outcome variables carries the risk of information loss, which can result in an underestimation of change or the concealment of the non-linear relationship between determinants and outcomes. Finally, it should be noted that the present study was conducted in a single-center analysis with no external validation, which may impact the generalizability and robustness of the research results.

Conclusion

In this study, we employed a combination of Random Forest and Lasso regression to identify seven predictors of optimal HRQoL. This has resulted in the construction of a predictive nomogram that can assist healthcare professionals, educators, and other organizations in identifying groups with poor HRQoL and in developing effective interventions.
