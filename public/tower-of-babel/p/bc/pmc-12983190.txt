Chemical Constituents of Taxodium mucronatum (Cupressaceae) and Some of Its Endophytic Fungi, and Their In Silico and In Vitro Evaluations as SARS‐CoV‐2 Inhibitors and as Cytotoxic Agents
Authors: Nadia Mendoza, Olivia Pérez‐Valera, Antonio Nieto‐Camacho, María Teresa Ramírez‐Apan, Marcela Guadalupe Téllez‐Martínez, Eleazar M Escamilla‐Silva, Guillermo Delgado
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTConsidering the importance of the relationships between plants and their fungi, the purpose of this research was to characterize the chemical constituents and bioactivity of Taxodium mucronatum (a tree used in Mexican traditional medicine) and its fungal endophytes. Conventional biotechnological procedures allowed the identification of 17 fungal species, and chemical analysis permitted the isolation of plant and fungal metabolites, characterizing 20 compounds. The inhibition of the SARS‐CoV‐2 spike protein:ACE2 complex was evaluated using a Ray Biotech kit, and the cytotoxicity against selected tumor cell lines was evaluated by use of the sulforhodamine B assay. The chemical analysis of T. mucronatum afforded diterpenes (1–6), cycloartanes (7‐‐10, not previously reported for this species), and sterols (11, 12). Complementing that analysis, endophytic fungi were identified, mainly from the genus Penicillium, and compounds 13–20 were isolated from some of the fungal cultures. The PASS calculations predicted antiviral activity for compounds 5, 8, 10, and 12, and cytotoxic activity for almost all the isolated compounds. The in vitro results indicate that T. mucronatum and some of its endophytic fungi constitute new sources of bioactive substances that inhibit the formation of the SARS‐CoV‐2 spike protein:ACE2 complex (such as taxodione, 3) and inhibitors of certain cancer cell lines (5, 13, and 17).Keywords: antiproliferative agents, antiviral, natural products

Introduction

It is widely recognized that natural products from plants are an important source of new drugs [1]; however, little is known about the identities of their endophytic fungi and the structures of their secondary metabolites. This knowledge is necessary as a first step in understanding the relationships between plants and their endophytes. The discovery of taxol from Taxus brevifolia and from its endophytic fungus Taxomyces andreanae provides an example of the importance of exploring both sources [2], since it is recognized that fungal synthesis of secondary metabolites can complement or exceed that of plants.

This work is focused on the chemical study and biological evaluation of natural products obtained from T. mucronatum and its endophytic fungi. T. mucronatum is distributed in the southern United States, throughout Mexico, and in northern Guatemala [3], where it is commonly known as ahuehuete (original name in Náhuatl, meaning the tree that never gets old) [4]. Different parts of this tree (fruits, branches, leaves) have several uses in Mexican traditional medicine, such as healing wounds and ulcers, treating pain, as a laxative, for respiratory infections, and as an emmenagogue and diuretic [3, 4]. Some reported biological activities for T. mucronatum preparations include vasodilator, hypoglycemic, antibacterial, and antifungal properties [5, 6, 7, 8, 9], and diterpenes, lignans, flavonoids, and fatty acids are chemical constituents of some Taxodium species [10]. Complementarily, some endophytic fungi have attracted attention as sources of bioactive natural products with unique structures [11], such as taxol derivatives from Pestalotiopsis microspora, an endophytic fungus isolated precisely from T. mucronatum introduced to India [12]. Endophytes may contribute to their hosts by influencing host evolution and growth, thereby benefiting ecological adaptability via signal transduction [13].

Continuing the search for bioactive compounds from Mexican biota [14, 15, 16], the aim of this work was to contribute to the chemical and biological knowledge of different parts of the T. mucronatum tree (fruits, branches, leaves), the identification of its endophytic fungi, and the isolation and biological evaluation of the chemical constituents produced by some of these microorganisms. We also performed in silico predictions of the isolated compounds’ potential as inhibitors of influenza and rhinovirus, and as apoptosis agonists and antineoplastic agents. The available natural products were evaluated as inhibitors of the formation of the SARS‐CoV‐2 spike protein:ACE2 complex in an in vitro assay and also were evaluated as antiproliferative agents against six human tumor cell lines.

Results and Discussion

Identification of Endophytic Fungi

A total of 26 culturable filamentous fungi were isolated from the bark of T. mucronatum, and 17 different species were identified from fungal cultures by internal transcribed spacer (ITS) rDNA sequence analyses. The ITS sequences of identified fungi were submitted to the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) at the National Institute of Health (NIH). Based on the basic local alignment search tool (BLAST) results, the rDNA ITS sequences were assigned to the phylum Ascomycota. The results indicated that the dominant genus was Penicillium with six species, followed by two Alternaria, two Fusarium, two Cladosporium, two Curvularia, one Gloeophyllum, one Phaeoacremonium, and one Seiridium. These results were registered in the NIH GenBank database, and the sequences were compared with those of microorganisms already registered in the database. Figure 1 shows the phylogenetic tree based on ITS1 sequences, compared with reference fungi of the same genus taken from the GenBank database. There is a clear relationship between each isolated fungus and the genus of reference fungus, consistent with the results obtained from the DNA analysis. Figure 2 shows the phylogenetic tree based on ITS4; all the reference fungi are separate from the isolated fungi. The differences in the arrangement of fungi in the phylogenetic trees (Figure 1 vs. Figure 2) are probably due to the ITS4 region genes more accurately reflecting the relationships among microorganisms living on T. mucronatum. This result suggests that these microorganisms are genetically more similar to each other because they inhabit the same plant rather than because they belong to the same genera. These findings can be related to the co‐evolution of the host plant and its microbiota, since it has been found that plants are able to modify the soil to attract beneficial microorganisms, thus developing a phytobiota characteristic of the host species but governed by abiotic factors [17].

Strain NMD1 was identified as Seiridium ceratosporum, strain NMX3 as Gloeophyllum trabeum, and strains NM24 and NM25 as Phaeoacremonium iranianum, which have all been reported as phytopathogenic fungi [18, 19, 20, 21]. However, the collected plant did not exhibit disease to the naked eye. This observation can be explained by the fact that one or some of the endophytic fungi are producing secondary metabolites that inhibit the growth of these pathogens and thus prevent disease from developing in the plant. It is interesting to note that the fungal profile of T. mucronatum differed from that of T. distichum, where a close relationship was observed between factors such as salinity and soil moisture and the fungal profile [22].

Chemical Constituents of T. mucronatum and Its Endophytic Fungi

The analysis of the n‐hexane:CHCl3 (1:1) extract of fruits of T. mucronatum led to the isolation of the abietane diterpenes royleanone (1), taxoquinone (2), taxodione (3), sugiol (4), and 5,6‐dehydrosugiol (5) (Figure 3). The chemical study of leaves allowed the isolation of β‐sitosterol (11), 8β‐hydroxypimar‐15‐en‐19‐oic acid (6), cyclomargenol (7), 24‐methylenecycloartanol (8), cyclomargenone (9), and 24‐methylenecycloartenone (10). The analysis of branches led to the isolation of sugiol (4), β‐sitosterol (11), and β‐sitosteryl‐β‐D‐glucopyranoside (12) (Figure 3). Taxoquinone (2) was the major constituent of the fruit extract of T. mucronatum. Several bioactivities have been reported for 2, such as antifungal [23], α‐glucosidase inhibition, tyrosinase inhibition [24], cytotoxicity, H1N1 influenza virus inhibition [25], and antioxidant activity [26]. Sugiol (4) was present in fruits and branches and can be considered a characteristic Taxodium metabolite. Cytotoxic [27] and anti‐inflammatory [28] properties have been reported for compound 4. Interestingly, this is the first report of cycloartanes (7–10) as constituents of T. mucronatum, although several bioactive cycloartanes (named taxcycloterpenes) have been recently isolated from the leaves of T. scandens, a native American plant introduced to China in the early 20th century [29]. Cyclomargenol (7, isolated from Krameria racemosa, Krameriaceae) has been reported to reduce the activities of COX1 and COX2 [30].

Complementarily, the metabolites isolated from some culturable endophytic fungi included curvularine (13) from Penicillium crustosum (NM1); dehydrocurvularine (14) from P. roseopurpureum (NMB, NMY4), and P. sumatraense (NM3‐2A); 11α‐hydroxycurvularine (15) and 11β‐hydroxycurvularine (16) from P. sumatraense (NM3‐2A); mycophenolic acid (17) and demethylmycophenollic acid (18) from P. roseopurpureum (NMB); ergosterol (19) from P. commune (NM16A2); and tyrosol (20) from Cladosporium cladosporoides (NMX4) (Figure 4). It is important to mention that the macrolides 13–16 are characterized by their antifungal activities and cytotoxicity [31], whereas the mycophenolic acid derivative 18 has been reported to be an antimicrobial agent [32].

For the PASS prediction, we considered the models of influenza virus and rhinovirus for two reasons: first, the COVID virus is not yet available for modelling on the platform used, and second, influenza virus and rhinovirus are the pathogenic viruses that most frequently infect humans. Although their infection mechanisms are different, both viruses can be considered as a starting point for the theoretical exploration of bioactivity. Interestingly, some abietane diterpenes, such as sugiol (4), have been reported as inhibitors of other SARS‐CoV viruses [33]. These data are interesting, since 4 exhibited a predicted moderate Pa for influenza virus (Table 3).

It is worth mentioning that some substances of fungal origin exhibited activity against A1H1 and SARS‐CoV‐2 viruses, such as terrein and racemosol, both isolated from Aspergillus terreus [34]. However, in our analysis only 15 and 16 exhibited moderate Pa against the influenza virus.

Prediction of Activity Spectra for Substances (PASS Online) and Evaluation of Cytotoxicity

The probabilities of activity for compounds 1–20 as apoptotic agonists and antineoplastic agents were predicted (Table 3). The secondary metabolites that exhibited a Pa > 0.7 for the induction of apoptosis were compounds 2–9 and 17–19, while for antineoplastic activity, compounds 1‐‐3, 7–9, and 13–19 stood out. The compounds 2, 4, 5–7, 9, 13, and 17 were tested against human tumor cell lines of the central nervous system (U251), prostate (PC‐3), leukemia (K562), colon (HCT‐15), breast (MFC‐7), and lung (SK‐LU‐1) (Table 5). Compounds 2, 5, 13, and 17 exhibited > 50% growth inhibition of the six tumor cell lines. Taxoquinone (2) showed the highest inhibitory activity against U251 (87.91%) and 0.08% inhibition against the healthy cell line COS‐7 at 10 µM. Interestingly, several abietane diterpenes have been tested against different carcinoma cell lines, such as colorectal HT‐29 [27].

The PASS predictions of apoptosis‐inducing activity agreed with the experimental results, since taxoquinone (2) exhibited > 50% inhibition of all tumor cell lines. In turn, both the prediction of antineoplastic activity and the experimental results supported curvularin's (13) significant cytotoxicity. For mycophenolic acid (17), the PASS prediction results (Table 3) agreed with the experimental evaluation, since this substance exhibited an IC50 (0.15 µM) for SK‐LU‐1 cells that was lower than that of etoposide.

Conclusions

The distribution of metabolites was specific for different parts of the tree; the abietane diterpenes 1–5 were the major constituents found in the fruits of T. mucronatum, although sugiol (4) was also isolated from branches. Cycloartanes 7–10 were isolated exclusively in the leaves, and this is the first report of cycloartanes from this species. In this work, 17 fungal species were identified, represented by six Penicillium species, two Alternaria, two Fusarium, two Cladosporium, two Curvularia, one Gloeophyllum, one Phaeoacremonium, and one Seiridium. From five of these microorganisms, eight secondary metabolites were isolated, among which macrolides were the most representative. The results of the PASS prediction and the viral evaluation with the kit of the compounds’ activity against the SARS‐CoV‐2 spike:ACE2 complex indicated that some of these substances could exhibit antiviral properties; taxodione (3) displayed the highest in vitro inhibition in this assay. We also found that T. mucronatum and its endophytic fungi are sources of bioactive substances with cytotoxic activity against selected carcinoma cell lines (2, 5, 13, and 17). This is the first report on the chemical constituents of T. mucronatum, including the identification and the chemical constituents of some of its endophytes, to gain a better understanding of the production and biological properties of their secondary metabolites and their interactions.

Experimental Section

General

The natural products were separated by column chromatography (CC) and positive pressure chromatography (PPC). Merck silica gel (SiO2) mesh sizes 70–230 and 230–400 were the stationary phases. The separation processes were followed by thin‐layer chromatography (TLC) using silica gel plates of 60F254 supported on Merck aluminum. TLC plates were developed using a Spectroline ENF‐240 UV lamp (λ = 254 and 365 nm) and a 1% ammoniacal ceric sulfate ((NH4)4Ce(SO4)4) solution in 2 N sulfuric acid (H2SO4). Mixtures of solvents such as n‐hexane, ethyl acetate (EtOAc), methanol (CH3OH), dichloromethane (CH2Cl2), acetonitrile (CH3CN), and MiliQ water were utilized for the chromatographic procedures. The Thermo Scientific Ultimate 3000 chromatographer, coupled with a diode array detector (Dionex 3000), was used for some separations. The HPLC procedures were performed using a Luna column C18 Phenomenex 250 mm × 10 mm, particle size 5 µm, and 100 A. The PPC was developed using a Büchi Sepacore system consisting of a C‐660 fraction collector, a C‐620 controller, a C‐605 pump module, and a C‐640 UV photometer using two wavelengths, 254 and 200 nm. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were acquired using Bruker Avance III (700 MHz), Bruker Ascend 500 MHz, Bruker Avance III (400 MHz), and Bruker Fourier (300 MHz) instruments. The deuterated solvents employed in the NMR experiments were chloroform (CDCl3), acetone (CD3COCD3) methanol (CD3OD), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO‐d6), and pyridine (C5D5N).

Plant Material

The plant material was collected in September 2018, in Chamácuaro, Salvatierra, along the banks of the Lerma River in the state of Guanajuato, Mexico (20°06'17.6'' N, 100°49'56.4'' W; elevation of 1947 m above sea level). A specimen was deposited at Herbario Nacional de México under the identification number MEXU 1512397 and was identified as T. mucronatum Ten. by Dr. David S. Gernandt (Instituto de Biología, UNAM). The plant material was allowed to dry in the shade at room temperature and was subsequently milled.

Isolation and Identification of Endophytic Fungi

Samples of bark (living tissue) were cut into 1 cm2 sections, disinfected with 1.5% sodium hypochlorite and washed three times with distilled water, dried with sterile paper, and finally seeded in Petri dishes with Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) media at 28°C in an incubator for 3 days. The developed colonies were isolated and purified by monosporic culture or hyphal tip transfer and preserved in a 20% glycerol solution at −5°C for 3 days in a 20% glycerol solution at ‐10°C in test tubes [37].

DNA Extraction

Each fungus was inoculated on PDA medium in a laboratory dish and incubated at 29°C in the dark for 3 days following molecular identification. The growth medium was then centrifuged in a 1–5 mL Eppendorf tube at 8000 rpm for 3 min; the supernatant was removed and resuspended between each centrifugation. The cells were resuspended in 1 mL sterile water or 1X PBS, followed by another centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 3 min. The supernatant was removed to obtain a clean cell pellet. The cells were resuspended in 1 mL sterile water, and 10 µL of lysozyme was added, followed by a 15 min incubation at room temperature. After another centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 3 min, the supernatant was removed, and the cells were resuspended in 1 mL of 1X PBS. Then, 6 µL of proteinase K (Kit Invitrogen) was added, and the mixture was incubated for 1 h at 37°C. After centrifugation at 8000 rpm, the supernatant was removed. The cells were resuspended in 100 µL of the breaking buffer. Three glass beads were added, and the mixture was vortexed at maximum speed for 5 min with the addition of 100 µL of phenol‐chloroform‐isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1 ratio). Subsequently, 20 µL of RNase A (Invitrogen Kit) was added, mixed well by brief gentle vortexing, and incubated at room temperature for 2 min. Binding and purification of genomic DNA was performed using Invitrogen Kit columns. The extraction was performed according to the protocol described in the literature [38].

The classification of endophytic fungi was carried out by molecular techniques. Genomic DNA was extracted from each axenic culture for the amplification of ITS1 (5´ TCCGTAGGT‐GAACCTGCGG 3´) and ITS4 (5´ TCCTCCGCTTATT‐GATATGC 3´). The amplification reactions were carried out in a T1 thermocycler (Biometra GMbH, Göttingen, Germany); an initial denaturation cycle of 94°C was programmed for 3 min, followed by 30 denaturation cycles of 94°C for 1 min, alignment to 50°C for 1 min, and extension of 72°C for 1.5 min and a final extension at 72°C for 10 min [39].

Internal Transcribed Spacer Sequence Analysis

The sequences were obtained from Macrogen Inc. (Seoul, South Korea). The consensus sequence was obtained with the Bioedit program, which was compared with the original sequences with the CLUSTAL X 2.0 program; subsequently, the Nucleotide‐nucleotide BLAST algorithm (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool) of the National Center of Biotechnology Information (NCBI) was used to compare the consensus sequences with the database to identify each axenic culture. Evolutionary history was deduced using maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference methods. To select a nucleotide substitution model, the Bayesian information criterion was implemented and an ML analysis [40]. The initial trees for the heuristic search were obtained by applying the neighbor union method to a matrix of pairwise distances, estimated using the compound maximum likelihood approach. The trees were bootstrapped with 1000 replicates of each sequence to assess the reliability of the tree topologies. Bayesian phylogenetic reconstructions were composed with the MrBayes 3.2 program [41].

Phylogenetic Analysis

The sequences obtained were aligned with the MAFFT program [42]. The MEGA 7 program was employed to generate a dendrogram through the maximum likelihood method based on the Le and Gascuel model, with 500 bootstraps [43]. Phylogenetic dendrogram data were scaled with branch length in proportion to evolutionary distance (Figures 1 and 2).

Extraction and Isolation of the Chemical Constituents From T. mucronatum and From Some of Its Endophytic Fungi

The extracts were obtained through maceration with CH2Cl2:CH3OH (1:1) for leaves (2.5 kg) and branches (3.5 kg), and n‐hexane‐CHCl3 (1:1) for fruits (1 kg). This process was repeated three times for each part of the plant. Following these operations, leaf extract (200 g), branch extract (170 g), and fruit extract (25 g) were obtained.

The fruit extract was subjected to CC using n‐hexane:EtOAc mixtures increasing the polarity (100:0 to 0:100) and concluding with CH3OH. Ninety fractions were collected and subsequently combined into 10 fractions (A–J) based on their TLC profiles. Fraction B (1.7 g) was analyzed by CC utilizing a mixture of n‐hexane:acetone as the mobile phase with increasing polarity (100:0 to 0:100). This process yielded 12 fractions (B1–B12). Subsequently, fraction B2 was chromatographed (CC) using mixtures of n‐hexane:acetone in increasing order of polarity. Fifty‐eight fractions were collected and combined into eight subfractions (B2.1–B2.8) based on their TLC profile. From subfraction B2.2 were isolated orange needles identified as royleanone (1, 3.0 mg) by its physical and spectroscopic data and by comparison with data reported [44]. Fraction C (6.0 g) was collected from the primary CC and concentrated under vacuum conditions at 45°C, resulting in the formation of orange needles, which were recrystallized from acetone:iPr2O. This substance was identified as taxoquinone (2, 223.8 mg) by comparison of its spectroscopic data with those reported [45]. Fraction E (3.4 g) was concentrated under vacuum conditions at 45°C, yielding colorless powder, which was collected and recrystallized from acetone:iPr2O, and then from CH3OH. This procedure afforded colorless needles, which were characterized as sugiol (4, 20 mg) [45, 46]. Subsequently, the mother liquors were subjected to preparative TLC, eluting with a mixture of hexane:EtOAc (95:05) and developing the plate four times. This procedure led to the isolation of a red oil. The analysis of the spectroscopic and spectrometric data of this compound led to its identification as taxodione (3, 10 mg) in agreement with the data reported in the literature [44]. Fraction F (20.0 mg) was purified by CC using mixtures of n‐hexane:EtOAc (100:0 to 0:100), resulting in the isolation of a colorless powder. This was recrystallized from CH3OH:iPr2O, affording colorless needles, which were characterized as 5,6‐dehydrosugiol (5, 6.6 mg) by comparison of its physical and spectroscopic properties with those reported [47].

The leaf extract (200 g) was separated by CC, using a gradient of n‐hexane:EtOAc (100:0 to 0:100) as the elution system, and the column was washed with CH3OH. A total of 285 fractions were collected and joined according to their TLC profiles into 10 fractions (A‐J). Fraction B (0.120 g) underwent recrystallization from CH3OH:iPr2O to yield a solid that was identified as a mixture of two compounds, which was separated by semi‐preparative HPLC. The elution was developed by a constant composition of CH3CN and water acidified to 0.1% acetic acid (90:10). The temperature of the column oven was set at 30°C. The flow rate used was 1 mL/min. The detection was carried out at 200 nm. This chromatographic process yielded cyclomargenone (9, 5 mg) [30] and 24‐methylenecycloartenone (10, 6 mg) [48]. Identification of these substances was achieved by comparing their spectroscopic data with those reported. Fraction D (266 mg) was processed by CC, and some fractions afforded colorless needles. This material was crystallized from CH3OH:iPr2O and was separated by semi‐preparative HPLC. The mobile phase was composed of CH3CN and acidified water (0.1% acetic acid) (90:10). The temperature of the column oven was set at 30°C, and the flow rate used was 1 mL/min. This chromatographic process afforded cyclomargenol (7, 6.6 mg) [30] and 24‐methylenecycloartenol (8, 5.4 mg) [49]. The identification of these substances was made by comparing their spectroscopic data with those reported. Fraction G (7.0 g) was separated by CC. Mixtures of n‐hexane and acetone were used as the mobile phase (100:0 to 0:100), and a total of 79 fractions of 100 mL were collected and subsequently combined into 16 fractions (H1–H16). The subfraction H8 was separated by preparative TLC; the elution was performed using n‐hexane‐acetone (8:2) developing five times. This procedure led to the isolation of white needles of 8β‐hydroxypimar‐15‐en‐19‐oic acid (6, 5.1 mg), identified by comparison of its physical and spectroscopic data with those reported in the literature [50]. Fraction F was concentrated under vacuum conditions at 45°C, resulting in the formation of white needles, which were recrystallized from acetone. This process afforded β‐sitosterol (11, 20 mg) [51], identified by direct comparison with authentic samples available in our laboratory.

The extract of the branches (170 g) was separated by CC, using n‐hexane:EtOAc (100:0 to 0:100) as the mobile phase; 120 fractions were collected and grouped into 16 major fractions (A–O). From fraction B (5 g) were isolated colorless needles, which were recrystallized from CH3OH:iPr2O, affording sugiol (4). From fraction O (125 g) a white powder was obtained, which was filtered and washed with cold EtOAc. Subsequently, the powder was dissolved with methanol, filtered, and the solution was treated with iPr2O for recrystallization. After repeating this procedure several times, the mixture was filtered, and the recovered white powder was identified as β‐sitosteryl‐β‐D‐glucopyranoside (12) by its physical and spectroscopic data [52].

The isolation of compounds from the endophytic fungi was conducted by PCC. In the isolation method, mixtures of n‐hexane:EtOAc (100:0 to 0:100) were used as elution systems, and the columns were washed with methanol. The extract of strain NM1 (Penicillium crustosum, 120 mg) (see Tables 1 and 2 for identification codes) was fractionated into 11 fractions (A–K). Fraction I (15.6 mg) was concentrated under vacuum at 45°C, resulting in the formation of a solid that was recrystallized from methanol, yielding curvularin as white needles (13, 10 mg) [53], identified by the standard procedures mentioned above. The extract of strain NMB (Penicillium roseopurpureum, 260 mg) was concentrated under vacuum at 45°C, resulting in the formation of a white solid. This solid was collected and recrystallized from MeOH:iPr2O, yielding 10,11‐dehydrocurvularin (14, 12 mg) [54], identified by comparison of its spectroscopic data with those reported. The remaining part of the extract was concentrated and fractionated into 7 fractions (A–G). Fraction C was concentrated under vacuum at 45°C, resulting in the formation of a solid which was recrystallized from methanol. This process yielded the mixture of 11α‐hydroxycurvularin (15) and 11β‐hydroxycurvularin (16) (7 mg) as white needles. These substances were identified by comparing their spectroscopic data with those reported [54]. The extract of strain NM3.1Ba (Penicillium chrysogenum, 120 mg) was fractionated into 12 fractions (A‐H). Fraction F was concentrated under vacuum at 45°C, resulting in the formation of a solid, which was recrystallized from acetone, and identified as mycophenolic acid (17, 3 mg) by its spectroscopic properties [55]. Fraction I was also concentrated under vacuum at 45°C, resulting in the formation of a solid, which was recrystallized from acetone. This process yielded demethylmycophenolic acid (18, 2.1 mg), identified [56] by comparison of its spectroscopic data. The extract of strain NM16A2 (Penicillium commune, 114.5 mg) was fractionated into 13 fractions (A–H). Fraction E was concentrated under vacuum at 45°C, resulting in the precipitation of a white powder, which was identified as ergosterol (19, 2 mg), characterized by the standard spectroscopic comparisons [57]. The EtOAc extract of strain NMX4 (Cladosporium cladosporioides, 75 mg) was fractionated into 10 fractions, and fraction D was concentrated under vacuum at 45°C, resulting in the precipitation of small white needles, identified as tyrosol (20, 2 mg) [58] by the standard comparisons of the physical and spectroscopic data.

Prediction of Activity Spectra for Substances (PASS Online)

The PASS Online prediction was calculated for the isolated compounds 1–20. The selected substances were transformed into their Simplified Molecular‐input Line‐Entry System (SMILES) code and introduced in the free platform www.way2drug.com. Subsequently, the probability of activity (Pa) against influenza virus, rhinovirus, as apoptosis agonists and as antineoplastic agents was calculated.

Inhibition of the Formation of the Spike Protein of the SARS‐Cov‐2:ACE2 Complex

The compounds 2–7 and 9, 12, and 13 were tested in an in vitro assay as inhibitors of the complex formed between the spike protein of SARS‐CoV‐2 and human Angiotensin Converting Enzyme 2 (ACE2) on a RayBio COVID‐19 Spike‐ACE2 Binding Assay Kit I. The kit instructions provided by the manufacturer were followed for this assay, which includes zafirlukast as a control, which has previously been reported to have anti‐SARS‐CoV‐2 activity.

Evaluation of Cytotoxic Activity

The cytotoxic activity of compounds 2, 4, 5–7, 9, 13, and 17 was tested in a set of six human tumor cell lines using the method of sulforhodamine B. The cell lines used in this study were provided by the National Cancer Institute (NCI, USA), except for SK‐LU‐1 and COS‐7 which were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). The following cell lines were used: MCF‐7 breast adenocarcinoma (RRID: CVCL_0031), HCT‐15 colorectal adenocarcinoma (RRID: CVCL_0290), PC‐3 prostate adenocarcinoma (RRID: CVCL_0035), U251 glioblastoma multiforme (RRID: CVCL_0021), K562 chronic myelogenous leukemia (RRID: CVCL_0004), and SK‐LU‐1 lung adenocarcinoma (RRID: CVCL_0629), and African green monkey kidney fibroblast‐like (non‐tumor) COS‐7 (RRID:CVCL_0224). Cells were cultured in RPMI‐1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The compounds were initially subjected to screening at concentrations of 10 and 25 µM in triplicate wells per cell line. Results were expressed as a percentage of growth inhibition relative to untreated control cells after 48 h of exposure. The positive control used in this assay was etoposide. Colored solutions were extracted, and optical densities were read on an Ultra Reader of Microplate (Elx 808, Bio‐Tek Instruments, Inc.) at a wavelength of 515 nm [59].

Statistical Analysis

Inhibition of the SARS‐CoV‐2 spike–ACE2 complex (Table 4) is expressed as a percentage of inhibition. Data is reported as mean ± standard error of the means (S.E.M.). All groups were analyzed by one‐way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's multiple comparison post hoc test using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The differences were statistically significant at p < 0.05 (*) or p < 0.01 (**) when compared to the control group (n = 2). The IC50 values for the cytotoxic assay (Table 6) were obtained from three independent concentration–response curves by linear regression analysis and expressed as mean ± SEM (n = 3).(Supporting Information)
