Two Novel ACTC1 Variants Cause Arthrogryposis Multiplex Congenita
Authors: Lauren Kerr, Pierre Moffatt, Lauren Hyer, Sarah Nossov, Noémi Dahan‐Oliel, Frank Rauch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTArthrogryposis multiplex congenita (AMC) is a group of conditions with congenital joint contractures in at least two body regions. ACTC1 variants cause heart disorders but have also been reported in six families with AMC. We report on two individuals with AMC who were heterozygous for ACTC1 missense variants (NM_005159.5; c.325G>A, p.Glu109Lys and c.650A>C, p.Lys217Thr). Individual 1 had scoliosis and no reported cardiac phenotype. Individual 2 had Shone's complex with coarctation of the aorta, which has not been reported in ACTC1‐related AMC before. In vitro testing demonstrated that p.Glu109Lys caused impaired actin filament assembly and reduced ACTC1 protein levels, effects not apparent for p.Lys217Thr. Our findings characterize two novel variants that cause ACTC1‐related AMC and expand the list of phenotypes observed in this disorder.Keywords: arthrogryposis multiplex congenita, cardiac alpha‐actin, immunofluorescence, western blot

Introduction

ACTC1, the gene for cardiac alpha‐actin, has established gene‐phenotype relationships for the autosomal dominant disorders atrial septal defect type 5 (OMIM 612794), dilated cardiomyopathy type 1R (OMIM 613424), hypertrophic cardiomyopathy type 11 (OMIM 612098), and left ventricular noncompaction type 4 (OMIM 613424). A Noonan‐like phenotype can also occur [1]. Additionally, heterozygous missense variants in ACTC1 have been reported in five families with arthrogryposis multiplex congenita (AMC) [2], a group of disorders with congenital joint contractures in at least two body regions [3]. Heart abnormalities were found in all individuals with echocardiography data. A case series on fetuses with AMC included one additional individual with a heterozygous ACTC1 variant [4].

The amino acid sequence of ACTC1 is 98.9% homologous to that of ACTA1 (skeletal muscle alpha‐1 actin) [5]. ACTA1 variants cause congenital myopathy types 2A, 2B, and 2C (OMIM 161800, 620 265, 620 278). Like ACTC1, ACTA1 can also be implicated in congenital contractures. In a review on the molecular diagnosis of AMC, we found that ACTA1 variants were detected in 10/991 families (1%) reported in cohort studies, making them a relatively common etiology (manuscript under review). To our knowledge, no data on the in vitro effects of AMC‐causing ACTC1 variants have been published. In this report, we describe two families with heterozygous ACTC1 missense variants causing AMC and present in vitro functional studies.

Patients and Methods

Subjects, Ethical Considerations, and Clinical Assessments

Both individuals were participants of the Shriners Hospitals for Children AMC RE2GISTRY [6]. IRB approval and informed consent were obtained as described in the Ethical Statement. Clinical information was collected and outcome measure surveys were administered as described [6].

DNA Sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from saliva and used for paired‐end genome sequencing (2 × 150 bp) with the NovaSeq 6000 Sequencing System (Illumina) at the Shriners Genomic Institute (Tampa, FL).

Protein Modeling

Protein modeling was done with ChimeraX software, version 1.11.1 [7]. Structure 93BR [8], chain A from Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/) [9] was used.

In Vitro Studies

A pcDNA3.1‐based expression plasmid for human ACTC1 (NM_005159.5) (OHu19196, GenScript, Piscataway, USA) was modified by PCR, deleting the C‐terminal FLAG tag and inserting it at the N‐terminus. ACTC1 variants were introduced by site‐directed mutagenesis using PCR. Plasmid sequences were verified by Sanger sequencing. MC3T3‐E1 preosteoblastic cells were chosen for immunofluorescence due to their elaborate actin filament network. Cells were transfected with plasmids carrying wild‐type ACTC1, p.Glu109Lys, or p.Lys217Thr and processed for immunofluorescence. Protein extracts were prepared from transfected cells and Western blotting was performed as described [10].

Results

Individual 1

The girl was born by C‐section after 34 weeks of gestation (birth weight 1.64 kg, z‐score −1.5) as the first child of unrelated parents. Bilateral clubfeet, cleft palate, neck webbing, and spine deformity were noted. Her mother and one maternal aunt had been born with bilateral clubfeet and later developed scoliosis. The mother also had a history of cleft palate, but no history of cardiac issues.

Individual 1 began to walk and speak at age 2 years. She underwent cleft palate surgery at 2 years old and clubfoot surgery at 3 years. When examined at age 5 years, severe short stature (height: 90.5 cm, z‐score −5.4; weight: 12.8 kg, z‐score −4.2) and severe scoliosis (120 degrees) were noted (Figure 1A). At age 6 years, she underwent halo traction and scoliosis surgery with insertion of bilateral growing rods. At 8 years old, she had mild camptodactyly of both hands and mild contractures of the neck, left hip, and both knees and feet. She was highly independent for self‐care tasks. She had no significant mobility or cognitive difficulties. She reported back pain. At age 11 years, she was prescribed albuterol for wheezing. Menarche occurred at age 12 years. Cardiac problems were not noted.

Individual 2

The boy was born at 39 weeks of gestation (birth weight 3.94 kg, z‐score +1.4) as the first child of unrelated parents without family history of AMC. Limited range of motion in the wrists, fingers, thumbs, hips, knees, and bilateral clubfoot was noted. Echocardiography revealed Shone's complex, including coarctation of the aorta, which was repaired surgically.

During his first year of life, Individual 2 underwent bilateral plantar fascia release, bilateral posterior releases, and Achilles tendon lengthening with Ponseti casting. Examination at age 1 year showed clenched hands with deep single palmar creases, laterally deviated feet with overlapping toes and hypoplastic fifth toenails. Distal arthrogryposis was suspected. He had truncal hypotonia, mild lower extremity weakness and delayed achievement of gross motor milestones. At 2 years, he could stand with assistance. At age 3 years, height was 95.4 cm (z‐score −1.4) and weight was 14.2 kg (z‐score −1.1). Moderate contractures of wrists, thumb‐in‐palm deformities, and mild hip contractures were noted bilaterally. He crawled for mobility and required assistance with some self‐care tasks. He had no cognitive difficulties. At age 4 years, he began to walk independently. At age 5 years, he had further heart surgery and pacemaker insertion. At age 6 years, he underwent bilateral open tendon Achilles lengthening, anterior tibialis transfer, and right tibial rotational osteotomy. Spine x‐rays did not reveal scoliosis.

Genome Sequencing

Genome sequencing identified a heterozygous ACTC1 variant (NM_005159.5; c.325G>A, p.Glu109Lys) in Individual 1 and her mother. The variant was absent from gnomAD v2.1.1, had an AlphaMissense score of 0.99 and a REVEL score of 0.827. It was classified as likely pathogenic with ACMG criteria (PM1, PM2, PP1, PP2, PP3). A de novo heterozygous ACTC1 variant (NM_005159.5; c.650A>C, p.Lys217Thr) was found in Individual 2. It was absent from gnomAD and had an AlphaMissense score of 0.91 and a REVEL score of 0.918. It was also classified as likely pathogenic (PS2, PM1, PM2, PP2, PP3).

Both affected amino acids are conserved across ACTC1 orthologs and human ACTC1 paralogs (Figure 1B). For p.Glu109Lys, a likely pathogenic substitution has been reported in ClinVar at the homologous site in ACTA1 (NM_001100.4; c.327G>C, p.Glu109Asp, Variation ID 2582813). Both ACTC1 variants alter hydrogen bonding (Figure 1C).

Immunofluorescence and Western Blotting

MC3T3‐E1 cells transfected with wild‐type ACTC1 displayed staining across the cytoplasm (Figure 2A). The overlap with phalloidin staining demonstrated that these bands corresponded to the actin filaments.

(B) Western blot showing levels of the ACTC1 protein in cells with wild‐type ACTC1 and the two variants (top). Tubulin was used as a positive control (bottom). Cells with ACTC1‐p.Glu109Lys had diffuse anti‐FLAG staining that overlapped poorly with phalloidin staining, suggesting impaired filament formation (Figure 2A). Western blotting demonstrated a reduced level of ACTC1‐p.Glu109Lys protein compared to wild‐type (Figure 2B), which may also affect filament formation.

For cells with ACTC1‐p.Lys217Thr, staining and protein levels appeared similar to those of cells with wild‐type ACTC1 (Figure 2A,B). Thus, p.Lys217Thr did not have clear effects on filament formation or protein availability.

Discussion

We report two novel ACTC1 variants observed in unrelated individuals with AMC. p.Glu109Lys impacted ACTC1 protein levels and filament assembly in vitro, whereas p.Lys217Thr did not. Our findings contribute to the list of ACTC1 missense variants linked to AMC (Figure 2C) and to the range of phenotypes observed (Table 1).

The individuals we describe had phenotypes resembling multiple pterygium syndrome and distal arthrogryposis, consistent with previous findings. Although heart defects and cardiomyopathy have been reported [2], Shone's complex, observed in Individual 2, has not been found in other individuals with ACTC1‐related AMC. In contrast, neither Individual 1 nor her mother had a history of cardiac problems. Heterogeneous cardiac phenotypes have been reported in 7/11 individuals thus far and may be absent or undetected in the remainder. Other characteristics, such as short stature and scoliosis, also appear inconsistently.

ACTC1‐p.Glu109Lys caused decreased ACTC1 protein levels and an apparent lack of organized actin filaments, while ACTC1‐p.Lys217Thr had expression and localization comparable to wild‐type ACTC1. Our observations on p.Glu109Lys support in silico findings that other ACTC1 variants may affect thin filament assembly [2]. p.Glu109Lys could impede actin filament formation through structural instability and/or decreased protein availability for filament assembly, effects that support its pathogenicity.

p.Lys217Thr did not have a clear impact in our experimental system, but is likely to affect the function of ACTC1, considering that most residues in the actin proteins are completely conserved [5]. Indeed, known pathogenic missense variants in ACTC1 have been shown to assemble into filamentous actin while impacting other functions [11, 12]. Although the mechanism whereby p.Lys217Thr exerts its effect is not clear, the variant was de novo in the affected child and met ACMG criteria for being classified as likely pathogenic.

In conclusion, this report discusses two unrelated individuals with ACTC1‐related AMC, one of whom had Shone's complex and one of whom had no known heart defects. In vitro studies demonstrate that the ACTC1 p.Glu109Lys variant affects actin filament assembly, while the p.Lys217Thr variant does not appear to do so.
