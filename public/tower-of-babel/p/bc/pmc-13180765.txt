A novel Azospirillum vistecanum strain from methane digestate promotes plant growth via indole-3-acetic acid biosynthesis
Authors: Surat Moomthong, Thamonwan Woraruthai, Charndanai Tirapanampai, Sasithon Rungjroenchaiwat, Worarat Kruasuwan, Pichahpuk Uthaipaisanwong, Kanthida Kusonmano, Piroon Jenjaroenpun, Thidathip Wongsurawat, Thanyaporn Wongnate
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractThe urgent need for sustainable agricultural inputs has accelerated the search for microbial alternatives to synthetic agrochemicals. In this study, we report the isolation and comprehensive characterization of a novel strain, Azospirillum vistecanum VT-I1, obtained from a methane-enriched digestate system. This strain demonstrated exceptional plant growth-promoting potential through the biosynthesis of indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), a key phytohormone. Genome sequencing and annotation revealed the presence of genes associated with the indole-3-pyruvate (IPyA) pathway, aro9, ipdC, and aldA, with no detectable IAA-degrading gene clusters, supporting a high net auxin yield. Under optimized culture conditions, VT-I1 produced up to 1.206 mM of IAA, significantly surpassing the levels observed in A. brasilense. Functional assays confirmed the bioactivity of this microbial IAA, which enhanced root development in Exacum affine and improved seed germination in Andrographis paniculata. Collectively, these results establish A. vistecanum VT-I1 as a promising candidate for next-generation biofertilizers, offering a scalable, eco-friendly alternative to chemically synthesized auxins. This work expands our understanding of auxin biosynthesis in rhizobacteria and provides a strong foundation for future field applications and microbiome-based crop enhancement strategies.Key points• A novel Azospirillum vistecanum was isolated from methane digestate.• The strain produces high levels of indole-3 acetic acid via the IPyA pathway.• Microbial IAA enhances root growth and seed germination in model plants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-026-13808-y.Keywords: Indole-3-pyruvate pathway, Tryptophan-dependent auxin biosynthesis, Rhizosphere-associated bacteria, Genome-enabled strain characterization, Microbial biofertilizer

Introduction

Feeding a global population projected to reach 9.73 billion by 2050 presents a formidable challenge, especially as agricultural systems face intensifying constraints from land degradation and environmental stressors (Godoy et al. 2014). Approximately 45–47% of the Earth’s land surface is already affected by soil degradation processes such as desertification, salinization, and nutrient depletion, all of which severely undermine crop productivity and threaten food security (AbdelRahman 2023; Hassani et al. 2021). To maintain yields under such pressure, farmers have increasingly relied on synthetic fertilizers. However, the overuse of these agrochemicals has led to serious environmental consequences, including soil acidification, groundwater contamination, eutrophication, and greenhouse gas emissions (Chandini et al. 2019; Kumar Saranraj 2014). The agriculture sector alone contributes approximately 12% of total global greenhouse gas emissions, further exacerbating climate change (Chataut et al. 2023; Ogunniyi et al. 2024).

In light of these challenges, there is a growing demand for sustainable alternatives to conventional fertilizers. Among these, microbial inoculants or biofertilizers have emerged as promising tools to enhance soil health and plant productivity while reducing environmental footprints (Chakraborty & Akhtar 2021; Yadav & Sarkar 2019). In particular, plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) are widely recognized for their multifaceted benefits, including nitrogen fixation, phosphorus solubilization, and the synthesis of growth-stimulating phytohormones such as indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), cytokinins, and gibberellins (Molina et al. 2018; Nazir, Kamili, & Shah, 2018; Pham et al. 2022). PGPR-based biofertilizers have been shown to reduce dependence on synthetic inputs and improve crop resilience under adverse conditions (Sharma et al. 2025).

Among the PGPR, species in the genus Azospirillum are especially notable for their agricultural relevance and commercial success in countries such as Argentina, Brazil, India, and Italy (Bashan and de-Bashan 2010; Datta and Basu 1997; Santos et al. 2021). Azospirillum brasilense and related strains have been extensively applied in field trials and commercial formulations due to their ability to enhance root architecture, increase nutrient uptake, and improve stress tolerance in crops (Fukami et al. 2017). Recent developments in countries like Brazil and Argentina have introduced newer strains such as A. baldaniorum and A. argentinense, underscoring the ongoing diversification of Azospirillum-based biofertilizers (Fendrihan et al. 2017; Mora et al. 2023).

A key mechanism underlying Azospirillum’s efficacy is its robust production of IAA, a vital phytohormone that regulates cell division, elongation, and differentiation. IAA also promotes root exudation and rhizosphere colonization, thereby improving nutrient absorption (Duca & Glick 2020; Hungria et al. 2010). Despite IAA’s pivotal role in plant development, few studies have systematically isolated and characterized Azospirillum strains with exceptionally high IAA-producing capacity, particularly those adapted to nonconventional or stress-prone environments. Moreover, the genetic determinants responsible for high IAA production in these strains remain poorly defined, largely due to the limited whole-genome investigations. Elucidating biosynthetic gene clusters and regulatory elements through genome sequencing and annotation is crucial for understanding strain-specific IAA pathways. Such knowledge would not only enable more precise selection of biofertilizer candidates but also create opportunities for metabolic engineering to enhance phytohormone output and stress tolerance.

This study addresses that gap by isolating novel Azospirillum strains from a food waste digestion unit, an underexplored microbial niche with potential for harboring strains adapted to high organic load and variable conditions. The research focuses on characterizing strains with high IAA productivity and identifying optimal conditions for their growth and phytohormone synthesis. Particular attention is given to Azospirillum vistecanum VT-1, whose IAA biosynthetic pathways will be elucidated through whole-genome sequencing and pathway annotation. These insights aim to reveal genetic determinants that can be further optimized or engineered to enhance phytohormone output. By advancing a precision-based approach to PGPR selection and characterization, this study contributes to the development of next-generation biofertilizers tailored for use in saline, degraded, or nutrient-poor soils. The application of indigenous high-performing Azospirillum strains offers a sustainable strategy to reduce synthetic fertilizer inputs, promote plant health, and support climate-resilient agriculture. Furthermore, the integration of genomic, physiological, and environmental data provides a foundation for scalable biofertilizer deployment aligned with global goals in sustainable food production and ecological stewardship.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

This study employed two Azospirillum strains: a novel isolate, A. vistecanum VT-I1, obtained from a mesophilic food waste digestion system, and the reference strain A. brasilense ATCC 2914, which is widely used as a model plant-growth-promoting bacterium and IAA producer. Four culture media were used to evaluate growth characteristics and optimize IAA production: modified nitrate mineral salt (NMS), Luria–Bertani (LB), yeast extract mannitol (YEM), and nitrogen-free bromothymol blue (NFb) media. As these are standard formulations, their full compositions are provided in the Supporting Information (Table S1), along with relevant references.

Briefly, modified NMS medium was used for initial isolation and routine cultivation, including copper-supplemented experiments as previously described (Miyaji 2011). LB medium was used as a nutrient-rich reference medium (Hernaández-Esquivel et al. 2020); YEM medium was employed for cultivation of nitrogen-fixing rhizobacteria (Revision 2022; Sasirekha et al. 2012), and NFb medium was used to assess nitrogen fixation-related growth (Rodriguez Caceres 1982). The initial pH of all media was adjusted to 6.5–7.0 using HCl or NaOH prior to sterilization. Media were autoclaved at 121 °C for 15 min before use.

Bacterial isolation

This study aimed to isolate Azospirillum species adapted to methane-enriched environments. Recent reports have indicated that certain diazotrophic species, including A. brasilense, may utilize methane as a supplemental carbon source, especially under low organic carbon conditions (Choix et al. 2024; Doroshenko et al. 2007; Tikhonova et al. 2019). To exploit this adaptive trait, methane was used as the sole carbon source to enrich for Azospirillum strains from anaerobic digestate.

Sediment samples were collected at a 5 cm depth from the sludge zone of a food waste digestion system operating under mesophilic conditions. Samples were immediately transferred into sterile 50-mL polypropylene tubes and transported to the laboratory on ice. For enrichment, 1.0 g of wet sediment was added to 50 mL of sterile Nitrate Mineral Salt (NMS) medium in 100-mL glass serum bottles. Each bottle was sealed with sterile butyl rubber stoppers and aluminum crimps, maintaining a 1:1 ratio of liquid volume to headspace. The headspace was initially supplemented with 30% (v/v) high-purity methane gas (≥ 99.5%) using a gas-tight syringe fitted with a 0.22-µm filter. The cultures were incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm for 7 days. Following incubation, 1 mL of the supernatant was transferred to a fresh bottle containing 50 mL of NMS medium, and the headspace was replenished with 40% (v/v) methane in air. This serial enrichment process was repeated weekly for a total of four transfers, progressively increasing methane concentration from 30 to 70% (v/v) in the headspace. After the final enrichment cycle, aliquots were serially diluted and spread onto NMS agar plates (supplemented with 1.5% bacteriological agar). Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 72 h under microaerophilic conditions. Colonies exhibiting characteristic Azospirillum-like morphology (pink pigmentation, motile rods) were picked and re-streaked for purity. Representative isolates were preserved in 20% glycerol at −80 °C and subjected to further physiological and molecular characterization.

The type of strain Azospirillum vistecanum VT-I1 has been deposited in the Thailand Bioresource Research Center (TBRC), Pathum Thani, Thailand, under the accession number TBRC 18362. To obtain the strain, researchers should submit requests through the corresponding author, Assoc. Prof. Dr. Thanyaporn Wongnate (Vidyasirimedhi Institute of Science and Technology, Thailand). The strain will be made available for non-commercial academic research to ensure reproducibility and transparency.

Azospirillum vistecanum VT-I1 genome characterization

Genomic DNA of A. vistecanum VT-I1 was extracted using the ZymoBIOMICS® DNA Miniprep Kit (D4300, Zymo Research, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA concentration and purity were measured with a Varioskan™ LUX multimode microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). High-quality genomic DNA was subjected to long-read sequencing using the Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT, UK) platform with an R9.4.1 flow cell on a MinION device. Basecalling was performed in super-accuracy (SUP) mode with Guppy v5.0.11, which typically yields ~ 95–97% single-read accuracy on R9.4.1 chemistry, though occasional base errors can persist. Raw reads were quality-filtered and trimmed using Porechop v0.2.4 to remove adapter sequences (De Coster et al. 2018). A draft genome assembly was performed using Canu v2.1 with default parameters optimized for ONT reads (Lin and Liu 2022), and the assembly was polished through three iterative rounds using Racon v1.4.20 (Vaser et al. 2017). To quantify sequencing performance, reads were mapped back to the polished draft genome using minimap2 v2.28, and alignment files were processed with samtools v1.21 to compute coverage statistics. The final draft assembly achieved a breadth of coverage of 96.37% with an average depth of 171, supporting robust downstream genome analyses.

After polishing, taxonomic assignment and species-level relatedness were assessed using GTDB-Tk v2.4.0 against the GTDB reference package R220 (Chaumeil et al. 2020). We reported the closest GTDB representative genome together with average nucleotide identity (ANI) and alignment fraction (AF) values from the GTDB-Tk summary outputs to support taxonomic placement. Computational genome annotation was performed with Prokka v1.13 (Yoda et al. 2020) using default bacterial parameters, predicting protein-coding sequences, rRNAs, and tRNAs, and generating standardized GFF/FAA/FFN outputs for downstream analyses. The 16S rRNA gene was retrieved from the Prokka annotation and queried against the NCBI nt database using BLASTn (PMID: 20003500). Multiple sequence alignments and phylogenetic inference were performed using MEGA version 12 (Felsenstein 1981; Hall 2013) with the Tamura-Nei model (Tamura et al. 2011) under a maximum-likelihood framework. Branch support was assessed with 1000 bootstrap replicates. To identify genes associated with indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) biosynthesis, a targeted tblastn search was performed using curated reference proteins against the VT-I1 draft genome, with hits verified against the NCBI nt database. Putative functions and domain architectures were examined with the Pfam database (Coggill et al. 2008). Metabolic pathway reconstruction was conducted by mapping computationally annotated gene calls to tryptophan-dependent IAA biosynthetic routes using KEGG (Kanehisa et al. 2017). KEGG Orthology assignments were generated via BlastKOALA under bacterial settings.

Morphological characterization of Azospirillum vistecanum VT-I1

The cellular morphology of A. vistecanum VT-I1 was examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM; JSM-7610F, JEOL, Japan) following standard microbial imaging protocols (Gu et al. 2014; Gu et al. 2011). Cultures were harvested during the late exponential phase by centrifugation at 6000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The cell pellets were washed twice with sterile distilled water to remove residual media components. Samples were fixed by placing 50 µL of washed cell suspension onto sterile glass coverslips and air-dried at room temperature for 30 min. The coverslips were then mounted onto aluminum SEM stubs using conductive carbon adhesive discs. A thin layer of platinum (~ 5 nm) was sputter-coated onto the samples using a rotary sputter coater (JEOL Smart Coater) for 30 s at 10 mA under vacuum. SEM imaging was conducted in Gentle Beam mode to minimize charging and enhance surface resolution. Images were captured at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV at varying magnifications.

Optimization of cell growth conditions for Azospirillum vistecanum VT-I1

To optimize the growth conditions of A. vistecanum VT-I1, the effects of medium composition, incubation temperature, and initial pH were systematically evaluated. A single colony was first inoculated into 50 mL of three different media, Luria–Bertani (LB), yeast extract mannitol (YEM), and nitrogen-free bromothymol blue (NFb), in 250-mL Erlenmeyer flasks. Cultures were incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 100 rpm for 48 h to prepare the precultures. Each preculture was then adjusted to an initial optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.1 using fresh sterile medium. The inoculated cultures (50 mL in 250-mL flasks) were incubated under the same shaking conditions, and OD600 was monitored every 4 h over a 36-h period using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Varioskan™ LUX, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). All measurements were performed in triplicate, and uninoculated medium served as a blank control.

Preliminary screening indicated that LB medium supported the highest growth rate and was therefore selected for subsequent optimization experiments. The temperature (25–45 °C) and pH (5.0–8.0) ranges were chosen to reflect the reported physiological tolerance and optimal growth conditions of Azospirillum spp., which are mesophilic soil bacteria adapted to mildly acidic to neutral environments (Criollo-campos and Bonilla-buitrago 2015) (Lin et al. 2013). Most Azospirillum species exhibit optimal growth between 30 and 37 °C, while tolerating broader temperature ranges, with growth declining above ~ 40 °C. Similarly, these bacteria generally grow optimally near neutral pH but remain viable across pH values from approximately 5.0 to 8.0. For temperature optimization, cultures were incubated at 25, 30, 37, and 45 °C. For pH optimization, LB medium was adjusted to initial pH values ranging from 5.0 to 8.0 at 0.5-unit intervals using 2.5 M HCl or 10 M NaOH prior to autoclaving. All cultures were incubated with shaking at 100 rpm, and bacterial growth was monitored by measuring optical density at 600 nm (OD600). Growth curves were constructed to determine the conditions supporting maximal biomass accumulation.

Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) production by Azospirillum vistecanum VT-I1

The production of IAA by A. vistecanum VT-I1 was evaluated in batch cultures supplemented with L-tryptophan, a known precursor for microbial IAA biosynthesis. Based on previous studies, 5 mM L-tryptophan was selected as the optimal concentration for inducing IAA production in Azospirillum spp. (Pham et al. 2022).

Frozen glycerol stocks (20% v/v) of the isolate were revived on LB agar plates at 37 °C. A single colony was used to inoculate 100 mL of LB medium in a 250-mL Erlenmeyer flask, followed by incubation at 37 °C with shaking at 100 rpm for 48 h to prepare the starter culture. The culture was adjusted to an initial OD600 of 0.1 and transferred into fresh 100 mL LB medium supplemented with 5 mM filter-sterilized L-tryptophan. Cultures were inoculated at 37 °C and 100 rpm for 96 h. Aliquots (5 mL) were sampled at 24-h intervals, centrifuged at 8000 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22-µm nylon syringe filter to remove residual cells. The clarified samples were stored at 4 °C prior to IAA quantification. IAA concentrations were determined using liquid chromatography–quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (LC-QTOF-MS/MS). All measurements were performed in biological triplicate, and results are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) extraction and quantification

IAA produced by A. vistecanum VT-I1 was extracted from culture supernatants using a widely established organic solvent extraction method, followed by LC-QTOF-MS/MS analysis (Patten and Glick 2002) (Spaepen et al. 2007). After incubation, culture aliquots were centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant (20 mL) was carefully collected and acidified to pH 2.0 using 2.5 M HCl under cold conditions. The acidified supernatant was extracted twice with an equal volume of ethyl acetate (1:1, v/v) using a separatory funnel. After vigorous shaking for 2 min and phase separation for 10 min, the organic (upper) phase was collected. Combined organic extracts were evaporated to dryness at 45 °C under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator. The dried residue was reconstituted in 1 mL of 20% (v/v) methanol, vortexed, and filtered through a 0.22-µm PTFE syringe filter into amber glass vials. Samples were stored at −20 °C until LC-QTOF-MS/MS analysis. IAA was identified and quantified based on retention time, accurate mass (m/z 176.0706 for [M + H]+), and MS/MS fragmentation patterns, compared with an analytical-grade IAA standard (Arora et al. 2024).

Plant growth promotion assay

To evaluate the plant growth-promoting potential of bacterial-derived IAA, two assays were conducted: (1) in vitro root development of Exacum affine and (2) seed germination of Andrographis paniculata.

Exacum affine root growth assay

E. affine explants were cultured on Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium supplemented with 3% (w/v) sucrose to establish mother plants. MS medium is a widely used standard for in vitro plant tissue culture, providing a nutrient composition and ionic strength suitable for explant proliferation and regeneration under sterile conditions (Sarai et al. 2017). Cultures were maintained in a growth chamber at 22 ± 2 °C under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod for 2–3 weeks (Chen et al. 2024; Kalam et al. 2020). For IAA treatment, A. vistecanum VT-I1 was cultivated in LB medium supplemented with 5 mM L-tryptophan at 37 °C and 100 rpm for 48 h. Crude IAA was extracted from culture supernatants using ethyl acetate, evaporated at 45 °C, and the dried residue was dissolved in 20% (v/v) methanol. A solvent control containing 20% methanol without IAA was included in all treatments to exclude solvent-related effects. E. affine explants were transferred to MS medium containing 0 (control), 5, 10, or 20 µM IAA. These concentrations were selected based on previously reported effective ranges of auxin used in in vitro explant cultures, which allow evaluation of both growth-promoting and inhibitory responses (Malabadi, Teixeira, & Mulgund, 2009) (Javed et al. 2012). Each treatment consisted of five explants and was performed in triplicate. After 4 weeks, phenotypic parameters including plant height, root length, and dry biomass were measured. Roots were oven-dried at 60 °C for 48 h prior to weighing. Measurements were performed using digital calipers, and data are presented as mean ± SD.

(b) Andrographis paniculata seed germination assay

To evaluate the effect of IAA on seed germination, A. paniculata seeds were surface-sterilized by immersion in 70% (v/v) ethanol for 1 min, followed by treatment with 1–2% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite solution containing a few drops of Tween-20 for 10 min under continuous agitation. The seeds were then rinsed five times with sterile distilled water to completely remove residual sterilizing agents (Rout et al. 2000) (Eliwa et al. 2024). Sterilized seeds were subsequently primed with IAA solutions at concentrations of 0 (control), 5, 20, 50, 75, and 100 ppm. The selected concentration range was designed to cover both low and moderate auxin levels commonly used in seed germination and early seedling development studies, where concentrations within 10–100 ppm have been reported to promote germination, root initiation, and seedling vigor in various plant species, including medicinal plants, without inducing phytotoxic effects (Rawat Rakhi, 2011). Preliminary observations also indicated that concentrations within this range produced measurable physiological responses in A. paniculata, supporting its suitability for the present study. For germination assays, fifty seeds were placed in 90-mm Petri dishes lined with two layers of Whatman No. 1 filter paper and moistened with 5 mL of the respective IAA solutions. The dishes were incubated at 28–30 °C under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod for 7 days (Kumar et al. 2011; Rawat Rakhi, 2011; Zhao et al. 2020). Each treatment was conducted in replicate. Analytical methods

Quantification and structural confirmation of indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) were carried out using liquid chromatography coupled to quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (LC-QTOF-MS/MS; Bruker Daltonics, Germany). Chromatographic separation was achieved using an Agilent Polaris C18-A column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 µm particle size) maintained at 30 °C. The mobile phases consisted of solvent A: water with 0.1% formic acid, and solvent B: acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. The flow rate was set at 0.5 mL/min, and the elution followed a linear gradient: 5% B to 95% B over 15 min, held for 5 min, followed by re-equilibration. The injection volume was 10 µL. Mass spectrometric detection was performed in positive electrospray ionization mode (ESI+) with detection of the [M + H]+ ion for IAA at m/z 176.0706. MS parameters were set as follows: capillary voltage, 4500 V; nebulizer gas (N2), 2.0 bar; dry gas flow, 8 L/min; and drying temperature, 200 °C. MS/MS spectra were acquired using auto-MS mode with collision-induced dissociation. Data acquisition and analysis were performed using Bruker Compass Data Analysis software. IAA concentration was determined based on an external calibration curve prepared from analytical-grade IAA (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) over a range of 0.1–100 µM. Peak areas were integrated, and quantification was based on linear regression with R2 > 0.99. All samples and standards were analyzed in triplicate.

Cell growth was monitored by measuring OD600 using a Cary 60 UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies, USA) with a 1-cm pathlength quartz cuvette. Aliquots were taken every 4 h for a total of 36 h from batch cultures grown in 250-mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 50 or 100 mL of LB medium, depending on the experiment. Results

Isolation and morphological characterization of Azospirillum vistecanum VT-I1

A bacterial strain designated Azospirillum vistecanum VT-I1 was isolated from a methane-enriched enrichment culture derived from a food waste anaerobic digestion system. Although the enrichment environment contained methane, subsequent physiological evaluation demonstrated that VT-I1 does not utilize methane as a sole carbon source, as no detectable growth was observed when methane was provided as the only carbon substrate under controlled cultivation conditions. This observation indicates that the strain was likely enriched due to ecological adaptation to the digestion environment rather than methanotrophic metabolism.

The strain formed pink, round, mucoid, and viscous colonies on yeast extract mannitol (YEM) agar, which are characteristic features of Azospirillum spp. Such mucoid colony morphology is widely regarded as a phenotypic indicator of exopolysaccharide (EPS) production, as extracellular polysaccharides contribute to colony viscosity and surface glossiness. In Azospirillum, EPS production is closely associated with rhizosphere colonization, root surface attachment, biofilm formation, and enhanced tolerance to environmental stresses, supporting its role as a plant growth-promoting bacterium (Pereg & Mcmillan 2015) (Naseem 2018). Microscopic observations revealed that strain VT-I1 consisted of Gram-negative, rod-shaped, motile cells with an average length of approximately 2.99 µm. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) further confirmed elongated cells with smooth surfaces, while no visible flagellar structures were observed under the tested imaging conditions (Fig. 1). SEM images acquired at magnifications of ×20,000 and ×10,000 are presented in Fig. 1A and B, respectively.

Genomic identification and phylogenetic characterization of Azospirillum vistecanum VT-I1

Following the phenotypic and morphological characterization, the taxonomic position of strain VT-I1 was further verified using whole-genome sequencing and phylogenomic analysis. The draft genome was sequenced using Oxford Nanopore long-read technology, yielding high-coverage data for de novo assembly. The assembled genome comprised a single circular chromosome (2,872,242 bp) and seven plasmids: plsm1 (592,941 bp), plsm2 (418,726 bp), plsm3 (630,537 bp), plsm4 (747,470 bp), plsm5 (1,079,451 bp), plsm6 (45,551 bp), and plsm7 (884,270 bp), resulting in a total genome size of 6,834,379 bp with an average G + C content of 68.0% (Fig. S1). These genomic features are consistent with previously reported Azospirillum species (Anandham et al. 2019). Phylogenomic analysis placed A. vistecanum VT-I1 in close proximity to A. ramasamyi, A. zeae, and A. oryzae, while forming a distinct and well-supported branch (Fig. 2). To further substantiate its taxonomic status, average nucleotide identity (ANI) analysis was performed and visualized as a heatmap (Fig. 3). The ANI values between VT-I1 and its closest relatives ranged up to 92.68%, which is below the 95–96% species delineation threshold commonly applied in prokaryotic taxonomy (Imhoff et al. 2022; Pan et al. 2023; Richter et al. 2016). The heatmap-based ANI analysis clearly illustrates the genomic separation of VT-I1 from recognized Azospirillum species and corroborates the phylogenomic findings. Collectively, these results support the classification of A. vistecanum VT-I1 as a distinct taxonomic entity within the genus Azospirillum.

Optimization of culture conditions for Azospirillum vistecanum VT-I1 growth

To relate the genomic potential of A. vistecanum VT-I1 to its physiological performance, growth was examined under different media compositions, incubation temperatures, and initial pH values (Fig. 4A–C). VT-I1 exhibited significantly higher growth in LB medium, reaching an OD600 of 2.35 ± 0.15 and a specific growth rate (μ) 0.139 ± 0.001 h−1, compared to 1.82 ± 0.07 OD600 (μ = 0.079 ± 0.007 h−1) in YEM and 0.24 ± 0.02 OD600 (μ = 0.028 ± 0.007 h−1) in NFb (Fig. 4A). Temperature markedly influenced VT-I1 growth kinetics. The strain achieved the highest OD600 = 2.57 ± 0.03 and μ = 0.184 h−1 at 37 °C, followed by 30 °C and 25 °C, while growth decreased significantly at 45 °C (OD600 = 1.84 ± 0.13) (Fig. 4B). Growth was highest at pH 7.0 (OD600 = 2.41 ± 0.03; μ = 0.275 h−1), followed by pH 6.0. Growth declined under acidic (pH 5.0) and alkaline (pH 8.0) conditions (Fig. 4C). Collectively, these data indicate that LB medium, 37 °C, and pH 7.0 represent optimal growth conditions for A. vistecanum VT-I1. These conditions were therefore applied in subsequent experiments for IAA biosynthesis and plant growth-promoting assays.

IAA biosynthesis potential of Azospirillum vistecanum VT-I1

Based on the optimized culture parameters (LB medium, 37 °C, and pH 7.0), IAA production by A. vistecanum VT-I1 was evaluated in LB medium supplemented with 5 mM L-tryptophan, a known precursor for IAA biosynthesis (Spaepen and Vanderleyden 2011). In a short-term kinetic experiment, designed to monitor the temporal dynamics of IAA production, IAA synthesis was initiated within 24 h and reached a maximum concentration of 400 ± 22.23 µM (70.12 µg mL−1) at 48 h, followed by a gradual decrease up to day 4 (Fig. 5A). This decline likely reflects the onset of metabolic turnover, dilution effects associated with continued cell growth, and partial IAA conversion to downstream metabolites. In contrast, a long-term cultivation experiment, aimed at assessing net IAA accumulation capacity, revealed substantially higher IAA levels after prolonged incubation. Under identical culture conditions, A. vistecanum VT-I1 accumulated 1.206 ± 0.015 mM IAA after 10 days, significantly exceeding the level produced by A. brasilense (0.723 ± 0.006 mM) (Fig. 5B), corresponding to a 1.66-fold enhancement. A comparative analysis (Table 1) further demonstrates that A. vistecanum VT-I1 produces markedly higher IAA levels than other reported Azospirillum species, including A. zeae (39.11 µg mL−1) and A. oryzae (41.50 µg mL−1). Even the well-studied A. brasilense typically produces less than 60 µg mL−1 under nutrient-rich conditions (Pham et al. 2022; Tien et al. 1979). Together, these results indicate that VT-I1 exhibits both rapid early-phase IAA production and an exceptional capacity for long-term IAA accumulation, supporting its classification as one of the highest IAA-producing members within the genus and justifying further investigation into its biosynthetic regulation.

Genomic identification of the indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) biosynthetic pathway in Azospirillum vistecanum VT-I1

To uncover the genetic basis for the strong IAA production described above, genome-wide annotation of A. vistecanum VT-I1 was conducted to identify genes associated with tryptophan-dependent IAA biosynthetic routes. Three major pathways, the indole-3-pyruvate (IPyA), indole-3-acetamide (IAM), and tryptamine (TAM) routes, were used as reference frameworks (Abbas-Akbari et al. 2007; Prinsen 1993). Analysis revealed a complete IPyA pathway comprising three core enzymes (Fig. 6A):

A Overview of Trp-dependent IAA biosynthesis pathways, highlighting the IPyA pathway active in A. vistecanum VT-I1. B Genomic localization of key enzymes: aromatic amino acid aminotransferase, indole-3-pyruvate decarboxylase (IpdC), and aldehyde dehydrogenase (all on the reverse strand)

i. Aromatic amino acid aminotransferase (AspC/TyrB family; annotated as ARO9-like) predicted to catalyze the conversion of tryptophan to indole-3-pyruvate (coverage 100%, identity 87.44%).

ii. ThDP-dependent indole-3-pyruvate decarboxylase (ipdC) predicted to convert indole-3-pyruvate to indole-3-acetaldehyde (coverage 94%, identity 23.97%).

iii. Aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH) predicted to oxidize indole-3-acetaldehyde to IAA (coverage 100%, identity 85.57%).

Notably, these genes are colocated on the reverse strand of the chromosome (Fig. 6B), suggesting potential transcriptional co-regulation, consistent with reports in A. brasilense where ipdC functions as a key control point in the IPyA route (Somers et al. 2005). In contrast, genes characteristic of the IAM (e.g., iaaM/iaaH) and TAM pathways were not detected, supporting the IPyA pathway as the dominant route in VT-I1. No canonical iac gene clusters associated with IAA degradation, as reported in Burkholderia phytofirmans and Bradyrhizobium japonicum, were identified in the VT-I1 genome (Jensen et al. 1995; Zúñiga et al. 2013), suggesting a limited genetic capacity for dedicated IAA catabolism. However, we acknowledge that definitive confirmation of IAA degradation capability would require physiological validation, such as monitoring degradation kinetics following exogenous IAA supplementation, which was beyond the scope of the present study. Nevertheless, HPLC-MS/MS analysis qualitatively detected indole-3-glyoxylic acid in culture extracts (Fig. S2 and Table S2). The identification was based on retention time, accurate mass, and fragmentation pattern matching, rather than absolute quantification using an external standard. The presence of indole-3-glyoxylic acid suggests partial oxidative conversion of IAA, potentially mediated by nonspecific aldehyde or oxidoreductase activities rather than a dedicated IAA degradation pathway. Comparative qualitative analysis further showed that A. brasilense accumulated higher relative signal intensities of this metabolite (Fig. S3), consistent with its lower net IAA accumulation (Fig. 5B). Collectively, these observations indicate that A. vistecanum VT-I1 predominantly utilizes the IPyA pathway for IAA biosynthesis and likely exhibits limited IAA catabolic capacity, which may contribute to its exceptionally high net auxin yield.

Plant growth-promoting activity of IAA produced by Azospirillum vistecanum VT-I1

Building on the genomic evidence for IAA biosynthetic potential (see previous section), the physiological effects of A. vistecanum VT-I1–derived IAA were evaluated through bioassays involving Exacum affine and Andrographis paniculata.

Root development in Exacum affine

Purified IAA from A. vistecanum VT-I1 was applied to E. affine explants at 5, 10, and 20 µM concentrations in Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium. After 4 weeks, root elongation and biomass accumulation increased significantly relative to untreated controls (Fig. 7A). The maximum root length (4.54 ± 1.22 cm) was observed at 20 µM IAA, compared with 1.92 ± 0.65 cm in controls (Fig. 7B). Root dry weight and the number of roots per plant also increased, peaking at 107.03 ± 29.10 mg and 83 ± 14 roots plant−1, respectively, at 20 µM (Fig. 7C, D).

Seed germination in Andrographis paniculata

To test IAA effects on seed germination, A. paniculata seeds were primed with IAA at concentrations of 5–100 ppm. After 7 days, germination improved significantly at 20 ppm (13.9 ± 1.3%) and 50 ppm (14.1 ± 1.7%) compared with the control (~ 4%), while higher concentrations (75–100 ppm) inhibited germination (Fig. 8). Collectively, these assays confirm that IAA produced by A. vistecanum VT-I1 is biologically active and enhances both root morphogenesis and seed germination within optimal concentration ranges.

Discussion

The newly isolated strain Azospirillum vistecanum VT-I1 exhibits phenotypic features characteristic of the Azospirillum genus, including Gram-negative, rod-shaped morphology and pink mucilaginous colonies, which indicate robust exopolysaccharide (EPS) production. EPS is known to enhance root adhesion, desiccation tolerance, and soil aggregate formation, traits essential for effective rhizosphere colonization (Anandham et al. 2019). Unlike many Azospirillum species isolated from plant roots or rhizospheric soil, VT-I1 originated from a methane-enriched food waste digestion system, underscoring its adaptability to dynamic redox environments. This ecological origin may have selected for facultative microaerophilic metabolism and tolerance to fluctuating oxygen levels, traits beneficial for survival in both anaerobic digesters and plant rhizospheres (Bashan and de-Bashan 2010). Thus, VT-I1 extends the known ecological range of Azospirillum to anthropogenic, carbon-rich habitats, supporting its potential utility in organic waste valorization systems.

Genomic analyses corroborate the morphological classification of VT-I1 within the genus Azospirillum while indicating that it represents a novel species-level lineage. The genome size (6.83 Mb) and G + C content (68%) fall within the reported range for the genus; however, the presence of seven large plasmids suggests a high degree of genomic plasticity. In Azospirillum and other rhizosphere-associated bacteria, such plasmid-rich architectures are frequently associated with adaptive potential, horizontal gene transfer, and niche specialization rather than serving as direct evidence of specific functions (Molina et al. 2018; Pereg et al. 2016). Phylogenomic placement of VT-I1 as a distinct clade from A. ramasamyi and A. oryzae, together with an ANI value of 92.68%, supports its divergence at the species level (Imhoff et al. 2022; Pan et al. 2023; Richter et al. 2016). Based on analogies with other Azospirillum species, it is hypothesized that plasmid-borne genes may contribute to traits such as nitrogen fixation, auxin-related metabolism, and stress tolerance; however, these functional assignments cannot be conclusively inferred from genome architecture alone. Accordingly, the proposed roles of the plasmids should be regarded as working hypotheses, which will require targeted validation through plasmid-resolved annotation, comparative genomics, transcriptomic profiling, and functional assays. Nevertheless, the combination of a large genome, multiple plasmids, and species-level divergence highlights VT-I1 as a metabolically versatile bacterium with the potential to support both free-living and plant-associated lifestyles under variable environmental conditions.

The physiological growth profile of VT-I1 supports its genomic potential for nutrient flexibility and stress tolerance. Optimal growth occurred in nitrogen-rich LB medium, consistent with previous findings in A. brasilense and A. lipoferum (Abbas-Akbari et al. 2007), whereas weaker growth in NFb medium suggests partial dependence on organic nitrogen assimilation. Such behavior aligns with its isolation from organic waste, a nitrogen-rich environment. The temperature optimum at 37 °C demonstrates mesophilic adaptation typical of tropical Azospirillum spp. (Baldani et al. 2015), while growth inhibition at 45 °C reflects thermolabile enzyme stability as reported for other diazotrophs (Mattarelli et al. 1999; Zhang et al. 2018). Its pH tolerance (6.0–7.0) corresponds with rhizosphere conditions favorable to proton-coupled nutrient uptake and IAA synthesis (Fernández-Calviño & Bååth 2010; Jin & Kirk 2018; Razmi et al. 2023). Genomic annotation revealed proton-translocating ATPases and acid-resistance genes, reinforcing its resilience under fluctuating soil microenvironments. These characteristics establish VT-I1 as a physiologically robust candidate for biofertilizer development in warm, mildly acidic agricultural soils.

The strain’s exceptional IAA output can be interpreted as the result of a combination of physiological optimization and genomic features. The early-phase IAA accumulation followed by a transient decline is consistent with classical growth-associated auxin kinetics reported for plant growth-promoting bacteria (Bagheri et al. 2022; Tien et al. 1979). Compared with A. brasilense, the higher IAA yield observed in VT-I1 suggests enhanced enzymatic flux through the indole-3-pyruvate (IPyA) pathway, which represents the canonical route for IAA biosynthesis in Azospirillum spp. (Spaepen and Vanderleyden 2011). Genome analysis revealed the co-localization of ARO9, ipdC, and ALDH genes on the reverse strand of the chromosome, an arrangement that may facilitate coordinated transcription and increased metabolic flux through the IPyA pathway, as reported for other auxin-producing rhizobacteria (Masciarelli et al. 2013). In parallel, the absence of canonical iac gene clusters, previously implicated in IAA degradation in other bacterial systems (Jensen et al. 1995; Zúñiga et al. 2013), is consistent with reduced IAA turnover and may contribute to the higher net auxin levels observed in VT-I1. Taken together, these findings indicate that VT-I1 exhibits a biosynthetically dominant and potentially catabolically attenuated auxin metabolism, a genomic–physiological configuration that is strongly associated with sustained IAA availability in the rhizosphere, rather than implying a direct causal mechanism.

The plant bioassays provided functional confirmation of the microbial auxin’s activity. VT-I1-derived IAA stimulated Exacum affine root elongation, biomass accumulation, and lateral root proliferation in a dose-dependent manner, mirroring responses observed with A. brasilense (Spaepen et al. 2014). In Andrographis paniculata, moderate IAA concentrations (20–50 ppm) enhanced germination, whereas supraoptimal levels inhibited growth, demonstrating a classical biphasic hormonal response (Bharadwaj et al. 2017; Hossain & Urbi 2016; Shuai et al. 2017). These phenotypic outcomes, coupled with the absence of IAA-degrading genes, suggest prolonged auxin persistence and stronger signaling effects than in A. brasilense. Such characteristics enhance the strain’s potential as a bioinoculant for medicinal and horticultural crops requiring efficient early-stage root and seed development.

Integration of morphological, genomic, and functional evidence establishes A. vistecanum VT-I1 as a novel, high-performing Azospirillum species capable of thriving in both engineered and natural ecosystems. Its origin from a methane-enriched environment implies compatibility with waste-derived substrates, creating opportunities for circular-bioeconomy applications such as combined waste valorization and biofertilizer production. Microbial auxin production offers a sustainable alternative to synthetic hormones that are costly and environmentally persistent (Hossain et al. 2021; Kadapatti & Murthy 2022). By enhancing root development and nutrient uptake, VT-I1-based formulations could improve soil fertility, particularly in degraded or saline lands. These findings resonate with current efforts to integrate microbial biotechnology into the Bio-Circular-Green (BCG) framework for sustainable agriculture. Future research should involve transcriptomic profiling of the ipdC cluster, in-planta colonization assays, and field-scale validation of its biofertilizer potential. Such studies will clarify regulatory dynamics and evaluate its competitiveness in native soil microbiomes.

Conclusion and future perspective

In this study, we successfully isolated, characterized, and evaluated a novel strain, Azospirillum vistecanum VT-I1, derived from a methane-enriched digestate system, for its capacity to produce IAA and promote plant growth. Comprehensive phenotypic, genomic, and biochemical analyses confirmed that VT-I1 represents a distinct species within the Azospirillum genus and exhibits markedly enhanced IAA biosynthesis compared with well-established strains such as A. brasilense. Genomic analysis revealed the presence of key genes in the indole-3-pyruvate (IPyA) pathway, aro9, ipdC, and aldA, which are associated with efficient auxin biosynthesis. The absence of canonical IAA degradation genes is consistent with high net auxin accumulation in this strain. Functionally, IAA produced by A. vistecanum VT-I1 significantly enhanced root development in E. affine and improved germination in A. paniculata, demonstrating its biological activity as a plant growth-promoting phytohormone. While no direct comparison with chemically synthesized auxins was conducted in this study, the observed responses are comparable to those reported for exogenous auxin treatments in similar plant systems, highlighting the potential of microbial IAA as a promising and sustainable bio-based alternative for agricultural applications. Future studies directly comparing microbial and synthetic auxins under standardized conditions will be valuable to further substantiate this potential.

Looking forward, several directions merit further investigation to fully realize the agricultural utility of this strain. It should be noted that long-term plant growth experiments, including full life-cycle assessments and yield measurements, were beyond the scope of the present study, which focused on mechanistic characterization and short-term functional assays under controlled conditions. First, direct experimental validation of plant root colonization by A. vistecanum VT-I1 is required, using in planta assays such as root attachment and persistence studies, fluorescent or genetic labeling, and quantitative colonization analyses under controlled greenhouse and field conditions. These experiments will be critical to confirm its rhizosphere competence and practical applicability. Second, long-term greenhouse and field trials across multi-crop species should be conducted to evaluate sustained growth promotion, yield stability, and plant health throughout the cultivation cycle, including under abiotic stress conditions such as drought or salinity. Third, the development of scalable and stable formulations, such as encapsulated inoculants or co-culture-based products, will be essential to ensure shelf life, delivery efficiency, and consistent performance in agricultural applications. In addition, comprehensive environmental impact and biosafety assessments are necessary to confirm ecological compatibility and regulatory compliance. From a mechanistic perspective, transcriptomic and proteomic analyses could further elucidate the regulation of IAA biosynthesis, particularly the environmental cues governing ipdC expression. Finally, integrating VT-I1 into microbial consortia or microbiome engineering strategies may yield synergistic benefits for plant growth, nutrient cycling, and stress resilience. Collectively, these future studies will help establish A. vistecanum VT-I1 as a promising next-generation plant growth-promoting inoculant, contributing to more sustainable, efficient, and environmentally friendly agricultural systems.
