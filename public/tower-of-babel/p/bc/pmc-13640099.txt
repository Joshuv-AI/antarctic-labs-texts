Regulated cell death plasticity in cancer: thresholds, reversibility, and therapeutic failure
Authors: Tooba Jawwad, Sadaf Khursheed Baba, Anju Surendranath, Elena Chaudhary, Ghulam Jeelani, Ammira S. Al-Shabeeb Akil, Asma Al Menhali, Ajaz A. Bhat, Sameer Mirza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Therapy resistance and disease recurrence remain major challenges across cancer types despite substantial advances in targeted therapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy. In many clinical settings, treatments successfully engage stress and death signaling pathways and produce rapid tumor regression, yet complete and durable eradication is uncommon. A consistent pattern emerges in which most tumor cells are eliminated while a small fraction survives, persists as residual disease, and later drives relapse. These observations suggest that failure is not always due to the absence of death pathways, but rather to incomplete execution. Here, we synthesize emerging evidence supporting the view that regulated cell death operates as a threshold-governed, highly plastic process. Apoptosis, ferroptosis, and inflammatory death programs remain largely intact in many tumors but are tightly controlled by buffering networks that regulate mitochondrial commitment, redox balance, metabolic state, and inflammatory signaling. Under therapeutic pressure, cancer cells frequently activate proximal death signaling without progressing to irreversible collapse. Sublethal engagement can generate stressed but viable cell states that contribute to drug tolerance, minimal residual disease, and later resistance evolution. Clinical data across hematologic and solid malignancies reinforce this framework. Biomarkers of pathway activation often correlate with early response but do not reliably predict long-term benefit. Dose limitations, treatment interruptions, and adaptive rewiring further allow surviving populations to recover and expand. We propose that execution depth, rather than pathway activation alone, represents a critical determinant of durable response. This perspective supports therapeutic strategies that simultaneously drive death signaling while disabling buffering systems, prevent adaptive state transitions, and incorporate immune-mediated clearance to achieve sustained tumor elimination.

Introduction

Despite major advances in cancer therapy, long-lasting clinical responses remain uncommon [1]. Many tumors initially respond to chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy, yet a significant fraction of cancer cells survive treatment and later drive relapse or metastatic progression [2, 3]. While intratumoral genetic heterogeneity contributes to therapeutic resistance [4–6], accumulating evidence indicates that treatment failure cannot be explained solely by permanent loss of cell death machinery [7, 8]. Instead, cancer cells frequently retain intact regulated cell death pathways but adaptively modulate death thresholds, timing, and signaling strength, allowing survival under therapeutic stress [9, 10].

For decades, apoptosis was viewed as the dominant and therapeutically relevant mode of cancer cell death [11, 12]. This view shaped the development of strategies aimed at restoring apoptotic signaling, including death receptor agonists and inhibitors of anti-apoptotic b-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL-2) family proteins [13]. However, both preclinical models and clinical studies have revealed that pharmacologic reactivation of apoptotic signaling does not uniformly result in tumor eradication, as cancer cells frequently adapt to apoptosis-targeting agents such as Bcl-2 homology 3 (BH3) mimetics or death receptor agonists, leading to incomplete responses and persistent disease [8, 14, 15]. Mechanistic studies demonstrate that mitochondrial apoptotic signaling can be engaged without irreversible commitment to execution, revealing that apoptotic activation alone is insufficient to guarantee cell elimination [9, 16].

Over the past decade, research has expanded the spectrum of regulated cell death beyond apoptosis, showing that these pathways work as a connected system rather than separate routes with distinct biochemical and immunological effects [17, 18]. These death modalities are not mutually exclusive but are linked through shared signaling nodes involving mitochondrial function, redox balance, lipid metabolism, inflammatory mediators, and caspase activity [19]. For example, caspase-3 activation can redirect apoptotic signaling toward pyroptotic execution through gasdermin cleavage [20], while p53-dependent metabolic regulation can shift cells between apoptotic and ferroptotic death programs, showing how the same regulators can connect different death outcomes [21]. Recent work further supports this network view. Lee et al. described extensive biochemical crosstalk among apoptosis, pyroptosis, necroptosis, and ferroptosis, and introduced the concept of PANoptosis, in which multiple death programs are activated together under strong stress or inflammatory signals [22]. This suggests that cell fate is often determined by the combined activity of multiple pathways rather than a single dominant mechanism. Evidence from tumor biology also supports this idea. Zhang et al. reported that resistance to apoptosis is a major contributor to therapeutic failure, while alternative death pathways such as pyroptosis, ferroptosis, and necroptosis remain active and can be targeted to overcome treatment resistance [23]. This has shifted attention toward non-apoptotic cell death pathways as potential strategies to overcome resistance to conventional treatments.

In addition to genetic alterations, emerging studies indicate that epigenetic regulation can reshape death responses by altering chromatin state, DNA methylation, and transcription, allowing cancer cells to tune death signaling without deleting pathway components. He et al. summarized how these mechanisms change the expression of key death-associated genes and shift sensitivity across death programs [24]. This helps explain why many therapies clearly stress tumor cells but still fail to fully eliminate them.

Activation of regulated cell death signaling does not always translate into cell elimination. Death commitment is often conditional and variable over time, creating a window for adaptation before irreversible execution occurs. Sublethal death signaling can promote stress tolerance, inflammatory signaling, and phenotypic plasticity, enabling subsets of cancer cells to persist in drug-tolerant, stem-like, or dormant states that later drive disease recurrence [8, 9, 25, 26]. This concept aligns with fundamental biological observations that cell death is essential for maintaining tissue balance. Fernández-Lázaro et al. showed that normal tissues sustain equilibrium through continuous coordination between cell proliferation and controlled cell elimination, and disruption of this balance contributes to cancer development [27]. Insufficient cell death allows abnormal cells to accumulate, whereas excessive or mis regulated death can promote tissue damage and inflammation.

In this review, we argue that regulated cell death in cancer should be understood as a dynamic, stress-responsive decision network rather than a collection of independent execution pathways. We examine how modulation of death thresholds, timing, and reversibility underlies therapy resistance, cancer stemness, dormancy, and immune regulation. We further discuss why strategies focused on single death pathways have failed clinically and highlight how emerging single-cell and spatial approaches are reshaping our understanding of death decisions by revealing rare and context-dependent death-resistant states that bulk assays miss but are critical for long-term therapeutic failure. Cancer-type specific regulated cell death modalities and therapeutic vulnerabilities

Decision-making, thresholds, and reversibility in regulated cell death

While Table 1 summarizes the diversity of regulated cell death pathways and therapeutic responses across cancers, these observations raise a key question: why do some cells die while others survive despite engaging similar death pathways? Addressing this requires moving beyond pathway activation and focusing on the dynamic processes that determine whether cells cross the threshold for irreversible death. High-resolution single-cell and live-imaging studies show that cancer cells can activate regulated cell death signals and still survive [68, 75–77]. The outcome often depends on how strong the stress becomes over time, and whether it rises above the cell’s buffering capacity [77, 78]. In many cases, the key question is not “is the pathway on?” but “does the signal go far enough to force commitment?” Cells can show mitochondrial stress, early caspase activity, or oxidative damage without reaching a point of no return [79]. Mitochondria, redox systems, and metabolism act as major buffering hubs. If stress stays below a critical threshold, repair and survival programs can contain the damage. If stress keeps rising, execution becomes self-amplifying, and collapse becomes unavoidable [80]. This makes death commitment progressive rather than instant. For example, sublethal mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization, partial caspase activation [9, 81], or moderate lipid peroxidation can occur without immediate execution [82]. In such cases, cells may still recover through antioxidant responses, activation of autophagy, DNA repair, or metabolic adaptation. These observations reinforce that death commitment develops over time rather than occurring as an immediate event.

Regulated cell death is also linked to broader stress responses. When homeostasis is disturbed, cells often activate protective programs such as autophagy and the unfolded protein response (UPR) before they commit to apoptosis [83, 84]. During ER stress, the UPR can restore proteostasis through adaptive transcription, but it can also shift toward pro-death signaling if stress is not resolved [85]. Because tumor cells often operate near the limit of proteostasis, changes in hypoxia, nutrients, reactive oxygen species (ROS), or calcium can push cells into UPR states that delay execution and raise the death threshold [85]. In this context, early caspase activation can remain reversible until downstream effector cascades are fully engaged, highlighting that death commitment is not immediate [68].

This threshold-buffered delay in execution creates a biologically meaningful window for adaptation. During this period, cells can activate compensatory stress responses, remodel metabolic programs, or reroute signaling before irreversible damage accumulates [86]. In cancer cells, this adaptive phase may be especially pronounced due to enhanced metabolic flexibility and stress tolerance. These features allow tumor cells to survive transient therapeutic insults and later resume proliferation. Another key determinant of this decision process is signaling plasticity between pathways. Molecules such as caspase-8 function as central switches that can direct cells toward apoptosis, necroptosis, or survival depending on context, indicating that pathway engagement alone does not dictate outcome [87, 88]. As a result, regulated cell death in cancer behaves less like a binary switch and more like a threshold-based process, where execution occurs only when cumulative stress overwhelms the cell’s capacity to adapt [10]. This threshold model helps explain why some cells die rapidly while others survive the same treatment. Small differences in protein levels, metabolic state, cell cycle phase, or microenvironmental conditions can shift the balance between survival and execution.

Recent studies also suggest that reversibility may extend beyond what was previously assumed. Early stages of apoptotic or oxidative stress signaling can sometimes be halted or reversed if damaging stimuli are removed in time [89, 90]. This reflects that commitment to death occurs over time, governed by signal accumulation and cellular buffering capacity rather than a single decisive event. Such plasticity creates a survival advantage for subsets of cancer cells, allowing them to endure treatment, enter transient drug-tolerant states, and later contribute to disease recurrence [91, 92]. Together, these findings support a model in which regulated cell death is a dynamic decision-making process shaped by stress thresholds, signaling kinetics, and adaptive capacity. In this framework, execution occurs only when damage exceeds the cell’s ability to compensate, positioning death not as an immediate outcome but as the final stage of progressive cellular failure. These threshold-governed dynamics are most clearly illustrated by sublethal execution states that emerge during therapy exposure.

Sublethal death states and fractional killing

Many cancer cells survive despite partial activation of execution machinery. Mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization, caspase activation, and downstream signaling can occur transiently or at submaximum amplitude without resulting in cell death [9, 25]. These sublethal responses are not pathological failures of death pathways but reflect controlled intermediate states in which execution is restrained [16]. Rather than functioning as a binary on–off switch, apoptotic signaling behaves as a graded system in which cells integrate stress signals before committing to terminal collapse [93].

Sublethal engagement of apoptosis is not biologically neutral. Limited mitochondrial permeabilization can generate localized caspase activation that induces DNA damage and genomic instability while preserving viability, demonstrating that cells can traverse partially executed states that reshape their future behavior [9, 10, 94]. These findings suggest that early apoptotic signaling may contribute to tumor evolution by introducing genomic alterations and adaptive transcriptional responses even in surviving cells. Such observations challenge the classical model of apoptosis as an immediate and irreversible endpoint and instead support a view in which early signaling functions as a stress-sensing and adaptation phase [95].

A key mechanistic explanation for sublethal apoptotic signaling is the phenomenon of minority permeabilization (MOMP), in which only a subset of mitochondria within a cell undergoes permeabilization. This limited permeabilization generates low-level caspase activation that is insufficient to trigger full execution but still produces biologically meaningful effects. MOMP has long been considered a point of no return, as widespread mitochondrial destruction can commit cells to death through metabolic catastrophe even in the absence of caspase activity [96]. However, this commitment is not always absolute. Under certain conditions, cell death following MOMP can be averted if glycolysis is sustained and damaged mitochondria are removed through autophagy, allowing expansion of the remaining functional mitochondrial pool [81, 97].

Emerging evidence further indicates that when only a minority of mitochondria undergo permeabilization, or when death receptor signaling induces limited caspase activation, sublethal engagement of the apoptotic machinery can activate caspase-activated DNase (CAD) and trigger DNA damage without causing immediate cell death [9, 98]. As a result, surviving cells may accumulate genomic instability in a caspase- and CAD-dependent manner. This directly links sublethal apoptotic signaling to genomic instability and tumor evolution [7, 99]. In melanoma drug-treatment settings, single-cell studies show that mitochondrial permeabilization can be spatially restricted, producing sublethal caspase signaling without full execution [7, 9, 76, 77]. These sublethal states are not rare exceptions; they represent a controllable intermediate regime in which execution machinery is engaged but deliberately held below runaway amplification, creating room for repair and adaptation.

At the population level, this graded execution behavior provides a mechanistic explanation for fractional killing [76]. When genetically similar cancer cells are exposed to the same cytotoxic stimulus, only a fraction crosses the commitment threshold, while others survive despite engaging comparable upstream pathways [76]. Single-cell analyses indicate that differences in signaling kinetics, particularly the timing and amplitude of caspase activation, determine whether individual cells transition from reversible signaling to irreversible execution [77]. Mathematical and experimental models further demonstrate that small variations in protein levels, mitochondrial priming, or signaling dynamics can produce large differences in survival outcomes, giving rise to heterogeneous treatment responses within clonal populations [100, 101].

Single-cell studies of death receptor signaling provide direct evidence for threshold-governed persistence. In TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL)-treated populations, commitment depends on reaching a critical level of caspase-8 activity; cells that fail to cross this threshold survive and can display transient resistance to subsequent exposure to TRAIL or other death receptor ligands [76, 102]. Notably, this persistent state can be reinforced by periodic receptor engagement in the presence of caspase inhibitors and is associated with a nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) linked inflammatory signature, supporting the idea that survival can be actively induced rather than purely selected [102].

Survival following therapy does not imply permanent escape from death. Persisting cells often retain intact apoptotic competence and can die upon re-exposure, consistent with reversible state transitions rather than stable resistance [103]. In melanoma, drug-treated tumor populations frequently enter transient persister states characterized by altered signaling, reduced proliferation, and enhanced stress tolerance, yet these states can collapse once the stress is removed [92, 104]. Studies across multiple cancer types indicate that reversible survival states often arise from dynamic regulation of apoptotic execution thresholds through modulation of mitochondrial priming and survival signaling rather than from permanent loss of core death machinery [105, 106]. Evidence for an induced, state-dependent survival program also comes from persister models following chemotherapy. Across multiple agents and clonal lines, surviving cells frequently exhibit an emergent dependency on glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), rendering them selectively vulnerable to ferroptosis induction, whereas parental populations are often comparatively insensitive [103, 107]. The striking consistency of this GPX4 dependence across diverse stressors suggests that persistence reflects an acquired adaptive program rather than simple enrichment of rare pre-existing resistant clones.

While persistence reflects survival without full execution, additional evidence suggests that reversibility may extend even to cells that have already entered advanced stages of apoptotic signaling. Reversibility may extend beyond early signaling stages. The phenomenon of anastasis demonstrates that cells can recover after progressing deep into apoptotic signaling, including after caspase activation and mitochondrial disruption, if the damaging stimulus is withdrawn in time [25, 108]. This capacity for recovery reinforces the concept that death commitment is progressive rather than instantaneous. Cells may cross early biochemical checkpoints yet remain viable as long as downstream amplification loops do not fully engage. Such intermediate states create a window in which cells can repair damage, rewire signaling, and re-enter proliferation.

From a broader perspective, sublethal execution should be understood as a regulated adaptive phase. Early engagement of death pathways can activate stress response networks, remodel metabolic programs, and increase tolerance to subsequent insults [86]. These adaptive changes help explain how tumor cells survive initial treatment and later contribute to recurrence. Sublethal signaling may therefore play an active role in shaping cellular heterogeneity by selecting for states capable of enduring sustained stress [109, 110].

Overall, these studies challenge the idea that apoptosis is always an immediate, one-way endpoint. Instead, early death signaling often sits in a controlled, sublethal range. This explains fractional killing, reversible drug tolerance, and the survival of small subpopulations even when pathway activation is clear. The key therapeutic problem is therefore not turning death pathways on, but pushing enough cells past the execution threshold.

Metabolic and redox control of death decisions

Differences in death outcomes among genetically similar cancer cells are consistently observed even when upstream death signaling is comparably engaged. This variability is not explained by pathway defects but instead reflects differences in the ability of individual cells to cross execution thresholds. Experimental and computational studies increasingly point to cellular metabolic and redox states as key determinants of these thresholds [111, 112]. Programs that regulate redox balance, mitochondrial stability, and membrane integrity strongly influence whether death signaling is contained or progresses toward irreversible execution [113].

As tumors expand, metabolic resources become unevenly distributed. Nutrient limitation, accumulation of metabolic byproducts, and fluctuating oxygen availability generate pronounced metabolic heterogeneity within the tumor microenvironment [114, 115]. Rather than simply restricting proliferation, these conditions reshape the biochemical context in which death decisions are evaluated. Metabolic adaptations selected to tolerate nutrient and redox stress raise death commitment thresholds without eliminating death signaling itself. For example, enhanced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH)-dependent antioxidant buffering suppresses oxidative amplification of stress signals [116, 117], allowing survival despite sustained engagement of death-associated pathways [118].

Oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and membrane damage are central to the execution of multiple regulated cell death programs [112, 119, 120]. Each of these processes is strongly influenced by metabolic flux [121]. Proliferating cancer cells reroute central carbon metabolism toward anabolic output, increasing flux through redox-coupled reactions that generate Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide-reduced form (NADH) while consuming NAD+ [122]. Under hypoxic conditions common in solid tumors, excess NADH can exceed the regenerative capacity of the mitochondrial electron transport chain, promoting electron leakage and ROS production [123, 124]. These conditions activate stress and death signaling without necessarily triggering immediate execution.

Redox homeostasis therefore represents a critical determinant of death susceptibility [112, 125]. ROS occupy a dual role in this context. At moderate levels, they function as signaling intermediates; beyond a critical threshold, they act as execution triggers [125]. Cancer cells with enhanced capacity to regenerate NADPH most notably through the pentose phosphate pathway and associated dehydrogenase reactions can buffer oxidative stress sufficiently to prevent this transition [118, 126, 127]. Sustained antioxidant capacity allows prolonged engagement of death signaling while limiting escalation to irreversible mitochondrial failure or membrane rupture [128].

Membrane composition introduces an additional layer of control. The saturation state and remodeling dynamics of phospholipids determine how oxidative stress translates into structural damage, with enzymes such as acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4)-dependent incorporation of polyunsaturated fatty acids into membrane phospholipids acting as a key determinant of ferroptotic susceptibility [129–131]. Metabolic rewiring that favors phospholipid remodeling and lipid repair stabilizes membrane integrity under sustained stress, limiting damage propagation across organelles [132]. Oxidation of specific phosphatidylethanolamine species establishes a biochemical threshold at the membrane level, governing whether oxidative stress remains sublethal or progresses to execution [129]. Although most clearly defined in ferroptosis, this principle applies more broadly by constraining how damage spreads within the cell [133]. This implies that death sensitivity is, to a meaningful extent, an emergent property of metabolic organization: the same oncogenic program that drives growth can also expand the buffering headroom that delays commitment.

Metabolites themselves also function as regulators of death competence. Changes in NAD+ availability, acetyl-CoA levels, and tricarboxylic acid cycle intermediates reshape gene expression, chromatin accessibility, and protein modification states, thereby tuning responsiveness to death signals [134–136]. In parallel, metabolites within the tumor microenvironment influence stromal and immune cell behavior, indirectly shaping death susceptibility across the tumor ecosystem [137]. Death regulation therefore emerges as a product of dynamic metabolic crosstalk rather than a strictly cell-autonomous process. These metabolic and redox programs allow cells to engage stress signaling while preventing progression to irreversible execution, helping explain reversible resistance and therapy tolerance. Plasticity and pathway switching under therapeutic pressure

Therapeutic pressure creates a dynamic signaling landscape in which multiple death pathways are simultaneously engaged but rarely executed to completion [17, 68, 138]. Instead of committing to a single execution route, cancer cells frequently redistribute stress signals across overlapping regulatory networks that include apoptotic, ferroptotic, and inflammatory programs [69, 139]. This redistribution buffers execution intensity and allows survival competence to be preserved under fluctuating stress conditions [140, 141]. This process is not passive. It reflects active crosstalk between stress-response pathways, metabolic adaptation, and survival signaling networks that collectively restrain execution while maintaining signaling engagement. We interpret pathway switching as a symptom of a broader systems strategy. Cells redistribute stress across partially redundant modules to avoid any single pathway reaching an execution threshold.

Within this adaptive environment, a central feature of therapy response is the coexistence of genetic and non-genetic resistance mechanisms operating at different temporal scales. Even highly effective treatments leave behind small surviving populations that persist as minimal residual disease (MRD) and later contribute to relapse and genetic resistance evolution [142, 143]. These early survivors often do not escape through mutation alone. Instead, they rely on reversible phenotypic adjustments that transiently reduce dependency on the targeted pathway. This allows cells to endure the initial therapeutic assault. Plasticity therefore enables tumor cells to temporarily disengage from therapeutic targets while remaining viable. Cells can reversibly transition into alternative phenotypic identities that are less dependent on the inhibited signaling axis, supporting survival during treatment exposure [144]. These adaptive shifts are closely linked to epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and stemness-associated programs. Cellular plasticity is strongly connected with epithelial–mesenchymal transitions and acquisition of stem-like properties that promote tumor progression, heterogeneity, and therapeutic resistance [145, 146]. Through these programs, cells remodel their metabolic state and transcriptional identity. This, in turn, alters how death signals are interpreted.

Phenotypic transitions do not occur as abrupt binary switches. Transitions toward mesenchymal states can occur gradually and incompletely, producing partial or hybrid phenotypes in which functional traits evolve over time [147, 148]. These intermediate states often retain survival competence while engaging stress pathways at sublethal intensity, allowing persistence under therapy. As a result, death signaling remains active but contained, supporting adaptation rather than immediate execution. Such intermediate phenotypes also facilitate signaling integration across pathways, allowing apoptotic, metabolic, and inflammatory responses to be coordinated in a manner that favors survival rather than collapse. At the molecular level, these transitions are orchestrated through coordinated signaling and transcriptional networks. Regulatory control involves microRNAs and major pathways such as transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β), Wnt, Notch homolog 3 (NOTCH3), and Hippo. It also involves transcription factors including Snail family transcriptional repressor 1 (SNAI1), Snail family transcriptional repressor 2 (SNAI2), zinc finger E-box-binding homeobox (ZEB) family members, and Twist family BHLH transcription factor 1 (TWIST1). These factors dynamically reshape gene expression programs [149, 150]. These nodes provide a framework through which cells can redistribute stress responses without eliminating death machinery. Plasticity is further reinforced by epigenetic reprogramming. External stresses such as hypoxia and therapy-induced signaling changes can reshape chromatin accessibility and transcriptional identity, enabling phenotypic switching and adaptive survival [149, 151]. These epigenetic shifts stabilize adaptive states long enough for cells to endure treatment cycles.

Consistent with this view, pathway switching is not a random process but a regulated and reversible adaptation. Increasing evidence indicates that epigenetic remodeling, chromatin accessibility shifts, and transcriptional reprogramming enable rapid transitions between death-sensitive and death-tolerant states, generating heterogeneous survival outcomes even within clonal populations [152]. Plasticity is further reinforced by microenvironmental inputs. Hypoxia, nutrient limitation, and immune-derived cytokines reshape mitochondrial priming, redox buffering, and inflammatory signaling competence. Rather than simply activating or suppressing death pathways, these environmental signals fine-tune execution thresholds. This determines whether stress signals remain contained or progress toward irreversible collapse [7, 114, 123]. As a result, death pathway engagement becomes context-dependent, shaped by oncogenic state, microenvironmental cues, and treatment history [70, 153]. Supporting this model, single-cell trajectories of forkhead box M1 (FOXM1) dynamics under cell-cycle perturbations resolve distinct stress-response paths that map onto divergent fates. These findings support the idea that timing and state history determine whether stressed cells remain buffered or progress to execution [154]. These observations support the concept that temporal state dynamics, rather than static markers, influence whether stressed cells remain buffered or progress toward execution. Over repeated treatment cycles, these adaptive programs can consolidate into persister-like populations that remain death-competent but rarely cross execution thresholds, contributing to MRD and relapse [86, 103, 155]. Collectively, these observations suggest that regulated cell death plasticity represents a systems-level survival strategy. Rather than eliminating death pathways, cancer cells actively redistribute and buffer execution signals across multiple mechanisms, maintaining viability under sustained stress while preserving the capacity to die under different conditions [156, 157]. Conceptually, cancer stem-like cells (CSCs) and dormant cells can be viewed as long-lived solutions to the same problem: maintaining viability by keeping execution below threshold while preserving the capacity to re-enter growth when constraints ease.

Apoptosis suppression and adaptive rewiring

Apoptosis remains the most intensively targeted death pathway in cancer therapy [158]. Yet, clinical and experimental evidence consistently shows that suppression of apoptosis rarely requires a complete loss of the apoptotic machinery [159, 160]. Instead, cancer cells adjust apoptotic sensitivity by reshaping upstream regulators and execution thresholds, allowing apoptotic signaling to be engaged without progressing to irreversible execution [159]. This pattern supports a threshold-modulation model in which apoptotic signaling can be engaged yet remain functionally restrained below commitment levels. Plasticity provides a mechanistic explanation for this phenomenon. Surviving tumor cells can enter transient adaptive states during treatment and later either regain sensitivity or progress toward stable resistance phenotypes [86, 161]. In apoptotic terms, this suggests that mitochondrial death commitment is frequently delayed through buffering rather than permanently disabled. A key route for this buffering is reversible identity change, whereby cells adopt alternative phenotypic states that reduce reliance on the drug-targeted pathway, allowing continued survival despite persistent stress signaling [144]. EMT-linked transitions are particularly relevant to apoptotic rewiring. EMT involves structural and transcriptional reorganization, including cytoskeletal remodeling, polarity changes, and disruption of cell–cell adhesion, while mesenchymal–epithelial transition factor (MET) represents the reverse transition [162, 163]. These changes influence mitochondrial signaling, integrin engagement, and survival pathway activation, collectively altering apoptotic priming.

Building on this framework, plasticity-associated transcriptional programs further reinforce reversible identity shifts that influence apoptotic priming. During early therapy exposure, tumor cells frequently activate compensatory survival networks including Yes-associated protein (YAP) and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) signaling, accompanied by elevation of anti-apoptotic proteins such as myeloid cell leukemia 1 (MCL1) and enhanced receptor signaling through insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF1R), epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), MET, and AXL receptor tyrosine kinase (AXL). These adaptive signaling responses support survival during the initial therapeutic phase and can be targeted through combination strategies to reduce residual tumor cell persistence [157, 164–167]. Epigenetic remodeling further stabilizes apoptosis-buffered states. Therapy-associated environmental pressures can induce epigenetic alterations that reshape gene expression programs and sustain adaptive phenotypes [168–171]. Such changes can maintain survival states long enough to withstand repeated treatment cycles. At the same time, these adaptive states are reinforced by alterations in core apoptotic regulators. Disruption of tumor protein p53 (TP53) signaling illustrates this principle. Loss or mutation of TP53 does not abolish mitochondrial apoptotic capacity but alters how stress signals are integrated and amplified [172, 173]. TP53-deficient cells can still engage cytochrome c release and caspase activation. However, they require stronger or more sustained stress to reach execution thresholds [174]. This shift permits tolerance of DNA damage and therapeutic insult without immediate cell death, promoting survival under continued stress [174].

Overexpression of anti-apoptotic members such as BCL-2, b-cell lymphoma-extra-large (BCL-xL), or myeloid cell leukemia 1 (MCL-1) suppresses cytochrome c release and delays caspase activation. Importantly, these events are not completely blocked but are instead restrained [8, 175, 176]. This buffering is dynamic. Therapeutic exposure can transiently alter mitochondrial priming, allowing cells to survive initial treatment while remaining susceptible under altered conditions [105, 177, 178]. The clinical consequences of mitochondrial adaptability are particularly evident in responses to BH3 mimetics, where compensatory survival mechanisms frequently limit durable apoptotic execution. Although these agents directly target anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family proteins, cancer cells frequently survive by compensatory upregulation of alternative anti-apoptotic factors or by reducing mitochondrial sensitivity to permeabilization [179, 180]. Partial mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization and sublethal caspase activation are commonly observed, indicating restrained execution rather than pathway failure. Apoptotic suppression in cancer therefore reflects modulation of execution thresholds, not loss of apoptotic competence.

Persistence is not necessarily permanent. Residual surviving populations may later contribute to recurrence and accumulate genetic changes over time [142, 143]. This suggests a sequential process in which early reversible survival transitions are followed by more stable resistance consolidation. From a therapeutic standpoint, this reinforces the need for combination approaches. Targeting the biological pathways that regulate plasticity and adaptive state transitions has been proposed as a strategy to prevent the emergence of resistant tumor populations [156, 157]. In this framework, apoptosis suppression reflects modulation of execution thresholds rather than irreversible loss of death competence.

Ferroptosis escape as a central axis of therapy resistance

Evidence from diverse treatment settings increasingly implicates ferroptosis escape as a major contributor to therapy resistance [181]. Many anticancer therapies indirectly induce ferroptotic stress by promoting oxidative damage and lipid peroxidation [182]. Emerging evidence further indicates that immune-derived signals within the tumor microenvironment, particularly CD8+ T-cell–derived IFN-γ, actively modulate ferroptosis sensitivity through regulation of lipid metabolism and antioxidant defenses [183]. Radiotherapy produces reactive oxygen species that oxidize polyunsaturated membrane lipids [184]. In parallel, interferon-γ (IFN-γ) released by cytotoxic T cells suppresses solute carrier family 7-member 11 (SLC7A11) expression, thereby sensitizing tumor cells to ferroptosis during immunotherapy [185]. Together, these observations indicate that ferroptotic signaling is frequently engaged during treatment but may remain contained below execution thresholds. Recent work further positions ferroptosis as an immunomodulatory axis linking therapy-induced oxidative stress to antitumor immune activation, reinforcing its relevance to treatment response and resistance [183].

Plasticity provides a mechanistic explanation for why oxidative stress frequently occurs without catastrophic ferroptotic death. Cancer cells dynamically alter phenotype in response to therapeutic and environmental pressure, increasing heterogeneity and resistance potential [186–188]. Variability in metabolic state, lipid composition, and antioxidant capacity creates a spectrum of ferroptotic sensitivities within tumor populations, allowing some cells to tolerate oxidative stress that would otherwise be lethal. Identity transitions further shape this variability. EMT and stemness-associated programs are strongly linked to tumor progression, therapeutic tolerance, and metabolic rewiring [145, 146], influencing lipid handling, mitochondrial function, and redox balance. Cells undergoing EMT frequently exhibit altered membrane composition and enhanced antioxidant buffering, enabling survival in oxidative environments where ferroptosis signaling is present but restrained. These identity changes occur along a continuum rather than as binary switches; intermediate phenotypes can sustain oxidative signaling at sublethal levels while maintaining proliferative competence [147, 148]. In this context, ferroptosis escape reflects a regulated buffering process rather than complete pathway inactivation. It can be interpreted not as loss of pathway competence, but as a dynamic redistribution of oxidative stress across metabolic and antioxidant networks that prevent local damage from progressing to irreversible execution.

A complementary explanation for ferroptosis escape is the emergence of drug-tolerant persister states characterized by slow-cycling phenotypes and large-scale transcriptome reconfiguration under prolonged pharmacological pressure [189, 190]. These states are reinforced by convergent survival programs, including enhanced IGF1R signaling, increased drug-efflux activity, endoplasmic reticulum stress responses, and chromatin remodeling that stabilizes adaptive transcriptional states [191]. Mechanistically, persister-like survival is frequently accompanied by metabolic rewiring that sustains ferroptotic pressure while preventing execution. This metabolic rewiring effectively maintains a state of ferroptotic priming, in which ROS and lipid peroxidation are continuously generated yet functionally buffered, creating a metastable condition poised between survival and collapse.

Drug-resistant cells can increase glutamine uptake (e.g., via SLC1A5) and glutaminolysis mediated by glutaminase (GLS), fueling the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and elevating mitochondrial electron leakage, thereby maintaining ROS generation [192–198]. In resistant breast and lung cancer models, glutaminolysis inhibition lowers ROS and reduces the cytotoxic impact of ferroptosis inducers, supporting a functional link between metabolic adaptation and ferroptotic priming [199, 200]. This ROS is not intrinsically lethal, ferroptosis escape emerges when lipid-peroxide propagation is held below execution thresholds by reinforced antioxidant buffering and membrane-repair capacity.

In parallel, resistant tumors undergo extensive lipid remodeling that directly shapes ferroptosis susceptibility. Upregulation of lipid-activating enzymes such as ACSL4 and lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 3 (LPCAT3) promotes incorporation of polyunsaturated fatty acids into membrane phospholipids, increasing the pool of oxidizable substrates for lipid peroxidation [201–204]. Clinical and experimental observations indicate that ACSL4 expression is enriched in triple-negative and mesenchymal-like tumor states, particularly in therapy-adapted or resistant phenotypes [107, 131]. In these contexts, increased polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA)–phospholipid content correlates with heightened ferroptosis sensitivity and improved response to ferroptosis-inducing therapies [131, 205]. These findings suggest that lipidome remodeling represents a metabolic trade-off acquired during resistance evolution, creating vulnerability while enabling survival under chronic oxidative stress.

Alterations in iron metabolism provide an additional layer of ferroptotic priming. Drug-resistant populations frequently expand the intracellular labile iron pool through increased transferrin receptor–mediated iron uptake and nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4)-dependent ferritin degradation, resulting in elevated Fe2 + availability that catalyzes lipid peroxidation reactions [198, 206–208]. Studies show that resistant cancer cells maintain 40–60% higher levels of labile iron compared with sensitive counterparts. This generates a pro-oxidant intracellular environment that sustains ferroptotic signaling without necessarily triggering cell death [198, 206]. This iron accumulation therefore functions as a persistent amplifier of oxidative stress rather than an immediate execution signal.

The tumor microenvironment further stabilizes these adaptive states through immune–metabolic crosstalk. CD8+ T cell–derived IFN-γ has been shown to enhance ferroptosis susceptibility by suppressing system cystine–glutamate antiporter (system Xc−) activity and promoting lipid remodeling through ACSL4 regulation, linking immune activation to ferroptotic stress signaling [209, 210]. At the same time, regulatory immune populations such as Tregs, macrophages, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) modulate redox balance and antioxidant capacity through cytokine-mediated pathways, shaping ferroptosis sensitivity in a context-dependent manner [209, 211]. These observations indicate that ferroptosis regulation is not purely cell intrinsic but is dynamically influenced by immune-derived signals within the tumor niche. Collectively, these studies support a model in which metabolic rewiring, lipid remodeling, iron accumulation, and immune signaling converge to create a chronic ferroptosis-primed state. Rather than triggering immediate execution, these adaptations sustain sublethal lipid peroxidation and oxidative pressure. This allows tumor cells to tolerate therapy-induced stress while remaining poised for ferroptotic collapse under altered conditions [212–214]. A key question is how such ferroptosis-buffered phenotypes become stabilized over time.

One stabilizing layer is epigenetic locking-in of the persister state. In drug-tolerant cells, H3K9me3-associated heterochromatin formation has been linked to repression of LINE-1 elements and attenuation of interferon-stimulated gene programs, supporting immune evasion and stress tolerance under prolonged therapy [191, 215–218]. Consistent with this, epigenetic dependencies can emerge alongside tolerance, creating vulnerabilities to histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibition and chromatin-targeting strategies that destabilize the persister program and re-expose cells to lethal stress [191, 215–218].

Clinical observations support the importance of these adaptive states. Small fractions of tumor cells can survive treatment as MRD populations that later contribute to recurrence [142, 143]. Within oxidative stress–rich environments, survival likely depends on coordinated reinforcement of lipid repair pathways and redox buffering systems that prevent membrane damage from reaching lethal levels. Thus, ferroptosis escape may represent a common survival route during therapy-induced stress, particularly within heterogeneous tumors containing metabolically distinct subpopulations.

At the molecular level, buffering is achieved through reinforcement of antioxidant and lipid repair systems. Sustained cystine import through SLC7A11 supports glutathione synthesis and maintains intracellular redox balance [219], while GPX4 detoxifies phospholipid hydroperoxides before membrane damage propagates [82]. Additional ferroptosis-suppressing systems further expand buffering capacity. Ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1) regenerates reduced coenzyme Q independently of glutathione [220], and nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (NRF2)-driven transcriptional programs coordinate antioxidant, lipid metabolic, and NADPH-generating pathways [221, 222]. Together, these mechanisms elevate ferroptotic execution thresholds while preserving signaling competence, allowing cells to endure oxidative stress without undergoing catastrophic lipid membrane damage. This suggests that ferroptosis in treated tumors is often not absent but actively restrained, with cells maintaining a chronic, sublethal oxidative state that supports signaling adaptation without permitting catastrophic lipid membrane failure.

Notably, ferroptosis resistance is strongly associated with metastasis and MRD. Disseminated tumor cells experience elevated oxidative stress during circulation and colonization of distant organs, selecting for cell states with enhanced lipid and redox buffering [223]. Experimental models have further shown that ferroptosis-resistant subpopulations are preferentially enriched during metastatic outgrowth and persist following therapy even when primary tumors regress [107]. Ferroptosis escape therefore emerges as a core survival strategy that intersects with apoptotic suppression and metabolic adaptation. Therapeutically, these observations support targeting adaptive transitions rather than focusing solely on ferroptotic execution itself. Combination strategies aimed at disrupting plasticity-regulating pathways have been proposed to reduce resistance emergence and improve treatment outcomes [156, 157]. In this framework, ferroptosis escape should be viewed not as failure of the death pathway, but as a plasticity-mediated elevation of execution thresholds that enables tumor persistence under oxidative stress.

Tumor-intrinsic control of inflammatory death machinery

In addition to apoptosis and ferroptosis, inflammatory death pathways such as pyroptosis and necroptosis are tightly regulated within tumor cells [224–226]. Even when these pathways are activated, execution is frequently restrained, allowing tumor cells to engage inflammatory signaling without undergoing terminal lysis. This indicates that inflammatory death, like other regulated cell death programs, is controlled through modulation of execution thresholds rather than simple presence or absence of pathway components. Several checkpoints keep inflammatory death below the rupture threshold. Caspase-8 can suppress necroptosis by cleaving receptor-interacting serine/threonine-protein kinase 1 (RIPK1) and receptor-interacting serine/threonine-protein kinase 3 (RIPK3), limiting progression to membrane rupture while permitting upstream signaling to persist [227, 228]. Partial gasdermin activation can support cytokine release without full pore-driven collapse. This uncouples inflammatory output from terminal death [229, 230]. These mechanisms allow tumor cells to remain viable while still engaging stress-responsive inflammatory cascades. This uncoupling of inflammatory signaling from terminal rupture allows tumor cells to exploit stress-responsive cytokine networks for adaptation while avoiding the structural consequences of full pyroptotic or necroptotic execution. In parallel, studies have shown that activation of survival pathways such as NF-κB and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) downstream of inflammatory signaling can further buffer execution thresholds, enabling cells to maintain viability while sustaining inflammatory output [231].

Similarly, cellular plasticity provides a broader framework for understanding this restrained execution. Cancer cells adapt to environmental pressure by altering phenotypic identity, increasing heterogeneity and treatment resistance potential [157, 186]. Because inflammatory signaling is highly responsive to environmental cues, even subtle phenotypic shifts can reshape how strongly death machinery is engaged. In this context, inflammatory pathway activation may occur without progressing to full execution due to adaptive buffering of intracellular thresholds. Plasticity-driven transcriptional reprogramming has been shown to reduce dependency on specific signaling axes and redistribute stress responses across compensatory pathways, allowing survival despite sustained pathway activation [186, 187]. Likewise, drug-treated tumors can adopt lineage-like transcriptional shifts that bypass the inhibited dependency state. Transcriptional profiling after treatment has identified reprogramming and lineage-associated transcription factor (TF) programs as key determinants of post-therapy cell-state change [232]. In vivo and in vitro prostate cancer models further support reversibility at the level of state control. SRY-box transcription factor 2 (SOX2)-linked lineage switching can be functionally required for therapy escape, and suppressing SOX2 with shRNA reversed lineage transition and restored sensitivity to antiandrogen treatment [233–235]. These observations suggest that lineage plasticity not only alters drug sensitivity but also reshapes how inflammatory death signals are interpreted, allowing cells to engage stress pathways without committing to lytic collapse.

The same EMT/plasticity circuits that support state change intersect with inflammatory tone. AKT signaling has been reported to enhance EMT by activating NF-κB and increasing SNAI1 expression, illustrating a defined route by which a plasticity-associated pathway can simultaneously shift phenotype while amplifying inflammatory signaling potential without necessitating terminal death [236, 237]. Beyond intrinsic plasticity, the tumor microenvironment plays a decisive role in shaping these transitions. Interactions between stromal signals, immune pressure, and cellular reprogramming drive phenotypic alterations that support tumor progression and therapeutic adaptation [238]. These cues can enhance inflammatory competence while simultaneously reinforcing survival signaling, enabling tumor cells to respond to immune stress without committing to lytic death.

EMT-linked plasticity further influences inflammatory responsiveness. EMT-associated transitions and stem-like traits are closely linked to tumor progression, immune modulation, and resistance phenotypes [145, 146]. These programs alter cytokine sensitivity, survival pathway activation, and cell–immune interactions, creating a state in which inflammatory signaling can be engaged but execution remains constrained. In line with this, WNT–β-catenin signaling an EMT-linked axis has been described to promote treatment resistance in contexts including castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) and basal cell carcinoma, and suppression of WNT signaling (e.g., LGK974 or low-density lipoprotein receptor–related protein 6 (LRP6) blockade) has been proposed to reduce residual disease features and delay recurrence, supporting the idea that state programs can tune survival outcomes even when upstream stress/inflammatory signaling persists [239–242]. As a result, cells may sustain inflammatory stress responses while maintaining membrane integrity and survival.

Beyond cell-intrinsic regulation, the tumor microenvironment plays a decisive role in shaping inflammatory death responses. Hypoxia is a major driver of adaptive resistance and promotes acquisition of stem-like phenotypes, while cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF) and interferon-γ enhance stromal plasticity and modify tumor–stroma interactions [169]. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) further reinforce treatment resistance through direct contact and secretion of soluble mediators such as hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) [170]. In fibroblast-rich regions, increased MAPK signaling and matrix remodeling alter tissue stiffness and accelerate adaptive resistance development, creating conditions in which inflammatory signaling may be engaged without progressing to terminal execution [231]. Because these pathways intersect with inflammatory signaling circuits, they influence execution outcomes. Specifically, they determine whether pyroptotic or necroptotic activation progresses toward execution or remains sublethal. In addition, microenvironment-driven metabolic stress and redox remodeling have been shown to further modulate cell death sensitivity, altering mitochondrial priming and execution competence under inflammatory pressure [169, 231]. Residual disease populations provide additional context for this adaptive regulation. Cells that survive therapy often persist in inflammatory microenvironments where immune pressure remains high [142, 143]. Under these conditions, engagement of inflammatory death pathways may contribute to stress adaptation and signaling without necessarily inducing terminal rupture. This further supports a model in which survival depends on maintaining execution below lethal thresholds.

Overall, tumor-intrinsic control of inflammatory death machinery appears to reflect adaptive threshold regulation rather than pathway inactivation. The extent to which pyroptosis or necroptosis proceeds to full execution depends on intracellular buffering capacity and signaling context. It is therefore determined by execution competence rather than pathway availability alone [180, 181]. Therapeutically, this perspective suggests that targeting plasticity-regulating pathways and adaptive signaling networks may be necessary to overcome resistance and convert sublethal inflammatory signaling into effective tumor-cell elimination [156, 157].

Regulated cell death plasticity in cancer stem cells and dormancy

The preceding sections describe how cancer cells survive acute therapeutic stress by dynamically adjusting the strength and timing of death signaling. Under prolonged treatment, however, survival increasingly relies on entry into stable cellular states in which death execution is persistently constrained rather than transiently delayed. CSCs and dormant tumor cells exemplify this strategy. Although they retain functional death pathways, execution is maintained below commitment thresholds over extended periods [243–245]. This sustained suppression of death enables long-term persistence during therapy and provides a reservoir for relapse and late metastatic outgrowth [86, 246]. These states represent a long-term extension of the same buffering principles observed under acute stress, but stabilized over time through niche support, metabolic restraint, and epigenetic reinforcement.

A growing body of evidence indicates that therapy resistance is tightly linked to cellular plasticity and the emergence of stem-like phenotypes rather than the permanent loss of death machinery. Treatment with chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy often enriches cell populations with features of cancer stem cells, including quiescence, stress tolerance, and enhanced survival signaling [247–249]. These drug-tolerant states often emerge through adaptive responses rather than permanent genetic change. For example, Lu et al. (2017) showed that chemotherapy-induced Ca2 + release promotes expansion of stem-like populations in breast cancer models [250]. Similarly, in EGFR-mutant lung cancer cell-line models, researchers identified a rare drug-tolerant persister fraction that survives otherwise lethal EGFR inhibition through a reversible, chromatin-mediated state, supporting non-genetic buffering of death commitment rather than permanent pathway loss [86]. Stochastic shifts in gene expression can generate transient drug-tolerant subpopulations even before therapy exposure [92, 251]. Population-level studies further show that cancer cells can interconvert between phenotypic states and re-establish equilibrium over time, indicating that tolerant fractions may arise through reversible transitions rather than clonal selection alone [251]. Under treatment pressure, these cells can be selected and stabilized into persistent phenotypes that survive cytotoxic stress. This reinforces the concept that resistance frequently reflects a regulated, durable elevation of execution thresholds through state transitions and niche support rather than a permanent loss of the death machinery.

Multiple experimental systems further support this view. Studies in melanoma and lung cancer demonstrated that rare pre-existing subpopulations can transiently enter drug-tolerant states through stochastic transcriptional fluctuations prior to therapy and later expand under treatment pressure to drive relapse [89, 247]. Lineage-tracing and barcoding approaches provided direct evidence that these persister populations often arise from reversible phenotypic transitions rather than fixed genetic selection [247]. Single-cell analyses additionally showed that drug exposure can select for rare pre-existing transcriptional states while simultaneously inducing adaptive reprogramming into tolerant phenotypes, further supporting a plasticity-driven survival model [86]. Similarly, stress-adaptation programs reinforce this transition by stabilizing pro-survival transcriptional networks and limiting amplification of apoptotic and oxidative signaling cascades, thereby maintaining death signals in a sublethal range [252–256]. In addition to intrinsic plasticity, extrinsic regulatory cues from the tumor microenvironment also play a decisive role in stabilizing stem-like survival states. Signals from endothelial cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts, macrophages, and extracellular vesicles can induce stemness programs that enhance therapy tolerance and suppress cell death sensitivity [257–261]. For example, stromal-derived microvesicles transferring miR-221 have been shown to expand drug-resistant stem-like populations in endocrine-resistant metastatic breast cancer models [262], highlighting how niche interactions actively reinforce survival signaling. Perivascular niche signaling can also function as a dormancy shield. Carlson et al. (2019) used in vivo disseminated tumor cell settings and showed that targeting the perivascular niche sensitized dormant tumor cells to chemotherapy, directly supporting the idea that endothelial-associated microenvironments stabilize long-term, death-buffered persistence and can be therapeutically disrupted [263]. These microenvironmental inputs function by activating developmental signaling pathways, such as WNT and Notch, and growth factor–dependent survival cascades that increase stress tolerance and reduce death sensitivity, thereby raising the execution threshold required for irreversible commitment [258, 259, 261, 262].

At the far end of this plasticity spectrum, dormancy represents an extreme form of this survival strategy. Disseminated tumor cells (DTCs) can persist for years or decades in a quiescent state within distant organs, protected by niche-derived signals and intrinsic survival programs [264, 265]. A direct link between primary-tumor hypoxia and later dormant heterogeneity was provided by Fluegen et al. (2017), who showed that hypoxic microenvironments in the primary tumor preset phenotypic heterogeneity of disseminated tumor cells, consistent with early conditioning of long-lived, death-buffered dormant states [266]. These cells maintain viability while avoiding proliferative expansion, thereby escaping therapies that primarily target dividing populations. Over time, environmental changes or microenvironmental signals can trigger reactivation, leading to metastatic relapse. The overlap between stemness, dormancy, and therapy resistance is increasingly recognized across tumor types. Cells combining stem-like properties with slow proliferation and stress tolerance have been identified in pancreatic cancer, melanoma, glioblastoma, leukemia, and breast cancer [267, 268]. These populations often represent the fraction most capable of surviving therapeutic insult and repopulating tumors after treatment withdrawal.

At the cellular level, this adaptive continuum is also reflected in the emergence of drug-tolerant persister cells. Drug-tolerant persister cells represent a transient but critical intermediate state linking acute stress survival to stable resistance. These cells are largely quiescent, remain viable under drug pressure, and can later re-enter proliferation once therapy is removed [86]. In some contexts, prolonged stress stabilizes this transient tolerance into a durable, resistant phenotype, suggesting that dormancy and stemness may evolve through the progressive strengthening of survival-buffering mechanisms. Experimental and epigenetic studies support this progression, showing that chromatin remodeling and transcriptional reprogramming can convert transiently tolerant cells into more stable drug-resistant states while preserving functional death machinery [252–254, 256].

Rather than representing a fixed identity, stemness in cancer is now understood as a dynamic and reversible state influenced by microenvironmental signals, cytokines, hypoxia, and therapeutic stress [261, 262]. Soluble factors, extracellular vesicles, and interactions with stromal or immune cells can induce stem-like programs that enhance survival capacity and suppress execution of cell death. In this framework, resistance is sustained by a continuous recalibration of survival thresholds rather than a permanent escape from regulated cell death. In parallel, hypoxia and nutrient limitation reinforce these states by shifting cellular signaling toward stress-protective programs that suppress proliferation, stabilize mitochondrial function, and reduce activation of death-amplifying pathways [266, 269]. Together, these findings support a unified model in which CSCs and dormant tumor cells maintain long-term viability by chronically restricting execution signaling below lethal thresholds. This persistent buffering preserves tumor-initiating potential during therapy and establishes a reservoir from which relapse and metastatic progression can later emerge. Thus, long-term tumor persistence appears to be driven less by irreversible loss of death pathways and more by stable biological states that continuously restrain execution while maintaining survival competence. Conceptually, these persister states can be viewed as intermediate evolutionary solutions that bridge acute stress survival and stable resistance by maintaining execution signaling below lethal thresholds while preserving tumor-initiating capacity.

Cscs suppress death by raising execution thresholds

Clinical and experimental evidence consistently shows that CSCs survive therapies that effectively eliminate the bulk tumor population. This survival reflects reduced death sensitivity rather than irreversible loss of death machinery [86, 245]. In practical terms, CSC survival appears to be governed less by pathway disruption and more by a persistent upward shift in execution thresholds that limits stress signal amplification. This threshold buffering is thought to be maintained through adaptive cellular programs that enhance stress tolerance and stabilize survival signaling under treatment pressure. Resistance is often non-mutational and reversible. Along these lines, early models emphasized enhanced drug efflux and stress tolerance as intrinsic CSC properties [270], while subsequent work highlighted epigenetic regulation as a mechanism enabling adaptive resistance without permanent genetic change [271]. Clinical observations further support this concept. In non–small cell lung cancer, some patients who relapse after an initial response to EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors regain sensitivity following a drug-free interval, a phenomenon often described as re-treatment response [272, 273]. Similar patterns have been reported with platinum-based chemotherapies, taxanes, and hormonal therapies, suggesting that resistance can reflect reversible state transitions rather than fixed pathway disruption [274]. Direct experimental evidence reinforces this view. In acute myeloid leukemia, quiescent stem-like cells were shown to depend on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation programs that limit death commitment; pharmacologic disruption of this metabolic state selectively eliminated leukemia stem cells while sparing differentiated populations [275]. In solid tumors, including breast cancer, CSC-enriched fractions display reduced apoptotic priming and survive cytotoxic stress that eliminates non-stem cells, enabling efficient tumor regrowth following therapy [276]. These observations position CSCs as long-lived reservoirs capable of sustaining disease recurrence [277].

Consistent with this model, therapy-induced enrichment of stem-like populations has been reported across multiple tumor types, where cytotoxic treatments select for cells with enhanced plasticity, self-renewal capacity, and survival signaling [248, 278]. Rather than being intrinsically resistant, these cells often arise through stress-induced reprogramming that stabilizes survival pathways and raises the threshold required for death execution. Importantly, state transitions between stem-like and non-stem-like compartments occur frequently in tumors, generating heterogeneous populations capable of adapting to therapy pressure [279–282]. Epigenetic plasticity is a central mechanism enabling this adaptive resistance. Chromatin remodeling, histone modification, and transcriptional reprogramming can generate drug-tolerant persister states that survive therapy without permanent genetic alterations [252, 254–256, 283]. These transitions allow cancer cells to enter protective survival modes that can later revert when therapy pressure is removed, further reinforcing the dynamic nature of death suppression. Slow-cycling stem-like subpopulations have been identified in multiple cancers, including melanoma, breast cancer, leukemia, and colorectal tumors, where they exhibit resistance to apoptosis and enhanced tumor-initiating potential [283–285]. These cells often occupy protective niches within tumors or distant organs, where microenvironmental signals reinforce survival programs and further limit execution of death pathways.

Avoidance of ferroptotic execution further stabilizes stem-like and drug-tolerant states. Therapy-resistant persister cells require GPX4 activity to suppress lipid peroxidation, and pharmacological inhibition of GPX4 selectively collapses the persister population [107]. Related studies demonstrate that ferroptosis resistance is a shared feature of drug-tolerant cancer cells, reflecting active buffering of death execution rather than passive survival [103, 286]. In summary, CSCs survive not by eliminating death pathways, but by continuously adjusting the threshold required to trigger execution. Through epigenetic flexibility, metabolic rewiring, and niche-mediated protection, stem-like cells maintain death signaling at sublethal levels that support persistence and long-term tumor regeneration. This epigenetic flexibility also allows CSC populations to dynamically recalibrate death sensitivity in response to environmental and therapeutic pressure without permanently committing to a resistant genotype.

Metabolic quietness supports long-term survival

Low proliferative activity is a defining feature of CSCs and dormant tumor cells and is closely linked to metabolic restraint. This state reduces oxidative stress and limits amplification of death signals [287]. In hematopoietic stem cells, Ito et al. showed that elevated ROS activates p38 MAPK and drives stem cell exhaustion, whereas antioxidant control preserves long-term stem cell function [287]. Similar principles apply in cancer. Slow-cycling residual tumor cells maintain strong antioxidant and redox-buffering programs that stabilize mitochondrial function and protect membranes from oxidative injury [288, 289]. By limiting oxidative amplification and membrane damage, these cells prevent death signaling from reaching irreversible execution thresholds, thereby promoting persistence and relapse [68, 219].

A key idea is that metabolic quietness is protective but not rigid. Drug pressure can push cells into a low-growth mode that is better at stress control, yet the same cells may later regain proliferation when conditions improve. This helps explain why resistant tumors do not show one single metabolic pattern. In many settings, chemotherapy kills highly proliferative cells that depend on aerobic glycolysis, thereby selecting for slower-growing survivors that shift toward oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) [290]. However, resistant cells can also exhibit very different states over time and across contexts. Multiple studies report chemotherapy-resistant cells that become OXPHOS-dependent [288, 291–293], while others report resistant cells with high ATP levels [294] and increased glycolytic markers [295–298]. A practical explanation is timing; during therapy, survivors may slow proliferation and rely more on OXPHOS for efficient energy and stress control. But after therapy ends, some populations may re-enter a glycolysis-linked growth program. More broadly, the strongest explanation is metabolic plasticity, the ability to switch between OXPHOS and glycolysis to survive blockade of either pathway [299].

This plasticity matters because it raises the execution threshold in several ways. First, switching energy pathways can stabilize ATP supply under stress, which prevents energy-crisis signals that amplify apoptosis and other death programs. Second, rerouting metabolism can increase NADPH and antioxidant capacity, which blunts ROS escalation and limits lipid damage that would otherwise push cells into irreversible execution [300, 301]. Supporting this concept experimentally, one study showed that cancer cells can switch between OXPHOS and glycolysis to circumvent the inhibition of either process [299]. In the same work, targeting metabolic flexibility using intermittent fasting (to reduce glucose availability) plus the OXPHOS inhibitor metformin restrained tumor growth through PP2A and GSK3β activation and effects on the pro-apoptotic protein Mcl-1 [299]. These findings support the idea that the goal is not only to block a pathway but also to prevent cells from entering an alternative metabolic state.

Within this framework, dormancy should not be viewed as inactivity but as an actively maintained survival state [302]. Dormant cancer cells actively maintain survival programs that preserve cellular integrity over extended periods while retaining the capacity to rapidly re-enter proliferation when environmental constraints are relieved [303, 304]. In breast cancer, disseminated tumor cells residing in the perivascular niche are maintained in a non-proliferative, survival-focused state that later supports metastatic outgrowth [305]. Similarly, latent metastatic cells were found to persist through self-sustained survival signaling and immune evasion, and to form metastases when these limiting conditions are no longer present [303]. These observations fit a threshold model: dormancy protects cells by keeping stress manageable and preventing it from escalating into terminal death, but it also preserves the ability to restart growth when the environment becomes permissive.

This metabolic flexibility further strengthens survival capacity. Therapy-resistant cells can dynamically switch between glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation in response to nutrient availability and treatment pressure, thereby maintaining ATP production and redox balance under hostile conditions [291–293, 306]. This flexibility enables cells to avoid metabolic collapse and prevents activation of energy-stress-induced death pathways, especially when combined with autophagy inhibitors that remove a key survival support pathway. In other words, harsh niches can both protect dormant cells and create specific dependencies that can be exploited therapeutically.

Autophagy plays a central role in sustaining this metabolically restrained state. Dormant and drug-tolerant cells rely on autophagic recycling to maintain energy homeostasis and remove damaged organelles, supporting long-term survival in nutrient-limited or hypoxic environments [307–310]. In disseminated breast cancer cells, inhibition of autophagy has been shown to selectively eliminate dormant populations while sparing proliferating cells, supporting a direct survival requirement in dormancy [311, 312]. This is consistent with the broader idea that dormancy is not passive; it is an active maintenance program that depends on ongoing metabolic quality control.

Hormone therapy in estrogen receptor–positive (ER-positive) breast cancer provides a concrete example of therapy-induced metabolic editing linked to dormancy and stem-like survival. Hormonal therapy has been reported to promote the emergence of dormant CD133high / ERlow cells associated with metastatic progression and to induce an OXPHOS shift through IL6/ NOTCH3 signaling [313]. This supports a model in which therapy does not simply select resistant cells but can push cells into a survival state with distinct metabolism and signaling. Consistent with microenvironment involvement, hormone therapy resistance and generation of breast CSC features have also been linked to microvesicle-mediated horizontal transfer of microRNAs from stromal cells [313]. Beyond breast cancer, extracellular microRNAs can shape metabolic crosstalk more broadly; for example, tumor-secreted miR-105 can instruct cancer-associated fibroblasts to adopt different metabolic features to support tumor adaptation to changing metabolic conditions [314]. These studies reinforce the idea that metabolic quietness and plasticity are not purely cell-intrinsic; they can be influenced by the niche.

Metabolic reprogramming is also observed during anti-angiogenic therapy, where tumors adapt to reduced nutrient delivery. In glioblastoma, overexpression of the glucose transporter 3 (GLUT3) recapitulated metabolic features of bevacizumab-resistant cells, pointing to GLUT3 as a potential metabolic target in resistance [315]. Together, these reports support a broader conclusion: metabolic heterogeneity in drug resistance can be driven by intrinsic mechanisms (genetic or epigenetic) and by environmental adaptation, and it plays a key role in therapy escape [114].

Hypoxia and microenvironmental stress also reinforce metabolic quietness. Cells exposed to low-oxygen conditions activate dormancy programs, reduce proliferation, and enhance resistance to therapy, in part by inducing survival genes and suppressing metabolic activity [266, 269]. In parallel, hypoxic tumor regions can also promote enrichment of stem-like cells through calcium signaling and stress-response activation [250], further linking metabolic adaptation with survival dominance. These adaptations create a stable equilibrium in which death signals remain sublethal but persistently present. It confirms that metabolic restraint functions as a protective buffer that prevents execution signaling from reaching lethal thresholds. By limiting oxidative damage, preserving mitochondrial integrity, and maintaining energy homeostasis, dormant and stem-like tumor cells sustain long-term viability while remaining poised for reactivation. This balance between survival maintenance and death suppression forms a critical foundation for therapy resistance and disease recurrence.

In summary, metabolic quietness supports long-term survival by lowering oxidative stress, stabilizing mitochondrial function, and preventing stress signals from crossing execution thresholds [288, 289, 300]. However, long-lived persistence often requires more than quietness alone. It depends on metabolic plasticity (switching between glycolysis and OXPHOS) [299], niche-driven metabolic editing (including IL6/Notch3 and stromal microRNAs) [262, 313, 316], and sustained cellular maintenance systems such as autophagy [300, 301]. These adaptations create a stable equilibrium in which death signaling can be engaged without becoming lethal or irreversible. Together, these findings confirm that metabolic restraint functions as a protective buffer that prevents execution signaling from reaching lethal thresholds, allowing dormant and stem-like tumor cells to survive therapy while remaining poised for reactivation and relapse [288, 291–293]. These adaptations create a stable equilibrium in which death signaling can be engaged without becoming lethal or irreversible. Together, these findings indicate that metabolic restraint functions as a protective buffer that limits execution, allowing tumor cells to survive therapy while remaining poised for reactivation. Clinically, this manifests as incomplete tumor elimination and eventual disease recurrence.

Consequences of death plasticity for therapeutic failure

This pattern of incomplete elimination becomes especially clear in clinical outcomes. Across cancer types and treatment classes, therapies often induce strong stress responses and engage canonical death pathways [12, 317, 318]. Yet durable eradication remains uncommon. More often, tumors regress without disappearing, a residual fraction persists, and relapse follows with reduced treatment sensitivity [16, 91, 316].

In our view, this pattern is difficult to explain by a simple absence of death machinery. Instead, it fits a model in which death signaling is initiated broadly, but execution remains incomplete in a subset of cells. Some cells experience substantial stress yet do not cross an irreversible commitment point. They persist in a damaged but viable state and later reseed progression [16, 86]. This framework also connects several recurring clinical problems. Patients respond at first, but the disease comes back. Biomarkers that show pathway activation do not always predict long-term success. And different drug classes fail in different ways, even when they are designed to trigger cell death. A shared explanation could be that activating death pathways alone is not sufficient. What matters is how deeply the therapy pushes tumor cells toward irreversible execution. The real goal, therefore, may not be just to switch on death signaling, but to ensure enough cells are driven past the point at which recovery is no longer possible.

Clinical evidence of incomplete execution

If execution is frequently buffered, a recognizable clinical pattern should emerge. Many treatments produce rapid cytoreduction in the dominant population, while a smaller fraction survives and later expands. Hematologic malignancies provide the clearest readout because MRD can be measured far below conventional detection thresholds and reliably predicts relapse. In acute lymphoblastic leukemia, MRD positivity after therapy remains among the strongest predictors of recurrence, indicating that a small population retains long term survival competence despite major tumor reduction [319, 320].

These observations are consistent with a model in which early death signaling is broadly induced but does not uniformly reach irreversible commitment in all malignant cells. MRD therefore reflects incomplete elimination rather than simply insufficient pathway engagement [91]. This interpretation aligns with classical therapeutic kinetics models, where treatment reduces tumor burden but does not fully eradicate all clonogenic cells, leaving a residual population capable of regrowth [321].

Chronic myeloid leukemia provides a related example. Tyrosine kinase inhibitors can induce deep molecular responses through sustained suppression of BCR ABL signaling, yet relapse remains common after treatment discontinuation. In the STIM trial, a substantial proportion of patients experienced recurrence after stopping imatinib despite prolonged deep response, indicating persistence of disease-competent cells under long-term inhibition [322]. Similar findings from the EURO-SKI study further support the survival of residual populations despite extended target suppression [323].

In solid tumors, the same pattern is observed through imaging and time to progression rather than MRD assays. EGFR mutant non-small cell lung cancer often shows rapid regression on EGFR inhibitors, reflecting broad sensitivity in the dominant population. In the FLAURA trial, osimertinib improved progression-free and overall survival compared with earlier-generation inhibitors, yet most patients ultimately progressed, consistent with the persistence of a smaller surviving fraction [324, 325].

Clinical flare phenomena following treatment interruption provide additional evidence that therapy can actively suppress viable tumor cells. In EGFR mutant lung cancer with acquired resistance, Chaft and colleagues described rapid and clinically significant disease acceleration during mandated washout of erlotinib or gefitinib, with flare events occurring within days in a subset of patients [326]. A related prospective study by Riely and colleagues showed increases in symptoms, tumor size, and fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG PET) uptake after stopping EGFR inhibitors, with improvement after reinitiation. These findings support the idea that residual tumor populations can remain viable but suppressed under continuous inhibition and can rebound quickly when pressure is removed [327]. Such behavior is difficult to explain if therapy is no longer biologically relevant. It better aligns with the persistence of stressed but viable cells maintained below execution thresholds.

Heterogeneous responses across lesions within the same patient further support the idea that execution thresholds vary across tumor subpopulations. Mixed intra-patient responses have been documented in metastatic disease and are increasingly recognized as a clinical manifestation of spatial and state heterogeneity. Recent work in metastatic lung adenocarcinoma reported that mixed responses to EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibition occur more frequently in specific genomic contexts, linking lesion-level divergence to underlying biology rather than measurement variability [328]. More broadly, multiregion sequencing studies in solid tumors have shown that spatially separated regions can harbor distinct subclonal architectures and evolutionary paths, providing a mechanistic basis for discordant lesion responses and uneven vulnerability to lethal signaling [4].

Finally, standard response criteria such as response evaluation criteria in solid tumors (RECIST) measure changes in tumor size but do not assess elimination of regrowth-competent cells. Deep radiographic regressions can therefore coexist with persistence of rare survivors [329]. Experimental models support this interpretation. Drug-tolerant persister cells can survive initial treatment through reversible adaptations rather than immediate genetic resistance, and may later give rise to resistant populations [330]. Single-cell studies further show that rare pre-existing transcriptional states can predict survival under therapy [92].

Collectively, the clinic shows a consistent pattern. Many therapies cause strong early shrinkage, but a small fraction of cells survive. These cells can stay alive under drug pressure, rebound quickly when treatment stops, and slowly adapt until relapse and resistance emerge. This pattern fits better with incomplete execution and state-dependent survival than with simple failure to activate death pathways.

Why pathway-activation biomarkers fail: activation ≠ execution

A major gap between laboratory readouts and patient benefit comes from what we choose to measure. In many trials, a treatment is judged biologically active when it shows pathway engagement. Typical readouts include receptor binding, early caspase cleavage, short-term mitochondrial stress, or induction of stress response genes [14, 81, 316, 331, 332]. These signals confirm that the drug reached the tumor and triggered a response. However, clinical outcomes repeatedly show that early activation markers do not reliably predict long-term control, because activation does not guarantee that tumor cells have lost the ability to survive and regrow [91, 268, 333]. A key explanation is that a cell can start a death program but still recover if the signal does not reach an irreversible commitment point. When this occurs in only a small fraction of cells, bulk assays still show strong activation, yet the surviving fraction can later expand and produce relapse.

This limitation is evident across multiple therapeutic settings. In the death receptor field, TRAIL receptor agonists were designed to directly trigger apoptosis through activation of DR4 or DR5. Although several agents demonstrated target engagement and were generally well tolerated, clinical benefit remained modest and rarely durable. For example, recombinant TRAIL (dulanermin) showed limited improvement in outcomes in advanced non-small cell lung cancer despite clear pathway activation [334]. Similarly, mapatumumab and conatumumab demonstrated pharmacologic activity but failed to produce consistent durable responses in clinical trials [335, 336]. Receptor-level activation alone is therefore insufficient to ensure complete downstream execution.

A similar pattern is observed with targeted therapies. In melanoma, BRAF and MEK inhibitors produce rapid tumor regression and clear suppression of MAPK signaling. Combination therapy improves outcomes compared with single-agent treatment; however, resistance still develops in many patients over time [337, 338]. These data show that even strong pathway suppression does not necessarily translate into irreversible elimination of all tumor cells.

In hematologic malignancies, this limitation is highlighted by MRD measurements. Even when early response markers indicate a strong therapeutic effect, sensitive assays can detect small populations of surviving cells that predict relapse [320]. A minority fraction therefore retains long-term survival capacity despite broad pathway activation in the bulk population.

Because of these limitations, there is growing interest in biomarkers that sit closer to functional commitment rather than upstream activation. A useful example is dynamic BH3 profiling, which measures early shifts in mitochondrial readiness to undergo apoptosis after drug exposure and has been shown to predict response in cell line systems and in clinical sample-based testing across agents and tumor types [339]. Importantly, biomarkers that reflect proximity to commitment are more informative than those measuring early pathway activation.

Similar limitations are observed in circulating tumor DNA studies. Early reductions in circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) levels often correlate with initial response and treatment engagement. However, long-term follow-up shows that ctDNA clearance does not always indicate complete tumor eradication. In EGFR-mutant lung cancer, early ctDNA decline predicts improved progression-free survival, yet resistance still emerges in many patients [340, 341]. Comparable findings in colorectal cancer show that recurrence can occur despite initial molecular responses [342]. These observations suggest that ctDNA dynamics capture bulk tumor reduction but may miss rare surviving cells with regrowth potential.

In short, clinical trials across death receptor agonists, targeted pathway inhibitors, and residual disease monitoring support a consistent conclusion. Many biomarkers capture that a pathway has started, but they do not establish that execution has finished. A strong pharmacodynamic signal, therefore, means that the therapy is engaging the tumor, but it does not guarantee durable benefit. The long-term outcome depends on whether the surviving fraction has been eliminated or remains in a stressed but recoverable state that can later reseed progression.

Why drug classes fail differently

Different types of cancer drugs often fail in different and predictable patterns. This makes sense if tumor cells can delay or buffer the final steps of cell death. Many treatments can start the death process, but each one meets a different barrier that prevents the complete and irreversible elimination of all tumor cells. Sometimes the barrier is inside the cancer cell itself. Sometimes it comes from the surrounding tissue environment. In other cases, treatment intensity cannot be increased further because of side effects. These class-specific limits help explain why many therapies show clear biological activity yet still do not produce lasting cures.

Death receptor agonists are one of the clearest examples. These drugs were designed to directly trigger apoptosis by activating surface receptors such as TRAIL receptors or Fas. In laboratory models, receptor activation can lead to the formation of signaling complexes and the activation of caspases, which should push cells toward death. Early clinical studies confirmed that these agents were biologically active and generally safe. However, trials with recombinant TRAIL agents, such as dulanermin, and agonistic antibodies, including mapatumumab and conatumumab, have produced limited durable responses in most solid tumors [334–336]. Receptor engagement alone was therefore not sufficient as many tumor cells likely received the initial signal but did not move forward to full mitochondrial commitment. Experimental studies have shown that resistance in this setting can be linked to reduced receptor signaling, elevated levels of anti-apoptotic regulators, or limited intracellular signal amplification [140, 343–345]. In simple terms, the process started but did not spread strongly enough to guarantee collapse.

Drugs that act at the mitochondrial level show a different pattern. BH3 mimetics such as venetoclax directly target anti-apoptotic proteins and can produce deep and rapid responses in diseases where tumor cells depend heavily on BCL-2 [120, 180]. This is especially clear in some leukemias, where venetoclax can induce strong cell death and high response rates. However, relapse still occurs [346]. Importantly, relapse often happens even though the basic death machinery remains intact. Instead of losing the pathway, tumor cells shift their dependence from BCL-2 to other protective proteins such as MCL-1 or BCL-XL. Studies in leukemia and lymphoma have repeatedly shown that this change in survival dependence is a common mechanism of resistance [346, 347]. This suggests that execution is not permanently blocked but rather held below a critical threshold by alternative protective systems.

Second mitochondria-derived activator of caspases (SMAC) mimetics and inhibitor of apoptosis proteins (IAPs) antagonists highlight another type of limitation. These agents remove internal brakes on death signaling and can increase sensitivity to inflammatory death pathways linked to TNF signaling [348, 349]. In preclinical systems, they can clearly enhance cell death [350]. However, their effects depend strongly on the surrounding signaling environment [351, 352]. TNF activity can either support cell killing or promote survival and inflammation, depending on the cellular context [353]. In addition, excessive stimulation of inflammatory signaling can generate TNF-α–dependent autocrine feedback that amplifies caspase activation and drives extrinsic apoptosis, highlighting the central role of cytokine context in determining therapeutic response [140, 354]. Early clinical studies showed that these drugs could activate the intended pathways, but also revealed safety limits that restricted how far treatment could be intensified [351, 355]. This creates a practical ceiling, where further increasing the signal might improve tumor killing, but cannot be done safely.

Strategies targeting ferroptosis face a different challenge. Ferroptosis is driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, and many cancer cells rely on antioxidant systems, such as GPX4 and cystine transport pathways, to prevent this damage [82, 103, 205]. Because these protective systems are also important in normal tissues, the margin between effective tumor killing and normal tissue injury can be narrow. In addition, tumors are highly diverse. Different regions of the same tumor can vary in lipid composition, iron levels, and antioxidant capacity [103, 107, 356]. This means that ferroptosis may be strongly triggered in some areas but less so in others. Even when the mechanism is engaged, this variability can lead to incomplete killing across tumor sites. [70, 219].

Immune-based therapies provide an important contrast because they introduce an external source of cell killing. Treatments such as immune checkpoint inhibitors and chimeric antigen receptor T cells (CAR-T) cells can produce deep and sometimes long-lasting responses by allowing immune cells to directly recognize and eliminate cancer cells [357–360]. In some patients, this can overcome internal survival mechanisms within tumor cells. However, even here, there are clear limits. Strong immune activation can cause serious side effects, including cytokine release syndrome, neurotoxicity, and damage to normal organs [361]. This means treatment intensity cannot be increased indefinitely. In addition, not all tumors are equally visible to the immune system, which limits how consistently immune killing can reach every disease site [362, 363].

Taken together, these examples show that each drug class faces its own execution barrier. Death receptor therapies may fail because the signal does not spread effectively within resistant cells. BH3 mimetics may fail because tumor cells switch to alternative survival proteins. SMAC mimetics may be limited by inflammatory toxicity and variable signaling context. Ferroptosis strategies may be constrained by tissue sensitivity and metabolic differences. Immune therapies may be limited by toxicity and by the extent to which immune cells can reach the tumor.

Across all these settings, the early steps of death signaling can still occur. The key difference lies in how far the process can be pushed before it is stopped. This helps explain why many treatments show strong initial responses yet still lead to persistence and relapse.

Dose and scheduling constraints

A common assumption is that incomplete tumor killing can be overcome by increasing dose, duration, or treatment intensity. In real clinical settings, this approach is limited by a simple reality. Patients often reach toxicity limits before all tumor cells are eliminated [159, 249, 364]. This is not just a technical issue of drug delivery. It directly shapes how much pressure can be applied, how long it can be sustained, and how consistent treatment can be given. When tumor cells can delay or buffer the final steps of death, these limits become particularly important. Pushing cells from severe stress into irreversible collapse often requires continuous pressure, yet toxicity makes that difficult to maintain.

This effect is well known in chemotherapy. Dose intensity has been studied for many years [365–367]. Many patients cannot receive full planned treatment because of neutropenia, infections, mucositis, fatigue, or organ toxicity [368, 369]. When treatment is delayed or doses are reduced, the relative dose intensity drops [365, 366]. Several clinical analyses have shown that lower relative dose intensity is associated with worse outcomes in breast cancer and other solid tumors, with thresholds near 85% often linked to better survival in multiple settings [367, 370]. Breaks and dose reductions are therefore not neutral events. When pressure decreases, the surviving tumor fraction has time to recover, and the next cycle is applied to a population already enriched for cells that tolerated the previous treatment.

A similar constraint exists in targeted therapy, although the side effects are different [249]. EGFR inhibitors, for example, can strongly suppress signaling, but chronic toxicities such as rash, diarrhea, and lung inflammation often lead to dose interruptions or reductions [371–373]. Real-world studies have shown that treatment modification during EGFR inhibitor therapy is common, and outcomes may vary depending on how consistently the drug is maintained [374, 375]. Biologically, targeted therapy does not act as a simple on–off switch. If pressure is reduced for safety, some cells can remain in a stressed but viable state instead of being pushed into irreversible death [86, 249].

There is also direct clinical evidence that tumor cells can exploit treatment pauses. In EGFR-mutant lung cancer, rapid disease flare has been reported after stopping gefitinib or erlotinib in patients with acquired resistance. In one study, a subset of patients experienced fast symptomatic progression within days of discontinuation [326]. This suggests that therapy was actively preventing the growth of a population of viable tumor cells. When pressure was removed, these cells expanded quickly.

Timing matters for another reason. Tumor cell plasticity is not fixed. Experimental studies show that small subpopulations can survive strong drug exposure by entering reversible tolerant states rather than acquiring permanent resistance right away. Sharma and colleagues showed that cancer cells can enter a temporary drug-tolerant condition supported by changes in chromatin and survival signaling [86]. Later work by Hata and colleagues demonstrated that resistant populations in EGFR-driven cancers can emerge from these early tolerant cells [330]. These studies help explain why treatment breaks matter. When therapy is paused, tolerant cells can recover, regain growth capacity, and eventually give rise to more stable resistant populations. This also explains a common clinical observation. A drug may work very well at first, then appear less effective over time, even without a clear new mutation. Over repeated cycles, treatment removes the most sensitive cells, leaving behind those that can survive under stress. Over time, the tumor population becomes enriched for cells that sit below the threshold for execution. Clinically, this can manifest as shorter response durations across later lines of therapy, even when early biological activity remains visible.

Immunotherapy highlights these scheduling limits in another way. Immune checkpoint inhibitors can produce durable control in some patients, but immune-related side effects are common and can require treatment interruption and steroid therapy [376–378]. Conditions such as colitis, hepatitis, and pneumonitis often lead to pauses in treatment [376, 378]. Several studies have reported that high-dose steroid use for severe immune-related adverse events may affect treatment outcomes in some settings, likely because immune pressure is temporarily reduced [379–381]. The priority is always patient safety, but these pauses change the timing and strength of anti-tumor activity.

Taken together, these observations support a practical conclusion. Increasing the dose alone cannot fully solve incomplete tumor killing because the main limitation is often the patient’s tolerance rather than the tumor itself. In real clinical practice, therapy is delivered with built-in gaps, including dose reductions, treatment delays, and interruptions. These windows allow tumor cells to survive, recover, and adapt between tumor cycles. For this reason, treatment timing is therefore biologically meaningful rather than purely logistical. The schedule itself shapes which cell populations persist, how survival systems rebuild between cycles, and how quickly the remaining fraction can grow once pressure is reduced.

The measurement problem

A major but often overlooked issue in cancer therapy is how treatment success is measured. Most clinical tools capture the average tumor response rather than the survival of individual cells [382]. Imaging measures tumor size using standardized response criteria such as RECIST [382], and blood markers are commonly used as surrogates of overall tumor burden [383]. Molecular assays can track pathway activity or target suppression, yet these readouts typically reflect population averages and do not guarantee that all tumor cells have crossed an irreversible commitment point [76, 77, 79]. These approaches are highly valuable, but they largely reflect population averages rather than functional eradication [86, 92]. As a result, treatments can appear highly effective in the short term even when a small but dangerous population remains [86]. Tumor shrinkage on imaging reflects reduction in bulk volume but does not directly measure whether residual cells have permanently lost the ability to regrow [329, 382]. Patients can therefore show strong radiologic responses yet still relapse months or years later [329, 382], indicating that reduction in size and complete elimination are not the same.

This limitation becomes clearer when considering cellular heterogeneity. Even when most tumor cells respond to therapy, a small fraction can survive in drug-tolerant states. Studies in EGFR-mutant lung cancer and melanoma have shown that rare subpopulations can persist under treatment and later expand, despite strong initial responses [86, 92]. These findings reshape how treatment success should be interpreted. If 95–99% of tumor cells are eliminated, bulk measurements will show major regression and strong biological activity. But if the remaining 1–5% retains the ability to regrow, the disease can return. From the patient’s point of view, that small fraction is what ultimately matters. From a measurement perspective, it is often invisible. These findings demonstrate that average measurements can mask biologically important differences between subpopulations.

A similar principle is reflected in MRD testing in hematologic malignancies. Patients can achieve deep remissions in which disease is no longer detectable by routine methods, yet sensitive assays reveal small numbers of remaining cells that strongly predict relapse [319, 320]. This provides a clear clinical example where population-level response appears favorable, but long-term outcome is determined by a small surviving fraction.

Laboratory experiments have long used clonogenic survival assays to study this problem. Instead of measuring immediate cell death, these assays test whether any cells can still form colonies over time, providing a functional readout of long-term survival [384, 385]. Often, short-term measurements suggest strong killing, while longer-term assays show that a small number of cells can recover and grow, a phenomenon commonly referred to as fractional survival [79, 385]. These surviving cells are often the ones that matter most for resistance and relapse [86]. In contrast, clinical practice rarely directly measures long-term functional survival, creating a gap between early response and eventual outcome.

Bulk RNA and protein measurements have similar limits. When gene expression is measured in a tumor sample, the result reflects an average across many cells, which can mask important biological differences between subpopulations [386, 387]. If most cells show strong activation of death pathways, the signal looks convincing. But if a small subset shows strong survival signaling, that signal may be diluted and hidden in the average [76]. Single-cell sequencing studies have repeatedly shown that tumors are not uniform. They contain a mix of cell states, including rare populations that are more stress-tolerant and more likely to survive treatment [92, 386]. This creates a consistent measurement bias. Treatments can appear highly effective when judged by average tumor shrinkage or average pathway activation. But what determines long-term outcome is whether the surviving fraction has truly crossed an irreversible commitment point. If those cells remain viable, even in very small numbers, they can eventually rebuild the disease [86, 92].

For this reason, there is growing interest in methods that measure functional elimination more directly. Long-term follow-up, repeated sampling, MRD testing, and single-cell approaches can help detect the rare populations that population-level readout often miss. These tools move closer to answering the most important question: not whether death signaling has started, but whether it has been completed in every cell that matters. In simple terms, clinical medicine captures average response well, but long-term outcomes are often determined by rare surviving cells. If death plasticity allows a small fraction of cells to remain below the point of irreversible collapse, then measuring only the average response will always give an incomplete picture.

Design rules for next-generation therapies

If treatment failure is driven not by the absence of death signaling but by incomplete execution, then future therapies must be designed differently. For many years, the main strategy has been to activate death pathways more strongly using higher doses, stronger inhibitors, or broader combinations, based on the idea that deeper pathway engagement would produce better tumor control [143, 164]. Clinical experience now shows that tumors often adapt to this pressure. They can shift survival programs, change cellular state, and remain just below the point where death becomes irreversible, reflecting adaptive resistance and phenotypic plasticity rather than simple pathway failure [86, 92, 249]. The ideas below are therefore not a single treatment model but a set of practical design principles that follow from what is now understood about plasticity, persistence, and resistance.

Rule 1: target execution and buffering at the same time

A consistent lesson from many studies is that activating a death pathway alone is often not enough. Tumor cells can survive by strengthening internal protection systems that keep execution below a lethal level. Effective therapy may therefore need to act on two levels at once. It should push the death pathway forward while also removing the mechanisms that prevent full commitment. Mitochondrial apoptosis targeting provides a clear example. Venetoclax, which blocks BCL-2, can produce deep responses in leukemias that depend heavily on this protein. However, resistance frequently develops through increased reliance on other survival proteins such as MCL-1 or BCL-XL. Experimental work has shown that blocking MCL-1 can restore sensitivity in venetoclax-resistant models, suggesting that the apoptotic machinery remains intact but buffered [346, 347]. These findings suggest a practical direction. Durable apoptosis-based therapy may require blocking both the execution trigger and the protective system at the same time. Rather than increasing pressure, treatment must also close the escape routes that prevent commitment.

Rule 2: Prevent adaptive rewiring before it stabilizes

Tumor plasticity allows cancer cells not only to survive but to adapt. Under drug pressure, cells can shift identity, change signaling patterns, and enter states that are less sensitive to treatment. This process has been directly observed in multiple systems. Studies have shown that small groups of cancer cells can survive drug exposure by entering reversible tolerant states. These cells are not initially permanently resistant. Instead, they exist in a temporary condition that allows survival under stress. Over time, this state can stabilize and lead to genetic resistance. Sharma and colleagues first described this reversible drug-tolerant population, and later work showed that resistant clones in EGFR-driven cancers can evolve from these early survivors [86, 330]. Similar observations in melanoma have shown that rare pre-existing cell states can predict which cells will survive targeted therapy and later expand [92]. These findings suggest that if therapy allows time for adaptation, tumors will take advantage of it.

This leads to an important principle. Treatment strategies should not only aim to kill cells. They should also reduce the tumor’s ability to reconfigure itself. This can mean limiting compensatory survival signaling, preventing induction of alternative anti-apoptotic proteins, or targeting stress-adaptation pathways that help stabilize tolerant states. The goal is not only to apply pressure, but to remove the routes that allow the tumor to adjust to that pressure.

Rule 3: Control cell state to increase vulnerability

Sensitivity to death is not fixed. It depends strongly on cell state. Cells that are actively dividing or already close to the apoptotic threshold are often easier to kill. In contrast, slow-cycling or stress-adapted cells are more resistant. Research on mitochondrial priming has shown Unstructthat some tumor cells sit closer to the point of commitment than others. Functional assays developed by Letai and colleagues demonstrated that cells with higher apoptotic priming require less additional stress to undergo death [105]. This suggests that altering the cell state before applying a death-inducing therapy may improve the response.

In practice, this could mean first pushing cells into a more vulnerable condition and then applying the execution trigger. Changes in metabolism, redox balance, or cell cycle activity can increase susceptibility to apoptosis. The key idea is to lower the threshold and keep cells in a vulnerable window long enough for irreversible collapse to occur.

Rule 4: Treat time as part of the therapy design

Treatment is usually delivered in cycles, but timing is often viewed as a logistical issue rather than a biological factor. Evidence now suggests that time strongly influences how tumors adapt. Drug-tolerant persister cells can appear during treatment and expand during drug-free periods. Experimental studies have shown that resistant populations may evolve through these intermediate states rather than appearing suddenly [86]. This means that treatment breaks are not neutral. They can allow surviving cells to recover and rebuild.

This suggests that therapy should be designed as a sequence. A priming phase could increase vulnerability. A second phase could apply strong death pressure. A later phase could prevent recovery of surviving cells. The central idea is that therapy is not a single event. It is a time-dependent process. How pressure is applied over time can influence whether cells commit to death or find a path to survive.

Rule 5: Link intrinsic cell death to immune clearance

One reason buffered execution is dangerous is that cells that do not die immediately can recover. If a cell is stressed but survives, it can later repair damage and regrow. Immune clearance provides a second line of defense. Instead of relying only on the tumor cell to complete its own death program, the immune system can recognize and remove damaged or abnormal cells. This is supported by the success of immunotherapy in some cancers. In patients who respond, immune cells can maintain long-term pressure and eliminate cells that escape initial therapy. There is also growing evidence that certain forms of cell death can make tumors more visible to the immune system by releasing signals that attract immune cells [68, 363].

This suggests a useful strategy. Treatments that induce cell stress or sublethal injury could be paired with approaches that enhance immune recognition and clearance. The goal would be to convert incomplete execution into elimination by an external system. If a cell does not fully die on its own, it may still be removed by immune mechanisms.

Rule 6: match therapy to the dominant buffering system in each tumor

Not all tumors resist death in the same way. Some rely heavily on anti-apoptotic proteins. Others depend on strong antioxidant systems that protect against oxidative damage. Still others are buffered by inflammatory survival signaling. Studies of ferroptosis have identified GPX4 and cystine transport as key protective systems that prevent lipid damage and cell death [70, 219]. In apoptosis research, proteins such as MCL-1 and BCL-XL often act as central survival buffers. These differences mean that the same therapy may work well in one tumor but fail in another. This means the same drug may fail for different reasons in different patients. A therapy designed to trigger apoptosis may work well in a tumor with weak mitochondrial buffering but fail in one with strong anti-apoptotic redundancy. A ferroptosis-based approach may work in a context where antioxidant defenses are low, but not where redox buffering is dominant. The logical next step is to stratify tumors based on how they avoid death. Instead of asking only which oncogenic pathway is active, it may be just as important to ask which buffering system is most important for survival. Identifying the dominant escape route could help guide which combinations are most likely to collapse the network and push cells past the execution threshold.

In summary, these principles point toward a different way of designing therapy. The goal is not simply to trigger death pathways more strongly. It is to remove the barriers that keep cells from fully committing, prevent adaptation, control vulnerability states, use time strategically, and involve immune clearance where possible. As more studies continue to reveal how small surviving populations drive relapse, treatment design will likely move toward approaches that specifically aim to eliminate those buffered cells rather than only reducing the average tumor burden.

Toward network-collapse therapy

Across treatment classes, tumors rarely persist because death pathways are absent. The machinery for apoptosis, inflammatory death, and oxidative damage is often intact and can be activated. The more consistent limitation is that survival systems absorb stress and prevent a full collapse. In our view, this reframes therapeutic failure. The problem is often not initiating death signaling, but overcoming the buffering networks that keep execution below the point of no return. Clinical experience supports this. Death receptor agonists could engage receptors and activate proximal signaling, yet durable responses were limited, consistent with downstream buffering of commitment [334]. BH3 mimetics show that apoptosis can be triggered strongly in BCL-2 dependent settings, yet resistance frequently emerges through shifts toward alternative protective proteins such as MCL-1, implying adaptation of the buffering network rather than loss of the death program [346, 347]. Ferroptosis biology reveals a similar architecture. Lethal lipid damage can be induced, but execution is blocked when antioxidant defenses remain dominant, including GPX4 and cystine transport pathways [70, 219]. Across these examples, survival reflects network behavior rather than a single missing node. Taken together, these findings point to a shared conclusion. Tumor survival is rarely explained by a single missing signal or a single resistant mutation. Instead, it is supported by interconnected survival networks that absorb stress and maintain viability. As long as these systems remain intact, activating a single death pathway may produce a strong initial response while leaving a small group of surviving cells. This helps explain a common clinical pattern. Receptor engagement can occur. Caspases can be activated. Mitochondria can be stressed. Lipid damage can begin. Yet if survival systems continue to buffer these signals, execution may stop short of completion in some cells. Even a small surviving fraction can later drive relapse.

These observations suggest that future strategies may need to move beyond targeting single pathways. Instead of focusing solely on activating death signals, therapy may need to destabilize the broader survival network that protects tumor cells. In practical terms, this could mean combining treatments that activate death pathways with others that weaken the protective systems that limit commitment. It could also involve using treatment sequences that first reduce buffering and then apply lethal stress. This challenge must be approached carefully. Treatment intensity cannot simply be increased without limit. The goal is to weaken tumor survival systems while keeping normal tissues safe. Tumors harbor cells in many different cellular states simultaneously, including rapidly dividing cells, slow-cycling cells, and metabolically diverse populations. Each state may rely on a different type of protection. For therapy to be curative, it must push these different populations past their individual thresholds for collapse.

The implication is that future strategies must move beyond single-node targeting. Instead of focusing solely on activating death pathways, therapies may need to be designed to destabilize the broader survival network that protects tumor cells from committing to death. In practical terms, this means identifying and collapsing the buffering systems that maintain viability across different cellular states. This could involve simultaneously targeting execution triggers and the protective mechanisms that counteract them, or sequencing treatments in a way that weakens buffering before applying lethal stress. From this perspective, the central translational challenge is not whether therapies can start death signaling. Modern treatments often do this successfully. The deeper question is whether therapy can force a coordinated breakdown of the survival network across the whole tumor population, and do so within the narrow safety limits that patients can tolerate.

Conclusion and future perspective

Cancer therapy has traditionally been guided by a straightforward assumption. If a critical survival pathway is blocked or a death pathway is activated, tumor cells should die. Over the past two decades, advances in targeted therapy, apoptosis-directed drugs, ferroptosis research, and immunotherapy have confirmed that many of these pathways can be engaged in patients [17, 230]. Tumors shrink, signaling networks are suppressed, and molecular signs of stress are clearly visible. Yet complete and lasting eradication remains uncommon across most cancers [285]. A familiar clinical pattern continues to appear. There is an initial response, followed by persistence of a small residual population, and then eventual relapse [91, 319].

This recurring outcome suggests that treatment failure is not always due to an inability to activate death pathways. Instead, a more consistent explanation may be that execution is incomplete. Tumor cells often begin the death process, but a fraction do not cross the point of irreversible commitment. These surviving cells remain viable, sometimes in altered states that allow them to tolerate stress. Over time, they can recover, expand, and relapse.

Clinical evidence across multiple settings supports this view. In blood cancers, MRD has become one of the strongest predictors of relapse [319, 320]. Even when the most detectable cancer is cleared, the presence of a small surviving population is enough to determine long-term outcome. In chronic myeloid leukemia, long-term pathway suppression can produce deep molecular responses, yet disease frequently returns after treatment discontinuation in a subset of patients [285]. In solid tumors, targeted therapies often produce dramatic shrinkage, but progression eventually occurs [156, 248]. These patterns are difficult to explain if death pathways were never engaged. They are easier to understand if a fraction of cells experienced stress but did not reach full execution.

This perspective helps connect several long-standing clinical observations. Biomarkers that measure pathway activation often correlate well with early response but are less reliable for predicting durability. For example, discontinuation of EGFR inhibitors can lead to rapid metabolic and radiographic tumor rebound despite prior response, highlighting that initial pathway suppression does not necessarily reflect sustained tumor control [327]. Different drug classes exhibit distinct resistance patterns because each encounters a distinct barrier to full activity [86, 103, 388]. Dose reductions and treatment interruptions, which are unavoidable in clinical practice, may allow surviving cells to recover and adapt [330, 389]. This residual survival is often underestimated, as bulk response measurements can overestimate killing by missing rare cells capable of regrowth [76, 285].

If this framework is correct, then the central issue in therapy is not simply whether death signaling can be triggered. In many cases, it already can. Apoptotic signaling, for example, can be initiated without necessarily progressing to irreversible execution, as mitochondrial commitment depends on the balance between activating and sensitizing BH3-only proteins that regulate BAX/BAK activation [390]. The deeper challenge is whether enough tumor cells are pushed beyond a point from which recovery is no longer possible. This shifts attention toward execution depth as a critical therapeutic variable. What ultimately matters is not only how strongly a tumor responds initially, but whether the surviving fraction has been eliminated.

This idea has several implications for treatment design. Therapies may need to target both the mechanisms that drive execution and the systems that buffer against it [391, 392]. Preventing adaptive rewiring may be just as important as activating death pathways. Scheduling may influence how tumors adapt, since the timing and continuity of pressure can determine whether cells commit to death or remain in a recoverable state [393, 394]. Linking intrinsic cell injury to immune-mediated clearance may provide an additional layer of elimination for cells that are damaged but not fully destroyed [395]. Patient selection may also benefit from identifying which survival mechanisms dominate in a given tumor, allowing combinations to be matched to the specific biological context.

At the same time, important questions remain. Execution thresholds are likely to vary across tumor types, cellular states, and microenvironments. A key limitation of this framework is the difficulty of directly measuring irreversible commitment in clinical settings, as current approaches do not allow precise quantification of sublethal signaling states or the point of no return in patient samples [10]. This challenge is particularly pronounced in solid tumors, where sensitive residual-disease markers are limited [91]. In addition, tumor heterogeneity [79] and dynamic microenvironmental influences further complicate the translation of these concepts into clinically actionable biomarkers. Addressing these gaps will require longitudinal studies, functional assays, and advanced single-cell and spatial approaches capable of capturing cell-state transitions under therapeutic pressure

Looking forward, it may be useful to think about cancer therapy less as the activation of a single pathway and more as the destabilization of a survival system. Tumors contain multiple cell states and overlapping protective programs. As long as these networks can withstand stress and remain viable, some cells may remain below the execution threshold. Durable control may therefore depend on strategies that weaken these protective networks enough that survival becomes unsustainable.

This does not diminish the impact of current therapies, many of which have significantly improved survival and quality of life. Instead, it provides a possible explanation for their limits. Understanding why some cells survive despite strong stress signals may help explain relapse and guide more effective strategies in the future.

In the end, the central question may not be whether therapies can activate death pathways, as this is often already achieved, but whether they can consistently drive execution to completion across the full diversity of tumor cell states. Addressing this challenge will require strategies that not only trigger cell death but also overcome the buffering mechanisms that allow a fraction of cells to remain below the threshold of irreversible commitment. A deeper understanding of execution depth and its determinants may therefore be critical for achieving durable therapeutic responses and preventing relapse
