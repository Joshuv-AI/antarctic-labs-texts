Identification of LsPROG gene in Leymus secalinus and validation of its function in Arabidopsis thaliana
Authors: Xin Zhang, Jialin Li, Di Zhao, Xueru Feng, Siqi Wang, Hongmei Li, Wenxing He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeymus secalinus, a perennial grass in the Poaceae family, exhibits multiple stress resistances including drought, salt-alkali, and trampling tolerance, with developed rhizomes enabling adaptation to harsh environments such as sand dunes, thus holding significant value for grassland improvement and forage breeding.ResultsThis study functionally characterized the stress-related LsPROG gene of L. secalinus, revealing that it encoded a 184-amino acid protein. Subcellular localization showed that it was localized to the cell membrane, and contained a conserved zinc-finger domain indicative of potential transcriptional regulatory functions. Using Arabidopsis as a heterologous system, we generated 35S:LsPROG overexpressing Arabidopsis lines. In Arabidopsis transgenic lines, we confirmed that LsPROG significantly promoted lateral root development (about 2-fold) and significantly increased leaf number (by about 1.5-fold at the seedling stage) in transgenic lines compared to the Col-0. Notably, under drought, salt (NaCl), and ABA stresses, the 35S:LsPROG transgenic lines exhibited significantly even more pronounced phenotypic advantages over the Col-0 including improved root system development in seedlings (specifically higher lateral root numbers under drought and enhanced primary root growth under salt/ABA) and better overall plant growth, accompanied by significantly enhanced activities of antioxidant enzymes (SOD increased by 35%, POD increased by 40%).ConclusionThrough heterologous expression in Arabidopsis, this study systematically unveiled for the first time the pivotal role of LsPROG in plant growth, development, and stress responses, with its functional characteristics elucidated via cross-validation with multiple experimental approaches. These findings lay a critical theoretical foundation for the future breeding of high-quality forage varieties.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13168-x.Keywords: Abiotic stress, Leymus secalinus, LsPROG, Root development

Background

Plants worldwide face severe survival pressures from abiotic stresses (drought, salinization, extreme temperatures), which disrupt physiological processes and limit growth [1–5]. To thrive, they have evolved sophisticated adaptive strategies, with gene regulation as a key driver [6–8]. Among these, the Leymus genus stands out for its exceptional stress resilience, making it invaluable in ecological restoration, forage production, and crop improvement [9–13]. Core species like Leymus chinensis and L. secalinus dominate harsh environments—arid/semi-arid zones, desertified lands, and saline soils—due to the robust drought, salt, and cold tolerance conferred by their genetic makeup [14–17]. As foundational components of grassland ecosystems, they support large-scale feed production while stabilizing landscapes through adaptive traits [18–20].

As a key species within the genus Leymus, L. secalinus exemplifies the ecological resilience of Leymus through its unique biology. Distributed across northwestern, northern, and northeastern China, as well as Russia, North Korea, and Japan [21–23], it thrives in diverse habitats, including sandy lands and mountain grasslands, via a well-developed underground reticular rhizome system [24–26], which directly enhances its stress adaptation by improving water and nutrient acquisition and mitigating the impacts of drought, salinity, and soil instability in harsh environments. Its horizontal rhizome spread and large tiller angles (unlike the upright growth of rice) enhance colonization in harsh conditions while reinforcing soil stabilization as an ecological barrier [27–31].

Biochemically, the genome of L. secalinus harbors stress-resistant (drought, salt-alkali) and pest-resistant genes [32–37], making it a valuable resource for Triticeae crop improvement through molecular breeding. These genetic traits hold promise for enhancing crop resilience and yield stability, with broader commercial potential in agricultural biotechnology [38–40]. Thus, in-depth study of its molecular mechanisms is critical for unlocking the genetic basis of stress resistance in Leymus and optimizing the utilization of gene resources [41].

The PROG gene, first identified in wild rice, encodes a zinc-finger protein with transcriptional activity on chromosome 7 [42–45], deeply involved in the domestication of wild rice to cultivated rice by regulating the structural transformation from prostrate to upright growth through amino acid substitutions in the PROG protein [46]. This transformation optimized plant architecture and increased yields, with fixed mutations in the PROG coding region across 182 cultivated rice varieties [42]. In A. thaliana, the PROG gene promotes ABA-induced stomatal closure via the ROS pathway, enhancing drought tolerance by scavenging excess ROS through increased antioxidant enzyme activity. In Bermuda grass (Cynodon dactylon), tiller-angle-regulating genes including PROG showed adaptive evolution, providing insights into stolon growth regulation [40, 47, 48]. While PROG is known to regulate above-ground architecture and stress responses in these species, the unique rhizome-dependent stress tolerance of L. secalinus leads us to hypothesize a distinct, yet critical, role for its PROG ortholog. We hypothesized that the PROG gene may play a critical role in mediating L. secalinus resistance to complex stresses by specifically regulating rhizome growth. However, the specific functional mechanisms of the PROG gene in L. secalinus remain uncharacterized, presenting an unexplored research direction in understanding its environmental adaptability.

In this study, we identified LsPROG from L. secalinus, analyzed its sequence and evolutionary relationships via bioinformatics, and since L. secalinus lacks a reliable genetic transformation protocol, we generated LsPROG-overexpressing Arabidopsis lines via genetic transformation to rapidly validate the function of the gene. Phenotypic analysis showed enhanced root and leaf growth in overexpressing lines compared to wild-type, and improved stress adaptability under salt, drought, and ABA treatments. These results provide a basis for understanding LsPROG function in stress resistance and offer potential for crop improvement.

Results

Identification of LsPROG Gene in L. secalinus

The PROG (prostrate growth) gene is first reported in wild rice, another plant within the Poaceae family. A functional gene related to rhizome development was identified in L. secalinus and named LsPROG (SRA database of NCBI: PRJNA776697). The protein sequence encoded by the open reading frame (ORF) of the LsPROG gene contains 184 amino acids (Fig. 1A; Fig. S1). Prediction of the protein transmembrane domain indicates that the entire peptide chain of the LsPROG protein is located outside the cell membrane (Fig. 1B). However, given the subsequent experimental results, this prediction does not preclude the possibility of its association with the cell membrane. In addition, the subcellular localization of the LsPROG protein was preliminarily predicted, and the results suggest that the protein encoded by the LsPROG gene may be localized in the nucleus (Fig. 1C). It is noteworthy that this computational prediction presents a hypothesis for its localization, which will be empirically tested against experimental observations.

Analysis of the evolutionary relationships of PROG proteins in different plant species

Phylogenetic analysis of amino acid sequences from L. secalinus and other plants was conducted using MEGA5.0. First, phylogenetic tree construction revealed that the LsPROG protein isolated from L. secalinus showed the closest genetic relationship with TdPROG, HvsPROG, and TaPROG proteins, indicating potential functional similarity (Fig. 2A). Based on phylogenetic results, amino acid sequences of the three most closely related Poaceae species (TdPROG, HvsPROG, and TaPROG) were selected for multiple sequence alignment. The results showed high similarity between the LsPROG protein and these three species, with extremely high conservation in specific sequence regions, suggesting similar functions of PROG in L. secalinus (Fig. 2B). Further analysis of conserved domains using MEME demonstrated that the LsPROG protein shared identical numbers, high similarity, and consistent arrangement orders of Motif regions with its Poaceae homologs (Fig. 2C), such as containing conserved motifs like Motif 1–5, which implied functional similarity. Additionally, domain prediction revealed that the LsPROG protein contained not only the zf-C2H2_6 domain but also the PRK13875 superfamily domain, which is a feature absent in other Poaceae plants that only exhibit different domains belonging to zf-C2H2. Given its link to signal transduction, this unique domain may potentially enhance the capacity of LsPROG to integrate environmental cues in L. secalinus. Notably, the TaPROG protein from wheat shared the zf-C2H2_6 domain with LsPROG, further supporting their similar gene functions, while the unique PRK13875 domain likely endows LsPROG with distinct functional properties.

Subcellular localization of the LsPROG protein and analysis of its tissue expression in L. secalinus

Building on prior bioinformatics predictions that localized the LsPROG protein to the nucleus (Fig. 1C), we experimentally validated its subcellular distribution in tobacco leaves. Agrobacterium-mediated transformation with 35S:LsPROG-GFP and control plasmids revealed pan-cellular GFP expression, whereas LsPROG specifically localized to the plasma membrane (Fig. 3). This discrepancy between in-silico prediction (nucleus) and experimental observation (cell membrane) might link to the unique PRK13875 superfamily domain of LsPROG (absent in most Poaceae homologs; Fig. 2C), which could mediate membrane association and potentially aids rapid stress signal perception, consistent with its role in environmental adaptation. To further characterize the functional relevance of the LsPROG gene, a tissue-specific expression analysis was conducted in L. secalinus. RNA was extracted from the roots, leaves, stems, and flowers, reverse-transcribed into cDNA, and subjected to qRT-PCR using Actin as an internal reference. The results showed the highest LsPROG transcript levels in roots, followed by leaves, stems and flowers (Fig. 4).

Heterologous overexpression of LsPROG gene promotes growth and development of Arabidopsis

Previous studies have demonstrated that PROG genes play a critical role in plant growth and development. Given the underdeveloped genetic transformation system in L. secalinus, we employed Arabidopsis as a model to characterize LsPROG function via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation (Fig. S2). The T1 generation of LsPROG transgenic Arabidopsis thaliana was screened (Fig. S3), and homozygous T3 lines with stable LsPROG expression were obtained (Fig. S4; Fig. S5). Phenotypic analysis revealed that transgenic lines exhibited significant leaf proliferation: Col-0 produced approximately 8 leaves, while 35S:LsPROG plants averaged 12 leaves with a significant 1.5-fold increase (Fig. 5A-B). During bolting, Col-0 retained around 8 rosette leaves (only increasing in size, Fig. 5C), whereas transgenic lines showed a significant 2.25-fold increase to approximately 18 leaves (Fig. 5D). At the pod stage, Col-0 had 8–10 senescing leaves, compared to approximately 34 healthy leaves in transgenics with a significant 3.7-fold difference (Fig. 5E-F). The hemispherical canopy and robust growth of transgenic plants indicate that LsPROG promotes leaf proliferation, consistent with reported roles of PROG genes in regulating plant developmental plasticity.

Following the discovery that the LsPROG gene was expressed in all tissues of L. secalinus with the highest levels in roots (Fig. 4), we investigated its role in root development. One-week-old seedlings of Col-0 and transgenic lines were transferred to vertical plates and cultured for 10 days. While primary root length did not differ significantly between control and transgenic lines, transgenic plants developed significantly more lateral roots (mean ± SE: Col-0, 9.0 ± 1.5; LsPROG lines, 16.3 ± 2.0; n = 3 for all groups, representing 3 biological replicates per genotype), representing approximately a 2-fold increase (Fig. 6A-C). This phenotype aligns with the highest expression level of LsPROG in roots, suggesting a tissue-specific functional correlation. This indicates that LsPROG participates in root development regulation, primarily by promoting lateral root formation. Collectively, these results demonstrate that LsPROG coordinately regulates leaf number in shoots and lateral root formation in roots, thereby significantly enhancing overall plant growth and development in Arabidopsis.

Overexpression of LsPROG enhances drought tolerance in A. thaliana

Existing studies have demonstrated that the PROG gene family is involved in plant stress responses. To verify whether LsPROG from L. secalinus exhibits similar functions, we conducted drought stress and other experiments to investigate the potential of the gene to enhance plant stress resistance. Drought, salt and ABA treatments significantly induced the expression of the LsPROG gene (Fig. S6). Col-0 and 35S:LsPROG seedlings were transferred to 50 mM mannitol MS medium and cultured vertically for 14 days, primary root length and lateral root number were counted, with Arabidopsis cultured in mannitol-free MS plates as control to compare root development changes, and sampled for detection (Fig. 7A). The same batch of germinated seedlings mentioned above were transplanted into nutrient soil, cultured normally until they reached 4 weeks old, and then subjected to natural drought by withholding water for seven days. Under this stress condition, both Col-0 and 35S:LsPROG transgenic seedings exhibited reduced primary root length and significantly reduced lateral root number compared to the control treatment (Fig. 7A-B), indicating that drought stress generally inhibited root development. However, the reduction of primary root length was not statistically significant different between control and transgenic lines. Notably, under drought stress, only the transgenic lines L1 and L3 showed a significant reduction in lateral root number compared to their respective untreated controls (Fig. 7C). Nevertheless, the transgenic lines maintained significantly more lateral roots than Col-0 under stress, with better root system architecture (Fig. 7C), suggesting that LsPROG overexpression enhances drought resistance in Arabidopsis. Under drought stress, all Arabidopsis lines showed smaller leaves and growth retardation compared to controls. Col-0 exhibited leaf shrinkage and seedling mortality, while 35S:LsPROG lines showed no mortality and maintained turgid, fresh leaves (Fig. 7D). The activities of SOD/POD, measured based on fresh weight, were similar in the control treatment but increased more significantly in transgenics under drought stress (Fig. 7E-F), indicating LsPROG enhances drought resistance via antioxidant enzyme activation.

Overexpression of LsPROG increases salt tolerance in A. thaliana

To investigate whether LsPROG could improve plant salt tolerance, salt stress experiments were conducted. One-week-old seedlings of Col-0 and 35S:LsPROG transgenic lines were transferred to MS medium containing 50 mM NaCl (vertical culture, 14 days) for root phenotypes observation, and three-week-old plants were further subjected to salt treatment up to 200 mM NaCl, with leaves sampled for detection after 7 days. Under salt stress, Col-0 showed a significant reduction in primary root length, while the decrease in transgenic lines was significantly less pronounced (Fig. 8A-B). Looking at Fig. 8C, the number of lateral roots of all transgenic lines was significantly higher than that of the control line under untreated control conditions. Under salt stress, however, only the L3 transgenic line maintained a significantly higher number of lateral roots compared to the Col-0 control line, while lines L1 and L2 showed no significant differences from Col-0. Overall, their overall root development remained superior to Col-0, indicating that LsPROG overexpression enhances salt stress resistance in Arabidopsis.

Under salt treatment, all lines showed leaf wilting (Fig. 8D), but transgenic plants were larger with more turgid leaves than Col-0. While SOD and POD activities were similar under normal conditions, transgenics exhibited significantly higher induction of both enzymes under salt stress (Fig. 8E-F), indicating LsPROG enhances salt tolerance via antioxidant enzyme activation.

Overexpression of LsPROG boosts the tolerance in A. thaliana to ABA

Following the same seed sterilization, germination, and transfer to square MS plates as above, Col-0 and 35S:LsPROG seedlings were subjected to 50 µM ABA stress (cultured vertically on corresponding square plates). Under this stress treatment, Col-0 exhibited significant reductions in primary root length and lateral root number compared to the control (Fig. 9A), while 35S:LsPROG transgenics showed significantly longer primary roots, significantly more lateral roots, and improved root development under stress treatment (Fig. 9B-C), indicating enhanced ABA tolerance. In pot experiments, after vernalization and soil growth, transgenic plants under gradient ABA stress (100 µM) remained larger with healthy, non-wilting leaves and normal bolting, whereas Col-0 showed stunted growth and stress-induced leaf damage (Fig. 9D). Transgenics also displayed significantly higher induction of SOD and POD enzyme activities under ABA stress compared to both controls and Col-0 (Fig. 9E-F), suggesting LsPROG enhances ABA resistance by activating antioxidant enzyme pathways. Unlike the integrated role of ABA within complex stress combinations in the field, the exogenous application used here isolates its signaling pathway for functional analysis.

Discussion

L. secalinus forms a well-developed root and rhizome system in sandy habitats, showing strong adaptability to wind erosion and sand burial. As a conserved gene in Poaceae, the PROG is associated with root and stem growth, particularly regulating tiller angle and inclination [49]. Integrating molecular genetics and multi-omics helps identify stress-responsive functional genes, while gene editing can enhance forage stress tolerance. Bioinformatics analysis reveals the 184-amino-acid protein encoded by LsPROG gene in L. secalinus is closely related to PROG proteins from gramineous plants such as Triticum dicoccoides [50] and Hordeum vulgare [51] (Fig. 1), with phylogenetic analysis further showing LsPROG clusters with TdPROG and HvPROG. Both share high conservation in the zf-C2H2_6 zinc finger and PRK13875 superfamily domains, hallmarks of functional conservation in Poaceae (Fig. 2). Subcellular localization experiments, however, reveal a discrepancy: predicted nuclear localization contrasts with observed membrane localization in tobacco transient expression assays, suggesting LsPROG may have evolved specialized roles in membrane-associated stress signaling or structural maintenance (Figs. 1 and 4), potentially enhancing its adaptive capacity in harsh environments. This observed membrane localization, differing from the in silico prediction, leads us to hypothesize that specific regulatory mechanisms, such as post-translational modifications or interaction with membrane-associated proteins, may determine its final subcellular destination under different conditions.

Phenotypic analysis of LsPROG-overexpressing transgenic Arabidopsis revealed a significant increase in lateral root number (approximately twice that of Col-0) and leaf number at the seedling, bolting, and pod stages, suggesting LsPROG plays a role in root system development and shoot morphogenesis (Figs. 5 and 6) [52, 53]. Tissue expression analysis showed high LsPROG transcript levels in roots, leaves, and stems (with the lowest expression in flowers), consistent with the functional importance of these tissues in stress responses and supporting a role for LsPROG in growth and stress tolerance coordination across multiple tissues (Fig. 4) [54–56].

In the seedling root experiments under abiotic stress conditions, the following specific observations were made (Figs. 7, 8 and 9). All 35S:LsPROG transgenic lines exhibited significantly higher primary root length than control seedlings under salt (50 mM NaCl) and ABA (50 µM) stresses, while no significant differences in primary root length were observed between transgenic and control seedlings under drought stress (50 mM mannitol). Regarding lateral root number, all 35S:LsPROG transgenic lines showed a significantly higher number of lateral roots than control seedlings only under drought stress. Under salt stress, only the LsPROG-L3 line showed a significantly higher number of lateral roots. Notably, no significant differences in the number of lateral roots were observed between control and 35S:LsPROG transgenic lines under ABA stress conditions [57].

Two-way ANOVA revealed significant main effects of genotype and treatment for almost all investigated root phenotypic and biochemical variables, except for primary root length under drought stress (Table S2). Furthermore, the genotype time treatment interaction effects were highly significant (p < 0.01 or p < 0.001) for lateral root number and antioxidant enzyme activities across all stresses, indicating that the impact of LsPROG overexpression is highly context-dependent and varies significantly under different environmental conditions.

Our functional characterization of LsPROG demonstrated a unique root-related phenotype, primarily characterized by a two-fold increase in lateral root number under normal conditions and the maintenance of a robust root system architecture under abiotic stress (Figs. 6 and 7). These results further expand our understanding of the PROG gene family, which has been predominantly studied in the context of above-ground architecture, such as regulating tiller angle and prostrate growth in wild rice (Oryza rufipogon) [22, 35, 49, 58] and bermudagrass (Cynodon dactylon) [23, 59]. Unlike its orthologs that prime shoot-related plastic responses, LsPROG exhibits a distinct tissue-specific functional correlation with root development, consistent with its exceptionally high transcript levels in the roots of L. secalinus (Fig. 4). Similar root-promotive activities under stress have been reported for other zinc-finger and stress-responsive transcription factors in Poaceae, which optimize resource acquisition and cellular protection under adverse conditions [60]. The functional divergence of LsPROG highlights the adaptive evolution of Leymus species to sandy and arid habitats, where subterranean investment is favored over shoot orientation. It is important to interpret these findings in the context of baseline differences observed under non-stressed control conditions. In the drought seedling experiment, significant differences in lateral root number between control and transgenic seedlings were already present under untreated conditions, and these significant differences persisted after drought stress (Fig. 7). In the NaCl seedling experiment, significant differences between control and the L1 and L2 transgenic lines were also observed in the untreated control; however, under NaCl stress, no significant differences were observed between control and these two transgenic lines, although the reduction in lateral root number compared to the untreated control was greater in the transgenic lines. For the L3 line, significant differences from the control were observed in the untreated control and remained after salt stress (Fig. 8). In the ABA seedling experiment, significant differences in lateral root number between control and transgenic seedlings were again present in the untreated control. Under ABA stress, no significant differences were observed between control and transgenic lines, but the reduction in lateral root number relative to the untreated control was greater in all transgenic lines (Fig. 9). These observations suggest that LsPROG overexpression may enhance root system plasticity under optimal conditions, and its protective effect under stress is context-dependent, varying with the specific stress and transgenic line [61].

Furthermore, the transgenic lines showed less severe shoot wilting and no mortality under drought/salt stresses compared to wild-type plants. Antioxidant enzyme assays demonstrated that while basal SOD and POD activities were similar under normal conditions, stress treatments significantly induced higher enzyme activities in transgenic lines (Figs. 7, 8 and 9). This indicates that LsPROG enhances antioxidant defense capabilities to mitigate stress damage [62]. Although numerous studies have been conducted using these methodologies to evaluate abiotic stress tolerance, it should be noted that the controlled osmotic (mannitol) and defined ionic (NaCl) stresses applied here represent simplified experimental models that do not fully correspond to the multifaceted water deficit and complex variable salinity encountered in natural field conditions. Notably, under ABA stress, transgenic plants maintain longer primary roots and more lateral roots than Col-0, possibly due to LsPROG-mediated membrane stabilization or enhanced ABA signal transduction (Fig. 9).

Collectively, these results from the heterologous Arabidopsis system show LsPROG can confer improved stress tolerance in this model plant by promoting lateral root development for enhanced resource uptake, increasing leaf number (and thus biomass accumulation), and activating antioxidant enzyme systems [63]. Its conserved domains and membrane localization provide a molecular basis for these functions, while tissue expression patterns align with multi-organ stress adaptation [62]. Notably, the functional characterization was performed on Arabidopsis, and validation in forage grasses will be required to confirm its applicability in gramineous species. Key next steps include the development of a stable transformation system for L. secalinus or a closely related forage grass to directly validate LsPROG function in its native genetic background, as well as detailed investigations into its upstream regulators and downstream target genes within the stress-responsive network. These findings clarify the potential role of LsPROG in L. secalinus stress resistance and offer insights for gramineous stress-gene mining and forage breeding. Further study of its downstream networks and membrane-targeting mechanisms will deepen our understanding of its stress-signaling roles.

Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

L. secalinus: Seeds from the Forage Research Laboratory of Inner Mongolia Agricultural University were subjected to dormancy-breaking treatment: 7days cold storage at 0–4 °C in the dark, followed by 24 h soaking in running water. Germination was induced in a 28 °C light incubator for 3–5 days after spreading seeds on a moist gauze-lined glass dish. Arabidopsis thaliana (Col-0): The Col-0 was stored in our laboratory. Nicotiana benthamiana: The seedlings are stored in the plant light incubator located in University of Jinan.

Bioinformatics analysis

Protein sequence analysis: SnapGene Viewer 5.3.2 was used to determine the molecular weight, isoelectric point, and primary sequence properties of the LsPROG protein by direct amino-acid sequence input, requiring no special parameters. Transmembrane domain prediction: The TMHMM2.0 online tool (http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/TMHMM) (version 2.0) was employed to predict transmembrane domains via automatic analysis of the input amino-acid sequence. Tertiary structure prediction: The Colab version of AlphaFold2 was used with the num_recycles parameter set to 5 for tertiary structure prediction, followed by structural visualization using open-source PyMOL. Subcellular localization prediction: Wolfpsort (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp) was utilized to predict subcellular localization by analyzing the input amino-acid sequence against known localization patterns. The phylogenetic tree was constructed by MEGA 7.0 (https://www.megasoftware.net/) [64]. Several genes closely related to the target genes were selected for multiple sequence alignment using Jalview software (version 2.11.2.6) [65].

Prediction of protein motifs and domains

Motif and domain predictions were conducted using MEME (https://meme-suite.org) (version 5.5.2), CD-search (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi) and HMMER (http://hmmer.org) (version 3.3.2). For all tools, an E-value threshold of 0.05 was set; for HMMER, a coverage threshold of ≥ 50% was additionally applied. Subsequently, the Gene Structure View plugin of TBtools was employed to generate a schematic diagram of the LsPROG by integrating the predicted exon/intron and conserved domain information.

Subcellular localization

Tobacco leaf epidermal cells were injected with Agrobacterium containing plasmids 35S:LsPROG-GFP or 35S:GFP, respectively. The inoculated tobacco was cultivated for approximately 48 h under normal conditions. The lower epidermis of tobacco leaves was placed on a slide and the instantaneous gene expression was observed by a confocal laser scanning microscope LSM800 with excitation at 488 nm and emission at 500–530 nm.

Construction of LsPROG overexpression vector and Arabidopsis transformation

The full-length coding sequence of LsPROG was amplified from L. secalinus cDNA and cloned into the pEASY-Blunt Simple vector for sequencing. The validated fragment was double-digested with SmaI and SpeI and ligated into the plant expression vector pCAMBIA1300-GFP. The resulting construct was transformed into E. coli DH5α, and positive clones were verified by colony PCR and sequencing. The correct plasmid was then introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens LBA4404 via freeze-thaw method. Transgenic A. thaliana (Col-0) was generated by floral dip transformation with the recombinant Agrobacterium, and T3 homozygous lines were screened on MS medium containing 100 mg/L hygromycin for subsequent functional analysis. The primers used in this study were shown in Table S1.

Tissue expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted from roots, stems, leaves, and flowers of L. secalinus, then reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed to analyze LsPROG expression, with the ACTIN gene as an internal reference. Specific primers for LsPROG and ACTIN were designed. and qRT-PCR reactions were conducted in a mixture containing cDNA template, primers, and SYBR Green dye. Three independent biological replicates were persormed for each tissue sample, with three technical replicates per sample. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method [66].

Phenotypic analysis of transgenic lines

Seeds of A. thaliana wild type (Col-0) and 35S:LsPROG transgenic Arabidopsis lines (T3 homozygous) were surface-sterilized, sown on MS medium (13 cm × 13 cm), and incubated at 4 °C in the dark for 2 days. Seedlings were transferred to vertical MS plates and grown at 22 °C under a 16 h light/8 h dark cycle for 14 days. Root phenotypes (primary root length, lateral root number) were imaged and measured using Image J. Shoot traits, including rosette leaf number, leaf area, and bolting height, were recorded at the seedling, bolting, and pod stages. Data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test for significant differences (at the 5% level), with three biological replicates for each genotype.

Abiotic stress tolerance assays

Three biological replicates were performed. For each replicate and genotype, phenotypes were scored on ≥ 15 seedlings, and enzyme assays used leaf tissue pooled from ≥ 10 plants. To analyze the function of the LsPROG gene under abiotic stress, wild-type Arabidopsis (Col-0) and homozygous LsPROG overexpression lines (T3 generation) with uniform growth were surface-sterilized, then sown individually on MS plates and vernalized at 4 °C in the dark for 2 days. MS plates were incubated at 22 °C with 16 h light/day for 7 days to germinate. Post-germination, Col-0 and transgenic seedlings were split into two groups: one transferred to 13 cm×13 cm square MS plates with 50 mM mannitol, 50 µM ABA, or 50 mM NaCl, vertically cultured for 14 days. Root growth was photographed; root length and lateral root number were measured via Image J for analysis. The other group was transplanted to nutrient soil, grown at 23 °C with 14 h light/day until 4 weeks old for subsequent treatments. Post-treatment, above-ground leaves were harvested, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored for further processing.

For drought stress, withholding water, subjected to natural drought for 7 days. For salt stress, treated with NaCl solutions of different concentrations (50 mM on day 1, 100 mM on day 4, 200 mM on day 7). In ABA stress experiments, treated with ABA solutions of different concentrations (50 µM on day 1, 75 µM on day 4, 100 µM on day 7); all treatments included three biological replicates. The tested concentrations (e.g., 50 mM mannitol, 50/200 mM NaCl, and 50/100 µM ABA) are result of preliminary analyses to ensure they effectively induced stress responses, resulting in significant differences in root development and antioxidant enzyme activities between 35S:LsPROG transgenic lines and Col-0, while avoiding excessive stress from masking genotypic differences. Root parameters were measured using Image J software, and data were statistically analyzed via one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test (at the 5% level) to ensure phenotypic evaluation rigor.

SOD and POD activity assays

After subjecting wild-type A. thaliana (Col-0) and homozygous LsPROG overexpression lines (T3 generation) to drought, salt, and ABA stress treatments, 0.2 g of stressed Arabidopsis leaves were ground in pre-chilled phosphate buffer (50mM, pH 7.8 for SOD; pH 7.0 for POD). The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected as the crude enzyme extract. SOD activity was measured using a WST-8 kit by calculating the enzyme activity required to inhibit 50% of nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) reduction, expressed as units per gram fresh weight (U/g FW). POD activity was determined via a colorimetric method by monitoring absorbance changes at 470 nm, with results calculated as units per gram fresh weight per minute (U/g FW/min). Three independent biological replicates were analyzed for each treatment, with each sample measured in three technical replicates. Data were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s test for significant differences (at the 5% level).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were set up with three biological replicates (n = 3). For phenotypic data under normal conditions (Figs. 4 and 5, and 6), one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to evaluate the differences among different treatment groups. For all results presented in Figs. 7 and 8, and 9, a two-way ANOVA was utilized to analyze the main effects of the two factors (genotype and treatment) and their interactions. Tukey’s test was combined for multiple comparisons, with the significance level set at the 5% level.
