Longitudinal contrast-enhanced cone beam breast computed tomography for monitoring treatment response in breast cancer neoadjuvant therapy: a comprehensive assessment
Authors: Yafei Wang, Yue Ma, Aidi Liu, Mengran Zhao, Yueqiang Zhu, Lu Yin, Hong Lu, Zhaoxiang Ye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo validate cone-beam breast computed tomography (CBBCT) for evaluating NAT response using Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1, and to explore correlations between multidimensional CBBCT features and pathological complete response (pCR).MethodsWe prospectively analyzed 73 patients who underwent pre- and post-NAT CBBCT, of whom 41 also received paired magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Inter-modality and imaging-pathology concordance were assessed using Cohen’s κ statistics. Determinants of CBBCT accuracy were identified through univariate and logistic regression analyses. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis with DeLong’s test was used to determine the optimal quantitative parameter for discriminating pCR from non-pCR. Associations between pCR and changes in calcification, vascularity, and background parenchymal enhancement (BPE) were evaluated using Pearson’s χ2 and Fisher’s exact tests.ResultsCBBCT showed excellent agreement with MRI (κ = 0.809) and good pathological concordance (κ = 0.618; MRI κ = 0.700). The area under the curve (AUC) for distinguishing responders from non-responders was 0.950 for CBBCT and 0.905 for MRI. For pCR assessment, the AUC values were 0.643 and 0.786, respectively. Targeted therapy (odds ratio [OR] = 0.059, p = 0.013) and linear/segmental calcifications (OR = 0.127, p = 0.042) were independent factors affecting CBBCT accuracy. The enhanced degree change (ΔE) on CBBCT demonstrated strong predictive performance for pCR (AUC = 0.941) with high computational efficiency. Significant associations with pCR were observed for reductions in calcification extent (p = 0.021), adjacent vascular sign (AVS) grade (p = 0.004), and BPE levels (p = 0.001).ConclusionCBBCT demonstrates high accuracy in assessing NAT response, with excellent agreement to MRI. ΔE is recommended as an optimal quantitative parameter for predicting pCR, supported by dynamic changes in calcification extent, AVS, and BPE grade as valuable markers, positioning CBBCT as a comprehensive tool for breast cancer management.Clinical relevance statementCBBCT enables a comprehensive and efficient tool for monitoring NAT response, showing excellent concordance with MRI, and its multidimensional features are also valuable for predicting pCR.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13058-025-02145-z.Keywords: Cone beam breast computed tomography, Breast cancer, Neoadjuvant therapy, Pathological complete response, Magnetic resonance imaging

Introduction

Locally advanced breast cancer (LABC) represents a clinically significant disease entity marked by substantial primary tumor burden or regional lymph node metastasis in the absence of distant dissemination [1]. The adoption of neoadjuvant therapy (NAT) has reshaped the treatment landscape for LABC, particularly in human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-positive and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), by facilitating tumor downstaging and increasing the feasibility of breast-conserving surgery [2–4]. Although histopathological evaluation remains the gold standard for assessing treatment response, its post-operative nature restricts utility in real-time clinical decision-making.

Modern imaging techniques have become indispensable for monitoring NAT response, offering comprehensive tumor characterization beyond conventional pathological measures. While Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1 provides standardized size-based response criteria [5], contemporary imaging strategies now integrate multiple functional and morphological parameters. The evolution from unidimensional assessment to multiparametric analysis—encompassing tumor morphology [6–8], vascular characteristics [9, 10], and metabolic activity [11, 12]—has significantly improved the precision of treatment response evaluation. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is currently the most advanced modality, providing exceptional soft-tissue contrast and functional imaging capabilities. However, its broad clinical application is constrained by high costs, extended acquisition times, and patient-specific contraindications. These limitations underscore the need for alternative imaging techniques that balance diagnostic accuracy with clinical practicality.

Cone-beam breast computed tomography (CBBCT) is an emerging imaging technology that integrates advantages of mammography (MG) and MRI while mitigating several of their inherent limitations. As a dedicated breast imaging modality, CBBCT rapidly acquires high-resolution volumetric data [13], effectively eliminating tissue superposition artifacts characteristic of conventional MG [14]. Contrast-enhanced CBBCT (CE-CBBCT) further enables comprehensive tumor assessment through evaluation of morphological features, enhancement kinetics, calcification patterns, and vascular architecture. Although current CBBCT research has primarily focused on diagnostic applications, preoperative planning, and biologic characterization [15], its potential role in monitoring NAT response remains inadequately explored. Preliminary investigations into CBBCT’s ability to predict pathological complete response (pCR) have been constrained by methodological limitations, including reliance on single-timepoint analyses [16, 17] or narrow radiological indices [18, 19].

To address these knowledge gaps, we designed a longitudinal study evaluating the utility of CBBCT for NAT response monitoring. Our investigation comprises two principal components: first, a systematic comparison between CBBCT and MRI for assessing NAT response using RECIST 1.1 criteria; and second, a comprehensive analysis of multidimensional CBBCT-derived radiologic biomarkers for therapeutic monitoring in clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Study cohort

This prospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board and conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to CBBCT examinations.

From August 2020 to August 2024, a total of 97 consecutive breast cancer patients who had completed both pre- and post-NAT CBBCT scans at our institution were initially identified. After applying the following exclusion criteria: (a) absence of contrast-enhanced or unilateral breast scanning (n = 21); (b) inadequate image quality (n = 2); and (c) incomplete pathologic data from biopsy or surgical specimens (n = 1), 73 patients were included for multidimensional CBBCT response assessment. Within this cohort, a subgroup of 41 patients who underwent paired CBBCT and MRI examinations within seven days before and after NAT completion was further selected, allowing direct comparison of treatment response assessment between the two modalities using RECIST 1.1 criteria. CBBCT protocols

All imaging was performed using a dedicated CBBCT system (KBCT-1000, Koning Corporation). Patients were placed in the prone position with the ipsilateral arm raised and the head turned contralaterally, allowing the breast to depend freely within the scanning field. The imaging protocol began with a non-contrast enhanced CBBCT (NCE-CBBCT) acquisition, followed by contrast-enhanced scanning initiated 120 s after intravenous injection of 100 mL iodixanol (Omnipaque® 270, GE Healthcare) administered at a rate of 2 mL/s. Bilateral imaging was performed sequentially.

The system operated at a tube voltage of 49 kVp with automatic current modulation ranging from 50 to 80 mA, adjusted according to individual breast thickness and density. The total radiation exposure per unilateral examination ranged from 11.6 to 14.4 mGy. All images were reconstructed using a dedicated soft-tissue algorithm producing isotropic voxels measuring (0.273 mm)3. Multiplanar reformations and three-dimensional maximum intensity projection (3D-MIP) images were generated from these reconstructions for clinical evaluation.

MRI protocols

MRI examinations were conducted using either 1.5 T or 3.0 T scanners (GE Healthcare) with dedicated 4- or 8-channel breast coils. The protocol included an axial T1-weighted fast spin-echo sequence (TR/TE = 622/10 ms), a fat-suppressed axial T2-weighted sequence (TR/TE = 6330/68 ms) with 5 mm slice thickness and 0.5 mm gap (matrix size 384 × 224), and a sagittal volumetric imaging sequence (VIBRANT, TR/TE = 6.1/2.9 ms) with 2 mm slice thickness (matrix size 256 × 128). Following intravenous Gd-DTPA administration (0.2 mL/kg, 2.0 mL/s), dynamic contrast-enhanced acquisitions were obtained, dynamic contrast-enhanced acquisitions were obtained, consisted of five sagittal acquisitions obtained at 90–100 s intervals, supplemented by delayed axial imaging.

To minimize the risk of contrast-induced nephrotoxicity, all CBBCT and MRI examinations were scheduled with a minimum interval of 4 h between contrast administrations.

Images interpretation

Image analysis was performed independently by two radiologists (Reader A: 5 years; Reader B: 8 years of breast imaging experience) using the BI-RADS lexicon (5th edition). Disagreements were resolved by consensus. Quantitative measurements were averaged across both readers.

Pre- and post-treatment CBBCT images were quantitatively evaluated using six parameters: length (L, cm), volume (V, cm3), density (D, HU), enhanced degree (E, HU), lesion-to-parenchyma density enhancement difference (DED, HU), and ratio (DER, %). RECIST 1.1 criteria were applied to classify responses. Tumor volume was measured using semi-automatic segmentation; density and enhancement were assessed via standardized regions of interests (ROIs) on 2.7-mm coronal reconstructions after fat-correction. The formulas for the rate of change of each parameter are detailed in Additional file 1.

NCE-CBBCT evaluated fibroglandular tissue (FGT) and calcifications, including morphology, distribution, and changes in number (categorized as decreased, stable, or increased) and extent (with > 15% increase or < − 15% decrease from baseline). CE-CBBCT assessed background parenchymal enhancement (BPE) and lesion characteristics. Vascular features were analyzed on 3D-MIP images, including: (a) ipsilateral increased vascularity (IIV), quantified as the relative increase in prominent vessels compared to the contralateral breast (graded 0-II); (b) adjuvant vascular sign (AVS), classified as grade 0 (absent), I (small vessels), or II (tortuous or enlarged vessels whose diameter ≥ 2 cm). Changes in BPE and vascular metrics were categorized as none (unchanged), mild (one-grade reduction), or marked (two-grade reduction), establishing objective metrics for response assessment (Fig. 2).

Collection of clinicopathological data

Clinical characteristics, including age, menopausal status, and treatment regimen, were prospectively recorded. Molecular subtypes were classified based on hormone receptor (HR) and HER2 status as follows: Luminal (HR-positive, HER2-negative), HER2-positive (HER2-positive regardless of HR status), and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC; negative for all markers).

Pathological response was evaluated on surgical specimens using the Japanese Breast Cancer Society grading system [20]. Responses were categorized into three tiers: (pCR (grade IIIa–IIIb), partial pathological response (pPR; grade Ib–IIb), and pathological stable disease (pSD; grade 0–Ia). For analysis, responses were dichotomized into: (i) responded (pPR + pCR) vs. non-responded (pSD), and (ii) pCR vs. non-pCR (pPR + pSD).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 and R 3.4.2. Intraclass correlation (ICC) and Cohen’s κ were used for interobserver agreement analysis. Quantitative data distribution was assessed using Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. Imaging-pathology correlations were evaluated with Pearson coefficients (PCC: < 0.25, poor; 0.25–0.49, fair; 0.50–0.74, moderate; ≥ 0.75, strong), while interobserver agreement used Cohen’s κ (< 0.40, poor; 0.40–0.59, moderate; 0.60–0.79, good; ≥ 0.80, excellent). Diagnostic performance was assessed via receiver operation characteristic (ROC) analysis. Univariate analyses (t/χ2/Fisher’s tests) identified CBBCT accuracy predictors (p < 0.05) for subsequent binary logistic regression. Treatment effects were analyzed with Wilcoxon signed-rank and McNemar tests. pCR-associated parameters (Mann–Whitney U, p < 0.05) entered multivariate models. The area under curve (AUC) comparisons used DeLong’s test with Youden-index optimized cutoffs. Categorical changes were evaluated with χ2/Fisher’s tests. All tests were two-tailed with a significance level of α = 0.05.

Results

Patient clinicopathological characteristics

In the MRI-comparison cohort (n = 41), the median age was 45.0 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 36.0–50.0); among these patients, 80.5% were premenopausal and all had invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC). In the overall cohort (n = 73), the median age was 46.0 years (IQR: 38.0–53.0), with 72.6% premenopausal patients and 75.3% IDC cases. Reproducibility analysis

ICC analysis demonstrated excellent inter-reader agreement for quantitative measurements on both pretreatment CBBCT (range: 0.822–0.966) and MRI (ICC = 0.823). Post-treatment quantitative evaluations on CBBCT also showed strong consistency (ICC range: 0.742–0.923).

Qualitative assessments exhibited good to excellent reliability for both pretreatment (κ range: 0.714–0.891) and post-treatment (κ range: 0.731–0.884) CBBCT interpretations, as detailed in Additional file 2.

CBBCT versus MRI in response assessment per RECIST 1.1

Quantitative analysis demonstrated strong positive correlations between pre- and post-treatment measurements from CBBCT and MRI (PCC = 0.820 and PCC = 0.767, respectively; Fig. 3A, 3). Both modalities exhibited moderate correlations with pathological findings (CBBCT PCC = 0.552; MRI PCC = 0.518; Fig. 3C, 3).

Evaluation of treatment response using RECIST 1.1 criteria revealed excellent agreement between CBBCT and MRI (κ = 0.809; 95% CI 0.676–0.941). Compared to pathological assessment, both imaging techniques showed good concordance (CBBCT: κ = 0.618, 95% CI 0.388–0.848; MRI: κ = 0.700, 95% CI 0.468–0.932), with detailed efficacy matrices provided in Fig. 3E–G.

Factors affecting CBBCT accuracy per RECIST 1.1

Histopathological correlation analysis revealed concordance between CBBCT assessments and pathological results in 30 cases (73.2%), with discordance observed in 11 cases (26.8%). Univariate analysis identified significant associations between CBBCT assessment accuracy and molecular subtype (p = 0.009), NAT regimen (p = 0.007), and calcification distribution (p = 0.002). No significant associations were found with age, menopausal status, clinical stage, fibroglandular tissue, background parenchymal enhancement, lesion multiplicity, or enhancement patterns (all p > 0.05).

Multivariate logistic regression identified two independent predictors of CBBCT accuracy: the use of targeted therapy within the NAT regimen (odds ratio [OR] = 0.059; 95% CI 0.006–0.554; p = 0.013) and the presence of linear or segmental calcifications (OR = 0.127; 95% CI 0.017–0.931; p = 0.042). Complete statistical results are provided in Additional file 3.

Predictive value of CBBCT quantitative parameters for treatment response

Post-treatment analysis revealed significant reductions in L, V, D, E, DED (all p < 0.05; Additional file 4). The pCR group showed significantly greater reductions in ΔL (p = 0.021), ΔV (p < 0.001), ΔE (p < 0.001), and ΔDED (p < 0.001) compared to non-pCR cases, while changes in ΔD and ΔDER did not differ significantly between groups (both p > 0.05; Fig. 5A).

Correlation between CBBCT calcification evolution and treatment response

In patients with calcifications, post-treatment morphological features and distribution patterns showed no significant change (p = 0.578 and p = 0.965, respectively). New calcifications developed in three previously calcification-free cases. Quantitatively, calcification extent decreased in 40.0% (12/30), increased in 33.3% (10/30), and remained stable in 26.7% (8/30) of cases. Similarly, calcification number decreased in 43.3% (13/30), increased in 40.0% (12/30), and was unchanged in 16.7% (5/30). A reduction in calcification extent was significantly associated with pCR (p = 0.021); all cases with increased extent were non-pCR. No association was found between change in calcification number and pCR (p = 0.489).

Reductions in extent and number predominantly involved fine or branched calcifications (p = 0.001 and p = 0.018, respectively), while increases typically exhibited non-circumscribed morphology. A reduction in calcification number correlated with linear/segmental distributions, whereas increases were associated with grouped patterns (p = 0.036). Association between CBBCT vascular/BPE dynamics and treatment response

Comparative analysis revealed significant post-NAT reductions in tumor-associated vascularity and BPE, with statistically significant decreases in IIV grade (p = 0.005), AVS grade (p = 0.001), and BPE levels (p < 0.001). While changes in IIV grade showed no significant association with pCR (p = 0.632), the pCR group demonstrated significantly greater reduction in AVS grade compared to non-pCR cases (p = 0.004). BPE analysis revealed distinct response patterns between groups: patients without pCR predominantly exhibited stable or minimally decreased BPE, whereas 35.0% of pCR patients showed marked BPE reduction, compared to only 5.7% in the non-pCR group. Discussion

To our knowledge, this pilot study represents the first systematic evaluation of CBBCT for longitudinal monitoring of NAT response in breast cancer using paired pre- and post-treatment analyses. In our exploratory cohort, CBBCT demonstrated diagnostic accuracy comparable to MRI when evaluated against RECIST 1.1 criteria. Furthermore, we established the clinical utility of multidimensional CBBCT indicators—including quantitative parameters, calcification features, vascular morphology, and BPE—for comprehensive response assessment. Notably, the quantitative parameters exhibited particularly significant predictive value.

We selected MRI as the reference standard for this comparative study based on the technical profiles of available imaging modalities. While conventional MG and ultrasound are clinically invaluable, they are limited in therapy response assessment due to factors such as poor residual lesion conspicuity [21] and the challenge of measuring non mass enhancement or distinguishing fibrosis from residual disease [22]. In contrast, the three-dimensional, contrast-enhanced nature of CBBCT is more closely aligned with MRI, making it a functionally analogous and technically appropriate benchmark for this initial evaluation of CBBCT’s diagnostic performance.

In this comparative analysis, CBBCT demonstrated diagnostic accuracy comparable to MRI for evaluating NAT response, supporting its potential as a viable alternative for patients with contraindications to MRI. When applying RECIST 1.1 criteria, however, both modalities exhibited limited efficacy in differentiating pCR from residual disease. CBBCT, in particular, demonstrated low sensitivity and a high false-negative rate for pCR detection, underscoring a fundamental constraint of relying solely on morphological assessment—a limitation also well-documented for breast MRI [10, 23]. These diagnostic challenges were influenced by tumor biology and imaging characteristics. For instance, the assessment of HER2-positive tumors was complicated by treatment-induced inflammatory reactions [24, 25] and the presence of post-therapeutic calcifications that can obscure residual tumor margins [26, 27]. To overcome these limitations, we developed an enhanced evaluation framework that incorporates multidimensional CBBCT features. The integration of quantitative enhancement parameters significantly improved diagnostic performance, increasing sensitivity for pCR identification. This approach mitigates the shortcomings of morphological criteria alone and reinforces the reliability of CBBCT for monitoring NAT response in patients for whom MRI is unsuitable.

Quantitative analysis revealed that the dynamic enhancement dynamics (ΔE) was superior to morphological changes (ΔL, ΔV) in predicting pCR, highlighting the importance of hemodynamic assessment. Both ΔE and the normalized parameter ΔDED—derived from established MRI principles [28]—were identified as independent predictors of pCR. Among these, ΔE emerged as the most clinically feasible biomarker due to its optimal balance of predictive accuracy and computational simplicity. CBBCT’s unique capacity for simultaneous evaluation of enhancement and calcifications provided additional insights. While calcification patterns often remained stable post-treatment [29], a reduction in their extent served as a reliable marker of response. Furthermore, fine or branched morphologies were associated with treatment sensitivity [30], while linear or segmental distributions—common in HER2-enriched or DCIS-abundant phenotypes—correlated with superior response to targeted therapy compared to clustered patterns [31]. Leveraging its high spatial resolution, CBBCT identified a decrease in AVS as a correlate of treatment efficacy, suggesting anti-angiogenic effects via VEGF inhibition [26, 32, 33]. A reduction in BPE during treatment also robustly predicted higher pCR rates, consistent with MRI studies [34–36]. However, the current limitation of asynchronous bilateral imaging precludes fully quantitative BPE assessment, necessitating further validation.

Although a formal cost-effectiveness analysis was beyond the scope of this trial, CBBCT may offer a favorable economic profile compared to MRI through lower direct costs, shorter examination times, and faster patient throughput [37]. Its superior diagnostic accuracy over ultrasound in complex cases could also prevent costly treatment delays. Therefore, CBBCT is positioned not as a replacement for MRI, but as a complementary, cost-efficient alternative for specific scenarios, such as for patients with MRI contraindications or in resource-limited settings. Future studies incorporating formal health-economic evaluations are warranted.

Based on our findings, we propose a phased integration pathway for CBBCT. Upon completion of NAT, CBBCT could serve as an initial assessment tool. A CBBCT result indicating a high probability of pCR would bolster clinical confidence, while a clear indication of residual disease could directly guide surgical planning. In cases of indeterminate findings, subsequent MRI would be recommended for definitive evaluation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this initial study. The modest sample size, although appropriate for an exploratory investigation, may affect generalizability of the findings and warranting validation in larger, multicenter cohorts that would also enable reliable cost-effectiveness analysis. The scarcity of mid-treatment CBBCT scans underscores the need for standardized imaging protocols to optimize assessment timing. Current radiation exposure restrictions limit serial multiphase imaging, constraining comprehensive hemodynamic analysis, future developments should optimize scanning protocols to balance radiation safety with enhanced quantitative capabilities. Finally, this analysis focused on RECIST 1.1 to provide initial evidence of CBBCT’s efficacy; a comprehensive multiparametric comparison with MRI remains an essential goal for future research.

Conclusion

CBBCT demonstrates strong concordance with MRI in evaluating NAT response using RECIST 1.1 criteria and shows good correlation with pathological outcomes, establishing its viability as an alternative imaging modality when MRI is contraindicated or unavailable. In cases involving targeted therapies or linear/segmental calcifications, multimodal imaging is advised to enhance diagnostic accuracy. The ΔE emerges as the optimal quantitative biomarker for treatment monitoring, complemented by dynamic changes in calcification extent, AVS grade, and BPE as valuable ancillary metrics.
