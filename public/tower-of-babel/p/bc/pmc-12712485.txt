Distal Bicep Tendon Repair Utilizing Knotless Retensionable All-Suture Anchors
Authors: Shawn Yeazell, Natalie Clark, Michael McNally, Sean Parker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The incidence of distal bicep tendon ruptures is low, yet surgical repair is important to restore function. The retensionable knotless distal biceps tendon repair with the all-suture anchor technique involves placing 2 knotless all-suture anchors in the radial tuberosity. Repair sutures are passed through and around the distal biceps tendon, and a tensioning strand is tightened to reduce the tendon down to the bone. One limitation of this technique is that the biomechanical strength utilizing a suture anchor technique has a lower load to failure compared to the classic cortical button technique. Despite this shortcoming, the potential advantages should not be ignored. This includes the ability to place the repair in an anatomic position and the ability to retension the construct as many times as necessary. It also minimizes potential complications, such as fracture and injury to the posterior interosseous nerve.

Distal biceps tendon ruptures most commonly occur in men over the age of 40, with an overall incidence of 1.2 of 100,000.1 The mechanism of injury is typically an eccentric force while the elbow is flexed. Complete ruptures are more common compared to partial ruptures and are associated with a popping noise, swelling, bruising around the elbow, and a visible deformity. Other than male sex and age, additional risk factors include smoking, elevated body mass index, and steroid use.2, 3, 4 The distal bicep tendon consists of a long and short head with distinct insertions on the radial tuberosity. The long head inserts more proximally and posteriorly, while the short head inserts more distally and anteriorly on the radial tuberosity. Together, they function to assist with elbow flexion and forearm supination.

Nonoperative treatment is acceptable in a low-demand patient but can result in weakness of forearm supination and elbow flexion.5 It can also result in a cosmetic reverse Popeye deformity. Tendon repair is the most effective method to restore forearm supination and elbow flexion strength. There are multiple surgical techniques and fixation methods, all with respective advantages and disadvantages. Biomechanical studies have shown higher load to failure and fixation strength with cortical buttons.6 All-suture anchor repair studies have also shown a high load to failure and fixation strength, as well as no difference in range of motion or strength.7, 8, 9

In this Technical Note, we present a distal bicep tendon repair technique utilizing a single-incision approach and 2 knotless all-suture anchors. These anchors provide subcortical fixation, eliminating the need for bicortical drilling, and the construct is retensionable.

Surgical Technique

Patient Setup

Materials necessary for the procedure include a free needle, FiberLink (Arthrex), two 2.6-mm knotless FiberTak anchors (Arthrex), and a 2.6-mm drill bit (Fig 1). The patient is positioned supine on a standard operating room table with a hand table extension (Fig 2). General anesthesia is administered, and preoperative antibiotics are given. Initial Dissection

A volar longitudinal incision is made just distal to the elbow crease over the proximal radius in the radial third of the forearm using a scalpel. Careful dissection down to the level of the fascia is performed, being mindful of the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve. Volar forearm fascia is incised; the distal bicep tendon is identified utilizing blunt dissection proximally and freed from surrounding adhesions. The devitalized tissue of the tendon stump is sharply excised, and the remaining tendon stump is tagged with a suture (Fig 3).Fig 3Superficial dissection. Deep Dissection

With the forearm held in maximal supination, the interval between the brachioradialis and pronator teres is developed utilizing blunt dissection. A small blunt Hohmann retractor can be placed medially, and an Army-Navy retractor is used laterally for clear visualization. The radial tuberosity is exposed and decorticated with a curette or a rasp (Fig 4).Fig 4Deep dissection. Anchor Placement

Two 2.6-mm hybrid and/or double knotless FiberTak suture anchors (Arthrex) are utilized in the proximal and distal aspects of the radial tuberosity (Fig 5). With the forearm held in maximal supination, the anchors are drilled and inserted through the drill guide. An alteration to the placement is that the anchors can be inserted without the drill guide to better visualize insertion depth (Video 1). Suture Passage and Tensioning

The double knotless anchors have 2 preformed knotless loops (blue and black) with respective tensioning tails. The hybrid knotless anchor has a blue preformed knotless loop (blue) and 2 black sliding limbs with needles attached. Beginning proximally in the distal bicep tendon, a FiberLink suture is passed from deep to superficial using a free needle. The looped end is kept on the deep surface of the tendon. The blue knotless loop of the proximal anchor is shuttled through the tendon using the FiberLink. The distal bicep stump is passed through the loop to create a circumferential cinch stitch. This is repeated for the second (black) knotless loop on the proximal anchor and the blue knotless loop of the distal anchor (Fig 6). Once all sutures are passed through and around the tendon stump, the tensioning tails are pulled to reduce the tendon down to its footprint on the radial tuberosity (Fig 7). The elbow can be ranged to remove any creep from the construct and retensioned. The tensioning tails of the knotless anchors are tied together. Finally, the black sliding limbs of the hybrid anchor can then be passed through the tendon using the attached free needle and tied. Excess suture is cut, and the final construct is seen (Fig 8). Closure and Postoperative Care

The tourniquet is deflated, and hemostasis is obtained. The elbow is ranged to ensure a stable construct before incision closure. The wound is irrigated and closed in layers. A sterile dressing and well-padded posterior splint are applied in 70° of flexion and neutral rotation. The splint is removed at the first postoperative visit 10 to 14 days after surgery. A hinged elbow brace is applied, limiting full extension. Patients remain in the hinged elbow brace until 6 weeks postoperatively, gradually increasing elbow range of motion, with a goal of full range of motion by 6 weeks postoperatively. Strengthening begins 12 weeks postoperatively.

Discussion

There are multiple strategies for surgical treatment of distal bicep tendon ruptures, but there is no clear consensus on the ideal fixation method. Historical fixation methods include cortical buttons, interference screws, bone tunnels, and suture anchors. A systematic review by Kodde et al.9 found no significant difference in range of motion based on the fixation methods listed previously. Assessing functional outcomes of the historical methods, previous biomechanical studies have shown equivalent time-zero strength when comparing titanium and all-suture anchors.8

The presented technique, utilizing a single-incision volar approach with 2 small-diameter all-suture anchors, has practical benefits. The risk of iatrogenic fracture is minimized, which allows for anatomic placement of the repair and reduces the risk of iatrogenic injury to the posterior interosseous nerve from intraosseous anchor deployment.6 Another advantage is the reproducibility of tensioning the repair. The knotless anchors can be retensioned after ranging the elbow, removing creep from the construct. This can be particularly useful if the repair must be completed with the elbow to a degree of flexion.

Despite the benefits, this technique is not without its limitations. There is a small learning curve with anchor insertion and suture passage for ensuring precision of the technique and its benefits. Overtensioning is a potential risk, as the knotless construct cannot be loosened once placed. In addition, as shown by Garvin et al.,10 unicortical all-suture anchors have lower ultimate load to failure compared to bicortical extramedullary fixation. Pearls and Pitfalls of the Described Technique

In conclusion, the knotless retensionable distal biceps tendon repair using a single incision is a novel technique that builds upon classically utilized techniques. Intraoperatively, it is reproducible, while minimizing the risk of complications that can occur. Further research is necessary to determine a superior technique for distal bicep tendon repair that will minimize risk and optimize outcomes.
