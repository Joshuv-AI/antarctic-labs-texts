Methods for the evaluation of corneal nerve fibres in diabetes mellitus by in vivo confocal microscopy: a scoping review
Authors: Siv A Sandvik, Tove Lise Morisbakk, Eilin Lundanes, Jorunn Lid, Neil Lagali, Vibeke Sundling
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractAimTo map, summarise and analyse the methodologies of corneal nerve fibre imaging in studies using in vivo confocal microscopy (IVCM) in diabetes and assess the potential for standardising methods and reporting.MethodsA scoping review was conducted using five databases, MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane, Web of Science and Scopus, with search terms related to IVCM, diabetes and corneal nerves. Two researchers independently screened the studies for eligibility. Relevant data were extracted and systematically analysed.ResultsOut of 1533 screened articles, 195 met the inclusion criteria. Spanning from 2000 to 2024, the studies showed high variability in image acquisition, image processing, image analysis and reporting detail. Most studies used laser scanning confocal microscopy, examining the central cornea in both eyes in patients with type 2 diabetes, manually selecting 3–6 images from each eye, and using ACCMetrics and CCMetrics for quantifying corneal nerve fibre length in mm/mm2. Many studies lacked sufficient methodological details for reproducibility.ConclusionIVCM studies of corneal nerve fibres in diabetes show substantial methodological heterogeneity and inconsistent reporting, limiting reproducibility. We propose a guideline to support standardisation and improve the reliability of future research.Keywords: Methods, Protocols & guidelines, DIABETES & ENDOCRINOLOGY, Review

Background

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a growing global health concern,1 and the most common complication is diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN), found in about half of patients with DM.2 3 The cornea provides a window for assessing thin nerve fibres in the living eye, which are part of the peripheral nervous system. By using the technique of in vivo confocal microscopy (IVCM), the morphology and nerves of the cornea can be non-invasively imaged with high resolution and precision, and the thin corneal nerve fibres can be further quantified.4 5

Several studies have explored the association between corneal nerve fibres and DPN. A meta-analysis including 38 studies and approximately 4000 participants demonstrated a significant reduction in the corneal nerve fibre density (CNFD), corneal nerve branch density (CNBD), corneal nerve fibre length (CNFL) and inferior whorl length (IWL) in subjects with DM with/without DPN versus controls, and in subjects with DM with/without DPN.6 Another meta-analysis included 13 studies and 1680 participants, reporting that CNFL, CNFD and CNBD were significantly reduced in patients with DPN compared with healthy controls; however, the authors concluded that comparison across studies is challenging due to the diversity of image acquisition and analysis methods used.7

IVCM has the potential for enabling early diagnosis of DPN, and IVCM measures have been shown to correlate with the onset and severity of DPN.8,10 However, translation of IVCM into clinical practice requires evidence-based knowledge about corneal nerves in healthy and diabetic eyes, as well as standardised methods. Although published protocols and methodological studies are available,11,18 there is no accepted gold standard for IVCM image acquisition, image selection or quantitative image analysis of corneal nerve fibres. Therefore, this scoping review aims to map, summarise and analyse the methodology of corneal nerve fibre imaging in studies using IVCM in DM subjects and relevant control groups, with an aim to determine the potential for developing standardised methods and reporting guidelines.

The inclusion criteria have been outlined in the published protocol.20 The eligibility criteria were subjects with DM and healthy controls and use of IVCM for quantitative analysis of corneal nerve fibres in the subbasal nerve plexus. Data sources included empirical and research articles published in peer-review journals, written in English, German or Scandinavian languages. The scoping review includes randomised controlled trials, controlled trials, intervention studies, experimental studies, quasi-experimental studies, observational studies with a control group, within-subject/repeated measures studies and case–control studies, study protocols and observational studies without a control group. Studies on animals, qualitative studies, literature reviews, commentaries, letters, editorials, conference papers or proceedings, opinion or discussion papers, dissertations/thesis, abstracts/presentations, grey literature and brief reports were excluded.

Search strategy

The search strategy was developed in collaboration with an academic librarian who performed the search in the following databases: MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus and Cochrane. The search was conducted from inception to 13 September 2024. The search strategy and the full search are available in onlinesupplemental files 1 2. The search was conducted with keywords from Thesaurus/Medical subject headings and text words (keywords) for “in vivo confocal microscopy” AND “cornea” AND “diabetes mellitus”.

The search results were imported to Endnote for removal of duplicates and exported to Rayyan21 for screening of titles, keywords and abstracts. The screening and selection of studies were completed in two steps (figure 1). First, two independent reviewers screened the title and abstracts (VS and SAS) to identify eligible studies. In cases of disagreement about inclusion, the reviewers discussed and reached consensus. Second, the full texts of the potentially eligible studies were divided and screened by pairs of independent reviewers (SAS and TLM, SAS and EL, and SAS and JL). Disagreement about inclusion was resolved by a third reviewer (VS).

PRISMA flow diagram illustrating the search strategy.

Data extraction

The data extraction followed a deductive approach. A template was developed in Microsoft Excel for Microsoft 365 MSO (V.2311) for extraction of key findings about the methods used for image acquisition, image processing, image selection and image analysis of the corneal nerve fibres in subbasal nerve plexus. The data extraction tool was piloted by two reviewers (VS and SAS), who extracted information from two included articles to ensure consistency and accuracy. Further, minor adjustments were made during the extraction process (SAS in consultation with VS).

Data about methods were extracted by pairs of two independent researchers (SAS and TLM), and consensus was reached. Details about the publication (author, year, journal and title), study sample, study methods and outcome variables were recorded (online supplemental file 3).

Data analysis and presentations

The extracted data were descriptively analysed and presented graphically and in frequency tables to provide an overview and understanding of published studies. Attention was given to the methods of IVCM image acquisition, image selection, image processing and image analysis to identify potential limitations and/or areas for improvements in reporting IVCM-based measures of the corneal subbasal nerves.

In the current review, the different nerve analysis tools are categorised into manually, semi-automated and fully automated, based on degree of human involvement. Manual analysis represents human tracing of the nerve fibres, semi-automated refers to human tracing assisted by a computer algorithm, and automated corresponds to fully automated tracing and quantification of the nerve fibres without human involvement in identifying nerves.

The country of the study was determined by identifying locations of the study or their ethical approval in the methods section. Combining country and author information enabled identification of collaborating research groups. This analysis made it possible to identify the authors or research groups with the most publications in this specific field of research.

Statistical methods

Frequencies and summary statistics were performed in Excel for Windows, V.2411 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, Washington, USA).

Deviations from the protocol

The protocol published previously20 included the use of risk-of-bias tools (AXIS, ROBBIN and ROB2). However, risk-of-bias tools was excluded from this scoping review as these belong to the systematic review format. The same applies to the evaluation of statistical power of the included articles.19 Uncertainties regarding the content in the included papers were treated as missing information, and the authors were not contacted for clarifications.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Discussion

This scoping review provides a detailed and systematic overview of the methods of imaging acquisition, image selection and quantitative analysis of corneal nerves using IVCM among subjects with DM and relevant control groups.

The most striking feature was the high variability, lack of rigour and inconsistency in applied methods and reporting. The absence of rigorous methodology may affect the reported quantitative parameter values and lead to uncertainty when comparing studies and their reported results and conclusions. Therefore, methodological details must be considered when interpreting and comparing findings across studies.

Image quality

Accurate visualisation and quantification of nerve fibres are dependent on image quality. Laser scanning confocal microscopy was the most frequently used image acquisition method. In accordance with prior evidence indicating superiority of this imaging method compared with slit scanning confocal microscopy or tandem scanning confocal microscopy, laser scanning confocal microscopy provides higher image contrast and resolution enabling enhanced nerve fibre detection.214

Only one in four studies reported information about the number of microscope operators and/or provided details about the operator’s experience level. Previous studies have highlighted operator experience as a critical factor influencing image quality and the sensitivity and specificity of assessment; hence, specification of the operator’s experience has been recommended.215 216 It is important to consider the reliability of the data collection when interpreting the value and meaning of the study results. With multiple operators, which is comparable to a clinical setting, variability between operators exists. Agreement between operators is termed inter-rater reliability, and, according to McHugh, with multiple data collectors, inter-rater reliability should be reported. Within a single observer, it is also of interest to know the intra-rater reliability, which is whether the operator consistently achieves the same result.217 Failure to report the instrument used and to provide information about the image operator limits critical assessment of the image acquisition method, which in turn impacts the reliability of the findings.

Corneal region of image acquisition

One in four studies did not provide information about the corneal region imaged. This information is highly important for the quantitative outcome and to be able to replicate the study. One IVCM image represents less than 1% of the total subbasal nerve plexus.18 Multiple images are therefore necessary for quantitative analysis to differentiate healthy from pathologic nerves, as shown by Badian and colleagues124. They did not find a clinically significant difference in CNFL between subjects with DM compared with healthy controls when analysing a repeatable region of interest with dimensions 650×1000 µm124, whereas a difference was noted across a larger area of the same subbasal nerve plexus.127 The corneal nerve parameters are thus highly sensitive to the region of image acquisition. Previous studies have shown that the CNFD across the corneal surface is inhomogeneous,218,221 even in the central cornea.218 The nerve density is the highest in the inferior whorl, followed by the central cornea, and lowest in the periphery.218 Only 14% of the studies included images of the inferior whorl. The inferior whorl has a unique appearance and is used as an anatomic landmark and as a reference point for consistent image acquisition.16 Without the inclusion of a specific landmark, it is difficult to ensure the same region of interest is examined across different subjects. Kim et al222 demonstrated this by employing an Amsler chart fixation target to enhance the standardisation of image acquisition procedures. Although aiming for repeatable fixation by using a white fixation light overlapping the red laser light, they reported poor reliability for corneal nerve measurements using a single image.222 The method did not apply corneal structural landmarks as reference points for image acquisition, which may have improved repeatability; however, the study authors suggested calculating the mean value of a given parameter across all sampled locations to improve reproducibility and achieve more clinically meaningful results.

Using multiple images and referencing these to a specific landmark may increase the repeatability and reliability of corneal image acquisition. Conversely, lack of information about the imaged corneal region limits both the ability to interpret the results and compare results to other studies.

Image selection

As many as 18% of the studies did not provide details concerning the image selection process, potentially leading to selection bias.223 Among studies providing such information, the number of raw images acquired for further selection ranged from 3 to 1400. Further, image selection criteria were applied to a varying extent. Few studies randomised the image selection. The impact of different selection criteria or area covered has been explored by multiple studies1852 124 222 224,228

First, studies apply varying criteria for nerve fibre density to guide image selection, this may impact the reliability of results. Studies using the same analysis software but different selection criteria and varying numbers of images for analysis also demonstrate varying inter-observer reliability.229 230

Kalteniece et al230 applied a detailed image selection protocol selecting 3–6 high-quality images from each eye with subjectively high, medium and low nerve fibre density. The nerve fibres were vertically oriented within the central cornea, and the authors used the orientation of the nerve fibres both to facilitate repeatable image acquisition and image selection. The study included four different observers selecting images and found an interclass correlation (ICC) (95% CI) of 0.95 (0.95 to 0.98), 0.93 (0.93 to 0.97) and 0.96 (0.95 to 0.98) for CNFL, CNFD and CNBD, respectively. By contrast, Ostrowski et al229 selected a single image, the image with the highest density and technical quality, and found lower ICC (95% CI) of 0.75 (0.58 to 0.86), 0.68 (0.48 to 0.81) and 0.40 (0.13 to 0.61) for CNFL, CNFD and CNBD, respectively. The two studies highlight that despite using the same analysis tool, the image selection criteria and extent can have a large impact on the reliability of the study.

Schaldemose et al224 explored the difference between a randomised selection of 8 images, 4 images with vertical nerve fibres and 2 images from diagonal left and right nerve orientation compared with a non-randomised selection of 3–6 images with low, medium and high nerve fibre density.224 The authors found a statistically significant difference in CNFL between methods. We propose that the reported difference may be explained by the different number of images and the location for sampling. The non-randomised selection of 3–6 images had vertical orientation, indicating sampling of central corneal nerves, whereas the randomised selection of 8 images, including obliquely oriented nerve fibres, suggests sampling of both central and more peripheral corneal nerves, supporting Schaldemose’s emphasis on the importance of providing clear guidelines for image selection.224

Second, the total number of images selected from one eye for analysis varied between 1–12 and 1–6 for the central cornea and inferior whorl regions, respectively. The number of images selected has an impact on the quantitative outcome of the study. As a single image represents only 0.4 mm × 0.4 mm (0.16 mm2) of the cornea,218 selecting multiple images covering a larger area is required to reduce the variability of the nerve fibre density.224 Parissi and colleagues225 explored the difference for maximum error in CNFL between the mean CNFL of all preselected images (mean 4.3 images) compared with randomly selecting 1–4 images of the preselected images, with preselection based on strict image quality criteria. The authors found a decrease in maximum error from 4.7% to 1.2% by increasing the number of randomly selected images for analysis from 1 to 4 images, concluding that the number of images influences the impact of the inhomogeneous nerve fibre density and single image size on the outcome measurement.225 The optimal number of images for analysis has been previously explored, and Vagenas and colleagues227 suggested that five randomly selected images from the central cornea, not overlapping more than 20%, compared with a reference standard represented by 16 central images, gives an average CNFL within 13% of the true mean 80% of the time.227 Whereas Kheirkhah and colleagues226 concluded that CNFL (mm/mm2) measured based on three single images compared with a wide field mosaic covering an area of 1.29±0.64 mm2 did not differ significantly.226 In contrast, Allgeier and colleagues suggest that collecting images covering at least 1.5 mm2 of the central cornea is required to achieve reliable measurements of CNFL.13 Lagali and colleagues18 compared analysing 1–20 single non-overlapping images (0.16–3.2 mm2) with full mosaics. They found a large difference in CNFL even when up to 10 single images (1.6 mm2) were randomly selected for analysis compared with the full mosaic analysis.18 Hence, these studies reflect two factors affecting reliable measurements, the inhomogeneous nerve fibre structure and the area and size of image acquisition. There are mixed findings regarding the optimal total area for analysis and the advantages of creating mosaic images. The preferred method remains an open question. Mosaic imaging allows for the analysis of a larger corneal area, which can enhance diagnostic accuracy, whereas single images are more practical for clinical use due to their simplicity. Although the time required to acquire mosaic images has traditionally been a limitation, advancements in extended HRT software now make it feasible to obtain mosaics within a clinically acceptable timeframe. This remains an active area of research. Determining the most suitable method and the ideal area for analysis warrants further investigation.

In summary, the image selection process has great importance for both repeatability and reliability of the study. Considering the existing research that highlights the variability resulting from the different methods applied, it is essential to account for selection criteria when critically assessing and comparing conclusions related to normal findings. We suggest that selection bias exists in most studies, and a detailed description of image selection methods should be reported, to enable reproducibility and risk-of-bias assessment.

Analysis of images

Three-quarters of studies referred to an analysis protocol, and 1 in 10 studies did not provide any information about the analysis tool. The analysis tool may introduce analysis bias to the results. Several comparative studies have investigated the differences and intercorrelation between analysis tools. Automated ACCMetrics has been shown to underestimate the quantitative nerve fibre parameters compared with manual (CCMetrics) and semi-automated methods (NeuronJ)166 199 229 231 232 because of the inability of ACCMetrics to detect the low contrast, fainter nerve fibres against the background.229 Still, the correlation between automated and manual ACCMetrics and CCMetrics has been reported to be high. CNFL is the quantitative outcome variable with the best correlation across the different tools (r>0.8).94 166 232 233 CNFL is also considered to be the most reliable parameter for beginners using manual CCMetrics,231 providing the ability to differentiate between groups and preserve the relationship to neuropathy status.166

Quantitative corneal subbasal nerve outcome variables

CNFL was the most frequently reported outcome variable in papers assessed within the current review. The outcome variable itself is potentially biased due to the abovementioned differences in methodology related to image acquisition, image selection and quantitative analysis. This raises the need for careful interpretation of the value itself.

CNFL has shown the best repeatability in multiple studies using different analysis tools and selection criteria52 143 229 230 and has the most consistent definition across studies. CNFL has been defined as the cumulative length of all nerve fibres within the total area of analysis in mm/mm2. In contrast to CNFL, CNBD and CNFD are based on a discrimination between ‘main’ nerve fibres and ‘branches’ which gives a lower degree of correlation, r=0.609 and r=0.471, respectively, compared with CNFL (r>0.8).233 Consequently, studies publishing normative values and cut-off points need to be seen together with the methods used, as they may vary. Based on the methods illustrated by the studies by Parissi and Tavakoli,225 234 Parissi reported mean CNFL (SD) varies from 17.2 (5.4) to 19.8 (4.7) mm/mm2 across different age groups. Tavakoli reported median CNFL varies from 19.97 to 26.43 mm/mm2, 0.05 quantile 12.48 to 19.19 mm/mm2, respectively, for healthy subjects aged <16 to 61+. Hence, for clinical use, normative values need to be based on valid studies using the same methodology.

Nerve fibre tortuosity is the outcome parameter with the most varied definitions and interpretations. Some authors defined this parameter qualitatively using a grading scale and others mathematically.196235,237 Lagali et al235 recommend a definition specifying the tortuosity based on short-range and long-range tortuosity assessing the most tortuous nerve in an image, for increased repeatability. From a biological perspective, nerve tortuosity is suggested to be an indicator of nerve degeneration and regeneration.238 Further, a strong correlation between progression of DM and the grade of tortuosity has been demonstrated.134 Without a consistent or standardised definition or reliable assessment, however, tortuosity suffers from both poor repeatability52 and reliability.

One or two eyes for analysis

The current review revealed that almost half of the studies collected images from both eyes. However, almost a third did not provide information about the number of eyes used for analysis and there was uncertainty in how many images were selected for analysis from each eye. The number of eyes used for analysis is important for interpretation, repeatability and reliability of the study. The correlation between both eyes of the same subject needs to be considered if both eyes are eligible and contribute to the statistical analysis, and appropriate statistical correction must be used.239 If the inter-eye correlation is not taken into consideration, p values and CIs will be artificially reduced.240 Averaging information collected from both eyes is a statistically valid method; however, it reduces the power of the study and results in less precise estimates, and the poorer the correlation between the eyes the more information is lost.240 To achieve a higher power, as well as a more efficient study, both eyes can be used for analysis, handling the eyes as a ‘within subjects’ factor and using an appropriate statistical test for the relationship based on the aim of the study.239 240

The inter-eye correlation of subbasal nerves has been explored by several studies finding a high correlation between the two eyes for the same subject.18 225 238 However, the correlation differs between studies and study samples. It is recommended to provide a rationale for choosing one or both eyes, and if one eye is used for analysis an explanation for the selection criteria is needed.

In summary, the method of image analysis strongly impacts the quantitative outcome parameters and consequently, the study results. Therefore, comparison across studies requires critical review of the methodology and assessment of the risk of bias. Insufficient information about methods limits replication, risk of bias assessment and study validity. We propose CNFL as the outcome variable most robust to bias, but methodological details can still impact CNFL significantly. Further, for assessing CNFD and CNBD, a clear and reproducible description of differentiation between main nerve fibres and nerve branches is required.

Publishing landscape

This scoping review has shown that most research in the field of diabetes and corneal nerves has been conducted in Europe and Australia by a small number of authors and research groups. Although the use of IVCM remains limited in many countries, its adoption is steadily increasing. However, there is currently no consensus or gold standard protocol for image acquisition, processing, selection or analysis. Furthermore, substantial methodological heterogeneity and inconsistent reporting hinder reproducibility. Existing protocols12 22 222 230 241 242 may serve as a checklist, but dedicated reporting guidelines are needed to enhance reproducibility, support the assessment of clinical validity and facilitate comparisons across studies

Summary of findings

There was a high methodological variability, and lack of information across all aspects of IVCM methodology. This included image acquisition (image quality), image selection and image analysis methods, all of which affect the repeatability and reliability of the measurement and screening efficacy. Without sufficient details and description, studies suffer from a lack of reproducibility. Due to the differences between the various studies, to have a protocol and consistent image acquisition, image selection and image analysis is recommended and beneficial for better agreement and replicability.225

Proposed recommendation of reporting details in the methodology in research studies evaluating the subbasal nerve plexus by IVCM and future research

To ensure transparency and reproducibility of future research quantifying corneal subbasal nerve parameters in DM, authors should report detailed information/protocols for image acquisition, image selection and image analysis in within the methods or supplementary methods sections of publications. We propose a reporting guideline in table 1 as a recommendation to improve the quality, consistency and reproducibility of future studies.

Strengths and limitations of this study

Strengths of this scoping review include the use of the updated JBI guidelines for scoping review,19 and PRISMA-ScR guidelines, collaboration with an academic librarian and the transparent approach in inclusion and data extraction based on a previously published protocol.20 Nevertheless, some limitations need to be addressed. The aim of this scoping review was limited to methods used in IVCM evaluation of corneal nerve fibres in subjects with DM and relevant control groups. Findings of inconsistent methodologies might not be representative for the use of IVCM in other study populations. The search strategy for this scoping review was restricted to five databases and articles written in English, German and the Scandinavian languages. Finally, a scoping review is not as extensive as a meta-analysis, and risk-of-bias assessment is not provided in this review.

Conclusion

Mapping and summarising methods over two decades of studies using IVCM evaluating corneal nerve fibres in subjects with DM and relevant control groups revealed a high variability and diversity of methods. Detailed reporting of methods in imaging, image selection and image analysis is lacking. These deficiencies increase the likelihood of bias and impact the reproducibility of studies. IVCM examination protocols, image selection and analyses require better standardisation and detailed reporting of methods. We provide a guideline for reporting and methodology, aiming to improve the reproducibility of methods and reliability of results in future studies.
