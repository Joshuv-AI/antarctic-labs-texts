Association of Limited Health Literacy With Clinical and Patient‐Reported Outcomes in Individuals With Systemic Lupus Erythematosus
Authors: Mithu Maheswaranathan, Andrea D. Boan, Viswanathan Ramakrishnan, Hetlena Johnson, Jillian Rose, Clara L. Dismuke‐Greer, Jim C. Oates, Leonard E. Egede, Edith Williams
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveHealth literacy is an important social determinant of health, with limited health literacy associated with worse health outcomes. This study examined the associations between limited health literacy with patient‐reported outcomes and disease activity/damage among 267 Black women with active systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) enrolled in the Peer Approaches to Lupus Self‐Management (PALS) program.MethodsThe three‐item Chew Health Literacy Screening was used to dichotomize those reporting in the “limited” range on any item with outcomes compared via generalized linear models. Baseline surveys and assessments obtained at study entry as part of the PALS study were used. Primary outcomes included disease activity and lupus damage; other secondary outcomes included patient activation, self‐efficacy, physician/patient communication, and quality of life.ResultsThe study included 267 Black women with SLE. In covariate‐adjusted analyses, participants with limited health literacy (88 [33%]) were more likely to have lower patient activation (Patient Activation Measure P < 0.0001), lower self‐efficacy (Lupus Self‐Efficacy P < 0.0001), higher lupus damage (self‐administered Brief Index of Lupus Damage P = .016), higher disease activity (Systemic Lupus Activity Questionnaire symptom severity P = 0.006), and worse physician/patient communication (patient‐centered care P < 0.0001) compared to those with adequate health literacy. Those with limited health literacy also reported worse lupus quality of life (P = 0.0004) and greater levels of stress (Perceived Stress Scale‐4 P < 0.0001) and were 2.4 times more likely to have probable major depression (Patient Health Questionnaire Depression Scale‐8 of ≥10 P = 0.004) and probable anxiety disorder (General Anxiety Disorder‐7 of ≥10 P = 0.007) compared to those with adequate health literacy.ConclusionBlack women with SLE and limited health literacy have worse clinical outcomes and represent a particularly vulnerable population with significantly disparate health outcomes. These findings suggest health literacy and complexities of managing SLE may impair clinical care in multiple domains, ultimately contributing to higher disease activity and death/damage, and are important to address in clinical care and future interventions in patients with SLE.

INTRODUCTION

Health literacy is defined as the process of obtaining, communicating, processing, and understanding basic health information and services needed to make appropriate decisions about one's health. 1 Limited health literacy is associated with worse clinical outcomes including increased hospitalizations and readmissions, greater use of emergency care services, and higher disease activity. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 Recent studies have demonstrated limited health literacy and numeracy are associated with worse patient‐reported outcomes and higher disease activity in individuals with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). 6 , 7 However, the exact mechanisms by which limited health literacy may contribute to worse outcomes in patients with SLE have not been entirely elucidated.

Self‐efficacy and patient motivation are factors that have been identified as contributory in the relationship between health literacy and health outcomes. 8 Limited health literacy is associated with poorer self‐management behaviors. 9 , 10 The Peer Approaches to Lupus Self‐Management (PALS) program aimed to determine efficacy of a peer mentorship intervention in Black women with SLE on disease self‐management and health‐related quality of life (HRQOL). 11 Upon enrollment into the program, a variety of measures are obtained including health literacy, patient activation by the Patient Activation Measure (PAM), and HRQOL by the lupus quality of life (LUP‐QOL) questionnaire. Other factors that may contribute as mediating factors between limited health literacy and poor outcomes include navigation of the health care system and patient–provider communication. 8 The purpose of this study was to determine associations between limited health literacy with clinical factors and patient‐reported outcomes (such as patient activation and self‐efficacy) that may help explain associations, and potential mediating factors, between limited health literacy and more distal clinical outcomes. Additionally, we evaluated the association between limited health literacy and clinical outcomes (disease activity and lupus damage) as well as psychosocial factors (quality of life, anxiety, and depression).

PATIENTS AND METHODS

This study used baseline data obtained from the PALS study, 11 a randomized controlled trial to examine whether a culturally tailored peer mentoring intervention improves disease self‐management, disease activity, and HRQOL in Black women with SLE. The study population included Black women with active SLE who met criteria based on the 1997 ACR 12 revised classification criteria for individuals with SLE. English‐speaking participants seen at clinics at the Medical University of South Carolina (MUSC) were recruited via physician referrals for enrollment in the PALS program. An eligible pool of 520 individuals were targeted using direct mailings; primary reasons for nonenrollment included lack of response or individuals not interested in participating. For this study, we used baseline surveys and assessments obtained at study entry. The MUSC Institutional Review Board approved this study (PRO00080875). Primary outcomes included disease activity and lupus damage; secondary outcomes included patient activation, self‐efficacy, physician/patient communication, and quality of life.

Measures used

Health literacy

The Chew Health Literacy Screening Survey 13 is a three‐item instrument used to identify patients with potential problems with health literacy, previously validated against the Short Test of Functional Health Literacy in Adults. 14 Responses were recorded on a five‐point Likert scale for all three items (see Table 1 for item questions and scoring). Participants who responded on any of the bottom three options for each question item were classified as having limited health literacy. Responses were dichotomized as reporting “limited on any” item, indicating limited health literacy based on prior reports using this instrument in the population with SLE. 6

Patient activation

Participants’ active behavior and engagement in managing their health and health care was assessed using the PAM survey. 15 , 16 The PAM is a well‐validated, highly reliable measure of a patient's knowledge, skill, and confidence for health care self‐management of chronic illness measured on a continuum. Higher “activated” patients are thought to better understand the importance of a proactive role in managing one's health and typically display the skills and confidence to do so. 15 , 16 The 10‐item version of the PAM was used for this study, with responses scored on a four‐point Likert scale from 1 (“disagree strongly”) to 4 (“agree strongly”). Scores were summed to create a composite score ranging from 10 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater activation. Questions that targeted health literacy tasks were further dichotomized by responses reporting “disagree” or “strongly disagree” versus “agree” or “strongly agree” for three items: (1) “I know what each of my prescribed medications do,” (2) “I am confident that I can tell a doctor concerns I have even when he/she does not ask,” and (3) “I am confident I can figure out solutions when new problems arise with my health.”

HRQOL

The LUP‐QOL questionnaire was used to assess quality of life, which incorporates the Medical Outcomes Study Short Form 36 Health Survey and the Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy – Fatigue, which are reliable and valid instruments that are frequently used in quality‐of‐life studies in people with SLE. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 The patient‐reported overall impact of lupus on patients’ QOL was assessed via a 17‐item questionnaire with scores scaled to a range of 0 to 100 for each item and then averaged to create a composite score of 0 to 100; higher scores indicate worse HRQOL and thus SLE having a greater negative impact on the participant's life. The LUP‐QOL questionnaire single question of “In general, would you say your health is:” with responses recorded as a five‐point Likert scale from poor to excellent was also included for descriptive purposes.

Disease activity: Systemic Lupus Activity Questionnaire

Patient self‐reported disease activity was assessed via the Systemic Lupus Activity Questionnaire (SLAQ), 21 which includes three scoring components. A single Patient Global Assessment question about presence and severity of lupus activity (flare) over the past month was dichotomized as reporting a flare (mild, moderate, or severe) versus not reporting a flare. A questionnaire on 22 specific symptoms of disease activity, each measured on a 0 (“no problem”) to 3 (“severe problem”) Likert scale, was assessed via a summed specific symptom severity score (0 to 66) and a count of positive responses for specific symptoms score (0 to 22, with reports of mild to severe problems considered a positive symptom response on each of the 22 items). A single Numerical Rating Scale to rate disease activity on a scale from 0 to 10 over the past three months was also used for comparisons, with higher scores indicating greater disease activity.

Anxiety

Anxiety was assessed using the General Anxiety Disorder‐7 (GAD‐7), a seven‐item validated screening tool for patients’ generalized anxiety disorder and the severity it. 22 Response options ranged from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”), scored on a 0 to 3 Likert scale. Scores were summed for the seven items (0 to 21), with 21 indicating the most severe anxiety. A GAD‐7 score ≥10 meets the criteria for major anxiety disorder, analyzed as proportion meeting criteria for this analysis.

Depression

The Patient Health Questionnaire Depression Scale‐9 (PHQ‐9) is a brief, well‐validated screening questionnaire for depression, scored as 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”). 23 We used a modified version of PHQ‐9 that does not include the question about suicidality, making it the Patient Health Questionnaire Depression Scale‐8 (PHQ‐8). Summed scores range from 0 to 21, with 21 indicating the most severe depression. A score of ≥10 is indicative of major depression, analyzed as proportion meeting this threshold.

Perceived stress scale

The Perceived Stress Scale‐4 (PSS‐4) is a four‐item scale that assesses the degree to which the respondent finds situations stressful. 24 Responses range from “never” to “very often” in relation to feelings surrounding events in the previous month. Items were scored 0 to 4 and summed (range 0 to 16), with higher scores indicating greater stress.

Coping

Coping and ability to manage aspects of SLE (self‐efficacy) were assessed by the “Lupus Self‐Efficacy Scale,” which was adapted from the Arthritis Self‐Efficacy Scale Other Symptoms Subscale and reworded to reflect lupus rather than arthritis. 25 The Lupus Self‐Efficacy Scale consists of six items measuring confidence in one's ability to manage pain, fatigue, frustration, and other aspects of having SLE. 26 Respondents rated certainty on a scale of 0 (“very uncertain”) to 100 (“very certain”) in 10‐point increments for each item. The six items are prorated averaged to form a coping composite score, with a score of 100 indicating the greatest self‐efficacy. If >25% of items were missing, the questionnaire was considered invalid, and thus, composite scores were not calculated; otherwise, prorated averages were calculated based on completed data.

Patient‐centered care

Patient‐centered care was measured using seven questions from the Picker‐Commonwealth Survey of Patient‐Centered Ambulatory Care, measuring patient experiences with the physician. Response options were recorded as “always,” “usually,” “sometimes,” and “never,” scored on a 1 to 4 scale, with lower scores indicating ideal care. 27 Continuous scores across the seven items were summed for analyses, with lower scores representing more patient‐centered care (range 7–28). Three questions of interest related to health literacy and physician communication were explored further for this analysis. Each question was dichotomized as a “problem experience” if they responded “usually,” “sometimes,” or “never” (vs “always” representing ideal care) for the following three items: (1) “When you ask questions, do you get answers that are understandable?” (2) “Does your physician take enough time to answer your questions?” and (3) “Do you get as much medical information as you want from your physician?” based on Keating et al. 27 Satisfaction with care was also assessed based the proportion reporting “very dissatisfied” (vs “very satisfied”) on care received from their primary care physician over the past 12 months.

Lupus damage by self‐administered Brief Index of Lupus Damage

The 26‐item self‐administered Brief Index of Lupus Damage (SA‐BILD) was developed as a patient‐reported measurement of lupus disease damage designed to quantify cumulative organ damage due to SLE regardless of attribution. As a proxy for morbidity, we used the SA‐BILD measure of damage‐scoring algorithm based on the Yazdany method; scores range from 0 to 34, with higher score indicating greater damage. 28

Statistical analysis

Baseline sample characteristics were described as frequencies (in percent), with health literacy compared using chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests. Generalized linear models were used to examine the associations of limited (vs adequate) health literacy status with clinical and patient‐reported outcomes in unadjusted and adjusted models. Covariate‐adjusted models controlled for low education (<12 years), income below poverty based on <125% of federal poverty guidelines (<$15,000, ≥$15,000, and missing), and age group (<25, 25–55, and ≥ 55 years old). Model‐derived estimates are presented as least squares means or predicted probability (denoted by percentage) and SE, with each outcome treated in a separate model. Model assumptions were assessed diagnostically. Violations to these assumptions were modeled under an assumed lognormal distribution to achieve normality or homoscedasticity. Omega method was used to back transform model–scaled estimates to original scales for interpretation purposes. Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute).

RESULTS

Among the 267 Black women with SLE included in this study, 88 (33%) were classified as having “limited” health literacy. When comparing sociodemographic characteristics, participants with limited health literacy were significantly more likely to be younger in age, have education of less than 12 years, and have an annual household income below 125% of federal poverty guidelines (Table 2) compared to those with adequate health literacy. Those with limited health literacy were also significantly less likely to be currently working than those with adequate health literacy. There were no significant differences between the limited and adequate health literacy groups with respect to marital status, having health insurance, or residential status. With respect to patient‐reported general health, a higher proportion of participants with limited health literacy reported their health quality as “fair” or “poor” compared to those with adequate health literacy (61.4% vs 40.8%, respectively; P = 0.0002). In this study, health literacy was measured by the Chew Health Literacy questionnaire, 13 which asks participants to self‐report their ability to perform tasks requiring health literacy with response options of “none,” “a little,” “some,” “most,” and “all of the time.” Approximately 20% of participants were in the limited range for having problems learning about their medical condition because of difficulty understanding written information, 25% needed assistance in reading hospital materials, and 12% were limited in their confidence or ability to complete medical forms (Table 1).

Patient activation

In unadjusted analysis, participants with limited health literacy had an overall lower composite score on the PAM of 29.7 compared to 32.8 in those with adequate health literacy (P < 0.0001, Table 3). As mentioned, we intentionally selected three items within the PAM that queried health behaviors that would require significant health literacy skills to complete in order to assess group differences. A significantly higher proportion of participants with limited health literacy stated they did not know what their prescribed medications do, were not confident they could tell the doctor concerns they had even when they did not ask, and were not confident they could figure out solutions when new problems arose with their health (all P < 0.05). Of these items, the strongest difference was observed in confidence to tell a doctor concerns they had, with participants with limited health literacy being almost four times more likely to report lack of confidence in speaking to their doctor regarding these concerns compared to those with adequate health literacy (odds ratio 3.85, 95% confidence interval 1.1–13.6).

In the multivariable model, after adjusting for education, income, and age, participants with limited health literacy were still more likely to have lower patient activation than those with adequate health literacy (P < 0.0001, Table 4). Though overall patient activation scores were lower in the limited health literacy group, the specific questions of knowing what each of the prescribed medications do and confidence to be able to tell a doctor concerns even when they did not ask were no longer significant in the adjusted model; however, having confidence to figure out solutions when new problems arose with their health remained significant (P = 0.041).

Self‐efficacy

In unadjusted analysis, participants with limited health literacy had an overall lower coping composite score on the Lupus Self‐Efficacy Other Symptoms Subscale compared to those with adequate health literacy (51.1 vs 64.0, P < 0.0001; Table 3). After adjusting for education, income, and age, this association remained significant (Table 4).

Patient‐centered care

For the patient‐centered care measure, we examined the overall total composite score as well as the percentage of patients reporting a problem experience on specific items that were related to health literacy. In unadjusted analyses, participants with limited health literacy had worse patient‐centered care scores compared to those with adequate health literacy (11.95 vs 10.3, P = 0.0002, Table 3). Those with limited health literacy were significantly more likely than those with adequate health literacy to report problems getting answers that were understandable (52.3% vs 31.5%, P = 0.0002) and reported issues with their physician taking enough time to answer their questions (39.1% vs 21.8%, P = 0.0037); however, no significant difference in obtaining as much medical information as wanted from their physician was noted between groups. Those with limited health literacy were also more likely to report being “very dissatisfied” on care received from their primary care provider over the past 12 months compared to those with adequate health literacy (16.5% vs 6.3%, P = 0.0117).

After adjusting for education, income, and age, the associations for cumulative score and specific items remained significant between groups (Table 4). Additionally, differences in the ability to obtain information from the physician was significant in adjusted analyses (P = 0.0236). Those with limited health literacy were nearly three times more likely to report problems in getting answers that were understandable when asking questions and issues with their physicians taking enough time to answer questions compared to those with adequate health literacy in adjusted analyses.

SLAQ disease activity and SA‐BILD

With respect to disease activity as measured by the SLAQ, those with limited health literacy had significantly higher specific symptoms severity score compared to participants with adequate health literacy (23.2 vs 18.2, P=0.001, Table 3), with significance persisting in covariate‐adjusted models (Table 4). However, no significant group differences in the Numeric Rating Scale were noted. Participants with limited health literacy also had higher lupus damage as measured by the SA‐BILD score (3.3 vs 2.3, P = 0.0013; Table 3), with significance remaining in adjusted analyses (Table 4).

Psychosocial factors/quality of life

Participants with limited health literacy reported significantly worse LUP‐QOL (P = 0.0002) as measured by the LUP‐QOL assessment overall impact and significantly greater levels of stress on the PSS‐4 (<0.0001) as compared to those with adequate health literacy (Table 3). As well, those with limited health literacy were 2.2 to 2.4 times more likely to have probable major depression (PHQ‐8 ≥ 10) and probable anxiety disorder (GAD‐7 ≥ 10) compared to those with adequate health literacy. After adjusting for education, income, and age group, these differences remained significant (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

We demonstrated that Black women with SLE and limited health literacy were significantly more likely to have worse patient‐reported and clinical outcomes across multiple domains, including lower patient activation, lower self‐efficacy, higher likelihood of anxiety and depression, and higher lupus damage and disease activity compared to those with adequate health literacy. Previous studies have demonstrated similar results, including association of limited health literacy with worse patient‐reported outcomes 6 and limited numeracy with higher disease activity 7 in patients with SLE. However, our study adds to existing literature by providing some novel insight into potential variables that may mediate the pathway between limited health literacy and adverse outcomes.

The Paasche‐Orlow and Wolf Model (Figure 1) offers a theoretical framework of causal pathways that could explain well‐described associations between limited health literacy and clinical outcomes, influenced along a continuum of health care. 8 Three categories are mentioned as areas in which health literacy may interact with outcomes: (1) access and use of health care, (2) patient–provider relationship, and (3) self‐care. This framework acknowledges causal mechanisms between health literacy and outcomes are also due to health system characteristics in addition to patient‐level factors. Examples of the individual‐ and system‐level factors within the framework include patient–provider interaction, self‐efficacy, motivation, problem‐solving, and communication. Patients with limited health literacy were more likely to have lower patient activation, lower self‐efficacy, and report worse patient–provider communication. Although we did not pursue a formal mediation analysis, these results suggest that these factors may be in the causal pathway between limited health literacy and adverse clinical and patient‐reported outcomes and the potential ways to intervene with future intervention development. Future studies can explore mediation analyses to further investigate how these variables may mediate the relationship between health literacy and clinical outcomes.

Paasche‐Orlow and Wolf model illustrating the causal pathways between health literacy and health outcomes.

There were no statistically significant differences between the limited and adequate health literacy group for questions on the PAM related to knowing what prescribed medications do and confidence to tell a doctor their concerns. However, significant group differences related to physician communication in the patient‐centered care survey were noted, with those with limited health literacy more likely to report difficulty in (1) getting answers to questions that were understandable, (2) having the physician take enough time to answer questions, and (3) getting as much medical information as they wanted from their physician, compared to those with adequate health literacy. These results suggest significant efforts are needed to improve how physicians and rheumatology providers communicate with patients and spend time in helping clarify misunderstandings using an approach that is sensitive to health literacy and clear communication. These findings suggest, at least based on patient perceptions, that there are less questions related to medication indications and expressing concerns to a provider and more issues related to provider communication.

Previous research has demonstrated that internal medicine physicians commonly overestimate patients’ health literacy levels, 29 rarely consider health literacy when providing care, 30 and infrequently use clear health communication techniques or assess understanding. 31 , 32 Our findings suggest patients with limited health literacy may be having difficulty understanding provider instructions and directions, a particular problem given the intricacies of rheumatologic disease management, complex medication regimens, and limited time during clinic visits. Strategies for improving patient–provider communication include using plain language, limiting the amount of information, providing recommendations in specific action‐oriented steps, and assessing comprehension. 33 Novel education formats have been implemented in rheumatology treatment, including a patient‐facing dashboard to display patient‐reported outcomes in individuals with rheumatoid arthritis and an education event for patients with lupus. 34 , 35 However, further work is needed on ways of improving the communication between patients and providers within the clinic encounter itself.

Limitations of the study include that the health literacy measure used is a brief self‐report scale, which may limit a more comprehensive evaluation of health literacy and potentially underreport the true extent of limited health literacy. Because many patients with SLE experience brain fog and cognitive dysfunction, it is possible some patients may have experienced survey fatigue or misinterpreted some of the questions in answering; additionally, these questions have not been formally validated in patients with SLE. Finally, this cohort was comprised of all Black women and were all English speaking, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other racial and gender groups and non‐English speakers. However, this study nevertheless provides evidence of the adverse patient‐reported and clinical outcomes that are associated with limited health literacy and offers plausible strategies that could be implemented to mitigate these negative effects. For example, a higher proportion of patients with limited health literacy stated they did not know what their prescribed medications do and were not confident they could figure out solutions when new problems arose with their health. This suggests that providing education in the clinic visit using plain language and clear communication could help with improving understanding of medication intent and may lead to improved downstream clinical outcomes, such as a higher adherence to receiving medication. This study offers unique and novel insights into associations of limited health literacy with patient‐reported and clinical outcomes, as well as potential mediators that may explain how limited health literacy contributes to more distal adverse health‐related outcomes.
