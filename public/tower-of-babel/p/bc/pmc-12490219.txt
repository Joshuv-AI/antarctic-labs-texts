Engineered T cells stimulate dendritic cell recruitment and antigen spreading for potent anti-tumor immunity
Authors: Zhen Xiao, Jiajia Wang, Shidian He, Lin Wang, Jingxing Yang, Wenhui Li, Kaili Ma, Yabo Zhou, Xiaowei Liu, Shiyou Wang, Yu Yang, Minmin Ge, An Gao, Kun Tang, Jing Huang, Chen Wang, Liyuan Zhang, Hai-Xi Sun, Lianjun Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryCurrent T cell-based immunotherapeutic strategies show limited success in treating solid tumors due to insufficient dendritic cell (DC) activity, particularly cross-presenting conventional type 1 dendritic cells (cDC1s). DC scarcity and dysfunction hinder T cell expansion and differentiation, greatly limiting anti-tumor responses. In this study, we propose a T cell engineering strategy to enhance interaction with XCR1+ cDC1s. Adoptively transferred T cells engineered to secrete Flt3L and XCL1 (FX) promote DC trafficking and maturation and improve DC-T cell interaction, while maintaining a pool of TCF1+SlamF6+ stem-like T cells. Importantly, FX-engineered T cells trigger robust antigen spreading and potent endogenous polyclonal T cell response, enabling the recognition and elimination of tumors with heterogeneous antigens and preventing immune escape. The therapeutic efficacy of FX-armed chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cells is further validated in the Flt3KO&hFLT3LG humanized mouse model. This strategy offers a promising avenue for enhancing DC-T cell interactions, paving the way for more effective immunotherapy against solid tumors.Keywords: dendritic cell, CAR-T cell, endogenous T cell, antigen spreading, immunotherapy, stem-like T cells, humanized mouse model

Introduction

Adoptive cell therapy (ACT) has revolutionized cancer treatment, with chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cells achieving remarkable success in B cell malignancies. However, their efficacy in solid tumors remains limited due to poor tumor infiltration, reduced functionality and persistence, antigen heterogeneity, and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME).1,2 For instance, clinical trials targeting EGFRvIII in glioblastoma3 and CD19 in leukemia4 have shown tumor escape through antigen loss, highlighting the need for strategies to overcome antigen escape.

T cell receptor (TCR)-engineered T cells (TCR-T) and tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy have shown promise in solid tumors.5,6,7,8 Unlike CAR-T cells, TILs or TCR-T cells are broadly activated by dendritic cell (DC) in lymph nodes (LNs) and even within the TME,2,9,10,11,12 emphasizing the importance of DC-T cell interactions. TILs’ polyclonal nature also enables broader antigen recognition, offering a potential advantage.13 Drawing insights from these therapies, strategies to harness DCs may improve the efficacy of CAR-T therapy against solid tumors. Indeed, enhancing CAR-T-DC interactions, as seen with platforms like Amph-vax, can promote CAR-T persistence and function.14 Recently, an elegant study demonstrated that the formation of a three-cell cluster (triad) consisting of DCs, CD4+ T cells, and CD8+ T cells is required for optimal CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity and cancer cell elimination.15 However, DC paucity and dysfunctional states can greatly limit anti-tumor responses.

Conventional type 1 dendritic cells (cDC1s) are specialized for antigen cross-presentation and T cell priming.10,16 Anti-tumor responses of endogenous T cells often involve antigen spreading (AS), where immune responses broaden to target additional tumor antigens, which are critical for durable efficacy.17 Accumulating evidence suggests that AS significantly contributes to the overall therapeutic outcomes of cancer therapies,17 emphasizing the importance of manipulating DCs to potentiate polyclonal T cell response. While CAR-T cells armed with molecules like D40L,18 Flt3L,19 and interleukin (IL)-7/CCL1920 and Amph-vax vaccine17 have been shown to enhance AS in preclinical models, many studies focus only on DC quantity or function, not both, and are limited to immunodeficient models.

Here, we show that progenitor exhausted CD8+ T (Tpex) cells exhibit high expression of XCL1, correlating with favored prognosis. Given the critical role of the XCL1-XCR1 axis in recruiting cDC1s21,22 and that Flt3L promotes DC development,23 we engineered T cells to overexpress Flt3L and XCL1. We demonstrated that these engineered T cells fostered potent AS, reshaped the intratumoral immune landscape, and suppressed antigenically heterogeneous tumors. Importantly, we validated the therapeutic efficacy of CAR-T cell-induced AS in a preclinical setting using a Flt3KO&hFLT3LG humanized mouse model. These findings provide compelling evidence of the feasibility and effectiveness of this optimized CAR-T cell therapy for treating solid tumors.

Results

XCL1 expression positively correlates with CD8+ Tpex signature in both mouse and human tumors

To explore the transcriptional profiles of antigen-specific CD8+ T cell subsets across species, we analyzed publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets of tumors initiated by intratracheal instillation of lentivirus containing LucOS and Cre recombinase in KrasLSL−G12D/+; p53fl/fl mice containing SIINFEKL (SIIN) and SIYRYYGL (SIY) antigens (Figure 1A),24 referencing the “ProjectTILs” T cell atlases.25 SIIN-specific T cells were categorized into five subpopulations, including Tpex and terminally exhausted T cells (Tex) (Figures 1B and S1A). Tpex cells exhibited higher expression of Xcl1 and Tcf7, while Tex cells expressed the exhaustion-related genes Tox and Havcr2 (Figures 1B–1D), consistent with earlier reports.25 A similar pattern was observed in SIY-specific CD8+ T cells (Figures S1B–S1D). We next assessed whether this pattern holds in human cancers by examining human papillomavirus (HPV)-specific CD8+ T cells in head and neck cancer.26 Consistent with findings in mouse models, human stem-like T cells displayed elevated expression of XCL1, TCF7, IL7R, CCR7, and SLAMF6 compared to terminally differentiated (TD) or exhausted T cells (Tex) (Figures S1E and S1F).

To experimentally validate this, we transferred naive TCF1+(GFP+) OT-I cells isolated from Tcf7-DTR-GFP OT-I reporter mice into ovalbumin257-264 (OVA)-expressing B16 (B16-OVA) tumor-bearing mice. Two weeks later, we isolated distinct T cell subpopulations from B16-OVA tumors and performed quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (RT-qPCR). Tpex (GFP+TIM3−) cells expressed higher levels of Xcl1 and Tcf7 compared to Tex (GFP−TIM3+) cells (Figures 1E, 1F, and S1G). Additionally, GFP+CD8+ T cells in the tumor-draining lymph nodes (tdLNs) also showed 2-fold higher XCL1 expression than GFP− counterparts (Figure 1G), and confocal microscopy confirmed co-localization of TCF1 (GFP+) and XCL1 within tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells (Figure 1H). These findings together suggest that XCL1 expression may play a potential role in preserving CD8+ Tpex cell and that the XCL1-XCR1 axis is a potential therapeutic target. Trajectory and RNA velocity analysis27 using the scRNA-seq dataset28 showed that Xcl1+ T cells expressed progenitor-associated genes such as Klf2, Id3, and Tcf7, while late effector cells showed increased expression of effector-associated genes GzmB and Ezh2, indicating that Xcl1+ T cells occupy an early differentiation stage (Figures 1I, 1J, S1H, and S1I). These observations also reinforce the notion that XCL1+CD8+ T cells may represent a distinct Tpex population.25,29

XCL1 expression correlates positively with cancer patient survival and response to ICB therapy

Given the established association between Tpex cells in both the tumor and tdLNs and favorable responses to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB),30 we evaluated whether the XCL1 signature correlates with patient prognosis. Analysis of the XCL1 signature in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) melanoma dataset revealed that higher XCL1 expression is associated with improved overall survival (Figure S1J). To assess the prognostic value of the XCL1 signature specifically within CD8+ T cells, we accounted for potential confounding from natural killer (NK) cells, which are known to express high levels of XCL1.22 Utilizing publicly available scRNA-seq datasets,30,31 we found NCAM1 as a specific marker for human NK cells (Figure S1K). Thus, we constructed a Cox proportional hazards model: logh(t) = logh_0 (t)+ β_1⋅XCL1+ β_2⋅CD8B+ β_3⋅(XCL1 × CD8B)+ β_4⋅NCAM1. In this model, survival risk is predicted based on the expression levels of XCL1, CD8B, and NCAM1, with an interaction term capturing the synergistic effect of XCL1 and CD8B abundance. Our analysis suggested minimal NK cell contribution to survival prediction (Table S1). To further evaluate gene interactions, we multiplied gene expression values and found that the XCL1∗CD8Bhi signature was associated with improved survival in cancers such as skin cutaneous melanoma and uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (Figures 1K and S1L), though bulk RNA-seq data have inherent limitations. Integration of ICB survival cohorts (GSE78220 and PRJEB23709) confirmed that higher XCL1 expression in tumor-infiltrating CD45+ cells is linked to better outcomes (Figure 1L). scRNA-seq data (GSE235863) showed that XCL1 expression in CD8+ T cell correlates with improved ICB response (Figure 1M). Given that XCL1 recruits cDC1s, and DCs support TCF1+Ly108+ T cells in tdLNs,32,33,34 we proposed that XCL1+ Tpex cells may recruit XCR1+ cDC1s through the XCL1-XCR1 axis to sustain self-renewal, promote effector differentiation, and initiate the de novo priming of polyclonal T cells, thereby contributing to durable tumor control.

XCL1 remodels the landscape of the immunosuppressive TME and enhances tumor control

To elucidate the role of XCL1 in tumor progression, we engineered B16F10 melanoma and MC38 colorectal cancer cells to stably express XCL1 (Figures S2A–S2C). In vitro chemotaxis assays using iCD103 DCs35 and splenic cDC1s showed that XCL1-expressing tumor cells effectively recruited XCR1+ DCs (Figures S2D–S2F). In xenograft models, XCL1 expression significantly inhibited tumor growth of B16F10 and MC38 model (Figures 2A–2D, S2G, and S2H), increased infiltration of CD3+, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, and reduced frequency of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) (Figures 2E, 2F, and S2I). XCL1 also promoted substantial recruitment of DCs, especially cDC1s (Figures 2G and 2H), supporting its role in promoting immune cell infiltration. We next evaluated the immune phenotype of intratumoral DCs, given that the TME can induce tolerogenic states in DCs. In tumors expressing XCL1, DCs exhibited elevated co-stimulatory molecules (CD40, CD80, and major histocompatibility complex [MHC] class II) and IL-12, without significant changes in immunoregulatory markers such as CD6336 and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) (Figures 2I–2L and S2J), suggesting a shift toward an immunostimulatory phenotype.

Given that DCs have the superior ability to initiate CD8+ T cell immune response in the tdLNs, and that the therapeutic efficacy of XCL1 was also dependent on host T cells (Figure 2M), we optimized a flow cytometry-based TCR Vβ screening system to assess CD8+ T cell clonal diversity in the tdLNs (Figures S3A and S3B), which revealed a significant expansion in the frequencies of various Vβ clonotypes (Figure 2N), consistent with increased CD8+ T cells in tdLNs (Figure S2K), indicating oligoclonal expansion. To determine whether XCL1-mediated effects depend on cDC1s, we utilized a Batf3−/− mouse model that lacks cDC1s.37 In both tumor models, XCL1’s anti-tumor effects were abolished in Batf3−/− mice (Figures S2L and S2M), with a reduced proportion in PD1+CD39+CD8+ T cells (Figure S2L), indicating impaired antigen-specific responses.38 These findings underscore the requirement of cDC1 and polyclonal T cell for XCL1-mediated tumor control.

Beyond increasing DC numbers, spatial proximity to T cells is crucial for anti-tumor responses. Spatial profiling revealed that XCL1 facilitated the infiltration and co-localization of both DCs and CD8+ T cells in tumors (Figure 2O), further supporting its role in reshaping immune landscape of the TME and enhancing tumor control.

XCL1-engineered T cells enhance T cell expansion and tumor suppression in a cDC1-dependent manner

Next, to apply our findings in an ACT setting, we engineered OT-I cells to secrete XCL1 (OT-I/XCL1) via retroviral transduction and determined their effectiveness against solid tumors (Figures S4A and S4B). OT-I/XCL1 cells attracted significantly more XCR1+ cDC1s in vitro (Figure S4C). In a CpG and OVA peptide immunization model, the frequencies of XCL1-expressing OT-I cells significantly increased in both spleen and LNs (Figures 3A and 3B).

We then tested the anti-tumor activity of OT-I/XCL1 in MC38-OVA and B16-OVA models. To optimize ACT efficacy, lymphodepletion is required, but sublethal total body irradiation can impair cDC1-mediated immunity. To address this, we evaluated the impact of varying irradiation doses on immune cell populations and found that a 2 Gy dose best preserved host CD8+ T cells and cDC1s while supporting transferred T cell survival (Figures S5A–S5H), which are essential for mobilizing endogenous immune response. Consequently, the suboptimal lymphodepletion dose of 2 Gy was employed in further experiments in this study. At this dose, adoptive transfer of OT-I/XCL1 cells significantly inhibited B16-OVA tumors and showed similar effects in MC38-OVA tumors (Figures 3C–3G and S4G). Compared to control OT-I, OT-I/XCL1 treatment significantly promoted the intratumoral infiltration of both transferred and endogenous CD8+ T cells (Figures 3H and S4H) and elevated the proportion of interferon gamma (IFNγ+) T cells (Figure 3I). Additionally, OT-I/XCL1 treatment significantly enhanced TCF1 expression in the adoptively transferred T cells (Figure 3J). In Batf3−/− mice models, OT-I/XCL1-mediated tumor suppression was absent, emphasizing the essential role of endogenous cDC1s in the anti-tumor effects of XCL1 (Figures 3K–3M, S4I, and S4J). These results demonstrate that XCL1-engineered T cells potently enhance anti-tumor immunity in a cDC1-dependent manner.

XCL1 expression enhances the quantity and functionality of intratumoral DCs

The quantity and quality of DCs are pivotal for effective tumor immunotherapy.9 Following adoptive transfer of OT-I/XCL1 cells, we observed that, while the frequency of tumor-infiltrating DCs remained unchanged, the absolute number of intratumoral DCs was significantly increased, including both CD103+ cDC1 and CD11b+ cDC2 cells (Figures 4A–4D). Given that IRF8, crucial for cDC1 development,39 can be inhibited by PGE2 in the TME,22,40 we also focused on a CD103−CD11b−XCR1+IRF8+ DC population that is characterized as cDC1 precursors and found that OT-I/XCL1 treatment obviously increased the number of these cells in the tumors (Figures 4C and 4D). The immunogenic function of DCs is essential for the efficacy of tumor immunotherapy. Next, we assessed DC activation and functionality within the tumor and observed significant upregulation of co-stimulatory molecules, including CD40, CD80, and CD86, in intratumoral DCs following OT-I/XCL1 treatment. Additionally, there was a notable decrease in the immunoregulatory molecule CD63 (Figures 4E–4G).36 Importantly, OT-I/XCL1 treatment significantly enhanced the production of IL-12 and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) by intratumoral DCs (Figure 4G). We also observed significant secretion of CXCL9 (Figure 4H), a key chemokine for T cell trafficking.41 Simultaneously, we also analyzed changes in the host CD8+ TCR repertoire using TCR Vβ screening, which revealed a significant expansion in the frequency of various Vβ clonotypes within the tdLNs following OT-I/XCL1 treatment (Figures 4I and 4J). Collectively, these findings indicate that the XCL1-engineered T cells boost both the quantity and functionality of host DCs, enhancing priming of endogenous T cells.

FX-engineered T cells enhance the control of tumors with pre-existing antigenic heterogeneity

Cytokines like Flt3L are critical for DC development and have shown tumor-suppressive effects when delivered through CAR-T cells, oncolytic viruses, or intratumoral injection.19,42,43 We hypothesized that co-expression of Flt3L and XCL1 (termed FX) in T cells could further enhance anti-tumor efficacy, especially in “cold” tumors that typically exhibit limited T cell response due to a paucity of DCs.44 In the B16-OVA melanoma model, FX-engineered OT-I cell (OT-I/FX) therapy significantly reduced tumor burden compared to T cells expressing Flt3L or XCL1 alone (Figures 5A and S6A). TCR repertoire analysis in tdLNs showed that OT-I/FX substantially amplified the clonal expansion of host CD8+ T cells (Figure S6B). Growing evidence highlights DCs’ importance in the therapeutic efficacy of anti-PD-L1 therapy, and we assessed the combination therapeutic effects of anti-PD-L1. Both XCL1- and FX- engineered T cell therapies synergized with anti-PD-L1 treatment, significantly boosting anti-tumor responses and prolonging survival of tumor-bearing mice (Figures 5B and S6C). Importantly, FX-engineered T cell therapy induced a marked increase in SIIN-specific CD8+ T cell responses (Figure 5C). Furthermore, using Tcf7-DTR-GFP OT-I reporter mice, we found that OT-I/XCL1 and OT-I/FX significantly increased the proportion of TCF1+Slamf6+ subset within the adoptively transferred cells in the tdLNs (Figure 5D), consistent with previous findings.32

Tumor antigenic heterogeneity is a major driver of tumor immune evasion and presents a formidable obstacle in cancer therapy. Harnessing the polyclonal responses of endogenous CD8+ T cells holds promise for counteracting immune escape. To test effects on tumors with antigenic heterogeneity, we used a mixed B16-OVA/B16F10 model. OT-I/FX therapy consistently curbed tumor growth across various B16-OVA/B16F10 ratios, outperforming control OT-I treatment (Figures 5E and S6D). Tetramer-staining revealed that OT-I/FX treatment markedly increased the frequency and number of intratumoral SIIN- and p15E-specific CD8+ T cells (Figures 5F–5H and S6E), with significantly elevating the proportion of IFNγ+ and CD107a+ p15E-specific T cells (Figures 5I and S6F). Single-cell TCR sequencing (scTCR-seq) further confirmed enhanced cytotoxicity among T cells targeting SIIN or p15E (Figure 6K). These results collectively indicate that OT-I/FX therapy increases the number of intratumoral polyfunctional tumor-specific CD8+ T cells. Given that XCL1 expression upregulated CD40 on tumor-infiltrating DCs (Figure 4E), combining OT-I/FX with CD40 agonist (αCD40) further enhanced tumor suppression, prolonged mouse survival, and amplified the endogenous tumor-specific T cell responses (Figures 5J, S6G, and S6H). Together, these results show that FX-engineered T cells stimulate robust AS and polyclonal CD8+ T cell activation, enhancing ACT efficacy against antigenically heterogeneous tumors.

scRNA-seq reveals that FX-engineered T cells significantly enhance both the infiltration and activation of T cells

To investigate the remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment induced by OT-I/FX treatments at single-cell resolution, we thus performed single-cell transcriptomic and single-cell immune repertoire sequencing analyses on tumor-infiltrating CD45+ cells isolated from B16-OVA tumor-bearing mice (Figure 6A). To accurately distinguish adoptively transferred CD45.1+ cells from host-derived CD45.2+ cells within the tumor, we developed an algorithm that classifies individual cells by analyzing allele-specific sequencing coverage at variant sites between the CD45.1 and CD45.2 genes. Unsupervised clustering of the single-cell gene expression profiles of host-derived CD45+ cells identified 32 distinct clusters (Figure S8A), representing 12 immune cell populations, including T cells, B cells, NK cells, monocytes/macrophages (Mono/Mac), and DCs (Figures 6B and S7A–S7C). The distribution of these populations varied notably across treatment conditions, with OT-I/FX therapy, whether or not combined with αCD40, significantly increasing the proportion of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells while reducing the proportion of Mono/Mac (Figures 6C and S7B). Further analysis revealed an increased percentage of “anti-tumoral” lymphocyte clusters in tumors treated with OT-I/FX or OT-I/FX plus αCD40 (Figure 6D). Flow cytometry analysis further confirmed these results, showing a substantial increase in the absolute number of intratumoral endogenous CD8+ T cells following OT-I/FX therapy (Figures 6E and 6F). Interestingly, while scRNA-seq data suggested a relative decrease in the proportion of intratumoral DCs, flow cytometry showed an increase in the absolute increase in DC numbers in tumors treated with engineered T cells compared to control T cells (Figures 6C and 6F). Concurrently, we also observed an elevated ratio of DCs to pro-tumoral Mono/Mac following OT-I/FX therapy (Figure S8E). This discrepancy is likely due to the significantly increased proportion of CD8+ T cells in the CD45+ populations, which reduced the relative proportion of DCs despite the increase in their absolute number.

To gain a deeper understanding of the changes within the TME, we profiled subpopulations of CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, DCs, and Mono/Macs. Upon re-clustering CD8+ T cells, we identified 9 distinct subpopulations based on known markers (Figures S8C and S8D). Specifically, we observed a significantly increased frequency of effector memory CD8+ T cells (CD8 Tem), effector CD8+ T cells (CD8 Teff), and proliferating CD8+ T cells with OT-I/FX treatment (Figures 6G and 6H). Additionally, the ratio between “non-exhausted” and “exhausted” CD8+ T cell clusters was the highest with combination therapy (Figure S8F). Transcriptomic profiling of tumor-infiltrating host CD8+ T cells across treatments revealed a robust activation signature in response to OT-I/FX treatment. In detail, we observed upregulation of Tbx21, Klrg1, and Id2, along with various effector molecules (e.g., Ifng, Prf1, Gzmb, and GzmK), co-inhibitory receptors (e.g., Lag3 and Havcr2), and co-stimulatory receptors (e.g., Cd27, Cd28, Tnfrsf9, and Tnfrsf18) in line with a strong activation and differentiation of effector T cells (Figure 6I). Furthermore, FX-engineered T cells induced increased expression of genes associated with TCR signaling pathways, including Ifng, Nfatc1, and Nr4a1/2 (Figure 6I). Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis also revealed upregulation of key immune function genes in combination therapy with OT-I/FX and αCD40, including Gzmb, GzmK, Ifng, and Mki67 (Figure S8G). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of intratumoral host CD8+ T cells further revealed that the combination therapy maintained a high proliferative potential and significantly upregulated multiple metabolic pathways, including oxidative phosphorylation, mTORC1 signaling, fatty acid metabolism, and pyruvate metabolism (Figure S8H). These results suggest that FX-engineered T cells significantly enhance the activation and functionality of endogenous CD8+ T cells within the TME.

Among the CD4+ T cell populations, eight distinct subpopulations were identified (Figures S8I and S8J). Notably, CD4+ regulatory T cells (Tregs) were primarily enriched with both OT-I/FX and OT-I/FX plus αCD40 treatments (Figure S8K). Although FX secreting increased Treg infiltration, recent studies have identified a novel Treg subpopulation, the “fragile” Tregs, that promotes anti-tumor immunity.45 Fragile Tregs retain Foxp3 expression but lack NRP1 and exhibit reduced levels of suppressive molecules such as CD73 and IL-10.45 We observed that the majority of Tregs exhibited deficiencies in Nrp1, Nt5e, Pdcd1, and Il2ra, alongside increased expression of Satb1 and Gzmb with OT-I/FX and combination therapy (Figure S8L). These characteristics suggest that these Tregs may represent the fragile Treg subset. Additionally, we found that combination therapy resulted in enhanced cytotoxic activity of intratumoral CD4+ T cells (Figure 6J). We also noted a significant shift in the Tpex to Tex ratio among endogenous T cells, as well as an increased CD8+ T cell-to-Treg ratio (Figures S8M and S8N). Collectively, these results indicate that FX-engineered T cells enhance the infiltration and activation of endogenous T cells and reshape the anti-tumor landscape within the TME.

Single-cell sequencing reveals that FX engineering enhances DC-T cell interactions and promotes T cell clonal expansion

The observation that OT-I/FX therapy effectively drives tumor rejection, even in the presence of antigenic heterogeneity, prompted us to further investigate T cell dynamics using scTCR-seq. Analysis of scTCR-seq data revealed a higher number of highly expanded CD8+ T cell clones (>30) and moderately expanded CD4+ T cell clones (5 < X ≤ 10) following OT-I/FX therapy (Figures S9A–S9D). Using a cytotoxicity scoring system we developed, both SIIN- and non-SIIN-specific T cells from tumors with OT-I/FX plus αCD40 therapy exhibited significantly higher cytotoxicity scores. While the increase in cytotoxicity score for p15E-specific T cells was observed, it did not have statistical significance (Figure 6K). Furthermore, we sorted CD3+ T cells from tdLNs and performed scTCR-seq. Analysis of CDR3 sequences in host TCR clones revealed an elevated abundance of certain TCR sequences in OT-I/FX-treated mice (Figure 6L). Notably, the top 10 expanded clonotypes, which represented the majority of all cells sequenced, were primarily expanded in the OT-I/XCL1 and OT-I/FX treatments (Figure 6L). We also evaluated the frequency of TRBV-TRBJ pairing usage and found an enrichment of specific TRBV-TRBJ pairings in the OT-I/FX-treated groups compared to control, indicative of oligoclonal T cell expansion (Figure S8E). These findings suggest the emergence of an endogenous tumor-specific T cell response.

Given that XCL1 expression enhances the quantity and functionality of tumor-infiltrating DCs (Figures 4A–4H), we investigated intratumoral DCs in more detail. After unsupervised clustering of the initial DC/pDC clusters, 7 subpopulations were identified, including classical DC subpopulations cDC1 and cDC2, proliferating DCs (previously identified as cycling DCs),46 as well as mregDC1 and mregDC2 (Figures S9F and S9G). A significant increase in the proportion of cDC2 was observed in the FX-treated group (Figure S9G). Previous reports have shown that the inflammatory TME can induce IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs) cDC2 (ISG+ cDC2) to support anti-tumor immunity.47 We found that the gene expression profile of these cDC2 from OT-I/XCL1- and OT-I/FX-treated tumors closely resembled that of ISG+ cDC2, indicating enhanced anti-tumor activity (Figure S9H).

The activation and antigen presentation of DCs are crucial for promoting antigen-specific CD8+ T cell immunity. By scoring cDC clusters for inflammasome activation, mregDC signature, and MHC class I presentation signature, we observed a significant variance of score related to inflammasome activation and the mregDC signature following OT-I/FX treatment (Figures 6M, S9I, and S9J). Although the MHC class I signature did not show significant changes, several related genes (Figures S9I–S9K), including H2-M2, H2-T23, Tap1, Ctss, Ctsb, and Cybb, were upregulated. Furthermore, mregDC signature was characterized by the enhanced expression of immunostimulatory molecules and signals (Figures 6N and 6O), while the expression of immunoregulatory molecules remained unpredictable (Figures S9L–S9N). Notably, DCs in the OT-I/FX therapy group exhibited elevated expression of key activation markers, such as Cd40 and Cd83, along with co-stimulatory cytokine genes, including Il1b, Il12b, Il15, Cxcl9, Cxcl10, and Cxcl16 (Figures 6N and 6O). Additionally, these DCs also showed reduced expression of Treg-recruiting chemokines, such as Ccl17 and Ccl22 (Figure 6O). Although mregDC can have dual immunostimulatory and immunoregulatory roles, OT-I/FX therapy favored the upregulation of immunostimulatory signals in DCs, thereby enhancing their immunogenic function.

Emerging evidence suggests that spatial interactions between DCs and T cells are critical for inducing and sustaining T cell responses.15,48,49 Cell-cell communication analysis between DCs and T cells revealed several known interaction pairs, including CXCL16-CXCR6, IL15-IL-15R, and CXCL9/10-CXCR3, which contribute to recruiting cytotoxic T cells and sustaining their survival (Figure 6P). We also identified other DC-T cell interactions, such as the upregulation of TNFSF9-TNFRSF9 (4-1BBL-4-1BB) interactions and a reduction in ENTPD1-ADORA2A interactions within OT-I/FX treatment (Figure 6P). These DC-T cell interactions are also known to enhance DC functionality. Notably, we observed increased TNF superfamily interactions (e.g., TNFSF11-TNFRSF11A, TNFSF13B-TNFRSF13B, LTB-LTBR, and IFNG-IFNGR) between T cells and DCs (Figure 6Q), which are known to induce the non-canonical nuclear factor κB pathway and promote DC activation.50,51 Notably, the XCL1-XCR1 interaction specifically between CD8+ T cells and DCs was upregulated following both XCL1 and FX treatment (Figure 6Q). These findings suggest that FX-engineered T cells stimulate the immunogenic function of DCs and significantly enhance DC-T cell interactions within the TME.

scRNA-seq analysis reveals that FX-engineered T cells reprogram intratumoral Mono/Mac phenotype

TAMs exhibit diverse roles in tumor immunity, with M1-like TAMs exhibiting anti-tumor activity.52 Immunotherapy-induced CD8+ T cells attract macrophages and skew their differentiation toward M1-like TAMs,53 which are crucial for tumor control. In our analysis, 6 Mono/Mac subpopulations were identified, including 4 macrophage subpopulations (C1qb, mt-Co1, Plin2, and Birc5) and 2 monocyte subpopulations (Ace and Chil3) (Figures S10A–S10C). DEG analysis highlighted the heterogeneity in expression profile (Figure S10D). RNA velocity inference with scVelo and trajectory analysis with Monocle3 to characterize cellular state transitions indicated that Mono-chil3 and Mac-plin2 represented early differentiation states within the tumor-infiltrating myeloid compartment (Figures S10E and S10F). However, due to dataset limitations (e.g., lack of cross-tissue scRNA-seq and lineage tracing models such as Ms4a3Cre mice), we were incapable of providing definitive evidence for this relationship.

Across different treatment conditions, a pattern reflecting anti-tumor efficacy emerged. OT-I/FX and OT-I/FX plus αCD40 therapies showed enrichment of the Mono-Chil3 cluster within the tumor, while controls favored Mac-C1qb cluster (Figure S10G). Notably, Mono-chil3 exhibited particularly higher Cd40 expression levels (Figures S10H and S10I), supporting Mono-Chil3 potential in promoting anti-tumor immunity. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that over 300 genes were upregulated following OT-I/FX treatments (Figure S10J), with αCD40 further boosting the expression of pro-inflammatory genes (e.g., Il1b, Tnf, Irf1, and Cxcl10) (Figure S10K). Further analysis indicated that Mono-Chil3 upregulated expression of pro-inflammatory genes, including Cxcl2, Cxcl10, Ifitm6, Tnf, and Il1b, whereas Mac-C1qb was more closely associated with regulatory or immunosuppressive functions (Figures S10L and S10M). To better assess the functional characteristics of these subpopulations, we performed an “anti-tumoral and pro-tumoral gene cluster” scoring analysis, which revealed that Mono-Chil3 adopted an M1-liked anti-tumoral phenotype (Figures S10N and S10P). Moreover, FX engineering inhibited the expression of genes linked to M2-skewed pro-tumoral phenotypes, such as Mrc1, Trem2, Apoe, and Lpl (Figure S10O). Overall, these results indicate that FX-engineered T cells drive a shift from a pro-tumoral to an anti-tumoral phenotype in Mono/Mac population.

FX-armed CAR-T cells demonstrate superior tumor control in both murine and Flt3KO&hFLT3LG humanized mouse models

Encouraged by prior results, we explored whether FX-armed CAR-T (CAR-FX) cells show enhanced tumor control in vivo. Although CAR-T cells do not necessarily depend on DCs for antigen presentation, we hypothesized that engaging DCs might amplify endogenous T cell responses, addressing antigen heterogeneity in solid tumors. To test this, we generated mouse CAR-FX T cells targeting humanized CD19 or B7-H3 (Figure 7A) and confirmed comparable CAR expression with control CAR-T cells (Figure S11A). In B16F10 tumors expressing humanized B7-H3 or CD19 (B16F10-B7-H3 and B16F10-CD19), CAR-FX T cells outperformed control CAR-T cells in tumor suppression and extended mouse survival (Figures 7B–7D, S11B, and S11C). Similar to that observed in the OT-I/FX system, CAR-FX T cell therapy also greatly increased the intratumoral infiltration of CAR-T cells and endogenous CD8+ T cells (Figures 7E, 7F, S11D, and S11E) and expanded TCR Vβ clonotypes (Vβ8.1/2, Vβ10b) in tdLNs (Figure S11F). Tumor-infiltrating CAR-FX T cells exhibited a Tpex cell phenotype, accompanied by increased cytokine production, whereas control CAR-T cells primarily exhibited a Tex phenotype (Figures S11G and S11I). Notably, the frequency of Tpex cells approximately doubled with CAR-FX T cell therapy, and similar patterns of PD1+TIM3+ and/or PD1+TIM3− were observed for both transferred and endogenous CD8+ T (Figures S11G and S11H). We also observed a significant increase in infiltration of both total DCs and cDC1s within the tumors (Figure S11J). Combining CAR-FX T cells with αCD40 therapy strongly suppressed tumor progression (Figures 7G and S11K), and using Batf3−/− mice confirmed that CAR-FX efficacy depends on endogenous cDC1s (Figure S11L).

For clinical translation, we designed a anti-B7-H3 humanized CAR-T (hCAR) construct to overexpress humanized FLT3LG and XCL1 (termed hCAR-FX T) (Figures 7H and S12A), and we then evaluated the anti-tumor activity of hCAR-FX T cells in NOD scid gamma (NSG) mice bearing PC3 human prostate cancer cells, which naturally express high levels of B7-H3, and the A375-B7-H3 CDX tumor model. The hCAR-FX T cells exhibited comparable tumor control capabilities to that of control hCAR-T in NSG mice, which lack an endogenous immune system (Figures 7I and 7J). To model human immunity, we engrafted human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) into Flt3KO&hFLT3LG NSG mice to specifically facilitate the development of human DCs (Figures 7K and S12B). In this system, hCAR-FX T cells treatment significantly inhibited the progression of A375-B7-H3 melanoma and the PC3 prostate cancer (Figures 7L, 7M, S12C, and S12E) and extended the survival time of the PC3 tumor-bearing mice (Figure 7N). Analysis of infiltrating immune cells in the A375-B7-H3 melanoma revealed that hCAR-FX T cell therapy enhanced the number of both CAR-T and DC cells (Figure S12D). These findings demonstrate that strengthening Flt3L and XCL1 expression in CAR-T cells effectively limits tumor progression across multiple models, highlighting its potential for clinical translation.

Discussion

Antigenic heterogeneity and antigen loss represent major barriers to immune surveillance and contribute to the development of resistance against T cell-based immunotherapies, posing significant challenges to current immunotherapeutic strategies. While CAR-T therapies have yielded remarkable success against hematologic malignancies, their efficacy in solid tumors remains limited by these issues.1,2 The clinical success of TIL therapies in solid tumors suggests that the induction of productive polyclonal T cell responses to multiple tumor antigens may provide an effective strategy to overcome antigenic escape. In this study, we proposed a promising approach to tackle antigenic heterogeneity and antigen loss in solid tumors by engineering T cells to express Flt3L and XCL1. These “FX-armed” T cells improved DC recruitment and activation in the TME, facilitated DC-T cell interaction, induced AS, and elicited broad polyclonal T cell responses.

Specifically, FX-armed T cells exhibited increased production of IFNγ by T cells and IL-12 by DCs, two key cytokines that synergistically drive AS and amplify immune responses.17 The IFNγ-IL-12 feedback loop between T cells and DCs, a mechanism consistently linked to successful immunotherapy, aligns with our findings.54 Comprehensive analysis of tumor-infiltrating immune cells following FX-armed T cells treatment revealed significant reshaping of the immune landscape of the TME, with increased infiltration of CD8+ T cells, DCs, and NK cells, along with reduced immunosuppressive populations. These changes indicate that FX expression reprograms the TME from an immunosuppressive to an immune-inflamed state. Interestingly, while FX-armed T cell treatment increased the proportion of Tregs among CD4+ T cells, these Tregs exhibited a “fragile” phenotype associated with anti-tumor activity.45 Previous reports have shown that IFNγ can induce the formation of fragile Tregs, further emphasizing the central role of IFNγ in our strategy.45 In analyzing myeloid populations, we observed that FX expression enhanced DC infiltration and activation, particularly by promoting expression of co-stimulatory molecules such as CD40. Consequently, combining FX-armed T cells with αCD40 significantly improved survival of tumor-bearing mice. Given the crucial role of DCs in delivering TCR and co-stimulatory signals necessary for T cell activation and persistence,12 these findings raise the possibility that optimizing intact signals of DC-T cell interaction could further boost CAR-T cell efficacy, supported by recent evidence showing superior anti-tumor activity of dual TCR/CAR-T cells compared to conventional CAR-T cells in a humanized solid tumor mouse model.55

Further analysis of Mono/Mac populations showed that CD40 signaling was not restricted to DCs. FX-armed T cells treatment promoted the expansion of a Mono-Chil3 cluster characterized by high Cd40 expression and an M1-skewed anti-tumor phenotype. Recent evidence has demonstrated that CD40 signaling can stimulate macrophage anti-tumor functions through metabolic reprogramming,56 suggesting that FX expression may induce broader systemic shift toward a pro-inflammatory immune landscape. cDC1s are critical for cross-presenting antigens and activating CD8+ T cells in both tdLNs and the TME, and their involvement in AS is increasingly recognized.2,17,19,57 Immune repertoire analysis demonstrated that FX-armed T cells enhanced the expansion of diverse TCR clonotypes and increased the production of endogenous antigen-specific CD8+ T cells. Using Batf3−/− and Rag1−/− mice, we confirmed that the anti-tumor effects of FX-armed T cells largely depended on cDC1s and host T cells. Cell-cell communication analysis and immunofluorescence imaging further revealed the XCL1-XCR1 axis as a key driver of the endogenous T cell response. This axis appears to support the maintenance of Tpex within tdLNs and tumors, although the mechanisms by which cDC1s regulate Tpex formation remain to be fully elucidated.

We further validated this strategy in a humanized mouse model containing functional DC compartments, confirming that AS contributes to tumor control during CAR-T therapy. Nevertheless, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of this model. Most humanized mice lack fully functional LNs, which are essential for initiating immune responses. Moreover, immune dysfunction in cancer patients often extends beyond the TME, involving systemic defects such as impaired DC activity across tissues.58 This may partly explain the limited clinical success of some DC-targeted therapies.18,19,20 Notably, the ability of FX-armed T cells to enhance both the abundance and function of DCs could provide an advantage in overcoming these limitations.

In summary, our findings suggest that FX engineering offers a promising approach for addressing the limitations of conventional CAR-T therapies in solid tumors. By promoting the quantity and quality of DCs, FX-armed T cells enhance AS and broaden the immune response, offering a potential solution to tumor heterogeneity. Future research should explore the clinical potential of this strategy, including integrating synthetic Notch (synNotch) receptors to enable precise intratumoral delivery of immunostimulatory molecules, as well as combining FX-armed T cells with immune adjuvants or cancer vaccines to further enhance therapeutic efficacy in solid tumors.

Limitations of the study

This study highlights the therapeutic promise of FX-engineered T cells, yet several limitations must be considered. The humanized mouse models employed lack fully functional LNs, compromising accurate assessment of immune activation. Moreover, experimental conditions may not fully recapitulate the systemic immune dysfunction observed in cancer patients, such as defective DC activity. Although combining FX-armed T cells with adjuvants or cancer vaccines is proposed for poorly immunogenic tumors, their optimization and clinical feasibility remain untested. Additionally, the precise mechanisms by which DC-T cell interactions, particularly those involving cDC1s, support the maintenance of the Tpex subset require further investigation. Lastly, pooling cells from seven mice per group for single-cell sequencing limited statistical analysis.

Experimental model and subject details

Ethics statement

All animal experiments were performed with the approval of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Suzhou Institute of Systems Medicine (ISM-IACUC-0055). All mice were housed under standard conditions in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility, and were examined daily. All tumor burdens remained within the standards permitted by the IACUC of Suzhou Institute of Systems Medicine (not exceeding 1500 mm3 in all animal experiments). Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) were received from healthy volunteers (NO.D22C023, 30 years old, male) with written informed consent (Shanghai Junx-bio Technology Co., Ltd.), and the protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Changsha Jingkai Hospital (JH-WBC-20230417).

Mice

Wild-type female C57BL/6N mice (6 weeks, CD45.2) were purchased from Vital River Co, Ltd (Beijing, China). CD45.1 and CD45.1/CD45.2 OT-I TCR transgenic mice on a C57BL/6 background, and were generated by crossing OT-I mice to CD45.1 or CD45.2 mice. For some experiments, Tcf7-DTR-GFP OT-I reporter mice were generated by crossing OT-I mice to Tcf7-DTR-GFP mice. Batf3−/− mice (C57BL/6N background) were kindly provided by Prof. Sudan He. Flt3KO and Flt3KO&hFLT3LG NSG mice were provided by Dr. Jing Huang. Rag1−/− (C57BL/6JGpt-Rag1em1Cd3259/Gpt) mice aged 6–8 weeks were purchased from GemPharmatech. Experimental procedures in mouse studies were approved by the IACUC of Suzhou Institute of Systems Medicine (ISM-IACUC-0055).

Cell lines

HEK293T and B16F10 cell lines were purchased from ATCC. B16-OVA and A375 cell lines were provided by Prof. Bo Huang. MC38 and PC3 cell lines were sourced from our laboratory. B16F10 was transduced with lentivirus to express murine XCL1 (B16-XCL1), human CD19 extracellular domain (B16F10-CD19) or B7-H3 extracellular domain (B16F10-B7-H3). B16F10-Vector, B16F10-XCL1, B16F10-CD19, B16F10-B7-H3, and B16-OVA were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. MC38 was transduced to express ovalbumin (MC38-OVA) or murine XCL1 (MC38-XCL1). MC38, MC38-OVA, and MC38-XCL1 were maintained in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. A375 cells were transduced to express B7-H3 extracellular domain, termed A375-B7-H3. A375-B7-H3 and PC3 were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. All the above reagents were purchased from Gibco. All cell lines were maintained at 37°C with 5% CO2, and the cell lines used in the experiments were checked regularly for contamination with Mycoplasma using RT-qPCR.

Method details

Generation of mouse and human viral plasmid constructs

All plasmid constructs were generated by overlap extension PCR (Vazyme, P520-02) and recombinant gene expression cloning into the pLV-EF1a-puro lentiviral or MSGV retroviral vector. Murine XCL1, human CD19, and B7-H3 extracellular domain as well as luciferase cDNA were integrated into a pLV-EF1a-puro vector to construct stable gene-expressing tumor cell lines. To generate mouse T cell co-expressing Flt3L and XCL1, murine XCL1 and Flt3L cDNA were linked by a self-cleaving P2A sequence integrated into pMSGV-Thy1.1 retroviral vector.

Construction of mouse and human CARs

Murine and human CAR constructs targeting CD19 or B7-H3 were generated as previously described.59 Briefly, the complete murine CAR construct consists of a mouse CD8a signal peptide, antigen-specific single-chain variable fragment (scFv), CD8α hinge region, mouse CD28 transmembrane and co-stimulatory domain, and CD3ζ intracellular domain, subsequently subcloned into pMSGV vector to create murine CD19 or B7-H3 CARs. Full-length murine Flt3l and Xcl1 were amplified and integrated by over-lap PCR, termed mFX. The murine FX-armed CAR constructs were generated by fusing a self-cleaving T2A peptide and murine Flt3l and Xcl1 cDNA fragments into the murine CARs vector. For human CARs (termed hCAR), the B7-H3 CAR comprised as scFv specific for human B7-H3 preceded by a human CD8A leader peptide and followed by human CD8A hinge-transmembrane-CD28 co-stimulatory domain and CD3ζ intracellular domain, subsequently was subcloned into pRetroX vector to create B7-H3 hCARs. Similarly, full-length human FLT3LG and XCL1 were amplified and integrated by over-lap PCR, termed hFX. The human FX-armed CAR constructs were generated by fusing a self-cleaving T2A peptide and Human FLT3LG and XCL1 cDNA fragments into the human CARs vector. A detailed schematic of the construct is shown in Figures 7A and 7H.

Retrovirus and lentivirus production

To generate retroviral particles, HEK293T cells were transfected with a mixture of plasmids, including the retroviral transfer vector (pMSGV for transducing mouse T cell or pRetroX for transducing human T cell) and the envelope plasmids. In detail, 3×106 cells were seeded in a 60 mm dish and cultured for 12 h till confluency reached 60–70%. For mouse retrovirus packaging, pMSGV and pCL-Eco were mixed in a 1:1 ratio (4 μg of total plasmids) and added to 400 μL of basic DMEM medium, followed by the addition of 12 μL of TransIT-293 Transfection Reagent (Mirusbio, China). After 15 min incubation at 25°C (room temperature, RT), the transfection mixture was gently added to HEK293T cells culture in 60 mm dishes and replenished with fresh DMEM medium 12 h later. Viral supernatants were collected at 48 h and 72 h post-transfection from HEK293T cells and centrifuged at 1400 rpm for 5 min to remove the cell debris and aliquoted. For human retrovirus packaging system, 5 μg of plasmid DNA (2 μg of pRetroX CAR plasmid, 1 μg RD114 plasmid, and 2 μg pHIT60 packaging plasmid) was added to 500 μL basic DMEM medium. The subsequent procedures were identical to those used for mouse retrovirus packaging. Lentiviral packaging was performed using a four-plasmid system, which included the transfer vector (3.4 μg of pLV-EF1a-puro), the packaging plasmids (3.4 μg of pMDLg and 0.34 μg of PRSV-Rev), and the envelope plasmid (1 μg pMD2.G). To produce lentiviral particles, HEK293T cells were seeded in a 60 mm dish and transfected with these plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. After 48–72 h of transfection, viral supernatants were collected and filtered through a 0.45 μm filter (Merck), and used to construct stably expressing tumor cell lines. All virus-containing supernatant was aliquoted and stored at −80°C.

Generation of gene-expressing tumor cell lines

Tumor cell lines stably expressing control or target genes were generated by lentiviral transduction. In brief, cells were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured for 12 h to reach approximately 60% confluency. Lentiviral supernatants encoding the target gene were then added in the presence of 10 μg/mL polybrene (Santa Cruz) to enhance transduction efficiency, and cells were incubated for an additional 12 h. After transduction, the medium was replaced with pre-warmed complete culture medium supplemented with puromycin (Roche) for selection (4 μg/mL for MC38 cells, 8 μg/mL for B16F10 and A375 cells). Positively transduced cell clones were enriched and expanded for subsequent experiments.

Primary mouse T cell isolation and transduction

Naive CD8 T cells were purified using the Mouse CD8 Naive T cell Isolated Kit (MojoSort, Biolegend) from spleen of CD45.1 OT-I mice (6–12 weeks) and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco) containing 10% FBS (Hyclone), 2 mM glutamine (Gibco), 1 mM Pyruvate (Gibco), 10 mM HEPES(Gibco), 1×NEAA (Gibco), 100 units Penicillin-Streptomycin (Hyclone) and 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol (Sigma). For naive T cell activation, 48-well plates were precoated with 250 μL of αCD3 (2 μg/mL, Invitrogen) and αCD28 (1 μg/mL, Invitrogen) at 4°C for overnight. Cells were cultured at 1×106 cells/mL in 48-well with hIL-2 (10 ng/mL, Peprotech) for 48 h. Virus supernatant was mixed with activated OT-I cells in the presence of polybrene (8 μg/mL, Santa Curz), hIL-2 (10ng/mL) and αCD28 (1 μg/mL), and the mixtures were added to a 24-well plate precoated with RetroNectin (0.1mg/mL, Takara). Plates were centrifuged at 2000g for 90 min at 30°C, and then cells were cultured at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 24 h. T cells were collected and expanded with fresh RPMI-1640 medium containing hIL-2 (10 ng/mL, Peprotech) and hIL-7 (10 ng/mL, Novoprotein). On day7, T cell transduction efficacy was evaluated by flow cytometry. For mouse CAR transduction efficiency, CAR-T cells were co-incubated with 2 μg/mL recombinant human B7-H3 (R&D) or biotin-CD19 (Yeasen) proteins at 4°C for 35 min, followed by incubation with PE anti-B7-H3 (BioLegend) or PE-streptavidin (BioLegend) for 25 min to evaluate CAR expression.

Generation of human CAR T cells

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) from peripheral blood of healthy volunteers were isolated by using Ficoll-PaquePLUS (GE Healthcare) and seeded onto precoated 12-well plates with 1.5 μg/mL αCD3 (Biolegend) for 48 h. PBMC were cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, 2 mM glutamine, 1 mM Pyruvate, 10 mM HEPES, 1×NEAA, 100 units Penicillin-Streptomycin, 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol, hIL-2 (3 ng/mL, Peprotech), and αCD28 (1 μg/mL, BioLegend). After 48 h of activation, T cells were transduced with CAR retrovirus supernatants on RetroNectin-coated 24-well plates (0.1 mg/mL) in the presence of polybrene (8 μg/mL), hIL-2 (3 ng/mL), and αCD28 (1 μg/mL). The plates were centrifugated at 2000g for 90 min at 30°C, and cells were cultured at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 2 h. The supernatant was then gently removed, and fresh RPMI-1640 medium containing hIL-2 (3 ng/mL) and αCD28 (1 μg/mL) was added. The method for assessing CAR transduction efficiency was the same as that used for mouse CAR-T cells.

Cytotoxicity assay of mouse and human CAR T cells

CAR-T cells cytotoxicity was assessed by the Bright-Lite Luciferase Assay System (Vazyme) with B16F10-B7-H3-luc cells (1×104/well) as target cells at various effector/target (E/T) ratios. CAR-T cells and CD276-CAR-T cells expressed with luciferase were co-incubated with B16F10-B7-H3-luc cells for 6 h. The Bright-Lite Luciferase Assay System was used according to the manufacturer′s instructions.

Flow cytometry

Single-cell suspensions from spleens, Lymph nodes, or tumor tissues were prepared. 1-2×106 cells were resuspended in 50 μL FACS buffer (PBS with 2% FBS), blocked with anti-mouse FcR mAb (Biolegend) for 25 min, and stained with Fixable Viability Dye eFluorTM 506 (1:1000, Invitrogen) at 4°C for 20 min. For cell surface staining, all antibodies were diluted 1:200 with FACS buffer for CD3+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, NK cells (CD45+CD3−NK1.1+), DCs (CD45+CD3−CD19−CD64−MHC-II+CD11c+), XCR1+ cDC1 (CD45+CD3−CD19−CD64−MHC-II+CD11c+XCR1+CD11b−), CD11b+ cDC2 (CD45+CD3−CD19−CD64−MHC-II+CD11c+XCR1−CD11b+), and macrophages (CD45+CD3−CD19−CD11b+CD64+F4/80+, TAM). For intracellular cytokine measurements, lymphocytes were stimulated with PMA (40 ng/mL, Sigma-Aldrich) and Ionomycin (1 μg/mL, Fcmrcs) in the presence of Brefeldin A (1:1000, Invitrogen) and Monensin (1:1000, Biolegend) for 4 h at 37°C. Myeloid cells were stimulated with CpG (5 μg/mL, SYNBIO) in fresh complete RPMI-1640 medium, and cells were treated with Brefeldin A and Monensin for 5 h at 37°C. Then, cells were collected and labeled with Fixable Viability Dye eFluorTM 506 as described above and subsequently stained for surface receptors in FCAS buffer at 4°C. Then, cells were washed twice with intracellular staining permeabilization wash buffer (BioLegend) and stained for cytokines in permeabilization wash buffer for 25 min at 4°C. For intracellular transcription factor staining, cells were pre-stained with Fixable Viability Dye and antibodies for surface markers. Then, cells were fixed and permeabilized using Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer (Invitrogen), and stained with antibodies in permeabilization Buffer (Invitrogen). All samples were resuspended in FACS buffer and detected by BD LSR Fortessa or Symphony A1 (BD Biosciences), and all flow cytometric data were analyzed using FlowJo 10.8.1 (FlowJo, LLC).

Animal experiments model

Mice were subcutaneously injected with 5 × 105 MC38-XCL1 or MC38-OVA tumor cells, 3 × 105 B16F10-XCL1, B16-OVA,B16F10-CD19, B16F10-B7-H3, or A375-B7-H3 cells, or 1×106 PC3 cells in the right flank. Depending on the experiment, sex-matched C57BL/6N mice, Batf3−/− and Rag1−/− mice, or Flt3KO&hFLT3LG NSG (with or without pre-transfer 5×106 PBMC) mice were used. Tumor cells suspended in 75 μL PBS were injected into the right flank of recipient mice to establish palpable tumors. For the mixed tumor studies, each mouse was inoculated in the right flank with a total of 4×105 cells consist of B16F10 cells and B16-OVA cells mixed at pre-defined ratios (4:1, 2:1, or 1:1, respectively). Once tumors grew to around 100–150 mm3, mice underwent a 2Gy dose of total body irradiation (TBI) and received (i.v.) 2×106 OT-I cells or 3×106 CAR T-cells via the tail vein after 24 h. For experiments involving ICB or αCD40 treatment, mice were administered anti-PD-L1 antibody (200 μg, Roche) or αCD40 (100 μg, BioXCell) intraperitoneally. Tumor sizes were measured every two days using the formula: (length × width × height)/2. For immunization model, C57BL/6 mice were received (i.v.) adoptive transfer of XCL1 engineered or control OT-I cells (1×106) and immunized with CpG (30 μg) plus OVA peptide (10 μg) at next day.

Humanized mouse tumor model

For the in vivo study using humanized mice, Flt3KO&hFLT3LG NSG mice were intravenously transferred with 5 × 106 PBMC. On day 8 post-transfer, the reconstitution efficiency of human DCs and T cells were assessed by flow cytometry. For the establishment of humanized mouse tumor model, Flt3KO&hFLT3LG NSG mice were intravenously infused with 5×106 PBMC one day prior to tumor cell inoculation, Once the tumor volumes reached approximately 100 mm3, tumor-bearing mice were intravenously transferred with 3 × 106 B7-H3 hCAR-T cells or hCAR-FX T cells. Tumors sizes were measured every two days using the formula: (length × width × height)/2.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) analysis

The protein expression of XCL1 was determined by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays. The XCL1 antibody (2 μg/mL, R&D) was coated to the bottom of the wells of a microplate and incubated overnight at 4°C. Supernatants from B16F10-Vector, B16F10-XCL1, MC38-Vector, MC38-XCL1, OT-I/Vector, OT-I/XCL1, B7-H3 CAR T, B7-H3 CAR-FX T cells were added to the wells for 2 h at room temperature. Then, mouse XCL1/Lymphotactin biotin antibody (0.2 μg/mL, R&D) was added to the wells for 1 h, followed by washing and reaction with Avidin-HRP (Invitrogen) for 30 min. After the addition of 100 μL 1×TMB substrate solution (Invitrogen) for 15 min, 100 μL 1M H2SO4 was added to stop the reaction and absorbance was detected at 450 nm. A serial dilution of purified recombinant murine Lymphotactin/XCL1 (PrimeGene) served as standards. The quantity of sample concentrations was determined using a standard curve.

In vitro chemotaxis assay of dendritic cells

Chemotaxis of the responder DCs was measured by migration through a polycarbonate filter of 5 μm pore-size in 96-well trans-well chambers (Corning). Supernatants of B16F10-Vector/XCL1 cells, MC38-Vector/XCL1 cells, stimulated Vector/XCL1/FX-expressing OT-I cells, and stimulated CAR-T and CAR-FX T cells were collected in the lower chambers, and induced XCR1+ DCs or splenic XCR1+ cDC1 cells prelabeled with PE were incubated in the upper chambers for 90 min. The DCs migrated from the upper chamber to the lower chamber were assessed by flow cytometry.

Immunofluorescence staining and confocal imaging

Stripped intact mouse tumor tissues and embedded in OCT compound (Sakura), 30 μm (B16-OVA tumor) or 8 μm (MC38-Vector, MC38-XCL1 tumor) sections were prepared using a CM1905 cryostat (Leica) and adhered to adsorbent slides (Citotest). Sections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, then permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100, and blocked with PBS containing 5% Bovine serum albumin (BSA), followed by staining with unconjugated primary antibodies, which were diluted using immunofluorescence antibodies dilution buffer (CST, 12378) overnight at 4°C. Fluorescent conjugated secondary antibodies were incubated for 2–3 h at room temperature in a dark humidified chamber. Sections were washed 3 times with PBS between each staining step. The following antibodies were used for staining: anti-CD8α (Abcam, ab217344), anti-XCL1 (R&D, AF486-SP), anti-CD11c (Abcam, ab254183). Images were captured with PanoVIEW VS200, and the images were analyzed with Olympus Stream software.

TCR β clonotype screening

The Mouse Vβ TCR Screening Panel (1:150, BD Biosciences, 557004) contains 15 pre-diluted FITC-conjugated mAb which recognize mouse Vβ 2, 3, 4, 5.1 and 5.2, 6, 7, 8.1/8.2, 8.3, 9, 10b, 11, 12, 13, 14, and 17a T cell receptors. 1-2×106 cells were resuspended in 100 μL FACS buffer stained with Biotin anti-mouse CD8b antibody (1:200, BioLegend) at 4°C for 30 min and then stained with streptavidin conjugates including APC, PE, PE-Cy7, BV605, APC/BV605, APC/PE-Cy7, BV605/PE-Cy7, PE/PE-Cy7 at 4°C for 30 min. Cells were washed with FACS buffer and then resuspended with 200 μL FACS buffer. After that, different biological samples in the same group were labeled with different fluorescence. Meanwhile, the different biological samples in the same group were mixed, divided into 15 equal parts, then added with 15 FITC-conjugated Vβ mAb, and stained with the fixable viability dye eFluor506 and antibodies for surface markers, at 4°C for 25 min. The specific operational procedures and the flow analysis method are illustrated in Figure S3.

Real-time PCR analysis

RNA was extracted from T cells using TRIzol (TIANGEN, dp424) and reverse transcribed to cDNA (TAKARA, RR036A) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RT-PCR was performed with SYBR green Master Mix (Vazyme, Q311-00) on Roche LC480. The sequences of primers used were listed. The primers for mouse B2m (forward: 5′-AGA CTG ATA CAT ACG CCT GCA G-3’; reverse: 5′-GCA GGT TCA AAT GAA TCT TCA G-3′), mouse Tcf7 (forward: 5′- AAC ACA GGC AGA ACC CAA GG-3’; reverse: 5′- GTG TGC ACT CAG CAA TGA CC-3′), and mouse Xcl1 (forward: 5′- TAG CTG TGT GAA CTT ACA AAC CC -3’; reverse: 5′- ACA GTC TTG ATC GCT GCT TTC -3′)

TCGA survival analysis and hazard ratio estimation

To excluded potential confounding from Bulk-RNA seq datasets, we constructed a Cox proportional hazards model allowing dynamic interaction to evaluate survival risk, specified as: ‘logh(t) = logh_0 (t) + β_1⋅XCL1 + β_2⋅CD8B + β_3⋅(XCL1 × CD8B) + β_4⋅NCAM1’. Survival analysis was performed using the ‘survival’ package (Terry M. Therneau, Patricia M. Grambsch (2000)) and results were visualized with ‘survminer’ package (Kassambara A, Kosinski M, Biecek P(2021)). Specifically, we obtained transcriptomic data of tumors from TCGA. To determine the impact of gene A on tumor progression, we first calculated the median expression of gene A across all samples. Based on this, samples were categorized into high and low gene A expression groups. A survival object was created using the ‘Surv’ function and survival analysis was performed using the ‘survfit’ function. When assessing the combined effect of multiple genes on tumor progression, the gene expression levels were multiplied. The effect of differential expression of gene A affects cancer prognosis based on the hazard ratio (HR). The results shown here are in part based upon data generated by the TCGA Research Network (https://www.cancer.gov/tcga). Specifically, the transcriptomic data of 33 tumor types from TCGA were downloaded. For each tumor type, we first calculated the median expression of gene A across all samples. Based on this, samples were divided into high and low gene A expression groups. Using the survival package, a Cox proportional hazards model was constructed to obtain the HR for gene A in that tumor type. An HR > 1 indicates that high gene A expression increases the risk of poor prognosis, while an HR < 1 suggests that high gene A expression decreases the risk. A p-value <0.05 indicates that the HR is statistically significant. To control the false discovery rate, all p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. To assess whether the expression of gene A in a specific cell type (CD8+ T cells) affects prognosis, the relative expression of gene A in CD8+ T cells was calculated and adjusted for the expression of gene A in NK cells. Specifically, this was done using the formula: ‘exp (gene A) ∗ exp (CD8B)’.

ICB therapy dataset analysis

To assess the association between XCL1 expression and clinical outcomes in patients receiving immune checkpoint blockade therapy, we collected and integrated bulk RNA-seq data and corresponding survival information from two publicly available cohorts (GSE78220 and PRJEB23709). Patients were stratified into XCL1-high and XCL1-low groups based on the upper (top 25%) and lower (bottom 25%) quartiles of XCL1 expression levels, respectively. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated to compare overall survival between groups. Statistical significance was evaluated using the log rank test. The analyses were performed in R (v.4.3.2) using the ‘survival’ (v.3.3-1) and ‘survminer’ (v.0.5.0) packages. Single-cell RNA-seq data from pre-treatment samples of patients receiving ICB therapy were obtained from the GSE235863 dataset. CD8+ T cells were identified based on canonical marker expression. XCL1 expression levels were compared between responders and non-responders. Statistical significance was assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as implemented in R.

Cell sorting for single-cell sequencing

C57BL/6 mice were inoculated (s.c.) with B16-OVA mouse melanoma cells (3 × 105) and received (i.v.) adoptive transfer of OT-I cells on day 9, and intratumoral CD45+ cells were isolated by flow cytometry for scRNA-seq on day 12 post-transfer (n = 7 mice per group). In brief, tumor-infiltrating CD45+ cells from per group were enriched by density gradient centrifugation using Percoll (GE Healthcare) and stained with surface markers (live CD45+ cells), followed by sorting with an FACS Aria III Cell sorter (BD Biosciences). The isolated CD45+ cell suspension from per group was pooled and loaded into Chromium microfluidic chips with 30 (v2) chemistry and barcoded with a 10× Chromium Controller (10× Genomics). RNA from the barcoded cells was subsequently reverse-transcribed and sequencing libraries were constructed with reagents from a Chromium Single Cell 30 v2 reagent kit (10× Genomics) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing was performed with Illumina (NovaSeq) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Illumina, Novogene).

Processing single cell immune repertoire data

FASTQ files generated from cell-barcoded 5′ gene expression libraries and V(D)J enriched libraries were processed using Cell Ranger (v.7.1.0; 10× Genomics).60 The gene expression reference (GRCm38) was constructed using the ‘cellranger mkref’ command, while the V(D)J reference (GRCm38) was obtained from the Cell Ranger Download Center. Raw sequencing data were processed with the ‘cellranger multi’ function to generate gene-barcode matrices and annotated TCR sequences for downstream analysis.

scRNA-seq analysis

Data analysis was performed using R (v.4.3.2)61 and Seurat (v.4.4.0).62 For quality control, cells with fewer than 200 detected genes were identified as low quality and excluded. To accurately identify apoptotic-like cells while minimizing the bias of an arbitrary mitochondrial DNA percentage threshold, we employed a strategy adapted from Osorio and Cai.63 Specifically, we fitted a linear model between log10(total counts) and log10(mitochondrial counts), with log10(total counts) as the independent variable. A 95% confidence interval was then computed, and cells with mitochondrial gene counts exceeding this interval were filtered out and classified as apoptotic-like cells with abnormally high mitochondrial expression. Doublets in each sample were predicted using ‘DoubletFinder’ (v.2.0.3)64 with a doublet rate of 5%. Filtered gene-barcode matrices were merged and normalized using the ‘NormalizeData’ function. Highly variable features were selected using the ‘FindVariableFeatures’ function, typically focusing on the top 2,000 features. Then, data were scaled using the ‘ScaleData’ function with ‘vars.to.regress’ parameter set to ‘percent.mt’ to regress out the influence of mitochondrial gene expression. The ‘RunPCA’ function was used to perform principal components analysis and the top 30 principal components were subjected to perform uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) and cell clustering (resolution = 2). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the ‘FindAllMarkers’ function, applying a Wilcoxon Rank-Sum test with a log2 fold-change threshold of 0.25. The expression level of cluster-specific and cell-type marker genes was visualized using dot plots, violin plots, and feature plots. The ‘AddModuleScore’ function in Seurat package was used to quantify the activity of specific gene sets within individual cells. We utilized the ggplot2 (v.3.5.0)65 package in R to graph volcano plots to visualize the DEGs between cells from different samples within the same cell type. Genes with a significant differential expression were highlighted to show the differences between samples of the same cell type. We next used the ‘jjVolcano’ function in ‘scRNAtoolVis’ (v.0.1.0) package to simultaneously display the DEGs between different samples for several cell types.

Classification of adoptively transferred and host T cells

Host CD8+ T cells express the CD45.2 allele, whereas adoptive CD8+ T cells express the CD45.1 allele. There are nine variant sites between the mRNA sequences of CD45.1 and CD45.2. For each sample, we used the ‘samtools mpileup’66 function to extract the base coverage across the exonic regions of the CD45 gene for each cell with the following parameter setting: ‘--no-output-ins --no-output-ins --no-output-del --no-output-del --no-BAQ --no-output-ends -q 0 -Q 13’. Cells were then categorized based on the comparison of the nine variant sites. Cells were labeled as “Not detected” if none of the variant sites were covered by reads, as “CD45.2” if all covered sites corresponded to the CD45.2 allele, and as “CD45.1” if all covered sites corresponded to the CD45.1 allele. If the covered sites included both CD45.1 and CD45.2 alleles, the cell was categorized as “Ambiguous”. To further evaluate the classification result, we calculated three independent metrics: Identity Ratio = (NCD45.1 + NCD45.2)/(NCD45.1 + NCD45.2 + NAmbiguous); CD8 Consistency = NCD45.1(CD8+ T cells)/NCD45.1; CDR3 Consistency = NCD45.1(CDR3: CASSRANYEQYF)/NCD45.1(TCR detected).

scTCR-seq analysis

For each sample, the Cell Ranger’s V(D)J algorithm outputted annotations of high-confidence cell barcodes into the “filtered_contig_annotations.csv” file. Cells are grouped into clonotypes based on the similarity of their CDR3 sequences. Further analysis was performed using custom scripts in R. For cells annotated with more than one TRA or more than one TRB chain, the chain with the highest unique molecular identifier (UMI) count and read count for each chain type were retained. Cells that were not annotated as T cells within the corresponding scRNA-seq dataset were filtered out, and cells with both TRA and TRB sequences were retained. Then, for CD8+ T cells and CD4+ T cells, clonotype frequencies were recalculated separately. To analyze the distribution and expansion of T cell clones, we categorized cells into five groups based on clonotype frequency: single (0 < frequency ≤1), small (1 < frequency ≤5), medium (5 < frequency ≤10), large (10 < frequency ≤30), and hyperexpanded (frequency >30). This classification allowed us to analyze the distribution and expansion of T cell clones, providing insights into clonal dynamics within the dataset. Clonotypes that existed in all samples were identified as “Shared clones”. Moreover, cells were identified as “p15E-specific” if their CDR3A sequences contained the motif “DYSNNRLT” or if their CDR3B sequences contained the motif “LELGG”. Additionally, cells with CDR3B sequences containing the motif “CASSRANYEQY” were classified as “SIIN- specific”.

RNA velocity analysis

RNA velocity analysis was used to infer the differentiation trajectories among CD4+ T cell subtypes and CD8+ T cell subtypes. First, ‘velocyto’ (v.0.17.17)69 was used to estimate unspliced and spliced mRNAs and generate spliced/unspliced expression matrices of cells in each sample. Then, we used ‘velocyto.R’ (v.0.6) to read the spliced/unspliced matrices of each sample. Those matrices were merged into their corresponding Seurat object and saved in h5ad format. RNA velocity was computed using ‘scVelo’ (v.0.3.2)70 by fitting a dynamical model to the spliced and unspliced mRNA counts for each cell. We then embedded velocities in UMAP.

Pathway enrichment analysis

To gain insight into the biological functions associated with different cell types, the ‘compareCluster’ function in ‘clusterProfiler’ (v.4.8.1)71 package was used to perform pathway enrichment analysis for DEGs of each cell type with the following parameter setting: ‘fun = enrichGO, ont = BP, OrgDb = org.Mm.e.g.,.db’. Specifically, for different subtypes of NK cells, we conducted pathway enrichment analysis using Biocarta gene sets from the ‘MSigDB’ database (v.2023).72 Pathways with a p-value less than 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. Additionally, we obtained all mouse gene sets from ‘MSigDB’ database and performed gene set enrichment analysis between different samples in T cells using the ‘GSEA’ function in ‘clusterProfiler’ package. The ‘gseaNb’ function in ‘scRNAtoolVis’ package was subsequently used to visualize significantly enriched pathways. The statistical test methods used were indicated in the figure legends. All calculations and visualizations were performed in R (v.4.3.2). No statistical methods were used to predetermine the sample size, and the experiment was not randomized.

Dendritic cell population profiling and cell-cell communication analysis

DC subsets, including cDC1, cDC2, DC-prolif, mregDC1, and mregDC2, were first extracted from all dendritic cells. Dot plots were then used to display the expression differences of genes of interest among OT-I, OT-I/XCL1, and OT-I/FX samples. Additionally, gene signature score was performed in the cDCs using the AUCell package, and the scored differences among OT-I, OT-I/XCL1, and OT-I/FX samples were visualized using empirical cumulative distribution function curves. We used the ‘CellPhoneDB’ tool (v.5.0.1)73 to infer signaling communication between cDCs population, CD4+ T cells, and CD8+ T cells.

Processing of public dataset

The single-cell RNA-seq data from Eberhardt et al. were downloaded from the GEO database (accession number: GSE180268). Cells with fewer than 1,000 or more than 6,000 detected genes and cells with a high mitochondrial gene percentage (>2.5%) were excluded. Cells from the primary tumor and metastatic lymph node were processed simultaneously. Downstream normalization and variable feature identification were performed. Dimensionality reduction was performed using PCA, followed by batch effect correction using the Harmony algorithm based on the first 20 principal components. Cell type annotation was performed based on the markers provided in the previous methods. The single-cell RNA-seq data from Escobar et al. were downloaded from the GEO database (accession number: GSE217038). Only a subset of samples was retained for analysis, including the following: GSM6704031, GSM6704033, GSM6704035, GSM6704037, GSM6704039, GSM6704041, GSM6704043, and GSM6704045. Cells with fewer than 200 or more than 5,000 detected genes and those with a high mitochondrial gene percentage (>5%) were excluded. Cells expressing CD4 or CD34 were also excluded. Normalization and variable features identification were performed and the mitochondrial gene expression effect was regressed out. Dimensionality reduction was performed using PCA, followed by batch effect correction using the Harmony algorithm based on the first 30 principal components. UMAP was then used for visualization and clustering analysis with a resolution of 0.7 and identified 14 distinct cell clusters. Subsequently, cell type annotation was performed using markers provided in the original publication. Three cell clusters that could not be reliably annotated were excluded from further analysis. Cells from B16-OVA or MC38-OVA-bearing mice were used to construct cell trajectories separately using ‘Monocle3’ (v.1.3.4)74 with the following parameter setting: nn.k = 25, branch_len = 10. In parallel, we inferred cellular dynamics using scTour to estimate pseudotime values independently. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to assess the concordance between pseudotime values inferred by ‘Monocle3’ and ‘scTour’. The single-cell RNA-seq data from Burger et al. were downloaded from the GEO database (accession number: GSE164177).24 Cells with fewer than 100 or more than 4,000 detected genes, with more than 20,000 reads, or with >5% of reads aligned to mitochondrial genes were excluded. Normalization and variable features identification were performed, and mitochondrial gene expression effect was regressed out. Dimensionality reduction was performed using PCA, followed by batch effect correction using the Harmony algorithm based on the first 30 principal components. The “ProjecTILs. Classifier” function from the ‘ProjecTILs’25 package was utilized to perform cell type annotation for each sample, retaining only cells identified as CD8+ T cells.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All calculations and visualizations of single-cell sequencing were performed using R. No statistical methods were used to predetermine the sample size, and the experiment was not randomized. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad PRISM version 8.0 (GraphPad software) except for scRNA-seq data. Experiments were performed at least three times and values were obtained as the mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was performed with Student’s t test, one-way or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test, and log rank (Mantel-Cox) test for survival analysis as mentioned in the figure legends. No statistically significant differences were considered when p values were larger than 0.05.
