Reinterpreting Amitriptyline in Extra-esophageal Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease Through the GABAergic Inhibitory Axis
Authors: Qing Xiao, Ziwei Li, Anlong Yuan, Jun Xiao
Published: 2026

TO THE EDITOR: In a single-center, single-blind, randomized crossover pilot trial, Woo et al1 evaluated the efficacy of low-dose amitriptyline (10 mg nightly) added to standard proton pump inhibitor (PPI) therapy for patients with extra-esophageal manifestations of gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) refractory to ≥ 4 weeks of PPI treatment. In this study of 40 patients, both the amitriptyline plus PPI arm and the PPI-only arm demonstrated substantial symptom improvement and quality-of-life gains; however, the addition of amitriptyline did not confer a statistically significant advantage over PPI monotherapy (65% vs 85% response rate, P = 0.273).1

This neutral result highlights important methodological considerations. First, participants were defined as “PPI-refractory” based on 4 weeks of acid suppression without objective physiologic testing (eg, pH-impedance and reflux symptom correlation), which may result in inclusion of patients with acid-responsive disease, functional heartburn, or visceral hypersensitivity that are indistinguishable by symptom assessment alone. Second, amitriptyline’s neuromodulatory effects—operating through enhanced descending inhibitory pathways—typically manifest over longer periods than the 4-week treatment window used in this trial. Third, the crossover design lacked a washout period, potentially blunting detection of cumulative neuromodulatory effects and introducing carryover in central sensitization. Collectively, these factors limit inference regarding amitriptyline’s potential efficacy among physiologically enriched subpopulations.

Comparative literature in functional esophageal disorders offers additional context. In a randomized placebo-controlled trial of nortriptyline in non-erosive reflux disease, 21 days of treatment significantly reduced acid-induced brain responses on functional magnetic resonance imaging, though clinical symptom differences were not significant, suggesting central modulation without detectable short-term clinical benefit.2 Similarly, imipramine did not outperform placebo in patients with esophageal hypersensitivity or functional heartburn, although modest quality-of-life improvements were reported.3 Meta-analyses indicate that while central neuromodulators may reduce symptom severity in conditions such as functional chest pain and globus, evidence for benefit in reflux hypersensitivity and functional heartburn remains inconsistent.4 Furthermore, a Bayesian network meta-analysis of antidepressants in GERD suggested that certain agents (eg, combined flupentixol-melitracen and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors) may achieve higher symptom remission rates compared to placebo, whereas traditional tricyclic antidepressants did not show superiority.5

These findings underscore 2 important points. First, antidepressant neuromodulators modulate visceral sensation and central pain processing, as reflected in increased pain thresholds and altered brain activity in experimental paradigms, even when clinical endpoints lag.6 Second, heterogeneity in pathophysiology matters: neuromodulators may benefit subsets of patients characterized by visceral hypersensitivity or central sensitization rather than those whose symptoms are driven predominantly by acid exposure or peripheral mechanisms.

Building on this nuanced evidence base, we propose a hierarchical mechanistic framework (Figure) in which amitriptyline is conceptualized as an anxiolytic-based, network-level neuromodulator that exerts indirect effects on GABAergic inhibitory circuits. In contrast to direct GABAA receptor agonists or positive allosteric modulators (Table), amitriptyline’s pharmacodynamics involve enhancement of descending monoaminergic inhibition and require sufficient treatment duration and appropriate patient phenotyping to reveal clinical efficacy. This perspective may help reconcile mixed clinical results and guide future trial designs in extra-esophageal GERD toward mechanism-enriched cohorts, extended treatment windows, and objective physiological subtyping.
