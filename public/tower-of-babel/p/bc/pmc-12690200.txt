Magnetic resonance imaging determination of tissue sodium across the CKD spectrum—associations and implications for health
Authors: Kylie Martin, Vijay Venkatraman, Sven-Jean Tan, Timothy D Hewitson, Patricia Robertson, Nigel D Toussaint
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIncreased tissue sodium (Na+) concentration is associated with adverse clinical outcomes in people with chronic kidney disease (CKD) including hypertension, inflammation and increased cardiovascular disease (CVD). 23-Sodium magnetic resonance imaging (23Na-MRI) can non-invasively quantify tissue Na+ concentration. However, in the CKD population, few studies have evaluated relationships between 23Na-MRI-derived tissue Na+ concentrations and measures of Na+ metabolism, surrogate markers of CVD and other CKD-related health complications.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional cohort study, involving 51 participants with CKD (28 with non-dialysis CKD, 23 on dialysis), to explore relationships between 23Na-MRI-derived tissue Na+ concentrations (including skin, bone, muscle and whole leg-tissue), measures of Na+ metabolism and CKD-related health outcomes. Multivariable regression and correlation analyses were used to assess associations.ResultsSkin and whole leg-tissue Na+ concentrations were positively associated with surrogate cardiovascular markers of troponin I and brain natriuretic peptide (P < .05). Bone and whole leg-tissue Na+ concentrations were negatively associated with bone mineral density. Higher muscle Na+ concentration was associated with lower levels of physical well-being. Tissue Na+ concentration was positively correlated with extracellular fluid volume as measured by proton (1H) MRI (P ≤ .001) and body composition monitor (P < .05).ConclusionWhole leg-tissue Na+ in addition to skin Na+ concentration may be a useful clinical marker of body Na+ with related health outcomes. Tissue Na+ may play a role in CVD, bone health and quality of life in people with CKD, representing a potential therapeutic target to improve clinical outcomes.Keywords: bone, cardiovascular disease, magnetic resonance imaging, quality of life, sodium

INTRODUCTION

Excess body sodium (Na+) is a leading modifiable risk factor for hypertension and cardiovascular disease (CVD), particularly in people with chronic kidney disease (CKD) [1, 2]. Excess Na+ exerts its effects on blood pressure and contributes to cardiovascular, cerebrovascular and kidney disease [3], via multiple pathways including extracellular volume expansion, increased tissue interstitial storage (such as in skin and muscle) and through its activation of an inflammatory milieu and downstream disruption of the metabolic profile [4, 5]. The negative impact of Na+ on health outcomes is accentuated in people with CKD as the capacity to excrete Na+ is decreased [3] and tubular Na+ reabsorption in the kidneys is increased as a result of higher circulating aldosterone levels [6], activation of the intrarenal renin–angiotensin system [6] and metabolic acidosis [7]. Sodium is also implicated in uraemia-related changes in metabolism and muscle mass [8], influencing oxidative stress pathways leading to inflammation [9, 10] and resultant sarcopenia, insulin resistance and obesity [11]. As the prevalence of CKD continues to rise globally [4], improved management of excess body Na+ to mitigate adverse effects is critical in improving health outcomes in this cohort with disrupted Na+ physiology.

Body Na+ is conventionally understood to be osmotically active intravascularly, regulated neuro-hormonally and by the kidneys [5]. However, several long-term studies in humans have shown that Na+ can be retained or removed from the human body without commensurate water retention or loss, suggesting that Na+ could be stored in a body compartment other than plasma contained in the intravascular space [4, 5]. Consistent with this, recently developed imaging tools, such as 23-sodium magnetic resonance imaging (23Na-MRI), have been used to visualize and quantify Na+ in the tissue interstitial compartment of skin, muscle and bone. This tissue interstitial compartment is increasingly recognized as having a role in body Na+ regulation [12].

Higher skin and muscle Na+ concentrations are associated with various pathological outcomes including hypertension, left ventricular hypertrophy, inflammation, insulin-resistance and adverse cardiovascular events (reviewed in [12]). However, there are several current knowledge gaps including (i) whether skin, muscle and bone Na+ concentrations are independently associated with clinically used Na+ measures and clinical parameters affected by Na+ metabolism (including CVD and bone health), and (ii) how excess tissue Na+, a possible ‘uraemic toxin’ [13], may impact quality of life. Although many studies have emphasized the considerable symptom burden and poor quality of life in people with CKD [14], little is known about the potential contribution of tissue Na+ on patient symptoms and the lived experience of kidney disease.

The aims of this study were therefore to assess 23Na-MRI-derived tissue Na+ concentrations and its associations with biochemical and clinical parameters related to Na+ metabolism and CKD-related health outcomes including CVD, bone mineral density (BMD) and quality of life, across the CKD spectrum.

Concise materials and methods

We undertook a single-centre, cross-sectional cohort study involving people with CKD. Supplementary material provides comprehensive details of materials and methods used in this study. The study protocol was approved by the Melbourne Health Research Ethics Committee (HREC 2021.027).

Study participants

Study participants were 18 years of age or over and provided written informed consent. Study participants belonged to three cohorts: (i) CKD stages 3–5 not on dialysis [according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines [7], where CKD stage 3a pertained to estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) 45–59 mL/min/1.73 m2, CKD stage 3b eGFR 30–44 mL/min/1.73 m2, CKD stage 4 eGFR 25–29 mL/min/1.73 m2 and CKD stage 5 eGFR <15 mL/min/1.73 m2], (ii) kidney failure on chronic thrice-weekly haemodialysis (HD) and (iii) kidney failure on peritoneal dialysis (PD). Patients on dialysis had been established on their respective modality for at least 3 months at the time of recruitment.

Clinical and biochemical assessments including body Na+ and volume measures

Demographic and clinical data were collected from participants and electronic medical records. To assess dietary Na+, we conducted a scored Na+ questionnaire (SSQ) [8] and a 24-h dietary record (Supplementary data, Table S1 for more details). Twenty-four-hour and spot urine Na+ collections were used to assess Na+ excretion. A body composition monitor (BCM) (Fresenius Medical Care Australia Pty Ltd, Macquarie Park, NSW, Australia) was used to assess fluid status. To assess surrogate cardiovascular markers, blood samples for troponin I and brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) were collected and the presence of aortic vascular calcification on a 24-point scale on lateral dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) was assessed. BMD was assessed via DXA. Health-related quality of life (HRQOL) was assessed using the Kidney Disease Quality of Life (KDQOL)-36 instrument [9].

23Na-MRI acquisition and tissue sodium quantification

Proton (1H) and 23Na-MRI scans of the left calf on the centre of a 23Na knee coil (Rapid Biomedical, Rimpar, Germany) were undertaken in each participant on a 3.0 Tesla whole-body MRI system (MAGNETOM Prisma Fit, Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany, software version VE11C) as previously described [10]. Tissue water content was quantified by 1H-MRI using the spectral pre-saturation with inversion recovery (SPIR) sequence (TA = 2.20 min, TR = 2800 ms, TE = 23 ms, TI = 210 ms, FA = 123º, resolution: 0.75 × 0.75 × 5.0 mm3). Regions of interest (ROIs) for tissue compartments included skin (encompassing the outer most pixel circumferentially), muscle excluding major vessels, tibial bone and whole leg-tissue (skin, subcutaneous tissue and muscle excluding major vessels). ROIs were manually segmented using Insight Toolkit (ITK—SNAP) software (version 3.8.0) on a T1 image co-registered to the 23Na image and maps of tissue 23Na concentration were generated as detailed previously [10] (Fig. 1). Results were expressed as an arbitrary unit (mmol/L equivalents) relative to the calibration tube [40 mmol/L sodium chloride (NaCl) [10, 11]]. A 10 mmol/L NaCl tube was used as a calibration standard to generate maps of tissue water content using FSL tools, as previously described [15]. The maps were co-registered to the 23Na map using a T1 image. The tissue water content values were represented in arbitrary units.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS for Windows version 30.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Parametric distribution was assessed both graphically and with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) if normally distributed and median [interquartile range (IQR)] if non-normally distributed. Categorical variables were expressed as proportions and percentages. Transformation of data was used as appropriate.

Differences between groups (CKD vs dialysis, CKD stages, CKD vs HD vs PD) were determined using independent-samples t-test or one-way analysis of variance, Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis tests, and Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests for normally distributed, non-normally distributed continuous variables and categorical variables respectively. Correlations between each ROI of tissue Na+ concentrations and clinical or biochemical parameters or KDQOL-36 composite and component scores were calculated using the Pearson correlation coefficient (r) for normally distributed and Spearman correlation coefficient (rs) for non-normally distributed variables.

Univariate linear regression was used to analyse relationships between (i) skin and whole leg-tissue Na+ concentrations and surrogate markers of CVD as suggested by previous published literature [16, 17], and (ii) bone and whole leg-tissue Na+ concentrations with DXA parameters to explore potential links between bone Na+ content and/or excess body Na+ with BMD as suggested by physiological mechanisms [18].

Further evaluation of whole leg-tissue Na+ concentration was undertaken to determine its potential role in clinical outcomes. Multiple linear regression using the enter method was used to model the variables associated with whole leg-tissue Na+ concentration. Unstandardized β coefficients, R2 and adjusted R2 values for goodness-of-fit were reported. 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed. P-values <.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline demographics

The study included 51 participants (28 people with CKD not on dialysis and 23 with kidney failure on dialysis). One participant initially enrolled had incomplete 23Na-MRI-derived Na+ concentration data and was removed from the analysis. All other data variables for participants were analysed. The CKD group consisted of stage 3a (n = 3), stage 3b (n = 11), stage 4 (n = 9) and stage 5 (n = 5) patients. The dialysis group consisted of 13 people on HD and 10 people on PD (automated PD except for one subject on continuous ambulatory PD). Dialysis vintage for participants on HD was 596 days (IQR 426–1411) and for those on PD was 396 days (139.3–764.8). Glucose- and icodextrin-based PD dialysate Na+ concentration was fixed (132 mmol/L). The mean dialysate Na+ concentration for those on HD was 137.9 ± 2.0 mmol/L. People on HD were considered anuric if they self-estimated their urinary volume <200 mL/day (n = 6). Two people on HD and one person on PD had a previous failed kidney transplant. Table 1 shows the main characteristics of the study cohort. There was a difference in vitamin D use between those with CKD vs on dialysis (P = .009). Only one person on HD was taking an oral bisphosphonate and none was prescribed denosumab. Other medical history relevant to tissue Na+ concentrations included that three participants had a history of hypothyroidism and were on oral thyroxine replacement (CKD n = 2, dialysis n = 1) and one person with CKD had a history of hypoaldosteronism managed with oral hydrocortisone and fludrocortisone replacement.

Surrogate cardiovascular markers

On univariate linear regression, whole leg-tissue Na+ concentration was positively associated with troponin I, where a 10% increase in whole leg-tissue Na+ concentration was associated with a 14.8% increase in troponin I (95% CI 0.45% to 31.2%). Whole leg-tissue Na+ concentration was also positively associated with BNP, where every 1 mmol/L equivalent increase in whole leg-tissue Na+ concentration was associated with an increase in BNP of 5.8% (95% CI 2.6% to 9%). Similarly, skin Na+ concentration was positively associated with troponin I, where every 1 mmol/L equivalent increase in skin Na+ concentration was associated with an increase in troponin of 6.2% (95% CI 2.3% to 10.2%). There was also a positive association between skin Na+ concentration and BNP, with every 1 mmol/L equivalent increase in skin Na+ concentration associated with an increase in BNP of 7.5% (95% CI 3.7% to 11.29%). In contrast, there was no correlation between skin or whole leg-tissue Na+ concentration and presence of aortic vascular calcification on lateral DXA.

Bone mineral density

On univariate analysis, there was a negative association between bone Na+ concentration and femoral T-score, where every 1 mmol/L equivalent increase in bone Na+ concentration was associated with a decrease in femoral T-score on DXA of 0.06 (95% CI 0.02 to 0.95). There was also a negative association for whole leg-tissue Na+ concentration with femoral T-score on DXA. These findings were consistent with assessment of global BMD. Those with osteopenia and osteoporosis on DXA had a higher mean bone Na+ concentration compared with those with normal DXA scan [median bone Na+ concentration in osteopenia 18.8 mmol/L equivalents (IQR 15.7–23.3, P = .03) vs osteoporosis 31.1 mmol/L equivalents (18.2–40, P = .006) vs normal BMD 13.2 mmol/L equivalents (10.5–17.6)]. Those with osteoporosis on DXA had a higher mean whole leg-tissue Na+ concentration compared with those with normal DXA [median whole leg-tissue Na+ concentration in osteoporosis 44.6 mmol/L equivalents (IQR 40.1–53.6) vs normal BMD 36.7 mmol/L equivalents (29.2–40.7, P = .04)].

Quality of life

There was a negative correlation between KDQOL-36 physical composite score and mean muscle Na+ concentration (rs = –0.32, P = .03). There was no correlation however between whole leg-tissue, skin or muscle Na+ concentration (Tables 5A, 5B and 5C, respectively) and kidney-disease targeted HRQOL domains of ‘symptoms of kidney disease’, ‘burden of kidney disease’ and ‘effects of kidney disease’.

DISCUSSION

We report, to our knowledge, the first study in an Australian CKD cohort to assess associations between 23Na-MRI-determined tissue Na+ concentrations and markers of Na+ metabolism, CVD, bone health and HRQOL. Although mechanisms require further elucidation, there is emerging evidence that increased tissue Na+ is associated with poorer clinical outcomes in people with CKD, over and above risks posed by traditional intravascular Na+ effects on extracellular fluid volume [19].

This study found whole leg-tissue and muscle Na+ concentrations were increased in people with kidney failure on dialysis compared with those with non-dialysis CKD. Another study similarly showed a difference in muscle Na+ concentration between dialysis and non-dialysis CKD patients [11], however their CKD cohort was smaller and only assessed the soleus muscle, whereas our study assessed all calf muscle groups. Like other studies, we found no difference in skin Na+ concentration between dialysis and CKD populations [11, 14].

Our study exemplifies the complex physiology of body Na+. Skin and whole leg-tissue Na+ concentrations were not correlated with most clinically used measurements of dietary Na+ intake and urinary Na+ excretion. Similarly, other studies have reported no differences in residual urine output, spot urine Na+ or 24-h urine Na+ with changes in skin or muscle Na+ concentration [16, 20, 21]. Positive correlations between Na+ concentration and water content in skin, muscle and whole leg-tissue regions suggest tissue water expansion parallels tissue Na+ accumulation. This finding reflects an increase in extracellular volume [22], in opposition to other studies that have suggested tissue Na+ accrual is water-free [23, 24]. In support of our imaging findings, BCM-determined extracellular water and overhydration measurements were positively correlated with skin Na+ concentration, and overhydration was positively correlated with whole-tissue Na+ concentration. Other studies found a correlation between skin Na+ and water content but did not find such a relationship in muscle regions [25] and this may be explained by differences in study population (including presence of CKD). Variables of age, serum albumin and overhydration associated with whole tissue Na+ concentration could also reflect extracellular volume expansion [22] in settings of reduced plasma oncotic pressure from lower serum albumin, potentially from inflammation and reduced protein-caloric intake or impaired endothelium, and resultant transcapillary leak of albumin [26, 27].

Our study highlights the potential role tissue Na+ may have in contributing to adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Tissue Na+ has been shown to drive a low-grade inflammatory and fibrotic response implicated in hypertension and cardiovascular disease [28, 29] and metabolic disease [30], particularly in people with CKD. Consistent with this, whole leg-tissue Na+ concentrations were positively associated with surrogate cardiovascular markers of troponin I and BNP. A retrospective study of dialysis patients by Salerno et al. demonstrated increasing skin Na+ concentration quartiles were a significant predictor of major adverse cardiovascular outcomes [16]. Similarly, a study by Friedrich et al. reported skin and muscle Na+ was higher in those on HD with a history of cardiovascular disease compared with those on HD without cardiovascular disease [31]. Cross-sectional evaluation of surrogate cardiovascular markers with measurement of 23Na-MRI-determined tissue Na+ concentrations could aid in risk-stratifying adverse cardiovascular outcomes in people with CKD. Another study of people on HD did not find a correlation between N-terminal pro-BNP (NT-proBNP) and skin or muscle Na+ concentration [32]. Prospective follow-up of our cohort which includes people with CKD in addition to people on dialysis could shed more light on potential implications of tissue Na+ on cardiovascular health across the CKD spectrum.

Higher bone and whole leg-tissue Na+ concentrations were associated with reduced bone density, both long-bones and global BMD. Excess salt intake has been linked to osteoporosis [18], with enhanced bone loss and impaired bone microarchitecture [33]. Bone Na+ concentrations may be a potential explanatory link between increased CVD and poor bone health in people with CKD [18], via inflammatory and calcification pathways [34, 35].

We also reported that those with lower muscle Na+ concentrations had higher quality of life physical health measures. Greater muscle Na+ concentration is correlated with insulin resistance and inflammation in CKD [14, 36] which can affect physical functioning. Specifically with sarcopenia in CKD [37], shrinkage of muscle fibres leading to muscle volume loss can result in an increase in extracellular Na+ content and higher muscle Na+ concentration on 23Na-MRI scans [38]. Therefore, higher physical functioning and less physical limitations in those with lower muscle Na+ concentration may be explained by greater muscle bulk (and increased insulin sensitivity and less inflammation).

Our recently reported findings suggest evaluation of whole leg-tissue Na+ concentration, in addition to skin, muscle and bone Na+ concentration as described in other studies [11, 14, 16, 39–42]. Inherent low resolution and partial volume effects of current 23Na-MRI technology, which routinely uses 30-mm-thick slices when cutaneous thickness is approximately only 1 mm, makes it difficult to differentiate between cutaneous and subcutaneous tissue [43]. Therefore, whole leg-tissue Na+ assessment has clinical utility, and is more practical and reproducible than measuring skin Na+ in isolation using currently available 23Na-MRI technology [17]. Assessment of whole leg-tissue Na+ concentration has also been suggested in a paediatric cohort [44]. Here, we show associations of whole leg-tissue Na+ concentrations with cardiovascular markers and bone health markers and assess explanatory variables of whole leg-tissue Na+ concentrations.

There were several limitations of this study. Although associations were identified, this is an observational, exploratory study and other potential confounders associated with outcomes were not included due to the small sample size. The study had small numbers, and inherent heterogeneity of multiple factors can influence tissue Na+ concentration. These include different dialysis modalities and residual kidney function, other medical history and medications. Other variables that may influence tissue Na+ accumulation include the pre- or post-menopausal state of our female cohort and medications that may affect endogenous hormone regulation [45, 46]. Future studies with larger numbers could explore these factors further, as well as include a healthy control group for comparison. This would also allow more complete evaluation of potential confounders in a multivariable analysis. Our DXA analysis routinely provides BMD outputs and does not routinely include other body composition analysis (e.g. muscle mass) but more detailed assessment of muscle mass would be useful in investigating muscle Na+ accumulation in relation to muscle mass (and sarcopenia) in CKD. Similarly, other novel assessments of muscle from MRI such as simultaneous measurement of psoas muscle bulk and muscle Na+ concentrations may be interesting approaches to explore potential relationships [47]. Further prospective evaluation of cardiac and bone health outcomes in larger prospective studies may substantiate the importance of tissue Na+.

In conclusion, assessment of tissue Na+ concentrations, not currently captured in routine measures of body Na+, may be clinically valuable, especially in people with CKD. 23Na-MRI-derived tissue Na+ concentrations may complement clinically used measures of body Na+ (including dietary intake and urinary excretion). Tissue Na+ concentrations may affect surrogate cardiovascular markers, bone density and patient physical wellbeing, potentially representing a therapeutic target for improving clinical outcomes in people with CKD.
