Extraction and characterization of antibacterial marine polysaccharide K-Carrageenan from Kappaphycus alvarezii against multidrug-resistant wound associated bacteria
Authors: Ishwarya Ramachandran, Vaseeharan Baskaralingam, Preetham Elumalai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Antimicrobial resistance (AMR)-related diseases have ruined the lives of many people and the multidrug-resistant bacterial infections have a key role in the mortality associated with antimicrobial resistance (AMR). The focus was shifted to natural resources for the development of antimicrobial compounds in order to overcome this resistance. In this study K-Carrageenan was extracted from Kappaphycus alvarezii and characterized by the Fourier transform infrared (FTIR), Gas chromatography mass spectrometry (GC–MS), Liquid chromatography mass spectrum (LC–MS) and Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (H-NMR) analysis. The antibacterial property of carrageenan was tested using the Minimum Inhibitory Concentration and the bacterial cell growth rate. The results showed higher antibacterial activity of K-Carrageenan against S. aureus, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, P. vulgaris, and V. parahaemolyticus. Well diffusion method, growth curve studies, anti-biofilm studies were done in K-Carrageenan. Intracellular protein molecules and nucleic acid leakage confirmed that 100 µg/mL of K-Carrageenan increase membrane permeability, leading to cell death. At a concentration of 100 µg/mL of K-Carrageenan was non-cytotoxic to RBCs cells and promoted antibacterial and antibiofilm properties against the wound pathogens. From this study, these results determine the strong potential of K-Carrageenan treating MDR bacteria-infected chronic wounds.

Introduction

Cutaneous injuries are a growing problem worldwide, around 300 million people worldwide suffer from chronic wounds and it is continued to be a major cause of morbidity and death for burn victims1,2. Multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria will kill 10 million people by year 2050, exceeding the 8.2 million people who die from all cancers worldwide, according to a United Nations ranking3. Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) related disorders have already claimed the lives of over 4.95 million people, while bacterial AMR infections alone have claimed the lives of nearly 1.27 million people. Deaths from deadly diseases including HIV/AIDS and malaria are less common than this number4,5. Infection of wounds following burn injury poses a significant risk to patients due to dysregulated and suppressed innate and adaptive immune responses, making it more difficult to eliminate infections6–8. Multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria have been associated with the progression of chronic wounds. These include Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, Acinetobacter baumannii, Enterococcus faecium, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Enterobacter spp., and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, among others9–13. Even worse, a meta-analysis of clinical trials found that bacterial biofilms are present in about 80% of these wounds. According to Frykberg and Banks (2015), these MDR bacteria create biofilms that hinder the response of dermal and epidermal tissues to therapy14. By producing antibiotic-inactivating enzymes, decreasing cell permeability, expressing efflux systems, and altering the antibiotic’s target location, these bacteria develop resistance to antibiotics15,16.

The global health industry now faces a greater financial burden from wound care as a result of the recent surge in AMR. Finding a different sustainable supply of innovative and promising antimicrobial drugs is therefore essential. It is important to note that microbial infection and the wound healing process are closely related. Bacterial growth and colonization can occur in the chronic wound microenvironment due to its alkaline nature17. The existence of bacterial biofilm and highly virulent and/or resistant wound pathogens present significant obstacles to the development of therapeutics for wound infections18. Because of its many positive health impacts, there has been a lot of interest recently in separating new bioactive chemicals from marine resources. Marine algae are an important source of structurally varied bioactive chemicals among marine resources. Sulfated polysaccharides (SPs), such as ulvans in green algae, carrageenan in red algae, and fucoidans in brown algae, are abundant in the cell walls of marine algae. The red algae species Gigartina, Chondrus, and Eucheuma contain the highly sulphated polysaccharide carrageenan. Numerous established biological activities, including anticancer19, antiviral20, and anticoagulant21 properties can be found in carrageenan. Antibacterial compounds based on carrageenan have gained increasing attention recently. The identification of a novel antibacterial agent was the aim of this investigation. The alkaline technique was used to extract the K-Carrageenan from K. alvarezii. The FTIR, GC–MS, LC–MS and H-NMR analyses were used to evaluate the structure and characteristics of K-Carrageenan. More specifically, K-Carrageenan’s antibacterial and antibiofilm properties against human wound pathogens such as S. aureus, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, P. vulgaris, and V. parahaemolyticus were thoroughly examined. Furthermore, experiments were conducted to evaluate membrane damage and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated by exposure to K-Carrageenan in order to provide insight into their mode of action. Experiments on apoptosis, intracellular protein, and nucleic acid leakage were also examined.

Antibacterial activity

Evaluation of the antibacterial activity of K-Carrageenan was determined initially by the Agar well diffusion method against five bacterial pathogens (Fig. 5). The highest zone of inhibition was recorded at a concentration of 100 µg/ml against all pathogens. A concentration-dependent increase in antibacterial activity was observed, with higher concentrations (100 µg/ml) producing significantly higher inhibition zones compared to lower concentrations (25 µg/ml).Fig. 5Agar well diffusion of bactericidal activity of K-Carrageenan against Amoxicillin (a), S. aureus, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, P. vulgaris, and V. parahaemolyticus 25 µg/ml (b), 50 µg/ml (c), 75 µg/ml (d) and 100 µg/ml (e).

Agar well diffusion of bactericidal activity of K-Carrageenan against Amoxicillin (a), S. aureus, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, P. vulgaris, and V. parahaemolyticus 25 µg/ml (b), 50 µg/ml (c), 75 µg/ml (d) and 100 µg/ml (e).

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)

The K-Carrageenan was subjected to the determination of minimum inhibitory concentration values. The MICs ranging from 10–100 µg/ml on S. aureus, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, P. vulgaris, and V. parahaemolyticus. The 24-h OD values of the microbiological growth in the microtiter plate are used to summarize the MIC values of K-Carrageenan (Fig. 6). The final bacterial concentration was substantially lower (P < 0.05) when the tested concentration of K-carrageenan was increased. The growth of bacterial pathogens was very less at a concentration at 100 μg/ml of K-Carrageenan.Fig. 6Minimum Inhibitory concentration of K-Carrageenan against the S. aureus, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, P. vulgaris, and V. parahaemolyticus.

Minimum Inhibitory concentration of K-Carrageenan against the S. aureus, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, P. vulgaris, and V. parahaemolyticus.

Growth curve

Growth inhibition against S. aureus, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, P. vulgaris, and V. parahaemolyticus was evaluated using optical density to see if K-Carrageenan at varying concentrations shown antibacterial properties. The growth curves of the five bacteria are displayed in Fig. 7 following treatment with varying concentrations of K-Carrageenan. The K-carrageenan’s OD 600 was lower than the control, and the inhibition increased as the concentrations of 50 and 100 μg/ml. The treated bacterial cells were stained with propidium iodide at various intervals and examined under a fluorescence microscope in order to confirm the bactericidal mode of action of K-carrageenan (Fig. 8). Only when the bacterial cell membrane is damaged may propidium iodide enter the cell. Biofilm inhibition

Based on the percentage of biofilm inhibition, the K-Carrageenan exhibited significant antibiofilm activity against all tested bacterial pathogens (Fig. 9B). For S. aureus, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, P. vulgaris, and V. parahaemolyticus the K-Carrageenan showed the highest antibiofilm activity at 100 μg/ml with inhibition percentages of 80.2%, 75.8%, 70.6%, 85.3% and 73.46%, respectively (Fig. 9B).

Antibiofilm activity

The ability of K-Carrageenan to suppress biofilm formation against known biofilm-forming bacteria, including S. aureus, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, P. vulgaris, and V. parahaemolyticus, was investigated. Here, optical microscopy and CLSM analysis were used to assess the surface morphology of this bacterial biofilm architecture and the antibiofilm effectiveness of K-Carrageenan. According to the results, K-Carrageenan caused the examined bacteria’s biofilm architecture to collapse, which led to the bacterial colonies becoming less rigid and smaller (Figs. 10, 11). The findings showed that, in comparison to 50 μg/ml, the MIC value of 100 μg/ml K-Carrageenan successfully decreased the production of biofilms in the studied bacteria. MDA levels were seen to be released from bacterial cells treated with K-Carrageenan. K-Carrageenan shown a greater release of MDA levels in tested bacterial pathogens as compared to control. Discussion

Numerous harmful bacterial pathogens can be prevented from infecting people by using carrageenan. Using K-Carrageenan oligosaccharides, the diameter of the inhibitory zone against the bacteria Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus was measured in order to assess the antibacterial activity22. The broad-spectrum antibacterial activity of oxidized kappa-carrageenan suggests that it might be a good candidate for the creation of a novel antibacterial agent. Carrageenan has been the subject of numerous studies and reviews23–33 due to its apparent potential as a pharmaceutical excipient and antibacterial properties against harmful bacteria like Aeromonas hydrophila, enterotoxigenic E. coli, Salmonella enteritidis, S. typhimurium, Staphylococcus aureus, and Vibrio mimicus. Carrageenan was discovered to have a bacteriostatic activity against S. enteritidis, and significant bacterial growth inhibition was attained, especially when I-CG was present; the sulfate residues within the carrageenan were essential to this process34.

The carrageenan extracted under optimal conditions is likely of the K-Carrageenan type (bands at 926 and 878 cm−1), as indicated by the almost identical FTIR spectra (Fig. 1). The two given spectra reveal that K-Carrageenan is present at the same time as sulfates in the C4 position (the ring of galactose) in the band 845–850 cm–135. Accordingly, the C-H stretching peak is located at 2897.26 cm−1, the sulfate stretch peak is located at 1459.75 cm−1, the ester sulfate group peak is located at 1230.51 cm−1, the glycosidic bond peak is located at 1036.54 cm−1, the 3,6-anhydro-d-galactose peak is located at 867.26 cm−1, and the secondary sulfate group peak is located at 841.42 cm−136,37. These findings demonstrated that the region of 1500–800 cm−1 had distinctive carrageenan peaks. The presence of 19 components at different concentrations at a particular retention period was determined by GC–MS analysis of the bioactive fraction of K-Carrageenan. Based on their mass/charge ratio at a specific RT, the components were divided. In the current investigation, we looked for evidence of bioactive compounds and antibacterial activity in K-Carrageenan. Astaxanthin shows promising antibacterial activity against wound pathogens, primarily by reducing oxidative stress, enhancing immune responses, and directly inhibiting bacterial growth38,39. The 3,4-Dimethoxy-benzoic acid (1-biphenyl-4-yl-ethylidene) hydrazide, a derivative of benzoic acid and hydrazide-hydrazone, exhibits antibacterial activity against wound pathogens, potentially serving as a promising antimicrobial agent40. Carotenoids, including β-carotene, exhibit potential antibacterial activity, particularly against Gram-positive bacteria, and can be used as natural antimicrobial agents in wound healing applications, according to research. Octasiloxane, specifically hexadecamethyl octasiloxane (OHM), has shown antibacterial activity against Streptococcus agalactiae and can modulate its negative impacts on immunity, antioxidants, and physiological indicators41.

Using LC–MS analysis, the phytocompounds in K-Carrageenan were discovered (Fig. 3a and b). Table 2 lists the phytocompounds that were observed. Figure 3 display the K-Carrageenan LC–MS chromatogram along with the compounds’ retention duration and intensity. Based on the degree of similarity between their molecular mass and structure, the K-Carrageenan compounds were found. Anthraquinones and angucyclines, a class of natural compounds, exhibit antibacterial activity against wound pathogens, potentially inhibiting biofilm formation, disrupting cell walls, and interfering with bacterial metabolism. Triacylglycerols (TAGs), a type of lipid, can exhibit antibacterial activity against wound pathogens, potentially due to their ability to disrupt bacterial membranes and inhibit cell wall synthesis, offering a natural alternative for wound care. Benzyl ether derivatives, particularly those with phenyl or benzimidazole substitutions, have shown promising antibacterial activity against wound pathogens like Staphylococcus aureus and Methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), with some compounds exhibiting potent activity43. Oligopeptides, specifically antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), play a crucial role in antibacterial activity against wound pathogens by disrupting bacterial cell membranes and potentially inhibiting biofilm formation, contributing to the body’s innate immune defense44. The ester compound 3β-olean-12-en-3-yl (9Z)-hexadec-9-enoate, isolated from Spondias mombin leaf, exhibits antibacterial activity, particularly against Gram-negative bacteria, and may have potential for wound healing applications45.

Bacteria can thrive in exposed wounds, particularly those that are on the skin. Antibiotic misuse can be avoided by using wound dressings that have the ability to either repel or kill bacteria46. Whereas the red dye (propidium iodide) only stains bacteria with membrane defects or failures, indicating only dead bacteria, the green dye (SYTO9) penetrates bacterial membranes and indicates live bacteria47. Figure 14 displays the percentage coverage and fluorescence microscopy images of living and dead S. aureus, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, P. vulgaris, and V. parahaemolyticus on the K-carrageenan after 6 and 12 h, respectively. The quantity of propidium iodide-stained cells increased over time in all of the K-Carrageenan-treated bacteria, indicating that the treated bacterial cells were destroyed as the incubation time lengthened. MDA generation was evaluated to confirm the mechanism by which K-Carrageenan ROS destroys the bacterial membrane via lipid peroxidation, given that the unsaturated fatty acids of cell membranes are extremely susceptible to ROS attack. Figure 15A illustrates the positive correlation between K-Carrageenan and MDA formation in bacteria, supporting the presence of ROS-induced lipid peroxidation in the bacterial membrane. The redox environment is maintained by the non-protein tripeptide GSH, which can shield cells from ROS-induced oxidative damage. GSH is then oxidized by the excess ROS to produce disulfide47. Therefore, the DTNB test was used to measure the level of GSH in order to directly confirm the oxidative damage caused by EG-induced ROS to bacteria. K-Carrageenan dramatically reduced the bacteria’s GSH concentration, according to the results (Fig. 15B). It showed that by lowering GSH, K-Carrageenan could impede bacterial growth and increase ROS expression. All things considered, this data points to K-carrageenan’s antibacterial action against Carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) through the induction of elevated ROS expression and GSH reduction, which subsequently ruptures the bacterial membrane. The SOD and POD activities of the bacterial cells treated with K-Carrageenan showed comparable outcomes (Fig. 15C, D). NAD(P)H oxidase in the respiratory chain catalyzes constant univalent electron transfer processes of oxygen molecules in cell membranes, which produces the majority of ROS in bacteria48,49. Prior research showed that animals’ DNA, RNA, lipids, and proteins were vulnerable to ROS, and that too much ROS might harm these materials50. Bacterial mortality would result from the lipid peroxidation’s alteration of the cell membrane’s properties and subsequent destruction of the membrane proteins51. Furthermore, ROS may damage DNA’s double-stranded structure, interfere with DNA’s normal replication, and raise the likelihood of mutation. Thus, in this situation, the elevated ROS expression brought on by K-Carrageenan has the ability to alter the bacterial membrane’s shape by causing lipid peroxidation of the cell membrane. This, in turn, makes the bacterial membrane more permeable, which can result in the leakage of cell materials, DNA damage, and even bacterial death52. This demonstrates how K-Carrageenan mediates the generation of ROS, which in turn promotes lipid peroxidation of the cell membrane, resulting in the destruction of the cell membrane. This directly impacts the bacterial leakage of biological molecules, ultimately causing bacterial death. Therefore, by looking at the DNA fragmentation pattern, the start of apoptosis was identified. DNA strand streaks are visible in the bacterial cells treated with K-Carrageenan (Fig. 16A, B). Since DAPI enters all cells or those going through late apoptosis when membrane integrity is gone, it was used in tandem to measure apoptosis. Nuclear condensation and fragmentation were seen in several apoptotic like dead cells (Fig. 12). Nuclear DNA in both living and preserved cells was seen using DAPI. Additionally, it combines with double-stranded nucleic acids to generate non-fluorescent intercalative complexes. Additionally, DAPI staining verified that the apoptotic nuclei were effectively increased in comparison to the control at a MIC concentration of 100 μg/ml K-carrageenan. Previous studies have shown a similar pattern, demonstrating that nanoparticles significantly increased either DNA fragmentation or death in bacterial cells53.

Materials and methods

Seaweed material and description of study area

The fresh, undamaged red algae Kappaphycus alvarezii (K. alvarezii) was collected on the Sea shores of Mandapam Region, the southeast coast of India, Gulf of Mannar (9.120388 N, 79.480894 E), Ramanathapuram District, Tamil Nadu, India. To get rid of any surface contaminants, the gathered seaweeds were soaked in a beaker filled with double-distilled water and then rinsed three times. The seaweed surface was treated with alcohol to get rid of free-floating bacteria. The cleaned sample was then allowed to dry at room temperature in a dark environment for 15 days. Following thorough drying, the seaweed samples were carefully packed, wrapped with a black cloth to keep out the sun, and kept at 4 °C for later use. The seaweed washing and processing was followed by the guidelines54.

Extraction of K-Carrageenan

The 20 g of “clean seaweed” samples were extracted for two hours at 85 °C in a 2% hot potassium hydroxide solution. The algae were neutralized by rinsing them with flowing tap water until their pH was between 8 and 9 following the alkaline extraction. The material was then transferred to an extraction tank that had temperature controls, extraction times, and ratios of seaweed to water. A revolving vacuum filter was used to filter the resultant paste using diatomaceous earth (celite) as a filter aid. In order to precipitate the carrageenan, a 1% KCl (potassium chloride) solution was added concurrently with the pumping of the filtrate into the precipitation tank. To extract as much water as possible, the carrageenan was separated on a filter press and then compressed in a hydraulic press. Following their recovery and 18 h of drying at 50 °C, the carrageenan fibers were weighed and ground into a fine powder that could pass through an 80 mesh55.

FTIR analysis

The 10 mg of K-Carrageenan were dispersed in 100 mg of potassium bromide to form a thin translucent sample for FTIR analysis. This characterization was performed using instrument equipped with a PerkinElmer 2000 infrared spectrometer (PerkinElmer, Inc. MA, USA) and the peak values of FT-IR were recorded within the range of 2500–500 cm−1, with a resolution of 4 cm−1.

GC–MS analysis

GC–Ms analysis of K-Carrageenan was performed using the Thermo GC-Trace Ultra Version: 5.0, Thermo MS DSQ II. The equipment has a DB5-MS UI capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm; 5% polydimethylsiloxane) and an autosampler (CTC Analytics) in splitless mode. The injector was operated at 230 °C and the oven temperature was programmed as follows: 60 °C for 15 min, then gradually increased to 270 °C at 3 min. The Willey and NIST libraries were used to identify the components, and their retention indices were compared. Following a comparison with those found in the computer library (NIST and Willey) connected to the GC–MS instrument, the constituents were identified and tabulated.

LC–MS analysis

The bioactive substances in the K-Carrageenan were identified using LC–ESI–MS (Liquid Chromatography-Electrospray Ionization-Mass Spectrometry). Prior to analysis, both methanolic extracts underwent a 10-min centrifugation at 12,000 rpm. Two pumps and an automated injector made up the HPLC apparatus. A C-18 column (Agilent Eclipse, 5 μ, 15 cm, 4.6 mm id) was used to achieve separation. In B, the LC conditions were 5% at 0–3 min, then increased linearly from 5–25% between 3–25 min, 20–40% between 25–40 min, and 40–50% between 40–55 min, before reaching 50–95% between 55–63 min. With the following settings, mass spectrometry ran in negative ESI mode: capillary voltage (VCap) of 3000 V; fragmentor of 150 V; skimmer of 65 V; octapole (OCT 1 RF Vpp) of 750 V; nebulizer pressure of 45 psi; drying gas temperature of 250 °C; and sheath gas temperature of 350 °C. Both MS and MS/MS modes were used to scan the mass range from m/z 50 to 1500 in order to record the mass spectra. Auto MS/MS acquisition mode was used to obtain tandem MS/MS spectra.

NMR analysis

The 10 mg of K-Carrageenan were dissolved in 600 μl of D2O containing a 0.1 mM solution of 3-(Trimethylsilyl)-propionic acid sodium salt (TSP, Sigma Aldrich) vortexed for 1 min sonicated for 5 min again centrifuged (13,200 rpm). The supernatant was transfred to a 5 mm NMR tube (Norell, NJ, USA). NMR analysis was done in automation using Bruker’s Assure-RMS 1.5 pl2 software package that utilizes an external quantification standard and the PULCON [9] equation for absolute quantification results.

Bacterial strains

The microorganisms used in this study were the common pathogens causing wound infections, Staphylococcus aureus (MTCC 737), Escherichia coli (ATCC® 700928™), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (ATCC® 27853™), Proteus vulgaris (HQ-640434), and Vibrio parahaemolyticus (KC-476545) strains was used throughout the experiment for antibacterial and antibiofilm assays. All strain were kindly provided by DAHM, Alagappa University, Karaikudi, Tamil Nadu.

Assessment of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)

The broth microdilution method used to determine the MIC on K-Carrageenan followed by CLSI guidelines. Briefly, 100 μL of nutrient broth different concentrations of K-Carrageenan extract (0, 10, 25, 50, 75, and 100 mg/mL) was prepared and transferred to each microplate well. Then, 10 μL of a fresh culture of test organisms was added. For 24 h, microplates were incubated at 37 °C. The OD600 values were recorded for microplate reader (Varioskan Lux, Thermoscientific), and the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of the carrageenan was defined as the concentration that inhibited the observable growth of the microorganism under test56.

Evaluation of antibacterial activity of K-Carrageenan

The bacterial strains were tested for antibacterial activity using the agar-well diffusion bioassay. Freshly prepared bacterial suspensions of 20 µL were inoculated onto nutrient agar plate. Then, using a sterile cork borer, wells measuring 6 mm in diameter were created, and 25, 50, 75, and 100 μg/ml of K-Carrageenan were added to the wells. Amoxicillin was used as a standard control and the plates were incubated at 37 °C at 24 h.

Time-kill kinetics assay

Time-kill assays were performed by the broth macro-dilution method in accordance with the CLSI guidelines. The K-Carrageenan at the concentrations of 50 and 100 μg/ml mixed with 100 μL of diluted bacterial suspensions and incubated at 37 °C, 100 rpm. The OD600 of the bacterial suspensions were measured at various time intervals (0, 3, 6, 9, and 12 h). The process was carried out three times, and the Optical density was plotted versus time on a graph57.

Propidium iodide staining

K-Carrageenan’s impact on the membrane integrity of tested bacterial pathogens was assessed utilizing a slightly modified protocol. According to time kill experiments for various pathogens, K-Carrageenan was given to the bacterial cells at a concentration of MIC (50 and100 μg/ml) and incubated at 37 °C for 6 and 12 h. The cell suspension was centrifuged for 10 min at 10,000 rpm following incubation. After dissolving the resulting pellet in PBS, it was incubated for 15 min at 4 °C in the dark with a solution of propidium iodide (10 µg/ml). Cell suspension (10 µl) was put on a glass slide and fixed with flour mount solution (5 µl) in order to view the cells stained with propidium iodide. After that, a coverslip was placed over the fixed cells, and observed under the Zeiss fluorescence microscope58.

Preparation of bacterial extract for antibacterial mechanisms assays

S. aureus, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, P. vulgaris, and V. parahaemolyticus (108 CFU/mL) were added to a test tube filled with nutritional broth (5 mL) in order to prepare the bacterial extract. After being treated with K-Carrageenan at concentrations of 50 and 100 μg/mL, the bacteria were cultured for a day. The crude enzyme extract was obtained by sonicating the pellet after the bacterial cells were centrifuged for 10 min at 7000 × g. The controls were bacterial cells that had not been exposed to K-Carrageenan.

DCF-DA (2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate) assay

Oxidative stress induced by K-Carrageenan the bacterial cells is reflected by ROS production. K-Carrageenan treated bacterial cells (50 and 100 μg/ml) and control cells are incubated with 10 μM DCF-DA in PBS at room temperature for 30 min. After the supernatant was collected by the centrifugation at 6000 rpm for 10 min and absorbance was measured at 525 nm (JASCO spectro fluorometer FP-8200). A fluorescent microscope was used to qualitatively image the production of ROS. K-Carrageenan was first incubated with bacterial cells for 12 h at 37 °C. After that, 100 mM DCFH2-DA was added to the bacterial solutions, and they were left to incubate for 30 min in the dark. Following that, 10 µl of the samples were put on a cover-slip glass slide and examined with a green filter under a fluorescence microscope59.

DAPI staining

DAPI labelling was used to assess the apoptosis like death ratio of the K-Carrageenan-treated cells (50 and 100 μg/ml) and control cells. Following their transfer to slides, the cells were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min at 37 °C and fixed with 3% paraformaldehyde right away. 20 ml of DAPI at a concentration of 5 mg/mL was then used to incubate them. Using fluorescent microscopy, the apoptosis like death were seen60.

DNA fragmentation assay

With a few modifications, agarose gel electrophoresis was used to analyse DNA fragmentation. Centrifugation was used to scrape and collect the treated and untreated bacterial strains. In 100 µl of lysis solution, the cell pellets were incubated for 60 min at 50 °C. After adding 250 µl of PBS buffer, an equal volume of CTAB, and 25 µl of 10% SDS, the mixture was incubated for an additional 60 min at 60 °C in a water bath. Equal volumes of chloroform and isoamyl alcohol (24:1) were added after one hour, and the mixture was incubated for two to four hours at − 20 °C. The aqueous layer was then moved to a new tube, centrifuged for 20 min at 12,000 rpm, and incubated for 20 min at − 20 °C. An equivalent volume of cold ethanol was then added. Following incubation, the pellet was centrifuged for 12,000 rpm, the supernatant was disposed of, and 70% ethanol was used three times. After that, the DNA underwent 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis, ethidium bromide staining, and UV light transilluminator visualization. Image J software (National Institutes of Health, USA). was used to calculate band intensity61.

Enzymatic assays

Malondialdehyde (MDA) level

The treated and untreated bacterial strains were mixed with 10% Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) and thiobarbituric acid (TBA) and kept in incubator at 95 °C for 60 min. After incubation, the cells were cooled down at room temperature and centrifuged for 10 min at 5000 rpm. Supernatant was collected and the absorbance was measured at 532 nm using a microplate reader (Varioskan Lux, Thermoscientific)62.

Glutathione reductase (GSH) level

The bacterial strains, both treated and untreated, were centrifuged for 30 min at 10,000 rpm. Following a phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) rinse, the pellets were lysed to extract the contents of their cells. The bacterial cells were treated with Ellman’s reagent. Following that, 200 μl of the bacterial lysate was combined with 1,800 μl of Tris buffer (30 mM, pH 8.3) and 100 μl of 0.1% DTNB (5,5-dithio-bis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid), and the mixture was allowed to settle at room temperature for 90 min in the dark. A microplate reader (Varioskan Lux, Thermoscientific) was then used to measure the absorbance of the solutions at 412 nm63.

Superoxide dismutase (SOD)

Nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT), phosphate buffer, methionine, riboflavin, and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) were used to create a reaction combination. Bacterial strains, both treated and untreated, were dissolved in PBS with the reaction mixture, then placed in a 96-well plate and left in the dark for 15 min. After that, the tubes were put in the shade, and the absorbance at 560 nm was measured at the microplate reader (Varioskan Lux, Thermoscientific)64.

Peroxidase (POD)

The POD analysis reaction solution included 1 mL of 0.01 M pyrogallol, 2 mL of 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 6.0), and 1 mL of 5 mM hydrogen peroxide. PBS-treated and untreated bacterial strains (1 mL) were added to begin the test, and it was then allowed to sit at room temperature for five minutes. After that, 1 mL of 2.5N H2SO4 was added to halt the reaction, and the amount of purpurogallin generated was calculated by measuring the optical density at 420 nm at the microplate reader (Varioskan Lux, Thermoscientific)65.

Quantitative analysis of biofilm formation

For Biofilm production, 200 μl of the bacterial suspension was added to each well of a 96-well flat-bottom microplate. After that, the plate was incubated for 48 h at 37 °C. After staining the bacterial biofilm for 15 min at room temperature with 200 μl of crystal violet 2%, the plates were rinsed three times with distilled water to get rid of any remaining stain. Thermo scientific’s Varioskan Lux microtiter plate reader was used to measure the optical density (OD) of the stained biofilm at 600 nm66.

Biofilm inhibition assay

The 100 μl of overnight culture from each bacterial strain was incubated with (50 and 100 μg/ml) of K-Carrageenan for 48 h at 37 °C. After this incubation period, the free-floating bacterial cells were gently removed by rinsing the wells three times with PBS (pH 7.3). Next, each well was stained with 150 μl of crystal violet 2% for 15 min. Absorbance values OD600nm were measured using the microtiter plate reader (Varioskan Lux, Thermoscientific)67.

Antibiofilm activity

K-Carrageenan (50 and 100 μg/ml) and glass pieces (13 × 13 mm) were added to 24-well plates containing 1 mL bacterial culture aliquots for antibiofilm tests. Under static circumstances, the 24-well plates were incubated for 16 h at 37 °C. Following a 0.01 M PBS rinse, they were put onto transparent glass slides, stained with 0.4% crystal violet and acridine orange for one min, and examined at 400 × magnification using a light field microscope and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) investigations respectively68.

Bacterial viability assay

The Invitrogen Kit L-7012 was used to conduct the bacterial viability experiment. Each 24-well plate was injected with bacterial cultures (1.0 × 105 CFU/mL) overnight. K-Carrageenan was then added to the wells, and they were incubated for one hour at 37 °C. After that, the bacterial cells were put into 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes and centrifuged for 15 min at 7500 rpm. After removing the supernatant, PBS buffer was used to wash the pellet. Two distinct dyes SYTO® 9 (3 mM) and propidium iodide (20 mM) were applied to the adhering bacteria and left for 15 min at 37 °C in the dark. With the use of fluorescence microscopy, the treated cells were observed. The number of viable cells and dead cells in each image were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institute of Health, USA). Live cell ratio (%) was calculated as the ratio of live cell nuclei (green) to the total cell nuclei68.

Hemocompatibility studies

The blood sample was willingly donated by our corresponding author Dr. Punnadath Preetham E. with informed consent. All the experiment was conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations as approved by Institutional Ethical Committee (IEC), Cochin University of Science and Technology, Kerala, India. To isolate RBCs, whole blood was centrifuged at 400 g for 15 min. The plasma and buffy coat were carefully removed, and then the RBC pellet was washed 3–4 times with PBS buffer by centrifugation at 500 g for 10 min. Hemolysis assays using erythrocytes (RBCs) were achieved to test the hemolytic ability of NiO-NPs. Four milliliters of fresh human blood from healthy volunteers and used with formal bioethics approval were obtained in an ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid anticoagulant tube.

After that, the RBCs were incubated for 20 min at 37 ℃ with the K-Carrageenan at 50 and 100 µg/ml at the different time interval (3 h and 6 h). Following incubation, the RBCs were fixed in 100% methanol for 6 min to analyse the morphology of the blood cells. They were then stained with Giemsa (0.4% Giemsa in 100% methanol that has been diluted 20 times in PBS pH 7.4) and viewed under a 40 X fluorescence microscope69.

Statistical analysis

Three duplicates were used for each assay, and the final results were mean ± standard deviation (SD). Significant differences between treatments and tested bacterial species were evaluated using Tukey’s HSD test and two-way ANOVA. Statistical significance was defined as a P-value of less than 0.05.

Conclusion and future prospects

Chronic wounds and MDR infections provide significant challenges in contemporary healthcare, necessitating creative treatment approaches. This study develops a novel approach to fighting wound infections by employing K-Carrageenan. In addition to having strong antibacterial and antibiofilm properties, these K-Carrageenan also showed exceptional biofilm disruption and bacterial growth inhibition capabilities against pathogens that are capable of generating biofilms. To guarantee their safe clinical use, future research should concentrate on comprehensive toxicological evaluations, such as long-term exposure studies and in vivo assessments. Furthermore, examining how these K-Carrageenan work in concert with current antibacterial and wound-healing treatments may result in the creation of all-encompassing therapy plans. A vital need in contemporary healthcare could eventually be met by incorporating these K-Carrageenan into clinical practice, which has the potential to transform the treatment of MDR infections and enhance wound care results.
