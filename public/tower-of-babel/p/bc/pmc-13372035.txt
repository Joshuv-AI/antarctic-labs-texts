An evaluation of the impact of a national facilitated antimicrobial stewardship e-learning course on learners’ practice in Egypt
Authors: Jacqueline Sneddon, Ryan A Hamilton, Yara Khalaf, Hannah Stewart, Tracey Guise, Elizabeth Tayler, Dilip Nathwani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn Egypt, the political commitment to addressing antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is increasing and there is an ambition to rapidly scale up antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) in hospitals. The British Society for Antimicrobial Chemotherapy (BSAC) partnered with World Health Organization (WHO) Egypt to support this goal through structured education and training. A country-specific, accredited, facilitated online AMS training course with an interactive live session was developed.ObjectivesTo evaluate participants’ feedback regarding this course and identify translation of knowledge into AMS interventions and programmes.MethodsFormal evaluation of learning (all 15 cohorts) and translation into action (cohorts 1–3) was undertaken via a post-course survey using a head, heart and hands approach, then follow-up surveys at 3 and 6 months were used to identify application of knowledge in practice.ResultsTo date, 1776 learners from across Egypt have undertaken the training over 15 cohorts, of which 74.7% passed the post-course test. Overall, feedback was positive, and participants highlighted several immediate actions they had planned in their hospital. At the 3-month follow-up the most frequently reported AMS interventions were new guidelines and education, and changes to microbiology testing. At 6 months, guidelines remained the most frequent AMS intervention. Staff collaboration was a reported facilitator to establishing AMS, while resistance from physicians was a perceived barrier.ConclusionsThis course presents an efficient way to roll out AMS education on a national scale with support from government authorities and healthcare organizations. This learning model could be replicated in other countries and contexts to support high-level commitment to AMS.

Introduction

In Egypt, growing high-level political commitment to addressing antimicrobial resistance (AMR), is reflected in a hospital action plan, that builds on the established Infection Prevention and Control strategy.1,2 This resulted in the need and ambition to rapidly develop and implement antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) at scale, and the education of government agency and hospital staff was identified as a priority for enabling this.

BSAC partnered with the WHO AMR team in Egypt to deliver an education initiative to support the development of sustainable antimicrobial stewardship programmes (ASP).

Given the need to rapidly implement and embed AMS at scale across Egypt, a range of healthcare professionals from relevant bodies in the Egyptian health system (Ministry of Health, Egyptian Health Authority and Supreme Council of University Hospitals) were required to undertake the course. These bodies also supported the enrolment of health professionals working in hospitals including hospital managers, microbiologists, and IPC specialists. Within Egyptian healthcare there are many well-trained clinical pharmacists but a relative shortage of infectious diseases physicians. Clinical pharmacists have therefore rapidly become leaders in AMR, AMS and IPC across Egyptian hospitals and were also identified as a key target audience.

The initiative consists of a bespoke, country-specific education programme via a closed cohort-based, facilitated online course accredited by the UK-based Royal College of Pathologists, with a certificate for 6 hours of Continuing Professional Development provided to participants passing the course test (see Figure 1 for a diagrammatic overview).

Overview of the education initiative.

The course, entitled ‘How to establish an antimicrobial stewardship program—a course for health care professionals in Egypt’, is available on the Easy Generator© web platform and managed by BSAC (https://egypt-ams.com/).3 Learners access the course via a weblink and log on using their email and a password. Learners can post questions via an online Padlet© forum that is monitored by the BSAC facilitator, and answers are provided. Other learners can also contribute answers or discuss the topic.

Multi-faceted AMS interventions underpin success as education alone does not translate into changes to clinical practice.4 Therefore, the aim of this report is to evaluate this e-learning course to determine whether the knowledge gained by learners had translated into action within their clinical settings.

Methods

Course development and delivery

The course was designed by BSAC to develop the knowledge of health professionals in relation to establishing an ASP in hospitals in Egypt. The course was an adaptation of a previous education package developed collaboratively by BSAC and ICARS, which was freely open access for LMIC hospitals. As the course was aimed at newly establishing a hospital ASP, the course focuses on the key principles of AMS and ASPs; how to establish an AMS committee and key activities, including surveillance and QI. The course incorporated the core requirements outlined in WHO toolkit for ASPs in LMICs5 and was supported by content from readily available BSAC education resources including the BSAC handbook for AMS.6 J.S. developed the programme, which was then reviewed by H.S. for optimal design and delivery; then reviewed for suitability for Egypt by a subgroup of Egypt’s National Antibiotic Policy, WHO staff (including Y.K.), and other AMR and AMS leaders in Egypt.

Learners joined the facilitated closed course in multi-disciplinary cohorts of ∼100 learners to facilitate active engagement and provision of support from the expert team. Cohorts 1 and 3 were used by the following organizations: Egyptian Drug Authority; EHA (Egyptian Health Authorities); CAPA (Central Administration of Pharmaceutical Affairs), and cohort 2 were coordinated through the Supreme Council of Universities. Cohorts 4–15 were recruited through the EHA, initially targeting the pilot governorates for the AMR programme; the Supreme Council of Universities; and the Ministry of Health and Populations (MoHP), through each phase of the AMR model hospital programme roll out. Learners were actively recruited by their host organizations with the aim to enrol a range of professionals working in government agencies and hospitals across Egypt. A weekly report of participation was shared with the cohort lead on a weekly basis for follow-up and encouragement of individuals who were not participating but there was no penalty for non-participation.

Each cohort has access to the course for 4 weeks course to complete six 1-hour sections followed by an opportunity to join a live session. The course was designed to provide basic knowledge and skills for participants to establish and manage an AMS programme, with additional resources for those seeking further knowledge. It was delivered in English (by J.S. and R.H.), with some facilitation in Arabic when required to enhance comprehension (by Y.K.). Ongoing access to the programme allows participants to refresh their knowledge and undertake further learning.

Course evaluation

Evaluation surveys were created using Survey Monkey© with the post-course survey using a model for evaluation of sustainable learning called ‘head, heart, hands’ that asks learners to reflect on (i) what they learned, (ii) their feelings about the learning and (iii) their intentions to apply learning to practice.7 Those who completed the course and achieved an end of course test score of at least 80% were sent a survey link via email. The survey also captured information about professionals’ discipline. Feedback on course content and delivery was encouraged. Participants not achieving the required pass mark were allowed to retake the assessment as many times as required to meet the pass threshold. The survey was in British English as this is the language used in medical and pharmacy undergraduate education in Egypt, therefore it was not deemed necessary to translate into Arabic.

The responses to each question were analysed through content analysis into categories to provide an overview for each question for cohorts 1, 2 and 3. Content analysis of responses from cohorts 4–15 was undertaken using the same coding framework and categories developed through the analysis of cohorts 1–3, and the results combined. J.S. and H.S. undertook the content analysis, with validation of coding and category generation performed by R.H.

Follow-up surveys were sent to learners from cohorts 1, 2 and 3 who passed the course, 3 and 6 months after course completion. These asked specific questions about team working, impact achieved, what helped and hindered them and any lessons to share with others. Learners could also share AMS interventions they had initiated including audit results or reports. Cohorts 1, 2 and 3 took part in the 3-month follow-up but only cohorts 1 and 3 in the 6-month follow-up.

Results

Across the 15 cohorts, 1776 learners from across all 27 Egyptian Governorates (Supplementary Figure S1, available as Supplementary data at JAC-AMR Online) undertook the training, of which 74.7% completed the course and passed the end of course test (mean 90.1% across cohorts 1–3) (see Supplementary Table S1). It took a mean time of 9 h and 24 min (SD ± 5 h 9 min) hours for learners to complete the training during the available cohort periods (see Supplementary Table S1). Most learners were pharmacists (59.4%), followed by IPC specialists (16.1%), physicians and surgeons (12.5%), then microbiologists (4.8%). Most learners were based in hospitals (92.4%) with 7.6% working in central government bodies such as the MoHP and the CAPA.

Initial feedback survey

Across the 15 cohorts 270 participants completed the initial survey (20.5% response rate). For cohorts 1–3 the response rate was slightly higher (40%).

Across all the cohorts, pharmacists made up nearly half (47%) of the respondents, followed by IPC staff (21.5%), medical microbiologists (14.1%) and non-infection specialist physicians (8.5%). The remainder of respondents were laboratory staff, quality team members and nurses. The profession or role was not clear for 6.7% of respondents.

Three-month follow-up survey

Ninety individuals completed the 3-month follow-up survey representing a response rate of 28.7% of those eligible from cohorts 1, 2 and 3 (cohort 1, 32/104, 30.8%; cohort 2, 33/94, 35.1%; cohort 3, 25/116, 21.6%). The most common actions reported (Table 2) were implementation of guidelines, including for prophylaxis and restriction of antimicrobials (38.9%); education of staff (21.1%) and microbiology testing and antibiograms (14.4%). Twenty-four respondents (26.7%) shared documents including hospital guidelines, restriction forms, reports on antibiotic consumption data, local antibiograms, audit forms and education resources to engage staff.

Six-month follow-up survey

Sixteen individuals completed the 6-month follow-up survey representing a response rate of 7.3% of those eligible from cohorts 1 and 3 (cohort 1, 11/104, 10.6%; cohort 3, 5/116, 4.3%). The most common actions reported at this stage (Table 2) were similar to those reported at 3 months, with guideline implementation being the most commonly reported activity (68.8%). Five respondents shared documents demonstrating ASP implementation and AMS committee actions including various data reports, hospital antibiotic protocols, an antibiotic review form and photographs of staff AMS education and awareness sessions.

Discussion

This evaluation demonstrates that the ‘How to establish an antimicrobial stewardship program—a course for health care professionals in Egypt’ provides healthcare professionals in Egypt with the knowledge to implement AMS programmes and interventions within their own hospitals. Initial feedback showed that the course was well received, with many participants reflecting how they were proud to have undertaken the course to improve AMS in their hospitals. Participants reported the course prepared them to implement AMS at their hospital and this was borne out in the 3- and 6-month follow-up surveys, which showed, with objective evidence, that participants were implementing core AMS interventions such as guidelines, education, antibiograms, and patient and prescription reviews. These interventions are all core components and activities in the checklist for healthcare facilities in the WHO practical toolkit for ASPs in LMICs,5 which informed the design of the course.

Education interventions can improve antimicrobial prescribing, but traditional teaching methods may not meet the complex needs of healthcare professionals working across a diverse range of healthcare settings, and can be expensive and difficult to deliver at scale, particularly across large geographic areas.8,9 Online education has an increasingly important place in raising awareness of AMR and AMS to support improvements in antimicrobial prescribing in a cost-efficient and consistent manner.10,11

Our course involves a closed, facilitated e-learning course with benefits of interaction with AMS experts and other learners to optimize learning and discuss issues identified. Attendance at the live facilitated sessions was high, which may have enhanced the engagement with, and utility of, the course.12 Egyptian medical and pharmacy professionals are trained in English, so there was no demand for course translation into Arabic. However, facilitation in a mixture of English and Arabic did enhance comprehension for some participants. Feedback identified improvements for future online AMS courses, which included videos, animations and practical examples, which have been shown to improve comprehension of complex topics and support learners to apply new knowledge in the workplace.13,14

Even at this early stage of follow-up, some participants described positive changes in antibiotic use with reductions in broad spectrum antibiotic use or increased awareness among clinicians about good prescribing practices. Several noted reduced antimicrobial costs and improved use of microbiology services. These were not quantified, however, and the study was not designed to undertake a formal impact or cost analysis of the educational programme.

Feedback was received on enablers and barriers to learners implementing AMS activities within clinical practice in their workplace. Enablers were collaboration with colleagues, a step-wise small change incremental approach, ongoing education, use of guidelines for all specialties and using data to demonstrate changes in practice. Barriers included physician resistance to change behaviour, issues with laboratory supplies or costs for tests, availability of antibiotics or approvals required for antibiotic restriction forms. These are well known phenomena within the AMS literature and suggest that these barriers exist in Egypt, as they do elsewhere, and that the course prepared learners to overcome some of these barriers.15,16

This training was one component of a wider programme led by the government to improve the use of antibiotics. The high-level institutional support for the course, the recruitment of participants in teams through their institutions and other improvements in the functioning and oversight of stewardship systems are likely to have enhanced its impact and translation of knowledge into action. This may also explain the high completion rate of 74.7%, which is far above the completion rates reported in the limited literature available that evaluates completion rates for online courses.17

Education programmes provided by AMS experts can increase the AMS capabilities of the healthcare workforce at scale, which is important to support government approaches to tackling AMR. Moreover, the recruitment of professionals working in the MoHP will further support the delivery of AMS within Egypt; by ensuring their teams are equipped with the knowledge required to lead, support and education hospital AMS teams. This is already being implemented, whereby the MoHP and CAPA are embedding accountability, support and education for hospital AMS teams and managers.

The first limitation to highlight is that the course and evaluation is for a single country, which has high political commitment to tackle AMR and embed AMS in hospitals, which may not be generalizable to other countries. Second, while the response rate for the initial feedback survey was acceptable, there was significant loss to follow-up, which limits the generalizability of the translation of knowledge into practice. Loss to follow-up in surveys is multifactorial and may have been influenced by the time intervals between surveys and conflicting priorities at the time of surveying. It is possible that our follow-up results are biased towards those who have implemented change following the course, with those who have not implemented interventions or change choosing not to respond. Finally, most respondents were pharmacists, which may also skew the results. However, it should be noted that clinical pharmacists make up a significant proportion of the clinical workforce in Egyptian hospitals, have higher awareness of AMR and AMS compared with other health professionals and are responsible for delivering many AMS and IPC activities and programmes at hospital and government level.18 Thus, they were a professional group that was targeted for recruitment onto the course and therefore made up a significant proportion of participants.

In conclusion, the course has increased participants’ knowledge of AMR and AMS and prepared them to implement AMS programmes and activities in their hospitals, with some early signs of improved antimicrobial and resource use. This AMS course could be replicated in other countries across the world to increase the AMS capabilities of the global healthcare workforce, while supporting national action plans for AMR. The course would require adaptation and examples from local and relevant contexts and will benefit from political support as demonstrated in Egypt.
