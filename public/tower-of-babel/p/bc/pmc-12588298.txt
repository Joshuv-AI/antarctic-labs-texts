Dopamine and serotonin cotransmission filters striatonigral synaptic activity via 5-HT1B receptor activation
Authors: Maya Molinari, Alina Aaltonen, Ori J. Lieberman, David Sulzer, Emanuela Santini, Anders Borgkvist
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The substantia nigra pars reticulata (SNr), a key basal ganglia output nucleus, is modulated by dopamine (DA) believed to be released locally from midbrain DA neurons. Although DA has been proposed to regulate γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) release from medium spiny neuron (MSN) terminals via presynaptic D1 receptors, the precise mechanisms remain unclear. Using presynaptic optical recordings of synaptic vesicle fusion, calcium influx in D1-MSN synapses together with postsynaptic patch-clamp recordings from SNr neurons, we found that DA inhibits D1-MSN GABA release in a frequency-dependent manner. Unexpectedly, this effect was independent of DA receptors and instead required 5-HT1B receptor activation. Using two-photon serotonin biosensor imaging in slices and fiber photometry in vivo, we demonstrate that DA enhances extracellular serotonin in the SNr via inhibition of serotonin reuptake. Our results suggest that serotonin mediates DAergic control of basal ganglia output and contributes to the therapeutic actions of dopaminergic medications for Parkinson’s disease and psychostimulant-related disorders.

INTRODUCTION

Dopamine (DA) exerts a powerful influence on basal ganglia circuit activity, enabling motor-related functions including action selection, learning, and habit formation. It is widely thought that the effects of endogenous DA release on motor behavior, as well as the therapeutic efficacy of medications that enhance DAergic signaling, such as levodopa and psychostimulants, originate from the activation of DA receptors within the striatum (1), the primary input nucleus of the basal ganglia. DA receptors are present in additional regions of the basal ganglia (2), although the understanding of their role in modulating local circuitry and affecting behavior is limited (3, 4).

Particularly high levels of DA D1 receptors (D1R), the most abundant DA receptor type in the mammalian brain, are found within the basal ganglia output nucleus substantia nigra pars reticulata (SNr) (5, 6). Ultrastructural studies indicate that these D1Rs are located presynaptically on the axons of the GABAergic striatal medium spiny neurons (MSNs) enriched in D1R expression (7–9). While the actions of DA on D1-MSN cell bodies and dendrites have been extensively studied, the role of DA in the regulation of neurotransmission by D1-MSN axons remains less clear. In the MSN cell body, stimulation of striatal D1Rs regulates gene transcription, neuronal excitability, and synaptic integration, which are processes critically involved in postsynaptic plasticity (10). In contrast, presynaptic plasticity relies on distinct mechanisms governing synaptic release probability and vesicular dynamics (11), but whether these processes are influenced by presynaptic D1Rs on D1-MSN axons is unknown.

DA is released in the SNr from the dendrites of substantia nigra pars compacta dopaminergic neurons (12). Thus, local DA could influence the inhibition of postsynaptic cells in the SNr by modulating D1-MSN γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) release. Most SNr cells are tonically active GABAergic projection neurons (13) and constitute the fundamental inhibitory unit in the disinhibition model of motor control in the basal ganglia (14). Consequently, presynaptic D1Rs are in a strategic position to control motor activity by regulating the strength and timing of inhibition in the SNr. Local stimulation of D1Rs in the SNr of rodents induces behaviors such as rotation and spontaneous locomotion (15, 16).

Despite research interest over many years (16–22), the function of D1Rs within the SNr remains unclear. This is, in part, due to technical challenges in isolating the effect of DA on the local synaptic circuitry in the SNr. Many studies use indirect methods, such as postsynaptic recordings of GABAergic SNr cells, which have shown that DA both enhances (18) and reduces (20) GABAergic synaptic transmission. However, in addition to D1-MSN inputs, SNr cells receive GABAergic input from the globus pallidus externa via pallidonigral projections (23, 24) and collateral inputs from GABAergic SNr neurons, which are tonically active even in brain slice preparations (25). Thus, DA could modulate GABA release from several different sources contacting SNr neurons.

To investigate how DA regulates GABA release in the SNr, we have imaged presynaptic Ca2+ influx and synaptic vesicle fusion at D1-MSN synapses in acutely prepared SNr slices and recorded D1-MSN GABA type A (GABAA) currents in SNr neurons using optogenetics. Unexpectedly, stimulation of D1Rs had no direct effect on GABA release. In contrast, both optogenetically induced and exogenously applied DA inhibited GABA release during low synaptic activity, indicating high-pass filtering of D1-MSN synapses. DA’s effect was dependent on 5-HT1B receptors, indicating the involvement of serotonin. A role for serotonin was further demonstrated by serotonin-selective biosensor imaging in slices and fiber photometry recordings in vivo, which showed that DA enhances serotonin efflux in the SNr. Our results show that DA and serotonin cooperatively fine-tune basal ganglia output, suggesting roles in movement control, antidepressant and psychostimulant actions, and disorders such as Parkinson’s disease.

RESULTS

To examine the presynaptic effects of DA and D1Rs, we used presynaptic optical recordings of synaptic vesicle fusion at D1-MSN synapses in the SNr using the styryl dye FM1-43 (26). This technique offers a critical advantage over postsynaptic recordings for evaluating dynamic presynaptic modulation, as it reports the presynaptic vesicle fusion rate, which is unaffected by changes in postsynaptic GABA receptor sensitivity.

FM1-43 was loaded into the lumen of recycling vesicles of D1-MSN synapses by applying a 10-Hz stimulus train to the D1-MSN fibers in a mouse oblique, acute brain-slice preparation containing intact synaptic connections between the striatum and SNr (Fig. 1A) (27). After >20-min perfusion of the slice with artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) containing ADVASEP-7 to remove extrasynaptic FM1-43 label (28), real-time fluorescence changes from FM1-43–labeled synaptic boutons were captured using two-photon microscopy.

The decay in FM1-43 fluorescence during subsequent stimulation reflects FM1-43 unloading as synaptic vesicles fuse with the presynaptic membrane (29). Active boutons were defined as puncta meeting shape and size criteria consistent with synaptic boutons and exhibiting over 10% fluorescence loss following stimulation of the D1-MSN axons (see Materials and Methods). Above this threshold, FM1-43 fluorescence loss reflects exocytosis sensitive to Cd2+, which blocks voltage-gated Ca2+ channels critical for neurotransmitter release (26). Using slices from D1Cre::Ai9 (expressing Cre-dependent tdTomato), we previously showed that 98% of the FM1-43 destaining puncta colocalize with D1-MSN axons (26). In aCSF, 2-Hz electrical stimulation of the D1-MSN fibers reduced FM1-43 fluorescence in 89 ± 1% (mean ± SEM; n = 45 slices) of the boutons, consistent with our previous report (26).

DA inhibits D1-MSN synapses independently of dopaminergic receptors

To quantify the rate of synaptic vesicle fusion across treatment conditions, we computed the fraction of the FM1-43–loaded vesicles that undergo fusion per action potential during stimulation (30). Like D1-MSN synaptic transmission (24, 31), FM1-43 destaining correlates with extracellular Ca2+ levels, displays short-term facilitation and is modulated by cannabinoid receptors selectively expressed on D1-MSN axons in SNr (26).

We first investigated the impact of presynaptic D1Rs on D1-MSN release by superfusing sagittal brain slices with DA (10 and 50 μM) during stimulus-evoked synaptic activity. DA dose-dependently slowed fluorescence decay during FM1-43 unloading (Fig. 1, B and C) and decreased the fractional release (Fig. 1D). With 50 μM DA, fractional release was significantly decreased by ~75% compared to controls (Fig. 1D).

We observed that the inhibition of synaptic vesicle fusion by DA was markedly frequency dependent. In slices treated with DA (50 μM), multiple FM1-43 fluorescent puncta showed no detectable fluorescence loss following 2-Hz stimulus (cf. Fig. 1C). To reveal active boutons in these DA-treated slices, we applied a subsequent 10-Hz stimulus train to the D1-MSN axons, which rapidly depleted FM1-43 fluorescence from boutons that were unresponsive to 2-Hz stimuli. DA significantly reduced the proportion of boutons exhibiting FM1-43 fluorescence decay to 2-Hz stimulation (DA, 28.2 ± 9% versus aCSF, 81.3% ± 4%, mean ± SEM, t(11.8) = 5.53, P < 0.001, Welch’s two sample t test, n = 10 to 11 slices). After 10-Hz stimulation, the number of FM1-43 unloading boutons was similar between aCSF and DA-treated slices (Fig. 1E). These findings show that DA inhibits synaptic vesicle fusion and that this effect is dependent on the synaptic activity rate, with an inhibition of exocytosis at low frequencies that is overcome at higher frequencies.

To corroborate these findings using pharmacologically relevant DA responses, we included amphetamine (10 μM) in the aCSF during imaging stimulus-evoked FM1-43 unloading (fig. S1). Amphetamine, which promotes DA release via reverse transport through the DA reuptake transporter, reduced the proportion of 2-Hz sensitive boutons (amphetamine, 66.8 ± 4% versus aCSF, 92.9 ± 2%, mean ± SEM, t(17) = 6.52, P < 0.001, t test, n = 9 to 10 slices), slowed FM1-43 fluorescence decay, and decreased fractional release (fig. S1).

Unexpectedly, the reduction in 2-Hz–sensitive boutons and the decreased fractional release produced by DA were not prevented by adding D1R (SCH39166, 1 μM) or D2R (sulpiride, 1 μM) antagonists before DA in the aCSF (Fig. 1, D and E). Moreover, the inhibition of synaptic vesicle fusion exerted by DA also occurred in brain slices from mice with genetic deletion of Drd1a (Drd1a−/−), which lack D1Rs (Fig. 1, F and G). While DAergic inhibition of synaptic vesicle fusion was preserved in Drd1a−/− mice, analysis of release across treatment (aCSF versus DA) and genotype conditions (WT versus Drd1a−/−) revealed a significant effect of treatment [F(2, 27) = 47.2, P < 0.001], a significant effect of genotype [F(1, 27) = 5.61, P < 0.05] and a significant treatment x genotype interaction [F(2, 27) = 3.75, P < 0.05]. Post hoc analysis (Tukey post hoc) showed that Drd1a−/− tended to have a higher fractional release in control conditions (P = 0.0759), suggesting that long-term loss of D1R signaling increases the intrinsic synaptic vesicle fusion rate in D1-MSN axons. Together, our pharmacological and genetic studies demonstrate that DA acutely inhibits exocytosis at D1-MSNs in the SNr, but this inhibition is frequency dependent and does not require the presence of D1Rs.

Previous postsynaptic patch-clamp studies in brain slices showed that D1R activation increases GABAA receptor–mediated synaptic currents in SNr cells (18, 21, 22), which were interpreted to indicate that D1R agonists increase GABA release in the SNr. We therefore examined whether selective D1R activation modulates the rate of synaptic vesicle fusion using the FM1-43–based assay. Two D1R agonists were tested at a range of concentrations (Fig. 2, A to D). Unexpectedly, neither the partial agonist (SKF38393, 1 to 10 μM; Fig. 2, A and B) nor the full agonist (SKF81297, 1 to 10 μM, Fig. 2, C and D) altered FM1-43 fluorescence decay or fractional release compared to drug-free aCSF. Moreover, the fractional release from D1-MSNs was unaffected by D1R blockade (SKF83566, 1 μM), adenylyl cyclase activation (NKH477, 10 μM), or by DA D2R stimulation (quinpirole, 1 μM) (Fig. 2, E and F). In summary, our results do not support a direct effect of D1Rs, D2Rs, or adenylyl cyclase–linked signaling on synaptic vesicle fusion rate in D1-MSN axons in the SNr (Fig. 2E).

Dopaminergic inhibition of D1-MSN synapses is GABA-receptor independent

Given that DA increases the spontaneous spike discharge of SNr neurons by activating postsynaptic D1Rs (32) and that we previously observed potent GABA-mediated presynaptic inhibition of synaptic vesicle fusion (26), we considered the possibility that DA-mediated presynaptic inhibition reflects D1R-stimulated extracellular GABA accumulation (33). To assess this, we examined whether GABA receptors are involved in DA-mediated suppression of synaptic vesicle fusion using two-photon optical recordings of FM1-43 destaining.

Consistent with previous studies (32), we found that DA application significantly increased the firing rate of SNr neurons [t(6) = 4.19, P < 0.01, paired t test, n = 7 neurons], as determined by extracellular recordings (fig. S2). The SNr firing rate increased from 24.8 ± 2.3 Hz in aCSF to 34.6 ± 2.9 Hz following DA exposure (10 μM; fig. S2, A to C), indicating a robust postsynaptic excitatory effect of DA on SNr neurons.

While application of muscimol (1 μM), a GABAA receptor agonist, had no effect on stimulus-evoked FM1-43 destaining (fig. S2, D and H), baclofen (1 μM), a GABAB agonist, inhibited FM1-43 destaining and fractional release at 2-Hz stimulation of D1-MSN axons similarly to DA (fig. S2, E and I), suggesting a potential involvement of GABAB receptors in DA-mediated suppression of D1-MSN synaptic vesicle fusion. However, prior application of the GABAB receptor antagonist CGP52432 (1 μM) did not prevent DA-mediated (50 μM) suppression of FM1-43 destaining (fig. S2, F and J), indicating that DA does not influence D1-MSN synaptic vesicle fusion by potentiating ambient GABA. Supporting this, we observed no suppression of FM1-43 destaining following blockade of GABA transporters with guvacine (100 μM; fig. S2, G and K). Together, these findings indicate that DA-mediated presynaptic inhibition of D1-MSNs is mechanistically distinct from its postsynaptic excitation of SNr neurons.

DA modulates Ca2+ influx during low-frequency D1-MSN synaptic activity

Our previous work showed that FM1-43 unloading at D1-MSN axons depend on Ca2+ influx and is blocked by Cd2+, while extracellular Ca2+ levels influence unloading kinetics (26). The absence of D1R effects on FM1-43 unloading suggests that DA regulates presynaptic Ca2+ influx in D1-MSN axons through a distinct mechanism. To investigate this, we conditionally targeted the genetically encoded Ca2+ indicator, GCaMP7s, to D1-MSNs by infusing adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) carrying floxed-GCaMP7s into the striatum of D1Cre transgenic mice. Histological examination conducted 3 weeks after surgery showed GCaMP7s expression in DARPP-32 (a selective MSN marker)–positive D1-MSN and their axons in the SNr (Fig. 3A).

As fluorescence in individual presynaptic puncta may be influenced by the GCaMP7s transduction efficiency, we quantified DAergic modulation of Ca2+ influx by measuring the relative fluorescence change in response to graded stimulation frequencies during control and DA exposure. Two-photon imaging of presynaptic Ca2+ transients was performed in the SNr of the same sagittal brain slice preparation used in the FM1-43 assay, while applying electrical stimulation to the D1-MSN axons at varying frequencies.

As shown in Fig. 3B, GCaMP7s fluorescence intensity in presynaptic Ca2+ hotspots increased with higher stimulus frequencies. DA (50 μM) blunted the stimulus-related GCaMP7s fluorescence increase, indicating a potent inhibition of presynaptic Ca2+ influx (Fig. 3C). Both control and DA-treated slices showed frequency-dependent increases in relative GCaMP7s fluorescence (∆F/F). However, DA significantly reduced GCaMP7s fluorescence by ~60% at 2-Hz stimulation but had no effect at 10-Hz stimulation (Fig. 3D). In contrast, application of a D1R agonist (SKF81297, 1 μM) did not affect Ca2+ influx across the range of stimulus frequencies tested (Fig. 3D).

The results from these presynaptic Ca2+ recordings are consistent with the FM1-43–based measures of synaptic vesicle fusion (Figs. 1 and 2). DA reduces stimulus-dependent D1-MSN presynaptic Ca2+ influx as well as synaptic vesicle exocytosis during 2-Hz stimulation but not 10-Hz stimulation in a D1R-independent manner.

DA inhibits D1-MSN GABA release independently of D1R activation

Because the evidence from our presynaptic optical recordings demonstrates that DA inhibits D1-MSN GABA release and stands in contrast with the D1R-mediated facilitation of GABAA currents previously reported in studies using postsynaptic recordings (18, 21, 22), we investigated the effect of DA in the SNr by performing whole-cell patch-clamp recordings of SNr cells. To achieve selective activation of D1-MSN synapses, we transduced the light-activated cation channel channelrhodopsin (ChR2) in D1-MSNs by infusing AAV-DIO-ChR2-mCherry into the striatum of D1Cre transgenic mice (Fig. 4, A and B). Subsequently, we prepared sagittal brain slices and recorded SNr neurons in voltage-clamp configuration, sampling neurons from regions with prominent mCherry fluorescence indicating ChR2-expressing D1-MSN terminals (Fig. 4C). Before whole-cell recordings, we measured the tonic firing activity in cell-attached mode, including only neurons with the characteristic firing rate of SNr GABA neurons [>10 Hz, (13)] for further analysis (Fig. 4D).

Brief photostimulation (0.1 to 0.5 ms) of the D1-MSN axons elicited optical inhibitory postsynaptic currents (oIPSCs) in SNr neurons (Fig. 4E). These oIPSCs were completely blocked by tetrodotoxin (TTX) (1 μM; Fig. 4E) and picrotoxin (100 μM), confirming that they consisted of a GABAA current produced by depolarization-induced GABA release from D1-MSN synapses.

We subjected the slice to intermittent (0.05 Hz) photo stimuli. After establishing a stable current response in aCSF, we applied DA (10 μM). Within 10 min, DA decreased the oIPSC by ~50% compared to baseline (Fig. 4, F and G), with this response persisting throughout DA superfusion.

To confirm whether DA reduces the oIPSC by activating DA receptors, we applied SCH23390 (1 μM) and sulpiride (1 μM), antagonists of D1-like and D2-like receptors, respectively, before DA (10 μM). The antagonists alone did not alter the amplitude of the oIPSC (Fig. 4H), suggesting that DA receptors do not tonically modulate GABA release from D1-MSN axons. DA treatment inhibited the oIPSC even in slices pretreated with DA receptor antagonists (Fig. 4H). In the slices treated with DA (DA and DA + Sulp + SCH), we observed a significant increase in the paired-pulse ratio (PPR) of the oIPSC elicited by two photostimuli spaced 50 ms apart (Fig. 4I), which is classically interpreted to indicate presynaptic modulation (34). In contrast to DA, the D1R agonist SKF81297 (1 μM) did not alter the amplitude of PPR of the D1-MSN oIPSC in SNr cells (Fig. 4, J and K, and fig. S4). These results align with the findings from the synaptic vesicle fusion (Figs. 1 and 2) and presynaptic Ca2+ optical recordings (Fig. 3), providing independent confirmation that DA reduces D1-MSN GABA release independently of DA receptor activation.

DA inhibits D1-MSN GABA release via 5-HT1B receptors

We reasoned that the inhibitory action of DA may arise from its effects on additional neurotransmitters that suppress D1-MSN GABA release. Previous studies indicate that DA enhances serotonin neurotransmission in the SNr (35), which in turn reduces GABA release through the activation of 5-HT1B receptors (36, 37). Therefore, to investigate whether DA depresses D1-MSN GABA release through a mechanism involving 5-HT1B receptors, we recorded D1-MSN oIPSCs in the presence of the selective 5-HT1B antagonist NAS-181 (5 μM) applied before DA (10 μM; Fig. 5A). NAS-181 had no significant effect on D1-MSN oIPSCs compared to aCSF alone (Fig. 5, B to D). In the presence of NAS-181, subsequent addition of DA did not significantly decrease the D1-MSN oIPSC (Fig. 5, B to D), suggesting that the potent inhibitory effect of DA on the D1-MSN synapse required the presence of 5-HT1B receptors.

To deplete monoamine neurotransmitters including DA and serotonin, we treated mice with reserpine [5 mg/kg, subcutaneous (sc)], an irreversible blocker of vesicular monoamine transporters, which prevents monoamine packaging into synaptic vesicles. Brain slices were prepared the following day for patch clamp recordings of D1-MSN oIPSCs in the presence of DA (10 μM). In monoamine-depleted slices, DA treatment did not significantly alter the amplitude of the D1-MSN oIPSC (Fig. 5, E and F, and fig. S4), consistent with the hypothesis that release of serotonin is required for DA-induced suppression of GABA release.

To determine whether 5-HT1B receptors mediate DA-dependent presynaptic inhibition, we virally transduced GCaMP7s in D1-MSN axons via striatal AAV injections and monitored stimulus-dependent presynaptic Ca2+ transients in the SNr using two-photon microscopy. NAS-181 (5 μM, 10 min) blocked the DA-mediated inhibition of stimulation-dependent axonal Ca2+ signals (Fig. 5, G and H). We conclude that DA suppresses D1-MSN GABA release through serotonergic activation of presynaptic 5-HT1B receptors on D1-MSN axons.

DA enhances serotonin in SNr by actions at SERT

To determine how DA promotes serotonin release in the SNr, we transduced the genetically encoded serotonin sensor g5HT3.0 (38) in D1-MSN neurons by injecting AAV-hSyn-g5HT3.0 into the striatum of wild-type (WT) mice. After a 3-week recovery period, g5HT3.0 expression was detected in the D1-MSN axons of fixed slices using anti–green fluorescent protein (GFP) antibody amplification (Fig. 6A). Using two-photon microscopy, we monitored real-time g5HT3.0 fluorescence changes at D1-MSN axons in acute brain slices. To validate that g5HT3.0 reliably reports D1-MSN serotonin binding, we examined fluorescence signals following exogenous serotonin application. Serotonin (0.1 to 10 μM, 1 min) produced dose-dependent increases in g5HT3.0 fluorescence, which returned toward baseline levels upon wash-out with aCSF (Fig. 6, B and C). Significant fluorescence increases were observed at concentrations >0.1 μM serotonin (Fig. 6D), consistent with the reported half-maximum activation concentration of 0.15 μM (38).

DA application (10 to 50 μM) induced robust increases in g5HT3.0 fluorescence in the SNr (Figs. 6E and 7, A and B), consistent with DA-stimulated serotonin efflux. To rule out nonspecific DA binding to g5HT3.0, we injected AAV-hSyn-g5HT3.0 in the cerebellum where serotonin innervation is relatively sparse (39). In cerebellar slices from these mice, serotonin (10 μM) robustly increased g5HT3.0 fluorescence; however, DA (50 μM) application had no effect (Fig. 6F). These results demonstrate that the DA-mediated increase of g5HT3.0 fluorescence is attributed to increased extracellular serotonin levels and not DA binding to g5HT3.0.

Previous studies suggest that DA interferes with serotonin extracellular clearance as a competitive substrate for the serotonin reuptake transporter (SERT) (35). To examine whether SERT is involved in DA-mediated extracellular serotonin accumulation, we pretreated slices with citalopram (1 μM, 10 min), a selective SERT inhibitor, followed by DA (10 or 50 μM). Citalopram alone significantly increased g5HT3.0 fluorescence, consistent with SERT inhibition and extracellular serotonin accumulation (Fig. 7, A and B). The magnitude of this effect was similar to that observed with 10 μM DA (Fig. 7, A and B). Citalopram pretreatment reduced additional DA-mediated increases in g5HT3.0 fluorescence (Fig. 7, A and B), supporting the hypothesis that DA causes the accumulation of extracellular serotonin by an SERT-dependent effect. Notably, 50 μM DA elicited a brighter g5HT3.0 fluorescence signal than citalopram alone (cf. Fig. 6E), indicating that DA either promotes serotonin release in addition to inhibiting SERT or further interferes with serotonin reuptake.

The vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2) is responsible for sequestering both DA and serotonin into synaptic vesicles (40). Previous studies have shown that the DA precursor L-dopa reduces serotonin transmission in the dorsal raphe, potentially by disrupting vesicular serotonin accumulation (41). To assess whether DA interferes with VMAT2-mediated serotonin transport, we monitored evoked g5-HT3.0 transients during electrical stimulation of the SNr (Fig. 7, C and D) and compared the effect of DA on serotonin depletion from synaptic vesicles with that of tetrabenazine (TBZ), a VMAT2 blocker.

As shown in Fig. 7E, aCSF-control slices maintained stable g5HT3.0 fluorescence peaks during trains of stimuli (10 Hz, 10 pulses). In contrast, TBZ application (10 μM) caused a gradual reduction in g5HT3.0 peak amplitude (Fig. 7, D and E), consistent with progressive vesicular serotonin depletion due to VMAT2 blockade (42). DA exposure (10 and 50 μM) produced a similar dose-dependent reduction in evoked g5HT3.0 peaks over successive stimuli (Fig. 7E), suggesting that DA may also interfere with vesicular serotonin accumulation, or with synaptic vesicle recycling and fusion. However, while TBZ had no effect on the overall g5HT3.0 fluorescence reflecting changes in extracellular serotonin levels, DA (50 μM) significantly increased g5HT3.0 fluorescence even in TBZ-treated slices (Fig. 7F). Together, these findings suggest that DA enhances serotonin in the SNr primarily by a SERT-dependent mechanism, with possible additional effects on VMAT2-dependent serotonin vesicular accumulation.

Amphetamine can stimulate monoaminergic neurotransmission by reversal of membrane transport. We therefore examined whether amphetamine induces serotonin release in the SNr, which would indicate that amphetamine reduces D1-MSN synaptic vesicle fusion via serotonin rather than DA. Amphetamine application (10 μM, 10 min) significantly increased g5HT3.0 fluorescence in the SNr (fig. S1), indicating that the amphetamine-induced reduction in D1-MSN synaptic vesicle fusion is mediated through serotonergic signaling.

Phasic DA activity reduces D1-MSN GABA release via 5-HT1B receptors

In vivo, DA neurons alternate between tonic firing and phasic bursts of higher activity in response to salient environmental stimuli, producing striatal surges in DA that are essential for postsynaptic receptor activation during reward processing (43, 44). Given that DA neurons were present (Fig. 6A) and maintain spontaneous activity in acute brain slices, we investigated whether tonic DA firing releases DA that elevates extracellular serotonin via SERT inhibition. To elevate ambient DA, we treated slices with the selective DA transporter (DAT) inhibitor GBR12909 (1 μM), thereby blocking DA extracellular clearance. However, GBR12909 application did not increase g5HT3.0 fluorescence (Fig. 8A), indicating that in slices, DA dendrites in the SNr do not release enough DA to trigger serotonin efflux. Subsequent DA application significantly increased g5HT3.0 fluorescence, showing that DA promotes serotonin efflux independently of DAT (Fig. 8A).

To determine whether DA neuron burst activity modulates D1-MSN GABA release through serotonin, we mimicked in vivo burst firing patterns in brain slices using optogenetics (Fig. 8B). We photoactivated DA neurons in sagittal brain slices derived from the offspring of DIO-ChR2–enhanced yellow fluorescent protein (EYFP) (Ai32) mice crossed with DAT-Cre animals while electrically stimulating the D1-MSN axons using a bipolar electrode (Fig. 8B). Using aCSF containing kynurenic acid (1 mM) to block glutamate receptors, we analyzed electrically evoked IPSCs (eIPSCs) that exhibited paired-pulse facilitation (50-ms interstimulus interval) during baseline recordings (Fig. 8C), a distinguishing feature of D1-MSN synapses (24). We confirmed that electrical stimulation alone does not evoke DA release in the SNr by measuring DA levels with the genetically encoded fluorescent sensor GRAB-DA3h (45), expressed in D1-MSN axons. No stimulation-related changes in DA fluorescence were observed (fig. S3).

Once stable eIPSCs were established, a train of photostimuli (60 pulses, 1 ms, 20 Hz) was delivered to increase spiking in DA neurons 3 s before the electrical stimuli. Photoactivation of DA neurons significantly reduced the amplitude of the D1-MSN eIPSCs, consistent with inhibition of D1-MSN GABA release (Fig. 8, C and D). The PPR significantly increased in cells exhibiting a photostimulation-induced decrease in eIPSC (light, PPR = 2.2 ± 0.2 versus no-light, PPR = 1.8 ± 0.1, mean ± SEM, t(13) = 2.62, P < 0.05, paired t test, n = 14 of 20 cells), indicating a presynaptic mechanism. To investigate whether the inhibition of the eIPSCs during DA burst firing was serotonin mediated, we applied NAS-181 (5 μM), which prevented the photostimulation-induced decrease in eIPSC amplitude (Fig. 8D). Together, these results show that DA released by burst firing of DA neurons increases extracellular serotonin in the SNr that in turn inhibits D1-MSN GABA release by presynaptic 5-HT1B receptors activation.

5-HT1B receptors mediate serotonergic inhibition of D1-MSN synapses

Our g5HT3.0 experiments showed that DA induces serotonin release in the submicromolar range (c.f. Fig. 6, D and E). To examine whether serotonin reduces GABA release from D1-MSN axons via 5-HT1B receptor activation similarly to DA, we applied serotonin at 200 nM, corresponding to the concentration induced by 10 μM DA, and measured its modulation of GABAA currents in SNr neurons evoked by optogenetic stimulation of D1-MSN axons (Fig. 9A).

Serotonin significantly reduced oIPSCs recorded in SNr neurons compared to baseline (Fig. 9, B and C). Preapplication of NAS-181 (5 μM) prevented the serotonin-induced oIPSC reduction (Fig. 9D). The serotonin-induced reduction in oIPSC amplitude was accompanied by a significantly increased PPR (Fig. 9E). Together, these findings suggest that serotonin reduces GABA release from D1-MSN axons by activating presynaptic 5-HT1B receptors.

We next examined whether 5-HT1B receptors modulate frequency-dependent presynaptic Ca2+ influx in D1-MSN axons. Slices from D1-Cre mice expressing GCaMP7s in D1-MSN axons were exposed to CP94253 (1 μM), a selective 5-HT1B receptor agonist, during electrical stimulation of D1-MSN axons at various frequencies (2 to 10 Hz). Two-photon imaging of GCaMP7s fluorescence in D1-MSN boutons within the SNr revealed a marked reduction in stimulation-induced fluorescence following CP94253 treatment, which was clear even at the single-bouton level (Fig. 9F). Similar to the effect of DA, inhibition by CP94253 was frequency dependent (Fig. 9G). While CP94253 significantly reduced GCaMP7s fluorescence in response to 2- to 6-Hz stimulation compared to aCSF-treated slices (Fig. 9H), it had no effect at 10 Hz. These results suggest that the frequency-dependent filtering of D1-MSN synaptic transmission observed with DA is shared by serotonin and reflects the frequency-sensitivity of 5-HT1B receptor-mediated modulation of presynaptic Ca2+ influx in D1-MSN axons.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we examined the synaptic effects of DA in the SNr, a major basal ganglia output nucleus that regulates motor activity, goal-directed behaviors and brain states such as seizures and sleep. As the SNr consists of GABAergic neurons that tonically inhibit their target regions (46), these diverse functions are determined by how synaptic input from topographically organized projections of other basal ganglia nuclei modulates its firing activity (47, 48).

Our key finding is that DA decreases GABA release during low-frequency activity of D1-MSN axons. Unexpectedly, the activation of DA receptors with synthetic D1R and D2R agonists, as well as chemical activation of adenylyl cyclase, a crucial mediator of DA receptor signaling in D1-MSNs, had negligible effects on D1-MSN GABA release. The inhibitory effect of DA persisted even in the presence of DA receptor antagonists and in D1R-deficient mice, indicating a mechanism independent of DA receptor activation. Instead, our data suggest that DA interferes with SERT-mediated serotonin clearance, thereby increasing extracellular serotonin, which in turn inhibits GABA release via 5-HT1B receptors. This finding aligns with previous reports that DA decreases GABA release in the striatum by activating 5-HT1B receptors (49) and extends this observation to the SNr to provide an underlying mechanism for the modulation of SNr activity by DA. Together, our study indicates that DA-induced serotonin release in the SNr and subsequent 5-HT1B receptor activation mediates the inhibition by DA on GABA release from D1-MSN axons, with ramifications in both the striatum and the SNr, and provides a previously unidentified physiological role regulated by DA release from SNc dendrites.

DA indirectly inhibits D1-MSN synapses via serotonin

Previous studies report conflicting effects of DA on SNr GABAergic transmission. Some studies indicated that DA enhances GABAA synaptic currents in response to SNr stimulation (18) and increases depolarization-induced release of radiolabeled GABA from midbrain slices (50), effects that were attributed to D1R activation. In contrast, Miyazaki and Lacey (20) showed that DA reduces GABAA currents recorded in SNr GABA neurons upon stimulation of SNr afferents. Notably, that study did not determine whether DA receptor antagonists could block this inhibition, leaving the underlying receptor mechanisms unresolved.

We demonstrate, using three orthogonal approaches, that DA inhibits activity-dependent GABA release from D1-MSN axons by (i) decreasing Ca2+ influx into D1-MSN axons during synaptic activation, (ii) decreasing synaptic vesicle fusion from D1-MSN boutons, and (iii) reducing D1-MSN GABAA receptor currents in SNr. Collectively, these findings show an inhibitory action of DA on D1-MSN synapses.

To define the mechanism through which DA modulates D1-MSN GABA release, we pharmacologically targeted D1 and D2-like receptors, which did not prevent the presynaptic inhibitory action of DA. Instead, DA-dependent presynaptic inhibition required activation of 5-HT1B receptors, which are highly expressed on D1-MSN terminals (51). Serotonergic and DAergic stimulation of 5-HT1B receptors regulate a wide range of synapses within the basal ganglia, including serotonin-induced inhibition of D1-MSN GABA release (36, 37), DA-dependent modulation of GABAergic transmission between MSNs in the ventral striatum (49), and DAergic inhibition of GABA release from D2-MSN axons in the ventral pallidum in response to cocaine, a psychostimulant that increases extracellular DA (52).

Further supporting the involvement of 5-HT1B receptors in the action of DA on D1-MSN GABA release, we observed that DA-mediated inhibition of Ca2+ influx and synaptic vesicle fusion are frequency dependent. The effect of DA on Ca2+ influx decreased as stimulation increased from 2 to 10 Hz, and FM1-43–labeled vesicles that remained after 2 Hz disappeared rapidly at 10 Hz even in the presence of DA. These DA-mediated effects resemble those of baclofen, a GABAB receptor agonist that inhibits FM1-43 unloading at D1-MSN (26) and hippocampal synapses (30). Such frequency-dependent inhibition is characteristic of Gi protein–coupled receptors, like 5-HT1B and GABAB receptors, which reduce neurotransmitter release at low stimulation frequencies but allow transmission at higher frequencies due to the dissociation of the Gβγ subunits from voltage gated Ca2+ channels (30, 53). These results further support that DA modulates D1-MSN activity through presynaptic Gi protein–coupled 5-HT1B receptors in the SNr, rather than Gs/olf protein–coupled D1Rs.

Although prior studies suggested that DA directly binds to 5-HT1B receptors in the ventral striatum (49), our findings indicate that DA reduces D1-MSN GABA release in the SNr indirectly via serotonin. This is supported by imaging with the serotonin-selective fluorescent sensor g5HT3.0, which revealed that DA application enhances extracellular serotonin in SNr (Fig. 6). Moreover, when monoamines were depleted using reserpine, exogenously applied DA no longer affected D1-MSN GABA release. If DA directly activated 5-HT1B receptors, one would expect exogenous DA to reduce D1-MSN GABA release in reserpine-treated mice as in control animals. The absence of this effect in slices from reserpine-treated mice suggests that serotonin-mediated inhibition via 5-HT1B receptors is the primary mechanism by which DA regulates D1-MSN synaptic transmission. We note, however, that reserpine depletes all monoamines, including noradrenaline, and we cannot exclude the possibility that DA modulates D1-MSN GABA release via noradrenergic mechanisms in intact animals (54).

Our findings are consistent with prior studies showing that DA and its precursor, L-dopa, enhance serotonin efflux in the cortex, striatum, and substantia nigra (55, 56). Unlike those studies, which measured release of exogenously applied, radiolabeled serotonin, our results show that DA increases endogenous serotonin in the extracellular space. This distinction is critical, as externally applied serotonin may be taken up and released by nonserotonergic cells.

Our findings add to a growing body of evidence highlighting the interplay between DAergic and serotonergic neuromodulation in the central nervous system (57). This interaction appears to be particularly relevant in the SNr, which receives dense serotonergic input from the dorsal raphe nucleus (58, 59) and is enriched in 5-HT receptor expression (60, 61). Serotonin reduces D1-MSN GABA release via 5-HT1B receptors while simultaneously exciting SNr GABA cells through 5-HT2C receptors (37, 62). The balance of these opposing serotonergic influences may be crucial in shaping basal ganglia output and its regulation by DA.

DA increases extracellular serotonin through SERT-dependent mechanisms

Our g5HT3.0 measurements suggest that DA increases serotonin levels by modulating SERT activity. Consistently, the serotonin reuptake inhibitor citalopram unmasked spontaneous serotonin release and prevented DA-induced serotonin elevation in the SNr. In addition, DA failed to increase serotonin levels in cerebellar slices, which have sparse serotonergic innervation (39), likely due to the absence of SERT in this region (63). SERT is well established to transport DA, albeit with lower efficiency than serotonin, and DA inhibits serotonin reuptake (64).

We found that serotonin levels measured during DA application were higher than those with citalopram alone, indicating that SERT blockade cannot fully account for DA’s effects. Our data do not indicate that DA promotes serotonin release through D2R activation (65), as the D2R agonist quinpirole did not affect synaptic vesicle fusion, consistent with previous findings (66). It is possible that high levels of DA trigger serotonin release or that DA inhibits serotonin degradation via monoamine oxidase. Alternatively, as DA is taken up by serotonergic terminals via SERT and sequestered into synaptic vesicles by VMAT2 (67), serotonin may be liberated from vesicular stores (55, 56) and released via a nonvesicular mechanism, presumably through SERT (68).

Our findings provide support for this mechanism. Specifically, DA reduced serotonin release via electrically evoked synaptic vesicle fusion and enhanced nonstimulated extracellular serotonin levels, consistent with vesicular serotonin depletion and nonexocytic serotonin release, respectively. However, the precise mechanisms for these effects remain unclear. DA-mediated inhibition of SERT alone may reduce vesicular serotonin release by lowering vesicular serotonin content and suppressing vesicle fusion via autoreceptor activation. We observed that DA still enhanced extracellular serotonin levels even when VMAT2-mediated DA transport was blocked by TBZ pretreatment, supporting nonvesicular serotonin release. Yet, the DA-induced elevation of extracellular serotonin in TBZ-treated slices can reflect DAergic inhibition of SERT as well as reverse transport. Our data do not distinguish between these two mechanisms. Further experiments are required to determine how DA alters serotonergic axon function.

The activation of DA neurons reduced GABA release via 5-HT1B receptor activation, implying that physiological levels of DA in the SNr can produce serotonergic inhibition of D1-MSN GABA release, similar to the effects of exogenous DA application. Although we used electrical stimuli that does not exclusively stimulate D1-MSN axons, analysis of inhibitory synaptic currents with paired-pulse facilitation allowed us to distinguish D1-MSN from pallidonigral inputs (24), providing strong evidence that DA neuron activation inhibits GABA release from D1-MSN synapses. Further supporting the hypothesis that physiological DA levels can enhance extracellular serotonin in the SNr, our fiber photometry measurements showed that in vivo inhibition of DA reuptake, which increases extracellular DA in the SNr (69), enhanced serotonin binding to the D1-MSN axons. Since GBR12909 did not affect SNr serotonin levels in brain slices, these results suggest that GBR12909 does not bind to SERT but rather increases in vivo extracellular serotonin by accumulating DA.

While optogenetic stimulation of DA neurons revealed a temporally precise DA modulation of D1-MSN GABA release via serotonin signaling, our experimental strategy prevents identification of the source of this DA. It is generally thought that DA within the SNr originates from dendrites of SNc neurons. However, since our recordings were conducted in slices from transgenic mice expressing ChR2 in all DA neurons, optogenetic stimulation could have evoked DA release not only from SNc dendrites but also from axons of ventral tegmental area DA neurons projecting to the SNr (70). Their distinct serotonergic influence on D1-MSN axons may regulate behaviors related to somatosensory and reward-based processes, respectively, and remains an intriguing topic for future investigation.

DA D1Rs do not modulate D1-MSN presynaptic activity

Most D1Rs in the SNr originate from the striatum (71) and are positioned extrasynaptically on D1-MSN efferents (7, 9). Our presynaptic analyses revealed, however, that activating D1Rs with synthetic agonists did not alter the activity of D1-MSN synapses. Similarly, optogenetically evoked GABAA currents in SNr GABA neurons remained unaffected by D1R activation. These findings are unexpected considering previous studies suggesting that D1R agonists increase SNr GABA neurotransmission (16–18, 21, 22, 33, 50, 72).

One possible explanation for this discrepancy is that prior studies examined GABA release indirectly, potentially capturing GABAergic transmission from multiple sources that respond differentially to D1R stimulation. For example, SNr activation by depolarizing solution (50, 72) or electrical stimulation (18) may induce GABA release from SNr neurons, which express D1Rs (32), via axon collaterals (73, 74). The possibility that SNr GABA neurotransmission is enhanced by activation of postsynaptic D1Rs is supported by reports that D1R activation depolarizes SNr GABA neurons (19, 32). Other possible sources of GABA include dendritic GABA release, a well-studied feature of thalamic interneurons (75), and astrocytic GABA release in the SNr (33), as astrocytes also express D1Rs (76). SNr stimulation also activates GABAergic pallidonigral synapses (23, 77), which are inhibited by D2R activation (21), complicating the interpretation of DA’s effects on SNr GABA release. Pallidonigral synapses contributed minimally to our presynaptic measurements as, quinpirole, a D2R agonist, had no effect on FM1-43 unloading and we conditionally expressed GCaMP7s and ChR2 exclusively in D1-MSNs.

We note that the conclusion that D1Rs do not modulate GABA release from D1-MSN synapses applies specifically to synchronous neurotransmitter release— i.e., release driven by action potentials and the opening of voltage gated Ca2+ channels—rather than to long-lasting presynaptic effects. Under certain conditions, DA and D1R agonists have been shown to increase SNr GABA release in the presence of TTX and Ca2+ channel blockers (18), suggesting that D1R stimulation increases asynchronous GABA release. This effect could be attributed to D1Rs increasing cAMP, either through forskolin-activated adenylyl cyclase (18) or phosphodiesterase inhibition (78), thereby facilitating GABA neurotransmission via increased vesicular recycling (79), regulation of the size of the recycling pool of vesicles (80), or recruitment of silent synapses (81). These mechanisms of synaptic facilitation are independent of rapid presynaptic Ca2+ influx (82), occur slowly, and may not be detectable in the acute brain slice preparation.

Our findings also suggest that DA and D1R regulate D1-MSN presynaptic plasticity over extended timescales, as we observed increased D1-MSN release probability in Drd1a−/− mice. Moreover, we previously reported that loss of DA signaling following neurotoxin-induced lesions of DA neurons, but not pharmacological depletion of DA with reserpine, increased D1-MSN release probability (26). These findings suggest that DA signaling in the SNr and presynaptic D1R on D1-MSN axons play a role in setting the overall tone of normal D1-MSN GABA release.

In the striatum, DA is well known to regulate MSN intrinsic excitability via D1Rs including by modulating voltage-gated Ca2+ channels (83), K+ channels (84), and Na+ channels (85). Similarly, a prolonged reduction in D1R signaling may alter channel gene expression, transcript slicing, ion-channel properties, and subcellular localization, ultimately leading to enhanced D1-MSN synaptic strength (86).

DA may shape motor behavior via frequency-dependent serotonergic filtering

DA released from nigrostriatal synapses regulates basal ganglia output through dichotomous modulation of D1-MSNs and D2-MSNs (1). According to the rate model of the basal ganglia–mediated control of movement, this promotes motor activity upon inhibition of SNr neurons (87, 88). Here, our evidence suggests that DA influences behavior by regulating D1-MSN activity locally within the SNr (Fig. 10E). However, rather than controlling the rate of SNr firing, we propose that DA released from SNc dendrites into the SNr participates in activity-based selection of D1-MSN synapses, determining the pattern of SNr firing involved in action selection by the basal ganglia (89).

SNr GABA neurons inhibit SNc DA neurons via collaterals of axons projecting toward motor centers in the thalamus and brain stem (46). Disinhibition of SNc DA neurons due to D1-MSN–mediated decrease of SNr activity allows DA neurons to burst (90, 91), promoting DA release in the striatum (92), which facilitates firing of MSNs upon cortical activation in vivo (93). Our results suggest that DA in the SNr increases GABA release during MSN burst firing by selectively suppressing low-frequency transmission. This effect is mediated by concomitant serotonin release and activation of 5-HT1B receptors, leading to short-term facilitation of D1-MSN synapses (24). The frequency-dependent filtering of GABA release in the SNr by DA and serotonin cotransmission could modulate how pauses and patterns in presynaptic activity are translated into postsynaptic inhibition affecting the firing pattern of SNr neurons, ultimately facilitating salient motor behaviors within the basal ganglia functional loops (Fig. 10E) (94).

The midbrain circuit, comprising SNc DA neurons and SNr GABA neurons, alongside high levels of both DA and serotonin receptors, may play roles in the emergence of motor symptoms of Parkinson’s disease and their response to levodopa. For example, in a mouse model with progressive DA pathology, parkinsonian features such as akinesia and gait disturbances appear after decreased DA release in the SNr (95). Impaired regulation of GABA release from D1-MSN axons by 5-HT1B receptors may contribute to abnormal SNr firing patterns associated with parkinsonism in animal models (96). Loss of DA in the SNr increases the probability of D1-MSN GABA release, which drives sensitized motor responses in parkinsonian mice after optogenetic activation of D1-MSN terminals in the SNr (26). On the basis of our findings, this increased D1-MSN GABA release in DA-depleted animals can be explained as an abnormal DA-mediated facilitation of high D1-MSN activity and may explain how levodopa triggers sensitized motor responses in Parkinson’s disease, such as dyskinesia, DA dysregulation syndrome, or impulse disorders. Emerging evidence indicates that dyskinesia is caused by dysregulated DA release from serotonin axons (97) and is further substantiated by the antidyskinetic effects of 5-HT1 receptor agonists in Parkinson’s disease animal models and patients (98–100). Our findings show that such effects could be mediated through the indirect DA interactions with serotonin receptors in the SNr, suggesting a mechanism for further development of antidyskinetic therapeutic strategies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental subjects

Experiments were performed on mice of both sexes, aged 5 weeks and older. WT C57Bl/6J mice and Ai32(RCL-ChR2(H134R)/EYFP) breeders (strain 012569) were purchased from the Jackson Laboratories (Bar Harbor, ME, USA). Tg(Drd1a-cre)EY262Gsat/Mmcd were from the GENSAT project at Rockefeller University (101). Heterozygous Drd1a+/− breeders (102) were a gift from the late M. G. Caron (Duke University). DAT-Cre breeders (103) were donated by C. Broberger (Stockholm University). Transgenic mice were bred into the C57Bl/6J background for more than 10 generations and experimental subjects were the offspring heterozygous breeding pairs. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the Columbia University and the Swedish Board of Agriculture (license: 18194-2018 and 19345-23) and followed the guidelines of the Research Ethics Committee of Karolinska Institutet, Swedish Animal Welfare Agency, and the European Communities Council Directive 2010/63/EU.

Surgical procedures

Mice were anesthetized with 4% isoflurane and positioned in a stereotaxic frame (Stoelting) equipped with a thermal pad to maintain normothermia. Anesthesia was maintained with 1 to 2% isoflurane. All animals were injected subcutaneously with Temgesic (0.1 mg/kg) at the start of the surgery. During stereotaxic virus vector injections, 250 nl of AAV5-Ef1a-DIO-ChR2-mCherry, provided by K. Deisseroth (Addgene, 20297), AAV9-Syn-FLEX-jGCaMP7s, provided by D. Kim (Addgene, 104491), or AAV9-hSyn-5HT3.0 (38) and AAV9-hSyn-DA3h (45), provided by Y. Li (BrainVTA, PT-4724, PT-4721), were infused with a heat-pulled glass micropipette attached to an injection apparatus (Stoelting). The injection was targeted at the striatum using the following coordinates relative to bregma and the dural surface: anteroposterior +0.8 mm, mediolateral −1.7 mm, and dorsoventral −2.1 mm. For cerebellar injections, 400 nl of AAV9-hSyn-5HT3.0 were infused at the following coordinates: anteroposterior −6.72 mm, mediolateral 0 mm, and dorsoventral −1.5 mm. Following the AAV infusion, the micropipette was left in place at the injection site for at least 5 min to allow diffusion of the viral particles and prevent backflow along the micropipette tract. Last, the incision was closed with self-absorbed sutures (Vicryl Rapid, 5-0, FS-2) and the mice were allowed to recover from anesthesia on a thermal pad. The mice received postoperative pain relief with bidaily injections with Rimadyl (5 mg/kg, sc) for a period of 2 days following the procedure.

Brain slice preparation

Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and sagittal slices (250 to 400 μm) were prepared with an angle of 10° to 15° to maintain rostrocaudal D1-MSN projections (27) using a vibrating microtome (Leica, VT1200). Slice preparation was performed in ice-cold sucrose-based aSCF containing (in mM) 180 sucrose, 10 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 25 NaHCO3, 0.5 CaCl2, 7 MgCl2, 1.25 NaH2PO4, and 10 glucose (pH 7.3, 300 to 305 mosmol/liter). Following preparation, the slices were left to equilibrate for 30 min in 34°C aCSF containing (in mM) 125 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 25 NaHCO3, 2 CaCl2, 1 MgCl2, 1.25 NaH2PO4, and 10 glucose (pH 7.3, 300 to 305 mosmol/liter), and were then transferred to room temperature (21° to 23°C) where they were kept until experimentation. In the two-photon imaging experiments with GCaMP7s and patch-clamp recordings, we prepared slices according to the N-methyl-d-glucamine protective recovery method (104). The slices were submerged in a recording chamber and continuously perfused at rate of 1.5 to 2 ml/min in aCSF. A slice anchor was placed on top of the slice to prevent drift. All solutions were saturated with carbogen-gas (95% O2 and 5% CO2) during slice preparation and experiments.

Patch clamp electrophysiology

Recordings were performed on a Slicescope Pro 2000 electrophysiology system (Scientifica) equipped with a MultiClamp 700B amplifier and digidata 1550B analog-to-digital converter connected to a personal computer running pClamp 11.0.3 (Molecular Devices). Brain slices were illuminated with a 780-nm light-emitting diode (LED) module. Cells were visualized with a 40 × 0.8 numerical aperture (NA) water-immersion objective (LUMPlanFLN, Olympus) and Dodt gradient contrast tube optics, and projected to a personal computer with an infrared-sensitive CCD SciCam Pro camera. Recording pipettes were fabricated with a vertical puller (Narishige PC-100) and had a resistance of 3 to 5 megohm when filled with internal solution composed of (in mM): 140 CsCl, 2 MgCl2, 10 Hepes, 2 EGTA, 2 MgATP, and 3 QX-314. pH was adjusted to 7.2 with CsOH. To register recorded neurons in slices immersion-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7.4), Neurobiotin (SP-1120, Vector Laboratories) was added to the internal solution to a final concentration of 0.2% (w/v).

Recordings were performed at 32°C in aCSF. Neurons were clamped at −70 mV (liquid junction potential not corrected), and analysis was restricted to cells with stable (<10% change) access resistance below 20 megohm, as assessed by a hyperpolarizing (−10 mV) step command in each sweep. Photosensitive synaptic currents were evoked in brain slices by delivering flashes (0.1 to 0.5 ms) of blue light from a CoolLED PE-300 Ultra LED module through the 40× objective. eIPSCs were triggered by 100- to 200-μs stimulus pulses delivered to D1-MSN fibers in brain slices using a bipolar stimulation electrode (World Precision Instruments). The stimulation current was controlled by an ISO-Flex unit and adjusted to evoke eIPSCs at approximately 20% of the maximum amplitude.

Analysis of patch clamp recordings

Analysis was performed using Clampfit 10.6 (Molecular Devices). The amplitudes of oIPSCs and eIPSCs were analyzed using the “Analyze > Statistics” function, measuring the negative-going current peak relative to the baseline within 50 ms following the light and electrical stimuli, respectively. The displayed amplitudes represent the average obtained from three trials, sampled every 20 s over a 1-min period. To monitor the access resistance in each trial, the same analysis was applied to measure the positive-going capacitance transient to a 10-mV hyperpolarizing voltage command. oIPSC amplitudes were normalized to baseline (5 min) before drug treatment. The PPR of two oIPSCs evoked with a 50-ms interstimulus interval was calculated by dividing the amplitude of the second oIPSC by that of the first.

Two-photon laser scanning microscopy

FM1-43 imaging was performed on a Prairie Ultima (Prairie Technologies, Middleton, WI, USA), while a Scientifica Hyperscope (Scientifica, Uckfield, UK) multiphoton imaging system was used for GCaMP7s, g5HT3.0, and GRAB-DA3h imaging. Two-photon excitation was provided by a tunable Chameleon Vision 1 femtosecond Ti-Sapphire laser (Coherent) and the fluorescence passed through T565lpxr, ET525/50m and ET620/60m filters (Chroma Technologies) before being measured by a set of bi-alkali photomultiplier tubes (PMT, R9880U, Hamahatsu). During FM1-43 imaging, we used 900-nm laser excitation, while GCaMP7s, g5HT3.0, and GRAB-DA3h were excited at 920 nm. Laser power delivered to the sample was adjusted by a voltage-controlled pockel cell and was maintained below 15 mW at the sample to minimize phototoxicity and photo bleaching. To activate D1-MSN axons, a concentric bipolar electrode was positioned in the slice, a few micrometers into the cerebral peduncle at approximately 1-mm rostral to the SNr. Stimulus length (200 μs) and frequency were controlled by an Arduino Uno connected to two ISO-Flex (AMPI, Israel) stimulus isolators to deliver intermittent bipolar current pulses of ±300 μA. To stimulate striatal DA release, the electrode was placed in the striatum.

FM1-43 loading, destaining, and analysis

FM1-43 imaging of vesicular fusion at D1-MSN boutons was conducted following a previously outlined protocol (26). In brief, FM1-43, dissolved in aCSF (10 μM), was applied to an acute brain slice for 15 min in the presence of NBQX (10 μM) and AP-5 (50 μM). Incorporation of FM1-43 into recycling vesicles was done by electrically stimulating the D1-MSN fibers at 10 Hz for 5 min. After at least 20 min of washout with aCSF containing 100 μM Advasep-7 (Biotium), a cyclodextrin that minimizes extraneous FM1-43 labeling (28), the slice was subjected to 2-Hz electrical stimulation for 4 min to unload FM1-43. Last, to ensure complete depletion of the FM1-43–labeled vesicle pool, the slice was then subjected to 10-Hz stimulation for 3 min. Using acute brain slices from D1Cre::Ai9 mice (105), which exhibit tdTomato expression specifically in D1-MSNs, we determined that 98% of the FM1-43–labeled boutons colocalized with tdTomato and are thus located in D1-MSN axons.

We acquired z-stacks consisting of five images (16 bit, 333 pixels by 333 pixels) separated by 1 μm at sampling frequency 0.1 Hz using PrairieView 4.0.50. The field of view was 37.5 μm by 37.5 μm (pixel size, 0.113 μm) and dwell time 5.2 μs per pixel, and images were acquired at 40 to 80 μm into the slice.

Images were imported into Fiji as time series and inspected for displacement in the x-y-z axes. Only time series exhibiting minimal slice movement were selected for further analysis using Fiji and R version 4.1.2. The z-stacks were collapsed as a maximum intensity projection and then registered with the StackReg plugin. Putative synaptic boutons were selected with the analyze particles feature. Regions of interest (ROIs) were delineated around fluorescence puncta using predetermined parameters: (i) 1.5 times the SD of the fluorescence intensity of the background, assigned from an area in the image lacking evident fluorescence; (ii) circular shape; and (iii) diameter ranging from 0.1 to 1.5 μm. The mean fluorescence intensity was extracted for each ROI across the time series. Fluorescence run-down during imaging (due to ADVASEP-7) was corrected for in each time point by dividing the mean fluorescence intensity of the ROI with the corresponding fluorescence value of the area outside the ROIs (26). The ROI values were then normalized to the fluorescent intensity just before electrical stimulation (t = 0; F0) and after the electrical stimulation with 10 Hz (Finf). The criteria differentiating active synaptic boutons from those showing insignificant FM1-43 destaining were determined empirically by measuring the minimal FM1-43 destaining in aCSF with Cd2+ (200 μM), an inhibitor of exocytosis dependent on presynaptic Ca2+ influx, and in slices exposed to Ca2+ free aCSF. ROIs exhibiting >10% reduction in relative FM1-43 fluorescence were included. Nondestaining boutons were defined as the boutons losing >10% FM1-43 fluorescence only to 10-Hz stimulation.

To visualize the time-dependent changes in FM1-43 fluorescence during electrical stimulation (cf. Fig. 1C), we first averaged the relative fluorescence of active boutons within each slice. Then, we averaged the values across slices and plotted this average ± SEM, calculated from the SD divided by the square root of the number of slices. Fractional release was calculated as ln(F1/F2)/∆AP, where F1 and F2 represent the averaged relative FM1-43 fluorescence per slice, and ∆AP denotes the number of stimuli delivered to the D1-MSN axons during the time interval separating F1 and F2.

GCaMP7s, g5HT3.0, and GRAB-DA3h imaging and analysis

Brain slices were prepared at least 3 weeks after striatal stereotactic injections of AAV5, which transduced Cre-dependent GCaMP7s or constitutive g5HT3.0 and GRAB-DA3h expression in D1-MSN neurons and their axon terminals in the SNr. After recovery at room temperature (~22°C), the slices were transferred to the recording chamber on the two-photon microscope stage and continuously superfused with aCSF. Drugs were dissolved in aCSF, and slices were perfused with the solution for at least 10 min. The complete solution exchange in the tubing and recording chamber took approximately 2 min.

Images (16-bit, 512 by 512, pixel size: 0.069 μm per pixel) were acquired from 80 to 100 μm into the slice, with a sampling frequency of 1 Hz using ScanImage version 2021.0.0.3baa4b74be. In experiments examining electrically evoked serotonin and DA (Fig. 7 and fig. S3), images (250 by 250, pixel size: 0.14 μm per pixel) were acquired at 3.3 Hz. The time series were imported into Fiji, where they were registered with Stackreg.

Imaging of presynaptic Ca2+ transients in D1-MSN terminals was performed during bipolar electrical stimulation with stimulus trains of 2 to 10 Hz, lasting 30 s. Electrical stimuli were applied first in aCSF and subsequently in drug-containing aCSF. Thus, each fluorescent spot with an evoked Ca2+ transient could be examined in relation to drug-free conditions. In some experiments, the superfusion was then switched to drug-free aCSF and the effect of electrical stimulation was reevaluated to determine whether the drug’s effects could be reversed.

To analyze GCaMP7s imaging, circular ROIs were manually delineated around puncta exhibiting fluorescence increase during 10-Hz stimulation. While fluorescent elements, mostly fibers, were present in the absence of electrical stimulation, they remained unchanged upon exposure to electrical stimulation, chemical depolarization with 20 mM K+, or DA. These structures are likely fibers that have been severed during brain slice preparation, resulting in the loss of the Ca2+ buffering capacity that maintains a low GCaMP7s signal in intact axons. The mean fluorescence intensity was measured for each ROI across the time series. For each ROI, the average intensity of the first 10 image frames was calculated, and then each frame’s intensity value was divided by this average to compute normalized values relative to the background control before stimulation (F0). The normalized values of the ROIs were then averaged per slice. The relative fluorescence change from F0 (∆F/F0) was used to compare between treatment groups, calculated by averaging the intensity of 10 image frames during electrical stimulation. The relative effect of DA, SKF81297, and NAS-181 was quantified by calculating the difference between ∆F/F0 during drug treatment and ∆F/F0 in aCSF.

To analyze g5HT3.0 and GRAB-DA3h recordings, we extracted mean fluorescence intensity from the time series and calculated ∆F/F0, using the average fluorescence of the first three image frames as the baseline (F0). To compare ∆F/F0 changes across treatments, we used ∆F/F0 values measured 10 min into the image acquisition. For sensor calibration experiments with serotonin and DA treatment, we used the maximal ∆F/F0 (c.f Fig. 6, C and D). Because of focus shifts during solution exchanges and differences in PMT baseline values, it was not possible to directly scale the fluorescence between time-lapse acquisitions (i.e., F0 in one sequence versus FLast the previous sequence). Therefore, the effect of treatment across groups is expressed relative to F0 within their respective image sequences.

Fiber photometry

Stereotaxic surgeries

Mice were intraperitoneally injected with buprenorphine (0.1 mg/kg) and anesthetized with 2% isoflurane. AAV injection was administered via a Quintessential Stereotaxic Injector (Stoelting) with a 30 nl/min flow rate. The mice received an injection of 300 nl of AAV9-hSyn-5HT3.0 (38) in the dorsal striatum [anterioposterior (AP) +0.8, mediolateral (ML) −1.7, and dorsoventral (DV) −2.3 taken from dura] and a fiber optic cannula (RWD Life Sciences: 0.5 NA, 400-μm core, 4.3 mm) was implanted in the SNr (AP −3.28, ML −0.5, and DV −4.3). Rimadyl (5 mg/kg) and buprenorphine (0.1 mg/kg) were administered for postoperative recovery. Mice were housed for at least 3 weeks before experimentation, to allow sufficient viral expression.

Recording

Recordings were performed on a setup manufactured of optical components from Thorlabs. Optical modulation and lock-in amplification was accomplished with a Field Programmable Gate Array board (sbRIO9637) controlled by a custom-made LabVIEW (National Instruments) routines, according to (106). The light power from the excitation LEDs was set to 50 μW. g5HT3.0 fluorescence was measured with a sampling frequency of 333 Hz. An optical fiber cord (2 m, 0.5 NA, 400-μm core) was suspended above the home cage to allow free behavior of the mice. Mice were habituated to recording conditions for at least 30 min, after which they were transferred to a separate cage (with bedding material) for recording. Following recording of g5HT3.0 baseline fluorescence, mice were intraperitoneally injected with citalopram (0.25 mg/kg) or GBR12909 (20 mg/kg) and g5HT3.0 responses were measured throughout.

Analysis

Recordings were analyzed using a custom-made MATLAB script. To preprocess the signal, we used a low-pass Butterworth filter of 25 Hz and performed exponential curve fitting of both the g5HT3.0 signal (470 nm) and the autofluorescence (405 nm), which were then fitted using scaled linear regression and Z-normalized before analysis (107). We then quantified the AUC from 3-min recording sections of baseline and peak drug responses and expressed the latter as a percentage change from baseline. Statistical analyses were performed using Graphpad Prism, and statistical significance was assessed using one-sample t tests.

Immunostaining and confocal microscopy

Mice were deeply anesthetized with pentobarbital (100 mg/kg, i.p.) and transcardially perfused with ice-cold 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.5). The brains were dissected and immersed in the same fixative over night at 4°C. Using a vibrating microtome (Leica), 30-μm-thick sections were cut through the striatum and the midbrain and were stored at −20°C in cryoprotectant solution containing (v/v) 30% ethylene glycol, 30% glycerol, and 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.5), until processed for immunofluorescence. Sections were washed three times for 10 min in tris-buffered saline [50 mM tris and 150 mM NaCl (pH 7.5) (TBS)] and then incubated for 1 hour in TBS containing 10% normal goat serum (NGS) and 0.3% Triton X-100. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4°C with gentle agitation in primary antibodies diluted in TBS containing 2% NGS and 0.3% Triton X-100. The primary antibodies were a rabbit anti-DARPP-32 (Cell Signaling Technologies, 2306) diluted 1:500 and a chicken anti-GFP (Abcam, Ab13970) diluted 1:2000. The following day, after another three 10-min rinses in TBS, the sections were put in TBS containing goat-raised fluorescently conjugated Alexa Fluor 488, Alexa Fluor 568, and Alexa Fluor 647 (Life Technologies) secondary antibodies, and 2% NGS and 0.3% Triton-X100, for 45 min at room temperature. After three washes in TBS, the sections were mounted onto Superfrost microscope slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Fluoromount-G anti-fading medium containing 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (SouthernBiotech).

Images were captured with a Zeiss LSM800 confocal system equipped with four laser diodes (405 nm, 488 nm, 561 nm, 647 nm), and the Zeiss Blue software. In multiplex-labeling experiments, the fluorophores were imaged sequentially with the detectors adjusted to prevent bleed-through between different channels.

Drugs

All drugs were dissolved as stock solutions at saturating concentrations on the day of experiments, in the vehicle suggested by the manufacturer. Aliquots of stock solution was diluted directly into the superfusing aCSF. DA (Sigma-Aldrich) was diluted to the final concentration aCSF right before application to the slice. The aCSF for DA experiments contained 50 μM sodium metabisulfite (Sigma-Aldrich) to delay oxidation. The following substances were purchased from Tocris (UK), dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and applied to the slice at the indicated concentrations: 1 μM SCH39166 (#2299), 1 μM sulpiride (#0894), and 100 μM picrotoxin (#1128). NAS-181 (5 μM) was supplied by Chemtronica (Sweden). The maximal concentration of DMSO in aCSF was 0.1% (w/w), which did not produce a discernable difference from DMSO-free aCSF in any of the experiments. The following drugs, also from Tocris, were dissolved in water before dilution in aCSF at the indicated concentrations: 10 μM d-amphetamine (#2813), 1 μM SKF83566 (#1586), 1 to 10 μM SKF81297 (#1447), 1 to 10 μM SKF38393 (#0922), 10 μM NKH477 (#1603), 1 μM quinpirole (#1061), and 1 μM SCH23390 (#0925). TTX (HB1035) was purchased from HelloBio, dissolved in water, and applied at 1 μM concentration in the aCSF.

Statistical analysis (table S1)

Two-photon and patch-clamp recordings were analyzed using two-sample t tests for comparisons between groups or paired t tests for experiments with a pre- and posttreatment design. For experiments with more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, followed by Holm-Sidak or TukeyHSD post hoc tests for pairwise comparisons. When analyzing two independent factors, a two-way ANOVA (2 × 2 design) was performed. The number of observations represents slices obtained from at least three mice per condition. Fiber photometry recordings were analyzed using a one-sample t test to compare fluorescence changes against 0%. In all analyses, the residuals were tested for normality with Shapiro-Wilk test.
