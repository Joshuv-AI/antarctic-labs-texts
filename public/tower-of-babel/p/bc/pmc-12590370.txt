Community‐based participatory research on Alzheimer's disease and related dementias among Tibetan Buddhist monks in South India: The Lifestyle Assessment and Monastic Aging Study (LAMAS)
Authors: Tenzin Namdul, Richard MacLehose, Tenzin Pasang, Odile Madesclaire, Gelek Gyatso, Austin Henderson, Dedra Buchwald
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONThis protocol paper describes the methodology used in designing and conducting community‐based participatory research (CBPR) to estimate the prevalence of Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) among Tibetan Buddhist monks. The CBPR approach laid the groundwork to examine monastic lifestyles, including meditative and cognitive practices.METHODSThe study used a CBPR approach to examine the prevalence of ADRD and assess lifestyle and meditative and cognitive practices among Tibetan Buddhist monks.RESULTSThe study recruited 205 monks and collected a wide range of data, including monastic lifestyle, meditative and cognitive practices, mental health, sleep quality, physical measures, biospecimens, and other information.DISCUSSIONThe study was a first of its kind to use CBPR in estimating the prevalence of ADRD among Tibetan monks. BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD), the most common form of dementia, has become a growing public health concern due to worldwide demographic shifts and the strong association between aging and neurodegenerative diseases. 1 , 2 Globally, 11.5% of adults 60 to 69 years of age and 21.3% > 80 years of age have evidence of cognitive impairments. 3 According to the United Nations World Population Prospects, the proportion of people > 65 is growing more rapidly than any other age group, and by 2050, ≈ 16% of people will be > 65. 4 Furthermore, the distribution of dementia varies widely across countries for only partially understood reasons. In 2013, a major systematic review of dementias worldwide observed that the age‐standardized prevalence of dementia among people ages ≥ 60 ranged from 8.5% in Central America to 2% to 4% in sub‐Saharan African regions. 5 An epidemiological study to estimate the prevalence of AD among the general Tibetan population aged ≥ 60 found the prevalence of AD was 1.3%, one of the lowest in the world. 6 The study also found a reduced prevalence of AD among Tibetans who reported specific meditative activities. 6

Although AD and related dementias (ADRD) has become a serious public health concern worldwide, few studies have explored whether Tibetan monastic meditative, cognitive practices, or lifestyles might be protective. Studies suggest that general meditative practices may be associated with a decreased prevalence of risk factors for, and decreased symptoms of, ADRD and mild cognitive impairment (MCI). 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 A small trial of meditation suggested improved performance on cognitive tests, and an 8 week meditation program was associated with improved cerebral blood flow and increased scores on neuropsychological tests in persons with MCI. 11 Furthermore, a systematic review of meditation and brain volume in healthy individuals and individuals affected by neurodegenerative diseases found that meditation was associated with increases in gray matter volume in all 13 studies examined, leading the authors to suggest that meditation “may offset gray matter atrophy.” 12 Taken together, these studies suggest potential mechanisms by which meditative practice could influence cognitive function.

Tibetan Buddhist meditative and cognitive practices are unique as there is a strong emphasis on integration of samatha (focused attention) and vipasyana (analytical meditation). 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Single‐pointed meditation consists of focusing on one thing, such as breathing, for a prolonged period. Analytic meditation involves sustained periods of rational thought used to attain a deeper understanding of emotions, phenomena, problems, or sacred texts. In addition to combining focused attention and analytical meditation, Tibetan Buddhist practitioners use a wide range of cognitive practices, such as reciting text or mantras, contemplating those mantras, visualizing deities, and experiencing the flow of energies. 16 Of importance to Americans, data from the National Health Interview Survey indicates that from 2002 to 2007, 13% of adults engaged in some form of mindfulness practice. 18 We propose that if aspects of Tibetan Buddhist meditative and cognitive practices, or other aspects of their lifestyle, can prevent or postpone ADRD, their adoption could have a profound public health impact not only in the United States, but worldwide.

To address questions regarding the links between meditation and cognition, our research team collaborated with monks living in six Tibetan monastic communities in southern India. Our goals were to estimate the prevalence of possible cognitive impairment and associated demographic, lifestyle, and clinical risk and protective factors. In this paper, we provide an overview of the Lifestyle Assessment and Monastic Aging Study (LAMAS)’s goals and the community‐based, highly collaborative research approach we used with our Tibetan monastic partners in southern India.

METHODS

LAMAS project timeline, overview, and setting

LAMAS project timeline

In 2018, two members of the research team were invited to teach a weeklong workshop on cognitive impairment and public health to Tibetan Buddhist monks in northeast India as a part of an educational program organized by Science for Monks and Nuns, an initiative created under the auspices of His Holiness the Dalai Lama. The workshop was enthusiastically received, and after collaboration and consultation with interested monks, the groundwork was laid for a pilot study to investigate Tibetan Buddhist meditative and cognitive practices, lifestyle behaviors, and cognitive function. During a visit to the largest Tibetan Buddhist monasteries in India in 2019, a member of the research team met with the abbot of each monastery and proposed the LAMAS project as a collaborative with the monastic community. Over 6 months, the study protocols and measures were developed by the academic and monastic teams through weekly videoconferences and phone calls. Upon approval from the abbots of all monasteries, the University of Minnesota Institutional Review Board, and a monastic ethics committee, the LAMAS team collected data from March 2022 to August 2023.

The study was unique in having community members (younger monks) as part of the field team who conducted many of the interviews. Each monastery had its own field team consisting of between four and eight monks and monastic science teachers. The United States–based members of the team held a full‐day workshop for the monastic field teams to provide interviewers with training on responsible conduct of research, research ethics, and proper data collection. In March 2023, the LAMAS study team visited monasteries to meet with the field team and abbot of each monastery and convened a half‐day meeting to present and discuss preliminary research findings.

Overview

We used a community‐based participatory research (CBPR) approach to examine the prevalence of ADRD and to assess the lifestyle and practices of 205 Tibetan Buddhist monks. CBPR provides a framework to incorporate the unique knowledge of community members who are engaged as equal partners during the research process. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 Importantly, the CBPR approach helped us to recruit participants and collect data in a culturally sensitive manner. For instance, we consulted closely with abbots and administrative heads from each monastery in the planning phase to design a study that is informed by both a rigorous Western scientific approach and Tibetan Buddhist cultural knowledge. One member of our United States–based academic team was a United States–trained medical anthropologist and a Tibetan medicine doctor who had lived in and taught at one of the monasteries for > 2 years and, therefore, had close connections to the local Tibetan and monastic communities.

Furthermore, we formed local field teams at each monastery to be part of the overall LAMAS team and to help implement the study. These local research teams consisted of both younger monastic students and monastery science teachers. The field teams were involved in every step of the project design and implementation. Collectively, the LAMAS team engaged with the broader monastic community to plan the study and design culturally informed questionnaires about lifestyle factors and cognitive measures. The field teams aided in the translation of data collection instruments from English to Tibetan, helped enroll participants in a culturally sensitive manner, and collected data from participants. The CBPR approach involved monastic partners at every stage of our research and helped us establish respectful and trusting relationships with our local partners and potential participants, develop cultural humility, and use culturally relevant communication materials and specific language. We have detailed how we enrolled study participants in a culturally sensitive manner in the recruitment and eligibility section.

Setting

The LAMAS study was conducted in Tibetan monastic communities at Drepung Losel Ling, Drepung Gomang, Gaden Jangtse, Gaden Shartse, Sera Jey, and Sera Mey monasteries. These six monasteries are the largest Tibetan monastic universities in India and are located in Mundgod and Bylakuppe in southern India. Mundgod and Bylakuppe are two of the largest Tibetan refugee settlements in India with a population of > 30,000 Tibetans. 23 These settlements were established in 1966 on land allocated by the government of Karnataka. 24 The settlements integrate a Tibetan monastic school system with the surrounding large lay Tibetan communities—a structure similar to many of the great population centers in pre‐1959 Tibet. Mundgod and Bylakuppe are therefore considered a bastion of Tibetan life and culture.

Although these monasteries are affiliated with the Tibetan Central Administration (the de facto Tibetan government‐in‐exile), they are largely autonomous in their administrative functions and education offerings. Each provides a comprehensive education in Buddhist epistemology, philosophy, and psychology. All six monasteries offer science education to monastic students through the monastery Science Centers, which are supported by a collaboration between Drepung Loseling and Emory University with the Dalai Lama's guidance and support. 25

Development and selection of measures

Advisory board

In consultation with the monastic research team partners, an advisory board was formed comprising five monks > 50 years of age recruited from the Drepung and Gaden monasteries. The advisory board met monthly for 4 months during the design phase of the study to: (1) recommend adaptations to standardized measures to ensure face validity for this population of Tibetan Buddhist monks; (2) guide the development of new questions on the monk's unique monastic meditative and cognitive practices; and (3) review all study measures to ensure their relevance, usability, and interpretability.

Translations and modifications

All study measures were translated into Tibetan by a certified translation agency and then reviewed for comprehensibility, accuracy, and cultural appropriateness by the monastic research team and advisory board. The questionnaire was crafted to maintain cultural appropriateness. Questions pertaining to meditation practices required a more nuanced and novel approach to asking. How meditative practices are conceptualized required detailed guidance from senior monks on the advisory board. The advisory board also reviewed questions that make sense in Western culture but might create confusion or come across as too direct or disrespectful. For example, it is generally inappropriate, and even disrespectful, to directly ask someone if they meditate because it could be interpreted as examining the quality of the participant's practice. Furthermore, talking about one's own meditation practice is considered bragging and could lead to an underestimate of the total amount of meditation reported. Hence, questions on meditative and cognitive practices were integrated with questions about the monks’ daily practices and history at the monastery to avoid possible bias and inaccuracies. In addition, questions were asked about the duration of their meditative practices rather than in a simple yes/no format. The questions included time spent in different types of meditation practices such as mindfulness meditation, analytic meditation, reciting and contemplating mantras, and visualizations.

In terms of cognitive measures, we changed the images of a rhinoceros and a camel in the animal naming section of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) to a yak and an elephant, which are more familiar to Tibetans, especially elders. Similarly, in the memory section, we replaced the image of a church and a daisy with a temple and a lotus, respectively. Likewise, the clinical cognitive interview was adapted in consultation with a clinical neuropsychologist versed in cross‐cultural research and a member of our team who is a Tibetan medicine doctor and anthropologist. We adapted the clinical cognitive interview used in clinical settings and included sections which asked about education, daily activities, medical history including medications and mental health, clinical symptoms, substance use, and health habits. To gather accurate information, the clinical neuropsychologist and a member of our study team interviewed five monks to test its validity, particularly to assess the meaning and flow of Tibetan translations and the duration of the interview. Additionally, they paid close attention to the flow of questions, volunteers’ responses, and unclear questions. During these interviews, a monastic field team member with a good command of English joined to observe and offer support and advice.

Training and fieldwork implementation

Training of monastic field team

We completed an intensive and comprehensive training for the field team interviewers at each of the monasteries. A full‐day workshop was conducted at each monastery by academic investigators on the background and purpose of LAMAS; responsible conduct of research; and procedures for recruitment, consenting, interviewing, the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) mobile application, and all study procedures (e.g., questionnaire administration, MoCA administration and scoring, blood pressure, and weight measurements). Because Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative training on the responsible conduct of research was not available in Tibetan, we offered an alternative course that covered the same material. Appropriately obtaining informed consent was emphasized to ensure that potential participants were fully aware of study procedures and that although the abbots had approved the study, they were free to decide whether to participate. The consent form clearly stated this as well.

Recruitment and eligibility

Our recruitment and enrollment processes foregrounded local cultural values and norms to be more culturally sensitive and reduce overall participant burden. Recruitment methods relied on recommendations of the monastic field teams so that the enrollment process was culturally sensitive. The data were collected by both the United States–based study team and the monastic field teams. Monasteries are divided into housing units called Khangtsen, which are often loosely based on the monks’ geographic region of origin in Tibet. Each Khangtsen has a representative who serves as an administrator of that house. Upon the advice of the field team, the administrator of each Khangtsen was asked for his permission to visit and recruit participants. Subsequently, field team members visited the Khangtsen multiple times until all eligible participants were contacted. This strategy maximized recruitment because some senior monks rarely left their Khangtsen. During the first contact, the field staff used a bilingual script (in Tibetan and English) to explain the study and assess eligibility, then potential participants were given the opportunity to accept or decline the invitation to participate. These conversations were held in Tibetan for ease of communication.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) being a Tibetan Buddhist monk at least 50 years of age residing at one of the six participating monasteries; and (2) ability to provide informed consent as determined by a version of the University of California San Diego Brief Assessment of Capacity to Consent 26 that we modified for the purpose of this study. For eligible and interested monks, the field staff obtained written consent, typically using forms translated into Tibetan. The consent form was also read out loud to ensure participants fully understood the study procedures. Eligible participants were given time to ask any questions related to the research and request additional time to decide whether to participate in the study. A copy of the consent form, translated into Tibetan, was left with each study participant.

Assessments

Participants could schedule their assessment for a later time, but most were conducted immediately after the consent process. Interviewers administered a 45 minute questionnaire, cognitive tests (MOCA, Eight‐item Informant Interview to Differentiate Aging and Dementia [AD8]), and objectively measured height, weight, and blood pressure. Smaller numbers of monks (Table 1) participated in additional collection of detailed data, including: actigraphy to measure physical activity and sleep (n = 28), a urine sample to measure exosome levels (n = 31), a saliva sample for apolipoprotein E (APOE) assays (n = 121), a clinical cognitive interview (n = 30) to assess cognitive status, and a semi‐structured interview (n = 20) to elicit qualitative data on Tibetan Buddhist cultural concepts, values, and indicators of cognitive and physical resilience. When possible, the same subset of participants recruited into all aspects of the additional data collection.

Brain MRI

Twenty participants who scored low on either the MOCA or AD8 screeners and had consented to provide additional information were invited to have brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The MRI was conducted in Hubli, the closest city with a radiology facility, located ≈ 31 miles (50 km) from the monasteries. Groups of monks were accompanied by members of the research team and were provided with taxi transportation and lunch for the day‐long trip. Digital copies of the MRIs were de‐identified and used to measure brain volume, cortical thickness, and other brain measures associated with dementia. Measures

Questionnaires

The questionnaire gathered information on demographic characteristics such as age, place of birth, education, number of languages spoken, and years of monastic training and were administered by the monastery research team using the REDCap mobile app on tablets. We also queried participants on routine activities using the Instrumental Activities of Daily Living measure 27 augmented by items on daily monastic life and meditative and cognitive practices, as well as use of technology, including cell phone use, texting, and social media. Sleep duration and quality were assessed using the Pittsburg Sleep Quality Index 28 , and the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale was used for a depression screening inventory. 29 Physical activity and emotional state were assessed using a modified version of the Godin–Shephard 30 and Positive and Negative Affect Schedule, 31 respectively.

Cognitive measures

We used a mixed‐methods approach to estimate the prevalence of MCI and ADRD and their risk and protective factors. We used the AD8, 32 a standardized measure that evaluates behavioral and mental changes caused by cognitive changes over the past year. The AD8 has been translated into 11 languages and has excellent operating characteristics. 33 The MoCA is commonly used to screen for MCI and early signs of dementia. In addition to these screening tools, the one investigator who is a Tibetan medicine doctor administered a clinical cognitive interview to evaluate possible MCI and ADRD on a subset of 30 participants. The clinical cognitive interview covered the participant's medical history, clinical symptoms related to cognitive function and mental health, medications, daily routine, and health habits.

Objective measures

We measured participants’ height and weight, as well as measured blood pressure three times, keeping the last two readings. For blood pressure readings, participants were asked to sit quietly in a chair with their legs uncrossed. Measurements were taken with a digital blood pressure machine (Life Source Premium upper arm blood pressure monitor). We assessed daily physical activity (type, distance, and duration) and sleep duration over 72 hours in 28 participants who were fitted with and instructed on the use of an actigraph (AMI Motionloggers). A sample of participants who agreed to provide additional data were asked to provide biospecimens. Urine samples were collected to estimate exosome levels, which are strongly associated with plaque formation in ADRD. 34 Saliva for the APOE assay was collected using an OG‐610 saliva kit. DNA was extracted using PT‐L2P‐5 reagents, and gene sequencing was performed with Oxford Nanopore technology. Monks were transported to a radiology facility in Hubli (the nearest city), where the brain MRI was conducted on a 1.5 Tesla magnet. MRIs were read by resident clinical radiologists in India and re‐read and scored at the University of Washington for any signs of degeneration in parts of the brain that are related to or could lead to ADRD. Average cortical thickness was calculated using FreeSurfer software 35 and the Lesion Segmentation Tool for the entorhinal cortex; inferior, middle, and temporal pole; inferior and superior parietal; precuneus and posterior cingulate; pars orbitalis, triangularis, opercularis, and hippocampus. Normalized white matter hyperintensities and total intracranial volume were also computed.

RESULTS

This study demonstrates how we used CBPR in the Tibetan monastic community in southern India. In doing so, we illustrate how we were able to work closely with our local monastic partners from the initial stage of designing the study to preparing culturally sensitive survey questions, translating survey and cognitive measures from English to Tibetan, recruiting participants, and collecting data. We were able to strategize with our monastic partners to provide research training to our monastic field team in recruiting participants and collecting data. Out of 233 monks we approached, we enrolled 205 monks. The 18 monks who did not participate cited various reasons—mainly traveling, time conflicts—for their unavailability. We collected a wide range of data, including monastic lifestyle, meditative and cognitive practices, mental health, sleep quality, physical measures, clinical cognitive evaluation, and biospecimens (Table 1). Further, this study illustrates the ways in which a research team can work with and for a local community partner. Importantly, the study shows the feasibility of conducting a larger study in this population on ADRD and aging.

DISCUSSION

We describe the application of a CBPR approach to assess a wide array of domains germane to MCI and ADRD among Tibetan Buddhist monks living in exile in India. LAMAS is led by investigators at the University of Minnesota and the University of Washington, in partnership with six monasteries in southern India. This study is the first of its kind with the principal aim of estimating the prevalence of possible MCI and ADRD and to examine the role of monastic lifestyles and monastic cognitive practices on MCI and ADRD. Our CBPR approach engaged the community of monks at every phase of the study, which helped us to design questions in a culturally sensitive manner, as well as incorporate relevant Tibetan Buddhist ideas and concepts. We are using this same approach to plan, design, and conduct a larger longitudinal study on healthy aging and ADRD prevention in the Tibetan monastic population. This study also provided insights that will be helpful in adapting and modifying some of the standardized measures used in the study. While CBPR can increase the complexity of conducting research, we found collaboration and regular communication led to highly successful recruitment and enrollment methods, the development of empirically grounded culturally appropriate questions, and the building of trust among all parties involved.

We note several modifications increased the rigor of LAMAS. First, we realized that the written Tibetan language uses a formal register that is absent in the colloquial spoken form. While doing a role‐play exercise during the training, our field staff interviewers noted that some questions did not flow well, and others had subtle differences between the English and translated Tibetan versions. Working together, we quickly made changes to increase comprehensibility before beginning the data collection. Second, although we adapted cognitive measures, such as the MoCA, certain subtle and unexpected limitations remained. For example, during the MoCA interview, we observed that many monks are not used to drawing; therefore, drawing hands on a clock or tracing a cube to assess visuospatial/executive function was difficult, even for those who clearly were cognitively unimpaired. Likewise, in assessing food preparation in the Instrumental Activities of Daily Living questions, we learned that most senior monks do not prepare their own food or do their own shopping, stating, “I don't do these things, or I am not good with it since I haven't done this for so long.” We discussed with our monastic partners about reevaluating these questions for our future larger study. Furthermore, some activities in the Godin–Shephard, such as cross‐country skiing, roller skating, and golf without a cart, were unfamiliar among the monastic community. We thus substituted them with “prostration” and “circumambulating the temple,” a common Tibetan cultural activity. Nonetheless, most participants shared after the interview that they found the research questionnaire intriguing and enjoyable. Indeed, many participants attempted to engage the interviewers in even longer discussions. We also learned that gleaning nuanced cultural factors is as critical as collecting data using standardized tools. Similar to strategies used in the Harmonised Diagnostic Assessment of Dementia for the Longitudinal Aging Study in India (LASI‐DAD) study in India, 36 , 37 harmonizing Tibetan translations and original versions of standardized measures may optimize both strategies.

Lastly, during our visit with monastics in March 2023, we scrutinized aspects of the research that proceeded well and those that should be modified for the larger study we are planning. Particularly, reevaluating and further modifications of items on meditative and cognitive measures informed by local cultural values and sensitivities will benefit future studies. Further, a strength that many monastic field team members mentioned was the educational benefit of participating in scientific research. Although science training is a part of the monastic curriculum, actively conducting scientific research was less emphasized, and they found their participation as field team members beneficial. Discussions with the abbots also indicated their strong support for our work. Further, the abbots offered many insights and interpretations into the preliminary results that were presented to them, which we will report in another paper focused on study results. The abbots’ insights motivated the research team to plan the larger LAMAS project.

In summary, LAMAS was designed to examine risk and protective factors related to MCI and ADRD using a CBPR approach. The study was successful because our team collaborated with monastic partners using a CBPR approach, which in turn, led to excellent participation and rigorous data collection. Our collaborative work enabled us to build trust, develop durable relationships with key stakeholders at monasteries, and to enroll 205 participants. Specifically, we emphasized the importance of cultural education exchange between our team and the local partners. For example, while we learned Tibetan Buddhist cultural worldviews and practices from monastic partners, we trained them in conducting ethically informed research and to engage in good science aligned with both the US institutional review board and the local ethical committee guidelines. Furthermore, we disseminated study findings to the monastic community and solicited their feedback and insights. Importantly, this paper illustrates an opportunity for continued work with this unique population on healthy aging. The successful implementation and conclusion of LAMAS demonstrates the feasibility of conducting a larger epidemiological study—first cross‐sectional and then longitudinal—in this population. Our ability to meet challenges was due to close communication and coordination with monastic coordinators and field team members, who were intimately involved in all phases of designing and implementing this project and brought critical knowledge of the community settings to this work.
