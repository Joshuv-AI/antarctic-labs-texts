Revisiting the Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction: A Case for Extraluminal Coronary Occlusion as a Distinct Entity
Authors: Bhargava V. Devarakonda, Shrinivas V. Gadhinglajkar, Mukul C. Kapoor, Prafull Sharma, Markose L. Paret
Published: 2026

The fourth universal definition of myocardial infarction (UDMI) provides a foundation for categorizing myocardial infarction (MI).[123] MI is defined as acute myocardial injury, indicated by a rise or fall in cardiac troponin with at least one value above the 99th percentile, along with clinical evidence of myocardial ischemia. Evidence may include ischemic symptoms, new Electrocardiogram (ECG) changes, pathological Q waves, imaging showing new loss of viable myocardium or regional wall motion abnormality, or identification of a coronary thrombus. The Fourth Universal Definition classifies MI into five types: Type 1 (atherothrombotic), Type 2 (supply–demand imbalance), Type 3 (sudden cardiac death [SCD]), Type 4 (percutaneous coronary intervention [PCI]-related), and Type 5 (Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG)-related). This classification is based on underlying pathophysiology and procedural context and hence guides diagnosis, management, and prognosis.

Although it provides a framework for the management of acute coronary syndromes, the growing complexity of structural and congenital cardiac interventions exposes an inherent limitation in this classification. These definitions are comprehensive in the context of coronary artery disease and common interventions for its management. However, they are inadequate to include MI resulting from direct mechanical compression, congenital anomalies, or iatrogenic (surgical or intervention-related) disruption of coronary blood flow. There is a substantial subset of patients suffering from inadequate coronary perfusion, especially in the perioperative period, due to extrinsic or iatrogenic coronary obstruction. In these scenarios, an acute regional wall motion abnormality on echocardiography or an acute ST-segment elevation may be the only presenting perioperative manifestation, while defining infarction requires additional biomarker context. Extraluminal compression arising from iatrogenic causes (resulting from surgery or structural interventions) mandates immediate rectification of the mechanical cause. This difference from conventional MI treatment methods underscores the mechanistic distinctiveness of the proposed subtype.

Left circumflex injury in mitral valve (MV) surgery: The LCx artery is anatomically adjacent to the posterior mitral annulus and is susceptible to iatrogenic trauma—suture entrapment, distortion, or ring-induced compression—during surgical MV replacement or repair. The LCx courses closely behind the posterior mitral annulus, making it vulnerable to suture entrapment, kinking, or compression by annuloplasty rings.[1112] Anatomical studies demonstrate that the LCx can lie within 1–4 mm of the mitral annulus, thereby heightening the risk during surgery. Following an injury to the LCx following MV surgery, hypokinesia or infarction of the lateral or posterior left ventricular (LV) wall is seen in the majority (80%) of patients. Regional ST-segment changes on the ECG are observed in 68% of patients, with variable treatment response.[11] Intraoperative echocardiographic and electrocardiographic monitoring ensure timely detection and treatment either by immediate venous grafting or PCI.

Coronary complications after arterial switch operation (ASO): The presence of intramural coronaries and complex coronary patterns is associated with high mortality in the immediate post-operative period.[13] Following an ASO, long-term coronary complications occur in 3%–8% of patients. Imperfect transfer of coronary arteries to the neoaorta may lead to MI after ASO. In addition, various other congenital anomalies may lead to acute ischemic changes and cardiac failure.[14] Further, several mechanisms may lead to long-term obstruction or distortion of the coronary arteries. These include acute coronary take-off angles <30° (27%), overstretching due to progressive neo-aortic root dilation, and positional changes during growth that lead to an interarterial course (6%).[15] These mechanisms collectively heighten the risk of myocardial ischemia and sudden cardiac events post-ASO.[16] The 2018 AHA guidelines recommend evaluating coronary artery anatomy and patency in asymptomatic adults following ASO.[17]

Coronary air embolism during various cardiac interventions: The RCA is especially susceptible to air embolism during cardiac surgery due to its typical anterior origin from the ascending aorta, positioning it among the first coronary vessels exposed to any residual air during de-airing procedures, particularly when the heart is being moved or adjusted.[19] Most cases, being benign and transient, do not require significant resuscitative effort. During catheter-based procedures, accidental RCA air embolism has been reported to cause abrupt right ventricular (RV) collapse and ventricular fibrillation, with rapid restoration of perfusion and RV function following immediate aspiration.[20] Sinha et al.[21] reported a massive air embolism to the LCA and LCx due to a loose Y-adapter during PCI, resulting in hypotension, ST elevation, and asystole. Prompt aspiration through the guiding catheter could successfully restore flow. Due to the unique pathogenesis that causes myocardial ischemia, coupled with the potential for amelioration through logical measures, this may warrant distinct identification in the UDMI.

Extrinsic coronary compression (e.g., chest tube, hematoma): Mechanical compression from surgical devices or post-operative hematoma can transiently or persistently occlude epicardial coronary arteries, leading to ischemic injury without intraluminal disease.[2223] Electrocardiographic and echocardiographic changes may be evident immediately. Hence, clinical suspicion, early detection, and prompt correction of extrinsic coronary compression by pericardial tubes could prevent unnecessary revision of the coronary anastomosis.

Anomalous left coronary artery from the pulmonary artery (ALCAPA): In ALCAPA, the LCA originates abnormally from the PA, resulting in chronic myocardial underperfusion. Instead of normal forward perfusion, blood flows retrograde from the left coronary system into the low-pressure PA. This persistent ischemia, unrelated to atherosclerosis or thrombosis, can lead to MIs, especially during infancy. The primary mechanism of ischemia is the “coronary steal” phenomenon, in which blood is diverted from the RCA via collateral vessels into the LCA and ultimately drained into the PA. As a result, patients may experience multiple MIs within the first year of life, often leading to significant myocardial scarring.[242526] Even after surgical correction, such as reimplantation, coronary ischemia may persist due to complications like kinking or anastomotic narrowing.

These pathologies, while distinct in mechanism, are unified by ischemia driven by mechanical obstruction external to the coronary lumen. Neither thrombosis nor atherosclerosis may be involved. The current five-type taxonomy[1] does not adequately represent this gap, necessitating further attention.

Recent analyses by Sandoval et al.[28] and Chapman et al.[29] underscore limitations in the current UDMI for estimating cardiac enzymes. Both groups note that the prevailing framework does not sufficiently discriminate between heterogeneous etiologies of myocardial injury, particularly in non-atherosclerotic contexts. Furthermore, the umbrella classification of Type 2 MI has become a repository for diverse conditions, many of which merit mechanistic and therapeutic distinctions.

The clinical significance of a distinct class in any clinical classification primarily lies in its implications for prognosis and treatment. Failing to identify mechanically induced infarctions as a discrete category may hinder timely diagnosis, appropriate management (e.g., mechanical correction vs anti-thrombotic therapy), and accurate prognostication. It may undermine efforts to maintain precise epidemiologic data in cardiac registries.

The UDMI has significantly advanced MI classification. However, it remains incomplete given the evolving field of surgical, interventional, and congenital cardiac interventions [Figure 1]. While the current framework focuses on intraluminal coronary pathology, it overlooks MI caused by extraluminal mechanisms, such as mechanical or surgical compromise of coronary flow. We propose recognition of an additional “Type 6 MI” defined by extrinsic or mechanical coronary occlusion (resulting from cardiac interventions or surgery) in the absence of intraluminal disease, or add a distinct subtype to “Type 2 MI” to address this gap.
