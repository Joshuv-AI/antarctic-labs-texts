Prolonged Dysesthesia After Massive Accidental Semaglutide Overdose: A Case Report
Authors: Massimo Quarenghi, Anastasia Stanga, Alessia Bergamaschi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe use of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) has expanded rapidly worldwide for the treatment of obesity, accompanied by a parallel increase in medication errors and accidental overdoses. Gastrointestinal symptoms are the most commonly reported adverse effects in cases of supratherapeutic exposure, whereas neurologic manifestations remain poorly characterized.We report a case of a 50-year-old man with obesity receiving weekly semaglutide 2.4 mg who accidentally administered a total dose of 9.6 mg due to a pen-device handling error. Two days after the injection, he developed generalized burning dysesthesia and marked asthenia in the absence of nausea, vomiting, or abdominal pain. Laboratory tests performed 4 days after exposure were unremarkable. Symptoms gradually improved and resolved completely within 7 days without specific treatment.Clinical trial data suggest a possible dose-related signal for dysesthesia with high-dose oral semaglutide; however, reports of acute parenteral supratherapeutic exposure presenting with isolated neurologic symptoms are scarce. This case suggests that isolated dysesthesia may occur following semaglutide overdose and may follow a benign, self-limited course, a presentation relevant to emergency physicians given the increasing use of GLP-1 RAs.Keywords: semaglutide, GLP-1 receptor agonist, drug overdose, dysesthesia, accidental injection

Introduction

The use of incretin-based therapies, including glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) and dual GLP-1/GIP receptor agonists, has rapidly expanded worldwide for the treatment of obesity and metabolic diseases.1 Alongside prescribed use, increasing access to these medications has contributed to a growing number of medication errors and accidental overdoses.2,3

The safety profile of GLP-1 RAs is well established for gastrointestinal adverse effects, whereas hypoglycemia and pancreatitis remain uncommon but recognized concerns.4,5 In contrast, potential dose-related neurologic adverse effects remain incompletely characterized. Pharmacovigilance analyses of the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Adverse Event Reporting System have highlighted a broad spectrum of neurologic complaints associated with GLP-1 receptor agonists, whereas clinical trial data, such as OASIS-1, suggest a dose-related signal for dysesthesia.6,7 Nevertheless, data on acute supratherapeutic parenteral exposure remain limited.

We report a case of accidental supratherapeutic semaglutide exposure presenting with isolated generalized dysesthesia in the absence of gastrointestinal symptoms, highlighting a possible neurologic manifestation of high-dose GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure relevant to emergency physicians.

Case

A 50-year-old man with obesity (BMI 36.7 kg/m2) and no significant metabolic comorbidities was receiving subcutaneous semaglutide for weight management for over 8 months. The patient followed the standard titration schedule to reach a maintenance dose of 2.4 mg weekly, which he tolerated well without relevant gastrointestinal adverse effects. His weight decreased from 106 kg to 94 kg during treatment.

During the first injection with a new pen, the patient administered the prescribed 2.4-mg dose but observed residual liquid in the cartridge. The patient injected semaglutide using a multidose prefilled injection pen, which delivered the selected dose. The patient misinterpreted the residual visible solution in the cartridge as incomplete delivery. Assuming the injection had failed, he administered additional doses until the pen was empty, resulting in an estimated total exposure of 9.6 mg (4 times the intended weekly dose).

Two days after the injection, he developed generalized burning dysesthesia and marked asthenia, without associated nausea, vomiting, or abdominal pain. Symptoms remained intense for approximately 2 days, followed by gradual improvement with complete resolution within 7 days. Discussion

Most reported semaglutide overdoses are associated with pen-device handling errors.8 Published human data on GLP-1 receptor agonist overdose are limited. Available poison center data suggest that most exposures are associated with mild or no symptoms and are frequently related to therapeutic errors, with gastrointestinal symptoms being the most common presentation.2 This case highlights a potential risk associated with pen-device use, as residual visible solution may lead patients to assume incomplete dose delivery and perform repeated injections.

The U.S. FDA has highlighted reports of dosing errors with compounded injectable semaglutide that have led to overdoses and has noted that prolonged observation may be necessary because of its long half-life.8

Dysesthesia, a grouped term describing altered skin sensation, is an uncommon adverse effect at therapeutic doses in the randomized STEP trials (2.1% vs 1.2% with placebo), and events were generally mild and self-limited.9

A possible dose-dependent effect is suggested by the OASIS-1 trial, in which oral semaglutide 50 mg was associated with a higher incidence of dysesthesia compared with placebo (13% vs 1%).7 The 25 mg dose resulted in lower rates of dysesthesia (4.9% vs 0%).10 The underlying pathophysiologic mechanism remains unknown.7 However, published trial data do not provide detailed information on the distribution or clinical characteristics of dysesthesia; therefore, it is unclear whether symptoms were generalized as observed in this case.

In cases of acute supratherapeutic exposure, isolated dysesthesia may occur even in the absence of gastrointestinal symptoms. Given the prolonged half-life of semaglutide, symptomatic patients may safely leave the hospital after initial assessment with clear return precautions and short-term clinical follow-up arranged for them.

This report has several limitations, including its single-patient design, reliance on patient-reported symptoms, and the absence of objective neurologic assessment at symptom onset. In addition, the delayed medical evaluation limits precise temporal correlation between exposure and symptom development.

Conclusion

In cases of semaglutide overdose, clinicians should recognize that self-limited dysesthesia may occur even in the absence of gastrointestinal symptoms, as illustrated by this case. Although evaluation for rare but serious complications, such as acute pancreatitis and clinically significant hypoglycemia, remains appropriate, this case suggests that isolated neurologic symptoms can represent a benign manifestation of supratherapeutic exposure.

The rapidly expanding use of GLP-1 receptor antagonists calls for enhanced postmarketing surveillance to better characterize potential dose-related neurologic effects and their clinical relevance.
