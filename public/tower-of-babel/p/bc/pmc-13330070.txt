Development and evaluation of ‘Learning-Bytes’: a mixed methods study of e-learning modules for clinical educator development in allied health professions
Authors: Sarah McMullen-Roach, Caroline Fryer, Helen A Banwell, Kitty Pham, Emma Hiscock, Gisela van Kessel, Anna Phillips
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe growing demand for student clinical placements has led to increased reliance on clinician educators, many of whom have limited teaching experience. To support these educators effectively, professional development must be tailored to their educational needs and accessibility requirements.MethodsA convergent mixed‑methods design was used to develop, trial, and evaluate Learning‑Bytes, a suite of five e‑learning modules for clinical educators. The program was co‑designed with an expert reference group and informed by self‑determination theory and the ADDIE instructional design model to ensure relevance, accessibility, and usability.Program evaluation was guided by the Kirkpatrick Model and incorporated quantitative and qualitative data from surveys (Additional File 2) and focus groups to assess usability and impact on clinical educators’ knowledge, confidence, perceived competence, and behaviours over 12 months. Reporting aligns with the GRAMMS (Good Reporting of a Mixed Methods Study) framework. Clinician educators supervising allied health professional students at our university were eligible to participate.Data analysis included descriptive statistics for quantitative data, traditional content analysis, and inductive thematic analysis for qualitative data. Following threads of key findings through iterative team discussions, an integrated report of findings related to each aim was produced.ResultsOf 123 participants who enrolled in Learning-Bytes, 64 completed all five Learning-Bytes modules and 32 responded to both enrolment and endpoint surveys. Usability feedback was provided by 49 participants, and 13 attended focus groups. Post-program surveys were completed by 13 participants at six months and 7 at twelve months. Across all timepoints, improvements were observed in knowledge, confidence, competence, and behaviour. Integration of qualitative and qualitative analysis revealed that the program positively influenced educator attributes and practices, encouraged behavioural change, and showed potential to enhance student learning experiences.DiscussionLearning-Bytes meets a clear need supporting novice clinical educators feel more confident, competent, and capable in their roles. Flexible, relevant professional development is essential to sustaining a high-quality clinical education workforce amid growing placement demands. The program’s modular structure and rigorous design ensure adaptability to evolving educational needs. Findings suggest that Learning-Bytes not only supports individual educator growth but also showed potential to enhance the student learning experience.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09564-2.

Background

Quality clinical education is considered a critical component in preparing health professional graduates to be ‘work-ready’ [1–3] and a requirement of health professional regulating authorities [4]. Clinical education is frequently provided as student placements within industry-based settings, such as hospitals, community or privately owned clinics, with clinicians fulfilling the role of clinical educator [5]. During placement, students apply their knowledge and skills learned in the classroom to real healthcare situations, with clinical educators providing guidance, feedback and assessment to facilitate student learning. Clinical educators are a strong influence on the student learning experience, and the quality of the clinical education they provide can positively or negatively affect student learning during placement [1, 6].

The demand for clinical placements is rising, driven by global healthcare workforce shortages, ageing populations, an ageing healthcare workforce, impacts of climate change, and rising burdens of non-communicable diseases [7]. To meet this demand, there is increasing reliance on clinical educators who are inexperienced in the role [8], and increasing demand on experienced clinical educators to take larger cohorts of students. Clinical educators, however, report a lack of confidence in their ability to manage students, particularly managing multiple students or students experiencing challenges on placement and managing competing demands between clinical education and workplace expectations [2, 9]. This situation risks the satisfaction and sustainment of a clinical educator workforce and the delivery of quality clinical education to prepare health professional graduates. Clinical educator stress is known to be a key influence on industry provider decisions to offer clinical placements [10]. Failures in clinical education also place significant emotional, productivity and financial burden on students, training institutions, placement providers and the clinical educator involved [11].

While the support for clinical educators can be provided through strategies at individual and organisational levels, improving knowledge and capability in the clinical educator role is commonly sought through professional development, professional networks and relationships [12, 13]. For professional development to be effective, it needs to be meaningful and impactful for the clinical educator’s practice, easily accessible around busy workloads [1, 3, 9, 14], and involve a social learning component [15, 16].

Contemporary learners increasingly turn to online learning methods to upskill, such as modules and podcasts, for the advantage of ‘anytime, anywhere’ learning. The few studies that have investigated online learning specific to clinical educators report the frequent use of email and discussion forums and sophisticated module-based learning methods [17]. Evidence for the effectiveness of e-learning for clinical educators is limited, but there is evidence to suggest that it is comparable to traditional professional development methods [16]. As training providers seeking to meet the high demand for healthcare workforce training with increasing reliance on inexperienced clinical educators, we sought to develop an e-learning resource to specifically meet the professional development needs of this group of novice clinical educators.

The specific study aims were to:

Develop a novel e-learning professional development resource for new or recent clinical educators from health profession disciplines,Evaluate the usability of the developed resource for clinical educators,Evaluate the impact of the resource on clinical educator knowledge, confidence, perceived competence and clinical education behaviour over a 12-month period.

Method

Development and evaluation of the professional development resource was conducted by a team of six female academics and one female PhD student from five different health professions: Medical Radiations, Nursing, Occupational Therapy, Physiotherapy, and Podiatry. Team members had a variety of clinical education experiences as placement coordinators, educators and students. The team included expertise in qualitative and quantitative research methodologies. The team shared a motivation to address an area of clinical education where they perceived a gap in evidence-based practice. To minimise the potential bias from the team favouring their personal beliefs about the project topic, a reference group were recruited and consulted about decisions and findings across project development and evaluation. The reference group comprised an Academic Developer; Academic Dean in industry-based clinical education, online educational designer, audio-visual production designer and a new graduate representative from the Podiatry program of the university.

Development of modules

A program of e-learning modules titled ‘Learning-Bytes’ was developed during 2022 and 2023. Development was guided by the ADDIE model of instructional design [18]. ADDIE is a widely known and recognised framework for designing and evaluating educational interventions including training delivered in online environments [19]. The ADDIE name is an acronym of its five basic steps: Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation, Evaluation.

For Analysis, the team reviewed empirical data related to experiences of novice clinical educators including published literature, written feedback from previous training seminars, informal feedback and personal observations of common issues during clinical placements [2, 12]. The analysis identified the need for the professional development to be easily accessible within busy clinical educator workloads and identified relevant topics to address.

Self-determination theory (SDT) underpinned the design of Learning-Bytes. SDT is known for supporting autonomy, competence and relatedness to engage intrinsic motivation that benefits learning, behaviour and wellbeing [20, 21]. Learner autonomy was supported by the design of asynchronous and self-paced e-Learning modules. Learner competence was supported by each module addressing a topic identified from the analysis as meaningful to novice clinical educator practice (Table 1). Opportunities for interaction were included in the design to foster learner relatedness to the clinical education community. Directions from the project reference group, supported by published evidence [15], informed the design of succinct and flexible delivery of resources, and inclusion of formal recognition of learner engagement and learning.

For Development, the team were assisted by the university’s media support to create five standalone e-learning modules (called ‘Bytes’) and a capstone event (called the ‘Mega-Byte’). Each e-module contained: a podcast discussion with guest clinical educator and research experts; video examples of relevant practice created by the development team or sourced from existing content; a self-directed practice activity; an interactive Padlet™ conversation for participants to share reflections on the topic; an online quiz to self-assess learning; and optional resources including journal articles, book chapters and website links [22]. Prototype modules were piloted with a sample of three clinical educators and minor changes made to wording. The capstone event included a keynote speaker on ‘Enhancing your career through clinical education’, a panel discussion and facilitated networking opportunities.

For implementation, Learning-Bytes was trialled as an online professional development program for clinical educators via an external website using Moodle™ software [23]. Module functionality was supported across electronic devices including computer, tablet, and smartphone. Modules were accessible for a total of 15 weeks. The capstone event was implemented as a hybrid online and in-person event at the end of the trial. Attendees who completed all five module online quizzes received a completion certificate for continuing professional development (CPD) hours [21, 24].

The evaluation of the Learning-Bytes program was framed by the Kirkpatrick Model [25]. This model evaluates the effectiveness of training programs at four levels: (1) Response of learner to the learning experience; (2) Learning outcomes and changes in knowledge, skills and attitude; (3) Changes in behaviour related to application of learning; (4) Results or impact of the learning experience. The method and results of the project evaluation are reported in the following sections.

Study design and participants

The study was conducted from May to October 2023 at the University of South Australia (entity change to Adelaide University in January 2026). Ethical approval was granted by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the University of South Australia (Application ID 204531).

The study employed a convergent, mixed methods design, integrating quantitative and qualitative approaches to evaluate the Learning-Bytes program [26]. The purpose of using a mixed methods design was for complementarity in understanding the program’s usability and its impact on clinical educator knowledge, confidence, perceived competence and behaviour. The inclusion and integration of qualitative methods was to explore aspects of the learning experience that may not be captured by quantitative analysis of closed survey questions. Both quantitative and qualitative evaluations were conducted during the implementation trial. Participants were clinical educators who enrolled in the implementation trial of the Learning-Bytes program. The program was offered as a free professional development opportunity. To be eligible to enrol, participants were currently supervising, or expected to supervise within the next 12 months, students from allied health programs at the university. Participants were not excluded if they also provided clinical education at other universities. Allied health programs included clinical exercise physiology, medical imaging, occupational therapy, physiotherapy, podiatry, speech pathology, radiation therapy and nuclear medicine.

Convenience and snowball sampling methods were used to recruit participants [27]. The university’s Clinical Placement Unit identified and sent recruitment emails to all registered clinical placement sites. The study was also promoted through professional networks on social media. The Learning-Bytes program was advertised as being relevant to novice clinical educators, however participants were not excluded based on number of years of clinical education experience. The project team anticipated that more experienced clinical educators would still find value from participating in the Learning-Bytes program as its topics were broadly applicable to their practice and they could provide insightful feedback about the program design and content. Participants enrolled via an online Microsoft Forms™ survey which included eligibility screening questions and consent.

Quantitative data collection and analysis

Purpose-built surveys were administered to all participants using Microsoft Forms™ software at trial enrolment (week 0), midpoint (week 7) and endpoint (weeks 9–12), then 6 months and 12 months post-program (Additional file 1). The enrolment survey collected demographic information including age, gender, profession, current area of practice and prior experience as a clinician and as a clinical educator. The enrolment, endpoint and post-program surveys collected comparative data on the impact of the program on discrete domains of clinical educator practice (knowledge, confidence, perceived competence, willingness to take on students) using five-point Likert scales (Table 2). The two post-program surveys also collected dichotomous data (yes/no) on implementation of learnings into practice, and Likert scale data on changes in participant satisfaction with their clinical educator role following completion of the program. To enable data matching between surveys, respondents remained identifiable in the survey software at these timepoints and were de-identified prior to data analysis.

The midpoint survey was anonymous and collected data on the usability of the program using five-point Likert scales (ease of navigation, format preference, usefulness) (Table 2). This survey was purposefully designed to be anonymous to encourage participants to provide critical and honest feedback about usability.

Quantitative survey data were manually downloaded from Microsoft Forms™ into an Excel spreadsheet, de-identified, and checked for accuracy prior to analysis [26]. Descriptive statistics were used to report participant characteristics. Likert responses were ranked into nominal groupings between 1 and 5, where 1 indicated the least perception of confidence, competence, or willingness. Mean responses were reported for each survey timepoint.

Site analytics available from Moodle™ software were used to determine participant engagement with the Byte modules including number of resource views and downloads, unique views, total podcast and video minutes delivered and results from the five quizzes. Descriptive statistics were used to report site analytics.

Qualitative data collection and analysis

The endpoint and post-program surveys included a question asking participants to describe examples of how they had applied their learning from the Learning-Bytes program into their clinical educator practice. The surveys ended with a question asking for any other feedback to explore if there was usability feedback or program impact not addressed by the closed survey questions. The text responses to these two questions were analysed using summative content analysis [28]. Initially, re-occurrence of words or phrases were identified within the open text then two team members undertook latent content analysis to examine the meanings of the words and phrases and categorise them for reporting. Examples of practice were analysed by two physiotherapist academics (AP and CF) and open feedback responses were analysed by an occupational therapy academic (SMR) and a Medical Radiations graduate (KP).

Three focus groups were undertaken synchronously at the endpoint Mega-Byte event to collect qualitative data for a richer understanding of how participants and their clinical education practice were impacted by the program (Table 2). Two groups were online via Zoom™ conferencing platform, and one group was in-person. Participants who completed all 5 e-Learning modules were eligible to participate in the focus groups. Focus groups were facilitated by five members of the project team who had experience in conducting focus groups (GvK, AP, KP, HB, EH), using an interview guide developed by the team (Additional File 3). Participants were invited to discuss their experiences of applying learning from the Learning-Bytes program to their clinical educator role and practice. Each focus group was audio recorded then transcribed verbatim and analysed using an inductive conventional content analysis approach [28]. Initially, two team members (SMR and KP) familiarised themselves with the data by reading transcripts [29] then coded the data independently using a line-by-line open coding method [28]. They met to compare initial impressions and coding with disagreements resolved through discussion. Consultation with other team members was sought if consensus was not attainable. Codes were then sorted into categories by all team members collectively until consensus was reached, and these categories were grouped into meaningful clusters [27]. Clusters were iteratively arranged by the authors into overarching categories. Links were explored between each overarching categories and the sub-categories to determine themes that explained the data [30]. Throughout this process, continuous peer debriefing and data triangulation enhanced credibility of the analysis [31].

Mixed methods integration

The integration of the study’s quantitative and qualitative methods was undertaken during interpretation and reporting of analyses. All members of the project team discussed the complementary analyses relating to the research aims of usability of the Learning-Bytes program and the program’s impact on clinical educators. Discussions and comparisons occurred over several team meetings. By following threads of key findings through these discussions, an integrated report of findings related to each aim was produced [26]. The study has been reported using the Good Reporting of A Mixed Methods Study (GRAMMS) checklist [32].

Results

One hundred and twenty-three participants enrolled in the Learning-Bytes program (Fig. 1). Forty-nine of these participants responded to the anonymous midpoint survey on program usability. Sixty-four participants (53%) completed all five modules, and their survey data were included for analysis on program impact from enrolment, endpoint, and post-program surveys. Of these, 32 participants completed the endpoint survey, 13 participants completed the 6-month post-program survey, and 7 participants completed the 12-month post-program surveys. Thirteen of the 64 participants attended focus groups.

Of the 123 participants who enrolled in the Learning-Bytes program, the majority were female (n = 95, 77%), practicing as allied health clinical educators in major cities of South Australia (n = 97,79%) and aged between 18 and 30 years (n = 53, 43%) (Table 3). Most participants reported 10 years or less of experience in their profession (n = 74, 60%) and less than six years of experience in clinical education (n = 83, 67%). All invited allied health professions were represented with largest participation from occupational therapy (n = 31, 25%) and physiotherapy (n = 27, 22%). For the sub-group of 64 participants who completed all e-learning modules, the demographic distribution was similar, except there was a greater proportion in the sub-group who had less than six years of experience in clinical education (n = 47, 73%).

Usability of Learning-Bytes

Predominantly positive feedback about the usability (Kirkpatrick Level 1: Reaction) of the e-learning modules was found from the anonymous midpoint survey (n = 49), ‘other feedback’ question in the endpoint survey (n = 14) and site analytics. Most participants indicated at the midpoint survey that the online site was “easy to navigate and supported engagement in the course” (strongly agreed, n = 17; agreed, n = 28) and the “use of podcasts and resources facilitated learning so far in this course” (strongly agree, n = 16; agreed, n = 30) (Fig. 2). When participants were asked to directly compare podcasts to traditional methods for learning, a majority agreed that podcasts enabled them to engage more (strongly agreed, n = 7; agreed, n = 26) but nearly a third indicated they were equivocal on this question (neither agree nor disagree, n = 13). Fig. 2Responses from the midpoint usability survey

Responses from the midpoint usability survey

Five participants at program endpoint provided feedback describing their enjoyment of the online delivery style and combination of podcast, videos and interactive posts, as one participant wrote, “Having a combination of audio and video helped keep me engaged”. Only one participant noted an issue with the podcasts as “hard to absorb the information”, which they associated with being “a visual learner”. A general preference for use of podcasts was supported by site analytics showing a consistent pattern of podcasts being more accessed than video resources in each module (Additional File 1). The value of peer collaboration opportunities via the online Padlets™ was also recognised in survey feedback.

Overall, participant engagement with modules varied across the program with the greatest number of unique views for Byte 1 (101 unique views) and the lowest number for Byte 5 (49 unique views). Engagement with video resources demonstrated a similar pattern with unique views ranging from 80 (Byte 1) to 49 (Byte 5). In endpoint survey feedback, three respondents identified that the content and the quiz for the first module was “too theoretical” and not clearly relatable to practice. Quizzes could be attempted multiple times to achieve completion. The median final score for each of the quizzes was 100% (Additional File 1).

Impact of Learning-Bytes

Participants’ self-rated knowledge of best practice, self-confidence in clinical education skills and perception of competence in clinical education all showed a mean increase in scores from an average rating of ‘neither confident nor not confident’ at enrolment to being ’confident’ at program endpoint (Kirkpatrick Level 2: Learning). Participants’ willingness to take on students (Kirkpatrick Level 3: Behaviour) demonstrated a smaller mean increase with an average rating of being ‘willing’ at enrolment moving towards being ‘very willing’ at program endpoint (Table 4). An increase in mean ratings from the enrolment timepoint remained at 6- and 12-month post-program for all measures.

The mean rating of participants’ satisfaction with their clinical educator role at 6 months after completion of the Learning-Bytes program indicated an average response between ‘I’m neither more or less satisfied’ and ‘I’m a little more satisfied’ (Table 4). At 12 months post-program, the mean rating was ‘I’m a little more satisfied’ for the seven participants who responded to the survey.

Implementation of learning from the modules into clinical education practice was reported by most survey respondents at program endpoint (Kirkpatrick Level 3: Behaviour) (n = 20). Reported examples of implementation included changes in student feedback and conducting difficult conversations (n = 7), such as “improved delivery of feedback, both in time frame of delivery as well as content” and feeling “more confident in having those uncomfortable conversations with students”. Participants also described implementing their learning into student orientations (n = 2), improved student outcomes (n = 3), enhanced self-reflection and affirmation of their existing skills (n = 4). Implementation of learning into clinical education practice continued to be reported by most respondents at both follow-up surveys (6 months, n = 8; 12 months, n = 7). Examples of implementation shared at follow up were mostly changes to student orientation processes, providing feedback and having difficult conversations.

Theme 1: attributes

The attributes that participants considered to be important to engaging with the Learning-Bytes program and developing in their role of clinical educator were identified as being life-long learners and reflective practitioners.

Sub-theme 1.1: “it’s up to you whether you choose to become a good supervisor or not” (FG2)

Participants described being intentionally open to learning and continuously developing new skills, acknowledging that even as a clinical educator they are still in the role of a learner.

Sub-theme 1.2: being a reflective practitioner

The importance of reflective practice as a clinical educator was emphasised, with participants reporting the Learning-Bytes had prompted them to reflect more deeply on their own experiences and how this shaped them as educators. Learning-Bytes helped them to identify different ways to approach situations.

Theme 2: practices

Learning-Bytes was perceived as an opportunity for participants to validate their clinical educator skills and continue existing practices with greater confidence. The value of a community of practice to professional development as a clinical educator was a strong finding in this theme.

Sub-theme 2.1: “it was good to kind of say, i have been doing that” (FG1)

Participants reported experiencing validation of what they had already been doing and appreciation of the importance of their role, in addition to new learnings from Learning-Bytes. They valued the impact this had on their confidence and their appreciation of aspects of their role as clinical educator.

Sub-theme 2.2: “i can do something different” (FG3)

Learning-Bytes was perceived to offer helpful insights that could “serve as support for supervisors” (Focus group 2) throughout their clinical education career, thus enabling participants to implement positive changes to practice whenever there was opportunity.

Sub-theme 2.3: “connecting with other clinical educators” (FG2)

The process of connection and peer support experienced by clinical educators through the module activities was valued. Whilst some participants were able to access workplace support, many participants identified a desire for a community of practice or a mentor.

Theme 3: impact

Participants described a flow-on effect from their engagement with Learning-Bytes to positively impact the student experience (Kirkpatrick Level 4 – Results). One of the most emphasised learnings was having empathy for the student experience and the importance of creating safe environments for students.

Sub-theme 3.1: “They need to hear that they’re safe” (FG3)

Participants recognised the importance of creating safe environments for the students whilst supporting them to become clinicians. The participants reported doing this through use of empathy and recognition of the student’s broader life experiences.

Sub-theme 3.2: valuing the relationship

Building trusted and respectful relationships with students was also recognised as important yet it was acknowledged that this could be challenging to achieve. Appropriate support for effective student learning could be provided if a student felt they were able to disclose any concerns.

Sub-theme 3.3: student learning outcomes

Participants evaluated their own learning in terms of the observable impact on their students’ immediate performance on clinical placement, for example an increase in the students’ confidence. While impacts may vary between different students, participants expected student outcomes to improve with time and their improved educator practice.

Discussion

The Learning-Bytes program met the study aim for developing and delivering a novel professional development program of e-learning modules for novice clinical educators. Evaluation of the program demonstrated a high level of engagement with the e-learning modules. Both quantitative and qualitative feedback indicated strong acceptability and perceived ease of use, with participants reporting improvements in their self-assessed knowledge of best practice, confidence in their clinical education roles, and overall perceptions of competence. Importantly, participants reported they had changed their practice as clinical educators, or had intent to change their practice, in response to their learning. Engagement and usability of the Learning Bytes program suggest strong demand for quality, online, asynchronous self-paced learning, with most participants reporting appreciation of the format of delivery.

Significant positive changes in confidence as a result of Learning-Bytes engagement are likely to be of professional and clinical importance as perceived confidence is a well-established barrier to clinicians taking on the role of clinical educator, likelihood to take students and job satisfaction for clinical educators [2, 5, 33]. Supporting clinicians to have the confidence to engage as clinical educators and then sustain their confidence and willingness to engage are key strategies for training institutions to meet the growing demand for student placements. The improvement shown by participants may have further positive impacts for students on clinical placement, as students’ perception of educator confidence can impact how they view the quality of the clinical placement [2, 34].

The comparatively smaller increase in participants’ willingness to take on students in clinical education following completion of the Learning-Bytes program was surprising, given the pattern of increases in self-reported confidence and competence. It is possible this small change was linked to participants’ overall low self-confidence in their roles and capacity to deliver quality education, as this is a common finding reported from younger, less experienced health professionals [2, 33, 34]. It may also be associated with a ceiling effect from the eligibility requirement for participants to already be, or planning to be, involved in clinical education so that most participants rated their willingness to take on students as high before commencing the program.

Learning-Bytes demonstrated a mixed methods approach in Educational Design to rigorously apply research knowledge into practice [35]. A strength of the approach was the application of accepted models for training development and evaluation with an integrated interpretation of evaluation findings to provide a comprehensive picture of the novel program’s usability and impact [19, 25]. Collaborating with a diverse group of skilled clinical education practitioners and researchers ensured the development of relevant, responsive learner-centred content that was consistent with the primary purpose of this project. Guidance from self-determination theory placed an intentional focus on relatedness in program development to meet the identified need of addressing feelings of isolation that contribute to educator stress and negatively influence industry provider decisions to offer clinical placements [10, 33]. Social learning opportunities offered in modules included listening to podcast discussions between clinical educators and research experts, observing video examples of peers in practice, and interacting in the Padlet™ online discussion board with peers. It was pleasing then to find from the focus group data that participants valued learning from others during the program and the connection facilitated by interactive module learning. The importance of connectedness, or lack of, is frequently reported in education, with Trust, Carpenter and Krutka [36] describing the need to move ‘beyond silos’ to develop professional learning networks in higher education. The design of Learning-Bytes as a modular program also allows it to be a ‘stackable’ resource that allows further modules to be added that address new challenges and topics in clinical education as they arise, for example the role of Artificial Intelligence in placement conduct.

Learning-Bytes reached its intended group of inexperienced clinical educators with the majority of enrolments and evaluation respondents having less than 6 years’ experience in the role. It is notable then that the highest proportional dropout of participants between enrolling and completing the Learning-Bytes program was in the 41–50 years age group, and less of this age group participated in the program evaluations to understand their views on usability. As the program was designed to provide foundational skills and knowledge in clinical education, it may be that this program content was not engaging for more experienced clinical educators. Or the finding may be related to age-related differences in preference of delivery mode or competing family and work commitments [37]. The allied health workforce in Australia has an age distribution with more middle-aged workers than the general working population [38] and so the cohort of 41–50 years is an experienced and valuable resource to exploit in the clinical training of students. An understanding of how to improve the engagement of this age group in professional development requires further investigation to support all clinical educators in the context of high placement demands.

The study findings position Learning-Bytes as a scalable, evidence-informed solution for building clinical educator capacity, with the flexibility and relevance needed to meet the evolving demands of contemporary clinical education.

Limitations

It is important to acknowledge limitations to this study. Firstly, this was a small sample of clinical educators working across Australia for one or more universities, which limits the generalisability of the findings. The reliability and broader applicability of the measures of programme effectiveness would therefore benefit from replication with a larger and more diverse participant cohort. Female participants were over-represented in the study when compared to the gender distribution of the Australian allied health workforce [39]. The gender distribution of clinical educators is not clear and so the self-nomination of the sample may reflect who is currently providing clinical education to allied health students. Secondly, while Learning-Bytes achieved positive impacts for clinical educator learning over a 12-month period, only a small number of clinical educators completed the follow up surveys. What is not yet known is how the change in confidence and competence can be sustained to maintain a clinical educator workforce that meets placement demand, and if the change in educator behaviour translates to changes in student learning on clinical placement. This gap in knowledge could be addressed by a longitudinal study design with inclusion of outcome measurement from students as well as educators.
