Background

Tuberculosis (TB), an infectious disease caused by bacteria (Mycobacterium tuberculosis) that primarily affects the lungs [2], is the world’s leading cause of death from a single infectious agent as of 2025, surpassing the coronavirus disease [2]. This is despite the disease being both curable and preventable. In 2023, approximately 10.8 million people were diagnosed with TB worldwide, comprising 6.0 million men, 3.6 million women and 1.3 million children [2]. The persistence of this burden cannot be explained by medical factors alone.

As early as the 19th century, Virchow conceptualised TB as a ‘social disease’ and stated that there was an indisputable link between poverty and social conditions with the spread of TB [3]. Nowhere is this more evident than in Bangladesh’s dense urban communities where TB continues to exact a heavy toll despite years of control efforts. In 2023 alone, Bangladesh saw an estimated 379,000 new TB cases, a number nearly equivalent to the population of Iceland, alongside an incidence of approximately 221 cases per 100,000 people and 44,000 TB-related deaths [4]. This burden places Bangladesh among the world’s 30 high TB-burden countries [5]. In addition, prior studies find that TB disproportionately afflicts the urban poor, e.g., TB prevalence is four times higher in the capital Dhaka’s slums than the city average, a disparity driven by overcrowding, limited health literacy and stigma [6].

Stigma, in particular, creates a vicious cycle of secrecy and delayed treatment. In Bangladesh, deep-rooted cultural narratives often frame TB as something shameful for the family, a form of divine punishment or curse [7]. Such beliefs drive patients into the shadows. Women with TB are especially affected by such social stigmas as they fear damaged marriage prospects or even expulsion from their homes if their diagnosis becomes known [6]. This stigma inflicts psychological trauma and actively discourages patients from seeking care, thereby undermining TB control by delaying diagnosis, increasing treatment default and limiting community support. Compounding this stigma are behavioural risk factors, particularly tobacco use among men. While men are directly affected by smoking, women, despite having a relatively low prevalence of tobacco use in Bangladesh, are disproportionately exposed to second-hand smoke.

Bangladesh ranks among the top 30 nations for tobacco consumption, with 29.7% of its population aged 15 and above using tobacco in some form [8]. Dating back to 2018, 23.5% of adults were current tobacco smokers in Bangladesh while 27.5% were current consumers of any smokeless tobacco product [9]. This smoking epidemic observed within Bangladesh directly fuels the TB crisis. Smokers face approximately a 1.73-fold increased risk of contracting TB infection and more than double the risk of developing active TB disease when compared to nonsmokers [10].

Recognising this public health problem at the intersection of TB and tobacco smoking, the ARK Foundation, a non-governmental organisation, launched the Quit4TB Trial in urban Bangladesh to explore how integrating smoking cessation into TB services could improve patient outcomes. This is the first trial evaluating the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of an mHealth-based mTB-Tobacco intervention to support smoking cessation among TB patients [11]. The mTB-Tobacco intervention is a mobile health (mHealth)-based smoking cessation programme developed for people with drug-sensitive pulmonary TB. Participants received mobile-phone text messages throughout their TB treatment, delivered daily during the first two months and then monthly for a further four months [11]. The messaging package combined behavioural support for tobacco cessation with motivational content to support TB recovery. Messages were organised around three broad themes: educational messages, medically focused messages, and invitations for follow-up support. A number of 539 patients from Bangladesh received the messages. Results from the Quit4TB trial demonstrated that the intervention significantly improved smoking abstinence compared with usual care. Within the present study, community engagement activities complemented the mHealth intervention by identifying barriers to smoking cessation, exploring patient and caregiver experiences, and informing the development and refinement of communication materials and cessation-support approaches that could be used alongside the digital programme. The community engagement activities were therefore designed not to evaluate the effectiveness of the mHealth intervention itself, but to ensure that tobacco cessation support and communication approaches were grounded in the lived experiences, preferences, and needs of people affected by TB.

Uniquely, this initiative, funded by the National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR) Global Health Research Unit on Respiratory Health (RESPIRE), embedded community engagement alongside the clinical intervention. In doing so, the project sought to meaningfully involve TB-affected communities throughout the research process, positioning them as active contributors rather than passive subjects. [12] .

Embedding community engagement within the Quit4TB trial was driven by both ethical and pragmatic rationale. From an ethical, rights-based perspective, decisions that affect the lives and well-being of people should involve those who are directly affected, recognising their agency and their legitimate role in shaping processes that influence their health and care. Engaging the community can elevate the quality and impact of health research in numerous ways. It grounds the work in lived reality where the priorities, language and concerns of those actually bearing the brunt of TB are clearly acknowledged, making findings more relevant while increasing the probability of interventions being accepted [13]. On the other hand, if done poorly, community engagement may add to the burdens on already challenged communities, and can be detrimental to their health and wellbeing, causing exhaustion, stress, and fatigue, which can, in turn be dispiriting and disempowering [14].

Why community engagement matters

By involving individuals affected by TB, survivors, caregivers, frontline workers, and community leaders from the design stage to dissemination, the work becomes a two-way dialogue in which communities shape both what is asked and how solutions are implemented.

Community engagement (CE) is increasingly recognised as vital for developing high quality, relevant, and implementable research, especially when working with marginalised groups whose voices are often excluded or overlooked. Evidence confirms that research grounded in community participation leads to better understanding, stronger trust, and interventions that respond more directly to people’s lived realities [15]. For individuals affected by TB, being invited to share their experiences does more than generate data, it shifts the research relationship from extractive to collaborative.

As with other health issues influenced by social stigma and patriarchal norms, these factors also shape health-seeking behaviour in the context of TB. Engaging communities throughout the research process helps bridge cultural and knowledge gaps between investigators and participants. It also provides a crucial mechanism for researchers to confront their own unconscious biases, ensuring a diversity of voices, and design programmes that reflect local constraints and priorities.

The value of this approach is supported by a growing body of evidence from low and middle-income countries. A multi-country review of TB programmes across Asia and Africa found that community participation strengthened every part of the TB care cascade, from identifying local needs and conducting active case-finding to supporting long-term adherence to treatment [16]. Community-based interventions increased case detection by more than threefold and improved treatment success rates by around 9%. In Ethiopia, a community-led strategy that trained female health extension workers to conduct home visits doubled TB case notifications and raised treatment success from 77% to 93% [17]. Patients and providers attributed this success to the programme’s accessibility and its ability to reach women, the poor, and other underserved groups who often remain invisible in traditional facility-based systems.

Similar benefits have been documented in tobacco-related interventions. In Indonesia, a smoke-free homes campaign co-developed with community leaders shifted smoking norms inside households. Instead of enforcing rules from above, the programme drew on local values and collective decision-making. As a result, the proportion of men who avoided smoking indoors increased from 11% to 54%, and adherence remained between 70 and 90% in follow-up assessments months later [18]. Women reported feeling empowered to speak up about tobacco use because the wider community supported their stance.

These experiences show that when communities lead, interventions become more acceptable and sustainable. People are more willing to act when programmes reflect their cultural norms, daily challenges, and priorities. This has direct relevance for the Quit4TB trial in Bangladesh. Ultimately, community engagement strengthens TB and tobacco control efforts not only by improving data, but by anchoring solutions in the lives, perspectives, and agency of the people most affected.

Moreover, CE is not only a means to improve the quality and relevance of research, but also an ethical imperative. For individuals affected by TB, whose experiences are often shaped by stigma, marginalisation, and structural inequities, meaningful inclusion in research processes is a matter of equity, dignity, and recognition of lived experience. Engaging communities therefore represents a shift towards more inclusive and participatory forms of knowledge production, where those most affected are not merely subjects of research but contributors to it. While CE can enhance the relevance, acceptability, and implementation of interventions, these pragmatic advantages should be understood as secondary to its intrinsic ethical value in promoting more just and equitable research practices.

Methods

Setting and design

Designed to assess the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of mTB-Tobacco, a mobile health–based programme, in a high TB-burden country like Bangladesh, the trial has embedded community engagement at every stage. This approach helps build trust with patients, brings to light gender and stigma-related barriers that conventional research may overlook, and ensures that tobacco cessation strategies reflect the real-life experiences of families affected by TB. By bringing together a wide range of stakeholders such as patients, their families, healthcare staff, community health workers, and government representatives, our aim was to create a safe and collaborative space where different groups could work together to encourage tobacco cessation, support TB recovery, and reduce the stigma that many individuals affected by TB face in their daily lives. Along with limited mental and behavioural support, this stigma often becomes a barrier to effective treatment. The community engagement meetings helped address these challenges by offering relevant support and resources tailored to the needs of the community. CE, as a pivotal element of research, has been an essential part of the Quit4TB Trial from the very beginning.

We worked in government facilities serving low-income urban outskirts around Dhaka, including Narayanganj, Narsingdi and Keraniganj Upazila Health Complexes (primary care centres) and associated DOTS (Directly Observed Treatment, Short-Course) centres. Meetings were held in neutral and accessible locations near health facilities, with careful attention to privacy given the stigma surrounding TB. Seating was arranged in a circular format to minimise hierarchical dynamics and encourage open interaction, allowing participants to engage on equal terms. No data were collected as part of the community engagement activities. All study data were collected as part of the Quit4TB trial.

At the start of each meeting, ground rules were established to promote respectful and inclusive dialogue. Facilitation ensured that all participants, regardless of their social or professional background, had an equal opportunity to contribute. Participants were offered the option to attend with a trusted companion or caregiver and were informed of their right to withdraw at any stage without consequence. Given the mixed composition of groups, particular care was taken to create a safe space for less formally empowered participants. Participants were not required to disclose sensitive identities, such as their TB status, thereby reducing the risk of unintended stigma in their daily lives.

Facilitation was gender-sensitive, with both male and female facilitators present. Deliberate efforts were made to create a supportive environment where women could speak freely without interruption, while actively addressing cultural norms that may constrain their participation. Facilitators had prior experience in qualitative research and community engagement, with training in participatory methods and group facilitation in public health settings.

Community engagement for this trial followed three linked phases: Connect, Co-create, and Collaborate. This phased approach aligns with participatory action research (PAR) principles, as articulated by Paulo Freire (1970), emphasising dialogue (Connect), co-learning and reflection (Co-create), and collective action (Collaborate) through iterative engagement with communities, consistent with broader PAR applications in public health. [19] This phased structure was selected to ensure participant involvement across multiple stages of the research process, consistent with PPI principles that emphasise continuous engagement rather than one-time consultation.

From September 2024 to October 2025, we implemented a combination of engagement activities including forums, group discussions, key informant interviews, short debriefs, validation workshop and dissemination meetings. Our goals included (i) developing community engagement materials on TB such as leaflets, brochures, and handouts in local languages, (ii) developing a policy brief with the findings from the CE meetings where recommendations from the members are incorporated for improving TB treatment and tobacco cessation practices, (iii) conducting a national advocacy meeting with the highest government authority for TB, and, (iv) disseminating the learnings.

Participants

In Bangladesh, TB care starts when patients reach out to the nearest public healthcare facilities, and the Tuberculosis and Leprosy Control Assistants register them after referral by doctors. TLCAs serve as the first point of contact and also act as focal persons, as they keep track of patient progress and coordinate with field staff.

These community engagement meetings included a total of 34 participants from the above listed stakeholder groups, with 9 from Narayanganj, 10 from Keraniganj, and 15 from Tongi wards (as shown in Table 1).

Participants were eligible to join the engagement meetings if they were fifteen years or older and could give informed consent. We identified community members purposively to reflect diversity by gender, age, marital status, socio-economic background, and tobacco-use status. For groups at higher social risk in this region, such as unmarried young women, we reached participants through trusted community workers and women’s groups using a snowball approach.

In most cases, these participants were involved across three phases of engagement – connect, co-create, and collaborate. During the connect phase, participants were engaged through initial meetings to understand community perspectives and build rapport. In the co-create phase, they contributed to the development of communication materials by sharing their experiences and feedback. In the collaborate phase, participants were engaged in refining these materials to ensure clarity, relevance, and acceptability. While not all participants were involved in every phase, the majority contributed across multiple stages, with some unable to attend all stages due to other personal or professional commitments.

Consent and ethical considerations were ensured through a careful, participant-centred process followed by ARK Foundation’s ethics policy. Before each activity, we explained the purpose, procedures, and use of information in simple, colloquial Bangla and obtained written or verbal consent depending on participant literacy. Participation was entirely voluntary, and we ensured confidentiality by avoiding personal identifiers. Activities were planned to minimise burden, avoid disrupting care, and prevent discomfort or stigma, especially for individuals affected by TB. Throughout the process, we maintained transparency by sharing back emerging insights through debriefs and member-checks, ensuring respectful, trustworthy, and ethically grounded engagement.

Approach for engagement

Across the three phases, participants were engaged in a continuous and iterative manner, where initial insights gathered during the connect phase informed co-creation activities, and subsequent collaboration ensured refinement and validation of outputs.

Connect

We introduced the community engagement work in primary healthcare facilities by involving individuals affected by TB, their families, healthcare staff, community health workers, and government representatives. With informed consent, we formed Patient-Public Involvement or PPI groups that were consulted in follow-up meetings and validation workshops in regular intervals. To facilitate these meetings, we reached out to the district and sub-district government authorities and convinced them how this community engagement work would enrich the government’s understanding of the social perspectives of TB and help them formulate policies responsively. We invited participants who lived in the research sites and ensured diversity among members by gender, age, marital status, socio-economic background, and tobacco-use status. Additionally, we mapped local barriers and facilitators, and ran listening sessions with individuals affected by TB and caregivers. Gender-sensitive facilitation, Bangla and local dialects, and the option to bring a trusted companion helped reduce power differences.

Listening sessions were conducted using a semi-structured format, allowing open discussion while guiding participants through key topics related to TB, tobacco use, and care experiences. Facilitators actively managed group dynamics by encouraging quieter participants to contribute and ensuring that no single group, such as providers, dominated the discussions.

Field notes captured access barriers, community norms, and early ideas for messages on health behaviours and care that could be shared with current and future TB patients as well as with the wider general audiences through brochures, flyers, or simple social media contents.

Co-create

In the co-creation phase, we invited participants to share their ideas and contribute to drafting materials that could be used to design communication outputs, such as brochures or flyers. Participants unanimously shared that it is crucial to visually show the suffering caused by TB in the materials. They therefore suggested that brochures or flyers carrying images of TB-affected lungs could be helpful in conveying the message. Another idea that emerged from the meetings was to include the fear-of-death in the knowledge products. For instance, if we were designing graphics, they suggested using images related to death or its consequences. Additionally, for text-based products, they recommended tailoring messages to include the number of TB-related deaths in comparison to other figures in order to highlight the severity of the disease.

While participants initially proposed fear-based or death-related imagery to communicate the seriousness of TB, these suggestions were carefully deliberated during iterative co-creation and validation processes. Through group discussions, participants expressed concerns that such approaches could unintentionally reinforce stigma or be perceived as blaming. As a result, a consensus emerged to prioritise non-alarmist, non-blaming language and visuals, framing tobacco use as a form of dependence requiring support rather than individual fault.

We used storyboards and think-aloud walk-throughs to test clarity, tone, and cultural fit. Revisions continued until participants said materials were clear, respectful, and useful. Decisions on message development were made through iterative discussion between participants and the research team, with participant inputs guiding priorities while facilitators ensured that final outputs remained contextually appropriate and ethically sensitive.

Collaborate

In the Collaborate phase, we continued working closely with participants through PPI meetings and validation workshops. We conducted 8 PPI meetings across three of our engagement sites, with three each in Keraniganj and Naryanganj, and two in Tongi. We also arranged two validation workshops in Keraniganj and Naryanganj, while one in Tongi.

During these sessions, we trialed draft communication messages and refined them based on participant feedback. We documented every change in a simple adaptation log that noted what changed, why it changed, and the effect we observed. After each activity we ran short member-check huddles to confirm that interpretations accurately reflected participants’ views.

PPI activities at a glance

As mentioned earlier, a total of eight PPI meetings and two validation workshops took place across three sites. In the first two meetings, we mainly focused on connecting with participants and listening to their ideas. For instance, on 8 October 2024 in Narayanganj, participants discussed tobacco-related risks during TB treatment and called for strong visual messages in simple Bangla, non-blaming language, caregiver involvement, and regular follow-up. On 2 October 2024 in Keraniganj, fourteen participants worked through two case studies of men who struggled to quit smoking and ignored advice from female relatives; the groups proposed gradual reduction strategies, alternative activities, peer and daughter prompts, and support from community leaders.

On 11 November 2024, participants in Tongi explored the root causes of TB and its connection to tobacco use, sharing experiences and challenges in managing the disease. On 19 November 2024 in Keraniganj, patients, caregivers, BRAC representatives, and the Resident Medical Officer refined a one-page resource titled ‘TB Can Be Cured with Care,’ recommending comparative lung visuals, a cause–effect–action sequence, larger fonts, clearer next steps, and short provider scripts.

Key informant and case-story interviews

To complement and deepen the insights from the community forums, we conducted key informant interviews and collected detailed case stories, which provided nuanced perspectives from healthcare providers and patients on the realities of integrating tobacco cessation with TB care.

Clinical perspectives on cessation and care

A Resident Medical Officer or RMO (Keraniganj) described common withdrawal symptoms when people try to quit. An RMO is stationed at government district- and sub-district-level health facilities with responsibilities ranging from daily patient care to overseeing administrative tasks. He also mentioned the way smoking worsens TB-related cough and delays healing, and the need for patient and family education, peer influence for youth, alternatives to smoking as ‘entertainment,’ and structured advice from health workers. He stressed the provider role from diagnosis to cure, the importance of consistent medicines and follow-up, and the value of engaging local leaders such as imams and teachers.

Frontline challenges and systemic evolution of TLCAs

A senior TLCA with three decades of service linked tobacco to a large share of local TB, noted heavy male smoking, and described persistent infrastructure constraints such as cramped rooms, broken X-ray machines, and unreliable equipment. A younger TLCA highlighted high monthly testing load, higher male caseloads, gendered stigma for young women, confidentiality needs, barriers in poorer areas, and recent gains from portable X-rays, free films, nearby GeneXpert sites, and Global Fund support.

Patient lived experience and cessation journeys

Mr. Rahim (pseudonym), age 78, reported reducing from more than twenty-five cigarettes a day to three after diagnosis. Mr. Rahim comes from a socio-economically marginalised background and lives in one of the research sites and has a family of eight members including his wife, his two sons and grandchildren. Our researchers have been working with him for some time as Mr. Rahim has been one of the recipients of mHealth messages. After working with our researchers, he expressed confidence about quitting soon. Israfil (pseudonym), age 32, a recovered patient, described how daily SMS prompts about medicines, family, and risks helped him reduce smoking and keep a disciplined routine through treatment.

Information handling and analysis

Audio files from all the community engagement activities were expanded into detailed notes in Bangla and translated into English with back-checks by bilingual staff. After each activity the facilitator and notetaker wrote a one- to two-page case story capturing context, who attended, memorable quotes, turning points, and immediate implications for messages. Team members highlighted key points on paper or in MS Word and compared cases in a simple matrix to see patterns across sites and roles. We then wrote plain-language themes that preserved participant voice and verified preliminary interpretations in member-checks.

We also used a tool called ‘Change Matrix’ to clarify link what participants shared with the changes we made. This matrix recorded the feedback we received, the exact wording or tool that was revised. These iterative comparisons and documented changes enabled the team to directly translate participant insights into communication outputs, ensuring that the final materials reflected synthesised inputs rather than isolated observations.

Ethical procedures included written consent or witnessed verbal consent where literacy was a barrier. For ages fifteen to seventeen, we obtained assent and guardian consent. Transport stipends and light refreshments were provided with amounts set in consultation with community partners.

Findings

The engagement activities across the PPI forums, workshops, interviews, and case story discussions collectively revealed how deeply smoking, stigma, gender norms, and systemic constraints shape TB recovery in everyday life.

The smoking-tb link: a gap in awareness and urgency

One of the strongest learnings emerging from the community engagement was that smoking significantly slows recovery from TB. Patients, caregivers, and providers repeatedly described how smoking keeps cough persistent, weakens immunity, and prolongs treatment. The Resident Medical Officer explained that nicotine dependence creates both physical and psychological discomfort during quit attempts, leading to sleeplessness, irritability, bowel changes, nausea, and a sense of restlessness. Many patients did not initially recognise these as withdrawal symptoms and often interpreted them as part of TB itself. Because TB already causes coughing, many patients underestimated the additional harm of smoking and did not see an urgent reason to quit. This highlighted the importance of clear, culturally grounded messages that show how smoking and TB together damage the lungs and delay recovery.

Low awareness about the smoking and TB connection was widespread. Most families associated smoking primarily with cancer and did not understand how it interferes with TB medication, increases the risk of relapse, and weakens the immune system. Across all PPI sessions, participants asked for strong visuals and simple sequences that would help them understand what happens inside the lungs. BRAC staff and the Resident Medical Officer encouraged the use of side-by-side visuals of healthy lungs, TB affected lungs, and smoker’s lungs to make the message clearer. This feedback directly shaped the refined Bangla and English photocard.

The pervasive influence of stigma and gendered norms

Gender dynamics played a powerful and often painful role in treatment experiences. Women diagnosed with TB faced intense stigma, including being sent back to their parental homes and being advised to hide their diagnosis to protect marriage prospects. In several discussions, healthcare workers shared deeply tragic cases where young women, overwhelmed by stigma, isolation, and the long treatment process, had taken their own lives. For many families, TB was described as a generational curse. Girls in particular were seen as carrying the burden of social shame, whereas boys and young men tended to receive more support and were shielded from blame. The RMO and district staff noted that women’s advice was routinely dismissed in many households. Men ignored their wives’ reminders to quit smoking or to take medicines, but the same men responded more positively to daughters or close male peers. TLCAs described the lengths to which they must go to protect confidentiality for young women, knowing the consequences they might face at home and in their communities.

At the same time, men faced their own challenges. They were more likely to smoke heavily, more likely to neglect treatment, and less likely to seek help until symptoms became severe. Stigma operated differently for men, often protecting their social image but exposing them to health risks because families hesitated to intervene strongly.

Quitting smoking during TB treatment was described as extremely difficult. Patients spoke about cravings, depression, fatigue, stress, and moments of relapse. Stress from work, family obligations, and financial pressures triggered smoking for many. Long-term smokers often relied on cigarettes as their primary source of comfort or entertainment. As a result, participants asked for practical strategies they could use at home and during work hours, such as drinking water, taking brief walks, breathing exercises, gradual reduction for long-term smokers, and replacing smoking with healthier activities such as conversations with friends, prayer, light exercise, or listening to music.

Caregivers described feeling exhausted and emotionally drained. They struggled to manage household responsibilities while also supporting a patient through a long treatment. Many female caregivers felt that their advice went unheard because of gender norms. Despite their limited influence, caregivers played an essential role in treatment adherence and relapse prevention. This shaped the development of simple caregiver prompts aimed at supporting routines, keeping the home smoke free, and praising small successes.

Navigating health system constraints and building trust

Health system constraints shaped the context in which all counselling occurred. TLCAs and doctors worked in cramped rooms, with long patient queues, broken equipment, and limited privacy. Providers described challenges such as malfunctioning X ray machines, high caseloads, and irregular supervisory meetings. Yet there were also improvements, such as portable X-rays, GeneXpert access, free films, and modest support funded through external programmes. These realities emphasised the need for extremely brief scripts that providers can use during routine pill count interactions without creating judgement or shame.

Initial skepticism also emerged among some participants. Several patients questioned why the research team did not provide direct medical services. This reflects a common expectation in low-resource settings where patients assume that outside organisations are service providers. Through transparent communication, repeated visits, and respectful engagement, trust was gradually built. Over time, participants became active contributors, openly sharing experiences, shaping messages, and checking whether the team’s interpretations matched their realities.

Digital interventions also showed promise. A recovered patient from Narayanganj described how daily SMS reminders about medicines, family responsibilities, and simple coping steps helped him reduce smoking and build a disciplined routine. Messages that mentioned loved ones were especially motivating.

Taken together, these insights shaped refinements across all communication tools. The updated photocard uses clear visuals, simple Bangla text, and a logical cause-to-effect to action structure. Providers now have a brief conversation script that fits within routine clinics. Caregivers have easy suggestions tailored to their limited decision-making power. Patient strategies include coping steps and alternative activities for moments of craving. Member checks confirmed that these changes made the materials clearer, more respectful, and more relevant to people’s lived experiences.

Overall, our experience demonstrated that co-produced, story-based, and non-blaming communication tools can address entrenched challenges related to stigma, gender inequalities, low awareness, nicotine withdrawal, and clinic time constraints. By grounding messages in lived experience, the project helped create a more realistic and compassionate approach to smoking cessation within TB care. This strengthened the likelihood that brief advice will be understood, remembered, and acted upon in real health system conditions.

Co-designed communication outputs

We developed several brochures and flyers based on feedback from our PPI group members. We also produced a policy brief for policymakers. Trial versions of the brochures and policy brief developed through discussions with PPI participants are included in the appendix.

Challenges and limitations

During our CE and PPI meetings, we faced several challenges, including bringing community members and stakeholders together in one place. As the activities required a suitable meeting or conference room, arranging these sessions within government health facilities was difficult. Community engagement is still an emerging approach in Bangladesh’s health landscape, and government authorities were initially skeptical. However, once they participated in the first meeting, officials became more engaged, openly shared their experiences, and encouraged the continuation of this work. These challenges influenced participation consistency and required adaptive engagement strategies, but also contributed to building trust and improving the relevance of outputs over time.

Another challenge was the inability to provide direct monetary incentives to participants due to ethical and budgetary constraints. However, refreshments such as tea and light snacks were offered during meetings, in line with common community engagement practices, and transport costs were reimbursed for participants travelling longer distances. As meetings, initially planned for one hour, often extended beyond that time and were conducted across multiple days, maintaining consistent attendance remained challenging. Some participants expressed expectations for transport support or financial compensation. Evidence from comparable contexts suggests that such forms of support (particularly refreshments and transport reimbursement) are frequently considered standard components of participation rather than direct financial benefits, although distinctions between reimbursement and incentives can remain blurred. Molyneux et al., [20] At the same time, these approaches may raise ethical and practical challenges related to fairness, expectations, and participation. We explained the purpose and ethical boundaries of the project to participants; however, this limitation may have influenced participation and engagement. We recognise these constraints and recommend that future research and funding structures more explicitly account for fair and context-sensitive compensation of participants.

A further challenge involved sustaining participants’ understanding of the purpose of community engagement. Some TB-free participants reported that repeated visits to health facilities triggered distressing memories of their illness. As a result, a few participants withdrew, and we continued the work with the remaining group members.

In addition to these operational challenges, the findings reflect experiences from only three engagement sites and may not be representative of TB care settings across Bangladesh. Some highly marginalised groups were likely underrepresented due to mobility, work schedules, or social barriers. In particular, we were unable to include a female TB patient or survivor who smoked and could share a cessation journey. This reflects the very low prevalence of tobacco smoking among women in Bangladesh, where only 0.8% of women aged 15 years and above are current tobacco smokers (GATS Bangladesh, 2017), as well as the smoking cessation focus of the Quit4TB study. Although female caregivers and family members participated and contributed valuable perspectives, the absence of a female patient cessation narrative limited our ability to explore gender-specific smoking cessation experiences among women affected by TB.

Despite these challenges, a key strength of this approach was the sustained and iterative engagement, which enabled participants to meaningfully shape the development and refinement of communication materials.

Dissemination

From the beginning of the engagement process, we intended to share our outputs with a wide range of stakeholders. In our initial plan, we aimed to present the outcomes and demonstrate the communication tools (flyer and brochure) to the Mycobacterial Disease Control (MBDC) unit, a government body under the Directorate General of Health Services (DGHS) within the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (MoHFW). On 24 September 2025, a dissemination meeting was held with MBDC, which was attended by the Director, Deputy Director and all the Focal Managers of MBDC. There we shared our observations and findings. Government representatives provided their perspectives, and a recurring question was whether this type of engagement could be integrated into the national system. Our researchers explained how this simple and cost-effective approach could strengthen government efforts to prevent TB from becoming life-threatening, particularly in cases complicated by tobacco use.

As we continued engaging with stakeholders at different levels, we realised that sharing the findings exclusively with government authorities would not fully reflect the breadth of the work. Therefore, we plan to present the study findings and communication tools at the ARK Health Summit, scheduled for 9–10 December 2026. The summit will bring together researchers, policymakers, healthcare providers, civil society representatives, and community stakeholders, providing an opportunity to further disseminate and discuss the lessons emerging from this community engagement process.

In addition to these dissemination plans, work is ongoing to share key messages on social media. Our researchers are also preparing case stories to be published in newspapers and online platforms through blogs and op-eds.

Beyond these activities, the co-developed communication materials are intended to be actively used and integrated into ongoing programme and policy engagement efforts. These materials will be shared with frontline providers, including TLCAs, through sensitisation sessions and routine programme interactions to support patient counselling and tobacco cessation practices. In the next phase of the Quit4TB trial, particularly during policy advocacy and stakeholder engagement, these materials will be incorporated into dissemination packages to demonstrate practical, community-informed approaches to addressing the TB–tobacco nexus. This approach aims to ensure that the outputs of community engagement are not only disseminated but also embedded within service delivery and future scale-up efforts across TB programmes.

Conclusion

What began as a conventional research study evolved into a co-creative process, with community members becoming key knowledge partners. Our experience demonstrates that even in resource-constrained settings, sustained and respectful engagement can successfully embed community voices into all stages of research, from design to delivery. This participatory approach is not only an ethical ideal but a pragmatic strategy, ultimately yielding public health solutions that are more trusted, relevant, and impactful.
