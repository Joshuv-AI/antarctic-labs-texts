Micellar catalysis: Polymer bound palladium catalyst for carbon-carbon coupling reactions in water
Authors: Mani Sengoden, Gulzar A. Bhat, Ryan J. Rutledge, Showkat Rashid, Aijaz A. Dar, Donald J. Darensbourg
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SignificanceMicellar catalysts are a class of catalysts that can be used in organic transformations that can be performed in water. The use of micelles in catalysis has gained importance in recent years due to their unique properties such as increased surface area, solubilization of reactants, and the ability to interact with various substrates. To avoid the use of volatile and sometimes toxic organic solvents as media for organic reactions, micellar systems represent much greener alternatives and have been utilized in the past in various carbon–carbon coupling processes.

Due to the attraction of utilizing water as a solvent in organic synthesis, catalysts that can be employed in aqueous media are becoming increasingly important (1). This issue remains a major principle of Green Chemistry of removing detrimental organic solvents from commercially viable processes (2). Pertinent to this concern, over the last decade significant advances have been made in the synthesis of degradable aliphatic polycarbonates from carbon dioxide and a wide variety of epoxide monomers (3–13). The intentional engagements of rapid and reversible chain-transfer reactions which occur during these polymerization processes (aka immortal polymerization), thereby affording macropolyols, have greatly expanded the potential applicability for these polymeric materials (14–19). We have recently developed the strategy of implementing dicarboxylic acid chain-transfer agents (CTAs) which contain a bidentate metal binding site to prepare amphiphilic CO2-derived polycarbonates. In this manner, a single metal complex (catalyst or catalyst precursor) can be covalently incorporated into the polymer’s main chain (20). Importantly, in most instances, the metal center can be installed in the copolymer during the synthesis or in a postpolymerization process (21). Scheme 1 illustrates the sequential copolymerization of propylene oxide (PO)/CO2 and allyl glycidyl ether (AGE)/CO2 employing a metal bound, CTA, where the AGE portion of the polycarbonate can be readily made water miscible following thiol-ene click chemistry and deprotonation. Interestingly, the rhenium complex shown embedded in the hydrophobic section of the micelle depicted in Scheme 1 has been found to be an effective visible light– driven catalyst for the reduction of CO2 to carbon monoxide in an aqueous medium (22).

Sequential copolymerization of PO/CO2 and AGE/CO2 in presence of metal bound CTA to form micelles.

In the past, micellar catalysis has been investigated for carbon–carbon bond-forming reactions catalyzed by metal complexes. For example, pioneering research by Lipshutz and coworkers has utilized various amphiphiles such as polyethanyl-α-tocopheryl sebacate or DL-α-tocopherol methoxy polyethylene glycol succinate, along with palladium complexes to examine metal-catalyzed cross-coupling reactions in water (23). These surfactants are made up of a lipophilic α-tocopherol moiety and a hydrophilic poly(ethylene giycol) (PEG) chain connected by way of a succinic acid linker. Other micellar catalysts for organic transformation in water include a N-heterocyclic carbene metallosurfactant as shown below in Fig. 1 (24).

Structure of N-heterocyclic carbene based metallosurfactant palladium catalyst.

Similarly, a series of metal catalysts derived from poly(ethylene glycol) grafted phenantroline and bipyridine ligands have been employed in organic reactions carried out in aqueous media (Fig. 2) (25). For a recent review article of metallosurfactants in catalysis, see Scarso and coworkers (26). In furtherance of these types of studies, herein we address utilizing well-defined amphiphilic polycarbonate derived metallomicelle catalysts for examining carbon–carbon coupling reactions in aqueous solution.

In order to better understand the micellar characteristics of this amphiphilic triblock terpolymer, the critical micelle concentration (CMC) was obtained by different methods. Fig. 7 shows the variation of the physical properties: surface tension, conductance, and pyrene I1/I3 values with the total amphiphilic triblock terpolymer concentration. The concentration corresponding to the break in the surface tension and conductance curves represents the CMC values of the amphiphile (Fig. 7A). In the case of fluorescence method, there is a sharp decrease in the I1/I3 ratio which is due to the progressive solubilization of pyrene into aggregates formed by the surfactant molecules (Fig. 7A). The midpoint of the curve showing a decrease in I1/I3 values marks the CMC of the amphiphile. The CMC values obtained from Fig. 7A are shown by the arrows and were found to be slightly different. It is well established that the CMC values of amphiphilic molecules are technique dependent as observed in our case (30). The average CMC obtained from all three methods is 0.0023 mM (2.3 μM). The ratio of the slope in the postmicellar region to that in the premicellar region in the conductance vs. surfactant concentration plot is a measure of degree of counter-ion dissociation, α, which is related to degree of counter-ion binding parameter β as β = 1−α. The amphiphilic block copolymer shows significantly high β of 0.93 indicating that about 93% of the counterions (here ammonium ions) are bound to the micellar surface.

Variation of (A) surface tension, conductance, and I1/I3 values; (B) hydrodynamic diameter, and (C) zeta potential of amphiphilic block copolymer with its concentration at 25 °C. (D) shows variation of viscosity of the aqueous solution of the polymer with the shear rate in the pre- and postmicellar concentrations of the amphiphile at 25 °C.

The amphiphilic character of the triblock terpolymer can be attributed to the presence of a large number of pendant hydrophilic carboxylate anions attached to the AGE/CO2 units and hydrophobic PO/CO2 segment, with Pd-CTA embedded in the hydrophobic segment. On increasing the concentration of the polymer in water, the macromolecules are expected to get adsorbed at the air–water interface with the AGE/CO2 units containing pendant COO– groups pointing towards water and the hydrophobic groups towards air (Scheme 3). This is evident by the significant decrease in surface tension of water up to the CMC of the amphphillic triblock copolymer. Thereafter, to minimize the free energy, the amphiphiles self-assemble into micelles. The self-assembly of such block copolymers in solution into nanostructures is most commonly driven by the hydrophobic effect, wherein the selective solvation of hydrophilic polymer block in water drives the polymers to arrange such that the water-soluble blocks are solvated in the aqueous phase, and the hydrophobic blocks are protected in the core of the nanostructure. We observed that before the actual CMC of the polymer, small premicellar aggregates are formed as evidenced from changes in the hydrodynamic radius from 20 ± 2 nm to 70 ± 3 nm (Fig. 7B) on going from low concentration to the CMC value (0.2 μM to 2.3 μM). After reaching the CMC value, the hydrodynamic radius remained approximately constant at 70 ± 3 nm up to about 10 times the CMC of the amphiphilic polymer. These results are evidence that the self-assembly process of these amphiphilic polymers is a gradual process (Scheme 3) and starts in a very low concentration region (much below the macroscopically determined CMC). The results are in conformity with the reports of other block copolymers (31). The zeta potential values are close to zero before CMC and attains a value or around –54.07 ± 2.70 mV above CMC as evident from Fig. 7C, advocating that the premicellar aggregates are in metastable state with no well-defined/permanent structures.

Schematic representation of adsorption of the polymer on the air-water interface and formation of pre-micellar and post-micellar aggregates in the bulk aqueous solution.

The Fig. 7D shows the variation of viscosity of the polymer solution with the shear rate at amphiphile concentrations spanning, from premicellar to postmicellar concentrations. Below CMC value, the viscosity of the polymer solutions is high at low shear rate which decreases with an increase in the shear rate, demonstrating a typical non-Newtonian behavior during which fluid flows in the direction of a high shear rate. However above CMC, viscosity remains almost constant at low shear rates demonstrating Newtonian behavior. Usually, such Newtonian behavior is characteristics of polymer chains which get aligned in the direction of applied shear and show a significant drop in the viscosity of the solution. In the pre-CMC region, the polymer molecules or premicellar aggregates are loose enough to get aligned in the direction of shear rate and decrease the viscosity. Above the CMC, thermodynamically stable spherical micelles are formed that have much lower viscosity due to low intermicellar interactions (micelles avoid interactions due to negative charge on their surfaces) compared to the random or open coil polymer structures in the pre-CMC region having significant intermolecular interactions. The variation of zero shear viscosity shown as an Inset to the Fig. 7 demonstrates significant decrease in the viscosity above CMC indicating the formation of stable spherical micellar structures. These micelles were found to be stable up to 100 °C as confirmed by dynamic light scattering (DLS) at different temperatures (SI Appendix, Fig. S26).

To further confirm the morphology of these negatively charged micelles, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) of the polymer solutions was recorded. The TEM analysis confirmed the spherical morphology of these negatively charged micelles with uniform size distribution ranging from 90 to 50 nm (Fig. 8). The size of the micelles obtained via DLS and TEM are quite comparable and the presence of Pd in these nanomicelles was also confirmed by performing TEM-energy Dispersive X-ray analysis (EDX) (SI Appendix, Fig. S52).

Suzuki Cross-Coupling Catalysis in Water.

As stipulated earlier, a significant aim of this study was to examine the use of these amphiphilic aliphatic polycarbonates derived from CO2 in metal-catalyzed transformations in aqueous solutions. In this regard, these Pd(II) containing polymers were investigated for catalyzing Suzuki cross-coupling reactions in water. Each spherical micelle has well-defined Pd(II) centers in the hydrophobic core and thus can act as a nanoreactor for catalyzing different C–C coupling reactions in water. The Suzuki coupling was carried out using a series of reactions of aryl halide with aryl boronic acid (Table 2). First, we carried out C–C coupling using iodobenzene and phenylboronic acid as substrates, in the presence of Pd-micellar solution and three equivalents of Cs2CO3 at 80 °C. Under these conditions, the iodide precursor gave 99% conversion after 24 h (Table 2, entry 1). In order to test the substrate scope, a series of halogenated benzene and aryl boronic acids with electron-donating and withdrawing groups were explored under similar conditions, affording the corresponding substituted biphenyl in high yields. For example, the aryl halide bearing methoxy and formyl substituents reacted to give corresponding biphenyls in 83 to 95% yield (Table 2, entries 2-4). Similarly, boronic acids having methyl, methoxy, and chloro-substituent on the aryl ring successfully lead to the coupling products with iodobenzene to yield biphenyls in 88 to 92% yield (Table 1, entries 5-7). We have also tested coupling reactions between coupling partners having both electron-donating and electron-withdrawing group and which successfully result in the formation of biphenyls in 85 to 91% yield (Table 2, entries 8-10). All the coupling products were isolated, and their purity was confirmed by NMR spectroscopy (SI Appendix, Figs. S27–S51 and Table S1). Besides achieving the C–C coupling reactions in water by using these micelles, the catalyst loadings employed is very low compared to other Pd-catalysts (32–34) and other Pd-catalysed carbon–carbon coupling reactions in water (35, 36). We have used 0.1 mol% (based on the Pd content) of these micellar solutions for catalyzing these cross-coupling reactions. The catalytic activity of these polymer micelles was further compared with the analogue palladium complexes Pd-1 (on water) (Scheme 4). That is under similar conditions, insoluble catalyst Pd-1 (0.4 mol%) gave 23% conversion after 24 h, whereas a micellar catalyst loading of 0.1 mol% was required to achieve a 99% conversion and the process was recyclable. That is, recycling of the polymer solution in reaction 1 of Table 2 provided a 40% conversion, with an additional recycling leading to only a 16% conversion. Concomitantly, the hydrodynamic radius of the micelles decreased significantly with each cycle, see SI Appendix, Fig. S53 showing recycling experiments.

Heck Reaction and Hydroxycarbonylation of Aryl Halides.

The Suzuki coupling reaction catalyzed by these nanoreactors (micelles) successfully lead to the desired products with diverse aryl halides and phenylboronic acids as reaction partners. To further extend the utility of this micellar catalysis other carbon–carbon coupling reactions such as the Heck reaction and hydroxycarbonylation of aryl halides were also evaluated. The Heck reaction was performed by reacting aryl iodides with ethyl acrylate, using the micellar solution of these triblock polycarbonates (Scheme 5). Under these conditions, 88 to 91% yield was obtained after 24 h at 100 °C, confirming the effectiveness of this micellar catalytic system for achieving this transformation in aqueous media. Base optimization of this process revealed that triethyl amine in the presence of PPh3 provided the best results (SI Appendix, Table S2). The hydroxycarbonylation of aryl halides for preparing aromatic carboxylic acids has also been successfully catalyzed by these micelles in water with yields up to 95% (Scheme 6).

Conclusion

The chemical community’s dedication to sustainable chemical practices embraces the need to replace volatile and toxic organic solvents in metal-catalyzed organic transformations. Indeed, in the fifth and ninth entries of the twelve principles of green chemistry, solvents should be avoided when possible or innocuous when used and catalytic reagents are superior to stoichiometric reagents. In this report, we have provided the synthesis and full characterization of Pd(II) containing complexes as part of the hydrophobic polymer backbone of amphiphilic aliphatic polycarbonates derived from CO2 and epoxides. These polymeric materials were demonstrated to provide uniform spherical nanostructures when added to water. Furthermore, these metallosurfactants (micelles) were found to be stable in an aqueous medium up to at least 100 °C. Reduction of the Pd(II) metal centers in these micelles by triphenylphosphine yielded very effective catalysts for Suzuki and other carbon–carbon coupling reactions in water at very low catalyst loadings (0.10 mol %). While the true nature of the catalytic metal species may not be defined in this study, it is apparent that these micellar catalysts, where the metal center is embedded in the hydrophobic cavity of these nanoreactors, are much more effective than their counterparts in the absence of polymer morphologies. Although, there are advantages in utilizing other micellar catalysts reported for carbon–carbon bond forming reactions, such as those occurring at ambient temperature and employing commercial surfactants (23, 37), the catalyst system designed herein offers unambiguous location of all of the active metal centers, allowing for extremely low catalyst loading. Furthermore, our approach provides for a general modular route for synthesizing a broad range of biodegradable micellar metal catalysts from renewable resources (38) for various organic transformations that can be carried out in an aqueous medium. Nevertheless, the design of improved and biodegradable surfactants for catalysis in water remains a challenging and important subject for study (39).

Materials and Methods

General Considerations.

Methods as reported in our recently published paper were also followed during this work (40). All reactions were performed under a nitrogen or argon atmosphere using standard Schlenk line and glovebox techniques. Glassware and stainless-steel reactors were dried at 150 °C for 24 h prior to use. Solvents were purchased from commercial sources and dried using MBraun Manual Solvent purification system packed with Alcoa F200 activated alumina desiccant. Starting materials such as epoxides, bases, aryl halides, boronic acids, and esters were purchased from commercial sources, Precatalyst (R,R)-N,N′-Bis(3,5-di-tertbutylsalicylidene)-1,2-cyclohexanediamino-cobalt(II) was purchase from Sigma Aldrich, whereas cocatalyst bis(triphenylphosphine)iminium chloride was purchased from Acros. Bone-dry CO2 was supplied from a high-pressure cylinder and equipped with a liquid dip tube purchased from Scott Specialty Gases. NMR spectra were recorded on a 400 MHz Bruker spectrometer with CDCl3 as an internal standard at 7.26 ppm. A Malvern modular GPC apparatus with ViscoGEL I-series columns (H&L) and THF eluent was used. Mw and Mn were calculated using data from refractive index (RI), right-angle light scattering, and low-angle light scattering detectors calibrated against polystyrene standards. TGA analyses were performed in Mettler-Toledo TGA/DSC 1 analyzer. DSC measurements were performed on a TA Instruments DSC 2500. Temperature and heat flow were calibrated by an indium standard. Ramp 10.00 °C/min to –20.00 °C; Isothermal for 1.0 min; Ramp 10.00 °C/min to 180.00 °C; Isothermal for 1.0 min; Ramp 10.00 °C/min to –20.00 °C; Isothermal for 1.0 min; Ramp 10.00 °C/min to 180.00 °C; Isothermal for 1.0 min; Ramp 10.00 °C/min to –20.00 °C (three cycles). The Tg was taken as the midpoint of the inflection tangent, upon the second cycle. In case of TGA, the polymeric sample were heated on a TGA Q500 thermogravimetric analyzer from room temperature to 500 °C at a rate of 5 °C min–1 under N2 flow of 20 mL min–1.

Synthesis of Chain Transfer Agent.

The chain transfer agent [dc-bpy PdCl2] was synthesized by a reported procedure. According to this procedure in a glovebox, the dry bis(acetonitrile)dichloropalladium(II) (0.766 mmol, 199.0 mg), dc-bpy (0.766 mmol, 187.2 mg), and dry dimethylformamide (DMF) (30 mL) were added to a Schlenk flask. The reaction was purged with nitrogen and then refluxed for 6 h. Yellow-orange ppt was filtered off and rinsed with DMF and then extensively washed with methanol (in order to remove all DMF). The yellow product was then dried overnight in vacuum oven at 60 °C and quickly transferred into the glove box. The purity of this metallochain transfer agents was confirmed by different spectroscopic techniques.

Procedure for chain transfer optimization.

In a glovebox, the catalyst, cocatalyst, monomer, chain transfer agent (X), and 0.7 mL solvent (dichloromethane:Toluene, 1:1) were added to a 10-mL stainless steel reactor vessel. All reactions were performed on the scale of 4 mg catalyst, using ratios of 1/1/1,000/X for species, respectively. CO2 was added to the vessel pressurizing to 25 bar, respectively. Following the indicated time, the vessel was depressurized, a crude product was taken, and polymer obtained was recrystallized. Initial recrystallization was performed by adding crude product dropwise to acidic methanol (4 drops of conc. HCl/50 mL methanol), followed by two recrystallizations in neutral methanol. The polymer product was dried for 24 h under vacuum. 1H NMR (400 MHz, CDCl3) δ = 5.20 [m, 1H, (CH2)CHO], 4.21 [m, 2H, O(CH2)CH], 1.35 ppm [d, 3H, HC(CH3)].

CMC Determination.

Tensiometry.

The CMC of the surfactants was determined using surface tension measurements (γ), using a Kruss 9 tensiometer with platinum ring detachment procedure. The surfactant concentration was varied by adding concentrated surfactant solution in small increments using a Hamilton microsyringe, and readings were taken after thorough mixing and temperature equilibration. The temperature was maintained at the desired value with an accuracy of within 0.1 °C by circulating water from a thermostat. The accuracy of the surface tension measurements was within 0.1 dyne cm–1.

Fluorescence measurement.

Fluorescence measurements were conducted by using Agilent Cary Eclipse Fluorescence spectrophotometer utilizing Pyrene as a probe with the excitation wavelength of 334 nm and slit widths of 5:5. CMC was determined by measuring the I1/I3 ratio of pyrene vibrionic peaks at 373 and 383 nm at different polymer concentrations ranging from below to above CMC.

Conductivity measurement.

Conductivity of the solutions were studied using a Mettler Toledo Seven Multi conductivity meter having uncertainty 1.0 μSm−1. The study was carried out in a thermostated water bath at 298.15 K with uncertainty ±0.01 K. Milli-Q water was used with specific conductivity of 14.0 μS m−1. The conductivity cell was calibrated using 0.01 M aqueous KCl solution.

Dynamic light scattering and zeta potential measurements.

The average particle diameter and zeta potential (ζ-potential) measurements were done with dynamic light scattering instrument (Litesizer 500, Anton Paar, Austria) fitted with peltier (0 to 90 °C) for which quartz cuvettes were used. Prior to dynamic light scattering measurements, samples were filtered using 0.22-μm filter. Each measurement was repeated thrice for better reproducibility of results.

Rheology.

The rheological behavior of the surfactant solution was investigated using an Anton Paar MCR-102 Rheometer equipped with a peltier system for precise temperature control with an accuracy of ±0.01 °C. The measurements were performed using a cone plate geometry (50 mm, d = 0.101 mm). To study the flow properties of the polymer solution, steady shear tests with the shear rate 0.001 to 1,000 s–1 at a constant frequency of 5 rad/s was performed at 25 °C. Before conducting the experiments, the samples were carefully loaded on the surface of the lower plate, left as such for 2 min to equilibrate the temperature and stress conditions. The optimum gap distance was set at 0.101 mm so as to have the proper grip and avoid slipping out of samples.
