Divergent ecological adaptation in allopatry leads to behavioral isolation through female resistance
Authors: Varpu Pärssinen, Kaj Hulthén, R. Brian Langerhans, P. Anders Nilsson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNatural selection can be a potent contributor to speciation, but how exactly this occurs remains unclear. Studying behavioral isolation and the divergence of mating behaviors among multiple isolated populations can help uncover the importance of divergent adaptation in the evolution of reproductive isolation. Here, we utilize common-garden reared virgin Bahamas mosquitofish (Gambusia hubbsi) derived from wild populations adapted to either high- or low-predation risk environments.ResultsWhile we know that parallel evolution of female mate choice has contributed to behavioral isolation between contrasting predation regimes in this radiation, we here discovered that male mating behaviors expressed during one-on-one encounters have also consistently diverged between predation regimes, but have not consequently increased behavioral isolation. On the other hand, we found that female resistance toward a potential mate may represent an underappreciated component of behavioral isolation. Female-to-male aggression during one-on-one interactions was stronger when interacting with foreign males compared to native males, especially males originating from the different predation regime that also elicited female aggression more quickly. Female aggression tended to lead to lower fertilization success, with the lowest fertilization success occurring for mating pairs from different predation regimes. Males displayed their genitalia more toward native females, but performed more copulation attempts toward foreign females—yet, copulation attempts were least likely to lead to genital contact or fertilization when attempting to mate with females from a different predation regime.ConclusionsOverall, behavioral isolation between divergent ecological environments in Bahamas mosquitofish appears to have arisen largely from the actions of one sex. Multiple female mating behaviors contribute to behavioral isolation, while males primarily showed behaviors that seem to attempt to enhance their mating success in the presence of resistant females. Thus, in coercive mating scenarios with indiscriminate males, ecological divergence in traits linked to female mate discrimination can still lead to parallel speciation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12862-026-02504-0.

Introduction

Natural selection is considered an integral part of the origin of new species, but there is less consensus around the details of this process [1–4]. Reproductive isolation can evolve as a by-product of disparate local adaptations that arise when populations face divergent selection, a process generally referred to as ecological speciation [4–6]. Alternatively, speciation can also arise from evolutionary responses to similar selection pressures [3], such as in mutation-order speciation [4]. While the role of ecological differentiation as a driver of speciation is evident in some, especially geographically overlapping, populations [7–9], its relative importance in speciation still remains elusive [10–13]. For example, in a recent analysis spanning birds, mammals and amphibians, Anderson and Weir [2] argue that most allopatric speciation events are characterized by adaptation to similar selective pressures. More studies in natural systems, especially in allopatric populations, are thus needed to determine how commonly reproductive isolation evolves as a result of divergent or similar selection. Notably, studies have more commonly focused on the divergence of ecologically relevant physical traits [2, 14], while the role of divergent behavioral traits needs further evaluation.

Behavioral isolation, where mismatches in mating signals or preferences lead to decreased likelihood of mating between populations, often constitutes one of the most important barriers to gene flow in closely related species [15–17]. Behavioral isolation may evolve as a result of both divergent and similar ecological selection, but the relative importance of these processes remains unknown [3]. For example, ecological context can determine the fitness consequences of different mating behaviors [18, 19], so divergent selection may lead to radiation of reproductive behaviors, and, consequently, mismatches in mating interactions between populations. Similarly, divergence in ecology-dependent traits that are also important in mate choice (so-called ‘magic traits’) can result in behavioral isolation [20–23]. On the other hand, under similar selection, different alternative traits can become preferred in mate choice through processes like Fisher’s runaway or sexual conflict [3, 24, 25]. In this case, the evolution of discrimination against non-native mates would be independent of their ecological origin [25, 26]. We can thus expect divergent selection to facilitate speciation to a greater extent than similar selection if behavioral isolation accumulates more rapidly between populations in divergent ecological regimes than in similar ones, irrespective of genetic relatedness. Such a pattern could arise if differences in phenotypes and reproductive behaviors consistently diverge more rapidly between divergent than similar ecological regimes [27, 28]. This prediction should ideally be tested on allopatric systems with replicated populations occurring in similar and divergent ecological settings [3, 29–31]. Allopatry is important for ensuring that behavioral isolation would have emerged as a by-product of divergent ecological adaptation, rather than through reinforcement, where selection directly favors assortative mating owing to the fitness costs of inter-population mating, such as the production of low-fitness hybrids [30, 32–34].

Estimates of mating preferences can be drawn from either “choice” (e.g. 2-choice designs) or “no-choice” paradigms [35, 36]. Although most tests have focused on discrimination in choice designs, absolute preferences manifested in no-choice scenarios provide an important and ecologically relevant measure of the intensity of sexual response preferences [35, 37]. Here we use no-choice trials to measure sexual behaviors as estimates of mate recognition and preference, as well as mating persistence and resistance, both within and among populations. In this way, we can identify potential mismatches in mating behaviors between populations, which could result from population divergence in ecologically-associated phenotypes, sexual signals, coercion, preferences, and resistance [38–42].

Here, we take advantage of the post-Pleistocene radiation of Bahamas mosquitofish (Gambusia hubbsi) inhabiting isolated blue holes to test how adaptation to divergent ecological environments has shaped mating behaviors and behavioral isolation between populations. Bahamas mosquitofish are small, live-bearing fish (Family Poeciliidae) occurring in distinct, isolated populations in the blue holes of Andros Island, The Bahamas (Figure S1). These populations represent largely independent evolutionary lineages replicated across two distinct ecological environments, either a high-predation regime (piscivorous fish present) or a low-predation regime (piscivorous fish absent). A broad suite of adaptive traits in these populations show predictable divergence between predation regimes [43, 44] and many of these traits, such as body shape, dorsal-fin coloration and gonopodium size, are also intimately linked to mate choice [44–47]. For example, females seem to prefer body shapes similar to their own regime [44, 48]. While previous work has revealed how female choice has led to parallel evolution of behavioral isolation between predation regimes in this radiation, the possible roles of male mating behaviors [49–53] and one-on-one interactions is still largely unknown. In addition to cooperative mating, Bahamas mosquitofish males often attempt forceful, or sneaky, mating attempts with unreceptive females, especially in high-predation environments, and females commonly exhibit aggression toward undesirable mates [54]. Moreover, males of many poeciliid species express preferences for female traits such as body size, so male behavior may also contribute to behavioral isolation [55]. Owing to the highly complex and rapid maneuvers required for successful copulation in mosquitofishes [56], even subtle avoidance by females could also play an important role in determining mating success.

In this study, we aim to quantify how adaptation to contrasting predation regimes has shaped the mating behaviors of female and male Bahamas mosquitofish, and test whether behavioral isolation across populations has evolved more rapidly between divergent ecological environments than similar ones. We applied a common-garden approach to reduce the likelihood that observed differences in mating behaviors reflect environmental effects, such as density- or predator-induced differences, copying preferences of more experienced individuals, or prior mating experience [57, 58]. Each individual fish was introduced to a potential mate originating from either (i) its own native population, (ii) a foreign population with the same predation regime, or (iii) a foreign population with the opposite predation regime. First, we used within-population trials to broadly quantify the mating behavioral syndrome in this species. If ecological differences have driven the divergent evolution of mating behavior [54], we expected that both female and male mating behavior would consistently differ between predation regimes in a common-garden environment. Second, we examined whether males and females exhibited different mating behaviors depending on the origin and the ecological phenotype of their prospective mate. If the populations followed the model of early speciation by divergent ecological selection, we expected focal fish to show the strongest preference/least resistance when interacting with native mates, weakest preference/strongest resistance with foreign mates from the opposite predation regime, and intermediate preference/resistance with foreign mates from the same predation regime. Alternatively, if behavioral isolation has evolved between the populations at the same rate regardless of divergent or similar selection pressures, we expected fish to show a preference for their native population and reject individuals equally from all foreign populations. Third, we examined if observed differences in mating behaviors could be explained by differences in body shape or the behavior of the other sex. Specifically, if individual mating behavior was largely based on visual assessment of the body shape of the prospective mate, we would expect that the morphological distance between the individuals would largely explain the behavioral differences between mates of different origin. If the fish instead adjusted their behavior in response to the behavior of the opposite sex, we would expect key male and female behaviors to correlate with each other. Finally, we monitored a subset of females used in the mating trials to directly assess if specific mating behaviors expressed during the beginning of the mating encounter predicted the probability of fertilization, with the overall probability of fertilization predicted to be lowest for pairs derived from different predation regimes if divergent ecological selection plays a paramount role in the evolution of behavioral isolation.

Material and methods

Study system

Bahamas mosquitofish colonized numerous inland blue holes on Andros Island, The Bahamas, following the last interglacial period ~15,000 years ago [59, 60]. Inland blue holes studied here comprise vertical water-filled caves that have an oxic freshwater lens on top of a stable anoxic seawater water column [61, 62]. High-predation blue holes are characterized by the presence of the piscivorous bigmouth sleeper (Gobiomorus dormitor), which has led to higher mortality rates and hence lower densities of mosquitofish populations, whereas low-predation blue holes are characterized by the lack of major predators and, consequently, higher mosquitofish densities [54, 63]. All blue holes in this study contain two fish species, as the low-predation blue holes have either the sheepshead minnow (Cyprinodon variegatus) or the crested goby (Lophogobius cyprinoides) in addition to Bahamas mosquitofish. The sheepshead minnow and the crested goby are small fish species that have never been shown to predate on mosquitofish [43, 44, 54], but may potentially act as competitors to mosquitofish (mainly via interference competition). However, the fish community of each blue hole is always dominated by mosquitofish. These blue holes are typically surrounded by pine forest, and lack any inlets or outlets except for underground tunnels that are filled with anoxic groundwater, suggesting strong geographical isolation of most blue holes for resident fish. Indeed, strong genetic isolation for fishes in these blue holes is evident, and genetic relatedness among populations of Bahamas mosquitofish does not correlate with the presence or absence of predatory fish [44, 60, 64–66]. Thus, blue-hole populations represent independent replicates of similar evolutionary events. However, some blue holes are geographically proximate (e.g. ~300 m apart, Figure S1), and widespread, seasonally dynamic freshwater marshes in between blue holes provide some form of occasional dispersal opportunities for Bahamas mosquitofish. Previous work suggests that low levels of movement may occur among some blue holes, and population pairs with greater mating opportunities differ more strongly in male and female genital morphology [67]. The relatively simple fish communities, similar environmental conditions, and evolutionary independence of blue holes make them ideal “natural laboratories” for studying evolutionary processes [43, 44, 61, 68].

Common-garden experiments

Wild Bahamas mosquitofish were collected from eight inland blue holes that differ in predation regime (4 low-predation, 4 high-predation; see Table 1, Figure S1 and [61] for details on the source populations) in 2016 and transported to experimental facilities at North Carolina State University. The wild-caught fish that formed the parental generation (F0) were collected as newborns to minimize any potential environmental effects and reared to adulthood in common-garden conditions (see e.g. [69] for collection and laboratory rearing details). When sexually mature, these lab-raised, wild-caught fish were used to establish F1 fish for this study by uniquely mating each female with a single male from the same population, with no fish used more than once (on average ~22 individual males and females used from each population of origin, range: 12–34, see [69] for more details). F1 fish were raised in the same common-garden conditions as F0 fish (families of similar age pooled within populations, 161 full-sibling F1 families, ~9 tanks per population). F1 fish from each population were reared in several separate aquaria without interacting with fish outside of their own population. F1 males were separated from females with a transparent mesh divider at first identifiable sign of maturation (gonopodium development). Thus, all fish used in the trials had no direct contact with the opposite sex and were presumed virgin in order to standardize current mating status and maintain high levels of motivation for mating. Mating trials and photography

To test for differences in native mating behaviors between predation regimes, we conducted 367 within-population no-choice mating trials using F1 fish originating from eight different populations (Table 1). To examine mating interactions between foreign individuals and test for behavioral isolation between populations, we performed 196 inter-population no-choice trials with F1 fish – these excluded two populations (one high-predation and one low-predation) to keep a high number of mating trial combinations manageable (Table 1). This resulted in 30 different population combinations for the inter-population no-choice trials: 12 foreign with the same predation regime (84 trials) and 18 foreign from different predation regimes (112 trials) (Table 1). Each individual was only used in a single trial, and males and females from the same tank or family were never combined in any trials. In analyses that included both inter-population trials and within-population trials, only the six focal populations were used (total of 482 trials). For analyses of male behavior, we excluded five trials where the female injured the male during the trial, as this likely affected the mating motivation of the male.

Prior to mating trials, fish were transferred and individually housed overnight in 7-L tanks (28 × 17 × 16 cm), which were covered on three sides with an opaque white plastic film to prevent visual interactions with adjacent tanks. Tanks were filled with water from their home recirculating system to a depth of 13 cm and aerated with an air-stone. Tanks containing focal male and female fish were placed side-by-side in a temperature-controlled room (25°C), and each trial started when a focal male was gently captured with a dipnet and transferred to the arena holding the focal female. Trials were video recorded (Canon Vixia HF R800) from a lateral view for 20 minutes, and we measured focal behaviors from the videos (see below). Most fish were 300–500 days old at the time of the trials. Ages should not have differed meaningfully between fish within trials or between predation regimes because we paired fish for trials using the same age classes each time, with both high-predation and low-predation combinations occurring simultaneously.

To measure body morphology, we photographed each fish from lateral view [44, 70]. To restrict movement of fish during photography, we placed each individual in a small, transparent container with a 50-mm ruler (1-mm resolution) for scale and photographed them from a lateral view using a DSLR camera (Canon T3i; Canon Inc., Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a macro lens (Sigma 50 mm f/2.8 EX DG Macro; Sigma Corp., Ronkonkoma, New York). Depending on the size of the fish, we used two differently sized containers (small: 30 mm long × 6 mm wide × 40 mm high; large: 41 mm long × 7 mm wide × 45 mm high) to optimize restriction of movement. Each container had small plastic pegs on the bottom to keep fish from resting flat on the bottom and obscuring morphological landmarks.

Fertilization success

To test for effects of mating behaviors on fertilization success in the inter-population no-choice mating trials, we allowed these mating pairs to interact for an additional ~22 hours after the video recording and monitored isolated females afterward for offspring production. We chose this timeframe to permit enough time to facilitate any possible chances of mating over a relatively lengthy period of time. Each tank also received an artificial plant for this period, providing hiding places for either sex to escape harassment or aggression. Fish from within-population no-choice mating trials were euthanized with quinaldine shortly after the recording as a part of another experiment, so offspring production could not be monitored for them. After being separated from the male, each female was moved into their own individual tank (22 × 10 × 14 cm) connected to a common re-circulating water system. Each tank had floating plastic vegetation, as well as one anchored piece of vegetation, providing refuge against potential cannibalism for any newborn offspring. We monitored females daily for 110 days, and recorded any offspring born during this period. After the monitoring period, females were sacrificed by placing them into water mixed with quinaldine, resulting in a total loss of consciousness in 2–3 minutes, after which they were preserved in 95% ethanol. Females that did not deliver offspring during the monitoring period were x-rayed to observe whether they contained fertilized oocytes using a custom-built digital X-ray unit comprising a micro-focus X-ray source (Hamamatsu L6731-01, Hamamatsu Corporation, Bridgewater, NJ, USA) and a digital X-ray detector (PaxScan 2520E, Kodex Inc., Nutley, NJ, USA) housed in a lead-shielded cabinet (e.g. [71–74]). Fertilization was recorded as successful if the female gave birth or had fertilized oocytes at the completion of the monitoring period. We acknowledge that fertilization success can involve additional isolating barriers (e.g. mechanical and gametic isolation), and indeed separate studies are explicitly examining the role of ecology in fertilization success in this system using an expanded experiment. Here we focus exclusively on the potential effects of mating behaviors on fertilization success, and the possible dependence of these relationships on the ecological combination of the mating pair (i.e. foreign fish that have evolved either under the same or different predation regime).

Data treatment and statistical analysis

We manually recorded male and female behaviors (Table 2) from the video files using the Behavioral Observation Research Interactive Software BORIS [75]. One person conducted all behavioral recordings, using a mixed order to reduce bias in the scoring. We selected to record a range of identifiable behaviors with suspected or known importance for mating success (Table 2). Male mosquitofish may court females by displaying their colorful dorsal fin or gonopodium, but male coercion is also common [54]. Females grow larger than males and frequently either chase males away or avoid their harassment by fleeing, but they may also express mating preferences by approaching the male or wiggling their body [54, 68]. For females, aggression and avoidance should reflect female mating resistance/rejection, as females can chase away, and even injure, unwanted males. Alternatively, female approaches and wiggles may reflect positive responses toward potential mates, although the function of these behaviors during these interactions is not yet clear. For males, all recorded behaviors reflect pro-mating behaviors, capturing different components or tactics of achieving mating success. For instance, males display their body, colored dorsal fin, and gonopodium to females, which are known to exhibit mating preferences for these traits (e.g. [45, 46, 48]). Males can also coax the female into a more favorable mating position, and must closely approach the female in a particular manner before attempting a copulation. We also quantified the occurrence of genital contact during a copulation attempt as a measure of mating success. Prior work has shown that such genital contact indicates successful sperm transfer in Bahamas mosquitofish [74], at least during mating between individuals originating from the same population. We also quantified the latency – time (s) until the behavior was first expressed – for two key behaviors, “female aggression” and male “close approach.” We did this to capture the overall eagerness of an individual to rapidly express a clearly negative (mate rejection) or positive (motivation to mate) mating behavior. where E is an eagerness index and t is the time (s) for the latency of the behavior, with a ceiling value of 1200 seconds (=20 min). Cases in which the behavior was never expressed during the trial received a value of 0.

Within each no-choice mating trial, we calculated the difference between individuals in an ecologically important trait known to evolve divergently between predation regimes and influence both mating preferences within populations and assortative mating between populations: lateral body shape. To do this, we digitized 16 landmarks on the lateral photographs (Figure S2a) using tpsDig2 software v. 2.31 [76, 77], employed geometric morphometric methods to quantify overall body shape of each individual, and calculated the difference between pairs of fish along a multivariate axis characterizing key difference between populations from different predation regimes. We first performed generalized Procrustes analysis to remove effects of position and size from the landmark coordinates using the MorphoJ program [78]. We then confirmed that populations inhabiting different predation regimes exhibited divergent body shapes matching results of prior work [44, 63, 74, 79] by conducting a mixed-model nested multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA) using geometric shape variable (relative warps) as dependent variables, and centroid size (geometric measure of body size), predation regime, sex, interaction between sex and predation regime, and population nested within predation regime (random effect) as independent variables (e.g. see [66] for analysis information). As expected, we found significant shape divergence between predation regimes (p < 0.0001), with fish derived from high-predation populations exhibiting deeper mid-body/caudal regions and larger, slightly more ventrally positioned eyes (Figure S2b). To estimate the morphological distance between each pair of fish in the mating trials, we calculated the absolute difference in linear discriminant axis scores between the male and female. In this way, we could focus on the most relevant aspects of body shape—note that using the three relative warp scores most associated with differences between predation regimes yields similar results (correlation among values = 0.94, p < 0.0001). Consistent with prior studies [43, 44, 63], body size did not differ between predation regimes in either sex (generalized linear mixed model with population as a random effect): females: χ2 = 0.45, p = 0.50, males: χ2 = 0.45, p = 0.50).

To test for differences in average mating behaviors between predation regimes, we separately examined each behavior from the within-population mating trials (see list of behaviors in Table 2). Principal component analyses (PCA) and inspection of correlation matrices (Tables S1, S2) revealed generally low inter-behavior correlations for both female and male mating behaviors for these trials. Note that similar analyses using PC scores instead of separate behavioral scores showed qualitatively similar results, but trait-by-trait analyses were more readily interpretable. Moreover, we provide detailed assessment of behavioral correlations within each sex in the online supplementary material. We conducted generalized linear mixed models with a negative binomial distribution (log link function) for all count variables, and general linear mixed models for the two “eagerness” continuous variables. The continuous variables were arcsine square-root transformed to bring residuals closer to normality. The models included the predation regime of the focal fish, the covariates female and male centroid size (to test, and adjust for, effects of body size), and the population of origin of the focal fish nested within its predation regime (random effect) as independent variables. Note that body size did not differ between predation regimes (see above), and discussion of any observed effects of body size on behaviors is included in the online supplementary material. In an effort to adjust for multiple comparisons, we chose to control for a false discovery rate (FDR) of 10% in our p values [80]. Throughout, we only interpret results that remained significant after our FDR adjustment. All terms with an original p < 0.05 remained significant after this adjustment, and thus we can straightforwardly interpret all significance values.

When analyzing mating behaviors in inter-population trials, we excluded male coaxing and female wiggle, as these were rarely expressed (~8% of trials). To test for effects of mate origin (native, foreign from same predation regime, foreign from different predation regime) on mating behaviors and mating success, we separately examined each behavior using the same distributional approaches to mixed models described above. These analyses excluded the two populations not used in the inter-population trials. Again, similar analyses using PC scores showed qualitatively similar results, and behavioral correlations were generally low (see online supplementary material for detailed assessment of inter-behavioral associations). The model for each female behavior included the female’s predation regime, mate origin (native, foreign – same regime, foreign – different regime), the interaction between female predation regime and mate origin, morphological distance between the pair, female and male centroid size, and both female and male population as random effects. Each model for male behavior was the same as for females, but female predation regime was substituted with male predation regime in the fixed factors. Mate-origin terms tested our key hypothesis of divergent selection driving behavioral isolation and its potential dependence on predation regime of one of the sexes, and morphological distance tested whether divergence in an ecologically important trait might underlie mating behaviors. Meanwhile, covariates for body size were included to test for, and statistically adjust for, any effects of body size on mating behaviors (discussion of any observed effects of body size on behaviors is included in the online supplementary material). We initially conducted all analyses both with and without the three morphological covariates, but only present the models including them because results were extremely similar—the only qualitative difference was a slight change from statistical significance (p = 0.0158) to marginally non-significant (p = 0.0596) for the interaction term in the model for male lateral displays. We used Tukey’s pairwise contrasts for post-hoc tests.

We examined which mating behaviors best predicted genital contact (as a proxy for mating success) and the between-population fertilization success after mating trials by using binomial generalized linear models (log link function) and model selection. The full model for genital contact included the number of female aggressions, eagerness of female aggression, female approaches, female avoidance, male lateral displays, gonopodium displays, male close approach, eagerness of male close approach, copulation attempts, mate origin, and the interaction between mate origin and copulation attempts. We included the interaction term to specifically investigate the effects of mating attempts on the probability of genital contact and its possible dependence on mate origin. If ecological divergence leads to reduced mating success per copulation attempt, we predicted that this relationship would be greatest (steepest slope) for native interactions, shallower for foreign-same regime interactions, and weakest for foreign-different regime interactions. The full model for fertilization success had the same model terms, with the addition of genital contact (binomial). In this case, the interaction term between mate origin and copulation attempts importantly provided a test for whether the effect of copulation attempts on fertilization success was reduced when attempting to mate with a foreign fish from a different predation regime compared to one from the same predation regime, as predicted if divergent selection was more rapidly driving isolating mechanisms than other factors. Each model was reduced by ranking possible model combinations using Akaike’s information criterion (AIC; [81]) using the dredge command in the MuMIn package in R [82]. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.1 [83]).

To test for differences in the covariance structure of mating behaviors between pairs of mate-origin combinations (native vs. foreign-same regime, native vs. foreign-different regime, foreign-same regime vs. foreign-different regime), we conducted a random skewers analysis [84, 85]. If the function and manner of behavioral expression depended on native vs. foreign interactions, we should find the greatest similarity (higher random skewers correlation) among the two types of foreign mating interactions, with similarly reduced similarity among pairs involving native mating trials. On the other hand, if ecological divergence drives differences in behavioral covariances, then the greatest similarity should be observed for the native vs. foreign-same regime pair. For each mate-origin pair, we generated 10,000 random ‘skewers’, applied them to both matrices and calculated the average vector correlation between their responses using PhyTools [86].

Results

Divergence of within-population mating behaviors between predation regimes

No female behavior differed consistently between predation regimes, while several male behaviors diverged between predation regimes (Table S3). Specifically, males from the high-predation regime performed more lateral displays, gonopodium displays, close approaches, and copulation attempts in comparison to males from the low-predation regime.

Effect of mate origin on female behavior

Mate origin influenced the frequency and eagerness of female aggression, as well as approaches toward the male (Table 3). Females directed the fewest aggressive behaviors toward native males, while showing similar levels of aggression toward all foreign males (native – foreign [same regime]: z = −2.829, p = 0.0130; native – foreign [different regime]: z = −3.517, p = 0.0013; foreign [same regime] – foreign [different regime]: z = −0.428, p = 0.9040; Fig. 1a). Importantly, females initiated aggressive behaviors most rapidly toward foreign males from a different regime (native – foreign [same regime]: t = −1.611, p = 0.2419; native – foreign [different regime]: t = −3.992, p = 0.0002; foreign [same regime] – foreign [different regime]: t = −1.861, p = 0.1515; Fig. 1b). Number of female avoidance responses was not affected by male origin or female regime (Table 3). Female approaches showed a significant interaction between female predation regime and male origin. Mating behaviors and mate origin as predictors of between-population fertilization success

The final model with the lowest AIC value for fertilization success (Table S4b) included number of copulation attempts (χ2 = 0.04, p = 0.8441), mate origin (χ2 = 3.99, p = 0.0459), their interaction (χ2 = 5.31, p = 0.0211), the occurrence of genital contact (χ2 = 9.53, p = 0.0020), level of female aggression (χ2 = 3.04, p = 0.0810), and number of lateral displays (χ2 = 5.06, p = 0.0244). All of these variables were included in at least nine of the ten top-ranked models (Table S4b). Fertilization success was greater for pairs from the same predation regime than different predation regimes. As expected, trials with observed genital contact led to a higher probability of successful fertilization. Importantly, we found that more copulation attempts increased the probability of fertilization for trials involving foreign mates from the same predation regime, but not for those from a different predation regime (Fig. 4b). Finally, trials with higher levels of female aggression (suggestive) or more male lateral displays had a lower chance of successful fertilization (Fig. 4c–d).

Effect of mate origin on the covariance between mating behaviors

Random skewers analysis revealed that the covariance structure of mating behaviors was generally quite similar among all mate origins (all pairwise correlations p < 0.001). However, the degree of similarity between trial types was consistent with the hypothesis that ecological divergence drives differences in behavioral covariances. That is, the similarity in covariance structure between trials involving native fish and those involving foreign fish from the same predation regime was much greater (r = 0.98, p < 0.0001) than the similarity in behavioral covariances among trials involving native fish and those involving foreign fish from a different regime (r = 0.86, p = 0.0010), with the similarity between the two types of inter-population trials showing an intermediate level of similarity (r = 0.93, p = 0.0003).

Discussion

We used common-garden derived fish to evaluate the role of behavioral interactions during one-on-one encounters in the evolution of behavioral isolation between multiple populations that have independently adapted to either similar or different levels of predation risk. We found that only male Bahamas mosquitofish, not females, showed pronounced divergence of mating behaviors between predation regimes when interacting with a native partner. We also discovered that mating interactions depended on the population origin and ecological regime of the potential mate. Behavioral isolation has emerged between both similar and different ecological regimes, but we also uncovered signatures of stronger behavioral isolation between populations evolving under divergent regimes. For instance, some patterns indicated the presence of behavioral isolation regardless of ecological regime (e.g. female aggression frequency), while others pointed to stronger isolation between divergent ecologies than similar ones (e.g. aggression eagerness, mating/fertilization success per copulation attempt, lower fertilization success with foreign mates from a different predation regime, morphology-dependent aggression). Based on our results, we argue below that even in an allopatric system during relatively early stages of speciation, females may discriminate against males adapted to alternative environments, leading more rapidly toward speciation via behavioral isolation between different ecological regimes than similar ones.

Within-population mating behaviors

Ecological selection can lead to divergence in behavioral repertoires linked to mating [21, 29, 87]. In our within-population mating trials, we found that common-garden males derived from high-predation environments exhibited higher overall mating motivation (i.e. more frequent displays, approaches, and copulation attempts) than their low-predation counterparts. This aligns with previously documented behavioral patterns expressed in the field in this system [54], and with prior work in Trinidadian guppies [88–90]. The evolution of higher mating motivation in male Bahamas mosquitofish living under higher predation risk likely reflects an adaptive response to higher mortality rates favoring greater mating incentive and reduced choosiness, especially in scenarios with no alternative mate option [36, 54, 88, 90]. Alternatively, the divergence in male behavior could have partly evolved in response to different female preferences [23]; but this seems less likely for most of the behaviors observed in this study.

Although prior work has demonstrated evolutionary divergence in female mate-choice behaviors between predation regimes [48], we did not find any similar evidence from female mating behaviors during one-on-one interactions. However, most female behaviors recorded during these interactions seemed to reflect forms of mate resistance or rejection, not preference. Indeed, even female approach behavior, which we originally suspected to reflect positive mating motivation, seemed to reflect anti-mating behavior based on its negative associations with male approach behaviors and mating success. Female mating resistance can represent a critical form of mating “preference” in some taxa, providing some control over mating outcomes even in the presence of coercive mating attempts [38, 42]. Females of many species, including mosquitofishes, commonly experience male harassment and coercive mating attempts, and suffer consequences such as energetic costs, injuries, sexually transmitted diseases, and reduced control over mating partners [38, 42, 91–93]. Effective female resistance can thus reduce direct or indirect costs of unwanted copulations and filter out undesired mates. We found that resistance behaviors were common in female Bahamas mosquitofish, but had not diverged in their frequency or eagerness between predation regimes, at least for within-population one-on-one interactions among virgin individuals.

Behavioral isolation and female behaviors

Previous work has shown that female mate choice is involved in the ecologically driven behavioral isolation in Bahamas mosquitofish [44]. Here, we discovered that mate rejection or resistance during one-on-one mating interactions could also play a critical role in the evolution of behavioral isolation. Female Bahamas mosquitofish were more aggressive toward foreign males, and initiated aggression more rapidly toward foreign males from a different ecological regime. Females also exhibited more aggression toward males with more dissimilar body shapes, suggesting lateral morphology serves as a visual cue used in aggressive decisions, similar to prior work showing that it serves as a visual cue for female mate choice [44, 48]. Female aggression further tended to show a negative association with fertilization success, suggesting it can successfully reduce the probability of fertilization. Perhaps the confined space and extended duration of interaction in our experiment can help explain why female aggression was not correlated with the likelihood of genital contact here. Yet, even if females could not fully combat all unwanted mating attempts using aggression, they might still have influenced the volume of sperm successfully inseminated [94], resulting in the reduced fertilization probability observed here. But not only can female aggression alter mating outcomes by affecting copulation success, it can also result in serious injury to the male (personal observations of bruises and impeded swimming ability in males after female aggression). As an extreme example, in three of our trials (all with pairs originating from different predation regimes), the male was killed by the female shortly after the video recording. Hence, female-to-male aggression should constitute an effective behavior to avoid mating attempts from unwanted males.

Moreover, low-predation females exhibited more approaches toward foreign males than native males, with the highest approach frequency toward males from a different regime. Since female approach frequency was negatively associated with genital contact in this study, female approaches may reflect a relatively non-aggressive form of mate rejection, such as intimidation. Why the use of approaches to reject undesirable foreign males manifested more strongly in low-predation females is not clear, but could involve factors such as costs or effectiveness of aggression behaviors [95].

Regardless, divergent ecological selection between predation regimes has apparently led to behavioral isolation through the action of multiple female mate-rejection behaviors, pointing to the potential importance of sexual conflict in speciation [96, 97]. This was evident even though female preferences are often less clear-cut in no-choice mating trials in comparison to choice trials [36], and rejection behaviors may be much less effective in the confined space and duration of these trials compared to the wild. We thus consider the results from our experiment as fairly conservative. Because females were physically separated from males upon maturation, these signs of behavioral isolation should not have resulted from previous mating experiences. However, because females could still interact visually and chemically with males of their own population prior to experimentation, behavioral isolation measured here could have partly involved prior experiences other than mating per se (e.g. visual/chemical imprinting) [98]. Thus, the behavioral isolation observed in this study could involve both innate and learned behaviors not directly associated with mating – and both sources can play important roles in natural speciation events [98–102].

Behavioral isolation and male behaviors

Male mating preferences have recently received more attention, and in some cases, male preferences appear to contribute to behavioral isolation just as much as female preferences [103]. Males in our study exhibited consistent divergence in mating behaviors between predation regimes, but this did not straightforwardly lead to behavioral isolation between ecologically divergent populations. These results are similar to other studies where males seem to recognize different types of mates and change their behavior accordingly, but do not necessarily show clear direction in preference toward conspecifics [104]. Perhaps these patterns partially reflected the use of virgin males in this study, as virgins could show especially high motivation for mating, with lower levels of discrimination [105]. Moreover, male mate choice could occur in this system if the males were allowed to choose among potential mates. Indeed, prior work has found that male mate choice across four Gambusia species shows stronger preference for conspecific females if the other female is from species that evolved under different predation regimes – this preference was additionally partially based on differences in lateral body shape [44].

While males performed more gonopodium displays toward native females than foreign females – perhaps reflecting a mating preference for females from their own population – they also performed more copulation attempts toward foreign females, not native ones. This latter pattern appears to reflect increased rates of attempting to copulate with unwilling females, for which males may repeatedly fail to successfully copulate after multiple attempts. Indeed, males performed more copulation attempts toward females that initiated aggression sooner or showed more avoidance behaviors (see online supplementary material). Importantly, the efficiency of achieving genital contact per copulation attempt was lowest with females from a different regime, reinforcing that ecology plays a major role in behavioral isolation in this system. Even further, while more frequent copulation attempts still improved fertilization success for males that tried to mate with a foreign female from the same regime, no similar positive effect was observed in males that tried to mate with a foreign female from a different regime. Similarly, males may have exhibited more lateral displays toward females with more dissimilar body shapes because these females were especially unwilling to mate, and males performed even greater frequencies of lateral displays in an attempt to change their behavior. Lateral displays were indeed positively correlated with more eagerness of female aggression and female avoidance (see online supplementary material). Yet, these increased frequencies of lateral displays were associated with lower, not higher, fertilization success.

Effect of ecological divergence on fertilization success

Foreign mating pairs from different predation regimes had a lower fertilization success in comparison to foreign pairs from the same predation regime. This clearly suggests that divergent ecological adaptation serves as a key driver of reproductive isolation in this system. Our results suggest that behavioral interactions involving female mating resistance may partially underlie this pattern, with the structure of mating interactions (i.e. behavioral covariances) more closely resembling typical within-population interactions when paired with foreign mates from the same predation regime than when interacting with potential mates from a different predation regime. In addition to behavioral effects, this pattern could also involve genital morphology or gametic isolation. Bahamas mosquitofish have evolved divergent male and female genital morphologies between predation regimes [64, 67], and thus genital contact during copulation between mates from different predation regimes may often result in reduced sperm transfer. This could help explain why males from a different predation regime than the female were not able to increase their fertilization success even with increased rates of mating attempts. It is also possible that some intrinsic gametic incompatibilities [106] have evolved more rapidly under different predation regimes than under similar ones. Future work should examine these possibilities, and the relative importance of behavioral isolation compared to other isolating mechanisms.

Behavioral isolation and its association with non-behavioral traits

Animals can use a variety of sensory cues and signals when making mating decisions, and behavioral isolation can involve multifarious phenotypes [1, 107, 108]. One visual cue that has been described as a “magic” trait in this system, and in other species of fish, is lateral body shape [44, 48, 109, 110]. This is because adaptive divergence in body shape, coupled with divergent mating preferences for body shape, can automatically lead to behavioral isolation between populations. Here we discovered that divergence in body shape also contributes to behavioral isolation via mating resistance in this system, with females showing more aggression toward males from a divergent predation regime, and female aggression tending to reduce the probability of fertilization during a given encounter. Other visual traits may also prove important, such as male coloration or genital size, as these traits have consistently diverged between predation regimes and females show mating preferences for the traits [45–47, 74]. Yet other sensory modalities may be involved, including olfaction, taste, and pressure (tactile and lateral line). For instance, olfaction may contribute to behavioral isolation in many species [111], and odor-related alleles show signs of adaptive divergence in cichlid fish [112]. Moreover, other poeciliid fish use olfaction, taste, touch, and mechanoreception via the lateral line in mating preferences [55, 113–116]. Similar mechanisms could have affected female behavior in our study. Future work should further investigate the sensory modalities and phenotypes involved in behavioral isolation in this system.

Conclusions

Behavioral isolation represents one of the strongest barriers to gene flow in several systems at the early stages of speciation [15–17], and is generally thought to largely result from female choice [117]. This study points to the potential importance of female resistance, and how the behavior of one sex can drive behavioral isolation even in the absence of divergence in that sex’s average behaviors exhibited within their populations. Under natural conditions, we suspect the behaviors quantified in our study may prove even more consequential for mating and fertilization success than observed here, as females would not be confined near a rejected male for extended periods in the wild.

Using a common-garden approach that examined multiple allopatric populations, we demonstrated that parallel adaptation to divergent predatory environments has repeatedly resulted in stronger behavioral isolation than that observed between populations that independently adapted to similar environments, even though behavioral isolation has evolved to some extent between all populations regardless of predation regime. This suggests that, while multiple mechanisms underlie the evolution of behavioral isolation, divergent selection appears to drive this isolation most rapidly as a by-product of parallel adaptation. This adds to the current debate regarding the roles of similar selection, divergent selection, and genetic drift in driving speciation. At least in the post-Pleistocene radiation of Bahamas mosquitofish, divergent selection between predation regimes seems a potent driver of behavioral isolation via female mating preferences and resistance, setting the stage for parallel speciation across the blue holes of Andros Island.
