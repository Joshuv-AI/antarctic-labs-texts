Exploring the association between asthma-related knowledge among caregivers and asthma control in children
Authors: Porrarat Chuaypanang, Chanon Supapon, Wanaporn Anuntaseree, Kantisa Sirianansopa, Kanokpan Ruangnapa, Araya Yuenyongviwat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAsthma is a common chronic disease among children. Despite the availability of effective asthma medication, a substantial proportion of children continue to experience poor asthma control. Caregivers play a critical role in asthma management in children. We aimed to examine caregivers’ asthma-related knowledge and its relationship with asthma control in children and identify other factors influencing asthma control.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted among children aged 2–15 years with asthma who received inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) between 2023 and 2024. Six months after ICS treatment, caregivers completed a validated 16-item questionnaire assessing asthma-related knowledge (score range: 16–64) and sociodemographic data. Asthma control levels were evaluated based on the Global Initiative for Asthma criteria. Poor control was defined as partial or uncontrolled. A multiple logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with poorly controlled asthma.ResultsThe caregivers of 151 children participated in this study. The most common caregiver misconceptions included exercise-induced symptoms (31.8%), ICS safety (26.5%), and avoidance of exercise despite well-controlled asthma (14.6%). Poor asthma control was observed in 31 (20.5%) children. No significant difference in knowledge scores was found between the poorly and well-controlled groups (mean [standard deviation], 55.4 [4.0] vs. 54.1 [4.8]). Poor control was more prevalent in children aged ≤ 5 years and those with poor ICS adherence. Multiple logistic regression revealed significant associations among poor asthma control, age ≤ 5 years (odds ratio [OR]: 3.38, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.38–9.2), and poor adherence to ICS therapy (OR: 5.2, 95% CI: 1.58–17.67).ConclusionsCaregivers’ asthma-related knowledge was not significantly associated with asthma outcomes. Adherence to ICS therapy was the only modifiable factor associated with asthma control, highlighting the need to prioritize interventions that improve medication adherence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06789-4.Keywords: Asthma, Caregiver, Child, Inhaled corticosteroids, Knowledge, Therapy

Introduction

Asthma is the most common chronic respiratory disease among children. It is characterized by recurrent episodes of wheezing, breathlessness, chest tightness, and coughing, often triggered by allergens, physical exercise, or infection. Asthma remains a major cause of pediatric morbidity worldwide. Approximately 10% of school-aged children develop asthma symptoms, and nearly half of them experience severe episodes [1]. Despite the availability of effective treatments, particularly inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) as the mainstay of long-term asthma control, poor asthma control continues to be observed in a substantial proportion of children [2, 3]. This discrepancy highlights the gap between treatment availability and efficacy in the real-world setting. The impact of asthma is multifaceted, affecting various aspects of a child’s life, including frequent school absences [4], sleep disturbances, psychological stress [5], and reduced physical activity [6]. This condition also imposes a substantial burden on families [7] and healthcare systems, contributing to increased emergency department visits and hospital admissions [8]. Furthermore, persistent childhood asthma may result in long-term lung function decline [9].

Multiple factors contribute to poor asthma control, including medication-related issues, environmental exposure, comorbidities, and inadequate caregiver support. Caregivers’ knowledge about asthma is a potential determinant of disease control. Adequate understanding enables earlier recognition of symptoms, improved medication adherence, appropriate use of ICS, and reduced exposure to known triggers. However, evidence regarding this relationship remains unclear. Although some studies have reported that higher caregiver knowledge is associated with fewer exacerbations and improved asthma control [10, 11], others have found no significant association [12, 13]. These discrepancies may reflect variations in study design, measurement tools, or contextual factors. Given these mixed findings, the relationship between caregivers’ asthma-related knowledge and treatment outcomes warrants further investigation. We aimed to assess caregivers’ asthma-related knowledge and its association with clinical outcomes in children with asthma and to identify other factors influencing asthma control. Understanding these relationships can guide the development of effective educational interventions to improve asthma management.

Materials and methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the asthma clinic of Songklanagarind Hospital, a teaching hospital affiliated with the Prince of Songkla University in Southern Thailand. The clinic operates once a week and is staffed by four pediatric pulmonologists and three allergists. The study included caregivers of children aged 2–15 years who had a physician-confirmed diagnosis of asthma, were initiating ICS therapy between July 2023 and June 2024, and completed at least 6 months of follow-up. Caregivers of children with chronic comorbid conditions (e.g., pulmonary, cardiac, neurological, or genetic disorders) and those who declined to participate were excluded.

Study procedure and ethical consideration

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Prince of Songkla University, Thailand (approval no. REC 66-228-1-1). At the initiation of ICS therapy, all participants were informed of the study objectives, and confidentiality was maintained throughout the research process. Written informed consent was obtained from all participating caregivers, whereas assent was obtained from children aged > 12 years.

Study tools

A structured questionnaire was developed to assess caregivers’ knowledge regarding asthma. The questionnaire items were derived from relevant literature and subsequently adapted for content appropriateness. Content validity was evaluated and approved by four pediatric pulmonology specialists from the Department of Pediatrics, Faculty of Medicine, Prince of Songkla University. Each item was reviewed for its relevance, clarity, and appropriateness in assessing caregivers’ asthma-related knowledge in the local clinical and cultural context. Based on their feedback, adjustments were made to the phrasing to ensure that the questions were understandable and culturally appropriate for the caregivers in our study population. The draft version was pilot-tested among 10 caregivers of children with asthma and refined based on their feedback. The final questionnaire comprised 16 items divided into the following two sections: (1) asthma-related knowledge (nine items) (including causes, symptoms, and common triggers) and (2) inhaled medication knowledge (seven items) (including the importance of inhaler use, potential side effects and health risks, and the appropriate use of bronchodilators during asthma exacerbations). A 4-point Likert scale was used, with the following response options: Level 1, strongly disagree; Level 2, disagree; Level 3, agree; and Level 4, strongly agree (see Supplementary File). The results were reported in two parts: (1) the percentage of correct responses for each item, with strongly disagree and disagree grouped together and agree and strongly agree grouped together, and (2) the total knowledge score, calculated as the sum of individual item scores, ranging from 16 to 64 points.

Data collection

At the time of enrolment, data on general characteristics, such as the child’s age, sex, asthma-related clinical details, and the type of ICS prescribed, were collected. Six months after initiating ICS therapy, additional information regarding the caregivers including age, education level, occupation, and household income was obtained. Adherence to ICS therapy was assessed by interviewing caregivers regarding missed doses during the previous month. The prescribed dosing regimen was recorded, and the proportion of missed doses was calculated. Caregivers’ asthma-related knowledge was assessed using a structured 16-item questionnaire.

Definitions

Atopic history and asthma-related comorbidities, including food allergies, atopic dermatitis, and allergic rhinitis, were determined based on physicians’ diagnostic records.

Poor adherence to ICS was defined as missing > 20% of the prescribed doses within the previous month.

Asthma control was assessed using the Global Initiative for Asthma guidelines, which classify asthma into well-controlled, partly controlled, and uncontrolled based on daytime symptoms, nighttime awakenings, reliever use, and activity limitation over the past 4 weeks. Well-controlled asthma was defined as not meeting any of these criteria; the presence of one or two criteria indicated partly controlled asthma, whereas three or more criteria indicated uncontrolled asthma. For analysis purposes, partly controlled and uncontrolled asthma were combined and classified as “poorly controlled asthma.”

Sample size

Sample size was estimated based on previous studies. For predictors of asthma control including allergic rhinitis, asthma exacerbation within the past year, and poor ICS adherence [14], calculations based on a two-proportion comparison (α = 0.05, 80% power, and a 4:1 ratio of well-controlled to poorly controlled asthma) indicated required sample sizes of 50, 160, and 110 participants, respectively. For the asthma-related knowledge score, the calculation was derived from a previous study reporting an association with asthma control (odds ratio [OR] = 7.22) [10] and an estimated prevalence of well-controlled asthma of 80%, yielding a requirement of 152 participants to achieve 90% power. To ensure adequate statistical power across all planned analyses, the largest estimated sample size of 160 participants was adopted.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the R software version 4.1.4 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Descriptive data were expressed as percentages, means with standard deviations (SDs), or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). The participant characteristics were compared between the two groups (well-controlled and poorly controlled asthma). Categorical variables (sex, atopic status, exposure to smoking, pet ownership, and ICS adherence) were analysed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables (child’s and caregiver’s age, weight-for-height, and asthma-related knowledge score) were compared using Student’s t-test for normally distributed data or the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. A multiple logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with poorly controlled asthma. Variables with a P-value of < 0.2 in the univariate analysis were included in the model, and backward elimination was applied for variable selection. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 186 caregivers were invited to participate in the study, of whom 151 caregivers and their children were enrolled in the analysis (Fig. 1). The median age of the children was 5 years (IQR: 3.4–7.4 years), and the median duration of asthma was 1.5 years (IQR: 0.5–3.8). Physician-diagnosed allergic rhinitis was identified in 60.7% of children, whereas poor adherence to ICS was observed in 11.3%. Among the caregivers, 81.2% were mothers, with a median age of 38 years (IQR: 34–42 years). Additionally, 76.1% had completed a bachelor’s degree or higher, and 41.7% reported a monthly household income of < 30,000 Thai baht (approximately 916 USD) (Table 1).

Caregivers’ asthma-related knowledge

Of the 16 items included in the assessment questionnaire, 13 were correctly answered by > 90% of caregivers. However, three items demonstrated comparatively high rates of incorrect responses: (1) 31.8% disagreed that coughing and shortness of breath after exercise are indicative of asthma, (2) 26.5% disagreed that inhaled asthma medications have fewer side effects than oral medications, and (3) 14.6% agreed that children should avoid exercise even when asthma is well controlled (Fig. 2). With regard to overall caregiver knowledge, the mean asthma-related knowledge score was 54.4 (SD: 4.7) out of a maximum possible score of 64. Notably, 79.5% of caregivers achieved a score exceeding 80% of the maximum possible score.

Asthma treatment outcomes and associated factors

The level of asthma control according to the caregiver’s asthma-related knowledge score and other potentially related factors was analyzed. Among the 151 children, 31 (20.5%) exhibited poor asthma control. The prevalence of poorly controlled asthma was significantly higher in children aged ≤ 5 years compared with their older counterparts, as well as in those exhibiting poor adherence to ICS therapy (Table 2). No significant difference in caregiver knowledge scores was observed between children with poorly controlled asthma and those with well-controlled asthma (mean [SD]: 55.4 [4.0] vs. 54.1 [4.8], P = 0.16) (Table 3). Other factors, including the child’s age, sex, asthma characteristics, comorbidities, exposure to passive smoking, pet ownership, and caregiver characteristics, did not differ significantly between the groups. Multiple logistic regression analysis identified two factors significantly associated with poor asthma control: age ≤ 5 years (OR: 3.38, 95% confidence interval [95% CI]: 1.38–9.2) and poor adherence to ICS therapy (OR: 5.2, 95% CI: 1.58–17.67) (Table 4).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study, conducted at a tertiary hospital, examined caregivers’ asthma-related knowledge and its relationship with childhood asthma outcomes. The findings suggest that, although caregivers generally possess a high level of asthma-related knowledge, specific misconceptions persist regarding certain asthma symptoms and the use of asthma medications. Approximately one-third of caregivers (31.8%) did not recognize that coughing and shortness of breath following exercise can indicate asthma. This finding is comparable to that of a previous study, in which 30.3% of caregivers failed to identify that a dry cough following exercise may signal an asthma attack [11]. Such misconceptions may hinder the timely recognition of uncontrolled asthma. Additionally, 26.5% of caregivers in the present study disagreed that inhaled asthma medications had fewer side effects compared with oral formulations. Similarly, a previous study found that only 54.9% of caregivers correctly recognized that ICS have fewer side effects than oral formulations [15]. Such misconceptions may adversely affect adherence to prescribed treatment regimens, thereby contributing to poor asthma control. Furthermore, 14.6% of caregivers in the present study believed that children with asthma should avoid exercise even when their symptoms are well controlled. This finding is comparable to that reported in the United States, wherein 11% of caregivers were either unsure or disagreed regarding their children’s participation in sports [12]. These results highlight a misconception regarding the role of physical activity in asthma management. This view is inconsistent with current evidence, which indicates that regular exercise should be encouraged in children with well-controlled asthma, as it supports overall health, enhances asthma symptom control, and improves quality of life [16]. The identification of several misconceptions among caregivers highlights the need for targeted education emphasizing the safety and role of ICS as controller therapy and improving understanding of asthma triggers and symptom management. In addition, appropriate physical activity should be encouraged in children with well-controlled asthma. Structured asthma education, clear communication during clinical encounters, and culturally appropriate educational materials may help address these misconceptions and support optimal asthma management in children.

No significant association was observed between caregivers’ asthma-related knowledge scores and asthma outcomes in the present study. This finding aligns with findings from previous studies [12, 13] but contrasts with other studies that have documented a positive correlation between higher caregiver’s asthma-related knowledge and improved asthma control [10, 11]. Such discrepancies may reflect differences in study design, outcome measures, or methods of assessing caregiver knowledge, as well as variations in population characteristics, socioeconomic status, and healthcare access. The caregivers in our study were responsible for children aged 2–15 years with a relatively recent diagnosis of asthma, most of whom were highly educated. This homogeneous, single-center sample may have limited variability in asthma-related knowledge and outcomes, reducing comparability with other populations and the ability to detect associations. However, a recent systematic review demonstrated that greater caregivers’ asthma-related knowledge is associated with improved pediatric health outcomes, including enhanced symptom control, medication adherence, correct inhaler use, fewer emergency visits, and reduced rehospitalization, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [17]. These findings suggest that broader, multicenter studies may more effectively elucidate this relationship. They also underscore the critical role of caregiver education in optimizing pediatric asthma management and highlight the importance of regularly assessing asthma-related knowledge during the care of children with asthma.

The incidence of poorly controlled asthma was found to be 20.5% in the present study. This rate is comparable to that reported in a multicenter study conducted across 42 tertiary hospitals in China (19.9%) [18] but substantially lower than the rates observed among children attending primary healthcare centers in Saudi Arabia (62.6%) [10] and among low-income populations in Florida, United States of America (84%) [12]. Given these variations in study populations, caution is warranted when comparing the findings across studies. We identified two factors that were independently associated with poor asthma control: age ≤ 5 years and poor adherence to ICS therapy.

The observed association between preschool age and poor asthma control may be attributed to the relative immaturity of the immune system in young children, which predisposes them to frequent and severe respiratory infections, thereby increasing the risk of asthma exacerbations. This finding is consistent with previous research indicating that preschool children with recurrent respiratory symptoms are more susceptible to severe or uncontrolled asthma [11]. Furthermore, a systematic review and meta-analysis reported that children aged ≤ 5 years are at higher risk of emergency department visits or hospital readmissions for acute asthma compared with older children. These events are predominantly triggered by viral respiratory infections [19]. In addition to physiological factors, asthma control in younger children may also be influenced by caregiving practices. Younger children rely entirely on caregivers for medication administration and symptom recognition, and difficulties in identifying early symptoms or implementing timely treatment adjustments may contribute toward suboptimal control.

Moreover, suboptimal adherence to ICS therapy has been identified as an independent determinant of poor asthma control. In the present study, the prevalence of poor medication adherence was 11.3%. Comparison with other studies is challenging due to variations in the definition and assessment of adherence as well as differences in follow-up duration. Nevertheless, the previously reported nonadherence rates have ranged from 30% to 70% [20, 21], with higher rates generally observed in studies with longer follow-up periods, reflecting the tendency for adherence to decline over time [20]. The relatively low prevalence of poor adherence observed in this study may be attributed to the short duration of asthma among participants, as ICS therapy had only recently been initiated and adherence was evaluated at 6 months of use. The observed association between nonadherence to ICS and suboptimal asthma control in the present study is consistent with the findings of previous research identifying poor adherence as a significant determinant of uncontrolled asthma in pediatric populations [22]. Moreover, a recent systematic review demonstrated that inadequate adherence to ICS therapy is associated with an increased risk of severe asthma exacerbation [23]. In children, adherence to ICS therapy often depends entirely on caregivers, highlighting their critical role in the administration of preventive therapy. These findings underscore the importance of interventions aimed at improving adherence to optimize asthma control.

Limitations and recommendations for future research

Some limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes determination of causal relationships between caregivers’ asthma-related knowledge and asthma control in children. Second, medication adherence was assessed using caregiver self-report, which may be susceptible to recall and social desirability biases and may therefore not fully reflect actual adherence behavior. Third, as the study was conducted at a single tertiary-care center, the study population may not be representative of the broader population of children with asthma and their caregivers, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare settings. Finally, although the sample size was determined a priori and the recruitment target was achieved, the relatively modest sample may still have limited power to detect small associations, particularly in the presence of unmeasured confounding. Future studies with larger and more diverse populations and longitudinal designs would help to further clarify the relationship between caregiver knowledge and asthma control.

Implications for practice

The findings of this study indicate that caregiver asthma education should focus on addressing specific misconceptions identified among participants, such as recognition of asthma symptoms, understanding the side effects of ICS, and the role of physical activity in children with asthma. Additionally, preschool-aged children remain at high risk of poor asthma control despite ICS use, highlighting the importance of educating caregivers about the characteristics and management of asthma in this age group. Efforts to improve adherence to ICS therapy should also be emphasized, as this is critical to optimizing asthma outcomes.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that caregivers’ asthma-related knowledge was not significantly associated with asthma control in children. Notably, a substantial proportion of caregivers held misconceptions regarding asthma symptoms, medication safety, and the role of physical activity in children with asthma. Preschool age and poor adherence to ICS therapy were identified as key factors associated with suboptimal asthma outcomes.
