Lauric acid and levodopa attenuate manganese-induced ovarian toxicity in balb/c mice via antioxidative, anti-inflammatory, and steroidogenic pathways
Authors: Olumayowa Kolawole Idowu, Monsurat Eniola Abdulgafar, Adebusola Zainab Sogbesan, Grace Ayobami Fajemidagba, Abddullahi Abiodun Mohammed, Tope Gafar Atere
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEnvironmental exposure to heavy metals such as manganese (Mn) is associated with reproductive toxicity, particularly in females. Mn-induced ovarian dysfunction involves oxidative stress, hormonal imbalance, inflammation, and disruption of steroidogenesis. Lauric acid (LA) and levodopa (LD) are bioactive compounds with known antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. This study investigated the protective effects of LA and LD against Mn-induced ovarian toxicity in female BALB/c mice.MethodsThirty female BALB/c mice were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 6 per group): control, Mn-only, Mn + LA, Mn + LD, and Mn + LA + LD. Treatments were administered orally for 45 days. Biochemical assays and ELISA were used to evaluate ovarian oxidative stress markers (GSH and MDA), reproductive hormones (progesterone, estradiol, FSH, and LH), proinflammatory interleukins (IL-8-like cytokine, IL-12, IL-15, and IL-18), and the activities of key steroidogenic enzymes (3β-HSD1, 11β-HSD1, and 17β-HSD1). The data were analysed via one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test (p < 0.05).ResultsMn exposure significantly increased oxidative stress and inflammatory cytokines while disrupting hormonal profiles and steroidogenic enzyme activity. Treatment with LA or LD independently alleviated these changes. Notably, coadministration of LA and LD enhanced protection by restoring oxidative balance, reducing proinflammatory interleukins, and normalizing steroid hormone levels and hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase activities.ConclusionsLauric acid and levodopa attenuate Mn-induced ovarian toxicity through antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and steroidogenic regulatory mechanisms. The combined treatment offers synergistic benefits, suggesting a potential therapeutic strategy for mitigating heavy metal-induced reproductive dysfunction.Keywords: Inflammation, Lauric acid, Levodopa, Manganese toxicity, Ovarian dysfunction, Oxidative stress, Steroidogenesis

Background

Manganese ranks as the 12th most abundant element in the Earth’s crust, accounting for approximately 0.1% of its weight. It is commonly present in the environment, including soil, water, and air, and occurs mainly in the form of oxides, silicates, and carbonates within rocks [1, 2]. Environmental exposure to heavy metals, such as manganese (Mn), poses significant health risks, particularly to the reproductive system [3]. Mn, a compound essential for various physiological processes, can become toxic when it is present in excessive amounts. Chronic exposure to elevated levels of Mn has been linked to oxidative stress and hormonal imbalances, which can result in gonadotoxicity and compromised fertility [4]. In females, Mn-induced toxicity is particularly concerning because of its potential to disrupt ovarian function and impair reproductive health.

Mn exposure disrupts mitochondrial function, impairing complexes I and III of the electron transport chain and leading to electron leakage and excessive ROS production [5]. This oxidative stress overwhelms antioxidant defences such as glutathione (GSH), catalase, and superoxide dismutase, creating an imbalance that damages lipids, proteins, and DNA, ultimately impairing cellular functions [6]. Furthermore, Mn toxicity disrupts the endocrine system and interferes with the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis by altering the secretion of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH), which regulates follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH). This dysregulation affects steroidogenesis by altering the activity of hydroxysteroid dehydrogenases (HSDs), leading to imbalanced estradiol and progesterone levels, ultimately impacting reproductive function [7].

Mn activates inflammatory pathways, including nuclear factor-kB (NF-kB) and mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), leading to the upregulation of proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukins (IL)-8-like cytokine, -12, -15, and − 18. These interleukins act as key mediators of immune responses, contributing to Mn-induced gonadotoxicity, ovarian dysfunction, and reduced fertility by exacerbating tissue damage and disrupting normal ovarian function [5, 8]. Addressing these multifactorial pathways of Mn toxicity requires interventions that can mitigate oxidative stress, regulate hormonal balance, and modulate inflammatory responses. Additionally, doses of approximately 100 mg Mn/kg/day over 8 weeks have been reported to induce systemic toxicity in rodents [9], while doses up to 200 mg Mn/kg/day over a period of 63 days have been reported to induce nephrotoxicity in Sprague-Dawley rats [10]. Importantly, administering 250 mg Mn/kg body weight for 45 days has been shown to reliably produce subacute ovarian toxicity, including increased follicular atresia, corpus luteum degeneration, and reduced ovarian weight, effectively achieving comparable cumulative tissue Mn levels and adverse effects within a shorter timeframe [11].

Lauric acid (LA), a medium-chain fatty acid found in coconut oil [12, 13], has been previously reported to demonstrate potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [14]. Its ability to scavenge ROS and modulate immune responses suggests its potential in mitigating the oxidative damage and inflammation associated with Mn toxicity. Similarly, levodopa (LD), a precursor of dopamine, has shown promise in protecting against oxidative stress and inflammation through its neuroprotective and antioxidative properties [15, 16].

This study is essential because of the increasing exposure to Mn, which poses a threat to female reproductive health. Its mechanisms of toxicity remain unclear, and effective treatments are lacking. Exploring the protective roles of LA and LD may offer valuable, non-invasive therapeutic options to mitigate manganese-induced ovarian damage and guide future reproductive health interventions. Therefore, this study investigated the protective effects of LA and LD against Mn-induced gonadotoxicity in female mice. Specifically, their roles in modulating oxidative stress, hormonal balance, HSD activity, and interleukin levels were examined. By elucidating the potential mechanisms of these compounds, this research aims to provide insights into therapeutic strategies for preventing Mn-induced reproductive toxicity.

Methods

Research design

Thirty female BALB/c mice weighing 20–22 g were kept in a supervised environment at the animal facility of Osun State University in Osogbo, Nigeria. The environment was maintained at 25 ± 2 °C, 70–80% relative humidity, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. The mice had free access to a regular pelletized diet and water. Before the trial began, the mice were allowed two weeks to acclimate before being randomly allocated to five groups of six mice each. The control group was administered distilled water, while the experimental groups were treated via the oral route for 45 days with the following regimens: a Mn dose of 250 mg/kg body weight [11]; a combination of Mn (250 mg/kg) and LA (250 mg/kg) [17, 18]; a combination of Mn (250 mg/kg) and LD (25 mg/kg) [19]; or a combination of Mn (250 mg/kg), LA (250 mg/kg), and LD (25 mg/kg). These doses were chosen to reliably induce measurable biological effects within the short experimental duration. At the end of the treatment period, the mice were euthanized, blood samples were collected via cardiac puncture, and their ovaries were harvested for biochemical assessment. These analyses included the measurement of GSH, malondialdehyde (MDA), progesterone, estradiol, FSH, LH, and interleukins including IL-8, IL-12, IL-15, and IL-18, and the activities of 3-β hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (3-β HSD), 11-β hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (11-β HSD), and 17-β hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (17-β HSD).

Measurement of oxidative stress and pro-inflammatory markers

To measure the concentrations of oxidative stress and pro-inflammatory markers, specific protocols were followed. The ovaries were carefully removed from the mice, weighed, and homogenized (using an improvised Dounce homogenizer consisting of a glass tissue grinder and pestle) in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) to ensure that the enzyme stability and pH conditions were suitable for further assays. The homogenized samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to extract the supernatant, which was then collected and used to measure these parameters [20].

To determine GSH levels, 25 µL of the sample supernatant was mixed with 12.5 µL of Ellman’s reagent (prepared by dissolving 19.8 mg of 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) [DTNB] in 100 mL of 0.1% sodium nitrate solution) and 75 µL of 0.2 M phosphate buffer (pH 8.0). The reaction mixture was allowed to stand at room temperature for 5 min, during which DTNB reacted with GSH to produce the yellow compound 5-thio-2-nitrobenzoic acid (TNB). The absorbance of the resulting solution was measured at 412 nm via a spectrophotometer, and the GSH concentration was calculated via the molar extinction coefficient (ε = 1.34 × 10⁴ M⁻¹cm⁻¹) [21].

To quantify MDA levels, 25 µL of the tissue supernatant was mixed with 50 µL of thiobarbituric acid (TBA) reagent (0.67% TBA in 50% acetic acid). The mixture was incubated in a boiling water bath for 15 min to facilitate the formation of the MDA-TBA complex. After cooling to room temperature, the mixture was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min to remove precipitates. The absorbance of the clear supernatant was then read at 532 nm via a spectrophotometer, and the MDA levels were calculated via the molar extinction coefficient for the MDA-TBA complex, with the results expressed in µmol/mL [22].

The concentrations of IL-8-like cytokine were assessed using macrophage inflammatory protein-2 (MIP-2) ELISA kits (Catalog No: MM200, R&D Systems, USA); IL-12 using ELISA kits (Catalog No: PKSM041070, Elabscience, China); IL-15 using ELISA kits (Catalog No: RK00106, ABclonal, China); and IL-18 using ELISA kits (Catalog No: PKSM041079, Elabscience, China). Each assay was performed using 25 µL of ovarian tissue homogenate supernatant, following the manufacturers’ instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a SpectraMax Plus 384 microplate reader to quantify interleukin expression levels.

Estimation of serum progesterone, estradiol, FSH, and LH levels

Serum concentrations of progesterone, estradiol, FSH, and LH were measured using ELISA kits with analytical grade reagents: progesterone (Catalog No: E-OSEL-M0006, Elabscience, China), estradiol (Catalog No: ES380S, Calbiotech, USA), FSH (Catalog No: FS232F, Calbiotech, USA), and LH (Catalog No: LH231F, Calbiotech, USA). The procedures were performed as previously described by Khatun et al. (2009). Mean absorbance values for standards, controls, and test samples were calculated, and standard curves were generated by plotting absorbance against standard concentrations to determine hormone levels accurately [23].

Determination of 3β-HSD1, 11β-HSD1 and 17β-HSD1 activity

The ovaries were homogenized in ice-cold 10 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) containing 0.25 M sucrose to maintain enzyme stability and tissue integrity. Protein concentrations in the homogenate were quantified via a BCA protein assay kit, with bovine serum albumin (BSA) serving as the standard. For enzyme activity measurements, 25 µL of the tissue homogenate, along with substrates and cofactors, were added to reaction tubes. The mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to facilitate enzymatic reactions [24]. The activity of 3β-HSD1 was evaluated by monitoring the conversion of pregnenolone to progesterone, a process that involves the reduction of NAD + to NADH. Similarly, the activity of 11β-HSD1 was determined by tracking the conversion of cortisone to cortisol, resulting in the oxidation of NADPH to NADP+. The activity of 17β-HSD1 was assessed by measuring the conversion of estrone to estradiol, which also involves the oxidation of NADPH to NADP+. These enzymatic activities were quantified spectrophotometrically at 340 nm, which corresponds to the changes in the absorbance of NADH or NADP+ [25]. Each assay was performed in triplicate to increase accuracy and minimize variability.

Determination of Mn concentration

Mn accumulation in ovarian tissue was quantified using spectroscopic analysis. Calibration curves were established from serial dilutions of a certified single-element Mn standard. Multi-element quality control standards were employed to validate the calibration and ensure the accuracy and precision of the system. To maintain consistency, blanks and quality control samples were run after every 10 test samples. All standards, controls, and blanks were prepared in the same acid digestion matrix. Ovarian tissue samples were digested in a mixture of 20% perchloric acid and 80% nitric acid, then the digests were diluted with 0.5% nitric acid to a final volume of 500 µL. Instrument settings included an RF power of 1200 W, a sample flow rate of 1.2 mL/min, argon gas pressure of 100 psi, and nebuliser pressure of 36 psi. To avoid cross-contamination, each sample run was preceded and followed by a 4-minute wash using the digestion solution. Spectral analysis and Mn quantification were carried out using Smart Analyser Software [26]. The method had a detection limit of 0.0002 ppm in solution, and results were expressed as micrograms of Mn per gram of ovarian tissue.

Statistical analyses

The results are expressed as the mean ± SEM. Group differences were analysed via one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Statistical analyses were performed via GraphPad Prism, with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05.

Results

LA and LD alleviate Mn-induced oxidative stress in female mice

Compared with the control, exposure to Mn resulted in a statistically significant (p < 0.05) reduction in the GSH level [F (4, 10) = 371.3] (Fig. 1A) and a significant (p < 0.05) increase in the MDA level [F (4, 10) = 711.9] (Fig. 1B). Compared with the Mn-only group, there were significantly (p < 0.05) increased GSH levels and decreased MDA levels in the LA- and LD-treated groups. Furthermore, the group receiving both LA and LD had significantly (p < 0.05) higher GSH levels and lower MDA levels than did those receiving either LA or LD alone. However, no statistically significant difference was observed between the LA-treated and LD-treated groups.

GSH level (A); MDA level (B). The data are shown as the means ± S.E.M. at *: p < 0.05 compared with the control group; #: p < 0.05 compared with the untreated Mn group; and α: p < 0.05 compared with the Mn + LA + LD group (LA: lauric acid, LD: levodopa, Mn: manganese)

LA and LD restore hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase activity in Mn-treated female mice

The activities of HSDs showed distinct patterns across the different treatment groups. Compared with that of the control, the activity of 3β-HSD1 was significantly greater (p < 0.05) in the LA- and LD-treated groups, whereas the activity of the group exposed to both treatment regimens did not significantly change [F (4, 10) = 106.4] (Fig. 2A). Additionally, 3β-HSD1 activity was significantly lower (p < 0.05) in all the LA- and LD-treated groups than in the Mn-only group, with lower levels observed in the LA- and LD-treated groups than in the combined treatment group. However, no significant difference in 3β-HSD1 activity was found between the LA-treated and LD-treated groups.

3β-HSD1 activity (A), 11β-HSD1 activity (B), and 17β-HSD1 activity (C). The data are displayed as the mean ± S.E.M. at *: p < 0.05 compared with the control group; #: p < 0.05 compared with the untreated Mn group; α: p < 0.05 compared with the Mn + LA + LD group; λ: p < 0.05 compared with the Mn + LA and Mn + LD groups (LA: lauric acid, LD: levodopa, Mn: manganese)

Compared with that of the control, the activity of 11β-HSD1 significantly decreased (p < 0.05) in all the Mn-exposed groups [F (4, 10) = 70.59] (Fig. 2B). Compared with Mn-only group, treatment with LA or LD led to a significant increase (p < 0.05) in 11β-HSD1 activity, although 11β-HSD1 activity in the LA- and LD-treated groups remained significantly lower (p < 0.05) than that in the combined treatment group. Similar to 3β-HSD1, no significant difference in 11β-HSD1 expression was observed between the LA-treated and LD-treated groups.

The activity of 17β-HSD1 was significantly greater (p < 0.05) in all the Mn-exposed groups than in the control group [F (4, 10) = 96.68] (Fig. 2C). LA and LD treatments significantly reduced 17β-HSD1 activity (p < 0.05) compared with the Mn-only group, with the LA-treated group showing higher levels (p < 0.05) than both the LD-treated group and the combined treatment group. There was no significant difference in 17β-HSD1 activity between the LD-treated group and the combined treatment group.

LA and LD mitigate Mn-induced hormonal imbalances in female mice

Compared with those in the control group, progesterone levels were significantly (p < 0.05) lower in all the Mn-exposed groups [F (4,10) = 152.7], whereas compared with the Mn-only group, treatment with LA or LD significantly (p < 0.05) elevated progesterone levels (Fig. 3A). However, progesterone levels remained significantly (p < 0.05) lower in the LA- and LD-treated groups than in the groups receiving both treatments, with no significant difference between the LA- and LD-treated groups. Conversely, estradiol levels were significantly (p < 0.05) elevated in all the Mn-exposed groups compared with the control [F (4,10) = 72.91]. Compared with Mn-only group, treatment with LA or LD significantly (p < 0.05) reduced estradiol levels (Fig. 3B). Compared with the group receiving both treatments, the LD-treated group presented significantly (p < 0.05) higher estradiol levels, whereas no significant differences were detected between the LA-treated group and the combined treatment group or between the LA- and LD-treated groups.

Progesterone (A), estradiol (B), FSH (C), and LH (D). The data are presented as the means ± S.E.M. at *: p < 0.05 compared with the control group; #: p < 0.05 compared with the untreated Mn group; α: p < 0.05 compared with the Mn + LA + LD group (LA: lauric acid, LD: levodopa, Mn: manganese)

FSH levels were significantly (p < 0.05) lower in the Mn-exposed groups than in the control groups but not in the combined treatment group [F (4,10) = 120.2] (Fig. 3C). Nonetheless, treatment with LA or LD significantly (p < 0.05) elevated FSH levels compared with those in the Mn-only group, although the levels remained significantly (p < 0.05) lower than those observed in the combined treatment group, with no significant difference between the LA- and LD-treated groups. Similarly, LH levels were significantly (p < 0.05) lower in all the Mn-exposed groups than in the control group [F (4,10) = 152.7] (Fig. 3D). Compared with Mn-only group, treatment with LA or LD significantly (p < 0.05) elevated LH levels. However, LH levels in the LA- and LD-treated groups remained significantly (p < 0.05) lower than those observed in the combined treatment group, with no significant differences between the LA- and LD-treated groups.

LA and LD modulate the Mn-induced dysregulation of proinflammatory interleukins in female mice

There were significantly (p < 0.05) elevated levels of IL-8 [F (4, 10) = 205.6], IL-15 [F (4, 10) = 102.9], IL-18 [F (4, 10) = 102.5], and IL-12 [F (4, 10) = 77.61] in all the Mn-exposed groups compared with those in the control group (Fig. 4A, C, and D). Compared with the Mn-only group, the LA- and LD-treated groups presented significantly lower IL-8, IL-15, IL-18, and IL-12 levels (p < 0.05). However, the LA- and LD-treated groups presented significantly higher levels of IL-8, IL-15, and IL-18 than the groups receiving both treatment regimens did, while the IL-12 levels were significantly higher in the LD-treated group than in the combined treatment group. No statistically significant differences were observed in the IL-8, IL-15, IL-18, or IL-12 levels between the LA-treated and LD-treated groups, nor was there a significant difference in the IL-12 levels between the LA-treated group and the group exposed to both treatments.

Ovarian manganese levels in Mn-exposed mice

The results revealed a significant increase (p < 0.05) in ovarian manganese levels in all Mn-treated groups compared to the control group [F (4, 10) = 161.1] (Fig. 5). However, treatment with either LA or LD did not produce a statistically significant reduction in ovarian Mn levels relative to the Mn-only group. Discussion

According to recent research, ovaries of animals are disrupted by high Mn exposure, leading to follicle loss, decreased ovarian weight, and changed hormone levels [27]. Higher serum Mn levels in women have been correlated with hormonal imbalances in PCOS, indicating a malfunction in the control of the ovarian-pituitary axis [28, 29]. These findings support a concerning association between elevated Mn exposure and ovarian dysfunction in women. This study explored the beneficial impacts of LA and LD on Mn-induced female gonadotoxicity in a mouse model, with a focus on redox status, hormonal homeostasis, HSD activity, and interleukin levels.

Lipid peroxidation, a consequence of oxidative stress, is linked to the development of various human diseases. This process involves the free radical-mediated oxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids, resulting in the formation of lipid hydroperoxides and a diverse array of downstream reactive carbonyl species. Extensive research has been undertaken to elucidate the complex kinetics, mechanisms, and products of lipid peroxidation, as these processes play pivotal roles in cellular signalling, cell death, and disease progression [30]. Exposure to Mn significantly disrupted the oxidative balance, as evidenced by decreased GSH levels and increased MDA levels. These markers are critical indicators of oxidative stress, with reduced GSH reflecting impaired antioxidant defences and elevated MDA indicating enhanced lipid peroxidation. Treatment with LA and LD effectively restored the GSH levels and reduced the MDA levels, confirming their antioxidative properties. The combined treatment of LA and LD showed superior efficacy, underscoring the potential synergistic effects of these agents. These findings align with previous studies emphasizing the role of antioxidants in counteracting Mn-induced oxidative damage [3, 4]. Moreover, the antioxidant capacities of these LA and LD as single therapeutic agents and the possible synergistic protective mechanisms of the two compounds were previously demonstrated in genetically modified Drosophila melanogaster [14]. The ability of LA to reduce oxidative stress may contribute to its potential to increase GSH production, improve mitochondrial metabolic activity, and generate ATP [31, 32]. Similarly, the antioxidant activity of LD may be linked to the synthesis of dopamine, a strong antioxidant [33].

Steroidogenesis, the biosynthesis of steroid hormones, is a crucial driver of reproductive function. It regulates the development and differentiation of undifferentiated gonads into either testes or ovaries, as well as their subsequent growth and maturation. The pivotal role of steroid hormones, such as testosterone and estrogen, in orchestrating the acquisition of reproductive competence is well established [34]. Disruption of normal steroidogenic processes can result in various reproductive disorders. These steroid hormones exert their effects by activating specific nuclear receptors, which then interact with target genes to modulate transcriptional regulation [35].

In the present study, Mn exposure dysregulated the activities of hydroxysteroid dehydrogenases (HSDs), including 3β-HSD1, 11β-HSD1, and 17β-HSD1, which are essential for steroid hormone biosynthesis. Reduced 11β-HSD1 activity is associated with elevated cortisol levels, impaired glucocorticoid metabolism, and subsequent reproductive dysfunction [36]. To date, there is no published evidence indicating that LA or LD directly modulate the activity of 3β-HSD, 1β-HSD, or 17β-HSD1 in ovarian tissue. However, the findings of this study indicate for the first time, that both LA and LD influence steroidogenic enzyme activity. These modulatory effects occur potentially through the bioactive effects of monolaurate derived from LA [37] and dopamine synthesized from LD [38].

The observed decreases in progesterone and FSH and LH levels and the increase in estradiol levels in Mn-exposed mice highlight the deleterious effects of Mn on hormonal homeostasis. The observed decrease in progesterone levels is associated with elevated 3β-HSD1 activity in the ovaries [39]. Although 3β-HSD1 facilitates the conversion of pregnenolone to progesterone, it also catalyses the conversion of progesterone to androgens. Increased 3β-HSD1 activity shifts the metabolic balance toward androgen synthesis, thereby reducing progesterone levels [40]. Similarly, elevated 17β-HSD1 activity enhances the conversion of estrone to estradiol. This upregulation leads to increased estradiol levels, contributing to an imbalance in steroid hormone levels and subsequent ovarian dysfunction [41].

The ability of LA and LD to restore these hormone levels suggests their potential to counteract Mn-induced endocrine disruption. The combined treatment group exhibited the most significant normalization, suggesting an additive or synergistic effect. These results are consistent with studies linking Mn toxicity to reproductive hormone dysregulation and oxidative stress-induced endocrine disruption [42]. Moreover, LA increases progesterone, FSH, and LH levels while decreasing estradiol, likely through the modulatory effects of monolaurate on gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) activity [43]. In contrast, LD influences these hormonal levels through its direct impact on pituitary gland function, potentially through dopamine signalling pathways [44, 45].

Mn exposure significantly elevated the levels of proinflammatory interleukins (IL-8, IL-12, IL-15, and IL-18), indicative of a systemic inflammatory response. Treatment with LA and LD effectively reduced these interleukin levels, with the combined treatment showing superior anti-inflammatory effects. The ability of LA and LD to modulate inflammatory pathways aligns with their reported immunomodulatory and antioxidative properties [14]. LA likely exerts its anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting the activation of NF-kB and suppressing MAPK phosphorylation, as suggested by prior studies [46]. Similarly, the anti-inflammatory properties of LD may be attributed to the capacity of dopamine to inhibit inflammatory pathways [47]. While direct studies on the anti-inflammatory effects of LD in mouse ovaries are limited, our findings indicate its potential to modulate inflammation in the ovaries. LD has been identified in human ovarian follicular fluid and shown to reduce oxidative stress in granulosa cells [33], supporting its possible therapeutic role in ovarian disorders. The distinct modulation of specific interleukins further underscores the intricate relationship between oxidative stress, inflammation, and endocrine regulation.

The observed Mn accumulation in the ovaries of BALB/c mice suggests that oral Mn exposure can lead to significant tissue retention. This observation is consistent with earlier findings indicating that Mn is efficiently absorbed and distributed to various organs, including the ovaries, especially under conditions of chronic or high-dose exposure [11]. Moreover, the data imply that co-treatment with LA or its LD does not appear to inhibit Mn uptake or alter its systemic absorption.

Conclusion

This study provides compelling evidence that LA and LD confer significant protection against Mn-induced ovarian toxicity in female mice. Both compounds attenuated oxidative stress, restored hormonal homeostasis, normalized the activities of key steroidogenic enzymes, and reduced proinflammatory cytokine expression. Importantly, the combined administration of LA and LD offered greater protective effects than the individual treatments did. These findings underscore the potential of LA and LD as promising therapeutic agents for mitigating heavy metal-induced reproductive dysfunction and contribute valuable insight into the development of interventions for environmental gonadotoxicity.

Limitations of the study

The use of an animal model may not fully translate to human physiology, and species differences could impact the findings. Additionally, the study lacked long-term exposure data and histopathological analysis of ovarian tissues. The reproductive effects and underlying molecular mechanisms have not been explored, highlighting the need for further research on fertility outcomes to increase the clinical relevance of these findings.
