Risk of Cardiovascular Events During Delivery Hospitalization Among Pregnant Patients With Mental Health Conditions
Authors: Kavya Parekh, R J Waken, Khavya Avula, Katelin B Nickel, Kathryn J Lindley, Cynthia Rogers, Karen E Joynt Maddox
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of maternal mortality. Mental health disorders are risk factors for cardiovascular disease in nonpregnant populations, but their association with delivery outcomes is unknown.MethodsThis cross‐sectional study used delivery hospitalizations from the 2021 National Inpatient Sample. We used generalized estimating equations to fit quasi‐Poisson regression models for cardiovascular severe maternal morbidity and mortality (SMM) events as defined by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and non‐SMM cardiovascular conditions or complications as defined by the Alliance for Innovation on Maternal Health. Incidence rate ratios were calculated for depression, anxiety, psychosis, bipolar, obsessive‐compulsive disorder, posttraumatic stress disorder, and other mental health conditions. Results were adjusted for clinical risk, social risk, and pregnancy characteristics, and clustered by hospital.ResultsOf 667 502 delivery hospitalizations, 82 543 (12.4%) had a mental health diagnosis. Patients with mental health diagnoses were more likely to be White and from suburban areas. Overall, 791 (0.1%) patients had >1 SMM event and 9007 (1.3%) had a non‐SMM cardiovascular condition or complication. Patients with mental health conditions had higher adjusted rates of cardiovascular SMM (adjusted incidence rate ratio, 2.36 [95% CI, 2.00–2.80], P<0.001) and other cardiovascular conditions and complications (adjusted incidence rate ratio, 1.94 [95% CI, 1.83–2.06], P<0.001). Stratifying by mental health conditions, depression, anxiety, and posttraumatic stress disorder were each associated with elevated rates of cardiovascular conditions and complications.ConclusionMental health conditions may be risk factors for adverse cardiovascular events at the time of delivery. Future studies should aim to understand the physiological underpinnings of this relationship.Keywords: cardiovascular disease, maternal morbidity and mortality, mental health

Cardiovascular disease remains a leading cause of maternal morbidity and mortality, contributing to 1 in 3 maternal deaths in the United States. 1 , 2 , 3 Moreover, pre‐existing cardiovascular disease has been estimated to be present in 1% to 4% of all pregnancies, with this incidence rising due to advancing maternal age; a higher proportion of people with congenital heart disease reaching childbearing age; and increasing prevalence of diabetes, hypertension, and obesity among young people. 4 , 5 The United States has the highest maternal mortality rate among developing countries, with 65.8% of those deaths being considered preventable. 6 It is thus critical to identify further risk factors in order to develop interventions that could decrease morbidity and mortality during and after pregnancy.

Severe mental illness is an established risk factor for cardiovascular disease in the broader nonpregnant population. 7 , 8 , 9 Prior literature has explored how depression and anxiety can increase risk for long‐term cardiovascular events or preeclampsia, which is a known risk factor for cardiovascular events. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 However, the impact on cardiovascular complications at the time of delivery has yet to be explored. Moreover, there is a gap in examining how preexisting mental health conditions other than depression can change individuals' risk for maternal cardiovascular events.

Understanding the outcomes of pregnant populations with mental health conditions could better direct their care and ultimately help to prevent adverse outcomes. Therefore, we used a cross‐sectional, nationally representative sample to compare rates of adverse cardiovascular events during delivery hospitalizations for pregnant populations with and without a mental health diagnosis. We investigated the impact of mental illnesses in aggregate, as well as the specific impact of depression, psychotic disorders, anxiety, obsessive‐compulsive disorder, and bipolar disorder.

METHODS

Data

The data for this project were accessed under a specific data use agreement and cannot be made directly available to other researchers, though others can apply to the agency for access. Analytic code is available upon request. Data from the 2021 National Inpatient Sample from the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project at the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality were used for this retrospective observational study. 14 The National Inpatient Sample is the largest publicly available all‐payer inpatient database and represents a 20% stratified sample of nearly all discharges from United States general acute‐care hospitals. Delivery hospitalizations were identified using the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD‐10‐CM) delivery indicator data element, which indicates the delivery of a newborn, excluding abortions. 14 We excluded hospitalizations that were missing race, insurance status, age, region, or ZIP code–based income. Ultimately, 667 502 hospitalizations were included in our analyses (Figure 1). Patient race and ethnicity are provided by hospitals in their Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project data submission and may be collected differently at each hospital.

Flow diagram of study sample.

Primary Explanatory Variable

Our primary explanatory variable was a composite of perinatal mental health diagnoses. Diagnoses included were defined and categorized by the Alliance for Innovation on Maternal Health, and include depression, bipolar disorder, anxiety, obsessive‐compulsive disorder, psychotic disorders, posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and other mental health conditions or symptoms complicating pregnancy, such as adjustment or conversion disorder or the ICD‐10 codes “other mental health conditions complicating pregnancy” (Table S1). 15

Primary Outcome

Our primary outcome of interest was the rate of cardiac severe maternal morbidity or mortality (SMM) events during the delivery hospitalization, as defined by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. 16 SMM events are 21 indicators related to unexpected outcomes of delivery. From these, we used the cardiovascular SMM indicators, which we defined as acute myocardial infarction, aneurysm (including aortic aneurysm and dissections), cardiac arrest or ventricular fibrillation, conversion of cardiac rhythm, heart failure or arrest during surgery or procedure, puerperal cerebrovascular disorders (including ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes, transient ischemic attack, and central venous thrombosis), pulmonary edema or acute heart failure, and cardiogenic shock. 17 ICD‐10 codes were referenced from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (Table S2).

Secondary Outcomes

We further assessed cardiovascular conditions and complications of pregnancy that may not be adequately severe or acute to be included in the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention SMM diagnoses but that can still complicate delivery. As defined by the Alliance for Innovation on Maternal Health (Table S3), these included cardiac valve disorder, coronary artery disease, cardiomyopathies of pregnancy, arrhythmias, pericarditis, myocarditis, pericardial effusions, cardiac tamponade, and other diseases of the circulatory system complicating pregnancy. 15 These events, added to the cardiovascular SMM events, made up the aggregate variable referred to as “any cardiovascular complication.” Due to data limitations in the National Inpatient Sample, namely the lack of linked outpatient data, we were unable to determine whether some of these conditions or complications predated pregnancy (eg, mitral stenosis) versus occurring as a result or complication of pregnancy (eg, cardiomyopathy).

We further assessed several in‐hospital outcomes including length of stay (in days) and death during hospitalization, as identified by the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project database.

Statistical Analysis

First, we assessed baseline characteristics of our study population, including age, race and ethnicity (White, Black, Hispanic, Asian or Pacific Islander, Native American, or other), insurance status (private, Medicaid, or Medicaid/self‐pay/no‐charge/other), quartile of estimated median household income based on patients ZIP code, and patient county urban or rural status (urban, suburban, rural). Because of small sample sizes, patients identifying as Native American or “other” were grouped in 1 category when analyzing race. For the same reason, patients with self‐pay, no‐charge, or “other” insurance status were grouped with Medicaid when analyzing primary payer status. We also compared rates of obstetric comorbidities, as validated by Leonard et al, excluding cardiac and mental health diagnoses. 18

Second, the incidence of cardiovascular events and in‐hospital outcomes were analyzed. We used chi‐square tests to evaluate for differences between crude rates of cardiovascular SMM events, non‐SMM cardiovascular events, any cardiovascular events, and in‐hospital mortality between deliveries with and without a mental health diagnosis. A t test was used to evaluate differences in mean length of stay between patients with a mental health condition and patients without a mental health diagnosis.

Finally, we used regression models to assess the rates of our primary outcome, cardiovascular SMM events per hospitalization, based on the presence of a mental health diagnosis. We used generalized estimating equations to fit quasi‐Poisson regression models to describe incident rate ratios (IRR) for cardiovascular SMM events per hospitalization by mental health status, clustering by hospital ID to account for within hospital similarities. Additional variables included social risk (race, insurance status, ZIP code–based income, regional differences), based on prior evidence that social risk influences pregnancy outcomes, and clinical risk and pregnancy characteristics, selected from the Leonard et al obstetric comorbidities (chronic hypertension, bleeding disorders, preexisting diabetes, previous cesarean sections, multiple pregnancies, pulmonary hypertension, and preexisting anemia). 18 Although race is a social construct and acts as a proxy for the experience of racism, we adjusted for race to isolate the effect of mental health conditions on cardiovascular conditions, given that Black patients have higher maternal morbidity and mortality rates but are underdiagnosed for mental health conditions due to a lack of culturally competent care. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 We excluded Leonard et al obstetric comorbidities in our risk adjustment from the model that directly occurred during the delivery (ie, placenta accreta, placental abruption, and preterm birth) as these are generally considered outcomes rather than comorbidities. 23 , 24 We repeated the same process for all secondary outcomes of non‐SMM cardiovascular events and any cardiovascular events.

In prespecified subgroup analyses, due to the heterogeneity of mental health conditions included, we assessed the adjusted IRR (aIRR) of any cardiac complication per hospitalization for each specific mental health condition, including depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder, obsessive‐compulsive disorder, psychotic disorders, and PTSD as defined by the Alliance for Innovation on Maternal Health. 15 We additionally ran 1 model that included all individual mental health conditions. This was done using similar models as described.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (Version 4.4, http://www.r‐project.org). P values <0.0125 (0.05/4, to reflect multiple comparisons given our 4 main outcomes) were considered statistically significant. This research was approved by the Washington University Office of Human Research Protection as nonhuman‐subjects research because of the deidentified nature of the data.

RESULTS

Patient Characteristics

Among the included deliveries, 82 453 (12.4%) hospitalizations had a mental health condition encoded (Table 1). In total, 27 992 (33.9%) hospitalizations had >1 mental health condition encoded. A higher proportion of deliveries with a mental health diagnosis were White (70.3%) as compared with those without a mental health diagnosis (50.3%). Otherwise, baseline characteristics were similar between the 2 groups, including payer status, age, urban versus rural location, and income quartile.

In‐Hospital Outcomes During Delivery Hospitalization

Overall, the proportion of delivery hospitalizations with a cardiovascular SMM was higher among those with a mental health diagnosis than among patients without a mental health condition (0.3% versus 0.1%, P<0.001) (Table 2). The same held true for other cardiovascular complications, respectively (2.7% versus 1.2%, P<0.001). Though mortality rates were exceedingly low, compared with patients without a mental health condition, patients with a mental health condition were less likely to die during their delivery hospitalization (0.01% versus 0.03%, P=0.001).

Rates of Cardiovascular SMM and Other Cardiovascular Events During Delivery Hospitalization

The IRR for cardiovascular SMM events in patients with a mental health diagnosis was 2.75 (95% CI, 2.34–3.21, P<0.001), 2.36 for other cardiovascular events (95% CI, 2.25–2.57, P<0.001), and 2.35 for the aggregate of any cardiovascular events (95% CI, 2.24–2.47, P<0.001) as compared with those without a mental health diagnosis. Even after adjusting for clinical risk and pregnancy characteristics, patients with a mental health diagnosis still had a higher rate of cardiovascular SMM (aIRR, 2.36 [95% CI, 2.00–2.80], P<0.001), although the effect was nominally attenuated compared with the unadjusted rates. The IRRs of other cardiovascular events (aIRR, 1.94 [95% CI, 1.83–2.06], P<0.001) and any cardiovascular events (aIRR, 1.94 [95% CI, 1.83–2.06], P<0.001) for those with a mental health condition were also elevated even after risk adjustment (Table 3).

Rates of Cardiovascular Conditions and Complications Among Specific Mental Health Conditions

The aIRRs of cardiovascular conditions and complications by specific included mental health conditions are shown in Figure 2. Depression, anxiety, and PTSD were the only mental health conditions that had a significantly higher rate of cardiovascular conditions and complications (for depression, aIRR, 1.53 [95% CI, 1.41–1.67], P<0.001; for anxiety, aIRR, 1.87 [95% CI, 1.75–2.01], P<0.001; for PTSD, aIRR, 1.52 [95% CI, 1.21–1.92], P<0.001). There were no statistically significant associations between psychosis, bipolar disorder, and obsessive‐compulsive disorder with all cardiovascular conditions and complications. When we included all mental health conditions together in 1 model, both depression and anxiety remained statistically significantly associated with poor outcomes, though the effects were slightly attenuated (for depression, aIRR, 1.13 [95% CI, 1.03–1.25], P<0.0001; for anxiety, aIRR, 1.70 [95% CI, 1.56–1.84], P<0.001) (Figure 2).

DISCUSSION

This study using a nationally representative sample of deliveries found that mental health conditions were associated with an increased rate of cardiovascular events during the delivery hospitalization. Cardiovascular SMM specifically occurred at more than double the incidence in patients with a mental health condition, as compared with patients without a mental health condition. Overall, this study demonstrates an important association between mental health conditions and cardiovascular events in the pregnant population.

The mechanisms linking mental health conditions with maternal cardiovascular events are unclear, but in nonpregnant populations, there are multiple proposed mechanisms linking mental health conditions with increased cardiovascular risk. Mental health disorders can cause dysregulation of the autonomic nervous system and hypothalamic–pituitary axis, inflammation, and oxidative stress, all of which can lead to an increased risk of coronary heart disease. 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 Depression, schizophrenia, bipolar, and anxiety are all also associated with various lifestyle factors and behaviors that can increase the risk of cardiovascular disease, such as a sedentary lifestyle or inadequate exercise, substance use, and high‐fat diets. 8 , 25 , 30 Additionally, it is possible that this association is bidirectional; patients with preexisting cardiovascular risk factors may have higher levels of depression and anxiety secondary to their higher levels of medical stress. Therefore, they may be predisposed to cardiovascular conditions inherently. 31 , 32 , 33 Finally, it is possible that medications used to treat mental health conditions could cause adverse delivery outcomes, as psychotropic medications have been associated with cardiovascular events in the general population. 34 , 35 Given that pregnancy occurs typically earlier in life and acts as a stress test itself, it is important to establish these relationships between mental health and cardiovascular outcomes in pregnancy specifically. There are few mechanistic data in the literature on how mental health conditions can cause cardiovascular changes for the pregnant population, so these possibilities warrant further evaluation in clinical data sets.

State‐specific or non‐US studies have linked both perinatal and prenatal depression to new cardiovascular disease after pregnancy. 11 , 12 , 13 However, these studies analyzed cardiovascular disease over the course of years, rather than during the delivery hospitalization or peripartum period. Given these prior results, our study adds to the evidence that mental health disorders can have an acute impact on pregnancy‐related cardiovascular disease and specifically investigates whether the effect may be relevant across multiple mental health conditions, other than depression.

Despite the higher rate of cardiovascular disease in pregnant patients with mental health conditions, we also found that mental health conditions were associated with lower in‐hospital mortality rates, albeit in the setting of exceedingly low mortality rates overall. This paradoxical finding may reflect the variability of severity of mental health diagnoses included in our study. It is also possible that among patients with a host of other severe medical issues—the highest‐risk group overall—mental health conditions are systematically undercoded because they are less acutely relevant than the more severe comorbidities. This should be considered hypothesis‐generating and warrants replication in other data sets.

Compared with all pregnant patients, there was a greater fraction of White patients present in the pregnant population with mental health conditions. This is consistent with prior evidence that mental health conditions are underdiagnosed in people of color. 19 , 36 Moreover, given that Black birthing people in particular have a known markedly higher rate of SMM is more prevalent with pregnant patients of color, the underdiagnosis of people of color may understate the effect of mental health conditions on pregnancy calculated here.

When stratifying by mental health condition, we found that depression, anxiety, and PTSD were associated with significantly higher rates of cardiovascular conditions and complications as compared with those without. This builds upon previous evidence noting the association of depression and anxiety with preeclampsia, which is a major risk factor for the future development of cardiovascular disease and potentially a starting point for a causal pathway. 37 , 38 Given that depression, anxiety, and PTSD often coexist, when adjusting for other mental health conditions, we found that only depression and anxiety were associated with significantly higher rates of cardiovascular conditions and complications. Therefore, further work should examine whether there are differences between conditions that might shed useful light on mechanistic underpinnings. Although relationships for the other conditions did not meet statistical significance, the relatively small number of deliveries among people with other mental health conditions may have resulted in limited power to detect differences if they exist, and this should be explored further in the future.

There are potential clinical implications of our findings. For example, current guidelines suggest screening for mental health conditions during and after pregnancy; our findings further support this recommendation, both for conditions that are associated with the peripartum condition and those that may have been preexisting. 39 , 40 , 41 Further, our findings would suggest that patients with any mental health condition should potentially be screened for cardiac conditions in the peripartum period. Given that mental health conditions are associated with cardiac complications outside of pregnancy as well, pregnancy could serve as a dedicated checkpoint to screen a large population for mental health conditions, working to prevent cardiac events both within and outside of pregnancy. During the delivery hospitalization, it also may be appropriate to consider augmented monitoring or other interventions for people with known mental health diagnoses, though further research is needed to determine the appropriate level of monitoring and follow‐on intervention. Additionally, perinatal mental health conditions are considered one of the leading causes of maternal morbidity in the United States. 42 , 43 These data further emphasize the need to connect peripartum patients to mental health treatment; making treatment more accessible may be another way to reduce perinatal morbidity and mortality both from direct causes like suicide as well as indirect associations such as the cardiovascular conditions highlighted here.

A significant strength of this study was that it used a recent, nationally representative sample, capturing the variance in economic, racial, and insurance coverage in hospitalizations, with data included from almost all US hospitals. Given the breadth of the data, we were able to assess rare cardiovascular SMM conditions.

However, there are limitations associated with this study. One limitation is that our ascertainment of mental health diagnoses depends on coding; ICD‐10 codes may not capture all diagnoses, particularly if the disease is mild or does not affect patient care. Other comorbidities are often undercaptured in administrative databases. 44 , 45 , 46 , 47 Relatedly, the duration or severity of the mental health condition is unknown; mental health conditions that are well controlled before pregnancy versus those that are severe or treatment resistant may have differing impacts on outcomes, and we could not assess this possibility. We do not have access to clinical information like blood pressure or medication use, as well as certain nonclinical information lifestyle factors or behaviors, with which to evaluate any physiologic intermediaries to adverse outcomes. Additionally, although our primary outcome, cardiovascular SMM, is made up of rare events that are acute, our broader secondary outcome includes conditions such as valvular disease that likely predate pregnancy and are more accurately viewed as comorbidities than complications. Our mortality outcome is also limited to only in‐hospital mortality, and we are therefore unable to assess pregnancy‐associated mortality in the broader pregnancy and postpartum periods.

CONCLUSIONS

We found that mental health conditions were associated with adverse cardiovascular events during the delivery hospitalization among pregnant people receiving care in US hospitals. Screening for mental health diagnoses to identify a high‐risk population that warrants closer monitoring during and after delivery could represent an important first step in reducing adverse pregnancy outcomes.
