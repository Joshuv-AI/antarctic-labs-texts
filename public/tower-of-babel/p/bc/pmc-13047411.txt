Balance Impairment, Muscle Oxygenation, and Functional Exercise Capacity in Patients With Primary Ciliary Dyskinesia
Authors: Şeyma Mutlu Kayaarslan, Meral Boşnak Güçlü, Tuğba Şişmanlar Eyüboğlu, Ayşe Tana Aslan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and ObjectivesExtrapulmonary effects are observed in patients with primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD), however, few studies have examined these manifestations, particularly regarding balance and muscle oxygenation during exercise. This study aimed to compare balance, functional exercise capacity, muscle oxygenation, respiratory and peripheral muscle strength, and inspiratory muscle endurance in patients with PCD and healthy peers.MethodsTwenty‐seven patients with PCD and 26 healthy peers were included. Balance (Biodex Balance System and Y Balance Test), exercise capacity (6‐min walk test [6MWT]), muscle oxygenation (Moxy device), respiratory muscle strength (maximal inspiratory [MIP] and maximal expiratory [MEP]), inspiratory muscle endurance (incremental threshold loading test), and muscle strength (quadriceps femoris) were evaluated.ResultsThe Y balance test right and left lower extremity posteromedial and posterolateral scores and left lower extremity posteromedial and posterolateral reach distances, the 6MWT distance, and lower extremity muscle strength (%), were statistically lower in patients with PCD compared with healthy peers (p < 0.05). The change in average quadriceps femoris muscle oxygenation (ΔSmO2averaged) during the 6MWT was higher in patients with PCD (p < 0.05). MIP, MEP, inspiratory muscle endurance, postural stability indices, and other parameters of the Y balance test were similar between groups (p > 0.05).DiscussionIn patients with PCD, dynamic balance, functional exercise capacity, and lower extremity muscle strength are impaired, whereas respiratory muscle strength and static balance are preserved. Inspiratory muscle endurance was similarly decreased in both groups. A greater decrease in muscle oxygen saturation was observed during submaximal lower extremity activity in the patients. These findings indicate that, beyond traditional respiratory evaluations, balance and muscle oxygenation should be assessed in patients with PCD, and early assessment may guide rehabilitation strategies and future clinical research. Introduction

Recent studies have increasingly examined balance, postural control, and reaction time in individuals with respiratory diseases, highlighting that impairments in these domains are associated with decreased functional exercise capacity and peripheral muscle weakness [1, 2, 3]. The maintenance of balance control is essential for the proper execution of all daily living activities, and one of the most critical consequences of impaired body stability is the increased risk of falls [4]. Postural control is impaired in individuals with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and muscle weakness, physical inactivity, advanced age, and the need for supplemental oxygen contribute to this impairment [2]. Similarly, in individuals with primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD), balance may potentially be affected due to these underlying factors. A single study investigating balance in patients with PCD has shown that these patients experience balance and vestibular dysfunction, which is often not recognized by clinicians, families, or patients [5]. Evaluating balance in children using valid and reliable measurement methods is crucial to ensure safe mobility and the development of independence in functional activities [6].

The assessment of functional status and capacity is a crucial element in evaluating treatment efficacy and determining prognosis in individuals with cardiorespiratory disease [7]. The assessment of exercise capacity in patients with PCD has been addressed in a limited number of studies. These included the 6MWT, modified Shuttle Walk test, and cardiopulmonary exercise tests [8, 9, 10]. As the majority of activities of daily living are undertaken at submaximal effort levels, this test may better reflect the functional exercise level for daily physical activities [11].

The effects of inflammation on muscle function, blood gas abnormalities, poor oxygen delivery, electrolyte imbalances, medications, malnutrition, and comorbid conditions result in skeletal muscle abnormalities, which in turn lead to decreased functional capacity [12]. It remains unclear whether the observed reduction in functional capacity in patients with PCD is attributable to the effects on oxygen delivery and consumption during exercise. In one study on muscle oxygenation in patients with PCD, changes in muscle oxygenation were recorded during exercise tests before and after the rehabilitation program [13]. Evaluating muscle oxygenation during exercise may help to clarify the mechanisms underlying exercise limitation and guide rehabilitation strategies in this population.

The effects of chronic lung inflammation, systemic inflammatory mediators, decreased oxygen delivery, chronic respiratory acidosis, malnutrition, and various comorbidities may result in reduced respiratory muscle strength and endurance [12]. There has been limited research on respiratory muscle strength and endurance in children with PCD [8, 14, 15]. Respiratory muscle weakness is a significant contributor to the decline in lung function observed in children with chronic lung disease [16]. The inspiratory muscles require a submaximal contraction throughout life [17]. Respiratory muscle strength and endurance should be distinguished from respiratory function abnormalities and should be evaluated separately [18]. A limited number of studies have demonstrated that the peripheral muscles are weakened in patients with PCD [8, 9, 14]. In our previous study, children with PCD demonstrated impaired lung function, reduced upper extremity muscle strength, and lower levels of physical activity. Although upper extremity exercise capacity was preserved, resting muscle oxygen saturation in the upper extremities was lower compared with that of healthy children [19]. Although previous studies have examined respiratory and peripheral muscle strength and exercise capacity in patients with PCD, information on balance and on muscle oxygenation during exercise remains limited. It is still unclear whether the reduction in functional capacity observed in PCD is related to impaired oxygen delivery and utilization during exercise. Therefore, this study aimed to compare balance, functional exercise capacity, muscle oxygenation, respiratory and peripheral muscle strength, and inspiratory muscle endurance between patients with PCD and healthy peers.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

The cross‐sectional study was approved by the Gazi University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (No. 408, 19.04.2021) and performed according to the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients diagnosed with PCD at the Gazi University Department of Pediatric Chest Diseases between May 2021 and 2022, who were referred to the Gazi University Department of Cardiopulmonary Physiotherapy and Rehabilitation, were included in the study. During the COVID‐19 pandemic, volunteers involved in the study were incorporated within the framework of the COVID‐19 safety protocols. Each child and their family were fully informed about the study's purpose and scope before their involvement, and they signed the necessary forms to give their informed consent. Sample size analysis (G*Power 3.0.10 system) was performed using 6MWT distance literature data [14] with an effect size of 1.24, 95% power, and a 5% type 1 error, calculated to include at least 18 patients with PCD and 18 healthy peers.

Participants

According to the American Thoracic Society (ATS) and European Respiratory Society (ERS), diagnosing PCD in patients with typical clinical symptoms requires excluding other diseases, such as cystic fibrosis (CF) and immunodeficiency, and demonstrating results that are consistent with a PCD diagnosis through genetic testing or high‐speed video microscopy [20, 21, 22]. Patients with PCD aged 6–18 years whose clinical state was stable and who volunteered to participate were included in the PCD group. Patients who were uncooperative or had a history of exacerbations in the previous month, a decrease in FEV₁ of more than 10% in the last 6 months, neurological or orthopedic problems that would interfere with the assessments, pneumonia, any acute infection, or a history of smoking were excluded. Healthy, non‐smoking peers within the same age range as the patients were included in the study. Those who were uncooperative, had neurological or orthopedic disorders, acute pneumonia, or any acute infection during the assessments, or declined to participate were excluded.

Assessments

The pertinent data and pulmonary function test results were extracted from the patient's medical records. Static and dynamic balance, functional exercise capacity, muscle oxygenation, respiratory muscle strength, inspiratory muscle endurance, and peripheral muscle strength were evaluated.

Static Balance

Static balance was evaluated using the Biosway Portable Balance System (Biodex Medical Systems Incorporated, Shirley, New York). The test was repeated three times with a 20‐s test duration, and a 10‐s inter‐evaluation period was permitted. The medial/lateral stability index (MLSI), anterior/posterior stability index (APSI), general stability index, and standard deviations obtained from the device were recorded for analysis. Lower values indicate better balance, whereas higher values reflect poorer balance performance.

Dynamic Balance

Dynamic balance was assessed using the Y‐balance test, repeated three times for both lower extremities in the anterior, posteromedial, and posterolateral directions. During the test, participants maintained a single‐leg stance while reaching maximally in each direction with the contralateral extremity. To normalize for differences in lower extremity length, reach distances were divided by leg length and multiplied by 100. The test was deemed unsuccessful and was repeated if participants could not maintain their balance, could not keep their reaching foot in contact with the indicator, or changed their stance foot [23]. In the Y Balance Test, greater reach distances indicate better dynamic balance.

Functional Exercise Capacity

Functional exercise capacity was assessed using the 6MWT according to ATS criteria [11]. The test was performed twice, with individuals resting for 30 min between tests. Before the tests, the participants' heart rate (HR), oxygen saturation (SpO₂), blood pressure (BP), breathing frequency, leg fatigue, overall fatigue, and dyspnea (Modified Borg scale) were recorded. These evaluations were repeated after the test and following a 1‐min recovery. The longest distance was recorded in meters (m) for analysis and calculated as a percentage of the expected [24].

Muscle Oxygenation

Muscle oxygenation (SmO2) was assessed using the near‐infrared spectroscopy device (Moxy, Fortiori Design LLC, Minnesota, USA) [25]. During the 6MWT, the device was placed on the QF muscle of the dominant leg, and measurements were recorded during the resting and recovery periods. Resting muscle oxygenation (SmO2rest) and total hemoglobin (THb) were recorded. Following a 1‐min recovery, total hemoglobin (THb) and the average of SmO₂ (SmO₂averaged) were recorded. Throughout the tests, the minimum and maximum values of SmO₂, SmO₂averaged, and THb (SmO2min, SmO2max, SmO2, SmO2averaged‐min, SmO2averaged‐max, SmO2averaged, THbmin, THbmax, THb) were recorded and analyzed.

Respiratory Muscle Strength

Respiratory muscle strength was assessed according to ATS/ERS criteria [26]. Maximum inspiratory (MIP) and maximum expiratory pressure (MEP) were measured using a mouth pressure device (Micro Medical MicroRM, England), and the results were recorded as cmH2O. The highest value of at least seven acceptable measurements was selected for the analysis. The measurements were expressed as a percentage; values below 80% of the predicted value were considered indicative of muscle weakness [27].

Inspiratory Muscle Endurance

Inspiratory muscle endurance was assessed using an inspiratory muscle training device (POWERbreathe, IMT Technologies Ltd., Birmingham, UK) in an incremental threshold‐loading test. The threshold loading began at 20% of the measured MIP and increased by 20% every 2 min. The test was terminated when participants felt fatigued, experienced breathlessness, or were unable to continue. HR and SpO₂ were measured before and after the test. The final result was the level achieved as a percentage of MIP maintained for at least 1 min [28].

Peripheral Muscle Strength

Quadriceps femoris (QF) muscle strength was assessed using a hand dynamometer (JTECH Power Track Commander, Baltimore, USA). Measurements were performed in a seated position with the knee fully extended, and each assessment was repeated three times on both the right and left sides. The highest value was selected for analysis and expressed as a percentage of the expected value based on age and sex [29].

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the Windows‐based SPSS 22 statistical analysis program. Visual and analytical methods (Shapiro‐Wilk test) were employed to assess the normal distribution of the variables. The Student t‐test, Mann‐Whitney U test, and Chi‐square test were utilized to compare normally distributed variables, non‐normally distributed variables, and nominal data, respectively. Descriptive statistics were used to express variables, including mean, standard deviation (SD), mean difference, lower and upper limits of the 95% confidence interval (95% CI), median, interquartile range (IQR 25%–75%), U value, frequency (n), and percentage (%). A post hoc power analysis was performed using the 6MWT and dynamic balance values. The relationship between dynamic balance outcomes and respiratory muscle strength, inspiratory muscle endurance, quadriceps muscle strength, and functional exercise capacity was assessed using Spearman's and Pearson's correlation coefficients, depending on data distribution. The significance level was set at p ≤ 0.05.

Results

In this study, 36 patients with PCD were included, and 27 healthy peers were matched. Seven patients refused to participate in the study, one had a history of COVID‐19, and one was unable to complete their evaluation due to exacerbation (Figure 1). As a result, 27 patients (14.11 ± 3.24 y, 13 F) and 26 healthy children (13.19 ± 3.84 y, 10 F) were included in the analyses. Patients with PCD had lower height, weight, and body mass index (BMI) z scores (p < 0.05, Table 1); prenatal exposure to cigarette smoke was significantly higher (p < 0.05), while passive smoking exposure and aerobic exercise habit rates were similar (p > 0.05). Activity‐related dyspnea was significantly greater in patients with PCD than in healthy peers (p < 0.05).

As shown in Table 2, the other parameters of muscle oxygenation, except ΔSmO2averaged (%), were similar in both groups (p > 0.05). Patients with PCD had significantly higher ΔSmO2averaged (%) values than healthy peers (p < 0.05, Table 3, Figure 2).

The MIP, MIP%, MEP, and MEP% of patients with PCD and healthy peers were statistically similar (p > 0.05). Among patients with PCD, inspiratory and expiratory muscle weakness were observed in 14.8% and 48.1% of the participants, respectively. Inspiratory muscle endurance, resting HR, resting dyspnea, ΔSpO2, and dyspnea of patients with PCD and healthy peers were statistically similar (p > 0.05, Table 4). The SpO2 (resting) of patients was statistically significantly lower than that of healthy children, and the ΔHR of healthy children was higher than that of patients (p < 0.05). Quadriceps muscle strength (%) was significantly lower in patients with PCD (p < 0.05, Table 4). A significant relationship was prevalent between dynamic balance outcomes and inspiratory muscle endurance, quadriceps muscle strength, and functional exercise capacity in patients with PCD. Posteromedial and posterolateral reach distances were positively correlated with inspiratory muscle endurance (%) and 6MWT distance (p < 0.05). In addition, several dynamic balance parameters showed significant positive correlations with quadriceps strength on both sides (p < 0.05, Supporting 1).

Discussion

The main results of our study indicated impaired dynamic balance, reduced functional exercise capacity, and decreased lower extremity muscle strength in patients with PCD. Respiratory muscle strength and static balance were preserved in patients with PCD. The patients with PCD and healthy peers exhibited similarly decreased inspiratory muscle endurance. A greater decrease in muscle oxygen saturation was observed during submaximal lower extremity activity in the patient group. These findings suggest that, beyond the known reductions in exercise capacity and respiratory and peripheral muscle strength, patients with PCD also have impaired dynamic balance and altered muscle oxygenation during activity. The inclusion of muscle oxygenation assessment contributes to a more comprehensive evaluation of peripheral muscle function in this population.

A few studies have investigated balance in patients with PCD. Zawawi et al. [5] evaluated the balance of patients with PCD using a one‐legged stance test with eyes open and closed. It has been reported that patients showed low performance in the one‐leg stand test, and as a result, the prevalence of vestibular and balance dysfunction may be high in children with PCD. A study of peripheral vestibular dysfunction in adult patients with PCD found that 60% exhibited reduced otolytic function at both the saccular and utricular levels [30]. Notably, despite these abnormalities, none reported issues with basic postural stability.

Postural control relies on the coordinated activity of the somatosensory, vestibular, and visual systems to maintain balance in daily activities [31]. Postural control is known to be reduced in individuals with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and the factors causing this are muscle weakness, physical inactivity, advanced age, and the need for additional oxygen [2]. Our study demonstrated that static balance was preserved in patients with PCD. This preserved balance may decline with age; therefore, they require regular monitoring. In contrast to static balance, patients' dynamic balance was affected. Dik et al. [3] evaluated reaction times and dynamic balance in children with CF and non‐CF bronchiectasis. Results indicated that patients exhibited longer reaction times and poorer dynamic balance compared to their peers. Lima et al. [1] demonstrated that weakened QF muscles in adults with CF lead to balance disorders. In our study, the reduced QF muscle in patients may have affected their dynamic balance. Respiratory muscles contribute not only to respiratory function but also to postural control [32]. In addition to group comparisons, correlation analyses provided further insights into the factors associated with balance performance in children with PCD. Similar to previous studies reporting that peripheral muscle weakness contributes to impaired balance in CF and non‐CF bronchiectasis [1, 3], our findings showed that dynamic balance was positively associated with quadriceps strength, underscoring the importance of lower extremity muscle strength in maintaining balance during movement. The moderate correlations between inspiratory muscle endurance and dynamic balance further support the idea that inspiratory muscle endurance may influence dynamic postural control, consistent with previous literature on the role of respiratory mechanics in balance [32]. Furthermore, the link between functional exercise capacity and dynamic balance aligns with studies showing that reduced aerobic performance is connected to poorer balance outcomes in chronic respiratory diseases [2]. The weak correlations between respiratory muscle strength and balance outcomes suggest that endurance‐related respiratory features, rather than maximal force, may more strongly influence dynamic postural control. These results emphasize the importance of focusing on inspiratory muscle endurance and lower‐extremity strength in rehabilitation to enhance balance and mobility in children with PCD.

The results of our study indicated that patients with PCD exhibited a decrease in their functional exercise capacity. Additionally, 74.1% of patients and 65.4% of healthy peers walked less than 80% of the predicted 6MWT distance. In the literature, results of studies evaluating functional exercise capacity in patients with PCD using the 6MWT vary. Denizoglu‐Kulli et al. [8] reported that the functional exercise capacity of patients with PCD was similar to that of controls, whereas Firat et al. [14] reported significantly decreased functional exercise capacity, consistent with our findings. Simsek et al. [9] and Sonbahar‐Ulu et al. [33] demonstrated a reduction in maximal aerobic exercise capacity using the modified shuttle walk test. Valerio et al. [10] showed a decrease in peak VO₂ during cardiopulmonary exercise testing. Oxygen consumption in patients with impaired lung function is significantly reduced, while oxygen consumption in patients with preserved lung function is reported to be normal. In our study, the patients exhibited lower resting SpO₂ values, and the decrease during the test was more pronounced; however, only two patients desaturated. In contrast, Firat et al. [14] reported that the SpO2 change was similar to that of healthy peers. Functional exercise capacity may deteriorate as a result of declining respiratory function. Wells et al. [34] showed that although the exercise capacities of PCD, CF, and healthy groups were similar, patients with PCD had the lowest results. Additionally, reduced muscle strength in patients with PCD has been associated with lower exercise capacity. It has been shown that adolescent and adult CF patients with decreased lower extremity muscle strength have a shorter 6MWT walking distance [35]. Body weight within the normal range has been demonstrated to be associated with better aerobic performance in healthy [36]. Reduced QF muscle strength, along with poor height, body weight, and BMI z scores, may have contributed to the decreased functional capacity in our patients. In addition, developmental factors such as gender, puberty‐related changes in lean body mass and body fat, hemoglobin concentration, and hormonal status may also influence decreased aerobic performance in patients with PCD [9]. Given that PCD is a chronic disease, it is essential to assess patients' exercise capacity regularly.

Peripheral muscle tissue oxygenation is associated with functional capacity in patients with chronic lung disease [37]. In our study, the oxygen saturation of the QF muscle and total hemoglobin levels were measured in PCD and healthy children at rest, during the 6MWT, and during recovery. During the test, SmO2averaged change values were higher in children with PCD. In the literature, a study examining the effects of active video games on patients with PCD evaluated muscle oxygenation during the Modified Shuttle Walk test and the Glittre Daily Living Activities Test [13]. The muscle oxygen saturations were shown to increase after treatment. Another study used magnetic resonance spectroscopy to assess muscle metabolism in patients with PCD, demonstrating delayed phosphocreatine recovery in skeletal muscle, indicating oxidative dysfunction [34]. There may be many reasons why patients show greater changes in muscle oxygen saturation during the test. Coelho et al. [37] evaluated the exercise capacity of CF using the Modified Shuttle Walk test. They observed that patients had lower functional capacity to produce energy during the exercise test, and that oxygen consumption could increase due to decreased metabolic efficiency. The elevated SmO₂averaged change observed in patients may be attributed to reduced functional exercise capacity and metabolic efficiency. This could lead to increased muscle oxygen consumption and, consequently, greater fluctuations in muscle oxygenation during activity.

Patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and respiratory muscle weakness demonstrate a heightened perception of dyspnea and reduced exercise capacity [38]. The results of studies investigating respiratory muscle strength in patients with PCD are contradictory in the literature. Denizoglu‐Kulli et al. [8] reported that inspiratory muscle strength was similar to that of healthy individuals, whereas expiratory muscle strength had decreased. In contrast to the findings of our study, Sonbahar‐Ulu et al. [33], demonstrated that patients had decreased inspiratory and expiratory muscle strength. Firat et al. [14] showed that although inspiratory muscle strength was within normal values, it was lower than in controls, while expiratory muscle strength was within normal limits. Although our results indicated that inspiratory and expiratory muscle strength in children with PCD were within the normal range, 14.8% of patients had inspiratory and 48.1% had expiratory muscle weakness. Even in patients with preserved respiratory muscle strength and endurance, respiratory muscle weakness may still occur due to increased respiratory workload, chronic inflammation, or airway obstruction.

There is limited research in the literature assessing inspiratory muscle endurance in patients with PCD, with one study reporting decreased endurance [14] and another indicating that it was preserved [15]. The inspiratory muscles are required to perform submaximal contractions throughout life. Therefore, endurance assessment may provide a more functional indicator of respiratory performance than maximal strength alone [17]. In the present study, inspiratory muscle endurance was reduced in patients with PCD; however, no significant difference was observed between groups. Our patients also reported a higher perception of exertional dyspnea. Previous research in CF has shown that inspiratory muscle endurance may be a relevant parameter for evaluating exertional dyspnea and respiratory muscle efficiency [39]. Nevertheless, an increased respiratory workload may lead to the development of inspiratory muscle endurance over time through endurance training [40]. Evaluating inspiratory muscle endurance is crucial to gain insight into respiratory muscle function [18]. The relationship between inspiratory muscle endurance and respiratory functions, respiratory force, exercise capacity, airway obstruction, and nutrition in patients with PCD remains to be elucidated.

Skeletal muscle abnormalities, including inflammation, blood gas abnormalities, low oxygen delivery, electrolyte imbalances, and the effects of medications, malnutrition, and comorbidities, lead to functional loss in chronic lung disease [12]. Few studies in the literature have explored muscle strength in patients with PCD [8, 9, 14, 15]. The findings of our study are consistent with the existing literature and demonstrate a reduction in knee extensor muscle strength in patients. Specifically, right knee extensor strength was below 80% of the predicted value in 37% of the children, and left knee extensor strength was below this threshold in 33.3% of the children. It has been shown that, in adolescent and adult CF patients, as lower extremity muscle strength increases, respiratory function improves, and as QF strength increases, BMI is higher [35]. Reduced peripheral muscle strength in children with PCD may be related to reduced respiratory function and lower BMI z‐scores.

Conclusions

The primary findings of our study were that patients with PCD had impaired dynamic balance, decreased lower‐extremity muscle strength, and reduced functional exercise capacity. A greater decrease in muscle oxygen saturation was observed during submaximal lower extremity activity in patients with PCD. In contrast, respiratory muscle strength and static balance were preserved. Patients with PCD and healthy peers exhibited similar decreases in inspiratory muscle endurance. Incorporating balance evaluations into pulmonary rehabilitation programs is crucial to ensure safe mobilization and the development of independence in functional activities for patients with PCD. Future research should further explore the mechanisms of balance and muscle oxygenation in this population and their role in pulmonary rehabilitation.

Limitations

During the COVID‐19 pandemic, our study was conducted. Due to the pandemic and the start of the school year, some healthy children were reluctant to participate in our research. Additionally, restrictions during the pandemic led to a decrease in physical activity levels among children, which may have resulted in greater impairments in outcomes. Although the PCD is a rare disease, the sample size was calculated to include a larger number of patients. However, the power for the primary outcome was 59%, and for dynamic balance, it ranged from 63% to 68%. Therefore, the sample size may not be sufficient for some study outcomes.
