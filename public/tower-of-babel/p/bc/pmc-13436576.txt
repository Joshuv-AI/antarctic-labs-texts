Topical Glycolysis Inhibition Restores MAVS-Associated Antiviral Signaling in Herpes Simplex Keratitis
Authors: Wei Xu, Huiru Liu, Zihan Cheng, Qixin Li, Xiaoqing Wu, Zesheng He, Decheng Xiao, Dan Jiang, Wei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeHerpes simplex keratitis (HSK) is a leading cause of infectious corneal blindness. Host antiviral responses, particularly type I interferon (IFN) signaling, are impaired during corneal herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) infection, partly due to viral immune evasion and metabolic reprogramming. This study investigated if topical glycolysis inhibition restores mitochondrial antiviral signaling (MAVS)-associated innate antiviral signaling and improves HSK outcomes.MethodsIn vitro and in vivo infection models were established using the HSV-1 McKrae strain. A stable hexokinase 2 (HK2)-knockdown human corneal epithelial cell (HCEC) line was generated using lentivirus. Protein/mRNA levels were analyzed via western blot (WB), immunofluorescence (IF), enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR). Protein interactions were confirmed by co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP). Murine corneal pathology and viral proteins were assessed by slit-lamp imaging, IF, and WB.ResultsElevated HK2 expression was detected in HSK models. HK2 knockdown or 2-deoxy-d-glucose (2-DG)-mediated inhibition reduced HSV-1 replication (lower infected cell protein 0 [ICP0], higher interferon-stimulated gene 56 [ISG56]). Co-IP confirmed the interaction between HK2 and MAVS protein, and HK2 inhibition activated the TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1)/interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) pathway. Topical application of 2-DG improved murine corneal epithelial integrity, reduced viral proteins (glycoprotein D, ICP0), and showed efficacy comparable to acyclovir.ConclusionsTopical glycolysis inhibition enhances corneal antiviral defenses via MAVS-mediated type I IFN signaling, controlling HSV-1 and alleviating disease. It reveals a novel corneal metabolism–antiviral immunity link, highlights HK2 as a therapeutic target for HSK, and supports metabolic therapies for HSK and ocular herpes virus infections.

Herpes simplex keratitis (HSK), mainly caused by herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1), is a major cause of infectious blindness worldwide.1–3 It is characterized by recurrent viral reactivation in the trigeminal ganglion and immunoinflammatory damage to the cornea. Despite available antivirals such as acyclovir, HSK frequently results in corneal scarring and vision loss. This occurs due to an inadequate early antiviral immune response combined with an excessive delayed inflammatory reaction.4–6 Achieving a balance between viral clearance and limitation of immunopathology remains challenging. Thus, developing novel strategies that enhance antiviral defenses and mitigate tissue damage is necessary.

The innate immune system of the cornea plays a critical role in resisting HSV-1 infection. This defense primarily involves type I interferon (IFN) production initiated by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), including Toll-like receptor 3 (TLR3) and RIG-I-like receptors (RLRs).7–9 These receptors recognize viral components and activate antiviral signaling pathways; however, HSV-1 has evolved strategies to evade these responses. The virus encodes proteins that disrupt PRR signaling, suppressing the interferon response and facilitating viral replication.10,11 Consequently, chronic inflammation and corneal scarring occur. HSV-1 infection also alters host-cell metabolism by promoting aerobic glycolysis, which enhances viral replication.12–14 This metabolic shift increases lactate production, potentially suppressing antiviral immune responses.13,14 Recent evidence indicates that lactate directly inhibits the mitochondrial antiviral signaling (MAVS) protein pathway,15 an essential mediator of RLR-driven immune responses. These findings suggest that metabolic reprogramming contributes to immune suppression during HSV-1 infection.

Our recent studies revealed that targeting metabolic enzymes, particularly hexokinase 2 (HK2), could represent a promising therapeutic strategy.16 HK2 participates in glycolysis and negatively regulates MAVS activation, thereby impairing antiviral immunity. By inhibiting HK2, we can deprive the virus of energy and strengthen antiviral immune responses simultaneously. Other studies on glycolysis and viruses have also demonstrated the crucial role of HK2.17,18 Systemic metabolic therapy with 2-deoxy-d-glucose (2-DG) during early replicative phases of HSV can worsen outcomes and risk encephalitis in mice, as shown by Rouse's group.19 Accordingly, we used short-course topical dosing initiated at 3 days post-infection to avoid early high-replication windows while targeting immunopathology.20 Our current investigation further explores how HK2 inhibition restores antiviral immune signaling in the cornea, offering potential new therapeutic avenues for HSK management.

Materials and Methods

Ethics Statement

All experimental procedures involving mice were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Wenzhou Medical University and performed strictly according to the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research.

Cells and Viruses

Human corneal epithelial cells (HCECs; American Type Culture Collection, Manassas, VA, USA) were cultured in Gibco Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F-12) medium (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Primary HCECs were isolated and cultured according to a previously described protocol.21 Vero cells (FengHui Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Hunan, China) were cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS. HSV-1 (McKrae strain) was obtained from Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (Shanghai, China) and were amplified and titrated using Vero cells.

Establishment of HSK Models

In Vitro Cellular Model

An in vitro model of HSK was established using cultured cells. When the cell confluence in the culture plate reached 80%, the medium was replaced with the corresponding serum-free medium. HSV-1 was then added according to the predetermined cell density and viral titer to achieve a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1.

In Vivo Animal Model

Female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Zhejiang Laboratory Animal Center (Zhejiang, China). Mice were randomly assigned to different groups (uninfected control, sham-infected, HSV-1+PBS, HSV-1+2-DG, and HSV-1 + acyclovir (ACV); these group labels are used consistently across figures and legends. The mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 2,2,2-tribromoethanol (Avertin, 250 mg/kg). Under stereomicroscope guidance, the central corneal epithelium was gently scarified in a 3 × 3 grid pattern using a 30-gauge needle. Immediately after scarification, 5 µL PBS containing HSV-1 (1 × 106 plaque-forming units) was applied to the ocular surface.

Topical HK2 Inhibitor Treatment

On day 3 post-infection, mice were randomly divided into three treatment groups: phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; vehicle), 5-mM 2-DG treatment, and ACV treatment. All interventions were applied topically three times daily. Each dose was 5 µL/eye prepared in sterile PBS (pH 7.4, preservative free); dosing was three times a day at ∼8-hour intervals. During treatment, mice were gently restrained without anesthesia, and their eyelids were manually held closed for 30 seconds immediately following application to enhance drug retention. Corneal tissues were harvested on day 6 for further analyses.

Western Blot

Cells were lysed using radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (P0013B; Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and protein concentrations were measured using the Bradford assay (P0006C; Beyotime). Proteins were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (10600002; GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Chicago, IL, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies. Primary antibodies included HK2 (1:1000, 66974-1-Ig; Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA), infected cell protein 0 (ICP0; 1:1000, ab6513; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), β-actin (1:1000, AF5003; Beyotime), glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH; 1:1000, 5174S; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), voltage-dependent anion channel 1 (VDAC1; 1:1000, 55259-1-AP; Proteintech), TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1; 1:1000, 3013S; Cell Signaling Technology), p-TBK1 (1:1000, 5483T; Cell Signaling Technology), interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3; 1:1000, 4302S; Cell Signaling Technology), p-IRF3 (1:1000, 4947S; Cell Signaling Technology), retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I; 1:1000, 67556-1-Ig; Proteintech), MAVS (1:1000, 81910-1-RR; Proteintech), and stimulator of interferon genes (STING; 1:1000, 50494S; Cell Signaling Technology). Following incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000) at 37°C for 1 hour, signals were visualized using an Odyssey CLx (LI-COR, Lincoln, NE, USA) or an Amersham Imager AI680 system (GE Healthcare Life Sciences) and quantified by ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Co-Immunoprecipitation Assay

Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) were first equilibrated with PBS containing 0.5% Tween 20 (PBST). For antibody coupling, the pre-washed beads were incubated with specific primary antibodies (or control immunoglobulin G [IgG]) at 4°C for 2 to 4 hours on a rotator. After magnetic separation and washing, the antibody-bound beads were incubated with 200 µg of cell lysate supplemented with 5 µL of phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride at 4°C overnight to capture the target antigen. Following extensive washing with PBST to remove non-specific binding, immunocomplexes were eluted by adding 50 µL of 1× loading buffer and boiling at 95°C for 5 minutes. The supernatants were subsequently collected after high-speed centrifugation and subjected to western blot (WB) analysis.

Real-Time PCR

Total RNA was isolated from HCECs or primary HCEC samples using RNA lysis buffer (79216; QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). Reverse transcription was performed with M-MLV Reverse Transcriptase (M1701; Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Real-time PCR was conducted using an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Q711-03; Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Primers were designed by Tsingke Biotechnology (Beijing, China); detailed sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Immunofluorescent Staining

Cultured HCECs

HCECs were seeded onto coverslips in 24-well plates. Cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), and permeabilized using 0.1% Triton X-100 (T8787; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). After blocking with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 hour at room temperature (RT), samples were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer. Secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit and anti-mouse IgG, 1:300; Invitrogen) were applied for 1 hour in the dark, followed by nuclear staining with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 1:1000; Invitrogen). Coverslips were mounted and imaged with a confocal microscope (LSM 880; ZEISS Microscopy, Jena, Germany).

Corneal Frozen Sections

Frozen sections were equilibrated to RT, and samples with intact corneal morphology were selected under a stereomicroscope. Sections were washed in PBS (50 rpm, 10 minutes), fixed with 4% PFA for 15 minutes, and permeabilized with 0.04% Triton X-100 for 15 minutes at RT. After three PBS washes, nonspecific binding was blocked using 10% goat serum (45 minutes, RT). Primary antibodies were applied overnight at 4°C. After additional PBS washes, sections were incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:300, 1.5 hours, RT, dark). DAPI staining and confocal microscopy were performed as described above.

Mitochondrial Isolation

Mitochondria were isolated using a commercial mitochondrial isolation kit (89874; Invitrogen) following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, harvested cells were homogenized in ice-cold isolation buffer with a Dounce homogenizer (357538; DWK Life Sciences, Millville, NJ, USA) for 30 gentle strokes. Samples underwent sequential centrifugation at 1000g for 5 minutes at 4°C to remove nuclei and debris, and at 12,000g for 15 minutes at 4°C to pellet mitochondria. The cytosolic fraction (supernatant) was retained for subsequent analyses.

Hexokinase Activity and Lactate Content Measurement

Hexokinase activity was assessed using a commercial kit (ab136957; Abcam). Following the kit protocol, cells were homogenized in ice-cold Assay Buffer LX (ab323982; Abcam) and centrifuged to obtain a clear lysate. The assay was performed by incubating samples with a specific reaction mix, and absorbance at 450 nm was recorded. Activity was quantified against a standard curve generated with the provided nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) standards.

Cellular lactate levels were measured with an assay kit (BC2235; Solarbio, Beijing, China). Briefly, cells were lysed, and the supernatant was deproteinized. After a series of enzymatic reactions according to the manufacturer's instructions, the final product was dissolved in ethanol, and the absorbance was read at 570 nm. Lactate concentration was determined from a standard curve and expressed relative to the cell count.

IFN-β Protein Quantification

IFN-β protein levels in cell culture supernatants were measured using an enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit according to the manufacturer's protocol. Clarified samples were incubated in antibody-coated wells at 37°C, followed by sequential additions of biotin-conjugated antibody, enzyme conjugate, and 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate. The reaction was terminated with stop solution, and optical density was read at 450 nm to determine concentrations.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). Normally distributed data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA or unpaired Student's t-tests, and nonparametric data were assessed by Kruskal–Wallis or Mann–Whitney U tests. All experiments included at least four independent biological replicates, and the data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

HK2 Expression and Glycolytic Activity Are Elevated in HSV-1–Infected Corneas

HSV-1 infection significantly elevated HK2 protein expression in both HCECs and primary human corneal epithelial cells (pHCECs) (Figs. 1A–D). This elevation was accompanied by markedly enhanced hexokinase activity and lactate production (Figs. 1E, 1F), indicating an upregulation of glycolysis. Subcellular fractionation revealed a time-dependent accumulation of HK2 in mitochondria at 6 and 12 hours post-infection (hpi), an increase in proportion to mitochondrial load control VDAC1, with increased mitochondrial localization evidenced by co-localization with mitochondrial outer membrane marker translocase of outer mitochondrial membrane 20 (TOM20) (Figs. 1G–I).

(E, F) Quantitative analyses of hexokinase activity and lactate concentrations in primary HCECs at indicated infection time points. (G) HK2 protein localization in mitochondrial and cytoplasmic fractions of infected HCECs. (H, I) Confocal IF images and colocalization analysis of HK2 (red) and TOM20 (green), including line-scan intensity profiles (H) and Pearson's correlation coefficients (I). Scale bar: 20 µm. Data are reported as mean ± SD from at least four independent experiments. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.

In the murine HSK model, corneal HK2 expression was persistently elevated from day 1 to 7 post-infection (Figs. 2A, 2B), primarily within the epithelial layers (Fig. 2C). Corneal lactate levels were significantly increased by day 7 post-infection compared with sham-operated or naïve controls (Fig. 2D), further confirming glycolytic activation in infected corneas. In contrast, sham-operated mice exhibited only transient increases in HK2 expression, which returned to baseline during corneal healing (Supplementary Fig. S1). These data collectively demonstrate that HSV-1 infection induces robust metabolic reprogramming mediated by HK2 during ocular infection.

(D) Quantitative analysis of corneal lactate levels among different groups at day 7 post-infection (n = 5; mean ± SD). *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001; ns, no significance.

HK2 Suppression Enhances Antiviral Responses Through Activation of the IFN-β Pathway

To evaluate the functional significance of HK2, we generated corneal epithelial cells with stable HK2 knockdown via lentivirus-mediated shRNA (Figs. 3A, 3B; Supplementary Fig. S2A). The shHK2-3 clone was selected as the representative model for subsequent analyses. Transcriptomic sequencing and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) indicated reduced activity of the HSV-1 infection-related pathway following HK2 knockdown (Fig. 3C). The raw sequence data have been deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive (GSA-Human: HRA017344). Consistent with these observations, ICP0 protein expression decreased markedly in the shHK2 group (Fig. S2B), accompanied by significant upregulation of IFN-β and interferon-stimulated gene 56 (ISG56) transcripts during HSV-1 infection (Figs. 3D, 3E). Importantly, this heightened transcriptional response corresponded with a substantial elevation in IFN-β protein levels, with the shHK2+HSV group exhibiting the most pronounced increase (Fig. 3F). Pretreatment with 5 mM of the glycolysis inhibitor 2-DG for 6 hours produced similar results, suppressing ICP0 (Supplementary Fig. S2C). Collectively, these complementary experimental approaches indicate that HK2 suppression promotes antiviral defense through the activation of IFN-β signaling.

(C) GSEA comparing enrichment of the HSV-1 infection pathway between the shHK2+HSV and vector+HSV groups. (D, E) mRNA expression of IFN-β and ISG56. (F) ELISA analysis of IFN-β secretion. Data are reported as mean ± SD from at least four independent replicates. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001; ns, no significance.

HK2 Suppression Promotes MAVS Expression and Enhances its Mitochondrial Localization, Amplifying IFN-β Signaling

Transcriptome sequencing of cells with HK2 silencing revealed increased MAVS expression within the HSV-1 infection pathway (Fig. 4A). Quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) validated increased MAVS mRNA (Fig. 4B), and WB analysis showed that MAVS protein levels were moderately higher in the shHK2+HSV group compared to the HSV-1 infected control (P < 0.05) (Figs. 4C, 4D). Furthermore, treatment of primary corneal epithelial cells with 2-DG also resulted in enhanced MAVS expression following HSV-1 infection (Figs. 4E, 4F).

HK2 inhibition upregulates MAVS expression. (A) Heatmap illustrating expression of key genes in the HSV-1 infection pathway. (B) The qRT-PCR analysis of MAVS mRNA in HK2 knockdown cells ± HSV-1 infection. (C, D) WB analysis and quantitative assessment of MAVS protein expression in HK2 knockdown HCECs. (E, F) WB analysis and quantification of MAVS protein levels in primary HCECs treated with 2-DG. Data are reported as mean ± SD from at least four independent experiments. *P < 0.05; ns, no significance.

To investigate whether MAVS mediates HK2-dependent IFN-β signaling, co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and immunofluorescence (IF) assays were performed. Immunoprecipitation using anti-HK2 antibody successfully pulled down MAVS, whereas no MAVS signal was detected with IgG controls (Fig. 5A). Reciprocal Co-IP using anti-MAVS antibody also confirmed the presence of HK2 in MAVS complexes (Fig. 5B). IF analysis showed increased MAVS fluorescence intensity and enhanced mitochondrial colocalization (TOM20 marker) in HK2 knockdown HSV-1–infected cells compared to vector controls (Figs. 5C, 5D). These data suggest a physical association between HK2 and MAVS and indicate that inhibiting HK2 promotes MAVS expression and its mitochondrial localization.

(C, D) Confocal IF microscopy of MAVS (red) and TOM20 (green), with co-localization analysis (right) and Pearson's correlation coefficients. Scale bar: 20 µm.

HK2 Suppression Activates the TBK1/IRF3 Pathway to Enhance Antiviral Responses

Given the crucial role of MAVS in antiviral signaling initiation, we next investigated whether suppression of HK2 influences downstream TBK1/IRF3 activation. In corneal epithelial cells infected with HSV-1, genetic (shHK2) or pharmacological (2-DG) inhibition of HK2 significantly enhanced phosphorylation of TBK1 and IRF3 (Fig. 6). This enhanced phosphorylation corresponded with increased expression of ISG56, aligning closely with the previously observed MAVS-mediated upregulation of IFN-β.

(D–F) WB analysis and quantitative data of phosphorylated TBK1 and IRF3 in primary HCECs treated with 2-DG. Data are reported as mean ± SD from at least four independent experiments. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001; ns, no significance.

Topical Inhibition of HK2 Reduces Corneal HSV-1 Replication and Disease Severity

To validate therapeutic efficacy based on the identified regulatory effects of HK2 on MAVS‑associated TBK1/IRF3 signaling, further experiments were conducted in vivo. Three days post-infection, mice received topical eye-drop treatments of PBS (vehicle), 2-DG, or ACV. Corneal fluorescein staining demonstrated improved corneal epithelial integrity in both the 2-DG and ACV treatment groups (Figs. 78B, 7C). IF assays revealed significantly reduced expression of HK2 and HSV-1 glycoprotein D (gD) in mouse corneas treated with 2-DG (Figs. 7D, 7E). WB analyses further confirmed that 2-DG treatment suppressed corneal HK2 and HSV-1 ICP0 protein expression to an extent comparable to ACV (P < 0.01) (Figs. 7F–H). ACV primarily targets viral DNA polymerase after phosphorylation by viral thymidine kinase and is not expected to directly modulate host glycolysis/HK2 expression, explaining the maintained HK2 levels despite ocular surface benefit. Collectively, these findings indicate that targeted HK2 inhibition effectively enhances corneal epithelial recovery and reduces clinical severity in murine models of HSK.

Schematic illustration. HSV-1 infection induces glycolytic metabolic reprogramming, leading to elevated HK2 activity, increased glucose conversion to lactate, and suppression of MAVS‑associated antiviral signaling. In contrast, HK2 inhibition restores MAVS-mediated signaling, enhances phosphorylation of TBK1/IRF3, and robustly increases IFN-β production, effectively restricting viral replication. This dual regulatory mechanism positions HK2 as a critical metabolic–immune checkpoint in HSV-1 infection.

Discussion

HSK exemplifies a disease state where the interplay among viral infection, immune pathology, and metabolic reprogramming defines the fate of the ocular surface. To our knowledge in the corneal HSV-1 model, we provide evidence that topical glycolysis inhibition restores MAVS-mediated antiviral signaling, extending prior systemic or non-ocular observations. Specifically, our data indicate that topical inhibition of HK2, a pivotal glycolytic enzyme, reactivates the MAVS-dependent IFN pathway, thereby enhancing viral clearance and simultaneously reducing corneal immunopathology. This study reports that, in ocular viral diseases, targeting a metabolic enzyme directly reinstates a defined immune signaling cascade, highlighting a potential shift from traditional antiviral therapies.

Our findings align with the expanding paradigm of immunometabolism, which proposes that cellular metabolic states critically influence immune function. Viruses are increasingly recognized to actively reprogram host glucose metabolism, particularly by enhancing glycolysis, to secure resources necessary for optimal replication.22,23 This metabolic hijacking simultaneously dampens host antiviral responses, fostering viral persistence. Conversely, host cells may exploit specific metabolic checkpoints to restore antiviral competency. HK2, the target of 2-DG, represents a crucial intersection between metabolic activity and innate antiviral pathways.17,24,25 By placing our work within this framework, we highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting vulnerabilities common to both the virus and the dysregulated host response.

Previous studies on HSK have documented a biphasic alteration in blood glucose levels during HSV infection. In the early viral replication phase, glycemic levels remain stable; however, systemic administration of 2-DG at this stage compromises host resistance, increasing susceptibility to severe herpes encephalitis. Conversely, during the later inflammatory phase, characterized by an approximate doubling of blood glucose, 2-DG treatment attenuates disease severity by limiting glucose utilization.20 Furthermore, metformin has been shown to prevent HSV dissemination to the central nervous system, thereby reducing encephalitic complications.19 Guided by this timing rationale and aiming to model clinically relevant scenarios, where patients typically present with established keratitis rather than primary infection, our study specifically applied therapeutic intervention after peak viral replication. Unlike systemic observations, topical ocular administration of 2-DG in our model reduced viral marker expression and improved corneal disease parameters. This discrepancy may result from differences in drug administration routes, as localized topical application likely minimizes systemic metabolic perturbations associated with intraperitoneal injection. This study extends our understanding of the therapeutic mechanisms underlying HK2 inhibition in alleviating HSK. Although earlier research identified HK2-mediated glycolysis as a therapeutic target,16 the current study uncovered the modulation of the MAVS/IFN axis through metabolic reprogramming. Our data are consistent with the model that glycolytic intermediates, particularly lactate, directly suppress MAVS aggregation and RLR signaling; thus, reducing glycolytic flux may relieve this brake to restore MAVS-mediated TBK1/IRF3 activation. Zhang et al.15 defined the specific mechanism by which these metabolic shifts regulate MAVS activity. They demonstrated that lactate binds directly to the MAVS transmembrane domain, physically preventing its prion-like aggregation, essential for RLR signalosome assembly. Therefore, in our model, HK2 inhibition likely decreases lactate production, thereby releasing MAVS from metabolic blockade. This restoration enables MAVS aggregation and subsequent TBK1/IRF3 activation, providing a structural basis for the enhanced antiviral response observed. However, further research is necessary to elucidate the precise temporal dynamics of this activation. Collectively, our findings indicate that HK2 inhibition restores antiviral immune signaling and is associated with reduced viral marker expression in HSK. Direct measurement of viral replication will be required to quantify the effect on viral yield.

Our results align with the growing concept in immunometabolism that host metabolic pathways serve as key sites of pathogen–host conflict.26,27 Viruses frequently manipulate the host cellular metabolism to optimize their own replication, unintentionally impacting immune signaling in the process. Zhang et al.15 first described such dual roles by demonstrating that lactate, a glycolytic metabolite, can suppress immune responses through MAVS. We extended these observations to an in vivo infection model, demonstrating that elevated lactate levels and increased HK2 activity in infected tissues correlate inversely with effective IFN responses.17,28 Conversely, HK2 inhibition restored IFN production, suggesting IFN-β signaling as a primary downstream effector of HK2 suppression, although involvement of other antiviral pathways cannot be excluded and would require further direct inhibition experiments for validation. Both our animal-based studies and cellular experiments confirmed that glycolysis negatively regulates RLR signaling, and that pharmacological blockade of glycolysis can effectively reverse this inhibition. These findings reinforce the hypothesis that a metabolic shift from glycolytic to oxidative pathways is essential for robust antiviral responses, a shift actively counteracted by viruses.18,24 In HSV-infected corneas, MAVS remains inactive despite the presence of viral pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), likely due to an unfavorable metabolic microenvironment promoted by HSV infection. By pharmacologically inhibiting HK2, we mimic the optimal metabolic condition required for antiviral immunity, thereby amplifying innate immune responses. Furthermore, detailed oxygen consumption rate (OCR)/extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) analysis using Seahorse assays and additional testing with glycolytic inhibitors could provide a comprehensive assessment of glycolysis and oxidative metabolism. These analyses may also elucidate potential resistance mechanisms, including viral strategies to bypass MAVS signaling or exploit alternative metabolic pathways, as well as host adaptations that could limit the long-term efficacy of HK2-targeted therapies.

Interestingly, there is existing evidence suggesting that metabolic enzymes can regulate innate immune receptors beyond RLR pathways. For example, previous reports indicated that STING, an endoplasmic reticulum-associated adaptor involved in DNA sensing, binds HK2 to inhibit its enzymatic function, creating a regulatory feedback loop whereby immune signals directly impact metabolic activity.29 Conversely, our current study identified a regulatory interaction wherein HK2 forms a complex with MAVS to suppress antiviral immune signaling. However, detailed domain-mapping experiments using truncation mutants, structural analyses, and definitive genetic tests of MAVS dependency will constitute important future research directions. Taken together, these findings illustrate reciprocal regulatory interactions between metabolism and immunity—a dynamic, bidirectional exchange capable of modulating the activity of each other. Importantly, identifying the HK2/MAVS axis not only clarifies the underlying mechanism in HSV-1 keratitis but also generates testable hypotheses for other herpesviruses and DNA viruses, representing a crucial direction for future investigation. This metabolic–immune crosstalk likely represents an evolutionary adaptation: Host cells undergoing antiviral responses typically adopt metabolic reprogramming strategies to enhance immune efficiency, whereas viruses seek to maintain metabolic conditions favorable for replication, even at the cost of suppressing host immunity. Accordingly, the combination of localized HK2 inhibition with conventional antiviral therapies (such as acyclovir or ganciclovir) could be explored as a complementary therapeutic approach. Such a combination may offer mechanistically complementary benefits; however, this possibility requires direct experimental validation.

In summary, by inhibiting the host glycolytic enzyme HK2, we disrupt HSV-1–mediated metabolic alterations, reactivate MAVS‑associated IFN antiviral signaling within the cornea, and control viral replication while minimizing tissue damage (Fig. 8). These findings establish a clear mechanistic connection between metabolic rewiring and innate immune activation, supporting the development of host-directed, topical therapeutic strategies as critical adjuncts to conventional ocular antivirals. Combining metabolic modulators with existing treatments, or designing optimized ocular-specific HK2 inhibitors, has the potential to significantly improve clinical outcomes in herpetic keratitis, exemplifying the effectiveness of integrating virological, immunological, and metabolic insights to combat ocular infections that threaten vision.
