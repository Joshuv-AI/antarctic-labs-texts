Outdoor Air Pollution, Perivascular Space Morphology, and Cognition in Preadolescence
Authors: Jessica Morrel, Kirthana Sukumaran, Carinna Torgerson, Michael A. Rosario, Haoyu Lan, Joel Schwartz, Jiu-Chiuan Chen, Jeiran Choupan, Megan M. Herting
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAmbient air pollution exposure is associated with structural brain differences and poorer cognition in children; however, mechanisms of toxicity remain unclear. Perivascular spaces (PVSs), key for brain waste clearance, may play a role in the neurotoxicity caused by air pollution. This study explored associations between air pollution exposure, PVS morphology, and cognition in preadolescents.MethodsWe analyzed cross-sectional Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study data from 6949 9- to 10-year-old participants. Annual average exposures to fine particulate matter (PM2.5), ozone, nitrogen dioxide, and 15 PM2.5 components were estimated using spatiotemporal models mapped to residential addresses. PVS count and volume were derived from T1-weighted and T2-weighted magnetic resonance imaging, and cognition was estimated using NIH Toolbox scores. Linear mixed-effects models examined independent associations between air pollution, PVS, and cognition; weighted quantile sum regression assessed coexposure effects of PM2.5 mixtures.ResultsLinear models revealed that exposures to zinc (Zn), ammonium (NH4+), and bromine were positively associated with PVS count in several regions. Higher PVS count in 5 key regions was associated with poorer cognitive performance across several NIH Toolbox domains. Higher calcium, Zn, and NH4+ exposures were associated with poorer cognition (false discovery rate–corrected p < .01). Higher frontal lobe PVS count mediated the association between Zn exposure and poorer total cognition (p < .01). Coexposure models revealed that PM2.5 mixtures were associated with higher temporal and cingulate PVS counts and poorer working memory and crystallized intelligence (p < .01).ConclusionsOutdoor air pollution was associated with higher PVS count and reduced cognition, suggesting that brain clearance may be a novel mechanism linking pollution to neurodevelopmental harm in preadolescents.

Outdoor air pollution is a ubiquitous neurotoxicant associated with structural and functional brain differences (1). Mounting evidence supports a link between prenatal and childhood air pollution exposure, particularly to fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and its components, and poor cognition, including worse inhibitory control (2,3), processing speed (4,5), attention (5,6), working memory (3,7,8), and verbal intelligence (9). While animal studies implicate microglial activation, inflammation, white matter injury, and alterations in synaptic plasticity (10), specific mechanisms by which air pollution impacts the developing brain and cognition remain unclear. Here, we investigated perivascular spaces (PVSs) as one potential target of air pollution–induced neurotoxicity.

PVSs are cerebrospinal fluid–filled spaces surrounding cerebral vasculature (Figure 1A) and a key component of the brain’s glymphatic system, with central roles in nutrient delivery, waste clearance, neuroinflammation, and immune cell trafficking (11, 12, 13). PVSs can be visualized using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (Figure 1B) and quantified by the number visible on an MRI (count), their volume, or other morphological and functional properties (14). Studies demonstrate that MRI-visible PVSs are present from infancy and increase in count and volume across the lifespan (15, 16, 17). Evidence suggests a link between enlarged PVSs, reduced glymphatic clearance, and poorer cognition in older adults (19,20). Mechanistically, there are several hypotheses. PVSs play an important role in neurovascular coupling (21), which has been positively associated with cognition (22,23). Furthermore, PVSs are a site of immune cell accumulation (24) and a key actor in neuroinflammatory processes (12). Therefore, in the presence of an immunologic challenge, such as air pollution exposure, it is plausible that PVSs may be a site of neurotoxicity (25). Findings from one study using light and electron microscopy in dogs, children, and teens found that exposure to ozone (O3) and PM2.5 was associated with perivascular and neurovascular unit dysfunction in the prefrontal white matter; authors note this may be a mechanism of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathogenesis (26). Furthermore, evidence suggests associations between PM2.5, nitrogen dioxide (NO2), O3, and PM2.5 components, including metals and ammonium (NH4+), and inflammation (27, 28, 29, 30, 31), providing mechanistic support for PVSs as a site of air pollution–induced neurotoxicity. While clinical studies have examined cognitive symptoms in hospitalized children with enlarged PVSs (32,33) and 2 studies have examined air pollution and PVSs in adults (34,35); none, to our knowledge, has tested the relationship between air pollution exposure, PVS morphology, and cognition in a large group of healthy children.

The current study fills this gap by exploring novel relationships between air pollution exposure, PVS count (number of MRI-visible spaces) and volume fraction (VF), and cognition in 9- to 10-year-old participants. Using linear mixed-effects (LME) models and weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression, we examined relationships between exposure and coexposure to 3 criteria pollutants (PM2.5, NO2, and O3) and 15 PM2.5 components, PVS morphology, and cognition. Considering that enlarged PVSs are rarely observed in healthy children (36), and that PM2.5 and metal components are associated with oxidative stress and inflammation (37), we hypothesized that exposure to PM2.5 and its metal components would be associated with higher MRI-visible PVS count, which would be negatively associated with memory and processing speed, considering the evidence linking PVS burden to these 2 cognitive outcomes (19). We expected that PVS morphology would mediate the relationship between PM2.5 and cognition.

Methods and Materials

Study Design

This study used cross-sectional imaging, air pollution, and cognitive data collected during the baseline visit of the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study (National Institute of Mental Health Data Archive 3.0 [for raw imaging data; release DOI: 10.15154/1520591] and 5.1 [all other data; release DOI: 10.15154/z563-zd24]). The ABCD Study enrolled 11,880 9- to 10-year-old children from 21 sites across the United States between October 2016 and October 2018 (38, 39, 40). Centralized institutional review board (IRB) approval was obtained from the University of California San Diego; each site obtained approval from their local IRB. Each child’s parent or legal guardian provided written consent, and each child provided written assent. Eligibility requirements included ages between 9.0 and 10.99 years at the baseline visit and English fluency. Exclusion criteria included severe sensory, neurological, medical, or intellectual limitations and inability to complete the MRI scan. Participants were included in the current study if they had residentially mapped air pollution data at baseline and passed all neuroimaging and PVS segmentation quality control (QC) criteria (see Neuroimaging Data; see Figure S1 for flowchart of participant exclusion).

Air Pollution Exposure

The PM2.5 component and criteria pollutant datasets have been widely used in previous studies (41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46), and their procedures for estimating annual average concentrations of residential outdoor air pollution exposure have been previously described in detail for the ABCD Study (45, 46, 47, 48, 49). Briefly, daily exposures to PM2.5 (in μg/m3), NO2 (in ppb), and the 8-hour daily maximums for O3 (in ppb) were calculated at 1-km2 resolution across the United States using hybrid spatiotemporal modeling that incorporates satellite-based aerosol optical depth models, land-use regression, meteorological data, and chemical transport model outputs (50, 51, 52) (Supplemental Methods A.1 and Table S1). A similar method was used to estimate monthly exposures to 15 PM2.5 components across the United States at a 50-m2 resolution, including bromine (Br), calcium (Ca), copper (Cu), elemental carbon (EC), iron (Fe), potassium (K), ammonium (NH4+), nickel (Ni), nitrate (NO3−), organic carbon (OC), lead (Pb), silicon (Si), sulfate (SO42−), vanadium (V), and zinc (Zn) (53, 54, 55, 56). Component selection was based in part by monitoring data availability for model training as well as relative toxicity, as evidence shows trace elements significantly influence PM2.5 toxicity despite comprising only a small fraction of total mass (57,58). Exposure estimates for criteria pollutants and PM2.5 components were averaged across the 2016 calendar year, corresponding to the baseline enrollment visit, and were assigned to each child’s baseline primary residential address.

Neuroimaging Data

Due to the sensitivity of PVS pipelines to motion and artifacts, only participants passing ABCD raw image and FreeSurfer QC were included in the current study (Figure S1). For PVS segmentation, raw T1-weighted (T1w) and T2-weighted (T2w) images were registered via the Human Connectome Project minimal preprocessing pipeline (59), where data were upsampled to 0.7-mm3 resolution to enhance signal contrast. Preprocessed T1w and T2w images were filtered using adaptive nonlocal mean filtering (60), and an enhanced PVS contrast (EPC) was obtained by dividing the filtered T1w image/T2w image to enhance PVS visibility (61). All EPC images underwent manual visual QC to remove participants with extreme noise or incidental findings. Next, the weakly supervised PVSs segmentation was applied as previously described (62) (Supplemental Methods A.2). Three-dimensional, in vivo segmentations of PVSs for a representative participant are visualized in Figure 1C, D. The white matter was then parcellated using the Desikan-Killiany atlas (63) and pooled into 6 key regions of interest—frontal, temporal, parietal, occipital, cingulate, and centrum semiovale (CSO). PVS volume and count were calculated for these 6 regions (see Figure S2 for distributions), and VFs were calculated by dividing the volume of PVS in each region by that region’s total volume to normalize PVS volume between regions.

Cognitive Data

The NIH Toolbox cognitive battery was collected at baseline, assessing receptive vocabulary (picture vocabulary task), expressive language and reading decoding (oral reading recognition task), processing speed (pattern comparison processing speed test), working memory (list sorting working memory test), episodic memory (picture sequence memory test), inhibitory control and attention (flanker task), and cognitive flexibility (dimensional change card sort task) (64). Three summary scores were derived: total cognition, fluid composite (card sort, flanker, picture sequence memory, list sorting, and pattern comparison tasks), and crystallized composite (picture vocabulary and oral reading recognition tasks). We used age-corrected scores (65). Composite measures demonstrate excellent intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) (range: 0.92–0.96), with subtests showing adequate to excellent test-retest reliability (ICC range: 0.76–0.92) (66).

Demographics and Covariates

Covariates were selected based on previous literature and the construction of directed acyclic graphs (67) (Figure S3). Sociodemographic variables such as parent-reported race/ethnicity (Asian, Black, Hispanic, other, or White) and average household income in U.S. dollars (<50,000; ≥50,000–<100,000; ≥100,000; and do not know/refuse to answer) were included due to known differences in air pollution exposure across socioeconomic and racial/ethnic groups (68,69). We included demographic variables known to be associated with PVS morphology, such as age at MRI, sex (female or male), and body mass index (BMI) (z scored) (15,17,70). Precision imaging variables included regional white matter volume in PVS count models and a combination variable of MRI model and head coil (32 or 64 channel) to account for PVS differences attributable to variability in cranial size and scanner differences. Finally, ABCD site was included as a random effect to account for the hierarchical nature of the ABCD Study.

Statistical Analyses

All data preparation and analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.2 (71). Three sets of single-pollutant LME models were run to test independent associations between 1) air pollution and PVS morphology, 2) PVS count and cognition, and 3) air pollution and cognition, adjusting for key covariates (Supplemental Methods A.4). Model diagnostics were evaluated using the performance package to confirm that models met assumptions of linearity, normality, homoscedasticity, residual independence, and validity of the random effects structure (72). First, 36 models (analysis 1) per region were performed to test associations between each pollutant and PVS count or VF. Analyses using PVS count as the outcome assess whether air pollution exposure is associated with a greater number of PVSs, reflecting increases in the frequency of small, MRI-visible PVSs, while analyses using VF evaluate whether air pollution is linked to increased cumulative PVS burden, which may reflect dilation or enlargement of individual PVSs. Due to widespread associations between air pollution and PVS count but not VF, we ran 60 post hoc LME models (analysis 2) testing associations between PVS count and the 10 cognitive outcomes. For analysis 3, we tested independent associations between the 18 air pollutants and 10 cognitive outcomes and performed mediation analysis (Supplemental Methods A.4.4) to determine whether PVS morphology mediates the relationship between air pollution exposure and cognition. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was implemented, and across all LME models, only results passing FDR correction for significance at pFDR < .01 were considered for mediation analysis. Cohen’s effect size was calculated as well. Associations were considered candidates for mediation testing if paths a (exposure→mediator), b (mediator→outcome), and c (exposure→outcome) reached pFDR < .01 in LME analyses; this conservative screening criterion was applied to identify robust pathways prior to formal mediation testing (73). Mediation analyses were conducted using the mediation package in R (74), which estimates the average causal mediation effect, average direct effect, and confidence intervals via quasi-Bayesian Monte Carlo simulation (1000 simulations).

Next, because pollutant exposure never occurs in isolation, we examined relationships between coexposure to the 15 PM2.5 components and PVS count, PVS VF, and cognition using WQS regression. WQS uses a generalized linear regression framework to model the exposure-outcome relationship (75) and creates a composite mixture index in which each component is assigned a data-driven weight, reflecting its importance in the association between the exposure mixture index and outcome (Supplemental Methods A.4.5). Separate WQS models were constructed for each outcome. Directionality of the associations was set a priori based on the observed trends from the LME results (i.e., positive for PVS and negative for cognition) along with toxicological and epidemiological literature linking PM2.5 and its components to PVS burden (26) and poorer cognition (2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9). This hypothesis-driven approach is consistent with established WQS methodology (75) and recent applications in PM2.5 mixture analyses (76). All results presented were adjusted for the previously mentioned set of confounders (Demographics and Covariates). Component weights >0.06, calculated based on the commonly used threshold, the inverse of the number of components (1/15) (75), were considered important contributors to the WQS index.

While parental education was considered during DAG analysis and did not emerge in the minimally sufficient adjustment set, we recognize that recent work in the ABCD Study suggests it may explain unique variance in brain outcomes beyond household income (77). Therefore, we conducted sensitivity analyses examining the robustness of all primary findings to its inclusion as an additional covariate.

Analysis 2: PVS Count and Cognition

We identified significant negative associations between PVS count and several cognitive domains (pFDR < .01) (Figure 3 and Table 2; Table S6). Higher PVS count in the temporal, frontal, and cingulate was associated with poorer cognitive performance overall (i.e., total composite). Higher PVS counts in the temporal, frontal, parietal, and CSO were also associated with lower fluid intelligence, whereas higher PVS count in only the temporal lobe was associated with poorer crystallized intelligence. Higher PVS counts in all regions, except the occipital lobe, were related to learning and memory performance. Sensitivity Analyses

To examine the robustness of findings to the inclusion of parental education as a covariate, we reran all LME (Tables S9–S11), mediation (Table S12), and WQS (Table S13) models. The majority of findings were retained, and the indirect effect of Zn on total cognition through frontal lobe PVS count remained statistically significant (proportion mediated = 4.3%) (Table S11). In the WQS models, the association between the pollutant mixture and cingulate PVS count remained significant (p < .001), while associations with temporal PVS count and list sorting working memory were attenuated to p < .05, and the crystallized composite association became nonsignificant (Table S13).

Discussion

Key Results

This study identified associations between air pollution exposure, PVS morphology, and cognition in a large, multiethnic U.S. sample of 9- to 10-year-old children. In single-pollutant models, NH4+ was associated with greater temporal PVS VF and poorer language and crystallized intelligence; Zn with higher frontal and CSO PVS counts and lower total cognition and language; and Br with higher cingulate PVS count, which was associated with lower episodic memory, working memory, and total cognition. Frontal PVS count partially mediated the negative association between Zn exposure and total cognition, supporting PVS morphology as one pathway through which air pollution may influence cognition. In multi-pollutant models, coexposure to 15 PM2.5 components was associated with higher temporal and cingulate PVS counts, driven by NO3−, Br, Cu, Si, NH4+, and V. Beyond its role in the mediation pathway, frontal PVS count was associated with poorer fluid cognition and episodic memory. Associations were predominantly observed for PVS count rather than VF. We speculate that ambient pollution exposure at the levels observed in this study may increase the number of small, MRI-visible PVSs without producing enough dilation to elevate VF, an interpretation supported by evidence that dilated PVSs are rarely observed in healthy children (36). Longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether sustained or higher-level exposure produces more pronounced morphological and cognitive changes over time.

Negative associations were identified between PVS count and memory and all 3 cognitive summary scores. While associations with cognition were most commonly observed in the temporal, frontal, and cingulate, significant associations existed for all examined regions except the occipital lobe (pFDR < .01), suggesting near global effects. These results are consistent with adult studies, which have tied lower glymphatic function and higher PVS burden to worse memory and overall cognition (19,20,78). One potential explanation for the observed results is that higher PVS counts are indicative of disrupted waste clearance and neuroinflammation (79, 80, 81), which may selectively harm brain regions involved in memory and executive function, such as the frontal and temporal lobes, and the cingulate cortex. That said, PVS morphology and its association with cognition is likely shaped by several factors, as suggested by the modest proportion mediated in the Zn-cognition pathway. Future work examining how other factors, including genetics (82,83) and physical health (83, 84, 85), relate to PVS morphology during development will be critical for clarifying its biological and environmental determinants.

Importantly, the associations in the current study are observed at low levels of air pollution exposure, highlighting that potential health risks are seen below traditional regulatory thresholds. These results also suggest that the chemical composition of PM2.5 may be critical for determining neurovascular and cognitive effects, underscoring the need for air quality regulations that consider particle chemical composition rather than the mass concentration alone.

Biological Mechanisms Linking Air Pollution Exposure to PVS Morphology

Considering the role of PVSs in glymphatic waste clearance and neuroinflammatory regulation (12), and evidence that PVS and glymphatic dysfunction contribute to neurological disease (26,79,81), pollution exposure may exacerbate neuroinflammation and increase brain metal load, thereby impairing glymphatic clearance and elevating risk for neurological disorders such as AD (25,26,86). As PM2.5 particles cross the blood-brain barrier, they can bind to metabolic waste, proteins, and neurotransmitters, upregulating neurotransmitter release and inhibiting waste clearance (87). NH4+ specifically has been associated with altered astrocyte and neuron morphology and apoptosis (88, 89, 90, 91), can compete at K+ channel binding sites to alter neuronal excitability (92), and may compromise astrocytic buffering by over activating the Na+, K+, and Cl− cotransporter, impairing cortical inhibitory networks (93).

Metal exposure is particularly complex, as normal physiological function depends on metal homeostasis, and metals such as Zn and Cu are essential for neuronal health (94,95). However, metals in air pollution generate oxidative stress in vivo, and metal dyshomeostasis (96) contributes to neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative conditions (30). For instance, Zn causes neuronal cell death at high doses (97,98) and secondary toxicity through interference with Cu uptake (99,100). While Cu can disrupt long-term potentiation (101,102), it is critical for normal cognitive function (103). These complex relationships may help to explain why Cu and Zn show divergent associations with cognition in the present study, emphasizing the capacity of air pollution to disrupt metal homeostasis and neurological function.

Our results extend previous studies linking PM2.5 and its components to brain structure in children and adolescents (44,104,105), supporting the hypothesis that outdoor particle pollutants have differential neurotoxic potential, consistent with toxicological evidence from animal and cellular studies (30). The regional shift between single- and multi-pollutant results may reflect statistical differences, biological differences, or both. Statistically, regions with more individually significant LME associations are better positioned to accumulate signal in WQS, while isolated single-pollutant associations (e.g., Zn with frontal PVS) may attenuate within the broader mixture covariance structure. Biologically, regional differences in neurovascular composition (106,107), astrocyte antioxidant capacity (108,109), and inflammatory susceptibility (110,111) may render some regions more vulnerable to mixture exposure than individual pollutants.

The PM2.5 components associated with PVS morphology and cognition span multiple emission sources (45). Zn, which appeared across both PVS and cognitive outcomes, primarily loaded on industrial processing, fuel combustion, and vehicular traffic (45). NH4+ reflects formation from agricultural emissions and coal combustion. Br, associated with PVS count, loaded onto biomass burning (45). Cu and Ca, associated with cognitive outcomes, reflect traffic-related emissions (exhaust and nonexhaust brake and tire wear) and crustal resuspension of dust particles, respectively (45). Together, these source profiles implicate vehicular traffic, agricultural emissions, and biomass combustion as potentially modifiable contributors to PVS count in preadolescents.

Broader Implications: A Potential Early Marker of Neurological Disease Risk?

PVS alterations in late childhood and early adolescence may represent an early biomarker of vascular or glymphatic dysfunction, with potential implications for long-term cognitive health. For instance, several PM2.5 components identified in the current study have also been linked to AD susceptibility and pathophysiology. Elevated ammonia (the neutral form of NH4+) can increase GABA (gamma-aminobutyric acid) release, upregulating inhibitory neurotransmission and contributing to cognitive deficits in AD (112). Higher brain Br levels have been associated with AD neuropathology (113). Accumulation of metals such as Fe, Cu, Ni, and Zn has been associated with amyloid-β (Aβ) and pTau aggregation (114), and Fe, Cu, and Zn have been found in elevated concentrations in AD senile plaques (115,116). Elevated serum Ca has been associated with faster cognitive decline in older adults (117) and AD risk (118). Finally, PVS burden and glymphatic system dysfunction, key for clearing interstitial solutes such as Aβ (119), have been strongly associated with cognitive decline and AD (81,120).

Strengths and Limitations

There are several strengths and limitations to note. First, this study provides novel insights into a highly understudied, yet critically important, research topic. The study uses a large, diverse sample of 9- to 10-year-old children across the United States, improving the generalizability of findings. Furthermore, the study leverages both single- and multi-pollutant models. While LMEs allow us to identify pollutant-specific exposure-brain-cognition relationships, WQS sheds light on the impact of coexposures by minimizing the impact of collinearity between the correlated components while also identifying cumulative associations. Sensitivity analyses including parental education confirmed that most significant associations were preserved across all 3 model sets. Some attenuation was observed, consistent with parental education explaining a portion of variance in cognitive outcomes in this age group, while the core mediation pathway remained statistically significant and largely unchanged.

Due to image quality constraints, we were only able to retain data from a subset of ABCD Study participants. Compared with the whole ABCD Study, our participants were older and more likely to be female, White, and scanned on a Siemens scanner (Table S2), limiting generalizability. Also, participants in this sample were exposed to relatively low levels of air pollution, meaning that these results only provide insight into the impact of one year of low-level air pollution exposure and do not necessarily translate to children living in highly polluted countries. Furthermore, exposure estimates reflect annual averages linked to participants’ primary residential addresses for the 2016 calendar year, as determined by residential data availability and the spatiotemporal resolution of the exposure models in ABCD (49). Residential mobility and temporal misclassification may therefore introduce nondifferential exposure misclassification that may bias associations towards the null.

Notably, participants had resided in their baseline address for a mean of 5.47 (SD = 3.76) years, suggesting reasonable representativeness of longer-term exposure. Annual PM2.5 concentrations have been shown to remain largely consistent across U.S. regions during and surrounding the study period (51,121), which may partially attenuate concerns regarding the use of a fixed exposure year. Future ABCD Study releases aim to include lifetime residential histories with greater spatiotemporal precision, enabling longitudinal assessment of cumulative exposure effects. Finally, the current study uses a cross-sectional design, limiting our ability to draw conclusions regarding the impacts of chronic exposure on neurodevelopmental and/or cognitive trajectories. Future studies should consider using longitudinal models to answer these questions.

Conclusions

Our study identified novel associations between PM2.5 component exposure, PVS count, and cognition in preadolescents from across the United States. Results from this study highlight the brain’s susceptibility to the toxic effects of air pollution during the transition to adolescence and, for the first time, suggest that PVS morphology may be one pathway by which air pollution harms cognitive functioning in preadolescents.
