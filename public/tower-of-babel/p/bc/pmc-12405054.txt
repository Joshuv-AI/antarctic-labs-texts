Data Sharing Experience, Guidance, and Resources From the Rare Diseases Clinical Research Network (RDCRN)
Authors: Elaine Schwendeman, Henry J Kaminski, Adeline Vanderver, Michael Wagner, Eileen King, The RDCRN Data Use & Data Sharing Committee, Maurizio Macaluso
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe Rare Diseases Clinical Research Network (RDCRN) comprises research consortia and other partners focused on the study of rare diseases. Its goals include sharing de‐identified data with the scientific community and other stakeholders to advance rare disease research. The RDCRN Data Use & Data Sharing Committee and RDCRN Data Management and Coordinating Center reviewed data sharing practices across established consortia and published literature to develop guidance documents. The Committee produced “RDCRN Suggestions for Establishing Data Sharing and Data Management Guidance for Individual Consortia”, which lays out the common elements of successful data sharing, and “Principles of Data Sharing Checklist,” which outlines the components of informed consent language, contractual language, and consortium policies that govern data sharing and use. Key principles of the guidance are: (1) informed consent language should allow data sharing while protecting participants' rights, (2) the research database should track participant's choices on how their data can be used and shared to ensure appropriate data use limitations are followed, (3) the research protocol and consortium agreements should include language stipulating that data will be shared with the consortium Administrative Core (Admin Core) for the purpose of further sharing according to NIH policies, and (4) consortia policies and agreements should emphasize the role of the Admin Core as the primary steward of the collective data and recognize its authority to further share consortium data. The RDCRN experience and learnings contribute to the advancement of Clinical and Translational Science and may help other research networks in designing data sharing policies.Keywords: data sharing, data use agreements, future data use, informed consent, regulatory compliance, subcontract awards

Introduction

The RDCRN was launched in 2003 following the enactment of the Rare Diseases Act [1]. It comprises Clinical Research Consortia (Consortia) organized by US‐based academic centers, a Data Management and Coordinating Center (Coordinating Center), a Coalition of Patient Advocacy Groups (Patient Advocates), and program officers and scientists from 10 Institutes, Centers or Offices (ICOs) at the NIH. The RDCRN has been operating over four consecutive 5‐year cycles and has a rich history of success, producing over 2500 publications, conducting 81 clinical trials, and contributing to the body of knowledge leading to the advancement of specialized treatments for rare diseases [1, 2]. For example, accomplishments by the Consortium of Eosinophilic Gastrointestinal Disease Researchers (CEGIR) (approval of budesonide (EOHILIA) and dupilumab (DUPIXENT) for Eosinophilic Esophagitis), the Lysosomal Disease Network (LDN) (updated prescribing information for alglucosidase alfa (LUMIZYME/MYOZYME) for Pompe disease), the Rett Syndrome, MECP2 Duplications, and Rett‐related Disorders (RTT) Consortium (approval of Trofinetide (DAYBUE) for Rett syndrome), the Porphyrias Consortium (PC) (Afamelanotide (SCENESSE) for Erythropoietic protoporphyria and Givosiran (GIVLAARI) for Acute Hepatic Porphyria) and the Global Leukodystrophy Initiative Clinical Trials Network (GLIA‐CTN) (standard of care off‐label use of Baricitinib (OLUMIANT) for Aicardi‐Goutières Syndrome) illustrate the impact of the RDCRN on rare disease treatment development (see Supporting Information). The treatments mentioned in these examples have enjoyed wide dissemination: most have been approved in the USA by the FDA, many in the EU by the EMA, and some are available in Canada, Australia, and Japan.

Supported by a cooperative agreement with the NIH, each host institution of a Consortium Administrative Core (Admin Core) subcontracts with clinical sites to enroll participants in research studies (Figure 1). Through a separate cooperative agreement, the Coordinating Center designs the data capture system and other tools employed by Consortia (Figure 1). Clinical sites agree to enter data in the Coordinating Center systems and may execute data sharing agreements with the Coordinating Center, whereas the Admin Core may enter into data sharing agreements with the Coordinating Center to manage combined study data (Figure 1). Although the RDCRN is based in the USA and the Admin Cores of all Consortia are at U.S. institutions, the network is global; in cycle 4 (2019–2025), 11 of the 20 Consortia had at least one site outside the USA. Patient Advocates (N = 186 groups in cycle 4) often include international members or are based in other countries. All RDCRN partners collaborate to conduct research, manage data, and disseminate key results to stakeholders and the public. In RDCRN cycle 4 (2019–2025), the Coordinating Center and RDCRN program officials from the National Center for Advancing Translational Sciences (NCATS) discussed collaborations with nonprofit and governmental organizations in North America and Europe focusing on training/career development opportunities for clinical researchers and to identify and consider adoption of data standards to facilitate data sharing. Many of the Consortia and Patient Advocates have maintained international collaborations.

The NIH expects that the data and results of the research it funds will be shared publicly. The first Request for Applications (RFA) for the RDCRN, released in 2003 (Table 1), emphasized the importance of publicly sharing RDCRN research data [3]. At that time, grants were subject to the NIH Grants Policy Statement (NIHGPS), which stated “It is NIH policy to make available to the public the results and accomplishments of the activities that it funds…” (Table 1) [4]. A few months later, the 2003 Final NIH Statement on Sharing Research Data (Table 1) introduced the expectation that applications seeking significant funding include a plan for data sharing and required timely release of results [5]. The RDCRN has kept current with changes in NIH policies with each RDCRN funding cycle. The RDCRN cycle 4 RFA pointed at data sharing as a critical goal: “deidentified data collected within the network would become a resource for the greater rare disease research community” (Table 1) [6]. The 2023 Final NIH Policy for Data Management & Sharing (2023 NIH Policy, Table 1) [7], stipulates that applicants must submit a Data Management and Sharing Plan and comply with the plan as approved by the NIH, making data available as soon as possible and no later than the time of an associated publication or the end of the performance period, whichever comes first (Table 1) [7]. The latest RDCRN Notice of Funding Opportunity (NOFO) reinforces the expectation that RDCRN data be shared broadly and in a timely manner (Table 1) [8].

The RDCRN Data Use and Data Sharing Committee (Committee) was established to support individual Consortia and foster data sharing within and outside the network. Primary tasks of the Committee were to identify policies and agreements that Consortia should adopt to facilitate data sharing, and the critical arrangements between parties that allow for future data use. The Committee posits that promoting common data sharing language across the RDCRN helps streamline the process and builds trust with advocates and other stakeholders. The purpose of this paper is to highlight the work done in collaboration by the Committee and the Coordinating Center, aimed at improving the use and sharing of data produced by a clinical research network. Our findings are generalizable and contribute to advancing translational science in the sense promoted by NCATS, as intended by the NIH Director when the Center was established [9] and reinforced by the NCATS Director in CTS [10].

Methods

Committee Composition, Objectives, and Milestones

The Committee (2019–2025) includes investigators and research program managers from individual Consortia, Patient Advocate members, NIH partners, and representatives of the Coordinating Center. The initial committee objectives were to: (1) identify guiding principles for data sharing and use within the RDCRN, (2) develop template language for legal agreements to facilitate the transfer, sharing, and use of RDCRN data, and (3) promote education of RDCRN members on network data sharing guidance and policies. Milestones included finalizing template language for data sharing agreements, describing common principles and best practices that facilitate data sharing within consortia, and providing guidance to Consortia on how data could be shared with external parties. We later added new objectives and corresponding milestones, including: (4) engaging in public education by making data sharing resources available via the RDCRN website, (5) compiling data sharing resources as part of an onboarding package for new Consortia, and (6) developing a communication plan to disseminate resources developed by the Committee.

Developing Resources and Gaining Network Approval

To obtain network‐wide approval of the resources developed by the Committee, we sought stakeholder input on milestones and draft language for data‐sharing agreements and other documents. As we achieved milestones, we solicited feedback from Consortia PIs, NIH partners, Patient Advocate members, and external advisors. After considering feedback, drafts were finalized and adopted for Coordinating Center operations, or if appropriate, were submitted for review by the RDCRN Steering Committee for approval as an official RDCRN policy. An iterative approach to document development facilitated acceptance within the network.

Review of Existing Policies and Procedures and New Development Work

Some Consortia had internal data sharing policies. The Committee reviewed established examples from existing Consortia prior to drafting resources for adoption by the entire network. The review identified proven language and practices which could become a common resource for the network. To draft the Guidance Document entitled “RDCRN Suggestions for Establishing Data Sharing and Data Management Guidance for Individual Consortia”, we extracted common elements of data sharing agreements from more than half of the Consortia. Next, we drafted templates for consortium data sharing policies based on the finalized Guidance document and other material. To develop recommended data sharing language for informed consent forms (Consent), we summarized language used in RDCRN protocols and language recommended by the NIH [11].

Development of Data Sharing Agreements

In cycle 4, it was necessary to develop multiple data‐sharing agreements to protect the interests of stakeholders and establish guardrails to ensure that data‐sharing efforts were secure and productive. Legal language must ensure that any regulatory requirements, data use limitations, and institutional policies are referenced in the relevant documents. The Committee and Coordinating Center teams developed the RDCRN data sharing agreements in consultation with Sponsored Research Services officials at Cincinnati Children's Hospital (CCHMC), which hosts the Coordinating Center. The Committee modified the CCHMC template language to accommodate specific data‐sharing needs. Once an initial data use agreement (DUA) was finalized, it was modified using the iterative approach described above. Currently, there are five commonly used data‐sharing agreements between the RDCRN Coordinating Center at CCHMC and other institutions participating in the RDCRN (Table 2).

Informed Consent Tracking Log

A new REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) form (Informed Consent log‐tracker) was added to every REDCap database hosted in the RDCRN NCATS cloud environment and to the standard REDCap project for new RDCRN protocols [12]. This form captures the version of the Consent signed by a participant and includes information about data use limitations stipulated in the Consent.

Principles of Data Sharing Checklist

We summarized the deliberations of the Committee in a checklist of data sharing principles based on the Guidance document and successful examples by Consortia.

Results

Consortia had developed a variety of data sharing agreements and practices, reflecting differences in governance and institutional policies. The Committee could not develop a uniform approach and instead proposed a set of data sharing fundamentals formalized in the Guidance Document (Table 3) [13]. The Guidance document was distilled into a Principles of Data Sharing Checklist (Checklist; Table 3) outlining the elements that need to be incorporated in the Consent, in contracts between the Admin Core and its sites, and in the Consortia policies governing data sharing, use, and publication within the consortium and with external users. Informed Consent Language

The Human Subjects research protocol provides the rationale for the Consent discussed with, and typically signed by, a research participant. It should allow broad data sharing while describing procedures to protect participant confidentiality and minimize reidentification risks. RDCRN projects varied in presenting data sharing to prospective participants and in the options for limiting data sharing. Such variability requires that to effectively manage participant choices about sharing data, participant‐specific data use limitations must be incorporated or linked to the research database by design. Ideally, the Consent would minimize the burden on participants by limiting the number of choices for sharing the participants' data. The Coordinating Center added a new form (Consent log‐tracker) to each RDCRN database to track participant Consent selections, to ensure that Consent‐imposed restrictions are implemented when the data are shared [7].

To assist in the design of RDCRN Consents, the Committee proposed essential elements and language permitting future data sharing with external parties and a data repository (Table 3). Consent language should allow participant data sharing in accordance with the Federal Policy for the Protection of Human Subjects (the Common Rule) through the removal of identifiable private information (Table 1) [14]. Special care should be taken to minimize the risk of reidentification of participants with rare diseases. The Consent should also mention data sharing with the Admin Core, Coordinating Center, other researchers, and a data repository in accordance with an approved IRB protocol, institutional policies, the 2023 NIH Policy (Table 1), and applicable laws [7]. If possible, it should also state which organizations will store and share the data, who will have access to the data, and how the data will be used now and in the future.

Subcontracts Between the Institution Hosting a Consortium Admin Core and Clinical Sites

An institution receiving a consortium award typically enters into subcontract agreements with institutions engaged in recruiting participants for the Consortium research projects (clinical sites). The language used to describe data ownership and the authority of the Admin Core in collating, managing, and sharing data collected by the sites varied widely. The Committee sought to clarify the key concepts and recommended that a subcontract specify where sites will enter data, who will maintain the data and have access, acknowledge that the data may meet the HIPAA definition of a limited data set (Table 1) [15], state that the Admin Core may collate data and share it with the Coordinating Center and other external parties through specific DUAs, and deposit the data in a repository. The Checklist Document includes suggested language to address these points (Table 3) [13].

Consortium‐Specific Data Sharing Policies

Consortia data sharing policies define the terms for data sharing within the consortium and with external parties; the Admin Core is the steward of the data and should have the authority to share it with third parties, including a data repository (Figure 1, arrow 7). The governance and procedures for sharing data among sites and with outside partners vary across Consortia. The Committee recommends that Consortia follow policies consistent with the Guidance document, consider the 2023 NIH Policy (Table 1) [7], adhere to FAIR (Findability, Accessibility, Interoperability, and Reusability) data principles, and to the policies of their sponsoring NIH institute or center [16]. Consortia should consider including in a data sharing policy: (a) who has the authority to make decisions about data sharing, (b) a publication policy, (c) procedures for managing conflicts of interest, (d) an intellectual property (IP) statement outlining rights to IP generated by the consortium, (e) procedures for managing data access requests with appropriate data use limitations defined, (f) and a continuity plan for sharing data after the end of NIH funding. Based on this guidance, the Committee developed a template intra‐consortium data sharing policy and is currently finalizing it for Consortia to review and adapt for their own use.

Data Sharing With the Coordinating Center and a Federal Data Repository

In partnership with the Coordinating Center, the Committee developed guidelines and language for legal agreements to facilitate data sharing within the RDCRN and with the Coordinating Center. RFA‐TR‐18‐020 indicated that deidentified data collected within the network will be shared with the Coordinating Center and made available to the scientific community and other stakeholders in a timely manner (Table 1) [6]. The RDCRN is committed to adhering to these requirements and will support the Consortia in making data broadly accessible in a data repository. Consortium policies should be consistent with RFA requirements, and Committee guidance documents have clarified that (a) the extent and timing of data transfer should be incorporated into the Consortium work plan and negotiated with NIH, (b) the consortium Admin Core should have the authority to manage data on behalf of the sites and to enter agreements with the Coordinating Center, allowing the Coordinating Center to assist with depositing data in a repository, (c) governance of data sharing in a repository is managed by the agency in charge of the repository, which will specify the terms for data use in appropriate agreements (Figure 1, arrow 8). Ultimately, data from an RDCRN study should be made available following FAIR principles in a data repository which allows broad access [13, 16].

The Committee and the RDCRN Data Standards committee recommend network‐wide data standards that facilitate sharing high‐quality datasets and allow for interoperability of data across the RDCRN. RDCRN data standards should be incorporated in the design phase of the study and associated database to support data reuse. The Coordinating Center has developed a template database containing standardized forms to facilitate adoption in new studies; this is critical to achieve interoperable datasets throughout the RDCRN (Table 3) [17]. A standardized approach is also used in naming data fields and forms to facilitate alignment of RDCRN data. Additionally, the cycle 4 RFA mandated a centralization of all computational infrastructure and software licenses to an NIH‐owned cloud environment operated by the Coordinating Center. This strategy to centralize data capture, analysis and reporting tools has been instrumental in facilitating multisite study operations and ensuring high quality data acquisition, enabling the Coordinating Center to enforce data quality standards.

The RDCRN, Coordinating Center, and Committee have put into practice the data sharing principles described in the Checklist and Guidance documents, sharing resources, data, recommendations, and information in venues and projects outlined in the following sections (see also Table 3).

RDCRN Data Sharing Resources Webpage

The Committee has developed a Data Sharing Resources webpage to disseminate RDCRN data sharing information and resources to stakeholders and the public (Table 3) [13]. The webpage hosts the Checklist and Guidance documents and reviews the fundamentals of data sharing within the network. It points to NIH resources that also provide tips for writing a Data Management and Sharing Plan.

Pan‐Network Data Sharing Successes

The Rare Disease Diversity Coalition (RDDC), an initiative of the Black Women's Health Imperative, requested basic demographic data from RDCRN natural history studies to produce a descriptive report in support of informed advocacy. The Coordinating Center and the Committee designed a DUA between the Coordinating Center and the participating Consortia that authorized the Coordinating Center to manage and release data to the RDDC and included an Addendum outlining the specific data elements to be shared with the end user (the RDDC) and a Unilateral DUA in which the RDDC pledged to use the data as stipulated. The Coordinating Center executed twelve DUAs with Consortia Admin Cores for the purpose of sharing data with the RDDC and successfully transferred the data. Participating Consortia and the RDDC have produced and submitted for publication a manuscript describing the results. This partnership exhibits a successful pan‐network data sharing effort with a group external to the RDCRN.

Another example of a successful data sharing project was the development of a survey for individuals living with rare diseases on experiences related to COVID‐19 infection, changes in rare disease symptoms, access to healthcare services, and mental and physical well‐being during the COVID‐19 pandemic [18]. Over 3000 participants completed the survey in a network‐wide effort to ascertain the effects of the COVID‐19 pandemic in the rare disease community [18]. As a follow‐up, the Coordinating Center partnered with the NIH and shared data for participants who agreed to be recontacted, enabling the NIH to invite survey participants in a follow‐up study.

Discussion

Scientists are willing to share data using common models [19, 20, 21]. Patient advocates use research results to inform patients, help interpret findings, and assess their relevance [22, 23]. Data sharing is common and is a necessary feature of team science; scientific journals and government agencies expect it [20, 24]. NIH guidance has issued increasingly specific expectations for when and how data should be made available through public repositories [24, 25].

RDCRN data is precious due to the rarity of the conditions studied, and sharing it is of utmost importance. As multi‐site studies are necessary to enroll sufficient numbers of participants, sites must share data for a study to succeed [26]. RDCRN studies may include clinical sites based in multiple countries. Research participants support data sharing that may lead to new treatments and improved diagnosis [26]. However, special considerations may be needed to maintain patient confidentiality [27, 28]. Social media and other publicly available data can make it feasible to reidentify a person with unique characteristics, especially in combination with de‐identified research data [29, 30]. Information on family members collected in a study may also increase the risk [26].

Research data sharing is primarily determined by the Consent. We observed heterogeneity in how Consortia present data sharing to participants in the Consent. The policies of the institutions receiving the RDCRN awards (i.e., Admin Cores) contribute to the variation of data sharing language we observed. Institutional guidance and legal language influence how investigators approach data sharing. We sought to reduce variation in RDCRN protocols and Consents. For this reason, the Committee and the Coordinating Center codeveloped data sharing language to be included at the design stage of new protocols and developed the Checklist to ensure that the critical data sharing elements are more uniformly adopted. To further promote consistency, the Coordinating Center has included data sharing language checks in the RDCRN protocol review process, promoting recommended language to encourage alignment with NIH policies and expectations as outlined in the RDCRN RFA [3, 6, 8, 31, 32].

Research networks vary in scope, from a focused approach of one disease and one type of study design, like the Precision Interventions for Severe and/or Exacerbation‐Prone Asthma Network (PrecISE) [33] to covering an array of diseases and endpoints, like the Early Detection Research Network (EDRN) [34]. The RDCRN faces unique challenges, the most important of which is the heterogeneity of diseases studied, research questions asked, and study designs adopted. The RDCRN guidance for sharing data is necessarily flexible. The master protocol approach employed by the PrecISE Network facilitates uniform data collection and sharing, but would be impractical for the vast array of studies conducted by the RDCRN [33]. The EDRN has developed robust policies and agreements that govern data sharing among consortium members [35]. The Trial Innovation Network (TIN), an initiative of the NCATS Clinical & Translational Science Awards (CTSA) program, supports the use of standard agreements such as the Federal Demonstration Partnership Clinical Trial Subaward Agreement (FDP‐CTSA) and the Accelerated Clinical Trial Agreement (ACTA) to help reduce negotiation time [36, 37]. The RDCRN has promoted standard agreement templates, but negotiation times remained significant [36]. Notably, ACTA and FDP‐CTSA agreements are primarily designed for domestic sites and do not address the privacy laws and regulations of other countries, though separate attachments are an option [36]. These standard templates offer limited options to modify the language, for example, Attachment #7 to the FDP covers data transfer between sites, but does not fully address the principles we have identified in the Checklist. On many occasions, we have found it productive to be flexible on the specific language preferences of an institution, and ensuring adherence of the agreement to the principles enunciated in the Checklist. The Checklist summarizes a consensus achieved through an iterative process that considered a variety of perspectives and, in our opinion, is an effective strategy for reducing fragmentation, maintains a coherent ethical and legal framework, and offers an efficient solution for negotiating individual agreements. The approach can be labor intensive to negotiate with each institution participating in the RDCRN, and we were delighted to notice that the Funding Opportunity Announcements for RDCRN cycle 5 (2025–2030) included requirements that resonate with our discussions and findings [8, 38].

As a final consideration, we must acknowledge that the full potential of data sharing in rare disease research can be realized only in a highly collaborative environment. Spurred by the impact of the COVID‐19 pandemic on patients with rare diseases, which demanded a collaborative effort to study these effects, in 2020 we recognized the need for enhanced cross‐consortia collaboration. We aggressively promoted network‐wide initiatives to prioritize areas for collaboration and formed ad hoc groups to identify research gaps and strategies to further enhance collaboration, engagement, and dissemination. A forthcoming paper will highlight the strategy, infrastructure and accomplishments of the RDCRN in promoting collaboration. Thus, the results presented in this article are the product of a concerted effort to foster a culture of collaboration in the RDCRN and attest to the foresight of NCATS in outlining the structure of the network that promotes collaboration among researchers, patients, and Patient Advocates.

Conclusions

The RDCRN has adopted four main concepts in its data sharing guidance: (1) the Consent language should allow data sharing, protect confidentiality, and minimize the risk of participant reidentification, (2) the research database should include data elements representing the future use limitations selected by each participant, (3) consortium agreements, policies, and practices should recognize the authority of the award recipient (Admin Core) as the steward of the consortium data with the authority to share it, (4) the research protocol and relevant legal agreements should acknowledge that the participant and the enrollment site agree to share data and delegate control of the data to the Admin Core. The concepts outlined in this report represent the critical elements of data sharing within a research network such as the RDCRN. We hope that the information described here contributes to advancing Clinical and Translational Science by helping other networks working to operationalize data sharing.
