Two Painful Occipital Nodules in a Child Returning from South America: A Quiz
Authors: Ilaria Salvi, Marco Del Monte, Sara Naim, Angelica Pisati, Gianmaria Viglizzo
Published: 2026

An otherwise healthy boy aged six years presented to the emergency department with 2 painful nodular lesions on the occipital scalp that had progressively enlarged over approximately 2 weeks.

The child had recently returned from a trip to Brazil. According to the mother, the lesions initially appeared as small “insect bites” shortly after returning to Italy and gradually evolved into firm nodules. Each lesion displayed a central punctum.

On examination, 2 erythematous, dome-shaped nodules with central openings were noted in the occipital region of the scalp (Fig. 1). Following gentle squeezing of the lesions, a small amount of seropurulent exudate drained from the central opening. No systemic symptoms were present.

Diagnosis: Furuncular cutaneous myiasis due to Dermatobia hominis

After an unsuccessful attempt to induce larval expulsion by occluding the central openings with petroleum jelly in the emergency department, surgical exploration under local anesthesia was undertaken.

Surgical incision of both nodules resulted in the extraction of 2 intact larvae (Fig. 2), subsequently identified as Dermatobia hominis, the human botfly. After the extraction, the patient was prescribed oral amoxicilin/clavulanic acid as preemptive treatment of secondary bacterial infection of the wound.

Two larvae of Dermatobia hominis extracted from the lesions.

Furuncular cutaneous myiasis is a parasitic infestation caused by fly larvae developing within the skin. D. hominis is endemic to Central and South America and represents the most common cause of furuncular myiasis in travelers returning from these regions (1).

Adult botflies deposit their eggs on foliage or directly on hematophagous insects such as mosquitoes, which then transfer the larvae to animal or human hosts during blood feeding. After hatching, the larva penetrates the skin and develops within a subdermal cavity for 5–10 weeks, while maintaining contact with the exterior through a central breathing pore. The most commonly infected areas are the scalp, face, and extremities.

Clinically, lesions resemble single or multiple furuncles or inflamed nodules with a central punctum. Patients often report pain, seropurulent discharge, pain, pruritus, or a characteristic sensation of movement due to larval activity (2, 3).

Treatment of myiasis normally involves the extraction of larvae, with or without the administration of antibiotics to reduce the risk of secondary infection. Three main larvae removal methods (manual, mechanical, and surgical extraction) have been described. Manual extraction involves gentle pressing on the foruncle with hands, after the application of occlusive ointments (e.g. petroleum jelly) that may induce larval emergence by causing hypoxia. Mechanical extraction involves specialized devices to facilitate the extraction of larvae. Surgical extraction under local anesthesia is frequently required to avoid rupture of the larva, which can lead to secondary inflammation or infection (4).

In conclusion, while the distinct appearance of facilitates the diagnosis of D. hominis infestation in endemic areas, its identification can easily be delayed in temperate regions due to its rarity (5). Cutaneous myiasis should be considered in patients presenting with furuncle-like lesions after travels to endemic areas, particularly when associated with a central pore and subjective crawling sensation.
