Severe Ecthyma Gangrenosum in a Pediatric Patient With Neutropenia and a Novel HELLS Variant: Case Report
Authors: Mohammed A Almatrafi, Dhuha Alidrisi, Elaf Alhusaini, Ertwaa Shareef Alamri, Noor Alharbi, Suliman Abdullah Sharbaji, Abdullah Aburiziza, Amer Khojah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEcthyma gangrenosum (EG) is a rare and severe skin infection typically caused by Pseudomonas aeruginosa , often seen in immunocompromised patients. This report describes a case of a 19‐month‐old boy who presented with high‐grade fever, diarrhea, vomiting, and skin lesions over 5 days. Examination revealed respiratory distress, fever, tachycardia, hypotension, delayed capillary refill, and multiple erythematous skin lesions. Laboratory workup revealed pancytopenia with severe neutropenia and elevated inflammatory markers. The patient was started on empirical meropenem and vancomycin and later switched to cefepime after cultures identified pan‐susceptible Pseudomonas aeruginosa . He required multiple transfusions and fresh frozen plasma for coagulopathy. Whole‐exome sequencing identified a novel HELLS variant (c.1904G>A; p.Cys635Tyr), classified as a variant of uncertain significance. This case underscores the diagnostic and management challenges of EG, highlighting the importance of early recognition and a multidisciplinary approach.Keywords: ecthyma, immunocompromised hosts, neutropenia, Pseudomonas Aeruginosa

Introduction

Ecthyma gangrenosum (EG) is a rare and severe cutaneous infection, predominantly caused by Pseudomonas aeruginosa [1]. It was first described in 1897 and is most often observed in patients with compromised immune systems, particularly those with neutropenia [1]. Although EG can occur in otherwise healthy children [2, 3], most cases are associated with underlying immunodeficiency [1, 4]. EG typically begins as painless macules that evolve into hemorrhagic bullae, which rupture and leave necrotic ulcers with surrounding erythema [5].

In the pediatric population, EG is associated with a high mortality rate, particularly when complicated by sepsis or significant neutropenia [4]. The early diagnosis of EG can be challenging for untrained clinicians due to the rarity of the condition and its potential for rapid deterioration [2, 4]. Prompt initiation of appropriate intravenous antibiotics is crucial in reducing mortality, particularly in neutropenic patients [5].

Herein, we present a case of severe EG caused by Pseudomonas infection in a 19‐month‐old boy with neutropenia. The patient presented with high‐grade fever, diarrhea, vomiting, and skin lesions, accompanied by septic shock. He was treated with intravenous antibiotics. Although a definitive immunodeficiency was not identified, genetic testing revealed a novel variant in the HELLS gene. This case underscores the diagnostic and management challenges of EG, as well as the importance of early recognition and treatment.

Case Presentation

A 19‐month‐old boy presents to our hospital with high‐grade fever, diarrhea, vomiting, and skin lesions for 5 days. His parents also reported decreased oral intake and reduced activity. Four days prior to hospital admission, he sought medical attention at a local clinic, where he was diagnosed with cellulitis and sent home on oral Amoxicillin‐Clavulanate. Unfortunately, his condition did not improve. His past medical history included a prolonged hospitalization in the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) for respiratory support due to severe COVID‐19 pneumonia. At that time, he experienced moderate to severe neutropenia, which eventually resolved. There was no history of trauma, recurrent infections, or animal exposure. Family history was remarkable for consanguineous marriage and a possible diagnosis of leukocyte adhesion defect in a first‐degree cousin; however, the diagnosis was not confirmed by genetic testing. His immunization status was up to date.

On examination, the patient appeared ill, hypoactive, and in respiratory distress. His temperature was 39°C, respiratory rate 50 breaths/min, heart rate 175 beats/min, and blood pressure 85/35 mmHg, with a wide pulse pressure. Physical examination revealed dry mucous membranes, a delayed capillary refill time of 4 s, and multiple skin lesions. There were tender plaques of different sizes with central puncta resembling ecthyma, predominantly on the dorsal aspects of his feet and a large well‐demarcated dark skin necrotic lesion over the lateral aspect of the left thigh (Figure 1).

(A) Tender plaques of different sizes with central puncta resembling ecthyma on his feet. (B) Large well demarcated dark skin necrotic lesion over the lateral aspect of the left thigh.

Initial laboratory tests revealed pancytopenia, with a hemoglobin level of 7.9 g/dL, platelet count of 5 × 103/μL, and leukopenia with severe neutropenia (WBC: 1.39 × 103/μL; ANC: 0.26 × 103/μL). Inflammatory markers were elevated, including C‐reactive protein (257.7 mg/L), procalcitonin (19.52 μg/L), and ferritin (755.15 ng/mL). A peripheral blood smear showed marked anemia with mild microcytosis, hypochromia, anisocytosis, and poikilocytosis. Additional findings on blood film include mild polymorphonuclear leukocytosis with a marked left shift (bands 50%, juveniles 2%), marked thrombocytopenia, and no abnormal cells. Renal and liver function profiles were within normal limits.

In the emergency department, the patient was resuscitated with two boluses of normal saline and empirically started on meropenem (10 mg/kg/dose every 8 h) and vancomycin (20 mg/kg/dose every 6 h) while awaiting blood culture results. He was subsequently transferred to the PICU for further management.

The following day, blood cultures grew pan‐susceptible Pseudomonas aeruginosa . Antibiotic therapy was adjusted to cefepime (50 mg/kg/dose every 8 h). The patient's clinical course was complicated by profound bone marrow suppression, requiring multiple blood and platelet transfusions. He also received fresh frozen plasma for coagulopathy and thrombocytopenia (PLT = 5 × 103/μL, PT = 17.3 s, INR = 1.29, PTT = 26.3 s). Although his bacteremia had resolved, his cutaneous lesions progressed to hemorrhagic bullae with erosions and subsequent ulceration (Figure 2). Tissue cultures were obtained from his lesions and grew pan‐susceptible Pseudomonas aeruginosa as well. Magnetic resonance imaging of lower limbs reveals myositis of the anterior musculature of the left thigh, but no obvious collection or osteomyelitis (Figure 3).

MRI of the pelvis and lower extremities with increase signal intensity over the left thigh anterior musculature with subfascial edema and facial thickening (red arrow).

An immunodeficiency workup revealed normal immunoglobulin levels in accordance with his age (IgM = 0.06 g/L, IgG = 343 mg/dL, IgA < 0.5 g/L, IgE < 10 IU/mL), and lymphocyte subset panel by flow cytometry was unremarkable, except for a low absolute lymphocyte count (Lymphocytes absolute count = 2.429 103/μl, T‐lymphocytes (CD3+) = 1.520 cells/μL, T‐suppressor cells (CD3+/CD8+) = 476 cells/μL, T‐helper cells (CD3+/CD4+) = 977 cells/μL, CD4+/CD8+ ratio = 2.05), NK‐cells (CD16+/CD56+) = 56 cells/μL, B‐lymphocytes (CD19+) = 824 cells/μL. HIV screening was sent and reported negative. Whole‐exome sequencing (WES) identified a homozygous variant in the HELLS gene (c.1904G>A; p.Cys635Tyr), which has been classified as a variant of uncertain significance.

The patient underwent daily wound dressing by pediatric orthopedic service and continued on cefepime for 4 weeks from the first negative blood. He showed significant clinical improvement at the end of therapy and was transferred to a tertiary hospital for further care and follow‐up assessment. The patient experienced no further significant clinical infections during follow‐up visits, and all wounds were healed (Figure 4).

Discussion

EG is a serious and uncommon skin infection, predominantly caused by Pseudomonas aeruginosa , either with or without accompanying septicemia [1]. Although EG can occur in otherwise healthy children exposed to P. aeruginosa through environmental sources like water, soil, or contaminated objects [6], the majority of the cases are associated with underlying immunodeficiency [1, 7, 8]. Therefore, an immunological evaluation should be considered as part of evaluation of the EG patients [7]. Children who develop EG in the context of sepsis or neutropenia face a high mortality risk [7, 9]. Therefore, early identification and prompt initiation of appropriate antibiotic therapy are essential to prevent severe complications such as sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation, multi‐organ failure, and death [5, 10, 11].

Our patient had a novel homozygous variant in the HELLS c.1904G>A which has not been previously reported in the literature as either pathogenic or benign. In silico predictive algorithms, including PolyPhen and SIFT, indicate that this missense variant may impact the function of the HELLS protein. However, we could not perform an experimental study to confirm the variant effect on protein function. HELLS protein is a lymphoid‐specific helicase which is needed for efficient DNA methylation [12, 13], defects in HELLS protein are associated with immunodeficiency‐centromeric instability‐facial anomalies syndrome 4 (ICF4) [14, 15]. ICF4 is characterized by recurrent infections during childhood, often associated with hypogammaglobulinemia, as well as variable dysmorphic facial features [14]. These patients typically have hypomethylation of pericentromeric repeats [14]. Additional features, such as developmental delay, vary among affected individuals [14]. Unlike previously reported cases, our patient had normal immunoglobulin levels and did not exhibit dysmorphic features, making it less likely that this variant caused the typical ICF4 phenotype. However, given the rarity of this disease and the presence of a novel variant, it is possible that this variant may have contributed to our patient's phenotype, despite the absence of typical ICF4 features.

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is the most common cause of EG, accounting for about 74% of cases [1]. However, other pathogens, including Staphylococcus aureus , Streptococcus pyogenes , Escherichia coli , fungi like Candida species, and viruses such as herpes simplex virus, have also been implicated [1, 16, 17].

The management of EG necessitates a multidisciplinary approach, involving specialists from critical care, infectious diseases, dermatology, immunology, hematology, and surgery [1, 18]. Empiric antibiotic therapy should cover Pseudomonas aeruginosa and other potential pathogens [19]. Once culture results and sensitivity data are available, therapy should be adjusted accordingly. In our case, the patient was started on empirical meropenem and vancomycin and later switched to cefepime after cultures identified pan‐susceptible Pseudomonas aeruginosa . Surgical intervention may be required in cases where tissue involvement is extensive or when there is no improvement with appropriate antibiotic therapy [18, 19].

Conclusion

Ecthyma gangrenosum is a rare but potentially life‐threatening infection, particularly in immunocompromised patients. This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges associated with pseudomonas sepsis and EG in a young child. Early recognition, prompt initiation of antibiotics, and a coordinated interdisciplinary approach are crucial for improving patient outcomes.
