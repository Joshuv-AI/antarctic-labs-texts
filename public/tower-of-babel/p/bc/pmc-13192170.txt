Dual TP53 mutations in ovarian high-grade serous carcinoma combined with squamous cell carcinoma: a case report and systematic literature review
Authors: Chao-lian Li, Jie-ping Lu, Qiu-xing Lan, Li Li, Sha-sha Lu, Zhi-kai Chen, Jing Shi, Qing He, Peng Hou, Wen-bin Dai, Yang Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study reports a rare case of mixed ovarian carcinoma composed of squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and high-grade serous carcinoma (HGSC) arising from endometriosis, and provides a systematic review of the relevant literature.Case presentationA 59-year-old female presented with bilateral ovarian cystic lesions (4.6 cm on the left, 9.6 cm on the right) and a mural nodule in the right ovarian cyst. Serum tumor markers were CA125 57.50 U/mL and CA19-9 6.74 U/mL. The patient underwent total hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, omentectomy, and appendectomy. Histopathological examination revealed a mixed carcinoma of the right ovary composed of SCC and HGSC, with adjacent serous borderline tumor and endometriotic cyst. The left ovary showed a serous borderline tumor with endometriosis. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) identified two distinct TP53 mutations: a missense mutation in the HGSC component and a splice-site mutation in the SCC component. The patient received six cycles of paclitaxel and carboplatin chemotherapy. Within two months after completing the treatment, tumor markers had normalized and no recurrence was detected.MethodsWe describe a 59-year-old woman diagnosed with stage IA mixed ovarian carcinoma (SCC and HGSC), in which two distinct TP53 gene mutations were identified. Background

Primary ovarian squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is an exceedingly rare malignancy, accounting for less than 1% of all ovarian cancers [1]. Its histogenetic origins are diverse. Most reported cases arise from malignant transformation of mature cystic teratomas, but SCC can also develop from squamous metaplasia in endometriosis, metaplasia of the ovarian surface epithelium, or malignant transformation within other ovarian tumors such as Brenner tumors or endometrioid adenocarcinomas [2–5].

Previous studies have suggested that SCC may be associated with more aggressive biological behavior and poorer outcomes. Compared with high-grade serous carcinoma (HGSC), patients with ovarian SCC in advanced stages (IIB–IV) exhibit significantly worse overall survival, while early-stage (IA–IC) patients have similar prognoses to those with HGSC [6, 7].

The coexistence of SCC with other ovarian epithelial carcinoma subtypes is even rarer, most frequently seen as a combination of endometrioid carcinoma and SCC [1, 8–10]. However, SCC may also coexist with other histological subtypes such as clear cell carcinoma [11], or mucoepidermoid carcinoma [12], forming mixed carcinomas of the ovary. Among these, the simultaneous presence of SCC and HGSC has not yet been reported. The pathogenesis, histological characteristics, and clinical behavior of such tumors remain poorly understood, and systematic analyses are lacking.

In this report, we describe the first documented case of mixed ovarian carcinoma composed of SCC and HGSC arising from endometriosis. Through comprehensive analysis of the clinical presentation, imaging findings, histopathological features, and immunophenotypic profiles, we aim to enhance understanding of this rare tumor type and provide insights for accurate diagnosis and differential identification. Moreover, by integrating detailed clinicopathological and molecular data with a systematic review of previously reported cases, this study underscores the importance of molecular testing—particularly TP53 mutation profiling—in elucidating the complex histogenesis and intratumoral heterogeneity of such rare mixed ovarian carcinomas.

Case presentation

A 59-year-old postmenopausal woman presented with a history of adnexal masses discovered incidentally five years prior, for which she had not received further evaluation or follow-up. She had no significant past medical history and was married with children. Several days before presenting to our hospital, the patient developed sudden right lower abdominal pain. Physical examination revealed a patient in good general condition. Gynecological examination showed normal external genitalia, vagina, and cervix. The uterus was slightly enlarged with an irregular surface and moderate mobility but no tenderness. A cystic mass measuring approximately 8 × 7 × 6 cm was palpated in the right adnexal region, well-defined and non-tender; the left adnexa was mildly full without tenderness.

Imaging findings

Transvaginal ultrasonography demonstrated a 35 × 34 mm cystic lesion in the left adnexal region with relatively clear margins and poor internal sound transmission, containing punctate echogenic foci, most pronounced on the posterior wall (Fig. 1A). In the right adnexal region, a 79 × 83 × 71 mm cystic lesion was observed, also with poor internal sound transmission and punctate echogenicity (Fig. 1B). These findings suggested bilateral adnexal cystic lesions (O-RADS category 2).

Pelvic MRI revealed a round cystic lesion in the left uterine region measuring 4.6 × 3.3 × 3.2 cm with ill-defined borders and low T1 signal intensity. Another oval cystic lesion was identified anterior to the uterus measuring 9.6 × 8.6 × 7.7 cm, with slightly high T1 signal intensity and a nodular mural projection along the right posterior wall (Fig. 1C). Contrast-enhanced scans showed cyst wall enhancement in the left lesion with a small, non-enhancing mural nodule, while the right lesion exhibited uniform enhancement of the cyst wall and the mural nodule (Fig. 1D). These findings suggested bilateral ovarian cystic neoplasms, likely serous cystadenomas with a possible borderline component. No significant lymph node enlargement was observed.

Surgical procedure and gross pathology

Intraoperative findings revealed a cyst in the left ovary measuring approximately 6 × 5 × 5 cm. A cystic-solid mass measuring approximately 9 × 8 × 8 cm was identified in the right ovary, with a smooth surface and no papillary projections; however, intraoperative tumor spillage was observed. Intraoperative frozen section examination was performed. The frozen section diagnosis suggested a borderline serous tumor in the left ovary. In the right ovary, a borderline serous tumor was also suggested; however, in a focally thickened area of the cyst wall, disorganized glandular structures with squamous-like areas were observed, and malignancy could not be excluded. Given the suspicion of malignancy based on the frozen section findings, comprehensive surgical staging was undertaken, including total abdominal hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, omentectomy (a common site of metastasis in ovarian carcinoma), and appendectomy to ensure accurate surgical staging and exclude possible appendiceal involvement. Intraoperative exploration revealed no obvious lymph node enlargement. Combined with the imaging findings, which showed no evidence of metastasis, lymph node dissection was not performed. Grossly, the left adnexa showed a multiloculated cystic mass measuring 5.5 × 4 × 1.5 cm. The inner surface of the cyst contained a 1.2 cm papillary projection, and gray-yellow to gray-brown material was adherent to the wall. The right adnexa presented as a multiloculated cystic tumor measuring 8.5 × 6.5 × 1.2 cm, with variably thickened walls (0.2–0.6 cm). Within the cyst wall, two solid, white nodules measuring 1.3 cm and 1.5 cm in diameter were identified and were later confirmed microscopically as foci of mixed carcinoma. The remaining cystic wall surface appeared rough and covered with gray-yellow and gray-brown deposits. The fallopian tubes were grossly unremarkable, and the uterine myometrium exhibited scattered focal hemorrhagic areas. The omentum and appendix showed no macroscopic abnormalities.

Microscopic and immunohistochemical findings

Microscopically, the right adnexa demonstrated a mixed carcinoma (Fig. 2A) composed of two distinct malignant components: high-grade serous carcinoma (HGSC) (Fig. 2B) and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) (Fig. 2C). These malignant foci were surrounded by areas of endometriosis (Fig. 2D), serous cystadenoma (Fig. 2E), and borderline tumor (Fig. 2F), indicating a complex background of epithelial transformation. In contrast, the left adnexa consisted of endometriotic cysts associated with serous cystadenoma and a serous borderline tumor.

Immunohistochemical analysis further confirmed the biphasic nature of the tumor. The HGSC component showed diffuse, strong nuclear positivity for p53 (Fig. 3A), indicative of a TP53 mutation, along with diffuse strong positivity for PAX8 and p16 (Fig. 3B). WT-1 (Fig. 3C) showed moderate to strong positivity. Estrogen receptor (ER) expression was weak to moderate, while PR, CK5/6, p63, and p40 were negative, consistent with Müllerian differentiation. In contrast, the SCC component exhibited diffuse p53 negativity (Fig. 3D), also suggestive of a TP53 mutation, with strong positivity for CK5/6 (Fig. 3E). Interestingly, p63 and p40 (Fig. 3F) showed moderate positivity in a subset of tumor cells, predominantly located at the periphery of tumor nests. PAX8 and ER showed focal weak positivity, whereas PR, p16, and WT-1 were negative, confirming squamous differentiation and a distinct immunophenotypic profile compared with the HGSC component.

Molecular analysis (next-generation sequencing)

The results indicated that the tumor was microsatellite stable (MSS), suggesting the absence of high microsatellite instability (MSI-H) and mismatch repair deficiency–related molecular features. Further analysis revealed distinct TP53 alterations in the two histologic components: in the HGSC component, a TP53 missense mutation (c.713G > T, p.C238F) was detected. This mutation, located within the DNA-binding domain of the TP53 gene, represents a classic oncogenic alteration frequently observed in high-grade serous carcinoma and is closely associated with genomic instability and high tumor aggressiveness. In contrast, the SCC component harbored a TP53 splice-site mutation (c.782 + 2T > G), which may lead to aberrant mRNA splicing and impaired p53 protein function, indicating an independent oncogenic molecular event in this component.

No additional pathogenic alterations were identified in other major cancer-related genes, including BRCA1, BRCA2, HER2 (ERBB2), RET, NTRK1/2/3, BRAF, KRAS, PTEN, ARID1A, PIK3CA, and CTNNB1. Moreover, no germline pathogenic variants were detected, suggesting the absence of hereditary cancer predisposition syndromes in this patient.

Postoperative course and follow-up

Cytological analysis of ascitic fluid was negative for malignant cells. No malignancy was detected in the uterus, omentum, or appendix. Surgical spill was observed during the operation; according to the 2021 FIGO staging system [13], this case should be classified as FIGO stage IC1. The patient recovered uneventfully after surgery. During the postoperative course, a PET/CT scan performed two months after the operation revealed no evidence of residual disease or distant metastasis, and all tumor markers had returned to normal levels, indicating complete clinical remission. According to the NCCN Clinical Practice Guidelines in Oncology: Ovarian Cancer, six cycles of postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy are recommended for patients with high-risk early-stage disease (e.g., stage IC, high-grade serous carcinoma, or clear cell carcinoma) as well as for those with advanced ovarian cancer [14]. Therefore, the patient received six cycles of adjuvant chemotherapy with paclitaxel (240 mg) combined with carboplatin (700 mg). The treatment was well tolerated, with no significant adverse effects observed, and the patient remained in good general condition.

Methods of literature review

We performed a comprehensive literature search in the PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science databases for studies published between January 2015 and October 12, 2025. The search strategy combined the following keywords: “adenosquamous carcinoma” AND “Ovarian Neoplasms”, or “Ovarian Neoplasms” AND (“Carcinoma, Squamous Cell” OR “adenocarcinoma”).

As illustrated in Fig. 4, the study flow diagram summarizes the systematic literature review process for the present case report. A total of 593 records were initially identified. After removing 188 duplicates, 405 articles remained for title and abstract screening. During this stage, 394 articles were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria—primarily because they described non-ovarian primaries, lacked adenosquamous components, or were unrelated to the study topic. The remaining 11 articles underwent full-text review. Following detailed evaluation, 8 articles were excluded due to reasons such as reporting only squamous differentiation without mixed carcinoma, insufficient extractable data, unavailability of full text, or discordant final pathological diagnoses. Additionally, 3 more cases were identified through manual review of reference lists. Ultimately, 6 studies [8–12, 15]were included in this systematic review—3 retrieved from databases and 3 identified from other sources.

Results

This systematic review included and analyzed a total of eight cases of ovarian adenosquamous carcinoma, including the present case (Table 2). The patients’ ages ranged from 32 to 71 years, with a median age of 55 years, indicating that this tumor primarily affects middle-aged to older women, although younger patients can also be affected. The main presenting symptoms were abdominal in nature, such as pain, distension, or palpable masses, reflecting the lack of specific early clinical manifestations and the potential for delayed diagnosis or misdiagnosis.

Most tumors arose from endometriotic cysts or mature cystic teratomas, with endometrioid adenocarcinoma combined with squamous cell carcinoma (EC + SCC) being the most common histologic subtype [8–10]. Other histologic combinations included clear cell carcinoma with squamous differentiation (CCC + SCC) [11], mucoepidermoid carcinoma with squamous components (SCC + MEC) [12], and, in the present study, high-grade serous carcinoma combined with squamous cell carcinoma (HGSC + SCC). This indicates that the squamous component can coexist with various ovarian epithelial malignancies, particularly in association with endometrioid adenocarcinoma and endometriosis.

Laboratory tests revealed that serum tumor markers CA125 and CA19-9 were elevated in most cases, with CA125 being almost universally increased, consistent with its role as a common marker for ovarian epithelial tumors. In some patients, elevations in CA19-9 or SCC antigen were also noted, reflecting the activity of the squamous component. For example, in case 6, CA19-9 exceeded 1200 U/mL, suggesting possible intestinal-type or squamous differentiation. Overall, combined measurement of CA125 and CA19-9 may provide supportive diagnostic value in this tumor type.

Regarding tumor laterality and size, all eight cases involved unilateral ovarian lesions, with left-sided involvement in five cases; the present case affected the right ovary. Tumor diameters were generally large, ranging from 5.5 to 22.5 cm, suggesting that these tumors are often detected only after significant growth. Some cases, such as case 7, reached 22.5 cm and underwent incomplete staging surgery, reflecting the tumor’s aggressive potential and capacity for rapid enlargement.

Surgical management varied according to tumor stage and preoperative assessment. Early-stage patients generally underwent total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (TH + BSO), whereas larger or advanced tumors required cytoreductive or comprehensive staging surgery. The present patient underwent standard comprehensive surgery, including total hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, omentectomy, and appendectomy. Among the reviewed cases, five arose in the context of endometriosis and one from a mature cystic teratoma, highlighting endometriosis as a key predisposing lesion. The present case also originated from an endometriotic background.

Most patients were diagnosed at FIGO stage I–II, indicating that early detection is possible. Treatment primarily involved cytoreductive surgery combined with platinum-based chemotherapy, most commonly the paclitaxel-carboplatin regimen. Some cases received alternative regimens, such as cisplatin with epirubicin and cyclophosphamide, achieving favorable responses. Notably, case 6, which demonstrated platinum resistance, was treated with a combination of anti-angiogenic therapy, PARP inhibition, and PD-1 immune checkpoint blockade, highlighting the potential value of molecular profiling and targeted therapy in these rare tumors.

Follow-up data indicated generally favorable outcomes. Five patients remained alive with no evidence of disease (ANED), with a maximum follow-up of 66 months; one patient was alive with disease (AWD) at 15 months; and one patient died of disease (DOD) after 7 months. Early detection and complete surgical resection were associated with good prognosis, while later-stage disease or insufficient treatment correlated with poorer outcomes. In the present case, the patient achieved normalization of tumor markers three months after six cycles of paclitaxel-carboplatin chemotherapy, with no recurrence observed, underscoring the importance of standardized therapy in improving prognosis.

In summary, ovarian mixed carcinomas containing a squamous component are extremely rare and exhibit marked histologic heterogeneity. They are frequently associated with endometriosis and predominantly affect middle-aged women. Early surgical resection generally results in favorable outcomes. The presence of a squamous component is likely related to TP53 mutations, and molecular profiling can provide further insight into the pathogenesis. The present case of high-grade serous carcinoma with a squamous component harboring dual TP53 mutations represents the first reported instance of this molecular profile, expanding the molecular spectrum of ovarian adenosquamous carcinoma and highlighting the importance of genetic testing and individualized therapeutic strategies in future clinical management.

Discussion

Ovarian mixed carcinoma is a rare malignant ovarian tumor. Although cases of high-grade serous carcinoma (HGSC) with squamous differentiation have been occasionally reported [16], to our knowledge, a case explicitly combining distinct squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and HGSC components has not been previously described. In the present case, both ovarian tumors demonstrated the presence of endometriosis and benign or borderline serous tumor components, while a fallopian tube origin was excluded. According to the WHO Classification of Female Genital Tumors (5th edition) and related studies [17–21], although HGSC most frequently arises from the fallopian tube, it can also originate from ovarian precursor lesions, including serous borderline tumors (SBT) and endometriosis.

More specifically, epithelial cells arising in the context of SBT or endometriosis may acquire a key driver mutation in TP53, which confers genomic instability and a proliferative advantage, leading to clonal expansion and transformation into invasive HGSC [22]. In our case, the patient had a five-year history of benign ovarian cysts, and the HGSC component harbored a TP53 missense mutation, supporting the hypothesis that the HGSC likely originated from an ovarian precursor lesion (SBT or endometriosis).

Most reported cases of ovarian adenosquamous carcinoma arise from endometriotic cysts, and our case similarly involved endometriotic cysts. Mechanistically, endometriosis is a chronic inflammatory condition, and repeated cycles of tissue injury and repair create a microenvironment conducive to genomic instability, increasing the risk of malignant transformation [23, 24]. This underscores the role of endometriosis in ovarian carcinogenesis. Prior studies have suggested that ovarian SCC can arise from either teratomas or endometriosis [25, 26], indicating that the SCC component in our case may have originated from preexisting endometriotic cysts.

The development and progression of endometriosis are driven by complex interactions between hormonal, immune, genetic, and epigenetic factors. Mollie E. et al. reported that women with endometriosis have an overall 4.2-fold increased risk of developing ovarian cancer compared with women without endometriosis, with a particularly elevated risk (6.5-fold) for type I ovarian cancers. In patients with ovarian endometriotic cysts and/or deep infiltrating endometriosis, the overall risk rises to nearly ninefold, and the risk of type I ovarian cancer increases to approximately 18-fold [27]. Molecular studies have further shown that dysregulation of key signaling pathways, such as PI3K/AKT/mTOR and MAPK, occurs both in endometriosis and in associated ovarian cancers, suggesting a potential causal relationship at the molecular level [28]. Based on these findings, patients with ovarian endometriotic cysts or deep infiltrating endometriosis should undergo systematic ovarian cancer risk assessment and individualized preventive strategies and may be considered as a target population for screening and prophylactic interventions [29–31].

More than 95% of HGSCs harbor TP53 mutations, most commonly missense mutations, which are considered a defining molecular feature of this tumor type [32, 33]. In the present case, the HGSC component followed this pattern, exhibiting a classic TP53 missense mutation. By contrast, the coexisting SCC component demonstrated only a TP53 splice-site mutation. Angel Chao et al. [34] performed targeted sequencing of cancer susceptibility genes in 11 cases of ovarian SCC and found that TP53 was the most frequently mutated gene (approximately 82%), followed by PIK3CA (36%), PTEN (27%), and KMT2D (27%).

The presence of distinct TP53 mutation patterns in HGSC and SCC challenges the conventional assumption that the SCC component merely represents squamous differentiation of HGSC. If this were the case, both components should share the TP53 missense mutation present in HGSC. Our findings contradict this notion, suggesting a multiclonal origin: separate clones independently acquired TP53 splice-site and missense mutations, subsequently evolving in parallel toward SCC and HGSC, respectively, and ultimately merging locally to form a mixed carcinoma. Early multiclonal origin is an important mechanism underlying tumor heterogeneity and has been widely confirmed in genomic studies [35]. Importantly, this indicates that ovarian SCC does not necessarily arise from preexisting glandular carcinoma through squamous metaplasia but can emerge as an independent clonal entity.

In conclusion, mixed ovarian carcinoma containing both high-grade serous carcinoma (HGSC) and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is a rare and highly heterogeneous malignancy. This case provides several important insights: the tumor may originate from ovarian precursor lesions, such as endometriotic cysts, suggesting that endometriosis could play a significant role in malignant transformation; the HGSC and SCC components each harbor distinct TP53 mutations, supporting a multiclonal origin rather than simple squamous differentiation and reflecting the complexity of tumor heterogeneity; and molecular profiling in this case provided additional information that may be helpful for understanding tumor characteristics. Overall, this report not only enriches the molecular and histopathological spectrum of ovarian adenosquamous carcinoma but also underscores the importance of integrating clinical, pathological, and genomic information in understanding tumor biology and optimizing patient outcomes.
