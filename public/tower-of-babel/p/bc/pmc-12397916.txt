Flavour formation in the coffee substitute ‘lupin coffee’ related to the formation of acrylamide☆
Authors: Sabrina Stranig, Zuzana Ciesarová, Kristína Kukurová, Jana Horváthová, Barbara Siegmund
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A coffee substitute derived from sweet lupins (Lupinus angustifolius L.) was investigated for the first time with regard to the impact of the roasting process on flavour formation, acrylamide generation, and amino acid behaviour. Roasting was carried out batchwise at temperatures between 98 °C to 220 °C. 55 volatile compounds were semi-quantified using headspace SPME GC–MS. Maillard reaction products, particularly substituted pyrazines and furans, constituted the majority of the volatiles formed. The flavour of “lupin coffee” was described as strongly roasted and reminiscent of coffee, though less complex than traditional coffee. Flavour development became evident at temperatures of 195 °C and above. The highest acrylamide concentration detected was 111 μg/kg at 220 °C which is considerably below the EU benchmark levels established for coffee substitutes. This work provides a solid foundation for future investigations aimed at minimising process contaminants while preserving the desired flavour.

Introduction

With their characteristic sensory properties and stimulating effects on the central nerval system, coffee belongs to the most popular hot drinks worldwide. Even though consumption rates are already high, an annual growth rate is expected for the next five years (Statista, 2024). However, depending on agricultural practices and the required transportation, coffee consumption significantly contributes to the global CO2 emission. The CO2 footprint of a cup of coffee is calculated to account for 50–200 g CO2. The increasing demand for coffee leads to deforestation of rain forests and the effect of climate change increases the distribution of pests and diseases (Bilen et al., 2023; Adi et al., 2025). With increasing consumer awareness regarding the sustainability of food commodities, several coffee substitutes produced from domestic raw materials have gained an increasing market share. Next to considerations regarding sustainability, coffee substitutes may be interesting from nutritional point of view. Most coffee substitutes are free from caffeine and are partially lower in acidity than coffee which might make these products easier to tolerate for sensitive individuals. Depending on the raw material, phytoconstituents of coffee substitutes are reported to show health beneficial effects such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, chemopreventive, antidiabetic or cholesterol-lowering activities (Mostafa et al., 2021; Tahmouzi et al., 2024). However, consumers need to be aware that – depending on the raw material – some coffee substitutes contain gluten or specific allergens.

For coffee substitutes, plant material such as leaves, roots, seeds or fruits are roasted to mimic the flavour of coffee and are brewed as an infusion. Traditional raw materials which have been used in Europe for this purpose are wheat, spelt, rye, barley, malt, buckwheat, chicory, figs, dates and acorns. Some of these products have a historical tradition back to times when coffee was not easily available. Coffee substitutes may also be used as blends, with or without a certain percentage of coffee (Majcher et al., 2013; Mostafa et al., 2021). In addition to the beforementioned raw materials, lupin seeds represent a very promising domestic crop which can be cultivated in the temperate climate of Europe. Interestingly, in a small village in the North of Italy (Altrei) “lupin coffee” (“Altreier Kaffee”) has a longstanding tradition where its use dates back to the middle of the 19th century. The used lupin variety Lupinus pilosus has low demands on soil and climate; it was cultivated in this high-altitude village in the Alps of South Tyrol to produce “Altreier Kaffee” and contributed to the income of the local population (Heistinger & Pistrick, 2007). Meanwhile, “lupin coffee” has gained new popularity and can be purchased from several small- and medium-sized companies in Germany, Austria and Switzerland.

Lupin seeds that can be used for nutritional purposes belong to the so-called sweet lupins (white lupins, Lupinus albus, or blue lupins Lupinus angustifolius) which are – in contrast to the wild lupin varieties – low in the amounts of quinolizidine alkaloids. From a nutritional point of view, lupin seeds are interesting mainly due to their high protein content (approx. 40 %) and their low-fat content (approx. 4 %) (Sujak et al., 2006). The total carbohydrate and soluble sugars content account for 40–45 % and 4 %, respectively. Several food products like flour and cheese-type products or crisps made from lupins are already available on the market. The use of lupin seeds as raw material for the coffee-substitute “lupin-coffee” is also attractive for a certain consumer group, as “lupin coffee” is free from caffeine and – in contrast to coffee substitutes produced from grain – also gluten-free, which makes it attractive for patients suffering from coeliac disease or gluten-intolerance. It must be mentioned that lupin consumption may elicit allergic reactions in a small percentage of the population. However, a recent investigation suggests that the roasting process of the lupin seeds for the production of “lupin coffee” leads to a reduced allergenicity of the product. Even at rather low roasting temperatures (i.e. 140 °C), the thermal treatment of lupin seeds shows significant impact on the extractability and detectability of lupin proteins and DNA. β-Conglutin could not be detected when the lupin seeds were roasted at temperatures of 195 °C and higher which is of particular interest as β-conglutin isoforms were reported as potential allergenic triggers in lupins (Beyer et al., 2024; Foley et al., 2015).

Even though most coffee substitutes have a long history dating back to the 19th century, little is known about the sensory properties and flavour formation of coffee substitutes (Majcher et al., 2013; Mostafa et al., 2021). Majcher et al. (2013) performed a study comparing odour-active compounds identified in coffee-substitutes produced from roasted cereals, roasted beet root and roasted chicory. Most of the compounds which are described in this study are derived from the Maillard reaction. The results of this study suggest that it is not variety-specific compounds that characterise the aroma of the investigated coffee substitutes, but rather the distribution of the concentrations of the volatile compounds resulting mainly from the Maillard reaction.

In addition to flavour active compounds, the formation of so-called thermal processing contaminants, which might have negative impact on human health, have to be considered in roasted products such as coffee and coffee substitutes. Acrylamide, which is rated as 2 A – a probably carcinogenic to humans – by the International Agency for Research on Cancer, is probably the best-known representative of this group (Kocadagli & Gökmen, 2022). Acrylamide is formed in foods which are rich in amino acids such as asapargine and reducing sugars when heated to temperatures higher than 120 °C. Based on the toxicological relevance of acrylamide, the European Commission has set benchmark levels for targeted foods (Commission Regulation (EU) 2017/2158) which benchmark levels between 400 and 4.000 μg/kg for coffee substitutes prepared from cereals and for chicory, respectively, with the aim to minimize the human dietary acrylamide intake. A recent survey from the Belgian market reported elevated acrylamide concentrations in coffee substitutes (Szternfeld et al., 2025). These results demonstrate that there is an urgent need to investigate the formation of acrylamide formation in thermally processed foods such as coffee substitutes, particularly for products which show increasing market share.

To the best of our knowledge, there is no comprehensive study available neither on the sensory properties and the flavour formation nor on acrylamide in “lupin coffee”. Thus, in this study we aimed to investigate the flavour formation during the roasting of lupin seeds to gain a deep understanding of flavour compounds related to sensory properties. Furthermore, we investigated the formation of acrylamide in the course of the roasting process together with free amino acids. The results from this study will help to understand flavour and acrylamide formation in the interesting product.

For coffee, several mitigation strategies have been proposed to minimize the acrylamide concentration while maintaining the characteristic coffee flavour (Lupaescu & Oroian, 2025). These strategies range from the selection and treatment of the raw material, fermentation and enzymatic treatment to post-roasting storage prior to consumption. Little knowledge is available on potential strategies to reduce acrylamide in coffee substitutes (Abdel-Haleem & Abdel-Aty, 2021; Pitsch et al., 2020; Tahmouzi et al., 2024). The insight that we gain from this study on “lupin coffee” will contribute to develop targeted mitigation strategies to improve the quality of coffee substitutes produced from lupin seeds.

Materials & methods

Sample material and roasting process

The lupin variety Lupinus angustifolius L. cultivar Boregine was selected for the production of the lupin coffee substitute. The lupin cultivar Boregine belongs to the group of sweet lupins, is poor in quinolizidine alkaloids and, thus, suitable for the use as raw material for food and feed. Furthermore, the soil composition and also the climate conditions at site correspond well with the requirements of this cultivar. The lupins were grown and harvested in the South of Austria in 2022 and were dedicated for the commercial craft production of a coffee substitute (“lupin coffee”).

The lupin seeds were roasted in the turbulent flow of a professional pilot roasting machine (“Novoroaster”; Novoroast BS2, Novoroast GmbH, Lübeck, Germany) which produces a fluidised bed in a vertical roasting cylinder equipped with temperature sensors to monitor both the air stream temperature and the temperature of the surface of the lupin seeds. Fig. 1 shows the temperatures on the surface of the lupin seeds as a function of the temperature of the air flow. Prior to the roasting process, the lupin seeds were pre-dried in an air-stream with a temperature of 100 °C. To obtain the samples of interest, roasting of the lupin seeds was performed batchwise. Samples were drawn after reaching a lupin seed surface temperature of 98 °C, 120 °C, 140 °C, 160 °C, 175 °C, 195 °C and 220 °C, respectively (seven steps) which allowed a thorough monitoring of the analytes in the course of the roasting. For each temperature step, a batch of 2 kg lupin seeds was used. The roasting procedure was stopped when the required surface temperature of the seeds was reached, the lupins were cooled with a fresh air stream before being removed from the roaster and let to cool down to room temperature. When the roaster had reached room temperature, the roasting process of the next batch was started. Roasted lupin seed were transported to the lab and were then stored in closed glass vessels in the dark at 4 °C until further use. Unroasted lupin seeds of the same batch were used as reference material. Analysis of the volatile compounds and sensory evaluation were performed as timely as possible to avoid alteration of the samples. Analysis of volatile compounds

For the analysis of volatile compounds, headspace solid phase microextraction coupled to gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME GC–MS) was used. The separation and identification of the volatiles was performed on two GC columns of different polarity; semi-quantification was performed on the polar GC column. For the enrichment of the volatiles by HS-SPME, freshly ground lupin seeds of different roasting steps were weighed into 20 mL headspace vials (50 mg per sample for the analysis on the semi-polar column; 500 mg for the analysis on the polar GC column). In both cases, a glass-coated magnetic stir bar was added. For semi-quantification, acetophenone-d8 (98 %D, Sigma-Aldrich, Austria) was added as internal standard (525 ng acetophenone-d8 per 500 mg sample). Sampling was performed using a CTC Combi Pal sampler (CTC Analytics, Switzerland). For both GC-systems, the same HS-SPME conditions were used (sample equilibration in the oven of the autosampler for 1 min at 40 °C, followed by the enrichment phase for 10 min at 40 °C). A DVB/Carboxen/PDMS 50/30 μm 2 cm Stable Flex SPME fibre was used (Supelco, Bellefonte, USA), which was pre-conditioned in the injection system of the GC oven for 20 min at 270 °C before the first measurement. Immediately after the exposure of the SPME fibre into the headspace of the sample, the SPME fibre was transferred into the injection system of the GC for thermodesorption. Between measurements, the SPME fibre was left in the injection port for 20 min at 270 °C for re-conditioning before being exposed to the next sample.

For the GC analysis on the nonpolar column (HP-5, 30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm, Agilent, USA) an Agilent system was used (Agilent 7890 A with Agilent 5975C MSD detector, Agilent, USA) with the following parameters: temperature program starting at −10 °C for 1 min with a temperature ramp of 8 °C/min up to 260 °C (holding time 1 min). -10 °C was reached by blowing liquid nitrogen in the GC oven via a cryo-valve. Splitless injection was applied with an injector temperature of 270 °C. A SPME liner with a constant inner diameter of 0.75 μm was applied. Helium was used as carrier gas with a flow rate of 33 cm/s (constant flow). For the GC analysis on the polar column (ZB-Wax, 20 m × 0.18 mm × 0.18 μm, Phenomenex, USA) a Shimadzu system was used (Shimadzu GCMS-QP 2010 Plus, Shimadzu Europa GmbH) with the following conditions: temperature program starting at 40 °C for 1 min with a temperature ramp of 15 °C/min up to 240 °C (holding time 2 min). Split injection (1:5) was applied with an injector temperature of 250 °C. Here again, a SPME liner was used. Helium was used as a carrier gas with a flow rate of 40 cm/s (constant flow). For both GC systems, mass selective detection was performed in the scan mode (35–350 amu, EI (70 eV)).

The peak areas and recorded mass spectra from each of the gas chromatograms were analysed using the associated system software packages as well as the data deconvolution software PARADISe (Johnsen et al., 2017). The identification of volatile compounds was done by comparing the recorded mass spectra with those of authentic reference compounds and mass spectra from libraries (NIST23, Adams Library for Essential Oils). In addition, the linear-temperature programmed retention indices (RI) of the volatile compounds were calculated.

As it was not the aim to fully quantify the amounts of volatile compounds, but to follow their formation behaviour along the roasting process, semi-quantification was performed. Semi-quantification was done after separation on the polar GC column by comparing the peak areas obtained from the total ion chromatograms after deconvolution with the area of the internal standard acetophenone-d8, assuming a response factor of 1 for each compound. Sample sizes were standardised against a sample weight of 500 mg ground lupin seeds. In Table 1, amounts are given as approximate concentrations relative to the internal standard. Sensory evaluation

A trained panel of 13 sensory experts was used for sensory evaluation of the lupin samples. The selection of the panel-size was performed according to current recommendations (Djekic et al., 2021). At the time of the experiments, all panellists (seven females and six males, aged between 26 and 60) met the requirements given in EN ISO 8586 and had at least five years of experience in analytic sensory evaluation of various types of food commodities and the use of different sensory methods. In the course of their work in the panel, the panellists have attended sensory test sessions at least once a week. Their ‘sensory performance’ (i.e. ability to recognise basic taste stimuli, differentiation between similar samples, ability to describe various food commodities with specific descriptors etc.) have been re-evaluated on a yearly basis and documented for quality assurance purpose. Due to their long-standing sensory experience, the panellists show pronounced descriptive skills. In addition, to make them aware of potential sensory properties of the coffee substitute “lupin coffee”, specific training sessions were performed in which different types of commercially available coffee substitutes and regular coffee were evaluated followed by a discussion of descriptors among the panellists.

Ground lupin seeds as well as their infusions were examined by sensory evaluation. For the analysis of the ground lupin seeds, 4 g of freshly ground “lupin coffee” were weighed into 50 mL glass vessels with screw caps one day before sensory analysis, giving the samples time to equilibrate. For the sensory evaluation, the panellists were asked to open the lids of the cups, smell the samples and describe the perceived odour in as much detail as possible.

Infusions of the ground lupin seeds were prepared using a standardised French press procedure. For seeds from each roasting step and also for the unroasted seeds, 8 g of freshly ground lupin seeds was brewed with 500 mL boiling tap water. The infusion was allowed to brew for two minutes before pressing the plunger of the French press. 30 mL of each infusion was poured into 100 mL glasses and served immediately for the evaluation. The panellists were asked to immediately evaluate the freshly brewed samples and to assess the overall impression of the brews.

As the complexity and intensity of the flavour increased with increasing roasting time/temperature, we decided to present the samples in the order of increasing roasting temperature/intensity. To characterise the sensory properties, descriptive analysis in the form of ‘open-ended questioning’ (OEQ) (Piqueras-Fiszman, 2015) was applied. Based on their long-standing work in the panel, the panellists were familiar with OEQ. Given their high level of training and limited time resources, we chose not to perform duplicates, as replicating this qualitative descriptive task was not expected to yield additional value or increase statistical power.

The panellists were asked to describe (i) the odour of the ground lupin seeds, and (ii) the odour, taste and trigeminal sensations of the infusions. The given descriptors were collected and transformed into a list of accurate descriptors by grouping terms according to their meanings. Similar descriptors such as “green” and “leafy” were combined as “green/leafy”. Descriptors that were named fewer than three times in total and that could not be associated with any other descriptor were eliminated. Results were collected in terms of contingency tables and further statistically analysed.

Ethical statement with respect to sensory experiments

At the time of the experiments, no ethics committee was established at Graz University of Technology. However, all sensory experiments were performed according to the Declaration of Helsinki. Panellists were informed about lupin allergens before the first session. All panellists participated on a volunteer basis and gave their verbal consent before the start of the investigation. They were informed on their right to withdraw from their participation at any time of the project. Storage of data was performed in accordance with the European Regulation on Data Protection GDPR. The panellists gave written consent on storage of their data. However, any type of processing of the results obtained from sensory evaluation was performed without referring to the panellists' identity. Furthermore, prior to the project, the “lupin coffee” was analysed by an independent, accredited analytical laboratory for the potential presence of harmful compounds.

Analysis of free amino acids

The analysis of 20 proteinogenic amino acids – alanine (Ala), arginine (Arg), asparagine (Asn), aspartic acid (Asp), cysteine (Cys), glutamine (Gln), glutamic acid (Glu), glycine (Gly), histidine (His), isoleucine (Ile), leucine (Leu), lysine (Lys), methionine (Met), phenylalanine (Phe), proline (Pro), serine (Ser), threonine (Thr), tryptophan (Trp), tyrosine (Tyr) and valine (Val) – was performed by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) using an Agilent system (Agilent 1200 HPLC) with an Agilent 6410 Triple Quad detector equipped with the electrospray ionisation (ESI) interface (Agilent Technologies, USA). The samples were extracted into the aqueous phase with acetic acid, cleaned and directly applied for LC-MS/MS analysis without further derivatisation. The procedure of sample treatment, extraction, chromatographic and spectrometric conditions are described in detail by Ciesarová et al. (2023). The analytical separation was performed on a Purospher STAR RP-Sec column (150 × 4.6 mm, 2.7 μm; Agilent, USA) using an isocratic mixture of 100 mL of acetonitrile, 500 mL of aqueous solution of perfluorooctanoic acid (0.05 mM) and 1 mL of glacial acetic acid at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min at ambient temperature (25 °C). The ESI mass spectrometric detection was used in a positive mode; data were acquired using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) of specific mass transitions at specific retention times only. Calibration curves of the individual amino acids were prepared in the range from 0.02 to 4.00 μg/mL; an internal standard Glu-D3 was used for exact quantification. Limits of detection (LOD) were 6–10 μg/L; limits of quantification (LOQ) were 20–30 μg/L depending on the individual amino acids. Duplicate analysis was performed for each sample.

Acrylamide analysis

For the analysis of acrylamide, the samples were homogenized and extracted to acetic acid (aqueous solution, 0.2 mM), then pre-extracted to ethyl acetate to avoid the negative impact of salts in the chromatographic system according to the previously used method (Ciesarová et al., 2023). LC-MS/MS analysis was performed with an Agilent HPLC system (Agilent 1200 series) coupled to an Agilent 6460 Triple Quad detector equipped with an ESI interface (Agilent Technologies, USA). The analytical separation was performed on a Atlantis dC18 (100 × 3 mm, 3 μm) column (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) using an isocratic mixture of methanol:acetic acid:deionised water (5:1:500, v/v/v) at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min at 25 °C. The ESI mass spectrometric detection was performed in a positive ESI mode with the following conditions: drying gas (N2) flow 8 L/min and 350 °C, nebuliser pressure 50 psi, capillary voltage 2.5 kV, sheath gas flow 11 L/min at 250 °C, fragmentor voltage 50 eV, and collision energy 10 eV. For data acquisition, the multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode was used with transitions for acrylamide: 72 → 55 and acrylamide-D3: 75 → 58. The quantification of acrylamide was based on a calibration curve of the standard compound in the range from 5 to 200 ng/mL using acrylamide-D3 as internal standard. LOD of the applied procedure was 10 μg/L; LOQ was 15 μg/L. Duplicate analysis was performed for each sample.

Statistical analysis

Statistical data treatment was performed to determine (i) statistically significant differences in analytes depending on the roasting step, (ii) to identify correlations between analytes and sensory properties and (iii) to follow the development of sensory attributes along the roasting procedure.

To determine statistically significant differences between samples (p = 0.05), one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied. If statistically significant differences were observed within the data sets, the ANOVA was followed by post-hoc pairwise comparison using Tukey's Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test control (p < 0.05) to check for differences between single samples. To identify correlations between samples and volatiles, principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted using the Pearson correlation (p < 0.05). To follow the development of the sensory attributes, correspondence analysis (Chi-square distance, p < 0.05) after collecting the data in contingency tables was performed. For all statistical analyses, XLSTAT was used (Lumivero (2024). XLSTAT statistical and data analysis solution. https://www.xlstat.com/en).

Results

Sample material and roasting process

The sweet lupin (Lupinus angustifolius L.) cultivar Boregine belongs to the group of blue lupins and was selected by the local producer. For the production of the coffee substitute “lupin coffee”, cultivation of this variety has been performed successfully in local climate conditions for several years. The applied professional roasting machine, which is frequently used by small coffee roasters, generates a fluidised bed in the vertical roasting funnel and allows for strict temperature control of the whole roasting procedure by monitoring both temperatures of the airstream and the surface of the lupin seeds. Thus, the roasting process yielded highly reproducible products. For sampling, temperature steps of 20–25 °C temperature difference (as technically feasible) were chosen starting at the end of the drying process to be able to monitor the development of the analytes along the roasting process. Fig. 1 shows the roasting temperature (i.e. the surface temperature of the lupin seeds) in dependence of the airstream temperature. An additional sample was drawn at a roasting temperature of 240 °C However, this sample was already heavily burnt and was thus not included in the investigations. This is in accordance with a previous study on a lupin coffee substitute from Lupinus albus in which the authors describe that a roasting temperature of 240 °C is too high (Zaouadi et al., 2023). The formation of brown pigments can be easily observed; light browning can already be seen at 140 °C, whereas clear melanoidin production can be observed from 160 °C onwards. Effect of roasting on sensory properties

The sensory evaluation of the “lupin coffee” was performed with the aim of following the flavour development in the course of the roasting process. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to monitor the flavour development in coffee substitutes throughout production. However, as the flavour of coffee substitutes is supposed to mimic coffee flavour as closely as possible, sensory evaluation is indispensable in this context. In this study, a well-trained panel was used to receive precise information on flavour attributes. As the panellists had been specifically trained to describe the perceived flavour, “open-ended questioning” (OEQ) (Piqueras-Fiszman, 2015) was selected as the method of choice. This gave the panellists as much freedom as possible while describing the perceived flavour. OEQ was originally developed as a rapid sensory method to be used with consumers. However, several studies have demonstrated its applicability for analytical sensory questions (Piqueras-Fiszman, 2015; Pontesegger et al., 2023).

Fig. 3 shows the biplot as a result of OEQ for odour evaluation of the roasted and ground lupin seeds. A clear U-shaped development of the samples can be observed, starting with the raw material in quadrant II and ending with the highest temperatures in quadrant I, with the principal coordinate F1 and F2 describing more than 72 % of the model. The unroasted lupins did not show any roasted odour, but were described as “dry yeast” and “leafy”. Lupin seed exposed to a low thermal load (120–160 °C) developed odour described as “vanilla”, “chocolate/cocoa”, “nutty”, “smoky”-like. Roasted aromas including “popcorn” and “coffee” were noted starting from roasting temperatures of 175 °C and higher. Interestingly, a burnt aroma was also attributed to lupin seeds roasted at 195 °C and higher, indicating that from a sensory point of view, a roasting temperature of 220 °C is the maximum level that should be applied for “lupin coffee”. Formation of volatile compounds during roasting

A total of 55 compounds was identified and semi-quantified by the use of headspace SPME GC–MS in the lupin seeds in the course of the roasting process. A detailed list of the identified compounds and their approximate concentrations is given in Table 1. While only a very small number of compounds was present in the raw material and in the seeds that were exposed to low thermal load, the number of compounds as well as their amounts increased significantly with increasing temperature. A striking change in the volatilome occurs starting at a roasting temperature of 175 °C. Many of the compounds – particularly those which are formed in the course of the Maillard reaction such as alkylated pyrazines and substituted furans – could not be identified in the raw material and low roast seeds, but showed a striking concentration increase from 175 °C onwards.

Some volatile compounds that were most likely formed during the ripening process of the lupin seeds, among them the alcohols 1- and 2-pentanol, hexanol, 1-octen-3-ol, 2-methyl-1-butanol, the esters methyl hexanoate and γ-butyrolactone and α-pinene, the unique monoterpene detected in the lupin seeds, showed an opposing trend to most other volatiles. All of these compounds were identified in the raw material, some in large relative amounts with their concentrations decreasing when roasting temperature increased. Some of them (e.g. hexanol) were still present in low concentrations at a roasting temperature of 175 °C, but not at 195 °C or 220 °C. The concentration patterns of these compounds clearly indicate that these volatiles are genuine to unroasted lupin seeds formed by the plant during growth, ripening and storage followed by evaporation when temperature increased during the thermal treatment. These findings underline the results obtained from sensory evaluation – the flavour of the raw material and of low roast seeds was described as weak, green and vegetable-like which correspond to the sensory properties of these compounds. In contrast to the above-mentioned alcohols, two further alcohols – namely maltol and 3-methylphenol – were observed after thermal treatment. Maltol – which is a reaction product from the caramelisation process – was determined for the first time after roasting of the seeds up to 160 °C. 3-Methylphenol, determined at the roasting temperatures 195 °C and 220 °C, is most likely a pyrolytic breakdown product of lignin which is described as part of the insoluble dietary fibre of lupins (Keller et al., 2022).

Interestingly, the aromatic compounds toluene, xylene and ethylbenzene were part of the volatilome of the roasted lupins. As none of these compounds was identified in the raw material and in the seeds that had undergone low roasting conditions, but rather at a minimum roasting temperature of 175 °C, it can be concluded that the aromatic compounds are not a contamination of the raw material, but formed in the course of thermal treatment. Thermal depolymerisation of lignin from feedstock as a sustainable source of BTX benzene-toluene-xylene was reported recently (Ramirez Brenes et al., 2023). Even though for industrial thermal depolymerisation of lignin, temperatures of 400 °C are required, the results of this study indicate that this process already starts at the temperatures that were applied for roasting the lupin seeds (Keller et al., 2022; Ramirez Brenes et al., 2023).

Sulphur compounds are reported to play an important role for the flavour of coffee (Dulsat-Serra et al., 2016; Toci & Boldrin, 2018). 2-Methylthiophene was the only sulphur compound that could be identified in the roasted lupin seeds when the seeds were roasted to temperatures up to 195 °C and 220 °C. It is well known that sulphur compounds show extraordinarily low odour thresholds. We suppose that one reason why only one sulphur compound could be detected in the present study was the lack of sensitivity of the used method for this compound class. First analyses were performed using comprehensive GC x GC coupled to a sulphur selective detector. The preliminary results indicate the presence of a large number of sulphur-containing compounds in roasted lupin seeds with increasing roasting temperature (results not shown). This compound class formed in this matrix will be in the focus of future investigations.

The compound group of substituted pyrazines and substituted furans formed the largest group within the “lupin coffee” volatilome. Volatile compounds from both groups were formed during the Maillard reaction and most likely contributed to the roasted, coffee-like and, specifically for furans, also to malty notes of the product. For the formation of most of the substituted pyrazines, a high thermal load was required. 2,5-Dimethylpyrazine was the only pyrazine that was already detected at a temperature of 98 °C, a temperature that is primarily used to dry the seeds prior to roasting rather than to actually roast the seeds. The highest relative amount of 2,5-dimethylpyrazine was observed at 195 °C; a further temperature increase led to evaporation of this compound. Comparable behaviour was observed for its isomer 2,6-dimethylpyrazine. In comparison with other pyrazines, the formation of 3-ethyl-2,5-dimethylpyrazine required low thermal input; minor amounts were already observed at a roasting temperature of 160 °C. As for all other substituted pyrazines, an exponential increase was observed from 175 °C to 220 °C. The high relative overall pyrazine concentrations correspond well with the sensory impressions of the roasted seeds as well as of the French press infusions, which were all perceived as strongly roasted and coffee-like.

Ten substituted furans were detected in the roasted seeds in sometimes high concentrations. Alkylated furans as part of the coffee volatilome were described earlier (Becalski et al., 2016). However, based on the furans' generally higher odour thresholds (Yuan et al., 2023), their impact on sensory properties is considered to be lower than that of the substituted pyrazines. The formation of furanoid compounds also requires high thermal load (175 °C and higher). An exponential increase in concentrations was observed for almost all of the identified compounds (Table 1). This is in accordance with data from the literature; in French fries, for example, 190 °C was required to form high levels of alkylated furans (Fromberg et al., 2014). In contrast to all other substituted furans, 2-pentylfuran is the only alkylated furan that was already detected at 140 °C, again with increasing amounts at higher temperatures. In contrast to other substituted furans that were detected in this matrix, 2-pentylfuran is not only formed via thermal browning reactions, but also well-known as a reaction product of lipidoxidation. The shape of the 2-pentylfuran formation curve is in accordance with the formation curve of the aldehyde hexanal. A strong increase in hexanal concentration was observed from 140 °C – similar as for 2-pentylfuran. As both compounds are derived from oxidation of linoleic acid, this behaviour supports the conclusion that in contrast to other substituted furans, the origin of 2-pentylfuran is more likely lipid oxidation than non-enzymatic browning reactions (Frankel, 2005).

While several substituted furans were detected in sometimes large relative amounts, it has to be noted that in this study 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) was only detected in concentrations below the LOQ at roasting temperatures of 195 °C and 220 °C (results not shown). HMF, an intermediate in the Maillard reaction and classified as probably carcinogenic to humans (2 A) by the IARC, is of interest in products such as coffee or coffee substitutes as, next to bread, coffee is considered the most important contributor to dietary HMF intake (Capuano & Fogliano, 2011) and seems to be an accelerating carbonyl compound for acrylamide formation (Gökmen et al., 2012; Hamzalioglu & Gökmen, 2020). In model studies, it was shown that depending on the composition, 180 °C for at least 5 min was necessary to produce HMF (Gökmen et al., 2012). In this study, we suppose that one reason for the low HMF concentrations is that the whole roasting process, starting at room temperature to the final roasting temperature did not last longer than 16 min, even when the final temperature of 220 °C was reached. However, further investigations are required as the use of HS-SPME under the described conditions might also be a limiting factor for the determination of HMF in roasted lupin seeds.

In studies on the development of flavour compounds in the course of production, it is relevant to correlate the results from flavour analysis with those from sensory evaluation to gain a deeper understanding of the impact of formed compounds on the flavour of the product. The formation of roast flavour showed a strong correlation with Maillard reaction products. Free amino acids

Free amino acids play an important role as reaction partners in the Maillard reaction. Thus, they are important reaction partners also in the course of roasting lupin seeds. Fig. 6 shows the concentrations of 19 free proteinogenic amino acids in the lupin seeds along the roasting process. The amino acid cysteine was not detected in any of the samples. Many of the investigated amino acids showed a similar concentration pattern in the course of the roasting process. Ala, Arg, Asp, Gly, His, Lys, Ser and Tyr showed a more or less strong concentration increase up to a roasting temperature of 175 °C, followed by a sharp decrease in concentrations with increasing thermal load. At temperatures of 195 °C and higher, only small concentrations of these amino acids were detected in the roasted lupin seeds. Asn, Gln, Ile, and Leu showed increasing concentrations up to a roasting temperature of 120 °C, lowered concentrations up to 175 °C, followed by a sharp decrease at higher roasting temperatures. Gln could not be detected from 195 °C upwards. Met showed a comparable pattern, however, the first decrease in concentration was already observed in the seeds roasted to 120 °C. Phe, Pro, Thr, Trp and also Glu and Val (even though not that expressed) showed interesting concentration curves. While concentrations of the free amino acids increased up to 120 °C, lower concentrations were observed in the seeds roasted at 140 °C, followed by a second increase in concentration up to 175 °C. The increase in concentration with increasing temperatures indicates a thermal degradation of protein structures, while the decrease in concentrations implies a reaction with any reducing sugar moiety or any other reactive intermediate in the course of the Maillard reaction. Amino acids are described to be reaction partners of the Maillard reaction not only in the initial step reacting with a reducing sugar under the formation of N-substituted glycosylamine followed by the reaction to the corresponding Amadori- or Heyns product, but also as reaction partners in the advanced stages of the Maillard reaction (de Oliveira et al., 2016; Shakoor et al., 2022). The differences in the concentration curves of the investigated amino acids as described in the previous paragraph might be an indicator of different reactivity at the different stages of the Maillard reaction. The generally observed decrease of free amino acids in relation to thermal load was also reported for bread (Çelik & Gökmen, 2020) and during the roasting of Arabica coffee (Casal et al., 2005) as well. The observed decrease in amino acids is in good accordance with results published in one of our previous studies showing a reduced extractability of lupin proteins roasted at 195 °C and 220 °C, indicating their contribution to the Maillard reaction (Beyer et al., 2024).

Free amino acids and umami-taste

The contribution of glutamate and – to a lower extent – aspartate to umami flavour is well-known (Diepeeveen et al., 2022). Even though umami taste does not play a significant role in coffee, it is worth considering the development of free amino acids and umami perception during the roasting of lupin seeds. The highest concentration for Asp was observed at a roasting temperature of 175 °C and for Glu at 160 °C, with concentrations still high at 175 °C. The results from sensory evaluation of the French press infusions of the lupin seeds roasted at different temperatures showed that umami perception played a role for low-roasted seeds (Fig. 4). The infusion of seeds roasted to 120 °C showed strong correlations with the attribute “soup seasoning” which is supposed to be an indicator for the perception of umami. Samples roasted to 160–175 °C were clearly described as “umami” which corresponded with the highest observed concentrations for Asp and Glu in these samples. French press infusions prepared from lupin seeds that were roasted to 195 °C and 220 °C did not show correlations with umami taste, but, in addition to roasted, coffee aroma, these samples exhibited bitterness and astringency. This is in good agreement with the sharp decrease in free amino acids at higher thermal load; free amino acids react with any reaction partner at any stage of the Maillard reaction. Thus, umami taste is lost, whereas bitterness and astringency increase, most likely due to the formation of the brown pigments (i.e. melanoidins) which are considered to account for bitterness and astringency (Cordoba et al., 2020). From the roasted lupin seeds, the formation of brown pigments at roasting temperatures of 195° and 220 °C can easily be observed from the optical impression of the samples (Fig. 2).

Free amino acids and acrylamide

Acrylamide was described as a Maillard reaction product in thermally treated food more than two decades ago (Mottram et al., 2002). Since then, the formation, presence and fate of acrylamide in various food commodities including coffee has been intensely investigated (Anese, 2023; Kocadagli & Gökmen, 2022; Soares et al., 2015). Acrylamide is classified as probably carcinogenic to humans in group 2 A by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (International Agency for Research on Cancer: Some industrial chemicals. IARC monographs on the evaluation of the carcinogenic risk of chemicals to humans. Lnternational Agency for Research on Cancer, 1994). Coffee is regarded as a main contributor to dietary acrylamide exposure (Anese, 2023; Nehlig & Cunha, 2020); however, the impact on human health is the subject of controversial discussion. Even though coffee is one of the main dietary sources for acrylamide, moderate coffee consumption reduces overall cancer incidence in humans (Nehlig & Cunha, 2020). Regardless of these findings, the European Union has set benchmark levels for several heat-treated food commodities, including coffee and coffee substitutes (Commission Regulation (EU) 2017/2158) with the aim to reduce the dietary exposure of humans. At present, the benchmark level is set to 400 μg/kg for roasted coffee, 500 μg/kg for coffee substitutes prepared from cereals, and as high as 4.000 μg/kg for coffee substitutes made from chicory. In the current version of this regulation, no benchmark levels are mentioned for coffee substitutes produced from pulses such as lupin seeds.

The amino acid Asn and, to a lesser extent, Gln serve as the amino compounds for the formation of acrylamide via the Maillard reaction (Anese, 2023; Ciesarová et al., 2023; Strocchi et al., 2022). In ground unroasted lupin seeds, the Asn concentration (171 mg/kg) was one of highest amino acid concentrations compared to other amino acids detected in this matrix which predicted a remarkable potential of acrylamide formation during heat treatment at 120 °C and above. Only three other amino acids in raw ground lupin seeds were detected at higher levels than Asn (Arg: 511 mg/kg; Glu: 398 mg/kg; Trp: 174 mg/kg) (Fig. 6). However, the free Asn content in “lupin coffee” was remarkably low compared to coffee beans and chicory roots. Green coffee beans are typically significantly higher in free Asn, ranging from 280 to 960 mg/kg (Murkovic & Derler, 2006). In raw chicory roots, which is a typical coffee substitute, the concentration of free asparagine was reported to be dependent on the chicory variety and the year of harvest and ranged from 444 to 2786 mg/kg (Loaëc et al., 2014). The concentration of most of the investigated amino acids in “lupin coffee” increased with higher roasting temperatures with a maximum at 175 °C. However, the concentration of Asn increased only up to a roasting temperature of 140 °C, with the highest concentration being approx. 222 mg/kg. A slight decrease in Asn concentration was observed with increasing temperatures of 160 °C and 175 °C. At 195 °C, the Asn concentration was determined to be less than 5 mg/kg (Fig. 6). Similarly, Gln concentration increased from a level of 70 mg/kg in unroasted ground lupin seeds to a maximum concentration of 105 mg/kg at 120 °C. A sharp decrease in Gln concentration to 25 mg/kg and below was observed when the temperature reached 140 °C and above (Fig. 6). The decrease in both amino acids correlated strongly with the simultaneous formation of acrylamide which was not detected in lupin seeds roasted at low thermal load. Acrylamide was detected in a low concentration (13 μg/kg) in seeds roasted to 175 °C, with a sharp increase when the temperature reached 195 °C and 220 °C. A maximum acrylamide concentration of approx. 111 μg/kg was detected in the ground lupin seeds roasted to 220 °C. It should be noted that this maximum acrylamide concentration is significantly lower than the EU benchmark levels set for coffee or coffee substitutes as mentioned above (Commission Regulation (EU) 2017/2158). In contrast, the highest average acrylamide levels were reported in coffee substitutes (890 μg/kg), roasted coffee (256 μg/kg), and instant coffee (229 μg/kg), respectively (European Food Safety Authority, 2012). Also two recently published surveys from the Belgian and the Spanish market reported average acrylamide concentrations higher than those found in this study (Delgado-Andrade et al., 2024; Szternfeld et al., 2025).

Conclusion and future perspectives

“Lupin coffee”, a coffee substitute made from sweet lupins, represents a promising alternative to traditional coffee, particularly for consumers seeking a sustainable, domestically produced and gluten-free hot beverage. This is the first comprehensive study investigating the dynamics of flavour formation in relation to acrylamide formation in lupin-based coffee substitutes.

The results indicate that strict temperature control during roasting is required. A minimum roasting temperature of 195 °C is necessary to develop the characteristic roasted flavour reminiscent of coffee, whereas temperatures exceeding 220 °C result in undesirable burnt flavour. Acrylamide was detected at a maximum concentration of approx. 110 μg/kg, which is significantly below the EU benchmark levels for coffee and coffee substitutes. Compared to other coffee alternatives – particularly chicory-based products, which have reported acrylamide levels of 2.500 μg/kg or higher – the consumption of “lupin coffee” contributes substantially less to the overall dietary acrylamide intake. A comparison of asparagine levels in raw materials for coffee substitutes with the resulting acrylamide concentrations suggests that, as in other coffee substitutes, asparagine is the main precursor for acrylamide formation in “lupin coffee”. However, this assumption requires further experimental validation.

Despite several limitations, our study concludes that sweet lupins – although historically used for this purpose since the 19th century – hold considerable potential as a substrate for a coffee substitute. While technological interventions such as precise temperature control appear straightforward, further research is required to evaluate the impact of growing conditions and sweet lupin varieties on the final product quality. It may also be worthwhile to explore acrylamide mitigation strategies developed for traditional coffee, which could help reduce acrylamide levels in lupin coffee while preserving or even enhancing its flavour complexity. Such approaches could contribute to creating a distinctive lupin-based coffee substitute with minimal contribution to dietary acrylamide intake.
