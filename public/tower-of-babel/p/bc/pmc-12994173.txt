Deubiquitinase OTULIN dampens RIG-I-dependent antiviral signaling by removing linear ubiquitination from TRAF6
Authors: Rong-Chun Tang, Zhiheng Tang, Shuang-Shuang Yu, Ao Zhang, Shijin Geng, Hengxiang Yu, Yunxuan Zhou, Lan Zhang, Xiaoyun Liu, Jun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceInnate immunity serves as the first line of the host defense against microbial invasion. Our study uncovers the previously uncharacterized role of OTULIN in RLR-mediated innate immunity. We identify TRAF6 as a substrate for linear ubiquitination, a modification crucial for the full activation of RIG-I-mediated IFN-I signaling. Mechanistically, LUBAC enhances antiviral immunity by promoting the linear ubiquitination of TRAF6. In contrast, OTULIN dampens antiviral immunity by facilitating the deubiquitination of TRAF6, thereby suppressing the antiviral innate immune response. These findings position OTULIN as a key modulator of immune homeostasis.

The RIG-I-like receptor (RLR) family, comprising RIG-I, melanoma differentiation-associated gene 5 (MDA5), and laboratory of genetics and physiology 2 (LGP2), constitutes a class of crucial cytosolic pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) for detecting RNA viruses (1). RIG-I and MDA5 serve as the primary sensors that directly recognize distinct viral RNA motifs and initiate antiviral innate immune signaling. Specifically, RIG-I detects short double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) bearing a 5’-triphosphate terminus, while MDA5 recognizes long dsRNA structures (2). Viral RNA binding to the helicase domain of RIG-I induces a conformational change that exposes the N-terminal caspase recruitment domains (CARDs) (3, 4). These exposed CARDs of RIG-I subsequently recruit and bind to the CARD of mitochondrial antiviral signaling (MAVS), a critical adaptor that triggers the recruitment and activation of downstream kinases, including TBK1 and IKKε (5–7). This signaling cascade culminates in the production of IFN-I and proinflammatory cytokines. LGP2, which lacks CARDs, does not initiate signaling directly but functions as a critical regulator by modulating the activities of RIG-I and MDA5 (8). Precise regulation of the RLR pathway is therefore essential to mount an effective antiviral defense while preventing excessive inflammation and maintaining immune homeostasis. Dysregulation may result in inadequate viral clearance or pathological immune activation (9). Thus, elucidating the molecular mechanisms that fine-tune RLR signaling is critical for understanding the signaling in both physiological and pathological conditions, as well as for developing targeted therapeutic strategies against viral infections and immune disorders.

Ubiquitination is a ubiquitous posttranslational modification that governs a wide range of cellular processes through both proteolytic and nonproteolytic mechanisms (10, 11). This process involves covalent attachment of ubiquitin to target proteins in multiple forms. Monoubiquitination adds a single ubiquitin to a substrate (12, 13), whereas polyubiquitination entails the formation of ubiquitin chains, where ubiquitin molecules are linked through one of their seven lysine residues (K6, K11, K27, K29, K33, K48, and K63) (14). Additionally, ubiquitin can be conjugated via its amino-terminal methionine residue (Met1), resulting in linear ubiquitination, also known as Met1 linkage (15). Linear ubiquitination is uniquely catalyzed by the linear ubiquitin chain assembly complex (LUBAC), a multisubunit E3 ubiquitin ligase complex comprising HOIP, HOIL-1, and SHARPIN (15). Like other ubiquitin modifications, linear ubiquitination is reversible and counteracted by deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs), which hydrolyze ubiquitin chains from target proteins (16, 17). While numerous DUBs regulate other types of ubiquitination, only a few exhibit specificity for linear ubiquitin chains, including OTULIN and CYLD-SPATA2 (16–18). OTULIN, also known as FAM105B or Gumby, is the principal mammalian DUB with exclusive specificity for cleaving linear ubiquitin chains, thereby playing a critical role in maintaining the balance of linear ubiquitination in cellular processes (16).

Linear ubiquitination is best characterized within the TNF signaling pathway, where it critically regulates NF-κB activation, inflammation, and cell death (19). The accumulation of linear ubiquitin chains on NF-κB Essential Modulator (NEMO/IKKγ) facilitates the activation of NF-κB (20). Linear ubiquitination broadly modulates different types of cell death such as apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, and autophagy (21). For instance, in the RIG-I-induced pathway of apoptosis (RIPA) during viral infection, linearly ubiquitinated IRF3 binds Bax and translocates to mitochondria to promote cytochrome c release and restrict viral infection (22). The linear ubiquitination of RIP1 regulates apoptosis and necroptosis (23, 24). Additionally, LUBAC has been reported to regulate the activation of key signaling molecules, including STAT1, STAT3, and STING activation, etc. (25–28). These established roles underscore the broad regulatory significance of linear ubiquitination in immune responses, cell death, and cellular homeostasis. Despite these advances, the specific function of OTULIN in RNA virus-induced IFN-I signaling remains to be elucidated.

This study identified the deubiquitinase OTULIN as a negative modulator of RLR signaling. We demonstrated that OTULIN or LUBAC components physically interact with TRAF6. Functionally, LUBAC catalyzes the linear ubiquitination of TRAF6, whereas OTULIN reverses this modification. Notably, the linear ubiquitination of TRAF6 is beneficial for the full activation of the antiviral innate immune response. Collectively, our findings elucidate a regulatory mechanism wherein the antagonistic actions of linear ubiquitination and deubiquitination precisely calibrate the magnitude of the antiviral immune response.

Results

OTULIN Attenuates RLR-Dependent IFN-I Signaling.

To investigate the relationship between viral infection and linear ubiquitination, HEK293T cells were initially infected with Sendai virus (SeV) and Vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV). Immunoblot analysis revealed that the linear ubiquitination levels gradually increased following RNA virus infection (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B). A similar increase in linear ubiquitination was observed in HeLa cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 C and D) and immortalized bone marrow–derived macrophages (iBMDMs) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 E and F), suggesting that upregulation of linear ubiquitination represents a common cellular response to RNA viruses across different cell types. This observation prompted us to examine the functional relevance of this modification in antiviral immunity. We focused on OTULIN, the principal deubiquitinase responsible for cleaving linear ubiquitin chains.

To delineate the functional role of OTULIN in antiviral innate immune response, dual-luciferase reporter assays were initially performed. Overexpression of OTULIN reduced NF-κB and IFNβ reporter activation induced by SeV infection in HEK293T cells (Fig. 1 A and B), and suppressed IFNβ reporter activation triggered by the RNA virus mimics poly (I:C) (Fig. 1C). Notably, the enzymatically inactive OTULIN mutant C129S failed to exert this inhibitory effect (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 G–I). Consistent with these findings, OTULIN downregulated the expression of IFNB1, CXCL10, and IL6 (Fig. 1 D–F). Furthermore, the bioactivity of secreted IFN-I was monitored using a luciferase reporter 2fTGH cell line in which luciferase is driven by the IFN-stimulated response element (ISRE). Overexpression of OTULIN reduced the production of IFN-I (Fig. 1G).

We next assessed the phosphorylation of key signaling molecules in the antiviral innate immune response. OTULIN potently inhibited SeV-induced phosphorylation of IκBα (S32), TBK1 (S172), and IRF3 (S396) in HEK293T cells (Fig. 1 H and I). Furthermore, to evaluate the effect of OTULIN on viral replication, we infected cells with VSV-GFP viruses. QPCR (Fig. 1J), immunoblotting (Fig. 1K), and fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 1 L and M) collectively revealed that overexpression of OTULIN enhanced VSV-GFP levels, indicating that OTULIN can promote viral replication. Taken together, these results demonstrate that OTULIN functions as a negative regulator of RIG-I-dependent IFN-I signaling.

OTULIN Knockout Augments RLR Signaling, While Its Reconstitution Restores Homeostasis.

To investigate the functions of OTULIN in physiological conditions, the endogenous expression of OTULIN was knocked out in HeLa cells and iBMDMs using CRISPR/Cas9 (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). In HeLa cells, OTULIN knockout augmented SeV-induced NF-κB and IFNβ reporter activation (Fig. 2 B and C), and poly (I:C) induced IFNβ reporter activation (Fig. 2D). Consistent with this potentiation, SeV-induced transcription of IFNB1, CXCL10, and IL6 was significantly upregulated (Fig. 2 E–G). Similar results were observed in iBMDMs, in which Otulin knockout enhanced the transcription of Ifnb1, Ifna4, Cxcl10, and Il6 upon SeV infection (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 B–E). Crucially, lentivirus-mediated reconstitution of OTULIN in OTULIN-KO cells effectively reversed this enhanced phenotype, restoring the suppression of SeV-induced transcription of IFNB1, CXCL10, and IL6 to near wild-type levels (Fig. 2 E–G). Moreover, OTULIN and Otulin knockout significantly increased SeV-induced production of IFN-I (Fig. 2H and SI Appendix, Fig. S2F), while lentiviral reconstitution of OTULIN in OTULIN-KO cells restored IFN-I secretion to wild-type levels (Fig. 2H).

The phosphorylation levels of IκBα, TBK1, and IRF3 induced by SeV were increased in OTULIN-KO and Otulin-KO cells (Fig. 2I and SI Appendix, Fig. S2G and Fig. 2J and SI Appendix, Fig. S2H). Importantly, this enhanced phosphorylation profile was normalized upon reintroduction of wild-type OTULIN (Fig. 2 I and J). The viral replication in OTULIN-KO and reconstituted cells was also examined. QPCR, immunoblotting, and fluorescence microscopy indicated reduced viral replication in OTULIN knockout cells (Fig. 2 K–N). Lentiviral reconstitution of OTULIN in OTULIN-KO cells restored viral replication to wild-type levels (Fig. 2 K–N). Collectively, these findings suggest that knockout of OTULIN augments RNA virus-induced innate immune response, and reintroduction of OTULIN restores the suppressive functions.

OTULIN Targets and Interacts With TRAF6.

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which OTULIN attenuates RLR signaling, we first sought to position its site of action within the signaling cascade. To address this question, dual-luciferase reporter assays were conducted by cotransfecting OTULIN-expressing plasmid and key signaling molecules of RLR signaling cascade into HEK293T cells. We found that OTULIN dramatically suppressed RIG-I(N) and MAVS-induced IFNβ reporter activation, but did not affect TBK1 and IKKε-induced IFNβ reporter activation (Fig. 3A), which suggests that OTULIN may act downstream of MAVS and upstream of TBK1. Our phosphorylation data from both overexpression and knockout experiments consistently indicate that OTULIN functions upstream of the kinase TBK1 (Figs. 1I and 2J). Further coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays revealed no detectable interaction between OTULIN and RIG-I, MAVS, TBK1, or IKKε (Fig. 3B).

TRAF family members are well documented to act downstream of adaptor proteins such as MAVS or STING (29–31). TRAF members, especially TRAF2, TRAF3, TRAF5, and TRAF6, play critical roles in recruiting several kinases such as TBK1, IKKε, IKKα, or IKKβ (29, 32). We therefore hypothesized that OTULIN may target TRAF family members. Eukaryotic Linear Motif (ELM) analysis revealed that OTULIN contains a TRAF6 binding motif. Additional Ubibrowser analysis also indicated TRAF1, TRAF2, and TRAF6 as candidate substrates of deubiquitinase OTULIN. Subsequent Co-IP experiments confirmed that OTULIN specifically interacted with TRAF6 but not with other TRAF members (Fig. 3C). Further mapping using truncated TRAF6 demonstrated that its C-terminal TRAF-C domain was responsible for binding to OTULIN (Fig. 3 D and E). Therefore, these data showed that OTULIN targeted the RLR signaling pathway at the level of TRAF6 via binding to the TRAF-C domain, suggesting that TRAF6 may undergo linear ubiquitination during viral infection, which is subsequently removed by OTULIN to fine-tune the signaling cascade.

LUBAC Catalyzes Linear Ubiquitination of TRAF6, Antagonized by OTULIN.

To investigate whether TRAF6 can be modified by linear ubiquitination, we initially explored the association between TRAF6 and HOIP, the catalytic core of LUBAC. Co-IP assays revealed that TRAF6 interacted with HOIP (Fig. 4A). Domain mapping showed that the TRAF-C domain of TRAF6 mediated this interaction (Fig. 4B). Additionally, TRAF6 was also associated with other LUBAC components, HOIL-1 and SHARPIN (Fig. 4 C and D), supporting that TRAF6 can interact with the LUBAC complex. Structural modeling using AlphaFold3 and PyMOL further predicted a specific binding interface between TRAF6 and HOIP within the TRAF-C domain (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). Furthermore, in vitro binding assays demonstrated a direct interaction between TRAF6 and HOIP (Fig. 4E).

Given the association of TRAF6 with LUBAC components, we reasoned that TRAF6 may be a substrate for linear ubiquitination. Indeed, immunoblot analysis of immunoprecipitated TRAF6 with a linear ubiquitin chain-specific antibody confirmed that LUBAC promoted the linear ubiquitination of TRAF6, whereas OTULIN attenuated this modification (Fig. 4F). Consistently, knockout of OTULIN enhanced the linear ubiquitination level of TRAF6 (Fig. 4G). To dissect this specific modification, we performed in vitro ubiquitination assays using a TRAF6 C70A mutant, which lacks the critical cysteine required for its autoubiquitination activity. The results demonstrated that LUBAC could directly catalyze linear ubiquitin chain formation on TRAF6, and this modification was efficiently removed by the deubiquitinase OTULIN (Fig. 4H).

To further determine whether OTULIN can cleave linear ubiquitin chains from TRAF6 during viral infection, we coexpressed Flag-tagged TRAF6 with His-tagged ubiquitin-7KR (all seven lysine residues are mutated to arginine and thus specifically support Met1-linked ubiquitination). SeV infection significantly enhanced the linear ubiquitination of TRAF6, and this enhancement was suppressed by OTULIN overexpression (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). This suppressive effect was further confirmed using a linear ubiquitin-specific antibody (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C). Time-course analysis further revealed that TRAF6 underwent dynamic linear ubiquitination following viral infection, progressively increasing to peak levels before gradually declining (Fig. 4I and SI Appendix, Fig. S3D). These findings define a regulatory axis in which LUBAC-mediated linear ubiquitination of TRAF6 is counteracted by OTULIN, revealing a potential dynamic antagonism between linear ubiquitination and deubiquitination in the regulation of antiviral signaling.

Linear Ubiquitinated TRAF6 Enhances RLR Signaling, Counteracted by OTULIN.

NEMO is a well-characterized substrate for linear ubiquitination to promote NF-κB activation (20). To determine whether the linear ubiquitination of TRAF6 functions independently of the canonical NEMO-dependent pathway in antiviral signaling, we sought to eliminate the impact of NEMO and the functional redundancy among TRAF family proteins. Since TRAF2, TRAF3, TRAF5, and TRAF6 are all critical components in immune signaling pathways, we utilized a HEK293T cell line with quintuple knockout of TRAF2/3/5/6 and NEMO (referred to as TRAFs-NEMO-KO) HEK293T cell lines to establish a simplified experimental system, as previously described (32).

Immunoblot analysis revealed that the linear ubiquitination of Flag-TRAF6 was increased in TRAFs-NEMO-KO HEK293T cells after SeV infection (Fig. 5A). Then, Flag-TRAF6 and HA-OTULIN were cotransfected into TRAFs-NEMO-KO HEK293T cells. It was shown that overexpression of OTULIN inhibited SeV-induced phosphorylation of TBK1 (Fig. 5B). These data indicate that TRAF6 is a distinct linear ubiquitination substrate that functions independently of NEMO. We next assessed the functional impact of this modification. In TRAFs-NEMO-KO HEK293T cells, coexpression of LUBAC and TRAF6 significantly enhanced NF-κB and IFNβ promoter activation, and this enhancement was counteracted by OTULIN (Fig. 5 C and D).

Following its recruitment to MAVS, the E3 ligase TRAF6 amplifies the RLR signal by generating K63-linked ubiquitin chains through autoubiquitination. These chains act as a molecular scaffold to recruit the TBK1/IKKε and IKK kinase complexes, thereby triggering antiviral immune responses (32, 33). Given this established role, we first investigated whether OTULIN influenced the K63-linked ubiquitination of TRAF6. Our results showed that SeV infection robustly enhanced K63-linked ubiquitination of TRAF6, which was attenuated by coexpression of HA-OTULIN (Fig. 5E). Since K124 is the major site for K63-linked ubiquitination of TRAF6 (34), we utilized the TRAF6 K124R mutant to dissect the interplay between K63-linked ubiquitination and linear ubiquitination. Upon virus infection, TRAF6 K124R mutant showed reduced linear ubiquitination compared with WT (Fig. 5F).

Studies have shown that K63-Ub and M1-Ub can form hybrid chains to regulate signaling transduction (35, 36). We therefore speculate that during RNA virus infection, TRAF6 functions by forming K63/M1-linked hybrid ubiquitin chains to fine-tune pathway activation. To further investigate the functional consequence of TRAF6 linear ubiquitination, we examined its interaction with key signaling molecules. Co-IP assays revealed that OTULIN did not affect its association with TBK1 or IKKε upon SeV infection (Fig. 5 G and H). Notably, OTULIN attenuated its interaction with MAVS (Fig. 5I), the key adaptor in the RIG-I-mediated signaling pathway. Collectively, these findings suggest that linear ubiquitination of TRAF6 may be a prerequisite for the full activation of the antiviral innate immune response.

Identification of TRAF6 Linear Ubiquitination Sites.

Although TRAF6 K124 has been implicated as a potential K63/M1-hybrid chain site, it is possible that there exist specific linear ubiquitination sites. To identify these lysine residues, we employed liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). To enhance detection sensitivity, we tried to overexpress the ubiquitin molecules. Given that the His-Ub.7KR construct used above contained an N-terminal His tag, which may not effectively mimic the natural linkage of linear ubiquitin molecules, we then constructed the INT-StrepII-Ub.7KR expressing vector as described (37), which carries an internal StrepII-tag and lacks all internal lysines, thereby enabling exclusive formation of head-to-tail ubiquitin chains. This design allowed a more accurate simulation of naturally occurring linear ubiquitination, thereby improving the reliability of our detection and analysis. IP and immunoblot analysis confirmed that INT-StrepII-Ub.7KR can effectively recapitulate TRAF6 linear ubiquitination as observed in earlier results (Fig. 6A).

Then, Flag-TRAF6 and INT-StrepII-Ub.7KR were cotransfected to HEK293T cells and the cells were harvested after SeV infection. Subsequently, LC–MS/MS was employed to identify potential linear ubiquitinated peptides of TRAF6, based on the fact that ubiquitination leaves Gly-Gly remnants after trypsin digestion (38). The experimental workflow is shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S4A. The LC–MS/MS analysis revealed that K104, K142, and K371 were three candidate lysine residues for linear ubiquitination on TRAF6 (Fig. 6 B and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). A multiple sequence alignment showed that these residues were highly conserved across species (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). However, these lysine residues were not necessarily linearly ubiquitinated, as other ubiquitin linkages could also leave the same diglycine remnant on conjugated lysines following trypsin digestion. To validate whether these sites are genuine linear ubiquitination acceptors, we generated TRAF6 single-point mutants and a TRAF6 3KR mutant (K104R/K142R/K371R). Upon SeV infection, all single mutants exhibited reduced linear ubiquitination levels (Fig. 6D), with the TRAF6 3KR mutant showing nearly complete abrogation of the linear modification (Fig. 6E), demonstrating that the three sites are linear ubiquitination sites of TRAF6. Functional analysis further showed that TRAF6 3KR markedly impaired SeV-induced activation of NF-κB and IFNβ reporters (Fig. 6 F and G). Moreover, the TRAF6 3KR mutant exhibited reduced ability to interact with MAVS (Fig. 6H). In summary, these results indicate that linear ubiquitination at these sites is required for TRAF6 function and the full induction of antiviral innate immunity.

Otulin+/− Mice Exhibit Enhanced Antiviral Immune Responses.

Since complete knockout of Otulin resulted in embryonic lethality (39), Otulin+/− mice were employed to investigate the physiological function of OTULIN in antiviral immunity in vivo. We first isolated primary peritoneal macrophages and confirmed the reduced expression of OTULIN protein in Otulin+/− mice. Upon SeV infection, primary macrophages from Otulin+/− mice exhibited significantly elevated transcription of downstream genes (Fig. 7 A–C) and enhanced phosphorylation of TBK1, IRF3, and IκBα (Fig. 7D), compared to those from Otulin+/+ mice. We next assessed the systemic immune response in vivo. Serum IFN-β levels were also substantially increased in Otulin+/− mice (Fig. 7E). Consistent with enhanced immunity, viral loads in multiple organs, including the lung, liver, and spleen, were significantly reduced in Otulin+/− mice (Fig. 7 F–H). Following VSV infection, liver tissues from Otulin+/− mice were further analyzed for the expression of downstream genes. Higher mRNA levels of Ifnb1, Il6, and Cxcl10 were observed in Otulin+/− mice (Fig. 7 I–K). Histopathological examination of lung tissue revealed attenuated inflammatory cell infiltration and tissue damage in Otulin+/− mice (Fig. 7L). Importantly, Otulin+/− mice exhibited a significant survival advantage over Otulin+/+ counterparts following lethal VSV challenge (Fig. 7M). In summary, heterozygous deletion of Otulin enhances antiviral immune responses in vivo, as evidenced by potentiated cytokine production, signaling activation, viral clearance, and host survival. These findings identify OTULIN as a key negative regulator of antiviral innate immunity by controlling TRAF6 linear ubiquitination (Fig. 7N).

Discussion

OTULIN is indispensable for maintaining homeostasis by regulating inflammation and cell death (39, 40). In mice, loss-of-function mutations in Otulin result in embryonic lethality (39). For instance, mice harboring OtulinC129A/C129A, OtulinW96R/W96R, or OtulinD336E/D336E mutations die at mid-gestation, likely due to dysregulated TNFR1 and RIPK1 signaling and systemic cell death (39, 41). Similarly, in humans, patients with OTULIN mutations spontaneously develop a severe autoinflammatory disorder, commonly known as “OTULIN-related autoinflammatory syndrome” (ORAS, also known as Otulipenia), a life-threatening condition characterized by uncontrolled systemic inflammation (40). Functioning as a deubiquitinase with exclusive specificity for linear ubiquitin chains, OTULIN counteracts linear ubiquitination mediated by LUBAC. However, the precise functions of OTULIN in anti-RNA viral innate immunity remain unclear. In this study, we observed a global increase in linear ubiquitination upon RNA virus infection, suggesting that linear ubiquitination may be a prerequisite for initiating antiviral innate immunity. Upon virus infection, the upregulation of HOIP and downregulation of OTULIN, which had been previously reported (22), were also observed in our study. These findings imply that the dynamic balance between linear ubiquitination and deubiquitination is essential for fine-tuning the innate immune response.

In this study, using a combination of various cell lines and in vivo models, we identified OTULIN as a critical negative regulator of RIG-I-dependent signaling. The suppressive function of OTULIN is dependent on its deubiquitinase activity, as the catalytically inactive mutant OTULIN C129S failed to inhibit the signaling pathway. This implies the presence of specific protein targets that undergo reversible linear ubiquitination to regulate RIG-I signaling. A recent study underscored the importance of LUBAC components in RIG-I-mediated antiviral signaling; however, the specific linear ubiquitinated substrates of LUBAC in this pathway remain to be identified (42).

Upon RNA virus infection, RIG-I recognizes viral RNA and recruits the adaptor protein MAVS, which in turn activates NF-κB/IRF3/IRF7 (1). This activation process depends on TRAF6-mediated recruitment of the IKKα/β/γ complex and TBK1/IKKε to MAVS (5, 31). TRAF6 possesses ubiquitin E3 ligase activity and undergoes autoubiquitination, primarily with K63-linked chains. This K63-linked polyubiquitination of TRAF6 enhances its E3 ligase activity and mediates the activation of RIG-I-induced signaling (34). Our results demonstrated that LUBAC and OTULIN dynamically regulated the linear ubiquitination level of TRAF6. The linear ubiquitination of TRAF6 was important for its K63-linked ubiquitination, its interaction with MAVS and the activation of downstream signaling. While TRAF6 K124 has been identified as the key residue for K63-linked ubiquitination of TRAF6 (34), we found that upon virus infection, the linear ubiquitination level of the TRAF6 K124R mutant was significantly lower than that of WT TRAF6, suggesting that K124 may be a site for K63/M1-linked hybrid ubiquitin chains.

However, mass spectrometry analysis did not detect K124 as a linear ubiquitination modification site of TRAF6. This could be attributed to the potential formation of K63/M1 hybrid ubiquitin chains on TRAF6, which was challenging to resolve with current mass spectrometry techniques. Previous studies have established that the signaling pathway like MyD88-dependent activation of TAK1 and the IKK complex involves the formation of K63/M1-linked hybrid ubiquitin chains on components of the signaling complex, including MyD88, IRAK1, and IRAK4 (35, 36). Therefore, we speculate that during RNA virus infection, TRAF6 may function by forming K63/M1-pUb hybrids, facilitating the recruitment of downstream kinases, and amplifying the signaling cascade. Further studies are needed to characterize hybrid chains.

Mass spectrometry identified three candidate linear ubiquitination sites on TRAF6. The TRAF6 3KR mutant (K104R/K142R/K371R) exhibited markedly reduced linear ubiquitination and impaired activation of innate immune signaling. K371 is situated within the TRAF-C domain, which is known to mediate the interaction between TRAF6 and MAVS (43), suggesting that linear ubiquitination at this site may promote TRAF6–MAVS association. In support of this, our results showed that OTULIN can disrupt the association between TRAF6 and MAVS, further underscoring OTULIN’s regulatory role in this process. Furthermore, C70, the key catalytic site for the E3 ubiquitin ligase activity of TRAF6 (34), along with K104 and K142, are all located within the N-terminal region critical for its E3 ligase function. We hypothesize that linear ubiquitination at K104 and K142 modulates TRAF6 E3 ligase activity, thereby affecting its autoubiquitination and downstream substrate ubiquitination. Notably, K104 and K142 are near K124, a key residue for K63-linked ubiquitination (34), raising the possibility that linear ubiquitination at these sites could also influence the formation of K63-linked chains on TRAF6. As observed in this study, the K63-linked autoubiquitination of TRAF6 was significantly attenuated by OTULIN. The precise molecular mechanisms underlying these modulations warrant further investigation.

TRAF6 is not the sole substrate of LUBAC or OTULIN in antiviral innate immunity. Other key signaling components, including NEMO (20), IRF3 (22), and even proteasome subunits (44), are established targets of linear ubiquitination. Notably, this modification is dynamically regulated: during IFN-I signaling, OTULIN removes linear ubiquitin chains from STAT1 to promote JAK-STAT activation, whereas LUBAC restores this modification to terminate signaling (25). Our study similarly observed dynamic linear ubiquitination of TRAF6 upon RNA virus infection. These findings suggest that reversible linear ubiquitination of STAT1 and TRAF6 may cooperatively fine-tune the activation and resolution of IFN signaling. Recently, the regulatory role of linear ubiquitination extends to STING-dependent antiviral signaling against DNA viruses (27, 28). Furthermore, given the broad involvement of TRAF6 in multiple pathways, its linear ubiquitination likely extends to TLR, NLR, IL-1, TNFR, CD40, and TCR signaling, etc. Identifying additional substrates within antiviral cascades remains an important direction for future research.

In conclusion, our study reveals that the dynamic equilibrium between linear ubiquitination and deubiquitination is crucial for regulating antiviral innate immunity. We identify OTULIN as a critical negative regulator of RNA virus–triggered IFN-I signaling by specifically removing linear ubiquitin chains from TRAF6 (Fig. 7N). These findings underscore the importance of coordinated action of linear ubiquitination and deubiquitination in modulating antiviral responses and maintaining immune homeostasis.

Materials and Methods

Cell Lines and Viruses.

HEK293T, TRAFs-NEMO-KO HEK293T, HeLa, OTULIN-KO HeLa, iBMDM, Otulin-KO iBMDM, Vero, L929-ISRE, and 2fTGH-ISRE cells were maintained in DMEM (GIBCO), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin. SeV was propagated by inoculating chicken embryos. VSV was propagated by infecting Vero cells. Virus titers were determined as TCID50/mL using the Spearman-Karber method. The VSV titers in the lung, liver, and spleen were determined by standard plaque assays as described (45).

Reagents, Plasmids, and Antibodies.

Poly (I:C) was from InvivoGen. Opti-MEM was from Gibco. PEI was from Polyscience. Protein marker was from TransGen. LipofectamineTM 2000 was from Thermo-Fisher. PMSF was from Sigma-Aldrich. Complete protease Inhibitor Cocktail and phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail were from Roche. Protein A-agarose beads were from Cytiva. ECL Chemiluminescence Detection Kit was from Biodragon. The plasmids and antibodies could be found in SI Appendix, Extended Methods.

Luciferase Reporter Assays.

Dual-luciferase reporter assays were performed as previously described (46). All reporter assays were performed in duplicate and repeated independently at least three times.

Generation of CRISPR/Cas9 knockout and rescue cell lines.

Human OTULIN gRNA was cloned into the PX459 vector (Addgene, #62988). Mice Otulin gRNA was cloned into the lentiCRISPR v2 vector (Addgene, #52961). The gRNA oligonucleotide sequences used in this study were as follows: Human OTULIN gRNA: ATTAAGCGTAGCTCCTGAAA (44). Mouse Otulin gRNA: GTTAAGTGTGGCTCCTGAAA. Details of generation of CRISPR/Cas9 knockout and rescue cell lines could be found in SI Appendix, Extended Methods. To generate OTULIN-rescue HeLa cells, human Flag-OTULIN was cloned into the lentiviral pCDH-Flag expression vector.

RNA Extraction and Quantitative Real-Time PCR.

Total RNA was isolated from TRIzol reagent (Yeasen). First-strand cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (Yeasen). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using the Power SYBR Green Master Mix (TransGen) on a TIANLONG Gentier 96E PCR System. Assays were performed in duplicate and repeated independently at least three times. Gene expression levels were normalized to internal control and analyzed using the comparative 2^(-ΔΔCt) method. The primers could be found in SI Appendix, Extended Methods.

Coimmunoprecipitation and Immunoblot Analysis.

Coimmunoprecipitation and immunoblot analysis were performed as previously described (46). For in vitro coimmunoprecipitation, the coding sequence of the gene of interest was cloned into pcDNA3.1 and translated in vitro using the TnT Quick Coupled Transcription/Translation System (Promega). The translated proteins were mixed and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The protein mixtures were then combined and diluted in 200 μL of IP lysis buffer with the indicated antibody, followed by 1 h of rotational incubation at 4 °C. The subsequent immunoprecipitation, washing, and immunoblotting steps were performed as described above.

In Vivo Linear Ubiquitination Analysis.

To analyze endogenous linear ubiquitination, cells were lysed in 100 μL buffer containing 1% SDS, protease inhibitor cocktail, and PMSF at 4 °C for 1 h and boiled for 10 min. Lysates were diluted 10-fold with standard IP lysis buffer to reduce SDS concentration and incubated with the indicated antibodies overnight at 4 °C with rotation. Immunoprecipitation was performed as described above, followed by immunoblotting with antilinear ubiquitin (LUB9) or anti-StrepII.

In Vitro Linear Ubiquitination and Delinear Ubiquitination Assays.

In vitro linear ubiquitination assays were performed in ubiquitination reaction buffer and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. For delinear ubiquitination, StrepII–OTULIN (in 150 mM Tris, pH 8.0, and 10 mM DTT) was added to the reaction mixture and incubated at 37 °C for an additional 1 h. Samples were analyzed by immunoblotting with an antilinear ubiquitin antibody. Details could be found in SI Appendix, Extended Methods.

Type I IFN bioassay.

The activity of IFN-I in cell culture supernatants was measured as previously described (47). Briefly, cells were infected with SeV for the indicated durations. The culture supernatants were then harvested, centrifuged to remove cell debris, and applied to fresh monolayers of 2fTGH-ISRE or L929-ISRE reporter cells. After a 6 h incubation, the reporter cells were lysed, and the luciferase activation was measured using the luciferase reporter assay kit described above.

LC–MS/MS Analysis.

Cells were collected, and Flag-TRAF6 was enriched by immunoprecipitation using anti-Flag agarose beads. The immunoprecipitated samples were then separated by SDS-PAGE and stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue, followed by destaining to clearly visualize the protein bands. The target bands were excised and diced into ~1 mm3 pieces. Proteins were subjected to in-gel digestion using sequencing-grade trypsin (Promega), following the method described previously (48). Details of LC–MS/MS analysis could be found in SI Appendix, Extended Methods.

Mice, Viral Infection, and Isolation of Primary Mouse Peritoneal Macrophages.

Otulin+/− (C57BL/6) mice were obtained from Cyagen Biosciences and maintained under SPF conditions. Mice (6 to 8 wk old) were used in all experiments. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Peking University Health Science Center. For viral challenge, age- and sex-matched mice were intravenously infected with VSV (5 × 107 pfu/g, i.v.). Organs and serum were collected at 12 h postinfection for qPCR, viral titration, and ELISA. H&E staining was performed on tissues collected at 24 h postinfection. For survival analysis, mice were intravenously infected with VSV (1 × 107 pfu/g), and survival was monitored daily. PMs were isolated using an optimized thioglycolate-elicited protocol as previously described (46).

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA).

IFN-β levels in mouse serum samples were quantified using a commercial ELISA kit (Multisciences), following the manufacturer’s protocol. In brief, blood samples were allowed to clot for 30 min at room temperature and then centrifuged at 1,000×g for 10 min to separate the serum. The serum samples were diluted as recommended and assayed immediately. After adding samples and reagents, plates were washed and the absorbance was measured within 30 min after adding the stop solution.

Statistical Analysis.

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software. Paired t test were used for comparisons within the same experimental subjects under different conditions, and unpaired t test were used for comparisons between independent experimental groups. Data were presented as mean ± SD. Statistical significance is indicated: ns = no significance, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001.
