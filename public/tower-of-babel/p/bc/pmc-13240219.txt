Diagnostic Accuracy of Cervical Auscultation for Aspiration Detection in Adult Dysphagia: Implications for FEES Prioritization
Authors: Nisreen Naser Al Awaji, Abdullah Mohammed Alfaris, Shaden Abdulmohsen Alabdulkarim, Lama Al Bashir, Wajd Dhafer Alqahtani, Haya Saleh Aldahmash, Rawan Yahya Alshareef, Lara Nawaf Almutairi, Rawabi Mesfer Alqahtani, Hajar Abdulaziz Almuqrin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective(s)To determine whether cervical auscultation (CA) provides a high‐specificity, rule‐in bedside signal to prioritize fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES) in adults at risk of aspiration.MethodsSingle‐center, cross‐sectional diagnostic‐accuracy study in a tertiary hospital. Adults referred for clinically indicated swallow evaluation underwent the same‐session, mutually blinded CA (index) and FEES (reference). The unit of analysis was the individual bolus; each bolus received paired CA and FEES ratings. We estimated sensitivity, specificity, predictive values, likelihood ratios (LR±), diagnostic odds ratio (DOR), and area under the ROC curve (AUC), with 95% confidence intervals; McNemar's test compared paired classifications.ResultsThirty‐two adults contributed 256 analyzable boluses (aspiration prevalence 19.5%). CA sensitivity was 62.0% and specificity 99.5%, with accuracy 92.2%, PPV 96.9%, and NPV 91.5%. LR+ = 127.7 and LR− = 0.38; DOR = 334.5; AUC = 0.808. McNemar's test (FP = 1 vs. FN = 19) was significant, indicating more false negatives than false positives. At pretest probabilities of 10%, 20%, and 40%, a CA‐positive result yielded post‐test probabilities of 93.4%, 97.0%, and 98.8%, respectively; CA‐negative results yielded post‐test probabilities of 4.1%, 8.7%, and 20.3%.ConclusionCA offers a high‐specificity rule‐in signal to triage and prioritize FEES rather than to exclude aspiration. Integrating CA into dysphagia pathways may improve access to instrumental assessment while maintaining safety.Level of Evidence2 (Diagnosis).

Introduction

Swallowing protects the lower respiratory tract while ensuring adequate nutrition and hydration. It comprises three stages: oral, pharyngeal, and esophageal, requiring precise neuromuscular coordination; each stage may be disrupted by neurological or muscular disorders, malignancies, or surgical interventions [1]. Dysphagia, defined as impaired swallowing, is associated with malnutrition, dehydration, aspiration pneumonia, chronic lung disease, choking, and increased mortality and carries psychosocial burdens including anxiety and social withdrawal [1]. The condition is common in older adults, across respiratory disorders, head and neck cancer, and those with neurological disease. Silent aspiration, entry of material below the vocal folds without overt symptoms, is a recognized complication after stroke, and undetected aspiration is linked to increased 1‐year mortality [2, 3, 4, 5].

Accurate detection of aspiration is therefore critical for clinical decision‐making and the prevention of adverse outcomes [6]. Common diagnostic approaches include the clinical bedside examination (CSE), videofluoroscopic swallow study (VFSS), and fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES). Bedside tools are ubiquitous but can miss subtle or silent events [7]. VFSS provides dynamic radiographic visualization of swallowing biomechanics, but it involves ionizing radiation and has limited accessibility. FEES is portable and radiation‐free, enabling direct visualization of pharyngeal and laryngeal events and residue and demonstrating strong diagnostic performance in many reports; nonetheless, it has limitations, including a lack of oral‐phase assessment, potential discomfort, brief “white‐out” during peak swallow, and the need for trained personnel [8, 9, 10, 11, 12].

Cervical auscultation (CA), auscultation at the lateral neck during and after swallowing, has long been incorporated into the CSE because it is rapid, low‐cost, and bedside‐ready [13, 14]. However, reported diagnostic performance varies widely, reflecting subjectivity, heterogeneous protocols, and variable training [15, 16, 17]. Much of the historical skepticism surrounding CA stemmed from methodological limitations in earlier studies, particularly the widely cited work by Leslie et al. [15], which involved heterogeneous bolus protocols, subjective rating criteria, and limited rater standardization, factors that restrict the generalizability of its conclusions. More recent work shows that structured training, explicit cue definitions, and standardized CA procedures improve rater reliability and diagnostic consistency [16, 17]. Bergström and Cichero further demonstrated that structured, competency‐based CA training significantly improves both the validity and reliability of cervical auscultation, reinforcing the importance of standardized instructional approaches in clinical practice [18]. Contemporary evidence also indicates that CA can contribute meaningfully to aspiration detection when implemented with standardized protocols and trained raters, as demonstrated in controlled studies and meta‐analytic findings [19].

Rationale and Evidence Gap

Access to FEES can be uneven due to equipment, personnel, and scheduling constraints; patient tolerance and infection control may further delay testing. A highly specific bedside indicator of aspiration may have operational value by helping clinicians decide which patients require more urgent FEES evaluation; however, this represents a potential clinical implication rather than the aim of the present diagnostic accuracy study. Prior studies often mixed populations and methods, and sometimes failed to specify bolus‐level performance, blinding, or same‐session timing, all recognized sources of potential bias [19, 20, 21]. Some reports also compare index‐test findings against reference‐test accuracy values derived from external literature rather than from the same dataset, a practice that can lead to inappropriate comparisons, overestimation of diagnostic performance, and overinterpretation of diagnostic value [22, 23]. Such errors violate the fundamental requirement that index test performance be derived exclusively from the study's own data.

Healthcare Relevance

From a service‐delivery perspective, delayed access to instrumental swallowing assessments, including FEES and VFSS, due to equipment, personnel, scheduling, and patient tolerance constraints, creates safety and throughput risks. A high‐specificity bedside finding can help prioritize FEES referral for patients most likely to aspirate, improving time‐to‐instrumental testing, allocation of skilled staff, and downstream outcomes such as pneumonia risk and length of stay. This study, therefore, evaluates CA not as a replacement for FEES but as a bedside screening adjunct within dysphagia pathways, with potential to support clinical decision‐making regarding the urgency of FEES referral.

Study Aim and Objectives

The study aimed to determine the diagnostic accuracy of CA (index test) compared with FEES (reference standard) for detecting aspiration in adults referred for dysphagia assessment. The objective was diagnostic: to quantify CA sensitivity, specificity, predictive values, likelihood ratios, and discrimination under same‐session, mutually blinded conditions [22]. The clinical relevance motivating this work is to determine whether CA can serve as a bedside rule‐in indicator to help clinicians prioritize FEES when instrumental testing capacity is limited.

Materials and Methods

Ethics

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board of KAAUH (IRB 2024–0162). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal representatives. A STARD flow diagram summarizing patient flow and analysis population is provided as Figure S1.

Study Design and Setting

This was a single‐center, diagnostic accuracy study conducted at King Abdullah bin Abdulaziz University Hospital (KAAUH), Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, reported in accordance with STARD 2015 [22]. The index test was cervical auscultation (CA); the reference standard was FEES. Index and reference tests were performed within the same clinical session to minimize temporal variability. All diagnostic accuracy estimates in this study were derived from a single cohort of participants who underwent both CA and FEES within the same clinical session; no separate samples or external datasets were used.

Participants

Eligibility: Adults (≥ 18 years) referred for evaluation of dysphagia and at risk of aspiration were eligible. Exclusions: < 18 years; acute medical instability (e.g., severe respiratory distress) precluding participation; inability to provide informed consent (personally or via legal representative); concurrent enrollment in interventional trials that could alter aspiration risk.

Sampling: Consecutive eligible patients were recruited from inpatient and outpatient services.

Sample Size and Precision

This exploratory accuracy study used a pragmatic sample determined by clinical throughput and feasibility. A formal hypothesis‐driven power calculation was not performed. Targeting ~250 boluses was expected to yield ~±10% precision around sensitivity estimates, assuming ~20% aspiration prevalence (The survey‐style “unknown population” sample‐size formula does not apply to diagnostic accuracy studies and was therefore not used.).

Index Test: Cervical Auscultation (CA)

CA was performed at bedside with a 3M Littmann Classic II SE acoustic stethoscope placed obliquely over the lateral neck near the hyoid‐laryngeal complex [20, 24]. A trained speech‐language pathologist (blinded to FEES findings) scored each bolus using a pre‐specified binary judgment (aspiration present/absent) per bolus based on established CA cues, including the timing and morphology of the swallow sound, the presence or absence of a characteristic swallow burst, and detection of abnormal post‐swallow respiratory sounds such as wetness, crackles, or delayed expiratory airflow [24, 25, 26]. No signal‐processing devices were used.

The clinician performing CA had completed structured training in cervical auscultation, including supervised practice with paired CA, instrumental comparisons, and competency sign‐off by a senior dysphagia specialist. This training covered the identification of key temporal–acoustic swallow cues and recognition of abnormal post‐swallow respiratory patterns.

Reference Standard: FEES

FEES was performed by a separate clinician blinded to CA results, using a 3.5‐mm flexible nasoendoscope connected to a Pentax DEFINA EPK‐3000 video processor and integrated LED light source. Patients were seated upright at 90° with the head in a neutral position. The nasendoscope was passed transnasally and positioned in the hypopharynx, above the level of the epiglottis, to allow visualization of the laryngeal inlet and pharyngeal structures. Standard clinical protocols were followed, including assessment of pharyngeal/laryngeal anatomy, secretion management, and swallow safety. Three boluses of each consistency (thin liquid, mildly thick liquid, and puree) were administered in fixed order from least to most viscous, yielding a maximum of nine boluses per participant. All boluses were administered in standardized 5–10 mL volumes, with green food dye added when clinically indicated to enhance visualization. A pre‐specified bail‐out procedure was in place: if frank aspiration with absent cough reflex or clinical signs of distress were observed, the protocol was terminated, and no further boluses were administered. Aspiration classification (present/absent) was based on direct visuoperceptual identification of material passing below the level of the vocal folds, consistent with established FEES criteria [26]. All examinations were video recorded for documentation and quality assurance.

Procedure

CA was performed first, immediately followed by FEES, to preserve blinding and minimize physiologic changes between assessments. The unit of analysis was the individual bolus. Each bolus received a paired CA judgment and FEES classification.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the diagnostic accuracy of CA at the bolus level. Each bolus received two binary classifications: CA Rating (aspiration present vs. absent), and FEES Classification (aspiration present vs. absent), where aspiration was defined as the entry of any material below the level of the true vocal folds [26].

These paired bolus‐level ratings were used to construct the 2 × 2 contingency table and calculate sensitivity, specificity, predictive values, likelihood ratios, the diagnostic odds ratio, and the AUC. Diagnostic accuracy was therefore derived from paired CA–FEES evaluations for each bolus rather than from participant‐level summary ratings.

Statistical Analysis

Primary Analysis

Analyses were conducted at the bolus level using paired classifications from the index test (CA) and the reference standard (FEES). A 2 × 2 contingency table (true positives, false positives, false negatives, true negatives) was constructed. Sensitivity, specificity, overall accuracy, positive and negative predictive values (PPV/NPV), positive and negative likelihood ratios (LR±; LR+ = Se/[1 − Sp], LR− = [1 − Se]/Sp), and the diagnostic odds ratio (DOR = [TP/FN]/[FP/TN]) were estimated, each with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Where applicable, CIs for proportions were calculated using exact (Clopper–Pearson) methods; CIs for likelihood ratios and DOR were derived from log‐transformed estimates. Discrimination was summarized by the area under the ROC curve (AUC) with standard error and 95% CI. McNemar's test (two‐sided, α = 0.05) assessed paired differences between CA and FEES classifications. Primary estimates treated boluses as independent. All analyses used SPSS v25 and MedCalc Diagnostic Test Evaluation v23.2.1. Two‐sided p < 0.05 denoted statistical significance.

Cluster‐Robust Sensitivity Analyses

As multiple boluses were obtained per participant (non‐independence within patients), we performed sensitivity analyses using Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) with patient ID as the clustering factor, binomial family/logit link, and an exchangeable working correlation.

Sensitivity was estimated in the FEES‐positive subset by fitting an intercept‐only model for CA positive; the model's marginal means and its sandwich (robust) 95% CI were taken as sensitivity. Specificity was estimated analogously in the FEES‐negative subset by modeling CA negative. For discrimination, we computed an AUC patient‐level bootstrap (resampling patients, not boluses; 2000 resamples) to obtain cluster‐aware CIs.

Sensitivity Analysis

CA and FEES were performed sequentially, not simultaneously, creating potential for bolus‐to‐bolus variability. To address this, a pre‐specified sensitivity analysis was conducted using only the first valid bolus from each of the 32 participants. The diagnostic accuracy for this subset was calculated using the same methods as the primary analysis.

Missing Data Handling

Only boluses with both CA and FEES classifications were included in the analysis set. No imputation was performed.

Diagnostic Accuracy of CA (Primary Analysis)

At the bolus level (N = 256; aspiration prevalence 19.5%), CA yielded sensitivity 62.0% (95% CI 47.2–75.4) and specificity 99.5% (97.3–99.9) versus FEES, with accuracy 92.2% (88.2–95.2). Predictive values were PPV 96.9% (81.3–99.6) and NPV 91.5% (88.3–93.9). Likelihood ratios indicated strong confirmatory value (LR+ 127.7, 17.9–913.3) but limited exclusion with a negative test (LR− 0.38, 0.27–0.54); the diagnostic odds ratio was 334.5 (43.2–2588.0). Discrimination was AUC 0.808 (SE 0.043; 95% CI 0.723–0.892; p < 0.001). The underlying cross‐tabulation appears in Table 2; the ROC curve is shown in Figure 1. Sensitivity Analysis: First Bolus Only

The sensitivity analysis was performed on the first bolus from each of the 32 patients. In this subset, the prevalence of aspiration was 34.4% (11/32). CA demonstrated a sensitivity of 72.7% and a specificity of 100.0%. The positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) were 100.0% and 87.5%, respectively. As each participant contributed exactly one bolus to this subset, this analysis provides a participant‐level perspective on diagnostic performance, free from within‐patient clustering effects, yielding results consistent with the primary bolus‐level findings. ROC Analysis

The ROC curve for CA versus FEES shows a single operating point derived from the predefined dichotomy (Se = 0.62; FPR = 0.005; Sp = 0.995). The area under the curve was 0.808 (95% CI 0.723–0.892), indicating good discrimination (Figure 1). The operating characteristics are driven by a very low false‐positive rate (FP = 1) with moderate sensitivity (FN = 19).

Discussion

Principal Findings

In adults undergoing the same‐session, mutually blinded testing, CA showed very high specificity (99.5%) and PPV (96.9%) but moderate sensitivity (62%) versus FEES at the bolus level, with AUC 0.81 indicating good discrimination. At the cohort's pretest probability (19.5%), a positive CA implied a = 97% post‐test probability of aspiration (LR+ 127.7), whereas a negative CA left an 8%–9% residual risk (LR− 0.38). Thus, CA is a strong rule‐in signal but does not safely exclude aspiration where clinical concern persists. Paired classifications differed by McNemar testing (p < 0.001), consistent with CA's lower sensitivity.

Relation to Prior Evidence

Historical CA studies documented heterogeneous accuracy and inter‐rater reliability due to subjective criteria and non‐standardized protocols [15, 16, 17]. With structured training and clearer decision rules, performance improves, but sensitivity typically remains imperfect, especially for silent or trace aspiration [16, 17, 27]. A recent meta‐analysis across pediatric and adult populations reports a pooled sensitivity of approximately 0.90 and a specificity of approximately 0.79 for CA under modern protocols [19], yet individual study estimates continue to vary with raters, devices, and case mix. The present findings, high specificity and moderate sensitivity, are directionally aligned with this literature and reinforce CA's role as an adjunct rather than a replacement for instrumental assessment. They also accord with evidence that bedside screens can miss silent aspiration, necessitating confirmatory instrumentation when risk remains [7, 12].

Beyond its role as a standalone screen, CA has been increasingly recognized as a valuable adjunct within the clinical swallowing evaluation (CSE), where its findings are integrated with other bedside indicators such as oral motor function, voice quality, volitional cough, and patient‐reported symptoms. Evidence suggests that combining CA with other components of the CSE improves overall diagnostic yield compared with either tool used in isolation [28]. The present findings reinforce this position: the high specificity of CA supports its use as a rule‐in signal within an integrated bedside evaluation framework, while its moderate sensitivity underscores the continued necessity of instrumental assessment, particularly FEES, when clinical concern persists despite a negative CA finding.

A more holistic approach to CA assessment has recently been proposed by Bergström, Cichero, Jaghbeer et al. [28], incorporating respiratory‐swallow parameters into a structured protocol for adult dysphagia management. This framework aligns with the present findings and supports the integration of respiratory‐swallow coordination monitoring alongside acoustic CA judgments to enhance diagnostic precision.

Implementation Considerations

Training and standardization are critical determinants of CA reliability. Earlier studies that reported poor or inconsistent performance often involved clinicians who relied on intuitive listening without agreed‐upon acoustic definitions or structured calibration procedures [15, 16, 17]. Reliability improves substantially when clinicians receive systematic training that includes explicit instruction in identifying key temporal–acoustic swallow features (e.g., swallow‐burst timing, acoustic morphology), recognition of abnormal post‐swallow respiratory sounds (e.g., wetness, delayed exhalation), and supervised practice with feedback to ensure alignment of perceptual criteria across raters. Evidence demonstrates that structured training reduces variability in cue interpretation and improves both inter‐rater and intra‐rater agreement, while inconsistent cue interpretation and lack of calibration meaningfully contribute to performance variability [16, 17]. These findings underscore that CA should not be implemented clinically without standardized training, competency verification, and periodic recalibration.

Population and clinical setting also substantially influence CA's diagnostic performance. Swallow–respiratory physiology, sensory function, secretion burden, and airway protection differ markedly across patient groups, which alters the acoustic signatures on which CA judgments rely. Evidence from FEES and VFSS studies demonstrates that patients in intensive care units, individuals with tracheostomy, and mechanically ventilated patients frequently exhibit disrupted respiratory–swallow coordination, reduced laryngeal sensation, atypical airflow patterns, and higher rates of silent aspiration [29, 30, 31, 32] These physiologic differences can distort swallow and post‐swallow breath acoustics, making CA interpretation more challenging and potentially less reliable than in ambulatory adults without respiratory support. Accordingly, caution is warranted when generalizing the present findings to medically complex populations.

Clear documentation is essential for reproducibility and safe clinical application. Clinicians should record the decision rule used, bolus types and volumes administered, patient positioning, and relevant respiratory patterns. When feasible, pairing CA with measurable respiratory–swallow parameters (e.g., airflow monitoring or respiratory inductance signals) can provide additional physiologic context, particularly by identifying dyscoordination patterns, such as post‐swallow inhalation or prolonged apnoea, that are independently associated with aspiration risk and may inform clinical decision‐making alongside acoustic findings.

Respiratory–swallow coordination is particularly relevant to the interpretation of CA. Normal swallowing typically occurs during a brief expiratory pause followed by expiration, a pattern associated with effective airway protection. Deviations from this pattern, such as inspiration immediately after the swallow, prolonged apnea, or uncoordinated timing, are strongly associated with penetration–aspiration events and are frequently observed in neurologic disease, respiratory compromise, or impaired laryngeal sensation. Such disruptions alter both swallow acoustics and post‐swallow breath sounds, reducing the reliability of CA in detecting aspiration. It should be noted, however, that post‐swallow exhalation is often brief and acoustically subtle, limiting its reliable detection through auscultation alone; dedicated instrumentation such as airflow monitoring or respiratory inductance plethysmography is required to capture respiratory–swallow timing with sufficient precision for clinical interpretation. When feasible, pairing CA with such instrumentation can provide additional physiologic context, particularly by identifying dyscoordination patterns, such as post‐swallow inhalation or prolonged apnoea, that are independently associated with aspiration risk and may inform clinical decision‐making alongside acoustic findings [9, 33].

From a service‐delivery perspective, CA offers potential advantages in settings where FEES access is constrained by equipment, staffing, or scheduling limitations. When implemented with standardized procedures and appropriate safeguards, CA can help identify individuals who may require expedited instrumental assessment, supporting safer triage pathways and more efficient resource utilization in clinical environments with limited FEES capacity.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several important limitations. First, although the clinician performing CA had completed structured training, inter‐rater or intra‐rater reliability for CA was not formally quantified within this dataset. The absence of reliability metrics limits the ability to determine whether diagnostic performance reflects the intrinsic capability of CA or the proficiency of the individual examiner. Future studies should incorporate multiple trained raters and formal reliability assessments to strengthen interpretability.

Second, CA and FEES were performed sequentially rather than through synchronized, time‐locked acquisition. As swallowing physiology can vary from bolus to bolus, this temporal mismatch is a potential source of measurement error that may contribute to discrepancies, particularly false‐negative CA findings. To investigate this, a sensitivity analysis was performed using only the first bolus from each participant, thereby minimizing the potential for within‐session variability. The results were consistent with the primary analysis, showing a modest, non‐significant increase in sensitivity (from 62.0% to 72.7%) while specificity remained exceptionally high (99.5% to 100.0%). This suggests that while bolus‐to‐bolus variability may have some effect, it does not fundamentally alter the conclusion that CA provides a high‐specificity signal. Nonetheless, future research with simultaneous, time‐locked data capture would be valuable to further refine these diagnostic estimates. Simultaneous or digitally synchronized data capture would minimize this source of measurement error and enable more precise temporal mapping between acoustic and endoscopic events.

Additional limitations include the single‐center convenience sample, which reduces generalizability; the modest sample size at the patient level; and the use of a binary CA decision rule without exploration of threshold optimization. Additionally, the present study focused exclusively on aspiration detection; the utility of CA for identifying swallow inefficiency, including pharyngeal residue, reduced hyolaryngeal excursion, and delayed swallow onset, was not examined and represents an important area for future investigation. Finally, FEES served as the sole reference standard. The absence of complementary VFSS data or combined reference‐standard approaches may have resulted in under‐detection of aspiration events not visible endoscopically. FEES and VFSS are generally considered complementary rather than interchangeable reference standards; using VFSS as the reference standard, FEES sensitivity for aspiration detection has been reported to range from 25% to 94% and specificity from 75% to 100%, while VFSS offers superior visualization of the oral preparatory and esophageal phases [34, 35].

Future Directions

Future studies should build upon these findings by implementing standardized CA training programs with measurable competency outcomes to determine how training quality influences diagnostic accuracy and rater reliability. Multicenter research with larger and more diverse samples would enhance generalizability and allow patient‐level, rather than bolus‐level, analyses using cluster‐robust statistical approaches. Time‐locked synchronization of CA with both instrumental imaging (FEES or VFSS) and respiratory–swallow monitoring parameters (e.g., airflow, respiratory inductance plethysmography) would enable more precise mapping between acoustic features, visuoperceptual events, and coordination patterns, reducing bolus‐to‐bolus variability and strengthening diagnostic inferences.

In parallel, emerging technological developments offer promising avenues for improving CA performance. High‐resolution cervical auscultation (HRCA), which employs highly sensitive accelerometers and microphones placed on the anterior neck to capture swallowing vibratory signals, has been investigated as a platform for automated dysphagia detection. Khalifa et al. (2020) demonstrated that deep learning algorithms applied to HRCA recordings could reliably identify swallowing events in a non‐invasive, real‐time fashion, achieving high accuracy in segmenting swallow from non‐swallow signals [35]. Building on this, Frakking et al. applied automated speech recognition and machine‐learning classification to digital CA recordings and provided preliminary evidence that aspirating swallowing sounds could be accurately distinguished from normal swallowing [13]. More recently, Khodami et al. demonstrated the utility of HRCA signals combined with deep learning for assessing swallowing functionality in clinical populations [36]. These approaches could increase sensitivity while preserving the high specificity observed in clinician‐performed CA and therefore warrant rigorous validation in adult dysphagia populations.

Finally, future research should examine implementation outcomes such as timeliness of FEES referral, cost‐effectiveness, prevention of aspiration‐related complications, and optimization of workforce allocation. Such work would help clarify how CA can be operationalized within modern dysphagia pathways to improve safety and resource utilization.

Conclusions

Cervical auscultation, when performed by trained clinicians, demonstrates high specificity but only moderate sensitivity for aspiration detection compared with FEES. These findings support its use as a screening and triage tool, particularly to prioritize FEES, rather than as a stand‐alone diagnostic. Structured training, standardized protocols, and integration into clinical pathways can enhance its utility while safeguarding against missed aspiration.
