MyCOACH (COnnected Advice for Cognitive Health): a digitally delivered multidomain intervention for cognitive decline and risk of dementia in adults with mild cognitive impairment or subjective cognitive decline–study protocol for a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Jessica G Amos, Lidan Zheng, Ranmalee Eramudugolla, Dinaz Parekh, Md Hamidul Huque, Kim Delbaere, Nicola Lautenschlager, Kaarin J Anstey
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDigital health interventions are cost-effective and easily accessible, but there is currently a lack of effective online options for dementia prevention especially for people at risk due to mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or subjective cognitive decline (SCD).Methods and analysisMyCOACH (COnnected Advice for Cognitive Health) is a tailored online dementia risk reduction programme for adults aged ≥65 living with MCI or SCD. The MyCOACH trial aims to evaluate the programme’s effectiveness in reducing dementia risk compared with an active control over a 64-week period (N=326). Eligible participants are randomly allocated to one of two intervention arms for 12 weeks: (1) the MyCOACH intervention programme or (2) email bulletins with general healthy ageing information (active control). The MyCOACH intervention programme provides participants with information about memory impairments and dementia, memory strategies and different lifestyle factors associated with brain ageing as well as practical support including goal setting, motivational interviewing, brain training, dietary and exercise consultations, and a 26-week post-intervention booster session. Follow-up assessments are conducted for all participants at 13, 39 and 65 weeks from baseline, with the primary outcome being exposure to dementia risk factors measured using the Australian National University-Alzheimer’s Disease Risk Index. Secondary measures include cognitive function, quality of life, functional impairment, motivation to change behaviour, self-efficacy, morale and dementia literacy.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval was obtained from the University of New South Wales Human Research Ethics Committee (HC210012, 19 February 2021). The results of the study will be disseminated in peer-reviewed journals and research conferences.Trial registration numberACTRN12621000977875.

Introduction

Dementia risk reduction is one of the key target areas for the WHO global action plan and public health response to dementia.1 2 In 2019, over 57.4 million people were estimated to have dementia globally. Without effective prevention or treatment, current societal costs are estimated at US$1.3 trillion worldwide3 and the number of cases is projected to reach 152.8 million by 2050.4

Dementia is characterised by a progressive decline in thinking skills, physical function and behaviour to the point where an individual can no longer function independently. Based on neuroimaging and biochemical studies, subtle neuropathological and cognitive changes can be identified years or even decades before possible dementia diagnosis.5 6 This enables identification of stages such as ‘mild cognitive impairment’ (MCI) which differs from dementia as cognitive decline is evident but functional impairment is largely absent,7 and ‘subjective cognitive decline’ (SCD) where decline is self-perceived and not measurable by clinical testing.8 Individuals with SCD or MCI are at an elevated risk of dementia, with SCD cases 2.15 times more likely to develop into MCI compared with healthy controls9 and between 5% and 15% MCI cases converting to dementia annually.10 11 However, not everyone with MCI or SCD goes on to develop dementia; in fact, approximately 24% of those with MCI revert to normal cognitive status12 suggesting that this may represent an optimal time frame for intervention.

Dementia risk reduction interventions often target modifiable medical, environmental and lifestyle risk factors. These factors can include cardiovascular health, diet, physical activity (PA), cognitive activity, depression, social contact, hearing impairment and excessive alcohol consumption.13 Given the multifactorial aetiology of dementia, it is likely that targeting a combination of risk factors may provide more synergistic benefits compared with just targeting one risk factor.14 15 As such, multidomain intervention trials have become increasingly prevalent in the dementia prevention space compared with single-domain or pharmacological trials,16 with PreDIVA,17 MAPT,18 FINGER19 and Body Brain Life (BBL)20 being examples of early trials.

As of mid-2022, there are 22 completed and 46 ongoing multidomain trials on dementia prevention.16 However, only seven completed multidomain trials have targeted people with MCI and none of these were defined as large-scale trials (defined by ‘>1000 sample and >13-month follow-up’ or ‘>100 sample and >24-month follow-up’).16 Of the completed short-term clinical trials targeting SCD or MCI, improved dementia risk and cognitive outcomes have been demonstrated over a 6-month follow-up period. For example, a Chinese intervention (N=112) targeting diet, exercise, vascular health and cognitive training saw improvements in cognitive function (Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score), physical function, quality of life (QoL) and mood after 6 months for older adults with MCI.21 Similarly, ‘BBL–Cognitive Decline’ (BBL-CD, N=119)22 compared e-learning plus face-to-face diet and exercise consultations with a control group who only received email information and found improvements in dementia risk scores and cognitive processing speed in older adults with MCI/SCD after 6 months. However, this study also noted that the benefits plateaued between follow-ups, suggesting a need for booster or refresher sessions.

An additional component of multidomain interventions that can be overlooked are behaviour change techniques (BCTs), which are defined as ‘active ingredients’ that can be replicated and evaluated.23 Interventions tend to use multiple BCTs; however, they are often mislabelled and rarely distinctly evaluated.23 Therefore, it is unclear which BCTs are effective at reducing dementia risk. BCTs have however been evaluated for individual behaviours such as physical activity, healthy eating and diabetes management providing promising evidence for techniques like goal setting, self-monitoring and feedback even when delivered digitally.24–26 Uniquely, Chen et al27 compared their regular multidomain dementia risk reduction intervention with one ‘enhanced’ to include techniques like goal setting, monitoring and empowerment, and found significant improvements in cognitive functioning after 9 months in the enhanced version compared with the original. This emerging research demonstrates promise for certain BCTs, such as goal setting and self-monitoring; however, further evaluation is needed to understand the efficacy in the context of dementia risk reduction.

Recent trials have often been limited in their scalability and accessibility due to the need for in-person assessments.28 Completely digitally delivered interventions are increasingly being developed in the dementia prevention space16 29 30 capitalising on the rise of digital therapeutics and increased digital literacy of older adults31 32 (eg, PRODEMOS,33 Maintain Your Brain34). However, currently, few randomised controlled trials (RCTs) are specifically designed to target people with MCI and SCD. While cognitive complaints or decline has been associated with lower engagement in digital interventions for dementia risk,35 web-based multidomain interventions have been found to be feasible and well accepted by people with SCD,36 37 and people with cognitive impairments are highly motivated to stay engaged and master technology.38

Therefore, as a natural evolution in this area of research, this study will focus on the design of a purely digital multidomain trial for people with SCD and MCI. Specifically, the current study will draw on previously successful trials to design a digitally delivered, risk reduction intervention for people with SCD and MCI using BCT components. If validated, this intervention could provide a widely accessible and low-cost tool to support healthy ageing and reduce dementia incidence associated with dementia risk factors.

Aims and objectives

The primary aim of this study is to evaluate the effectiveness of a digitally delivered multidomain intervention (MyCOACH (COnnected Advice for Cognitive Health)), compared with basic email-based psychoeducation, for reducing the risk of dementia after 12 months (measured by the Australian National University-Alzheimer’s Disease Risk Index (ANU-ADRI))39 in people with SCD or MCI.

Secondary objectives include: (1) evaluating MyCOACH against email control for the change in cognitive functioning, diet, PA, health-related QoL, daily functioning and psychological well-being; and (2) evaluating intervention feasibility and adherence, determinants of behaviour change as well as the effectiveness of participation in BCT components.

Method and analysis

Study design

This study is a two-arm single-blind RCT of a 12-week online behavioural and e-learning intervention. The intervention includes psychoeducation as well as active behaviour change components and focuses on multiple domains of healthy lifestyle and dementia risk factors including physical inactivity, unhealthy diet, lack of social connection, cognitive inactivity and stress. This trial will evaluate the superiority of this online intervention against a parallel active control group receiving only psychoeducation. A flow chart outlining the participant process is detailed in figure 1. Allocation will follow a 1:1 ratio and participants in both groups are evaluated at four time points: baseline, 13 weeks, 39 weeks and 65 weeks. All participants provided electronic or written consent to participate in the study, and this protocol was written in accordance with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials checklist.40 Details of measures and consent forms are included in online supplemental materials A and B.

Participants

Participants will be community-dwelling individuals aged 65 years and older with SCD or MCI. Recruitment uses convenience sampling via community newsletters, volunteer registries (eg, StepUp for dementia research41) and social media advertisements across Australia.

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility is assessed based on age ≥65 years, the presence of SCD (score ≥25 on Memory and Cognition Questionnaire (MAC-Q)),42 absence of severe cognitive impairment (score >21 on Telephone Interview of Cognitive Status (TICS)43 44 or absence of a self-reported dementia diagnosis), no current participation in a healthy ageing intervention, absence of significant psychiatric or neurological diagnoses, self-assessed ability to read, write and communicate in English to a comfortable level and self-assessed ability to engage in an online course and phone or video conference interviews, using their own devices.

Screening and enrolment process

After providing electronic or written consent, participants will undergo a formal telephone eligibility screening process with a trained researcher to discuss the inclusion and exclusion criteria and complete the MAC-Q42 and TICS.43 44 Participants with a TICS score of <21 will be offered a referral to their general practitioner. The TICS performance is comparable with the Telephone MoCA.45–47 It has shown high sensitivity (82%) for detecting cognitive impairment associated with vascular disease or stroke46 and high specificity (>74%) and sensitivity (>90%) for detecting cognitive impairment associated with Alzheimer’s disease (AD).47 Participants who meet all inclusion and no exclusion criteria will be invited for the baseline evaluation via telephone or video call before randomisation.

Randomisation and blinding

Participants are randomised to either the active group (MyCOACH intervention) or control group (email bulletins) following a 1:1 ratio and based on four stratification factors (sex: male and female and age groups: 65–74 and 75+ years). Randomisation will use a predetermined allocation sequence, generated by an independent statistician in Stata.48 The sequence will be concealed by REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture), an electronic data capture tool hosted by the University of New South Wales (UNSW).49 50 Participant allocation will be coordinated by the trial manager who will notify participants via email. Due to the nature of this RCT, participants, trial coordinators and staff delivering the interventions will be unblinded to allocation. All researchers involved in follow-up assessments and the statistician conducting analysis will however be blinded.

Interventions

Group 1: MyCOACH intervention

Participants allocated to the active group will receive the 12-week MyCOACH intervention. The intervention consists of three components, namely an e-learning course with six educational chapters, a subscription to computerised brain training, and tailored consultations with a dietitian and an exercise physiologist (EP). Participants are also offered a one-off tailored booster session 26 weeks post-intervention. Details of these components are as follows:

E-learning

The e-learning consists of six educational chapters, which take approximately 30–40 min each to complete and can be accessed on participants’ own devices (participants are advised to use a desktop or laptop but the course is also compatible with tablet or smartphone). Chapters are accessed through a unique login to the MyCOACH website and are unlocked according to a weekly schedule (see figure 1). Unlocking the next chapter will not be dependent on the completion of the previous and once unlocked, each chapter will be accessible until week 12.

Chapter topics include (1) Introduction and General Information, (2) Memory and Memory Strategies, (3) Physical Activities, (4) Healthy Diet, (5) Social and Cognitive Engagement and (6) Stress Reduction. Each chapter details how the topic links to brain ageing, provides psychoeducation in both a written and video format, includes interactive activities and real-life strategies presented in scripted videos using actors. Within the chapters, participants are prompted to set SMART (Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Realistic and Timely) goals51 and answer reflection questions to assist with behaviour change.

Consultations

For PA, participants attend two consultations with a qualified EP via video call or phone after they have completed the PA chapter (see figure 1). During the first session, the EP assesses the participants’ current level of PA and provides a tailored programme taking into account any comorbidities, resources and preferences.52 The goal of the EP programme is for the participant to increase their activity to meet the Australian Government’s PA guidelines which are 30 min of at least moderate-intensity activity per day or on most days and to include a range of strength, balance and flexibility exercises.

For diet, participants attend two consultations with a qualified dietitian via video or phone call after they have finished the diet e-learning chapter (see figure 1). A dietary assessment is conducted and from this, participants receive tailored dietary advice focused on meeting the requirements for the ‘MIND’ diet.53 Dietary advice is adapted to account for participants’ comorbidities, resources and preferences, thus enabling safe and effective implementation in this population. For both the EP and diet consultations, session attendance and information relating to SMART goal development (ie, goals set, attainment rating and barriers) will be recorded.

Brain training

At the beginning of week 1, each participant will be provided with a 12-week subscription to the BrainHQ programme (computerised cognitive brain training), developed by Posit Science.54 The BrainHQ account will be configured to include two executive function tasks (Double Decision and Divided Attention) and two memory tasks (Memory Grid and Syllable Stacks) per week. Participants will be asked to complete four 30-minute sessions per week, totalling 2 hours on their home devices. The programme will adapt task difficulty to individual performance such that users achieve around 70–90% correct answers and are consistently training at their threshold. Total usage (eg, time spent, unique logins) will be recorded and exported by researchers.

Booster session

At week 26, each participant will be provided with a digital booklet designed to refresh the e-learning materials and offered a 30–45 min one-to-one booster session (via video call or phone) with a practitioner trained in motivational interviewing (MI).55 The practitioner will be guided by a standardised template drawing on MI principles to discuss motivations for joining the study, perceived norms, goal attainment, goal satisfaction, barriers and facilitators to behaviour change and general feedback.

Group 2: active control

Participants allocated to the control group will receive basic email-based psychoeducation pertaining to the same dementia risk factors and memory strategies as the intervention group, delivered at the same rate (see figure 1). The email bulletins will cover each risk factor and how it relates to brain health; however, no active behaviour change components such as strategies, interactive activities or SMART goal setting will be included. Moreover, the control group will not receive diet or exercise consultations, brain training or a booster session. After the 65-week follow-up, the control group will be given access to the full MyCOACH e-learning.

Patient and public involvement

The overall design of MyCOACH was informed from prior interventions developed by the research group (ie, BBL research20 56) that had extensive consumer involvement in their design as well as adaptations for populations with MCI and SCD (ie, BBL-CD).22 During the development of the MyCOACH e-learning, an iterative feedback process was implemented with two consumer representatives for which ethical approval was obtained (HC200724). Consumers (both aged 65+ years) were given access to an intervention module in a drafted stage and asked to provide feedback through an anonymous feedback form hosted on REDCap.49 Consumers were asked a combination of closed and open questions about (a) usefulness, (b) ease of navigation, (c) enjoyability and engagement, (d) relevance of the topic, (e) design and (f) technical issues, and feedback was iteratively incorporated where possible. A survey of members of community groups was also conducted during the study design phase to obtain information on preferences for digital health interventions.

Primary outcome measures

AD risk score

The level of exposure to dementia risk and protective factors are measured by the ANU-ADRI.57 The ANU-ADRI is a self-report index that assesses 11 risk factors (eg, age, diabetes, depression, smoking) and 4 protective factors (eg, physical activity, cognitive activity, social engagement) to produce an individual risk score for AD dementia. Scores range from 14 to 73, with low scores indicating a protective lifestyle and low risk of AD, and high scores indicating a high-risk lifestyle and a high risk of AD. The tool uses an algorithm derived from ORs from systematic reviews and high-quality cohort studies. It has been externally validated in three large international cohort studies39 and is thought to be reliable for prospectively predicting the development of MCI58 and AD.39

The International Physical Activity Questionnaire short form59 and the Centre for Epidemiologic Studies Depression-2060–62 are administered as part of the ANU-ADRI.

Secondary outcome measures

Cognitive function

A global cognitive composite will be derived from a battery of cognitive tests as well as a memory composite using the verbal memory test scores alone. The cognitive battery is designed to be sensitive to changes observed in preclinical dementia (ie, SCD or MCI). Executive function63 is measured by the Trails B-oral version64 65 and Victoria Stoop Test-Colour Words66 67; verbal memory68 is measured by Hopkins Verbal Learning Test–Revised69 ; attention and processing speed are measured by Symbol Digit Modalities Test-oral version70 and Victoria Stroop Test-Colour Dots66 67; and premorbid verbal ability is measured using the Contextual AUSNART.71 72

Changes in cognitive functioning will be assessed by looking at the group by time interactions between the MyCOACH intervention and control groups. Composite scores will be created by computing the average z-score for each test relative to the scores at baseline. Data collection will be conducted via video call or phone; therefore, for measures that include visual stimuli (ie, Symbol Digit Modalities, Victoria Stroop and Contextual AUSNART), participants will be provided with these either (a) via post: printed stimuli with instructions not to open until researcher indicates, (b) via email: a password-protected file or (c) via share screen: researcher controlled. These steps are taken to avoid possible bias from participants viewing stimuli early. Online cognitive assessment methods have been validated for use in older adults73 and populations with cognitive impairment.74 75

Daily functioning

Daily functioning will be measured using the Instrumental Activities of Daily Living–Extended Scale (IADL-x).76 77 The IADL-x is a nine-item scale covering activities such as visiting friends or relatives, offering volunteer work and attending classes. The IADL-x was adapted from the original IADL Scale78 to detect subtle functional changes in healthy as well as mildly impaired populations. The adapted scale is thought to be valid for detecting subtle cognitive decline, in line with MCI, in older populations.79 80

Health-related QoL

General health-related QoL will be measured by the 12-item Short-Form Health Survey (SF-12),81 and component scores will be available to capture mental and physical health status individually. Scores range from 0 to 100, with higher total or subscale scores indicative of better health-related QoL.81 The SF-12 is a shortened version of the SF-36 and is thought to be less burdensome but equally as sensitive,82 with acceptable validity and reliability among older adults.83

Adapted MIND diet score

To obtain a holistic measurement of diet that is attributable to the neuroprotective MIND diet, an adapted version of the Australian Recommended Food Score (ARFS) Questionnaire84 was created. The ARFS is a 70-item questionnaire designed to align with the Australian Dietary Guidelines.85 It includes eight subscales related to vegetables (Q1–20), fruits (Q21–32), protein (Q33–39), vegetarian alternatives (Q40–45), grain (Q46–57), dairy (Q58–67), water (Q68), and sauces and condiments (Q69–70), and has demonstrated good validity and reproducibility.84

For this study, 12 additional items were included to cover recommendations in the MIND diet53; the items captured consumption of leafy green vegetables, wholegrains, sweet or carbonated beverages, fruit juice, wine, sofrito as well as an additional subscale with 5 items pertaining to oils and fast foods.

Additional outcome measures

A secondary aim of this study is to evaluate the possible social and psychological determinants of behaviour change and to evaluate the usefulness of the BCT of goal setting within a dementia risk reduction behavioural intervention. A detailed list of additional measures and the collection schedule can be found in the online supplemental material A and table 1.

Data management

There are four primary data collection points: baseline (week 0), post-intervention (week 13), 6-month follow-up (week 39) and 1-year follow-up (week 65). Not all assessments will be conducted at each time point (see table 1). Data collection will take place via video call or telephone and will be entered straight into REDCap, a secure data management system hosted by UNSW.49 50 All study and admin data will be treated with strict confidentiality—collected, managed and stored according to our ethics project description. Personal information will be stored on secure servers at Neuroscience Research Australia using permission-protected folders. Research data stored in REDCap and on data analysis software (eg, SPSS, SAS) will be de-identified using ID numbers and only accessible to researchers involved in the study.

Data monitoring

The project will be managed by the Trial Steering Committee (TSC) which will consist of the chief investigators and associate researchers. The TSC will be responsible for the overall supervision of the trial, monitoring trial progress, quality control checks and responding to recommendations from the Data Monitoring Committee (DMC). The DMC will receive annual trial updates and will provide independent oversight and advice around the general trial conduct and participant safety. This committee will be formed independently of the trial sponsor and consist of a postdoctoral researcher (with experience in clinical trials), a statistician, a data manager and an independent advisor (with a clinical or medical background).

Quality control checks

All researchers involved in data collection and eligibility assessments will be trained in standardised administration of the tools and measures. Data entry and accuracy will be checked through double data collection, entry, and scoring for 10% of baseline or follow-up assessments. Additional training will be provided if data entry or delivery deviates from standardised administration. The EP, dietitian and booster session practitioners are accredited and practising healthcare professionals and will be trained in study procedures, data collection and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Data collection forms completed by consultants will be checked by the trial manager and entered into the central data collection system (REDCap).49

Sample retention

Adherence to the MyCOACH programme will be monitored through website statistics (eg, chapter completion and BrainHQ logins) and participants will be sent up to three automated reminder emails per chapter. To maximise intervention adherence, minimise attrition and offer technical support, participants will be followed up via telephone if they are behind the course schedule (see figure 1). For 13, 39 and 65-week follow-up interviews, researchers make a maximum of five attempts to contact participants (via email and/or phone).

Adverse events

In this context, adverse events (AEs) are considered any untoward medical occurrence that participants experience, whether or not it is related to the intervention or treatment. All AEs and serious AEs will be recorded and reported to the chief investigator and Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) within 48 and 72 hours, respectively. Where relevant, a medical review will be conducted. Educational materials will not give medical advice, as they are based on guidelines available in the literature. Throughout the study, any changes to the protocol will be approved by the Ethics Committee and communicated to investigators and participants by email.

Sample size

The total sample size required for this project is 326 participants (163 per group). The sample size calculation was based on the assumption that the ANU-ADRI risk score will reduce by 0.5 SD at the end of the final follow-up (64 weeks) for the intervention group. We estimated the correlation coefficients between the outcome measures in 3 times points would be 0.7 and attrition would be 30% in 64 weeks.

Statistical analysis

The basic demographic and key study characteristics will be presented using mean, SD, count and proportion where appropriate. Longitudinal trajectories of the outcomes will be presented in graphs. The effect of the intervention on the change in the primary outcome (ANU-ADRI score) between baseline and 12-month follow-up will be evaluated using linear regression with interaction between intervention/control group and the time points. The association between the intervention and other outcome measurements across all time points will be examined using generalised linear mixed-effects models, as appropriate, with subject-specific random intercept. If the primary outcome over time is non-linear, the primary outcome will be assessed using linear mixed model with subject-specific random intercept. All the covariates will be included as fixed terms in the model. All models will be adjusted for basic baseline characteristics. For the above models, to allow for multiple testing of various outcomes, we will adjust the CI using the Bonferroni method. Missing data will be imputed using multiple imputation.

Discussion

The MyCOACH trial, to our knowledge, is one of a few studies that aim to evaluate dementia risk reduction in older adults with SCD and MCI, despite this population being at an increased risk of dementia. This trial will provide support for the feasibility of digitally delivered interventions with adults 65+ years with MCI and SCD and offer novel insights to the efficacy of BCTs in the context of dementia risk reduction. Collection of blood biomarker and neuroimaging data was outside of the scope of the current study. Nevertheless, if validated, the current intervention could provide a scalable and accessible tool to support healthy ageing and reduce rising dementia incidence rates associated with dementia risk factors.

Trial status

At the time of manuscript submission, this trial is in the recruitment phase. Participant recruitment opened on 8 November 2021, end of baseline data collection is expected in August 2023 and follow-up data collection in November 2024.

Ethics and dissemination

This study is sponsored by the UNSW; it has been prospectively registered with the Australian and New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ACTRN12621000977875) and approved by the UNSW HREC (HC210012). Results of the study will be disseminated in peer-reviewed journals and presented at both national and international research conferences. Study participants will be provided with a summary of results.
