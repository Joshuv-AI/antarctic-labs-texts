Strain-dependent glutathionylation of fibronectin fibers impacts mechano-chemical behavior and primes an integrin switch
Authors: Wei Li, Leandro Moretti, Xinya Su, Chiuan-Ren Yeh, Matthew P Torres, Thomas H Barker
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe extracellular matrix (ECM) is a protein polymer network that physically supports cells within a tissue. It acts as an important physical and biochemical stimulus directing cell behaviors. For fibronectin (Fn), a predominant component of the ECM, these physical and biochemical activities are inextricably linked as physical forces trigger conformational changes that impact its biochemical activity. Here, we analyze whether oxidative post-translational modifications, specifically glutathionylation, alter Fn’s mechano-chemical characteristics through stretch-dependent protein modification. ECM post-translational modifications represent a potential for time- or stimulus-dependent changes in ECM structure-function relationships that could persist over time with potentially significant impacts on cell and tissue behaviors. In this study, we show evidence that glutathionylation of Fn ECM fibers is stretch-dependent and alters Fn fiber mechanical properties with implications on the selectivity of engaging integrin receptors. These data demonstrate the existence of multimodal post-translational modification mechanisms within the ECM with high relevance to the microenvironmental regulation of downstream cell behaviors.Subject terms: Post-translational modifications, Biophysics, Cytoskeleton, Mechanotransduction

Introduction

The extracellular matrix (ECM, a.k.a. ‘the matrix’) is the three-dimensional polymeric network of macromolecules that is essential for all multicellular organisms. It supports cell attachment, organization, and functions within tissues. The ECM not only provides physical scaffolding for cells, but it also acts as a complex biochemical and biophysical stimulus. ECM directly binds cells through integrin receptors and it orchestrates the action of a host of growth factors and cytokines by their selective, local accumulation and release1. The critical importance of ECM is best demonstrated by the fact that selected removal of many ECM components at the genetic level leads to the loss of viability at some of the earliest developmental stages2,3.

The ECM is also highly dynamic. When an injury occurs, specific ECM proteins, such as fibronectin (Fn), are secreted and assembled into a fibrillar “provisional matrix”4 that acts as a potent biochemical and biophysical stimulator of tissue remodeling. The Fn-rich provisional matrix promotes new blood vessel formation and instructs immune cell, epithelial cell and fibroblast responses5. The ECM not only supports these cellular functions but is also modified by the cells it supports, thus establishing a “dynamic reciprocity” between the ECM and cells6. This ability to support and yet be modified by the temporal cellular and/or microenvironmental events occurring within the matrix hints at a potentially greater function for ECM, specifically event recording, processing, and translation7. We posed a unique hypothesis that the matrix protein Fn, due to its unique structure, is capable of ‘documenting’ and translating complex microenvironmental states through multimodal site-directed modifications, here, oxidative stress and mechanical signals. In this manuscript, we provide evidence that Fn is not only capable of multimodal site-directed modifications but that, here, strain-dependent glutathionylation significantly impacts its fundamental biophysical behaviors and subsequent mechano-chemical signaling to cells suggesting a form of information translation.

Fn, like other elastic proteins (e.g., Titin8), contains specific repeating domains called type-III repeats that are stabilized by van der Waals forces and hydrogen bonding. These noncovalent bonds can be temporally broken and reformed in response to force application and release9. Unusual in the highly oxidizing extracellular space, Fn contains two pairs of unmodified reactive cysteines, one on each 7th and 15th type-III repeat of the Fn dimer; their lack of modification protected by their cryptic nature10,11 (Fig. 1). Oxidant stress results from an imbalance in reactive oxygen species (ROS), the natural product of aerobic metabolism, and naturally occurring antioxidants12. Elevated systemic oxidative stress is strongly associated with obesity and metabolic disorders13, inflammatory diseases like arthritis, interstitial lung disease, and cardiovascular disease14, and cancer15. Thus, the potential impacts of oxidative modification of the ECM resulting in altered activity are quite broad across human tissue homeostasis and disease.

Results

Glutathionylation of single Fn fibers and Fn-rich ECM

ROS are capable of inducing both reversible and irreversible oxidative post-translational modification (PTM) of cysteine and methionine residues in proteins16. Glutathione, a prominent antioxidant, reacts with partially oxidized cysteines resulting in S-glutathionylation17. The addition of glutathione to cysteine is reversible and prevents irreversible hyper-oxidation of cysteine thiol side chains18. Thus, we hypothesized that Fn’s two sets of free cysteines, four in total, are susceptible to glutathionylation. Given the cryptic nature of the reactive thiols we further hypothesized that glutathionylation occurs following force/stretch-mediated unfolding of the 7th and/or 15th type III repeats. We took a reductionist approach to evaluate the mechanism of glutathionylation and subsequent impacts on cell-ECM biology. We first confirmed that exposure of Fn’s 4 cryptic cysteines results in their sensitivity to glutathione and that their glutathionylation is reversible by reducing agents using purified, soluble Fn under increasingly heat-denaturing conditions (Supplementary Fig. 1). We then set out to evaluate the effective sites of modification in single Fn fibers. Since Fn predominately exists in a stabilized fibrillar form in vivo, we manually deposited single fibers from purified soluble Fn onto micropatterned flexible silicone substrates to mimic this ECM form19,20. We applied uniaxial strain to the manually deposited Fn fibers by stretching the underlying silicone (Fig. 2a) and validated the strain-dependent glutathionylation of Fn fibers via fluorescence microscopy. Relaxed and strained Fn fibers were individually incubated with FITC-conjugated oxidized glutathione (FITC-GSSG) followed by extensive washing. The glutathione (FITC) signal was normalized to the total Fn signal to control for Fn content/abundance within the fibers (Fig. 2a). Fn fibers are demonstrably and quantitatively glutathionylated to a greater extent in a strain-dependent manner (Fig. 2b).

After establishing this PTM on single Fn fibers, we explored physiologically relevant decellularized ECM (dECM). Fibroblasts were seeded on flexible silicone substrates and stimulated to produce a diverse, but Fn-rich, ECM. We subsequently decellularized the samples and applied uniaxial strain to the dECM (Fig. 2c). Following treatment of dECM with FITC-GSSG, we again observe strain-dependent glutathionylation of the dECM. In both cases, the addition of the reducing agent dithiothreitol (DTT) at concentrations sufficiently low so as to prevent reduction of stabilizing intradomain and intermolecular cysteine-cysteine disulfide bonds within Fn fibers (1 mM) results in a quantitative loss of the Fn glutathionylation signal of equal amplitude as the strain-dependent increase in signal upon GSSG treatment. Given the Fn rich nature of fibroblast-derived ECM in vitro, the modification was presumed to reside on the Fn backbone. To validate the dECM FITC-GSSG observations, we analyzed unmodified and glutathionylated dECM by bottom-up mass spectrometry. Analysis of unmodified dECM illustrates the protein diversity within dECM (Supplementary Fig. 2, Supplementary Data 1), but confirms the dominance of Fn, and to a lesser extent collagen, within cell-derived ECM.

Fn’s cryptic cysteine located on the 7th, but not 15th, Type III repeat is glutathionylated

Mass spectrometry analysis of strain-mediated glutathionylated dECM reveals site-specific glutathionylation of the cryptic cysteine residue (Cys1232) within the 7th type III repeat, but not the 15th type III repeat (Fig. 3a, b, Supplementary Data 2). Although multiple ECM proteins were detected in the dECM samples, Fn was identified as the predominant protein within the sample and Cys1232 was also the most predominant glutathionylation site (of only two sites observed across all ECM proteins). Taken together with the irreversible ox-PTM data of glutathionylated and control dECM (Supplementary Data 3), these findings demonstrate that cryptic cysteine residues within Fn, particularly the cysteine within the 7th Type III repeat, is a strain-dependent target of S-glutathionylation in vitro.

Strain-dependent glutathionylation significantly impacts Fn fiber biophysical properties and displays evidence of persistence

The glutathionylation of Fn’s cryptic cysteine in the 7th type III repeat suggested the potential for significant structural disruption and/or sensitization through theoretical steric inhibition of refolding. We therefore sought to determine if glutathionylation impacts the fundamental biophysical properties of Fn fibers. The micropatterned flexible silicone substrate format described previously enables the use an atomic force microscope (AFM21) to both manipulate and measure the physical properties of parallel, individual Fn fibers (Fig. 4a) and to enable strain/extension-mediated unfolding representative of what might be encountered under physiological conditions. In the first set of experiments, strain was applied to manually deposited Fn fibers by stretching the underlying silicone, as before, followed by subsequent exposure to GSSG. The silicone membrane was subsequently relaxed and glutathionylated Fn fibers allowed to return to their original fiber length as confirmed by microscopy. (Fig. 4b) Glutathionylated Fn fibers were subsequently analyzed for their stress strain behavior by AFM and found to be significantly more extensible and an order of magnitude less stiff than non-glutathionylated Fn fibers (Fig. 4c). Fiber breakage was observed starting at a strain value of ~0.8 for non-glutathionylated Fn fibers and nearly double that (~1.5) for glutathionylated Fn. Unmodified fibers broke more frequently at lower strain/extension compared to glutathionylated fibers, which also displayed a higher maximal strain to breakage (Fig. 4c).

We then utilized the AFM for both force measurement and in situ reaction conditions allowing the determination of the elasticity of single fibers under multiple, sequential conditional states (for experimental sequence, refer to Fig. 4d). Fibers underwent repetitive stepwise incremental extensions and force measurements to a maximal extension of ~0.4λ, followed by complete relaxation to the original fiber state, in three stages. In stage one a single Fn fiber is measured in its unmodified state (red). In stage two, the same fiber is extended to double its native length (λ = 2.0) and treated with GSSG to induce glutathionylation, then the stretching force is released and the fiber is allowed to recover to its original length and excess GSSG removed by multiple exhaustive buffer exchanges. The glutationylated Fn fiber is measured by a second series of extensions (blue) followed by relaxation to the normal fiber length. Finally, in stage three, the fiber is once more extended to double its original length (λ = 2.0) is and treated with dithiothreitol (DTT) under the stretched condition in an attempt to remove the glutathione using conditions previously established (Fig. 2d), the fiber is then relaxed a final time, allowing it to fully recover its original length, and a final set of measurements taken with a third series of incremental extensions of the DTT-treated Fn fiber (green). As seen in the overlaid saw-tooth stress-strain plots from a single representative fiber (Fig. 4e), the unmodified fiber stress sharply increases due to incremental extension followed by stress relaxation due, in part, to mechanical unfolding of type III repeat domains and rehydration of the fiber. Strikingly, the glutathionylation of this same Fn fiber requires an order of magnitude less force to achieve equivalent extension. Moreover, DTT treatment, intended to remove the PTM, does not reverse the biophysical behavior of Fn fibers, suggesting a persistent biophysical adaption of unknown origin. We were able to rule out the alternative hypothesis that extension of a single Fn fiber leads to subsequent permanent alteration in its biophysical properties as sequential force-extension perturbations of an unmodified Fn fiber (following the same experimental sequence specifics outlined in Fig. 4d but without addition of chemical moieties; Fig. 4e, upper right panel) displayed no evidence of plastic deformation. We additionally ruled out the possibility that our DTT treatment conditions reduce the stabilizing disulfide bonds within Fn, since replicating that experimental condition had no effect on the biophysical properties of a non-glutathionylated Fn fiber (Fig. 4e, lower right). Finally, we tested the additional alternative hypothesis that higher irreversible oxidation states, such as sulfinic and sulfonic modifications, were the mechanism explaining the apparent persistence of the biophysical changes and found that while irreversible oxidation marks were observed broadly in both samples, they were, in fact, observed with greater frequency in the control compared to the glutathionylated case (Supplementary Fig. 3) arguing strongly against irreversible oxidation as the explanation for our observations. Consequently, these data suggest that strain-dependent glutathionylation of Fn’s cryptic cysteine residues may lead to a significant and persistent change in the fiber’s biophysical behaviors.

Mutation of Fn’s cryptic cysteines prevents strain-dependent glutathionylation-induced alterations to Fn fiber biophysical properties

To assess whether the cryptic cysteine residues in the 7th and/or 15th Type III repeats of Fn are absolutely essential to this unique behavior and thus confirming the hypothesis that strain-dependent glutathionylation of the cryptic cysteines is the specific stimulus inducing the altered biophysical properties, we performed identical AFM experiments using a previously published and validated recombinant Fn displaying cysteine substitutions in the 7th and 15th Type III repeats (FnC1232A/C2136V)11. Unmodified, FnC1232A/C2136V fibers display similar stress-strain profiles compared to wild-type Fn fibers. More importantly, FnC1232A/C2136V fibers display no alteration in mechanical properties in response to GSSG or subsequent DTT treatment (Fig. 5). They are also not biophysically impacted by multiple force-extension cycles (Fig. 5e, top right inset) nor by DTT treatment alone (Fig. 5e, bottom right inset) as was also observed with wild-type Fn fibers.

Strain-dependent glutathionylation primes the Fn integrin mechano-switch

In the intracellular environment, the effect of glutathionylation is well known and serves as a mechanism of signal transduction that mediates mitochondrial function22, receptor signaling23, and ion channel activity24. Dysregulation of intracellular S-glutathionylation can cause cell death associated with many diseases, like cardiovascular disease and diabetes25. Although it is known that ECM biophysical properties, like bulk stiffness (Young’s modulus), govern the impact of oxidative stress on cells26 and that several glutathionylated extracellular proteins and cytokines are released during inflammation27–29, ECM responses to oxidative stress have been largely ignored. The glutathionylation of Cys1232 within Fn’s 7th Type III repeat is a particularly interesting modification site based on its proximity to the primary binding sites for cell integrin receptors, specifically the peptide sequences PHSRN, a.k.a. “synergy site”, and RGD within the 9th and 10th Type III repeats, respectively (Figs. 1 and 3b).

Fn is one of many elastic proteins that are particularly sensitive to dynamic biophysical forces. Such forces are often generated by invading and contracting cells that induce strain or extension/elongation of Fn fibers. This lengthening of Fn fibers is accommodated by significant conformational changes in the domain structure of Fn that, in turn, provide a feedback stimulus to adjacent cells. Large-scale conformational changes in Fn regulate integrin receptor binding10,30, the binding and release of growth factors1,31, even its own polymerization32. We’ve previously demonstrated that in response to cell contraction-mediated strain of Fn matrices, the primary cell-binding domains of Fn (the 9th and 10th Type III repeats) display a conformational change that directs integrin binding preference33. Given the impact of glutathionylation on Fn fiber elasticity and the identified site of modification, we sought to measure this strain-mediated event (Fig. 1) predicted by Vogel et al. nearly two decades ago10 and made experimentally detectable by a single-chain antibody (clone H5) developed in our lab33. As before, fibroblasts were allowed to assemble a Fn-rich ECM on flexible silicone substrates. The fibroblasts were subsequently removed through a decellularization process leaving a Fn-rich dECM (Supplementary Fig. 2). The substrates were either left unstrained or extended to expose the cryptic cysteines followed by GSSG or control (buffer) treatment. dECMs were all returned to (or maintained at) their initial unstrained state. Glutathionylated and unmodified dECM was then either maintained in a relaxed, unstrained state (λ = 1.0) or uniaxially strained (λ = 2.0). H5 was subsequently used to detect the previously described strain-dependent conformational change in the integrin binding domain (Fig. 6a). Quantitation of the H5:total Fn signal ratio within ECM fibers confirms that this strain-dependent event is unaltered following glutathionylation (Fig. 6b). This result indicates that glutathionylation of Fn, by itself, does not disrupt or alter the previously identified strain-dependent behavior of Fn’s integrin binding domain. In the case of the integrin binding domain’s strain-dependent conformational change, which we’ve previously shown to be ‘activated’ at an extension ratio of ~1.4 (Strain ~0.4). The translation of the stress-strain curves established in Fig. 4 to this discrete mechanical switch behavior strongly argues that glutathionylation lowers the integrin switch’s activation force by an order of magnitude (Fig. 6c).

Focal adhesion signaling is highly dependent on the composition and diversity of different integrins within the macromolecular complexes that form cell-matrix adhesions. In fibroblasts, strain-dependent conformational change of Fn’s integrin binding domain abolishes integrin α5β1’s ability to bind. Thus, this conformational change represents an integrin ‘switch’ where the extended conformation predominately binds αvβ3. This effect can be promoted by artificially stretching Fn-rich ECM to trigger the conformational change (Fig. 6d, top; Fig. 6e, Unmodified λ = 2.0 versus λ = 1.0). Strikingly, the impact of Fn glutathionylation is that naïve non-contractile (i.e., normal, non-activated) fibroblasts are readily able to trigger the integrin switch (Fig. 6d, bottom; Fig. 6e, glutathionylated λ = 1.0 compared to unmodified λ = 2.0). Ratiometric imaging and quantitation of active αvβ3 versus active β1 within naïve fibroblast focal adhesions cultured on glutathionylated Fn-rich dECM demonstrates that even in the absence of artificial activation of the conformational change through substrate stretching (λ = 2.0), naïve fibroblasts display heightened αvβ3:β1 ratios (Fig. 6d, e), indicative of a true integrin ‘switch’. Importantly, the observed fiber-scale biophysical changes to Fn resulting from strain-dependent glutathionylation do not significantly impact the bulk material properties of the dECM (Supplementary Fig. 4) which are dominated by fiber alignment and porosity. Thus, glutathionylation within a dECM does not confer broader changes in ECM rigidity that could impact integrin binding dynamics and cellular mechanotransduction. Together these findings are significant since we and others have shown that integrin αvβ3-mediated myofibroblastic differentiation occurs, in part, due to elevated cytoskeletal activity and the concomitant activation and nuclear translocation of the transcription factor MRTF in response to the conversion of globular (g) actin to filamentous (f) actin34 as illustrated by increased cell spreading (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b).

Discussion

Here, we demonstrate that strain-dependent and site-specific glutathionylation of Fn fibers significantly reduces the force threshold required for cells to physically activate an important, potentially disease-relevant conformational change in Fn’s integrin binding domain, leading to a cellular integrin switch during their engagement of Fn. Fn has long been known to display force-mediated domain unfolding in its characteristic Type III repeat domains, though there has been significant debate as to the forces required to trigger domain unfolding when Fn is in fibrillar form, rather than in solution. Single molecule biophysical analysis has established that each individual Type III repeat displays different biophysical properties in isolation, and Fn contains up to 18 different Type III repeats depending on alternative splicing. However, adjacent Type III repeats within the full-length protein are known to thermodynamically stabilize their neighbors35. In 2002, Vogel and Schulten proposed the existence of a force-induced conformational change in the integrin binding domain consisting of the 9th and 10th Type III repeats36, yet whether cells could generate the forces necessary to activate this so-called “integrin switch” was hotly debated leading to a number of important studies defining the unfolding dynamics of Type III repeats within Fn fibers11. In 2017, our group was able to demonstrate for the first time, experimentally, that activated fibroblasts in culture and in fibrotic remodeling diseases as well as endothelial tip cells during angiogenesis can indeed generate the necessary force to trigger Fn’s integrin switch33. An observation made possible by a novel antibody tool developed to detect the modest conformational change. The present study expands these early findings and suggests that the dynamics of Fn’s integrin switch can be additionally controlled through complex environmentally-driven PTMs that are of high relevance in tissue injury repair and tissue remodeling diseases. Indeed, glutathionylation primes the activation of Fn’s integrin switch.

The role of PTMs within the ECM has only recently gained attention but has the potential to expose new forms of ECM signaling dynamics. It is tempting to speculate that various PTMs mark the ECM in specific conditionally-sensitive ways based on its exposure to various stimuli, like a contractile force or inflammation37. Whether such marks and their effects are reversible or not, these complex multi-stimuli dependent events potentially represent a form of environmental information processing, recording and transmission, either acutely or chronically. The potential for the accumulation of such marks leading to a new tissue homeostatic ground state, gross tissue remodeling, or triggering disease initiation is a question that will require significant additional inquiry, likely across many ECM proteins and environmental conditions. Regarding the impacts of Fn glutathionylation on integrin switch activation we report here, preferential engagement of αvβ3 integrin over integrins like α5β1 and α3β138 has been shown to alter angiogenic programs39, epithelial to mesenchymal transition40, and activation of naïve fibroblasts down a myofibroblastic lineage34,41. These prior reports point to the potential of the stretch-dependent glutathionylation of Fn to exert a significant impact on tissue fate (Supplementary Fig. 5c,d). The additional suggestion, from our data, that glutathionylation-induced alterations in Fn fiber biophysical properties persist after DTT treatment, hints at a form of molecular memory. Testing this hypothesis will undoubtedly require carefully designed experiments that target glutathionylated cysteine whilst leaving unmodified or disulfide cysteine residues unperturbed. Despite demonstrating the effectiveness of our DTT treatment conditions in removal of glutathione from Fn fibers (Fig. 2d), we cannot rule out the possibility that the DTT treatment on the AFM did not sufficiently remove the mark. One potential approach could be the use of glutathione-specific enzymes, like glutaredoxin, that remove glutathione specifically. However, enzymatic processing of strain-dependent Fn glutathionylation is also not a perfect solution. We cannot completely rule out the possibility that certain glutathionylated cysteines become structurally protected and display resistance to DTT and thus would certainly display resistance to the much bulkier glutaredoxin. Elucidation of the precise mechanism through future studies is certainly warranted by the evidence shown in this report. Elucidation of the mechanism should provide unique insights that could reveal similar forms of multimodal PTMs in other ECM protein polymers.

The observations in our study suggest potential relevance in the control of cell and tissue state and an interesting area for future investigation will be whether PTM-induced alterations in ECM mechano-chemical signaling are equally capable of inducing persistent cellular phenotype changes, such as cellular mechanical memory recently described by the Hinz group42. It is intriguing, albeit speculative, that such a paradigm could suggest a disease-driving, cell-ECM mechanobiological feedback loop capable of sustaining pathological progression where cell mechanics, oxidative stress and ECM physical alterations form an unvirtuous cycle, as has been proposed in organ fibrosis. Moreover, it is interesting to note that elevated S-glutathionylation has been reported in interstitial lung disease and lung cancer where contractile fibroblasts play a dominant role in disease initiation and progression. Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis has been linked to decreased enzymatic activity of glutaredoxin-1 and concomitant increases in protein glutathionylation. Conversely, it has been shown that reducing protein oxidation can reverse lung fibrosis43–53. Unfortunately, tools for specifically detecting glutathionylated Fn in human tissue samples are lacking and, ultimately, the extent to which Ox-PTMs, and PTMs more generally, modulate the function of the in vivo ECM remains a significant gap in the field requiring research beyond the scope of this initial report on the biophysical impacts of such PTMs.

In conclusion, multimodal PTM-induced regulation of ECM mechano-chemical behavior and the potential for PTM effect persistence unequivocally adds to the substantial mosaic of factors informing cell and tissue homeostasis with potential implications in tissue remodeling diseases. This type of complex environmental information processing suggests the ECM itself is perhaps more central to organizing and directly integrating and translating multiple synergistic cues than previously thought. Such potential in the ECM will be an important consideration in the development of ECM-targeting therapies as well as the application of human decellularized tissues for disease modeling, regenerative medicine and stem cell delivery. In addition to observations of ECM glutathionylation and oxidation there is also, at a minimum, evidence of enzyme-mediated arginine deamination and tyrosine phosphorylation of the ECM that provide additional avenues for multimodal, stimuli-specific ECM PTMs54–56. The ECM is a rich information center and information transmitter. It is not surprising that the complexity of ECM signaling is far greater than once envisioned.

Methods

Plasma fibronectin purification

Fn was purified from frozen human plasma by gelatin-sepharose affinity purification57. Thawed plasma was loaded into a freshly packed sepharose-4B column (4B-200, Sigma) after the addition of PMSF and allowed to gravity drip into a freshly packed gelatin sepharose column (17095 601, Sigma). Fn was eluted from the latter column with 6 M urea. Fractions were collected and buffer exchanged into 1x PBS overnight via dialysis. Fn preparations were only used at 95% purity or higher.

Mutant Fn (FnC1232A/C2136V) production and purification

Seven million HEK293 cells (CRL-1573, ATCC) plated in a 10 mm dish in DMEM + 10% FBS were transfected using upscaled Lipofectamine 3000 reagents (Invitrogen). Specifically, 60 μl of lipofectamine 3000 reagent were combined with 40 μl of P3000 and 20 μg of FnC1232A/C2136V expression vector (kindly donated by Dr. Chris Lemmon, Virginia Commonwealth University)11. The reagents were diluted in OptiMEM and administered to the cells according to the manufacturer protocol. Medium was replaced after 6 h with DMEM + 10% of Fn depleted FBS. Medium was collected, changed, and refrigerated every 2 days. Mutant-Fn was collected via gelatin-sepharose gravity filtration. After concentrating the eluted mutant-Fn, we obtained 250 μl at a concentration of 3.2 mg/ml.

Cell culture

Human foreskin fibroblasts (HFFs, SCRC-1041, ATCC) passage 3 to 9 were used in all experiments. HFF cells were cultured in DMEM medium with 15% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) for production of cell-derived matrices, while other experiments (integrin switch and MRTF analysis) were performed in serum free medium.

Functionalization and micropatterning PDMS

Thin PDMS sheets (0.005” NRV G/G 40D 12”×12”; Specialty Manufacturing, Inc.) were used as the material substrate for all experiments involving the application of stretch to either single Fn fibers or Fn-rich dECM. All PDMS sheets (both those micropatterned or smooth) were pretreated with 0.1 M NaOH, 3% 3-aminopropyltriethoxysilane (APTES) and 1% glutaraldehyde before depositing Fn fibers or functionalizing with Fn for subsequent cell culture to generate a dECM. Micropatterned PDMS sheets were fabricated by soft lithography with 100 μm high ridges 10 μm in width with 50 μm gaps separating multiple parallel ridges designed to suspend individual fibers. Masks (secondary molds) were made by standard photolithography.

Single fiber deposition and decellularized extracellular matrix (dECM) formation

Single fiber

Single Fn fibers were formed following a previously described method19,20. Briefly, a single Fn fiber was extracted from a 1 μl droplet of Fn solution (1.5 mg/ml) deposited on activated, micropatterned PDMS substrates using a 1 μl pipette tip. Fn fibers were deposited on striated PDMS sheets fabricated by soft lithography with patterned ridges of 10 × 100 μm and 50 μm spacing. Masks (secondary molds) were made by standard photolithography.

Decellularized matrix

HFF were cultured on flat, Fn functionalized PDMS sheets (0.005” NRV G/G 40D 12”×12”; Specialty Manufacturing, Inc.) at a seeding density of 10,000 cells/cm2. After 7 to 9 days of culture, HFFs were lysed and removed with Latrunculin B (2 µM) (Sigma-Aldrich), EDTA (50 mM) (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 2% sodium deoxycholate (DOC) and DNase I (1 µ/ml) (Amresco). Afterwards, the cell-derived dECMs were washed 3 times with PBS prior to being used in subsequent experiments. For experiments involving analysis of gluationylated and non-glutathoinylated dECM, the PDMS sheet with attached dECM was stretched to different strain levels (0% and 200% strain) and treated with 1 mL of PBS with or without 14 mM GSSG. Treated dECMs were subsequently washed 3x with PBS prior to further manipulation, cell seeding, immunofluorescent staining or sample preparation for mass spectrometry (see below). Further details are described in ref. 34.

Fn fiber and dECM (de-)glutathionylation and verification

Single fluorescently labeled Fn fibers were deposited on micropatterned PDMS sheets following the method described above19,20 using a 1.5 mg/ml Fn solution containing 5% Alexa 555-Fn (1.5 mg/ml) and 95% non-labeled Fn (1.5 mg/ml).

Fluorescently labeled decellularized ECM (dECM) was synthesized by HFF seeded (10,000 cell/cm2) and cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS on smooth, Fn-functionalized PDMS for 7–9 days. Forty-eight hours (48 h) prior decellularization, 1 µg/ml Alexa 555 labeled Fn was added into DMEM medium with 15% FBS to enable cell incorporation of labeled Fn into the ECM. HFF were subsequently removed with cell extraction buffer (2 μM Latrunculin B, 50 mM EDTA, 2% sodium deoxycholate (DOC) and 1 µ/ml DNase I) and dECM washed 3x with exhaustive PBS exchanges.

Glutathionylation was performed on both single Fn fibers and dECM subjected to either 0% or 200% strain by adding 1 mM FITC-GSSG (gift from Prof. Lisa Landino, The College of William and Mary) for either 30 min in the case of single Fn fibers or 1 h in the case of dECM at 37 °C. Excess FITC-GSSG was removed by washing with 3x exhaustive PBS exchanges.

De-glutathionylation was performed by incubating glutathionylated single fibers or dECM exposed to 200% strain with a 1 mM DTT solution at 37 °C for 40 min in the case of glutathionylated single fibers or 1 h in the case of dECM. In both glutathionylation and de-glutathionylation experiments, fluorescence images were taken with a UltraView Vox Spinning Disk Confocal Microscope (40X) (Revvity, formerly PerkinElmer) using Volocity 6.3.1 software for acquisition. Fluorescence signal ratio of FITC-SSG to AF555-Fn was calculated at each pixel position following masking on the AF555-Fn positive pixels. Images of each condition were taken from multiple random ROIs within the sample; each experiment was replicated three times. Total number of ROIs quantified is reported in the figure legend.

Sample preparation for mass spectroscopy

PDMS membranes with attached unmodified dECM or glutathionylated dECM (described above) were cut into 0.3 cm × 1 cm pieces and sonicated in 8 M urea, 20 mM HEPES for 30 min. For the unmodified dECM, the sample was centrifuged and the supernatant collected into a low-retention microfuge tube (Axygen) and reduced with Dithiothreitol (DTT; final concentration is 0.1 M) by incubation at 80 °C for 10 min. The sample was placed into a Vivacon 500 centrifugal filter tube with 30 kDa cutoff (Sartorius) and centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 min followed by alkylation with 300 μl 0.05 M iodoacetamide for 45 min in the dark. For glutathionylated dECM, reduction and alkylation were avoided to prevent loss of the modification. Both of the unmodified and glutathionylation dECM were washed three times with 300 μl 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate followed by centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 20 min each. Sequencing-grade chymotrypsin (Promega) was added to filter tubes containing glutathionylated dECM, while trypsin (Promega) was added to unmodified dECM with the same enzyme to protein ratio (1:50). Both trypsin and chymotrypsin samples were incubated overnight while shaking at 350 rpm at 37 °C and 25 °C, respectively. Digested peptides were collected into fresh 1.5 ml low-retention microfuge tubes by centrifugation at 14,000 × g speed for 5 min at room temperature and then desalted using a peptide desalting spin column (Pierce). Samples were then frozen at −80 °C for at least 20 h and sublimated to dryness via centri-vap (Labconco). Dried peptides were reconstituted by sonication in 50 μl 5% acetonitrile 0.1% formic acid for LC-MS analysis.

Mass spectrometry

Proteolytic peptides were analyzed by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) using a Q-Exactive Plus Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) coupled with an UltiMate™ 3000 RSLCnano UPLC system (Dionex) equipped with an Acclaim Pep Map C18 RP column run in data-dependent acquisition mode with HCD fragmentation. Samples (GSSG treated sample n = 2 technical replicates, control n = 1) were separated by gradient LC with mobile phase A (2% ACN with 0.1% FA) and mobile phase B (80% ACN with 0.1% FA) with gradient from 4% B up to 90% B for over 150 min at a flow rate of 0.3 μl/min. Data-dependent tandem MS was conducted with the full scans in the range from 200 to 1800 m/z using an Orbitrap mass analyzer where automatic gain control was set at 3e6 (MS1) and 1e5 (MS2), resolution at 70,000 (MS1) and 17,500 (MS2), and Maximum IT at 100 ms (MS1) and 50 ms (MS2). The top 15 most abundant precursor ions were selected at MS1 with an isolation window of 4 m/z and further fragmented at MS2 by high energy collisional dissociation (HCD) with normalized collision energies (NCEs) of 27. The resulting MS RAW files were analyzed using SEQUEST search algorithm via Proteome Discoverer 3.0 where glutathionylation (G) and oxidation (M) were included as variable modifications. MS fragmentation data were searched against the non-redundant UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/proteomes/UP000005640) using a maximum of 2 missed cleavage sites, peptide length from 6 to 150, a peptide tolerance of 10 ppm in the MS1 scan and a fragment ion tolerance of 0.02 Da in the MS2 scan, 0.01 false discovery rate. Glutathionylation, sulfenic acid (-SOH; Dm = 15.9949), the irreversible oxidation states of cysteine including dehydroalanine (DHA; Dm = −33.9877 Da), sulfinic acid (-SO2H; Dm = 31.9898 Da), and sulfonic acid (-SO3H; Dm = 47.9847) were searched as dynamic modifications. The modified PSM% of a modification site was calculated as the number of modified PSMs divided by the number of total PSMs containing this site. Modified PSMs number is the number of PSMs where the modification site has the modification (site localization probability higher than 90%). Total PSMs number is the number of PSMs that contain the modification site (modified and unmodified).

AFM manipulation

All force-extension data was collected by a MFP-3D Bio (Asylum Research) paired with a TiEclipse inverted optical microscope (Nikon).

Single fiber mechanics

Single Fn fibers were deposited onto the striated PDMS substrate (as described before) mounted on a 50 mm glass-bottomed Petri dish (FD5040-100, World Precision Instruments). Fn fibers were stretched (200%) with the underlying PDMS substrate, incubated with GSSG for glutathionylation, then relaxed to their original length. Each Fn fiber was pulled by an AFM tip (Model AC240TS-R3, Oxford instruments) with a spring constant of 2 N/m. The fiber was stretched at a rate of 320 nm/s. The strain at which the fiber breaks while pulling was reported as “extensibility”, and it is defined as ε=△L/Linitial, where △L=Lextended−Linitial. Young’s modulus data was determined by using the incremental stress-strain curve, Young’s modulus =σ/ε, where ɛ is the resulting strain and the applied stress σ=F/A, assuming fiber cross section is circular, A=π(D/2)2. Fiber diameter was measured by tapping mode AFM. Details are described previously58. Fibers of each condition are from three independent samples. For single fiber series pulling: STAGE 1: The AFM tip was used to repetitively extend a single Fn fiber incrementally up to 0.4 strain and the resulting resistance force was measured throughout the sequence. The fiber was relaxed. STAGE 2: After complete relaxation, the fiber was then extended to 200% strain with the AFM tip followed by incubation with 10 mM GSSG for 30 min. After the GSSG treatment the excess GSSG was removed by washing using 3 excessive PBS buffer exchanges. The fiber was again relaxed. Following complete relaxation, the fiber was again subjected to repetitive incremental extensions up to 0.4 strain while measuring resistance force, as before. The fiber was relaxed. NOTE: Stage 2 was omitted in the DTT only control experiment. STAGE 3: After complete relaxation, the fiber was then extended a final time to 200% strain and the glutathionylation mark removed with 1 mM DTT for 40 min. After the DTT treatment the AFM setup was washed using 3 excessive PBS buffer exchanges to remove residual DTT. The fiber was relaxed as before and was again subjected to repetitive incremental extensions up to 0.4 strain while measuring resistance force, as before. Experiments were independently replicated three times.

Integrin switch Immunofluorescence imaging

Imaging of Fn using H5 scFv

Forty-eight hours before decellularization, 1 µg/ml Alexa 555 labeled Fn was added into DMEM medium with 15% FBS to enable incorporatino into Fn-rich ECM. After decellularization (as described above), the dECM was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and incubated in 3% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) at room temperature for 1 h. Myc-tagged H5 antibody33 (1:100) (10 µg/mL) was added to the surface of the decellularized matrix at 4 °C overnight. Detection of H5 was enabled with anti-myc antibody (1:1000) (R950-25, Fisher Scientific) and an anti-mouse Alexa 647 secondary antibody (1:2000) (A32728, Thermo Fisher). Image analysis and ratiometric images were performed and generated by custom MATLAB code: Otsu’s method was used to threshold images, signal passed threshold on both Fn and H5 channel was masked, then the ratio of fluorescence intensity of H5 and Fn were compared among different groups. Images of each condition were taken from multiple ROIs within multiple samples, each experiment was replicated three times.

Integrin switch and MRTF

HFF cells were seeded at a density of 3000/cm2 and cultured for 2 h on dECM in serum free DMEM medium. Cells were then fixed by 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, permeabilized by 0.2% triton-X for 5 min and blocked with 5% normal goat serum before primary antibody incubation (1:200) overnight at 4 °C. Primary antibody used in these experiments: active αvβ3 (WOW-1, gift of Sanford Shattil, University of California, San Diego), active β1 (9EG7, BD Pharmingen), anti-MKL1/MRTF (HPA030782, Sigma-Aldrich), rabbit anti-paxillin (Y113, Abcam). Nuclei were stained with Hoechst (1:1000) (H3569, Thermo Fisher), while Alexa 488 phalloidin (1:40) (Thermo Fisher) was used for actin staining. All fluorescence images were imaged by an UltraView Vox Spinning Disk Confocal Microscope (PerkinElmer) (63X). For the MRTF nuclear translocation experiment, z-stacks of 10 slices through the whole cell thickness were recorded. For the integrin switch quantitation, paxillin-positive clusters greater than 0.5 μm were used to define focal adhesions and set the mask for integrin signal intensity analysis. All image analysis was conducted in Volocity software. In Volocity, threshold was set accordingly to different channels for all the samples, only signal above the threshold were accounted for in fluorescence intensity measurements. Each experiment was replicated three times.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism was used to do all the analysis, data were presented as mean ± SD. Analysis of statistical outliers was performed and outliers removed prior to graphical presentation. Two-tail Mann Whitney test and Kruskal–Wallis test were used. Significant differences were considered when P < 0.05, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001.
