Introduction

Congenital complete heart block (CCHB) without structural heart disease affects 1 in 25,000 live births and is frequently associated with maternal antibodies, particularly anti-Ro and anti-La,1 a condition termed autoimmune CCHB (CCHBAI).2 It has a reported perinatal mortality rate of 2.3%–14%, with 60% of affected patients requiring pacemaker (PM) implantation during the neonatal period.1, 2, 3

Despite the association with maternal antibodies, only 2%–5% of exposed fetuses develop CCHB, and the determinants of this outcome are unclear.1,3 Moreover, even monozygotic twins exposed to identical antibodies can demonstrate discordant outcomes, with multiple reports documenting CCHB discordance at birth between human leukocyte antigen-matched twins.2,4

The long-term outcomes of affected twins warrant particular attention, given their shared genetic and in utero environment. Although some cases have been followed during early childhood,2,4 there have been no reports with follow-up extending to 15 years. Nor have any existing accounts described late-onset CCHB. Here, we present a case of possible CCHB diagnosed 15 years after birth. The twin was initially unaffected but ultimately required PM implantation, mirroring the neonatal intervention required by her sister.

Case report

A female with no relevant medical history had a normal pregnancy with female monochorionic twin fetuses (G2 P0 A0) until week 22, when 1 twin exhibited an abnormally low heart rate (HR). The pregnancy continued until week 31, when atrioventricular (AV) CHB was detected via fetal Doppler scan (Figure 1). Until then, the only relevant clinical alteration in maternal laboratory testing was a reduced hemoglobin level (9.4 g/dL). After delivery, we found high positivity for antinuclear and anti-Ro and anti-La antibodies. As there were also clinical features of xerostomia and polyarthritis, we diagnosed Sjogren’s syndrome. Both twins were born without complications. The bradycardic twin was diagnosed with CHB using electrocardiography (ECG) in the neonatal intensive care unit. Although the neonatal intensive care unit ECG recording was unavailable, the available motion (M)-mode echocardiography (echo) images consistently showed CHB (Figure 1A and 1B). The patient underwent PM implantation (unicameral epicardial PM) during the first week of life.

The unaffected twin exhibited normal HR, and there were no abnormalities detected by ICU ECG monitoring. Echo revealed a structurally normal heart (Figure 1C and 1D).

The twin affected at birth underwent PM generator changes at the ages of 6 and 13 years. Following PM implantation, she remained asymptomatic, with normal growth and satisfactory weight gain.

The unaffected twin was followed closely by a pediatric cardiologist until the age of 3 years. No HR or ECG abnormalities were detected during this period. The growth and weight gain of the affected and unaffected twins were comparable. In subsequent years, the unaffected twin underwent 3–4 cardiovascular evaluations, all indicating a healthy clinical status. However, despite the absence of symptoms, her ECG at age 10 years showed a first-degree AV block, with a PR interval of 0.24 seconds. This had not been present in earlier childhood (Figure 2). Despite this new conduction disturbance, the patient remained asymptomatic. Therefore, Holter monitoring was not initiated at this time.

At age 15 years, the unaffected twin began experiencing dizziness at school, followed by near-syncope and syncopal episodes. Holter monitoring revealed intermittent CHB, but echocardiographic and laboratory findings remained unchanged, 3 months after symptom onset. Reversible causes of CHB, such as hypothyroidism and myocarditis, were excluded as laboratory tests (including a troponin test to assess possible damage to the heart) and echocardiography (ECHO) were normal. The patient underwent endocardial dual-chamber PM implantation (Assurity MRI DR, Abbott, USA), and her symptoms rapidly resolved. Currently, her heart rhythm exhibits first-degree AV block most of the time, but alternates with periods of second-degree AV block and CHB. The latter is prevented by ventricular pacing (Figure 4). The PM data indicate that ventricular pacing is required 30%–40% of the time. The patient has experienced no further episodes of syncope or symptoms of low cardiac output. Her echo was unremarkable at 6-month and 12-month follow-ups.

After 18 months of follow-up, we were able to complete more comprehensive laboratory and imaging investigations. This included Chagas serology (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay–IgG), which was negative. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed no alterations of any kind, with no myocardial late gadolinium enhancement (Figure 5). The patient’s symptoms remain resolved. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to describe a likely case of delayed CCHB in a twin who developed it 15 years after her sister, who was born with CCHB. Their mother was positive for anti-Ro and anti-La antibodies. Although discordant CCHB at birth has previously been described, long-term follow-up is scarce, with patients monitored only during early childhood.4

However, given the involvement of twins in this case, alternative CCHB diagnoses in a structurally normal heart must be considered, especially those genetic in origin. Non-immunological etiologies often involve pathogenic mutations of genes including SCN5A, SCN1B, SCN10A, TRMP4, and NKX2-5.5, 6, 7 A genetic etiology could be considered as the phenotypic discordance between the twins may result from variable genetic penetrance. Considering the evolutive nature of the disease in the previously unaffected twin, it could be the consequence of a progressive cardiac conduction disorder, as these are often related to SCN5A mutations.5,7 Genetic testing was not performed in the mother or the twins because of the mother’s positivity for anti-Ro and anti-La antibodies and the absence of any ECG features associated with genetic mutations, such as those on the SCN5A spectrum, or any other arrhythmias.

We investigated a differential diagnosis to eliminate other possible causes of CHB in a 15-year-old girl. No infection symptoms preceded the CHB diagnosis, and the symptoms persisted for 3 months and progressively worsened, ruling out infection. The patient’s normal troponin and C-reactive protein levels and the absence of any abnormalities on her echo made myocarditis unlikely. High-grade AV blocks associated with myocarditis are rare. In 31,760 patients, Ogunbayo et al found an incidence rate of 1.1%.8 Moreover, elevated troponin levels are a hallmark of myocarditis, even with mild symptoms or chronic presentation.9,10 An important feature in the present case was the progressive supra-Hissian disease, which ranged from first-degree AV block to CHB. This, together with the patient’s narrow escape QRS, increased the likelihood of a congenital cause, because CCHB is usually nodal or supra-Hissian, and the escape QRS is narrow.3,6,7 The patient’s normal cardiac MRI, with no apparent myocarditis sequelae, also made this diagnosis unlikely.

We further found other causes of CHB to be improbable. Lyme disease is not endemic in South America, only in North America and Europe,11 and the patient reported no travel history. Even if it were locally endemic, the probability of our patient having Lyme carditis was very low based on clinical scores for this condition.12 Again, evidence from her cardiac MRI did not support this diagnosis, as there were no sequelae of previous inflammation present. Chagas disease was also improbable, as it was not epidemiologically likely, and there were no clinical features, serology, or cardiac MRI findings compatible with this condition.

Hence, after excluding these other possible etiologies, CCHBAI remained the most plausible diagnosis, particularly as the patient’s twin had presented with CCHB in utero and high levels of the anti-Ro and anti-La antibodies were detected in the mother. In mothers with these antibodies, the fetus may be affected by AV node (AVN) injury between the 16th and 24th weeks of pregnancy. This is the stage at which these antibodies cross the placenta. The antibodies bind to L-type calcium channels, particularly in the AVN, leading to inflammation, fibrosis, and AVN calcification. Macrophages secrete transforming growth factor-beta1 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha, contributing to further fibrosis and AVN dysfunction.3,6 Importantly, Bornaun et al have identified a polymorphism in the transforming growth factor-beta1 gene as the causative factor for interindividual variability.13 This supports the hypothesis presented in this case report, as it demonstrates that monozygotic twins can present with different phenotypes when exposed to the same antibodies.

Another possible explanation for the differing phenotypes observed in the twins under discussion is Askanase’s contention that post-inflammatory injury can continue long after maternal autoantibody clearance.3 Thus, there is an ongoing fibrotic process even in the absence of anti-Ro and anti-La antibodies, with other factors potentially contributing to its perpetuation. This leads to mild but persistent injury insufficiently severe to affect serological markers of inflammation.3 The progression of conduction disturbances observed in the present case supports this thesis. A normal PR interval progressed to first-degree AV block, and then to symptomatic CHB, in accordance with Askanase’s observations.3

Bergman et al investigated the correlation between isolated CHB and maternal positivity for anti-Ro and anti-La antibodies in cases with no other plausible explanation, irrespective of age at CHB onset.14 They found positivity for maternal autoantibodies in 88% of such isolated CHB cases and the age at CHB onset to range from 4 months to 43 years, pointing out that late-onset CHB likely results from aggressive immune activity during fetal life, with multiple factors contributing to its progression after birth.14

Other possible explanations for different phenotypes in genetically identical monozygotic twins exposed to the same immunological aggression in utero include placental vascular flow distribution (territoriality), epigenetic factors, chromosomal mosaicism, and gene penetrance.15 The pathophysiology is likely intricate and remains to be fully elucidated.

Given the range of potential contributing factors, this case is particularly important, as the possibility of late-onset CCHB in a previously healthy twin has implications for causation. A genetic predisposition can result in varied clinical outcomes following persistent mild inflammation, emphasizing the need for long-term cardiological monitoring beyond the typical 2–3 years of both children affected at birth and their siblings.

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first case report of late CCHBAI in a structurally normal heart occurring 15 years after birth in a healthy twin. The factors contributing to the development of advanced heart block in the children of anti-Ro/anti-La-positive mothers have not been fully elucidated. The pathophysiology underlying the late onset of this condition is complex. It may be related to mild chronic autoimmune activation, as well as complex genetic factors leading to late AVN dysfunction.

These findings do not conclusively prove late-onset CCHBAI in our patient; however, they suggest that clinicians should closely monitor the healthy twin over an extended period when discordant CCHB occurs. Further studies are required to clarify the complex pathogenesis of CCHB, along with the evolutionary patterns and outcomes of monochorionic twins discordant for CCHB at birth.
