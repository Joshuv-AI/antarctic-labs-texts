Multifocal small bowel gastrointestinal stromal tumor causing perforation peritonitis: A rare cause of acute abdomen
Authors: Bharati Shringi, Vinita Rathi, Preeti Meena, Kalayan Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A female patient in late 40s presented with severe abdominal pain, distension and fever for a week. Ultrasound abdomen revealed ascites and a large indeterminate mass in the pelvis, but bowel loops were not dilated. Contrast-enhanced CT (CECT) abdomen revealed a large, exophytic, heterogeneously enhancing intraperitoneal mass, arising from a jejunal loop in the pelvis; showing fistulous communication with an adjacent small bowel loop. Multiple smaller enhancing rounded, serosal masses were also seen in the jejunum and ileum along with pneumoperitoneum, moderate ascites and peritoneal enhancement. A differential diagnosis of multifocal Gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST) or leiomyosarcoma was entertained on CECT. Exploratory laparotomy revealed one large and multiple small tumors along with and minute small bowel perforations; histopathology confirmed the diagnosis. Presentation of multiple small bowel GIST with perforation peritonitis on preoperative computed tomography (CT) has not been reported earlier.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal stromal tumors, commonly referred to as GISTs, are the most prevalent mesenchymal neoplasms found in the gastrointestinal tract, arising from interstitial cells of Cajal. The patient demographic encompasses an age range of approximately 10-100 years, with a median age situated in the mid-60s. Notably, the incidence of this condition appears to be evenly distributed between male and female patients [1].

GISTs typically present as solitary, well-defined masses [2]; and multiple lesions are very rare. The presence of multiple GISTs suggest underlying gene mutations [3]. Sporadic multifocal GISTs in adults account for only 11% of all multifocal diseases [2]. Therefore, patients with multiple GISTs should be screened for additional tumors and should be monitored appropriately [4].

The diagnosis of small bowel GISTs is often made only after complications such as hemorrhage, obstruction or perforation. They are generally associated with less favorable outcomes compared to those originating in the stomach [2]. High-risk GISTs occur more frequently in the ileum than in the duodenum and jejunum, or they may often arise from Meckel’s diverticulum [4]. None of the diagnostic procedures used can guarantee a 100% certainty of diagnosis. CT is the best imaging modality for an abdominal emergency and a contrast-enhanced CT can help in the diagnosis of GIST [2].

Small bowel GISTs can be asymptomatic and are usually discovered incidentally on abdominal computed tomography (CT) scans, during endoscopy, or surgical procedures for other indications [2]. However, GISTs can present diagnostic challenges in emergency situations, causing gastrointestinal hemorrhage, rupture and bowel obstruction, that require urgent care [3]. Acute abdomen due to perforation of solitary small bowel GIST in the peritoneal cavity is rare. Free perforation may occur as a result of hemorrhagic necrosis in large tumor masses, bowel obstruction or intussusception of the tumor [5].

Various imaging modalities are used to evaluate GISTs. These include ultrasonography, CT, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), fluorodeoxyglucose-positron emission tomography (FDG-PET), digital subtraction angiography and endoscopy. Among these, CT is most commonly utilized for diagnosis, staging, surveillance, and monitoring responses to molecularly targeted therapies in clinical practice [4]. A conclusive diagnosis can only be made through histopathology [2].

Surgery is the preferred treatment for patients with nonmetastatic disease. The treatment of GISTs is complete resection, with a 5-year-survival rate between 48%–80% [6]. Prognosis is worse in perforated GISTs and in these cases, complete surgical resection of the tumor must be followed by adjuvant therapy with imatinib [5]. We present a rare case of multiple small bowel GIST presenting with perforation peritonitis on CECT.

Case presentation

A female patient in late 40s presented to the surgery emergency of our tertiary care hospital with fever on and off for a week; sudden onset of severe abdominal pain and distension, with nonpassage of feces and flatus, for the past 3 days suggestive of subacute intestinal obstruction. There was no history of vomiting or diarrhea. She had a history of undergoing exploratory laparotomy 10 years back; no details were available but a previous ultrasound abdomen report mentioned a large uterine fibroid.

On examination, vital signs revealed tachycardia and tachypnoea, and temperature of 102.4°F while blood pressure and SpO2 were normal. She was conscious and oriented. Pallor and icterus were present but there was no cyanosis/clubbing/lymphadenopathy/pedal edema. Abdominal examination revealed distension, generalized tenderness, guarding and rigidity, with absent bowel sounds; and there was no organomegaly. The rest of the systemic examination was normal. On digital rectal examination, no mass was palpable, rectal tone was normal and no evidence of blood was seen. A clinical diagnosis of perforation peritonitis was made.

Investigations

Laboratory investigations revealed mildly elevated total leukocyte count (13.3 × 109 cells/L), anemia (hemoglobin 3.7 g/L), PT/INR 13.9 s/1.07, elevated serum creatinine (1.8 mg/L), blood urea (117mg/dL) and total serum bilirubin (2.7mg/L) with direct bilirubin (0.9 mg/L).

The abdomen radiograph-AP projection (erect and supine) showed no air under the diaphragm or any dilated small bowel loops with air-fluid levels. Ultrasound of the abdomen showed a moderate amount of free fluid with no echoes or septations. No dilated bowel loops were observed. A large heterogeneously hypoechoic, well-defined, lobulated soft tissue mass was seen in the pelvis, abutting the anterior aspect of the uterine fundus and urinary bladder inferiorly. The provisional diagnosis on ultrasound was moderate ascites with suspicion of an atypical subserosal uterine fibroid.

For further evaluation, CECT abdomen was done. It revealed a large, hypodense, heterogeneously enhancing, exophytic lesion measuring 7.0 × 7.2 × 6.6 cm, abutting a distal jejunal loop in the pelvis. It showed necrotic areas, intralesional tiny air foci and few calcific foci (Fig. 1). The mass appeared to show fistulization anteriorly into an adjacent small bowel loop and extraluminal free air foci (Fig. 2A) were seen in the peritoneal cavity. It was abutting the uterus and urinary bladder inferiorly (Fig. 2). On CECT, the differential diagnosis was primary/recurrent GIST with metastases (T3N0M1); or multiple small bowel gastrointestinal stromal tumors/leiomyosarcomas, with the largest mass showing fistulization into small bowel; with perforation peritonitis with [7]. Peritoneal dissemination of GISTs, referred to as peritoneal sarcomatosis, presents as spherical and hypervascular implants, without associated ascites or obstruction of hollow organs [4]; hence was not considered. Peritoneal carcinomatosis tends to appear as flat or ovoid lesions and can cause bowel obstruction and hydronephrosis [4], so was not entertained as a differential. Neuroendocrine tumors were not considered, as hypervascular pattern, typical of neuroendocrine tumor was not observed in our case. Lymphomas show a delayed enhancement pattern [4], so this diagnosis was also excluded.

Outcome

The patient underwent emergency exploratory laparotomy and on opening the peritoneum, approximately 800 mL of feculent fluid was drained. An exophytic mass measuring 10 × 8 cm, was found approximately 80 cm from the duodeno-jejunal junction. Postoperative course

The patient developed bile leak from the anastomotic site on the second postoperative day, causing skin excoriation; which was managed with meticulous care, as she did not give consent for re-exploration. During the postoperative period she required blood transfusion for correction of anemia (Hb improved to 8.2 g/dL), mechanical ventilation and pressor support along with antibiotics for ongoing infections. Her kidney functions deteriorated, necessitating hemodialysis. On postoperative day 18 she developed hypotension and tachycardia, followed by cardiac arrest. Despite cardiopulmonary resuscitation, she could not be revived.

Histopathological findings

The resected bowel measured 100 cm in length with multiple solid nodules (more than 20 in number) over the small bowel (third part of duodenum, jejunum, and ileum), largest measuring 7.0 cm in greatest dimension. Extent of necrosis present within the tumor was less than 50%. The mitotic rate was 8 per 5 mm2. The histologic grading done was “Grade 2″ ie, “high grade.” Seven mesenteric lymph nodes were identified, and all were negative for tumor. The proximal resected end was free while the distal resected end was involved by tumor. On immunohistochemistry tumor cells were positive for KIT (CD117). Discussion

Gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GIST) can occur anywhere along the gastrointestinal tract with 25%-30% occurring in the small intestine and jejunum in 10% of cases [8]. GISTs are usually solitary masses; multiple GISTs are very rare. Usually multifocal small bowel GIST are found intraoperatively, so examination of the entire bowel is necessary during surgery [2].

Our case is unique as the diagnosis of multiple GISTs (>10) was suspected on preoperative imaging by CECT. Jemedafe et al. [3] reported a low-grade ileal GIST, proven on image guided biopsy, showing multiple smaller lesions at different sites throughout the small bowel during diagnostic laparoscopy. Brogna et al. [2] reported that CT showed 2 masses (GIST) but exploratory laparotomy revealed additional small lesions. Graziosis et al. [9] reported a case of sporadic multifocal GIST comprising of 42 multiple GISTs in a young female patient. Li et al. [10] studied 20 patients with multiple GIST. Tarallo et al. [11] reported a case of multiple exophytic (1-9 cm) lesions proven as GIST on biopsy. Chaun Li et al. [12] described multiple sporadic GIST on PET- CT as multiple solid masses diagnosed on histopathology and genetic testing on excised tumors . Shen et al. [13] found that only 27 cases out of 44, were of truly multiple sporadic GIST.

It can be challenging to pinpoint the exact origin of a large GIST [14]. Large GISTs may mimic retroperitoneal, gynecological (ovarian), or inguinal hernia [14,15]. Radiologists may sometimes underestimate their diagnoses, particularly in emergency situations [2]. Ruptured GISTs show a heterogeneous texture, a laminated or whorled appearance on ultrasonography and CT scans [4]. The whorled appearance seen on ultrasound of the pelvis done in the emergency in our case, led to suspicion of a large subserosal fibroid. Brogna et al. [2] also stated that in multifocal GIST radiologists can have difficulty in defining the point of origin of large lesions; laparoscopy was mandatory for correct diagnosis.

The imaging characteristics of small intestine GISTs can vary, particularly in multifocal forms [4], as seen in our patient. Larger lesions usually display heterogeneous density on both noncontrast and contrast-enhanced CT scans. The larger GISTs may have a combination of intraluminal and extraluminal growths or may grow solely in the extraluminal space. Large GISTs can show areas of necrosis and hemorrhage; however, cystic degeneration and calcification are rare findings [2].

Calcification is seen in 7%-22% of small bowel GISTs. It tends to occur more frequently in larger GISTs as seen in our case; and may be linked to a higher risk [4].

In small bowel GISTs (≥5 cm), necrosis is observed in 66.7%–100% of tumors, and intratumoral hemorrhage, which is identified as a hyperdense area on noncontrast CT, is seen in 66.7%–88.9% of these tumors. Intratumoral hemorrhage or necrosis develops when the hypervascular tumor outgrows its blood supply [4].

In the case of large tumors, complications can include cavitation, fistula formation, rupture, and perforation. Even in presence of a large tumor, incidence of bowel obstruction is rare. On sectional imaging, ulceration and cavitation or fistula appear as recesses on the tumor's surface. These are associated with a tract leading to the lumen, which may contain gas or fluid, respectively. The presence of intratumoral gas could indicate a potential communication with the gastrointestinal lumen, as seen in our case [4]. Pneumoperitoneum, as seen in the present case, occurs in cases of perforated GISTs when there is a connection between the gastrointestinal tract and the abdominal cavity [4]. In multifocal GIST pneumoperitoneum has not been reported till date, to the best of our knowledge.

Kataria et al. reported a 30-year-old with GIST who presented in emergency with abdominal pain. Karagulle et al. reported a case of 70-year-old male admitted to emergency, who was found to have an intra-abdominal abscess on CECT, but laparotomy revealed multifocal jejunal stromal tumors with perforations [16]. Memmi et al. [17] reported a solitary GIST with central necrotic areas on CECT arising from the jejunum with a small perforation at the medial side, similar to the operative findings of the largest lesion in our case.

Multiple GISTs, also called multicentric or multifocal GIST do not have a formal definition at present [18]. Currently, there are no established criteria for confirming the diagnosis of primary versus metastatic tumors in patients initially presenting with multiple tumors. Multifocality may reflect metastatic spreading of the largest tumor of single primary GIST [19]. Li et al. defined multifocal GIST as ≥2 masses with histopathology positive and excluded those located in extra GIST sites which are considered to have metastasized including mesentery, omentum, peritoneum or abdomen [18].

In a study by Patnaik et al. [20] out of 42 GIST cases, 22 had metastasis. Mesentery, liver and omentum were most frequent sites for metastasis. Metastasis showed attenuation, enhancement similar to prior GIST and frequently showed hemorrhage, necrosis and calcification, which was not seen in our case [20]. GIST with peritoneal dissemination was also called peritoneal GISTosis by Sugarbaker [21].

Metastatic peritoneal leiomyomatosis may occur by intraperitoneal seeding with ascitic fluid, embolic metastasis or rupture of primary tumor; which is more common in large exophytic tumor [22]. Peritoneal tumor seeding manifest as well-defined peritoneal nodules or masses with or without smudged granular pattern of omental infiltration. Paraaortic lymph nodes are unusual. Differential diagnosis is peritoneal metastasis from ovarian or gastrointestinal malignancies [22].

Differentials of metastasis or multiple GIST represents a challenge for proper work up, predicting prognosis and therapeutic strategy. In multiple GISTs each lesion has different mutational panels while metastatic GIST shows same genetic patterns in all lesions [11]. Some researchers have proposed that lesions in multifocal disease may result from different somatic mutations in the KIT gene [2]. Different patterns of KIT or PDGFRA mutation were found in various paired lesions, indicating that the occurrence of multiple sporadic GISTs may represent a synchronous occurrence of independent sporadic GISTs [10].

Multifocal GIST is a poor prognostic indicator and typically occurs with specific syndromes such as neurofibromatosis and Carney–Stratakis Syndrome, in familial GISTs, and, in pediatric cases [2].

On the other hand, Chen Li et al. [18] have reported that multifocal GIST differs from single GIST in lower morbidity, female preponderance, young age, multiple organ involvement and possible syndromic GIST [Li Chen]. Overall survival is similar but, higher rates of metastasis in multifocal GIST patients results in poorer recurrence free survival rate.

No syndromic association was present in our patient; so metastatic GIST is a possibility, as our histopathology revealed a “high grade tumor” which can have a propensity for metastasis. However, a sporadic multifocal disease which is a rare condition and an underestimated entity, was also entertained in our case. Genetic mutation study was not conducted as the patient developed post operative complications; and these studies were not available at our institute.

The management of GIST necessitates a thorough evaluation of the disease's extent and progression, accompanied by appropriate risk stratification. Surgical intervention is the cornerstone of treatment [3].

Radical surgery remains the mainstay of treatment for multiple GISTs, while the administration of imatinib is recommended for the treatment of metastatic GISTs [10]. The goal is to achieve microscopic negative margins for an R0 resection without tumor rupture [2]. Our case which presented with perforation peritonitis due to the perforation of largest GIST, underwent resection of the small bowel with the tumor growths, but R0 resection could not be achieved; and she succumbed due to the complications in the postoperative period.

Conclusion

Multiple small bowel GIST is a rare entity that may be misinterpreted on imaging. The diagnosis is often delayed until complications arise. A preoperative differential diagnosis of multiple GIST on CECT as a cause of acute abdomen, which was made in our case, may not always be possible. Detailed intraoperative evaluation of the entire bowel is mandatory to exclude this possibility. The primary management remains complete surgical resection with use of adjuvant treatment in cases with high-risk features.
