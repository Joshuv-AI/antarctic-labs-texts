Evaluation of lipid nanoparticle adjuvants with different PEG lipid ratios and phospholipid modifications in non-human primates
Authors: Máté Vadovics, Maria Betina Pampena, Kieu Lam, Kendall A. Lundgreen, Hiromi Muramatsu, András Sárközy, Edit Ábrahám, Zoltan Lipinszki, Paul Bates, Michael R. Betts, James Heyes, Norbert Pardi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) used in mRNA vaccines possess adjuvant activity, but little is known about the composition-function relationships that drive their adjuvanticity. Recent studies demonstrated that, in addition to the ionizable lipids, the type of phospholipids and the ratio of polyethylene glycol (PEG) lipids in LNPs also influence the magnitude and quality of vaccine-induced immune responses. Here we evaluated the cellular and humoral immunity elicited by LNPs with different phospholipids and PEG lipid ratios in cynomolgus macaques. We observed differences in antibody and CD4+ T cell responses across LNP groups, although not all comparisons reached statistical significance, likely due to the limited sample size. While this study was exploratory, the findings differed from those previously obtained with these LNPs in mice, confirming the importance of assessing LNP formulations in non-human primates prior to clinical evaluation.

Introduction

Nucleoside-modified mRNA-lipid nanoparticle (LNP) vaccines can elicit protective immunity against infectious diseases.1 LNPs typically consist of four components: ionizable lipid, cholesterol, phospholipid, and polyethylene glycol (PEG).2 They are critical components of current mRNA vaccines as they protect the nucleic acid from degradation, facilitate cellular uptake and endosomal mRNA release, and possess adjuvant activity.2,3 Our previous work, and findings from other laboratories demonstrated that the ionizable lipids are critical for the adjuvanticity of LNPs.3,4 We and others also showed that, in addition to the ionizable lipids, the type of phospholipids and PEG lipid ratios also have an impact on the adjuvanticity of LNPs.5,6 Briefly, we found that an LNP with distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DSPC), a zwitterionic phospholipid, and 0.5% PEG lipid induced stronger humoral immune responses and better B cell memory than LNPs with the same ionizable lipid but higher PEG lipid content or negatively charged phospholipids such as 2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoglycerol (DOPG) or 1,2-dioctadecenoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoserine (DOPS). Interestingly, our LNP with DOPS and 1.5% PEG lipid induced the strongest CD8+ T cell responses. We concluded that it is possible to rationally design LNPs for future mRNA vaccines to elicit more robust antibody and/or T cell responses.5 These experiments were performed in mice; however, it is well-documented that adjuvant effectivity can vary across species.7,8 To better determine the translational potential of adjuvanticity of these LNPs to humans, we therefore assessed the humoral and cellular immune responses induced by these formulations in non-human primates (NHPs).

Results

No noticeable adverse events were observed after vaccine administration throughout the study period (Figures S1 and S2). Cytokine and chemokine levels were measured in serum samples collected before immunization (naive samples) and 4 h post boost vaccination. No major alterations indicative of systemic inflammation were detected (Figure S1). No significant weight loss was observed following either the prime or boost immunizations (Figure S2). Hematological and injection site observations (data not shown) revealed no vaccine-related abnormalities. All measured hematological values remained within or near published reference ranges,9,10 with no consistent trends indicative of immune-mediated toxicity or systemic inflammation. No severe or clinically relevant injection site reactions were observed; any local findings were minimal and transient.

Humoral and T cell immune responses were then analyzed. All formulations induced high levels of IgG antibodies against the SARS-CoV-2 spike receptor-binding domain (RBD) (Figure 1D) with strong neutralizing activity (Figure 1E). In our previous mouse studies, 4 weeks after a single dose, LNP H (DSPC, 0.5% PEG lipid) elicited over a 2-fold increase in binding antibody levels and neutralization activity compared to LNP Y (DOPG, 0.5% PEG lipid) and LNP W (DOPS, 1.5% PEG lipid).5 In contrast, in the present NHP study 4 weeks after a single immunization, LNP W was associated with the most robust binding antibody responses (Figure 1D). While differences were not statistically significant, LNP W induced higher binding antibody levels compared to other formulations, including over 3-fold higher binding antibody levels than LNP Q, and approximately 2-fold higher than LNP H and LNP Y 4 weeks post prime immunization (Figure 1D). These differences increased post-boost, with LNP W generating over 4-fold higher binding antibody levels than LNP Q. Moreover, unlike in mice, LNPs containing negatively charged phospholipids (LNP Y and W) elicited the highest levels of neutralizing antibody titers in NHPs, approximately 2-fold higher than those induced by LNPs with neutrally charged DSPC (LNP Q and H) at 4 weeks post-prime (Figure 1E); however, these differences were not statistically significant. After boost, only minor differences in neutralizing antibody titers were observed between the groups (Figure 1E). Although the differences in binding antibody levels or neutralization capacity between the LNP groups were not statistically significant, it is important to note that only four NHPs were used in each immunization group in these experiments. A larger sample size might have revealed statistically significant differences between the groups.

As we had previously observed robust but variable antigen-specific CD8+ and CD4+ T cell responses in mice vaccinated with these LNPs,5 we analyzed cellular immune responses in NHPs. In contrast to our previous mouse data, we measured nearly undetectable CD8+ (Figures 2A and 2B) but strong CD4+ T cell responses (Figures 2A and 2C) in monkeys. In NHPs, LNP Y induced significantly higher frequencies of TNF-α+IL-2+ and IFN-γ+IL-2+ CD4+ T cells than LNP Q at 2 weeks post-boost (day 42) vaccination (Figure 2C). Discussion

Nucleoside-modified mRNA-LNPs represent a milestone of vaccine development with broad potential for infectious diseases and cancer.1,11 LNPs not only facilitate the delivery of antigen-encoding mRNA but also possess adjuvant activity.3 However, how LNP composition shapes immune responses remains poorly understood. Most LNP adjuvant studies have focused on ionizable lipid optimizations,4 and the roles of other LNP components remain underexplored. Our previous work in mice demonstrated that the adjuvant activity of LNPs can be tailored by modifying the phospholipids and PEG lipid ratios.5 To assess the translational relevance of the findings in mice, here we evaluated the same LNP formulations in NHPs. Although several rodent studies have shown that phospholipid identity and charge can influence immunogenicity,5,6,12,13 no published comparative NHP study examined immune responses induced by LNPs containing different phospholipids and PEG lipid ratios, to date.

In our mouse study, LNP H (containing DSPC and 1.5% PEG lipid) induced the strongest binding and neutralizing antibody responses after a single immunization, outperforming LNPs containing negatively charged phospholipids such as DOPG or DOPS.5 In contrast, in this work, LNP W induced the highest binding antibody levels 4 weeks post prime vaccination of NHPs, with similar trends observed post boost vaccination. Species-specific differences were also observed in cellular immune responses. While all LNP formulations induced antigen-specific CD8+ T cell responses in mice, these responses were nearly undetectable in NHPs. NHPs showed strong CD4+ T cell responses across all formulations, consistent with immune responses reported in humans after SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccination.14

Similar to phospholipids, the impact of PEG lipid ratios on immune responses has not been directly examined in NHPs. NHP studies of PEG lipid ratios have focused on safety and formulation performance rather than immunogenicity. Lam et al. demonstrated that optimal PEG content is critical for LNP performance in NHPs and differs from observations in mice, highlighting the importance of species-specific formulation design.15 The study focused on protein expression, pharmacokinetics, particle size and stability, and repeat dose tolerability, without assessing humoral or cellular immune responses.

Notably, our present NHP study was exploratory. Some observed differences between LNP formulations did not reach statistical significance; therefore, we report them as preliminary observations to inform future studies. Within these limitations, our results provide initial insights into how LNP composition influences immune responses in NHPs. Specifically, our results suggest that phospholipid identity and PEG lipid ratio may influence both cellular and humoral immune responses not only in mice but also in NHPs. The different findings between mouse and NHPs further underscore the importance of evaluating LNP formulations in NHPs prior to clinical testing, as mouse and NHP immune responses to various adjuvants can significantly differ,8,16,17,18 due to species-specific anatomy, immune function, and genetic diversity.19

Materials and methods

Animals

The investigators faithfully adhered to the “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals” by the Committee on Care of Laboratory Animal Resources Commission on Life Sciences, National Research Council. Following approval by Biomere’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC protocol #15-03), NHPs were housed individually, received Monkey Diet 5038 daily and filtered tap water ad libitum, and were monitored at least twice daily for general health, with regular clinical observations and weekly body weight measurements. Immunization and sample collection were performed at the Biomere-Biomedical Research Models Laboratory (57 Union Street, Worcester, MA 01608, USA). The study utilized 20 cynomolgus macaques (Macaca fascicularis). The animals were 4–7 years old, of Mauritius origin, purpose-bred, and males only.

Immunizations and blood collection

NHPs received 50 μg mRNA-LNP on day 0 (prime) followed by 50 μg mRNA-LNP on day 28 (boost). mRNA-LNPs were diluted in 0.9% sodium chloride for injection, USP (sterile saline), and a total volume of 500 μL was injected into the right quadriceps muscle. Blood samples were collected from an appropriate peripheral vein 7 days before prime, 4 weeks post prime, and 1, 2, and 4 weeks post boost immunization.

Safety and reactogenicity assessment

Body weight was measured weekly, and various hematological parameters were assessed on day 7 (1 week post prime vaccination). NHPs were monitored daily for clinical signs and abnormalities at the injection site. Different serum cytokine levels were measured using a Luminex assay on serum samples collected on day −7 (pre-immune) and 4 h post boost vaccination. The induction of 18 selected cytokines (BCA-1, Eotaxin, IFN-γ, GM-CSF, IFN-α, IL-10, IL-2, IL-17A, IL-6, IL-1β, IL-12 (p70), IL-4, IL-5, IP-10, MCP-1, IL-7, MIP-3α, and TNF-α) was evaluated using the Luminex Custom Cynomolgus Macaques Cytokine/Chemokine Magnetic Bead Panel (Millipore Sigma, PRCYTA-40K-18 plex), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Serum samples were thawed on ice, clarified by centrifugation (10,000× g, 10 min, 4°C), diluted 1:2 in assay buffer, and 25 μL of each sample was transferred to the assay. Bead fluorescence was read using the FLEXMAP 3D instrument and Luminex xPONENT 4.2 software, and data were analyzed with Bio-Plex Manager Software 6.1. Blood samples were collected 7 days post prime immunization. Measured hematological parameters included red blood cells, hemoglobin, hematocrit, mean corpuscular volume, mean corpuscular hemoglobin, mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration, red cell distribution width, reticulocytes, immature reticulocyte fraction, low, medium, and high fluorescence ratios, reticulocyte hemoglobin equivalent, platelet indices, and white blood cell counts (data not shown).

mRNA-LNP vaccine production

The mRNA payload for the vaccines encoded the prefusion-stabilized spike glycoprotein (S-2P) from the original Wuhan strain of SARS-CoV-2 (Wuhan-Hu-1, GenBank: MN908947.3). The sequence was codon optimized, synthesized, and cloned into an mRNA production plasmid.20 N1-methylpseudouridine-5′-triphosphate (m1Ψ-5′-triphosphate; TriLink) instead of uridine 5′-triphosphate was used to generate the modified nucleoside-containing mRNAs, and the mRNAs were transcribed to have 101-nucleotide-long poly(A) tails. Co-transcriptional capping of the in vitro-transcribed mRNAs was performed using the trinucleotide cap1 analogue, CleanCap (TriLink). Cellulose (Sigma-Aldrich) was used for mRNA purification.20,21 mRNAs were evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis and were stored frozen at −20°C.

Cellulose-purified S-2P mRNAs were encapsulated by a controlled mixing, self-assembly process. An ethanolic lipid mixture of ionizable lipid (6Z,16Z)-12-((Z)-dec-4-en-1-yl) docosa-6,16-dien-11-yl 5-(dimethylamino) pentanoate (3D-P-DMA), phospholipid, cholesterol and PEG lipid was mixed with an aqueous solution containing mRNA at an acidic pH of 5. Post-formation, the ethanol was removed from the LNP via dialysis with 10,000 molecular weight cutoff, Thermo Fisher Slide-a-Lyzer cassettes (Thermo Fisher Scientific), concentrated with a Vivaspin (10,000 molecular weight cutoff; Cytiva), and further dialyzed into Tris-sucrose buffer at pH 8. Samples were sterile filtered, characterized, adjusted to approximately 0.5 mg mL−1 of total mRNA and frozen at −80°C. The ionizable and PEG-conjugated lipids were synthesized at Genevant. Cholesterol was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Synthechol) and the phospholipids from Avanti Polar Lipids via Millipore Sigma (DSPC, DOPS, and DOPG).

LNP characterization

Details on the design and characterization of LNPs are described in our previous publication.5

Recombinant protein production

The DNA sequence encoding the signal peptide (amino acids 1–14) and the (RBD; amino acids 319–541) of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein (NCBI reference sequence: YP_009724390) in fusion with a C-terminal hexahistidine affinity-tag (His6, GHHHHHH) and a stop codon (hereafter referred to as RBD) was codon-optimized for mammalian expression, synthesized (GenScript), and sub-cloned into the mammalian expression vector pCDNA3.1(−) (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Recombinant RBD was produced in Expi293F mammalian cells and purified from a cell culture supernatant by affinity chromatography following the protocol described previously.22 The purified RBD was buffer exchanged to phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and concentrated to 1 mg mL−1 using an Amicon Ultra centrifugal filter unit (molecular weight cutoff = 10 kDa; Merck Millipore), sterile filtered, and flash frozen in liquid nitrogen. The integrity and purity of the proteins were tested by reducing and non-reducing sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and western blotting techniques.22

Endpoint enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

In Corning 96-Well EIA/RIA Clear Flat-Bottom Polystyrene High-Bind Microplates (Corning Life Sciences), 1 μg mL−1 of purified RBD in PBS (100 μL per well) was added overnight at 4°C for protein coating. The following day, the coated plates were washed four times with a wash buffer (0.05% Tween 20 in PBS). The plates were then blocked with 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 2 h at room temperature (RT) and washed three times with the wash buffer. Monkey sera were diluted in the blocking buffer and incubated for 2 h at RT, followed by three washes. HRP-conjugated anti-monkey IgG secondary antibody (Sigma-Aldrich) was diluted 1:10,000 in blocking buffer and incubated for 1 h, followed by three washes. KPL two-component TMB Microwell Peroxidase Substrate (Seracare) was applied to the plate, and the reaction was stopped with 2N sulfuric acid. Using a SpectraMax 190 microplate reader, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm. A cutoff value of the average of the optical density values of blank wells plus three standard deviations was determined for each plate and used for calculating the area under the curve (AUC). The limit of detection of the assay was a titer of 1:100.

SARS-CoV-2 neutralization assay

Production of VSV pseudotypes with SARS-CoV-2 spike: 293T cells were plated at 3.5 × 106 cells per 10 cm dish; 24 h later, they were transfected using calcium phosphate with 25 μg of pCG1 SARS-CoV-2 Spike D614G delta18 expression plasmid encoding a codon-optimized SARS-CoV2 Spike gene with an 18-residue truncation in the cytoplasmic tail. Twelve hours after transfection, the cells were fed with fresh media containing 5 mM of sodium butyrate to increase the expression of the transfected DNA. Thirty hours post-transfection, the SARS-CoV-2 spike-expressing cells were infected for 2–4 h with vesicular stomatitis virus G protein (VSV-G) pseudotyped VSVΔG-RFP at a multiplicity of infection of ∼3–5. Following infection, the cells were washed twice with media to remove the unbound virus. Media containing the VSVΔG-RFP SARS-CoV-2 pseudotypes were harvested 28–30 h after infection and clarified by centrifugation twice at 1,250× g and then aliquoted and stored at −80°C until used for antibody neutralization analysis.

Antibody neutralization assay using VSVΔG-RFP SARS-CoV-2: all sera were heat inactivated for 30 min at 55°C before use in the neutralization assay. In a 96-well collagen-coated plate, Vero E6 cells stably expressing TMPRSS2 were seeded in 100 μL at 2.5 × 104 cells per well. The next day, 2-fold serially diluted serum samples were mixed with VSVΔG-RFP SARS-CoV-2 pseudotype virus (100–300 focus-forming units per well) and incubated for 1 h at 37°C. To neutralize any potential VSV-G carryover virus, this mixture also included 1E9F9, a mouse anti-VSV Indiana G, at a concentration of 600 ng mL−1 (Absolute Antibody). The media on Vero E6-TMPRSS2 cells were then replaced with the serum-virus mixture. After 22 h of infection, the cells were washed and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde before visualization on an S6 FluoroSpot Analyser (CTL, Shaker Heights). Individual infected foci were quantified, and the values were compared with control wells without antibody. Focus reduction neutralization titer 50% (FRNT50) was measured as the greatest serum dilution at which the focus count was reduced by at least 50% relative to the control cells that were infected with the pseudotype virus in the absence of NHP serum. FRNT50 titers for each sample were measured in at least two technical replicates and were reported for each sample as the geometric mean.

Collection and processing of PBMCs

Whole blood was collected into K2EDTA tubes (BD Biosciences). Following collection, the blood was diluted and transferred to SepMate tubes (STEMCELL Technologies) pre-filled with Lymphoprep (STEMCELL Technologies). Samples were centrifuged at 1,200× g for 15 min at RT. Immediately post-centrifugation, approximately half of the supernatant (plasma) was aspirated, ensuring the buffy coat cell layer remained undisturbed. The remaining plasma and buffy coat were transferred into a 50 mL polypropylene conical tube and the volume was adjusted to 50 mL with Dulbecco’s PBS (DPBS). The samples were centrifuged at 300× g for 8 min at RT. To remove residual red blood cells (RBCs), an RBC lysis buffer (eBiosciences) was added directly to the whole blood samples and incubated at ambient temperature for 10–15 min. For subsequent washing, DPBS was added to adjust the volume to 50 mL, and the mixture was inverted five times. The suspension was centrifuged again at 300× g for 15 min at RT. The supernatant was carefully aspirated, and the pellet was resuspended in 10 mL of DPBS. This suspension was mixed by inverting the tube five times and centrifuged at 300× g for 10 min at RT. The supernatant was discarded without disturbing the pellet layer. After the final wash, the cells were resuspended in cryopreservation medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and counted.

Spike-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cell studies

Lymphocyte stimulation

Cryopreserved PBMCs were thawed and rested overnight in sterile R10 media (RPMI 1640, Corning Life Sciences), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gemini Bio-Products), 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin and 1% L-Glutamine; plus 10U mL−1 DNAse I (Roche Life Sciences, Indianapolis, IN, US) at 37°C, 5% CO2 and 95% humidity incubation conditions. Cells were stimulated at 200 μL final volume in sterile R10 media. In the stimulated condition, cells were incubated with 1 μg mL−1 of SARS-Cov2 spike protein peptide pool (JPT Peptide Technologies). DMSO-treated cells served as a negative control. Positive control samples were stimulated using Staphylococcal Enterotoxin B (SEB, List Biological Laboratories, Campbell, CA, USA) at 1 μg mL−1. Co-stimulation was added to every condition: 1 μg mL−1 anti-CD49d (Clone 9F10, BioLegend) and CD28-ECD (Clone CD28.2, Beckman Coulter) at the start of stimulation. Brefeldin A (1 μg mL−1) (Sigma-Aldrich) and monensin (0.66 μL mL−1) (BD Biosciences) were added 2 hours after initiation of stimulation. Cells were incubated under stimulation conditions for a total of 9 h.

Flow cytometry

All following incubations were performed at RT. Cells were stained for viability exclusion using Live/Dead Fixable Aqua for 10 min, followed by a 20 min incubation with a panel of directly conjugated monoclonal antibodies diluted in equal parts of fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer (PBS containing 0.1% sodium azide and 1% BSA) and Brilliant stain buffer (BD Biosciences). Cells were washed in FACS buffer and fixed/permeabilized using the BD Cytofix/Cytoperm Fixation/Permeabilization Kit (BD Biosciences), following manufacturer’s instructions. Intracellular staining was performed by adding the antibody cocktail (CD14 [BV510, clone M5E2, BioLegend, 1/200], CD16 [BV510, clone 3G8, BioLegend, 1/200] CD20 [BV510, clone 2H7, BioLegend, 1/200] CD4 [BUV661, clone SK3, BD Biosciences, 1/67], CD95 [BUV737, clone DX2, BD Biosciences, 1/150] CD8 [BUV496, clone RPA-T8, BD Biosciences, 1/333] IL-2 [PE, clone MQ1-17H12, BD Biosciences, 1/100] IFN-γ [BV750, clone B27, BD Biosciences, 1/200] CD3 [BUV805, clone SP34-2, BD Biosciences, 1/200] TNF-α [PE-Cy7, clone Mab11, Invitrogen, 1/1400] CD28 [ECD, clone CD28.2, Beckman Coulter, 1/20]) prepared in 1X permwash buffer for 1 h. Stained cells were washed and fixed in PBS containing 1% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich), and stored at 4°C in the dark until acquisition. Flow cytometry data were collected on BD FACSymphony A5 cytometer (BD Biosciences). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (v.10.10, Tree Star, Ashland, OR, USA).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data were collected and expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism v.10.0.0 (GraphPad Software) software package. Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk normality test. Data showing a log-scale distribution (ELISA, neutralization assay) were log-transformed before assessment of normality. For comparisons multiple groups, normally distributed data were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. When data did not meet the assumption of normality, the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s multiple comparisons correction was used. For comparisons between two groups, datasets that passed the normality test were analyzed using an unpaired t test with Welch’s correction. Datasets that did not pass the normality test were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. The significance of the differences between the groups was assessed using the test specified in the legend accompanying the related figure. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.
