A multilevel variation of the sensory branches of the lumbar plexus with an accessory genitofemoral nerve
Authors: D. Stoyanov, S.P. Pavlov
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The lumbar plexus is one of the most variable nerve plexuses in the human body. Located posteriorly to the psoas major muscle, it is the origin of several nerves that cross the retroperitoneal space and are responsible for the innervation of the lower abdominal wall and a portion of the lower limb. Relative to their anatomical route, these nerves can be divided into several major sections: intrapsoas, retroperitoneal, and distal to the inguinal ligament, and all of those are liable to nerve injury during surgical interventions. Here, we describe a complex variation involving the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve, the femoral branch of the genitofemoral nerve, and the anterior femoral nerve, discovered during a routine dissection in an undergraduate human anatomy course for medical students.

Introduction

The lumbar plexus most commonly arises from the ventral branches of the spinal nerves at levels Th12 to L4, where Th12 and L4 contribute only partially to the plexus. There is also the so-called low form of the lumbar plexus, where L5 can also contribute to its formation [4]. The nerves originating from the lumbar plexus can be functionally divided into two groups: those that are purely sensory, including the lateral femoral cutaneous (LFC) and genitofemoral (GF, with its femoral and genital branches, GF-F and GF-G, respectively), and those that are mixed, including the iliohypogastric (IH), ilioinguinal (II), obturator (Ob), and femoral nerves. As this article primarily addresses the sensory components, the subsequent discussion will concentrate solely on their anatomical features. The LFC nerve is usually derived from the L2 and L3 nerve roots, emerges from the posterior aspect of the psoas major muscle, and travels across the iliacus muscle. It exits the abdominal cavity deep to the inguinal ligament and medial to the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS), where it forms a canal, and finally reaches the lateral femoral region. Here, it splits into anterior and posterior branches and provides sensory innervation to the overlying skin [4, 31]. The GF nerve originates from the L1 and L2 nerve roots, penetrates the psoas major muscle, and subsequently bifurcates into its two terminal branches. The genital branch, situated laterally, enters the inguinal canal through the deep inguinal ring and provides innervation to the cremaster muscle and the scrotum. Conversely, the femoral branch, located medially, traverses the lacuna vasorum beneath the inguinal ligament and innervates the skin of the upper anterior thigh [4, 31]. Finally, the femoral nerve is derived from the L1 to L4 nerve roots [4, 31]. The portion of the nerve contributing to the cutaneous innervation of the thigh is contained in the anterior and medial femoral cutaneous nerves (AFC and MFC, respectively) [31].

The cutaneous sensory component of the lumbar plexus, responsible for the innervation of the anterior thigh region, exhibits significant anatomical variability, and several major patterns have been described [4]. The femoral branch of the GF nerve (GF-F) may be missing, in which case its function is replaced either by the anterior femoral cutaneous branch (AFC) of the femoral nerve or a branch originating from the LFC [4, 22]. Alternatively, the LFC nerve may be absent, with the GF-F, AFC or the II nerves assuming its sensory distribution [4, 22]. It may also receive fibers from the AFC nerve [4, 22].

Currently, multiple variations are documented in the literature within these anatomical. Notably, the segmental origin of the LFC nerve is highly variable. Reports indicate that approximately 50% of individuals exhibit variations involving the LFC and GF nerves [2, 4]. Anatomical variations in the level of origin, intrapsoas and retroperitoneal course, and position within the inguinal or proximal thigh region of these nerves hold significant clinical implications for a wide array of surgical procedures [5–7, 18, 25, 36]. Here, we describe a complex anatomical variation affecting several sensory branches of the lumbar plexus, specifically the LFC, GF-F, and AFC nerves. In the course of a routine dissection, we observed the two roots of the LFC nerve emerged separately from the posterior aspect of the psoas major muscle and subsequently fused approximately 10 cm superior to the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS). This was accompanied by the formation of two additional nerves in the retroperitoneal space. Further dissection revealed that these two extra branches substituted for a portion of the GF-F nerve. Dissection of the cutaneous contribution to the thigh revealed that the MF and AF nerves remained intact, while the LFC nerve expanded its innervation territory to encompass a portion of the AF innervation zone. While each of the described variants have been reported separately as a variation with similar morphology in the literature, a comprehensive description of the observed combined, multilevel variation involving the sensory components of the lumbar plexus was not found.

Results

During routine dissection, a complex interconnected system of nerve branches was identified, ultimately forming the LFC nerve, accompanied by two aberrant nerve branches. Two main roots (hereafter referred to as R1 and R2) exited from the posterior aspect of the psoas major muscle and fused approximately at the midpoint of the iliacus muscle (or 10 cm cranial to the ASIS), forming the main trunk of the LFC nerve Fig. 1a. Each of the roots gave rise to separate nerves. The branch emerging from R1 coursed over the aponeurosis of the psoas minor muscle, medial to the femoral branch of the GF (GF-F), then passed beneath the inguinal ligament, entering the lacuna vasorum and terminating subcutaneously within the femoral triangle. This nerve appears to replace a portion of GF-F nerve Fig. 1a, d. The thin branch originating from R2 coursed beneath the aponeurosis of the psoas minor muscle, entered the lacuna musculorum just medial to the iliopectineal arch, penetrated the fascia of the iliopsoas muscle, and reached the subcutaneous tissue within the femoral triangle. Further dissection showed that the terminal branches of R2 terminated in the soft tissue on the medial side of the fascia. Given the presence of a GF nerve with both of its divisions intact, it is highly probable that the R1 and R2-derived branches replace a portion of the sensory component of the GF-F nerve, forming two accessory branches, analogous to the accessory femoral nerve. Subsequently, these branches will be referred to as Accessory 1 and Accessory 2 (A1 and A2, respectively). Continuing this dissection, we traced the GF-G nerve. As expected, it entered the inguinal canal and then joined the ilioinguinal nerve, a variation frequently reported in the literature Fig. 1a, d. We proceeded with the dissection of the LFC nerve. As anticipated, it entered the thigh medial to the ASIS. While the LFC is usually described as bifurcating into an anterior and a posterior trunk, in this case, it branched into three nerves. The posterior trunk divided at a relatively high level into two branches, hereafter referred to as the posterior superior and the posterior inferior branch Fig. 1a. The anterior branch followed its expected course to the lateral inferior thigh region. However, in addition to the innervation of the lateral inferior aspect of the thigh, it also provided sensory innervation to the suprapatellar region Fig. 1b. It is possible that the anterior branch of the LFC nerve had received fibers from the sensory component of the AFC nerve. To further investigate this discrepancy and determine if any of the aberrant branches contributed to the femoral nerve innervation territory, we proceeded with the dissection of the AFC and MFC nerves. Both were identified at their expected anatomical positions Fig. 1c. Notably, the AFC nerve passed posterior to and pierced the sartorius muscle before continuing distally Fig. 1c. It originated from the trunk of the femoral nerve. Discussion

The most likely model of lumbar plexus development and growth is established as a hypothesis by Mirnics and Koerber [21]. A three-dimensional framework of permissive openings which lead into specific permissive pathways or conduits into the developing limb are responsible for the early formation and separate elongation of the main nervous trunks of the plexus. Later development and musculo-cutaneous separation are guided by the limits of local ECM composition (already described effects of ECM glycoprotein expression) and bundling with motor axons for some of the growing axons and target specific guidance cues. At the final stage in the periphery, nerve branching is elevated as the corresponding target dermatomes expand and overlap during the limb morphogenesis. This hypothesis is additionally supported by the fact that although the variations of the lumbar plexus occur quite often, they present only in limited number of discrete variation patterns that rarely overlap. In this setting, a slight variation and disbalance (temporal and spatial) of permissive and restrictive cues may misdirect some of the growing axons down an “unusual” permissive channel causing aberrant branching or/and fiber exchange, which are “corrected” at later stage by the positive guidance produced by the targets.

The developmental origin of sensory nerve variations is rather ambiguous. Most of the data has been derived from chick embryos, and all information discussed in this section will refer to them unless stated otherwise. In the early stages of development, the limb bud is not innervated, and the nerve trunks are positioned at the base of the limb [19]. The skin of the developing limb bud gets innervated by axons of the dorsal root ganglion (DRG), which grow towards their respective dermatomes [28]. Even during these development, DRG axons invade the limb bud and innervate the dermatomes in a targeted and specific manner. There is no overlap in the innervated zones, nor is there initial axonal overgrowth followed by pruning [28]. When the limb bud of embryos is shifted by two to three segments, DRG are rewired to target new dermatomes while projecting to their corresponding spinal cord segment (new DRG and new spinal cord segments take up the innervation of the mispositioned limb bud) [33]. Regardless, the dermatomes are still quite similar to the normal embryos, but the dermatome regions were significantly enlarged [33]. At the same time, more extreme limb shifts prevent some nerves from forming altogether [14]. When removing DRG, the existing ones extend their zone of innervation, but can never cover the entire denervated region of the limb [29]. In short, it seems that DRGs and spinal cord nerves exhibit a high degree of positional plasticity, but up to a certain point. This might explain why we observe different versions of lumbar plexus formation (prefixed, postfixed, and normal), but it is lacking when it comes to variations in the branching pattern of peripheral nerves. Further information may be gathered by taking a closer look at the molecular guides that govern nerve extension and branching. In the plexi, motor neuron growth cones have many more lamellipodia than normal growth cones. This observation is consistent with axons growing in a decision-rich environment. Interestingly, sensory axons did not have the excessive lamellipodia morphology, suggesting that they may rely on motor neuron fibers for choosing the correct path [32]. Contrary to the previous report, even in the absence of motor neurons (achieved by ablating the motor part of the neural tube/DRG) cutaneous axons grew towards the skin was present and dermatomes formed relatively accurately. It is noteworthy that although dermatome regions were largely present, some borders were a little blurred in the absence of motor nerve fibers [27]. Although cutaneous nerves exhibit rather independent growth, there is some level of interplay between motor and cutaneous nerve fibers: either molecular guidance or competitive growth, which remains to be explored [27]. Conversely, guidance from the ectoderm seems to be crucial for nerve formation. Removing the region of ectoderm innervated by the LFC nerves prevents its formation. LFC nerve axons do not leave the plexus properly or are found in the trunks of other nerves, or undergo cell death [13]. Some molecules act as chemorepellents or chemoattractants, directing the axons to grow in a specific direction. Other molecules do not guide the axons but establish the already existing paths and compact them along the way. For example, the MFC and LFC have regions of high and weak tenascin expression [19]. Tenascins follow the extending nerves, ensheathing them in a sleeve of tenascin-positive regions. Tenascin expression was weak or lacking in the areas where nerve arborisations started. According to the authors, it seems that tenascin does not guide or precede nerve formation but follows it. It helps to keep all nerve fibers together by forming a “path” for axons that invade the limb at different times points and keeping them together in the confines of one nerve trunk. Conversely, in areas `where this sleeve is discontinued (a.k.a. tenascin expression is low), nerve arborisation occurs [19]. Peripheral nerve development and regeneration are also influenced by growth factors such as NGF and BDNF, which are of particular interest due to white adipose tissue’s ability to secrete them in an endocrine and paracrine fashion [24, 35].

Based on literature reports, variations of the lumbar plexus nerves can be classified in three clinically relevant groups: 1. Variations in the segmental origin [3, 4, 22], 2. Variations in the intra-abdominal branching pattern [5, 22, 22, 26, 34], and 3. Variations of the nerves distal to the ilioinguinal ligament [4, 5, 10, 12, 17, 25].

The origin of the cutaneous nerves of the plexus can also vary. The GF nerve typically starts from L1 and L2 but can rarely receive nerve fibres from both Th12 and L3 [3, 4]. The LFC often arises solely from L2, L3 or both L2 and L3 and it can receive contributions from one of the adjacent L1 or L4 segments as well [4, 22, 30, 34].

The deviations from the typical intra-abdominal anatomy of the IH, II, GF, LFC and Femoral nerves are quite frequent: the nerves may anastomose, completely replace each other or exhibit anomalous branching/fusion patterns. Here, we discuss only the data relevant to our report. Generally, there are eight patterns to the formation of the lumbar plexus in the abdominal cavity [34]. The intra-abdominal region of the LFC nerve has been reported to show early bifurcation [5, 22]. It is possible for the LFC to arise from the femoral nerve, communicate with it or an accessory LFC nerve may arise from the Femoral nerve [22]. Anastomosis may form between the LFC and GF or AFC [22, 22]. LFC may also replace the CF-F [4, 11] or it might be completely missing, replaced by AFC, II, or the GF-F [4]. The GF-F and GF-G can emerge in one trunk, or as two separate nerves [4]. They can also can branch before reaching the upper thigh region, just several centimetres before the inguinal ligament [15].

Lastly, distally of the inguinal ligament, the nerve forms a canal at a variable location – up to 7.3 cm medial to the ASIS. [10, 12, 25]. In some cases, it can pass lateral or over to ASIS, or form a canal through the iliac bone itself [5, 17]. After the exit point, it usually divides into two at variable distance from the ASIS, forming posterior and anterior trunk [10, 31]. The posterior trunk of LCF innervates the upper half of the lateral thigh, starting at the level of the major trochanter and reaching the midpoint, while the anterior trunk descends as far as the knee [10, 31]. When the GF-F is replaced by the LFC it manifests an atypical anatomical position in the upper thigh [11].

We did not find any sign of prior surgical interventions, nor any malformations of the regional muscles. In our case, the segmental contribution the LFC seems to follow the classical L2—L3 pattern, but the intraabdominal course of the nerve was atypical. The L2 and L3 ventral branches of the spinal nerves (referred in this work as roots – R1 and R2) emerged separately and fused several centimetres proximal to the ASIS, forming the LCF nerve. Furthermore, each of the roots had one aberrant early, intra-abdominal branch (A1 and A2). Lastly, in the thigh region, A1 and A2 passed under the inguinal ligament and eventually reached the lacuna musculorum. Most likely these are fibres that belong to the GF or Femoral nerves, that emerged as separate branches due to aberrant trajectory of the ventral branches of L2 – L3 (R1, R2). The LFC exits its canal as expected, medial to the SAIS, and splits into its terminals. The anterior LFC branch reached the distalmost lateral skin of the thigh and the suprapatellar region. The AFC and MFC nerves were also visible. This report bears some similarities with a variation reported by Webber in figures 1 through 8 of his article, where part of the LFC nerve arises together with the accessory femoral cutaneous nerve, and they share some communicating branches [34] and with Erbil et al. who describe a “double” LFC nerve with normal distribution over the skin of the thigh [20]. Unlike the case reported by Erbil et al., the roots (R1 and R2) forming the LFC nerve in our case carry over some fibres from the GF-F and possibly from the femoral. Most of these fibres diverge from the R1 and R2 roots, forming two separate nerves in the abdominal cavity, with separate trajectories in the anterior thigh region. Furthermore, some of the nerve fibres normally part of the AFC nerve joins the LFC nerve, changing its target dermatomes. This is summarized in Fig. 3, according to Gray’s Anatomy Fig. 78.12 describing the distribution of the cutaneous nerves of the lower limb. [31]. Since Webber did not report on the anatomy below the inguinal ligament, we can’t comment on this [34]. The distal formation reported by Webber could be due to the presence of an accessory femoral cutaneous nerve, allowing for some molecular interferences during the development, but that is not seen in our case. A limitation of this report is that we could not physically trace the GF-F branch in the medial thigh. However, its position in the abdominal cavity, as well as the fact that we could visualise the GF-I branch, the AFC, MFC and LFC leaves little doubt about its identity. Our inability to locate it in the dissections may be attributed to some deviation in its typical anatomic position due to the presence of the two accessory branches. Although early splitting of the GF-F and GF-I is known, it usually occurs several centimetres cranial to the inguinal ligament [15]. To our knowledge, formation of completely separate GF-F nerves has not been described so far, therefore, we suggest that we can identify A1 and A2 as accessory GF-F nerves.

The observed entrapment of the AFC nerve by the sartorius muscle is, to our knowledge, also a new finding. Previous studies have shown that although the sartorius muscle is innervated by the femoral nerve, variations do exist [16]. In our case, the normal innervation of the sartorius muscle by femoral nerve fibers is preserved, but the fibers travel together with the AFC nerve for a short while before entering the muscle.

The clinical significance of the described variation lies not in its existence per se but as an illustrative example. First of all, such an unusual course and pattern of LFC nerve formation and accompanying fiber exchange make the nerve more vulnerable to damage during certain surgical approaches. For example, with the observed variation, in an ilioinguinal approach to the acetabulum, only the superior root R1, together with the trunk of the LFC nerve, will be mobilized prior to the development of the surgical windows, thus leaving the inferior root R2 and the aberrant branches A1 and A2 particularly vulnerable [8]. Additionally, the accessory GF-F branches we visualized can be damaged during interventions in the femoral triangle for femoral vein access [6] or during retroperitoneal surgical interventions [1, 6, 23]. Chronic GF neuralgia is reported after clipping of the GF nerve during both nephrectomy and appendectomy [1, 23]. Finally, the entrapment of the AFC nerve can play an important role during the mobilisation of a sartorius muscle flap [9]. Beyond this increased vulnerability, variation patterns similar to the described that include unusual fibre course and exchange between the main branches of the lumbar plexus may lead to very unusual symptomatic presentations of the various entrapment syndromes of the frontal hip and thigh, leading to delayed (sometimes for years) and misdiagnosis. This is especially consequential given that the iliac fossa and adjacent regions are related to a multitude of space-occupying lesions (tumours, hematomas, herniations, aneurisms etc.)
