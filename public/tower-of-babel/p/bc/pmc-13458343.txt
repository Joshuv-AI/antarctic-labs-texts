A global helium clock for groundwater residence time
Authors: Takuya Matsumoto, Daniele Luigi Pinti, Wei Jiang, Zheng-Tian Lu, Guo-Min Yang, Peter Mueller, Rachid Abdelouahab, Adnane Souffi Moulla, Darren Hillegonds, Jennifer Mabry, Nicolo Romeo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Radiogenic helium (⁴He) has long been recognised as a potential indicator of groundwater residence time, but its quantitative application has remained largely site-dependent because helium concentrations integrate production, crustal fluxes, transport and mixing processes. In contrast, krypton-81 (⁸¹Kr) provides a conservative chronometer for old groundwater, yet paired ⁴He–⁸¹Kr datasets have remained extremely scarce. Here we compile a uniquely expanded global dataset of groundwater samples with paired dissolved radiogenic ⁴He concentrations and independently determined ⁸¹Kr residence times across diverse aquifer systems. Dissolved ⁴He increases monotonically with groundwater age over nearly three orders of magnitude and follows a simple empirical scaling for waters older than ~ 50 kyr that is robust to the exclusion of individual basins. More than 80% of helium-derived ages agree with corresponding ⁸¹Kr residence times within a factor of three under conservative cross-basin validation. These results demonstrate that radiogenic helium, when anchored to an absolute chronometer, can serve as a transferable first-order proxy for groundwater residence time, providing a practical basis for global groundwater age screening and targeted application of high-precision dating methods.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53445-z.

Introduction

Constraining groundwater residence time over geological timescales is fundamental to understanding aquifer sustainability, continental water cycling, and long-term water–rock interaction. Global syntheses of groundwater age indicators demonstrate that groundwater stored in the upper crust commonly contains a substantial fraction of fossil water recharged under past climatic conditions, often dominating storage at depths exceeding a few hundred metres in major aquifer systems worldwide1–3. Such systems integrate climatic, tectonic, and geochemical signals over timescales far exceeding those accessible through direct observation, rendering groundwater residence time a key state variable controlling aquifer renewal, vulnerability to depletion, and long-term water–rock interaction4,5.

Importantly, fossil groundwater does not imply complete hydrological isolation. Combined radiocarbon and tritium analyses show that deep wells dominated by old groundwater frequently contain measurable fractions of young recharge, indicating widespread age mixing through dispersion, convergent flow paths, or well-induced processes2,6,7. Thus, “old” groundwater commonly represents a mixture or residence-time distribution rather than a single isolated water parcel. Nevertheless, where the dominant residence times are tens of thousands to millions of years, replenishment occurs far more slowly than human extraction timescales. From a resource-management perspective, extraction from such aquifers therefore constitutes exploitation of an effectively non-renewable resource on human timescales, necessitating management frameworks that explicitly acknowledge long renewal periods and distinguish fossil groundwater reservoirs from shallow, rapidly renewed systems8–11.

For groundwater residence times exceeding several tens of thousands of years, krypton-81 (⁸¹Kr) provides a uniquely robust and conservative chronometer. Krypton-81 is cosmogenic, chemically inert, and in most groundwater settings subsurface production or loss is negligible relative to the atmospheric ⁸¹Kr inherited at recharge, enabling direct age determination with minimal reliance on site-specific geochemical corrections (e.g., 12). However, detectable subsurface production of ⁸¹Kr may occur in specific geological settings, particularly in uranium-rich fractured rocks where fissiogenic ⁸¹Kr production can become significant, and such cases require independent evaluation before ⁸¹Kr ages are interpreted as conservative residence-time constraints12,13. Early applications using accelerator mass spectrometry or resonance ionization spectroscopy demonstrated feasibility but were limited by isobaric interferences and low throughput14,15. The development of atom trap trace analysis (ATTA) represented a decisive technological advance, enabling isotope-selective counting of individual krypton atoms via resonance fluorescence and transforming ⁸¹Kr dating into a practical tool for groundwater age determination16,17. Subsequent applications established ⁸¹Kr as a reference chronometer for old groundwater in large aquifer systems worldwide18–20.

Radiogenic helium-4 (⁴He) provides a complementary perspective on groundwater residence time by recording the integrated effects of in situ production, crustal fluxes, and long-term transport processes21,22. Numerous site-specific studies report correlations between dissolved ⁴He concentrations and independently constrained groundwater ages, but quantitative interpretation has typically relied on detailed transport models accounting for aquifer geometry, porosity, and basal helium fluxes23–26. Correlations between radiocarbon (¹⁴C) activity or ¹⁴C-based ages and radiogenic ⁴He have also been reported at the basin scale (e.g., 27); however, ¹⁴C-based ages typically require aquifer-specific geochemical correction models and therefore do not provide a globally transferable chronological anchor. Consequently, ⁴He has long been regarded as inherently site-dependent and unsuitable as a general-purpose chronometer28,29.

A central question is therefore whether radiogenic helium—an integrator of production, external fluxes, transport, and mixing—can be rendered into a transferable chronometer when anchored to an independent, conservative age scale. Krypton-81 provides such a reference for old groundwater, defining a well-constrained temporal framework over 10⁵–10⁶ years, yet it remains unclear whether a coherent ⁴He–⁸¹Kr relationship emerges across aquifers with contrasting geometries, flux regimes, and mixing histories, or whether helium remains irreducibly site-dependent.

This question is now testable. A series of coordinated IAEA-supported projects completed by 2024 has generated a uniquely consistent compilation of paired ⁸¹Kr-derived groundwater ages and dissolved helium measurements across a broad suite of regional aquifers25,26,30–34(Methods). By strategically targeting diverse hydrogeological settings and prioritising internally consistent noble gas analyses, this coordinated effort enables, for the first time, a systematic evaluation of helium accumulation against an absolute groundwater chronometer at the global scale—an analysis that would not be feasible based on isolated case studies alone. We next outline the framework used to constrain helium sources and mixing, before evaluating whether a global empirical relationship between dissolved ⁴He and ⁸¹Kr groundwater residence time can be established.

Helium provenance and mixing

The provenance of terrigenic helium in groundwater can be assessed using three-isotope relationships combining ³He/⁴He and Ne/He ratios, which resolve mixing among atmospheric helium acquired at recharge, radiogenic helium produced in the continental crust, and mantle-derived helium35. Because mantle helium exhibits ³He/⁴He ratios approximately three orders of magnitude higher than radiogenic crustal values, even minor mantle contributions can substantially elevate ³He/⁴He. In contrast, such isotopic shifts exert little influence on the total dissolved terrigenic ⁴He inventory, which is controlled primarily by the dominant mass contribution of crustal radiogenic helium.

As shown in Fig. 1, groundwater samples define coherent trends consistent with three-component mixing. In many systems, samples plot close to the air–crust mixing trajectory, reflecting progressive accumulation of radiogenic ⁴He with increasing residence time and a concomitant decrease in Ne/He as the atmospheric component is diluted. Quantitatively, most samples fall within fields where more than 90–100% of terrigenic helium is derived from the continental crust (fcrust ≳ 0.9), demonstrating that crustal production overwhelmingly dominates the ⁴He mass balance.

Systematic departures from the air–crust mixing line are nevertheless observed, requiring the involvement of mantle-derived helium. Such signatures are common in tectonically active regions26,36, including subduction and extensional settings, where deep pathways facilitate the upward transport of mantle volatiles, as illustrated by groundwater from Thailand. Similar isotopic evidence is also observed in continental interior settings characterized by deep fault systems, such as China and Hungary, indicating that mantle helium is not restricted to plate boundary environments37,38.

Crucially for groundwater age applications, however, the influence of mantle helium on dissolved ⁴He concentrations remains limited. For the He–⁸¹Kr comparison, total terrigenic ⁴He was defined as the measured dissolved ⁴He concentration minus the atmospheric solubility-derived ⁴He component, approximated here as 4.5 × 10⁻⁸ cm³ STP g⁻¹ assuming air–water equilibrium at 15 °C. In the ⁸¹Kr dating range considered here, total dissolved ⁴He generally exceeds 10⁻⁶ cm³ STP g⁻¹, so this atmospheric correction is small. Even where mantle mixing is isotopically evident, mass-balance considerations indicate that more than 90–95% of total dissolved ⁴He is typically of crustal radiogenic origin. Accordingly, total terrigenic ⁴He can be used as a practical approximation of crustal radiogenic ⁴He for the samples considered here.

A global empirical relationship between helium and groundwater age

To evaluate whether helium accumulation defines a transferable age relationship across aquifer systems, dissolved radiogenic ⁴He concentrations were plotted directly against independently determined ⁸¹Kr groundwater residence times. The comparison was restricted to samples older than ~ 50 kyr, a threshold consistent with the lower end of the practical ⁸¹Kr dating range and with the age range in which radiogenic ⁴He accumulation becomes a dominant component of the dissolved helium inventory. Across this compiled global dataset, samples from diverse aquifer systems define a coherent, monotonic trend spanning nearly three orders of magnitude in age (Fig. 2). At the scale of individual case studies—where geological setting, flow architecture, and helium inputs are more tightly constrained—relationships between ⁴He and groundwater age can be markedly tighter, while a limited number of samples show clear departures from the dominant trend. Viewed in this context, the persistence of a well-defined global relationship despite pronounced inter-basin variability is itself a key result.

Despite substantial scatter at the regional scale, this formulation captures the central tendency of the global dataset without any region-specific tuning or basin-dependent parameterisation. The scaling exponent, estimated via non-parametric bootstrap resampling (n = 5000), yielded a median value of 0.220 (95% CI: 0.142–0.295). This result confirms a robust sub-linear relationship while allowing for inherent inter-basin variability. The sub-linear exponent (0.22) should be interpreted as an empirical Age–He mapping derived from the pooled global dataset, rather than as the temporal evolution of dissolved ⁴He within individual aquifer systems. Such behaviour should not be interpreted as evidence against simple closed-system accumulation at the aquifer scale—within individual aquifers, dissolved ⁴He concentrations typically increase approximately linearly with residence time—but rather reflects the aggregation of systems with different radiogenic production rates and external crustal helium fluxes, a pattern that is broadly consistent with long-standing conceptual models in which radiogenic production and external crustal helium fluxes are progressively moderated by transport, dispersion, and mixing processes operating over geological timescales21,22,24,28. That these competing processes combine to yield a transferable empirical scaling across aquifer lithologies, tectonic settings, and climatic regimes suggests that residence time exerts the dominant first-order control on the global Age–He relationship.

Transferability and systematic deviations relative to ⁸¹Kr ages

The predictive performance and transferability of the global helium–age relationship are evaluated by comparing helium-derived ages directly with measured ⁸¹Kr residence times. Across the full age range considered (> 50 kyr), helium-based estimates typically reproduce ⁸¹Kr ages to within a factor of two to three (Fig. 2). Given the simplicity of the empirical formulation and the absence of site-specific calibration or explicit transport modelling, this level of agreement is notable and comparable to that achieved by physically based helium transport models calibrated at individual sites23,25,26.

The robustness of this behaviour is further demonstrated by leave-one-basin-out (LOBO) analysis, in which each basin is withheld in turn from the regression and predicted using a scaling derived from the remaining basins (Fig. 3). The resulting residuals show that the global helium–age relationship retains useful predictive skill when transferred across basins, confirming that the fitted scaling is not driven by any single aquifer system. Across basins, predicted ages generally fall within a factor-of-two envelope, while the distribution of residuals remains centred close to zero for the global fit.

Importantly, the LOBO residuals are not random but exhibit systematic basin-level offsets (Fig. 3). While individual case studies may display tighter helium–age relationships over restricted age ranges, the residuals in the global analysis do not show a simple monotonic dependence on residence time. Instead, differences between basins dominate the structure of the residuals, indicating persistent basin-level controls beyond residence time alone. The reproducibility of these offsets across leave-one-basin-out tests suggests that they reflect enduring characteristics of individual groundwater systems rather than analytical artefacts or overfitting of the global regression.

Even under this conservative cross-basin validation, the global scaling retains substantial practical value: 85% of helium-derived ages fall within a factor of three of the corresponding ⁸¹Kr residence times (Fig. 3). This level of accuracy is achieved without site-specific transport modelling and using only dissolved radiogenic ⁴He, a tracer that is analytically accessible and increasingly amenable to rapid assessment. Recent developments in on-site dissolved gas analysis, including membrane-based extraction coupled to mass spectrometric detection, have demonstrated that helium and other noble gases can be measured at groundwater sampling points, with analyses performed on gas samples directly extracted by membrane contactors from flowing groundwater36,39. When combined with the global helium–age scaling established here, such measurements enable immediate, on-site estimation of groundwater residence times, allowing first-order age information to be obtained contemporaneously with sampling rather than after laboratory-based analysis. Importantly, by measuring atmospheric noble gases such as Ne or Ar together with helium, field-based analyses can also identify degassing in real time and provide a more reliable basis for on-site residence-time estimation. This capability opens the possibility of adaptive, age-informed field sampling strategies, in which decisions on where to collect samples for high-precision dating are guided in real time by helium-based age estimates.

Tracer selection enabled by helium as an operational gatekeeper

The global helium–age scaling established above not only enables first-order age estimation but also provides an operational framework for guiding tracer selection during field investigations. This concept is illustrated schematically in Fig. 4, which summarises how (on-site or conventional) helium measurements can be used to triage groundwater samples and prioritise complementary dating approaches across age ranges.

At very low helium concentrations (< 10⁻⁷ cm³ STP g⁻¹), corresponding to groundwater younger than several decades to centuries, helium accumulation is intrinsically insensitive. In this regime, age interpretation should rely on tracers with transient atmospheric input functions—such as tritium (³H), chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), SF₆, and ⁸⁵Kr—as well as decay-based approaches such as the ³H–³He method, which are well suited to resolving young groundwater residence times.

At intermediate helium concentrations (10⁻⁷–10⁻⁶ cm³ STP g⁻¹), characteristic of residence times spanning decades to several millennia, longer-lived chronometers such as ³⁹Ar and radiocarbon (¹⁴C) become appropriate tools for groundwater age determination. Conversely, elevated radiogenic helium concentrations (> 10⁻⁶ cm³ STP g⁻¹) identify groundwater systems with residence times extending into the late Pleistocene and beyond, for which long-lived noble gas and radionuclide chronometers such as ⁸¹Kr and ³⁶Cl are required. At the longest timescales, accumulated ⁴He itself remains an informative integrative tracer of groundwater residence.

Framed in this way, helium does not compete with established dating methods but organises them, acting as an operational gatekeeper that links rapid field observations to an optimal tracer strategy. This tiered, helium-guided framework formalises tracer applicability across age ranges into a transferable decision scheme, consistent with recent synthesis efforts in noble gas groundwater dating methodologies34.

Helium accumulation, basal flux, and effective residence time

Radiogenic ⁴He accumulation in groundwater reflects a balance between in situ crustal production, externally supplied crustal helium fluxes, and transport processes that redistribute or remove helium over time. In this framework, basin-scale differences in helium accumulation behaviour arise from variations in flux magnitude, flow architecture, aquifer extent and mixing efficiency, which together define an effective residence time that governs how efficiently helium inputs are retained along groundwater flow paths21,24. These processes do not invalidate the global helium–age scaling, but instead generate systematic basin-level offsets around the first-order trend, consistent with the reproducible residual structure observed in the LOBO analysis (Fig. 3).

Basin-scale studies have long demonstrated that crustal helium fluxes can vary substantially in space and magnitude, leading to systematic differences in apparent helium accumulation rates among aquifer systems (e.g., 25,26,40,41). Such variability reflects contrasts in crustal production rates, permeability structure, and the presence of deep pathways that facilitate upward helium transport, all of which influence the net helium input available for accumulation along groundwater flow paths.

Concrete physically based examples illustrate how these factors regulate helium retention over geological timescales. Cheng et al.42 developed a one-dimensional diffusion–production model for radiogenic ⁴He in a large sedimentary basin, explicitly accounting for basal crustal helium fluxes, in situ production, vertical diffusion, and helium loss toward the surface. By reproducing observed depth-dependent ⁴He distributions, their analysis demonstrated that diffusive loss and transport progressively limit helium retention within individual basins, causing accumulation to depart from simple linear growth within individual systems.

In this context, apparent helium accumulation behaviour therefore depends not only on groundwater residence time, but also on the efficiency with which helium inputs are retained, an effect commonly conceptualised in terms of an effective residence time that integrates production, external fluxes, mixing, and loss processes21,24,42. Within this perspective, deviations from the global helium–age scaling do not indicate a failure of the relationship. Instead, they reflect basin-specific differences in effective helium accumulation behaviour arising from variations in flux magnitude, flow architecture, and mixing efficiency, which manifest directly as systematic offsets in helium-derived age estimates relative to ⁸¹Kr ages, as revealed by the leave-one-basin-out residuals (Fig. 3).

Over geological timescales, diffusion, leakage, and large-scale mixing progressively limit helium retention and shape basin-specific He–age behaviour; when data are pooled across basins, these persistent differences yield an emergent first-order global Age–He relationship rather than a universal within-basin accumulation law28. Within this framework, the persistence of reproducible basin-level residuals is therefore expected and reflects enduring hydrogeological characteristics of individual systems rather than analytical uncertainty.

Outlook: from site-specific modelling to global screening

Groundwater age assessment has traditionally progressed through detailed, site-specific modelling efforts that seek to reconcile tracer behaviour with local hydrogeological complexity. While such approaches remain indispensable at the basin scale, the results presented here suggest that a complementary paradigm is now feasible at the global scale. The emergence of a transferable helium–age relationship indicates that first-order residence-time information can be obtained through systematic screening, even in the absence of site-specific calibration.

This perspective reframes the role of radiogenic helium in groundwater studies. Rather than serving solely as an input to mechanistic models, helium can function as a comparative metric that positions groundwater systems consistently within a global age framework. In doing so, it becomes possible to prioritise where detailed modelling and high-precision chronometers are most warranted, and where simpler age constraints are sufficient.

Recent continental-scale analyses further underscore this need2,43, showing that deep wells capable of accessing fossil groundwater are widespread and increasingly common, while groundwater depth alone remains an imperfect proxy for residence time. This disconnect between accessibility and age highlights the value of approaches that provide rapid, transferable age information without requiring detailed site-specific calibration.

As pressures on groundwater resources intensify worldwide, the ability to place groundwater systems rapidly and consistently within a common age context becomes increasingly important. Approaches that combine analytical accessibility with predictive power—such as the helium-guided framework developed here—offer a practical route for assessing groundwater renewal, vulnerability, and long-term sustainability at scales relevant to global water management.

Methods

Data set overview

This study compiles groundwater noble-gas data from major aquifer systems spanning Africa, South America, Europe, Asia, and Australia. With the exception of the Australian and Baltic datasets, all data were generated within the framework of IAEA-Coordinated Research Projects conducted in collaboration with national counterpart institutions. The compiled dataset encompasses a broad range of hydrogeological settings, including regional sedimentary basins, fossil aquifers, and mixed-flow systems.

For all IAEA-coordinated case studies, dissolved helium concentrations and stable noble-gas isotope ratios (including ³He/⁴He and Ne/He) were analysed at the Isotope Hydrology Laboratory (IHL) of the International Atomic Energy Agency using harmonised sampling, gas-extraction, and analytical protocols48. IHL also assisted gas sampling and krypton purification for projects in Algeria, China, Hungary and Thailand49. Krypton-81 measurements were performed independently using atom trap trace analysis (ATTA) at dedicated facilities at Argonne National Laboratory (USA) and the University of Science and Technology of China (USTC).

To extend the geographical coverage and age range of the global analysis, additional datasets were compiled from previously published studies. These include groundwater data from the Great Artesian Basin (Australia) and from the Baltic Artesian Basin (Europe), Cambrian Aquifer System, the latter representing a brine-influenced endmember with exceptionally high dissolved helium concentrations. For these published datasets, dissolved ⁴He concentrations and ⁸¹Kr-derived groundwater residence times were taken directly from the original sources.

Only samples for which paired measurements of dissolved radiogenic ⁴He and ⁸¹Kr-derived groundwater residence times were available were included in the analysis. An overview of the study areas and primary data sources is provided in Table 1. Information about IAEA’s unpublished datasets is documented in Supplementary Information.

Degassing was evaluated using atmospheric noble gases rather than helium alone. Samples showing substantial depletion of atmospheric noble gases relative to air–water solubility equilibrium were excluded from the He–⁸¹Kr calibration dataset where such depletion indicated gas loss that could have reduced the dissolved radiogenic ⁴He inventory.
