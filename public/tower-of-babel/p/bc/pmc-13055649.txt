Integration of Conventional and Radiomic Features From Fluorine‐18 Fluorodeoxyglucose Positron Emission Tomography/Magnetic Resonance Imaging for Multimodal Prediction of Symptomatic Carotid Atherosclerotic Plaques
Authors: Qian Li, Fan Fu, Jie Chen, Yu Zhang, Hairong Zheng, Dong Liang, Xin Liu, Zhanli Hu, Biao Li, Na Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aim of this study was to evaluate an integrated fluorine‐18 fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/magnetic resonance imaging (PET/MRI) model combining conventional and radiomic features for the noninvasive identification of symptomatic extracranial carotid atherosclerotic plaques.MethodsA total of 200 patients with advanced carotid plaques (78 symptomatic, 122 asymptomatic) underwent hybrid fluorine‐18 fluorodeoxyglucose PET/MRI. Six MRI morphological features (intraplaque hemorrhage, lipid‐rich necrotic core, ruptured fibrous cap, calcification, surface ulceration, and plaque enhancement) and 3 PET metabolic parameters—metabolic uptake, maximum standardized uptake value, and target‐to‐background ratio—were evaluated. A total of 1991 radiomics were extracted from each of the MRI and PET images, respectively. For each modality, 3 types of models were developed: conventional, radiomics, and combined (incorporating conventional and radiomic features). Diagnostic performance was evaluated using multivariate logistic regression and a linear support vector classifier.ResultsIn MRI‐based analysis, surface ulceration and plaque enhancement emerged as independent predictors of symptomatic plaques. In PET‐based analysis, standardized uptake value and metabolic uptake were identified as significant independent predictors. The PET conventional model outperformed the MRI conventional model in discriminative ability (areas under the curve: training, 0.899 versus 0.755; internal test, 0.926 versus 0.822; temporal validation, 0.878 versus 0.702). The integrated PET/MRI combined model achieved the best performance, with areas under the curve of 0.962 (training), 0.967 (internal test), and 0.926 (temporal validation), significantly outperforming PET‐ and MRI‐based combined models (all P<0.05).ConclusionsThe fluorine‐18 fluorodeoxyglucose PET/MRI combined model integrating morphological, metabolic, and radiomic features outperformed other models, supporting its potential as a noninvasive, high‐precision tool for cerebrovascular risk stratification.

Stroke remains one of the leading causes of mortality and long‐term disability worldwide. 1 Carotid atherosclerosis (specifically in the extracranial segment) is responsible for ∼15% to 20% of ischemic strokes, making early identification and risk stratification of vulnerable plaques a clinical priority. 2 , 3 The primary pathophysiological mechanism underlying carotid‐related stroke is plaque rupture, typically triggered by a combination of inflammatory activity, fibrous cap (FC) thinning, and necrotic core expansion, which collectively lead to local thrombus formation or distal embolization. 4

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is currently the most promising imaging modality capable of high‐resolution visualization of carotid atherosclerotic plaques with superior soft‐tissue contrast. 5 , 6 By employing specialized vessel wall imaging techniques, MRI effectively suppresses signals from intraluminal blood flow as well as surrounding cerebrospinal fluid and adipose tissue, thereby enabling detailed delineation of the vascular wall and plaque morphology. 7 Compared with ultrasound and computed tomography, MRI provides not only quantitative assessment of carotid plaques but also reliable identification of high‐risk features associated with plaque vulnerability, including FC integrity, intraplaque hemorrhage (IPH), and calcification patterns. 8 , 9 Despite these advantages, MRI remains primarily an anatomical tool and is limited in its ability to capture biological activity within the plaque.

Inflammation plays a pivotal role in plaque destabilization and rupture. 10 , 11 In contrast to structural imaging, positron emission tomography (PET) offers molecular‐level insights by detecting in vivo metabolic activity that often precedes morphological changes. 12 , 13 Among currently available PET tracers, fluorine‐18 fluorodeoxyglucose ([18F]FDG) is the most widely used and validated for imaging vascular inflammation. 14 , 15 [18F]FDG uptake correlates with macrophage infiltration—an established marker of plaque vulnerability—and has shown predictive value for future cardiovascular events in both primary and secondary prevention cohorts. 16 , 17

The advent of hybrid PET/MRI systems enables the simultaneous acquisition of metabolic and anatomical data, combining the molecular sensitivity of PET with the spatial resolution and tissue contrast of MRI. 18 , 19 This integrated approach has shown promise in improving diagnostic accuracy and providing a more comprehensive evaluation of plaque biology. However, existing studies remain limited in scope. Most have focused either on basic PET metabolic indices, such as maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) and target‐to‐background ratio (TBR), or on MRI radiomics to quantify plaque morphology. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 Some studies have attempted to combine MRI‐derived anatomical features with PET‐derived metabolic data. For instance, Yu et al. 20 demonstrated that integrated PET/MRI could simultaneously evaluate the morphological components and inflammatory features of advanced atherosclerotic plaques. This overlooks the significant potential of PET‐based radiomics, which extracts high‐dimensional spatial and textural patterns that reflect underlying biological heterogeneity. 24 Although the spatial resolution of PET is lower than that of MRI, the strength of PET radiomics lies not in resolving fine anatomical structures but in its unique ability to quantify functional and metabolic heterogeneity.

Radiomics has emerged as a powerful tool for noninvasive tissue phenotyping, providing quantitative insights beyond what conventional imaging parameters can reveal. 25 In the context of carotid atherosclerosis, most existing studies have focused on MRI‐based radiomics. For example, Shi et al. 21 demonstrated that radiomic features extracted from T1‐weighted images were associated with acute or subacute symptoms, and Zhang et al. 23 reported that an MRI radiomics model could accurately differentiate symptomatic from asymptomatic carotid plaques, outperforming traditional imaging metrics in identifying high‐risk lesions. However, to date, radiomic analysis of carotid plaques using PET imaging has not been systematically explored. In oncology, research has demonstrated that PET‐based radiomics is both valid and clinically meaningful. 26 Studies by Collarino et al. 27 showed that radiomic features derived from [18F]FDG PET could effectively characterize tumor heterogeneity, even in small lesions, and serve as independent prognostic markers. Despite these advances, no current framework systematically integrates MRI‐derived conventional features, PET metabolic parameters, and PET/MRI radiomics into a unified model for multimodal prediction of symptomatic carotid atherosclerotic plaques. This lack of holistic integration limits the diagnostic and prognostic potential of hybrid PET/MRI in vascular imaging.

In this study, we exploited the methodological opportunity of [18F]FDG PET/MRI to simultaneously assess the morphological and metabolic characteristics of advanced carotid atherosclerotic plaques. By incorporating both conventional and radiomics‐based parameters, we aimed to enhance the noninvasive identification of high‐risk carotid atherosclerotic plaques. This approach takes advantage of the spatiotemporal alignment inherent in hybrid PET/MR imaging, with the goal of improving early detection of plaque vulnerability and enabling more accurate, personalized cerebrovascular risk stratification.

METHODS

The data sets generated or analyzed during the current study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request. Relevant code is available upon reasonable request to the corresponding author.

Patients

This study enrolled patients with suspected cerebrovascular disease (or cervical carotid stenosis) who underwent carotid [18F]FDG PET/MRI between September 2022 and June 2025. To address the need for rigorous independent validation, data collection was strategically divided into 2 sequential phases. Patients enrolled from the initial phase (June 2024–January 2025) constituted the development cohort. To robustly assess generalizability, a prospective temporal validation cohort of consecutive patients from the later phase (February 2025–June 2025) was established. This cohort was reserved as a completely unseen data set to independently test the model's performance, simulating its application to future clinical data. The study design followed Figure 1.

All investigations were performed in accordance with the approved guidelines, including anonymization of data during analysis. Patient inclusion criteria were (1) ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack within 6 months in the carotid artery territory for the symptomatic group, with the event confirmed to be attributable to the carotid plaque (eg, via imaging evidence of plaque rupture, embolic signs, or luminal obstruction corresponding to the ischemic territory); (2) single carotid plaque with associated stenosis confirmed by MR angiography (ie, only 1 plaque present in the unilateral carotid artery); and (3) ≥1 atherosclerotic risk‐factors, including hypertension, diabetes, hypercholesterolemia, or cigarette smoking. Exclusion criteria were (1) nonatherosclerotic intracranial arterial disease, including vasculitis, moyamoya disease, dissection, reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome and intracranial dolichoectasia; (2) chronic ischemic stroke (>6 months) or lacunar strokes; (3) significant stenosis of the intracranial vertebral artery (>30%) as assessed by ultrasound; (4) ascending aortic arch atheroma; (5) suspected cardioembolic stroke; (6) known coagulopathy; and (7) presence of bilateral carotid plaques or significant stenosis (>30%) in the contralateral carotid artery.

Ethical Approval

This cross‐sectional study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiaotong University School of Medicine, in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (Approval Number: IRB2024528). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before their enrollment.

Acquisition Protocols With the Combined PET/MRI System

PET and MR vessel wall imaging were performed simultaneously. All patients were asked to fast for at least 6 hours before [18F]FDG PET imaging. Imaging was performed only if fasting glucose was <7.7 mmol/L before tracer injection. [18F]FDG was injected intravenously at a dose of 3 to 4 MBq/kg. The acquisition started 50 to 90 minutes after tracer injection using an integrated PET/MRI system (uPMR890, United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China). The PET/MR image acquisition range was centered on the carotid bifurcation, with an 18 cm coverage. MR vessel wall imaging was performed with an 8‐channel carotid coil. The following carotid MR pulse sequences were applied to obtain the cross‐sectional images centered at the carotid bifurcation: (1) 3‐dimensional (3D) time‐of‐flight, (2) 3D T1W turbo spin‐echo, (3) 3D T2W turbo spin‐echo, and (4) post‐contrast 3D T1W turbo spin‐echo sequence were acquired immediately after contrast agent administration. The detailed MRI acquisition parameters are listed in Table S1. PET images were reconstructed using the ordered subset expectation maximization algorithm (matrix size=192×192; slice thickness=1.4 mm) with time‐of‐flight information, following corrections for attenuation, scatter, and random coincidences.

Image Analysis

The PET/MR image clinical conventional analysis was performed using a dedicated plaque analysis workstation (United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China). The [18F]FDG PET images were initially coregistered to the corresponding 3D T1W images to ensure accurate anatomical alignment. Subsequent spatial normalization was performed using statistical parametric mapping (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/), transforming all images into Montreal Neurological Institute space with an isotropic voxel size of 1 mm. For clinical interpretation, all images were independently reviewed by 2 board‐certified radiologists (F.F. [Fan Fu] and Z.Y. [Yu Zhang], each with >8 years of PET/MR imaging), who were blinded to all clinical and imaging data. In cases of discrepancy, a senior radiologist (B.L. [Biao Li], with >15 years' experience in atherosclerotic vessel wall imaging) conducted a consensus review. Following normalization, region‐of‐interest segmentation was manually conducted on the fused PET/MR images by 2 board‐certified radiologists. To further ensure methodological consistency and accuracy, a senior radiologist independently reviewed the coregistration results and segmentation masks. Symptomatic plaques were defined as those associated with an ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack occurring within the preceding 6 months in the ipsilateral carotid territory, with imaging evidence confirming the plaque as the causative lesion. Asymptomatic plaques were defined as those in patients without a prior history of stroke or transient ischemic attack and without imaging evidence of cerebrovascular events attributable to the plaque.

Clinical Feature Measurement

Plaque major components including IPH, lipid‐rich necrotic core, ruptured FC (RFC), calcification, surface ulceration, and plaque enhancement were identified and recorded. IPH was defined as a signal intensity >150% of that of adjacent muscles on T1W sequences. 28 Lipid‐rich necrotic core refers to a heterogeneous tissue composed of cholesterol crystals and necrotic debris from apoptotic cells. 29 RFC refers to the disruption of the FC that covers the lipid‐rich necrotic core of an atherosclerotic plaque, which can expose the necrotic core and lead to thrombosis, potentially causing acute ischemic events. 30 Calcified plaques were identified based on their typical hypointense signals on T2‐weighted MRI. 31 Discontinuity of the FC on T1W (often accompanied by a depressed plaque surface and IPH) is defined as plaque surface ulceration. 32 Plaque enhancement was assessed after intravenous gadolinium injection. The degree of enhancement was graded on a 2‐point scale: grade 0, similar to or less than that of intracranial arterial walls; grade 1, greater than that in grade 0 and similar to or greater than that of the infundibulum. 33 PET images were visually assessed for radiotracer uptake, and fused images were used to accurately localize the corresponding areas in relation to the vessel wall plaques. Relevant focal plaque FDG uptake was defined as higher uptake than that in the adjacent vessel wall, as observed with the naked eye. 34 , 35 The SUVmax of the plaque was calculated as the maximum of all plaque slices by delineating the circular regions of interest of each slice. The TBR was calculated as the ratio of SUVmax to the venous blood pool SUVmean. 36

Radiomic Features Extraction and Model Development

Radiomic features were extracted using the open‐source Python package, PyRadiomics (version 2.2.0) and included 3 categories: (1) first‐order features demonstrating pixel values of images; (2) shape features (2‐dimensional and 3D) based on shape and size; and (3) texture features showing the internal and surface textures of lesions including gray‐level size zone matrix, gray‐level run length matrix, gray‐scale co‐occurrence matrix, and gray‐level difference matrix. To avoid data heterogeneity bias, the images were resampled to 0.5 mm×0.5 mm×0.5 mm for the same resolution, and their intensity was graded on a scale of 0 to 100 before radiomic features extraction.

The predictive performance of 3 model types—conventional models, radiomics models, and combined models (incorporating conventional and radiomic features)—for classifying high‐risk plaques was evaluated across MRI, PET, and PET/MRI modalities. The conventional model was developed based on the radiological variables evaluated in 200 plaques with P<0.05 in multivariate logistic regression analysis and the odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs werewas calculated. The following steps were used to build a radiomics model for identifying high‐risk plaques. (1) The development cohort was randomly divided into a training set (70%) and an internal test set (30%), with balanced patient characteristics. A prospective, independent temporal validation cohort was held out as a completely unseen set for final external validation. The features were standardized using Z score normalization to 0 mean and unit variance for the training set, and the distribution of the features in the training set was used to normalize the test set and validation set. (2) Student's t tests were used to select the features with P<0.05 in each sequence, and then the features with significant differences and an area under the curve (AUC) >0.65 by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) were selected. (3) For feature pairs with correlation coefficients >0.90, the feature with the better univariate predictive power (smaller Mann–Whitney U test P value) was retained. (4) Finally, a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression model was applied in the training subset for further feature selection and signature construction. Based on the model parameter λ, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator shrinks all regression coefficients toward 0 while the coefficients of many irrelevant features were set exactly to 0. Tenfold cross validation with minimum criteria was used to determine the optimal λ. (5) After the feature was selected, we constructed radiomics models with a linear support vector classifier. The 5‐fold cross‐validation training method was adopted to minimize the bias associated with overfitting and to evaluate the effectiveness of the model. The model's output was converted into a probability score, representing the probability of the plaque in question being a symptomatic plaque. Both selected conventional and radiomic features were used to develop the combined model, with the corresponding Shapley Additive Explanations interpretations and nomogram generated.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2), with key packages including “pROC” for ROC analysis, “rms” for validation, and “rmda” for decision curve analysis. Baseline clinical and demographic characteristics were compared between the symptomatic and asymptomatic groups. For continuous variables, normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene's test; Student's t tests were used for normally distributed data with homogeneous variance, and the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for nonnormally distributed data. For categorical variables, the chi‐square test was used, or Fisher's exact test when the expected frequency in any cell of the contingency table was <5. To develop the multivariate logistic regression models, a bidirectional stepwise selection procedure based on the Akaike information criterion was employed. This method iteratively adds and removes predictors to find the optimal model that best balances goodness‐of‐fit and complexity, thereby reducing the risk of overfitting. Crucially, model diagnostics, including the assessment of multicollinearity via variance inflation factors (<5) and sensitivity analyses comparing full and reduced models, were performed to ensure the stability and independent predictive value of all retained variables. Model performance was comprehensively evaluated. Discriminatory ability was assessed using ROC curve analysis, with the AUC calculated. Comparisons of AUCs between different models were performed using the DeLong test. Model calibration was visualized with calibration curves and quantitatively assessed using the Hosmer – Lemeshow test. Furthermore, the clinical utility of the models was evaluated using decision curve analysis. Other performance metrics—including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value—were calculated based on a probability threshold of 0.5. To provide an unbiased estimate of model performance, bootstrap validation with 1000 resamples was applied to the final model to derive an optimism‐corrected AUC. Interobserver reproducibility for feature extraction was evaluated. The interrater reliability of continuous radiomic features was assessed by calculating the intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) using a 2‐way random‐effects, absolute agreement, single‐measures model (ICC [1–2]); ICCs >0.8 were considered excellent. For categorical features, Cohen's kappa was used, with values >0.6 considered substantial. 37 , 38 A 2‐tailed P‐value <0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Conventional Assessment of the Carotid Plaques

Among 200 advanced carotid plaques characterized by MR vessel wall imaging, 78 were symptomatic and 122 were asymptomatic. [18F]FDG PET/MRI was performed a mean of 38 days (range 1–90) after the symptom. Our analysis revealed distinct predictive values of MRI morphological features and PET metabolic parameters in identifying symptomatic plaques.

For MRI morphological assessment, univariable analyses showed significant associations between symptomatic plaques and IPH, RFC, surface ulceration, and plaque enhancement (all P<0.05; Table 2). In multivariable logistic regression, surface ulceration (OR, 2.50 [95% CI, 1.20–5.25]) and plaque enhancement (OR, 4.74 [95% CI, 1.74–14.02]) remained independent predictors. Regarding the discriminative performance (Table 3), individual MRI features demonstrated moderate predictive value across all cohorts. Surface ulceration yielded AUCs of 0.702 in the training set, 0.709 in the internal test set, and 0.651 in the temporal validation set. Similarly, plaque enhancement achieved AUCs of 0.669, 0.742, and 0.679, respectively. Combining these 2 features into an MRI‐based conventional model resulted in improved discrimination, with an AUC of 0.755 in the training cohort, 0.822 in the internal test cohort, and 0.702 in the temporal validation cohort.

Predictive Ability of Conventional, Radiomics, and Combined Models Across MRI, PET, and PET/MRI Modalities in the Training, Internal Test, and Temporal Validation Cohorts

For PET metabolic assessment, univariable analyses identified metabolic uptake, SUVmax, and TBR as significant correlates of symptomatic plaques (all P<0.05). In the multivariable model, SUVmax (OR, 18.70 [95% CI, 6.11–66.37]) and metabolic uptake (OR, 6.67 [95% CI, 1.98–22.95]) were independent predictors. As shown in Table 3, the individual PET metabolic features exhibited strong predictive value. Metabolic uptake yielded AUC values of 0.824 in the training cohort, 0.819 in the internal test cohort, and 0.799 in the temporal validation cohort. The discriminatory performance of SUVmax was consistently higher, with corresponding AUCs of 0.878, 0.911, and 0.882. The integrated PET conventional model, which combined SUVmax and metabolic uptake, demonstrated further improved discrimination. It achieved AUCs of 0.899 in the training cohort, 0.926 in the internal test cohort, and 0.878 in the temporal validation cohort, outperforming the MRI conventional model across all 3 data sets. Radiomics Assessment of the Carotid Plaques

From each imaging modality, we extracted 1991 radiomic features on both the original and filtered images. Using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator feature selection, we retained 13 T1W radiomics, 18 PET radiomics, and 19 hybrid PET/MRI features (11 from [18F]FDG PET and 8 from T1W), respectively. These selected features were used to build 3 separate radiomics models. Detailed characteristics of these selected features are provided in Table S2 and Figure S1.

The predictive performance of each model was evaluated across 3 distinct data sets: the training cohort (n=101), the internal test cohort (n=44), and the temporal validation cohort (n=55) (Table 3). Among single‐modality models, the MRI radiomics model achieved AUCs of 0.866 (training), 0.861 (internal test), and 0.842 (temporal validation). The MRI combined model sustained robust discriminative performance across all cohorts, yielding AUCs of 0.895 (training), 0.893 (internal test), and 0.884 (temporal validation). In comparison to the MRI radiomics model, the MRI combined model exhibited a consistent increase in negative predictive value across all cohorts: from 0.816 to 0.843 in the training cohort, from 0.800 to 0.828 in the internal test cohort, and from 0.800 to 0.812 in the temporal validation cohort. The PET radiomics model demonstrated strong predictive performance, with AUCs of 0.929 in the training cohort, 0.889 in the internal test cohort, and 0.845 in the temporal validation cohort. The PET combined model further improved upon this, achieving higher AUCs of 0.945, 0.922, and 0.869, respectively.

The PET/MRI combined model demonstrated the best overall performance among all multimodal approaches evaluated. Its performance metrics across the three cohorts were as follows: in the training set (accuracy 90.1%, sensitivity 85.7%, specificity 92.4%, AUC 0.962), the internal test set (88.6%, 88.2%, 88.9%, and 0.967, respectively), and the temporal validation cohort (86.4%, 87.5%, 85.7%, and 0.926, respectively). These results are further supported by the analyses presented in Figure 5. The ROC curves (Figure 5A through 5C) visually confirm the model's superior discriminative ability in all cohorts. Calibration curves (Figure 5D through 5F) demonstrated good agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes, with low Brier scores of 0.073 (training), 0.100 (test), and 0.115 (validation), indicating reliable prediction calibration. Furthermore, decision curve analysis (Figure 5G through 5I) showed that the PET/MRI combined model yielded the highest net benefit across a wide range of clinical probability thresholds, suggesting its potential utility for optimizing patient selection for intervention while minimizing unnecessary treatments.

Reproducibility of Measurements

Cohen's kappa values for IPH, lipid‐rich necrotic core, RFC, calcification, surface ulceration, and metabolic uptake were 0.864, 0.859, 0.893, 0.851, 0.889, and 0.856, respectively. ICCs for conventional features (SUVmax, TBR) were 0.869 and 0.851, respectively. Average ICCs for radiomic features were 0.846 (range 0.798–0.881).

DISCUSSION

This study systematically evaluated the diagnostic performance of MRI morphological features, PET metabolic parameters, and PET/MRI radiomics in distinguishing symptomatic from asymptomatic carotid plaques. Our investigation unveiled that PET‐based assessments exhibited superior diagnostic performance compared with MRI‐based models. Notably, the integrated PET/MRI combined model achieved the highest discriminative performance, along with good calibration and high net clinical benefit. These findings underscore the clinical significance of integrated PET/MRI system in carotid plaque assessment, which enables concurrent analysis of structural features and metabolic activity, thereby improving the precision of high‐risk plaque detection.

Previous studies have largely focused on individual imaging modalities, particularly high‐resolution vessel wall MRI, to characterize plaque vulnerability. Established morphological indicators such as IPH, RFC, surface ulceration, and plaque enhancement have been linked with symptomatic events. 22 , 23 Our findings support these associations and further confirm surface ulceration and plaque enhancement as independent predictors in a multivariate context. Surface ulceration reflects FC rupture and endothelial damage, exposing thrombogenic plaque components and promoting thrombus formation. 32 Plaque enhancement, in turn, indicates neovascularization and inflammatory infiltration via leaky vasa vasorum, both of which are tightly linked to plaque destabilization. 33

Despite MRI's capacity to delineate plaque architecture with high resolution, it lacks the ability to capture inflammatory activity, which is a key contributor to plaque destabilization. In contrast, [18F]FDG PET visualizes glucose metabolism, serving as a direct surrogate for inflammation. A growing body of evidence has established the central role of inflammation in plaque rupture. For instance, Tahara et al. 39 demonstrated that arterial inflammation detected by [18F]FDG PET predicted future cardiovascular events independently of plaque morphology. Similarly, Tarkin et al. 12 emphasized that PET provides molecular‐level insights into residual inflammation and atherosclerotic activity, improving sensitivity and specificity beyond anatomical imaging. Yu et al. 20 also reported strong associations between PET parameters such as SUVmax and TBR with symptomatic plaques. Our study corroborates these findings, showing that PET‐derived SUVmax (OR, 18.70) and metabolic uptake (OR, 6.67) were powerful independent predictors of plaque symptomatology. Importantly, we found that integrating PET metabolic and MRI morphological features into a unified conventional model yielded greater diagnostic accuracy compared with PET or MRI conventional models alone. This multimodal approach improves overall diagnostic robustness by capturing complementary aspects of plaque biology, such as inflammatory activity and structural complexity.

Compared with conventional methods, radiomic analysis is more quantitative and may detect “agnostic” features that the radiologist cannot appreciate, such as uniformity or randomness (entropy) of the intensities on the image. 22 Unlike conventional metrics limited to capturing a few basic image characteristics, radiomics enables the extraction of comprehensive phenotypic details from plaques or tumors. 40 These details encompass voxel‐level intensity distributions, 3D geometric features, and complex textural patterns. Although surface ulceration has been investigated as a biomarker for vulnerable plaques, existing research predominantly relies on simplistic binary classifications (presence versus absence). 32 However, critical quantitative characteristics, such as signal intensity variations, volumetric measurements, morphological configurations, and spatial distribution patterns, remain understudied. This gap persists because conventional radiological assessments in clinical practice lack the methodological framework to capture such granular data. Radiomics has the potential to quantify subvisual information and subtle imaging phenotypes that are beyond human recognition.

The demonstrated methodological superiority establishes radiomics as a validated predictive framework with substantial clinical translational potential. As evidenced by Li et al.'s seminal work, 22 3D MRI‐based radiomics models exhibit superior discriminatory capacity in distinguishing symptomatic from asymptomatic intracranial arterial plaques compared with conventional assessment paradigms. Although their study focused on intracranial lesions, we hypothesize that a similar radiomic approach is equally applicable to extracranial carotid plaques. Our investigation represents the inaugural comprehensive evaluation of carotid atherosclerosis through PET and MRI radiomics profiling. For MRI modality, both radiomics and combined models consistently outperformed conventional approaches, aligning with existing evidence of radiomics'capability to detect subtle intraplaque heterogeneity beyond human visual perception. In PET analysis, although conventional metabolic parameters already achieved excellent diagnostic performance, incorporating radiomic features yielded further improvements in specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value. This enhancement likely stems from the ability of radiomics to capture complex spatial patterns and metabolic heterogeneity that are not reflected in summary metrics such as SUVmax or metabolic uptake. 41 Despite PET's inherently lower spatial resolution compared with anatomical imaging modalities, the strength of PET radiomics lies in its unique ability to quantify functional and metabolic heterogeneity, rather than resolving fine anatomical structures. This principle is well established in oncology, where PET radiomics effectively characterizes tumor heterogeneity. 26 , 27 The predictive power of our model was driven by texture‐based features that directly measure this heterogeneity (see Table S2 for details). For instance, entropy‐based features (eg, RunEntropy, ZoneEntropy) quantify the randomness of tracer distribution, while others like GrayLevelNonUniformity and ZoneVariance measure the variability of metabolic activity—key imaging biomarkers reflecting underlying inflammatory processes and plaque instability. Therefore, our study validates that PET radiomics extracts robust data by shifting the analytical focus from anatomical precision to the quantification of functional heterogeneity.

Crucially, a core innovation of this study is the systematic comparison of combined models. We demonstrated that the PET combined model significantly outperformed the MRI combined model across all 3 cohorts (all P<0.05). More importantly, the PET/MRI combined model achieved the highest overall performance, outperforming both single‐modality combined models with statistical significance. It also exhibited optimal calibration and the greatest net clinical benefit across a wide range of decision thresholds, as confirmed by decision curve analysis. This finding is clinically significant because it reflects the model's potential to shift the current paradigm from reacting to late‐stage stenosis to proactively identifying vulnerable plaques in asymptomatic patients before a cerebrovascular event occurs. By learning the signature of “clinically dangerous” plaques, our model enables the early identification of at‐risk individuals who might otherwise be overlooked. This facilitates a proactive management strategy, allowing clinicians to target aggressive medical or surgical interventions to those who will benefit most. Such an approach maximizes the potential for stroke prevention while simultaneously minimizing the risks and costs associated with unnecessary procedures in low‐risk patients. These findings therefore support the synergistic value of integrating radiomics‐enhanced structural and functional imaging, providing a powerful tool for personalized risk stratification and the prevention of devastating ischemic strokes.

The observed superiority of PET over MRI‐based models may be attributed to their distinct biological targets. Although MRI identifies morphological features that represent the end stages of disease progression, PET captures active inflammatory processes that precede structural changes and clinical events. 5 , 12 This mechanistic complementarity underpins the rationale for hybrid PET/MRI. In clinical practice, hybrid imaging may be particularly advantageous in cases with ambiguous or inconclusive findings on MRI alone. Radiomics further augments this synergy by enabling a data‐driven, high‐dimensional analysis that uncovers hidden imaging phenotypes linked to histopathologic activity. 40

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our study was a single‐center analysis with a modest sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings; external validation in larger, multicenter cohorts is warranted. Second, the clinical implementation of radiomics faces practical challenges, including the need for significant computational power and standardized protocols for image acquisition and segmentation to ensure reproducibility. This is particularly pertinent for PET radiomics, where due to low spatial resolution, certain radiomic features may not have physiological correlates. Third, our feature fusion approach was relatively basic. Future work should explore more advanced integration strategies, such as deep learning‐based fusion, to enhance model performance and robustness, thereby facilitating a smoother transition from research to clinical application.

CONCLUSIONS

The integrated [18F]FDG PET/MRI combined model further demonstrated that integrating structural, metabolic, and radiomic features yields superior diagnostic accuracy. These findings advocate for the adoption of hybrid PET/MRI as a noninvasive, high‐precision tool for identifying high‐risk atherosclerotic plaques. Future work should focus on expanding patient cohorts and incorporating deep learning strategies to automate and optimize model development, thereby accelerating the clinical translation of this promising approach.
