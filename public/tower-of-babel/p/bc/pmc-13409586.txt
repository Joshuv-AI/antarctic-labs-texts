Exact Combinatorial Density of States for the Critical 1D Ising Model
Authors: Bastian Castorene, Francisco J. Peña, Martin HvE Groves, Patricio Vargas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This work presents an exact microcanonical combinatorial analysis of the one-dimensional antiferromagnetic Ising model. At the primary ground-state level crossing B/J=2, degeneracies follow the Fibonacci and Lucas sequences for open chains and periodic rings, respectively. We extend this framework to the complete excitation spectrum, demonstrating that the density of states is constructed from topological defects governed by linear Diophantine equations and p-fold Fibonacci convolutions. Open boundaries act as fractional defects, densifying the chain spectrum into energy steps of 2J, whereas the closed ring remains quantized in units of 4J. Notably, this exact topological counting exposes non-trivial spectral gaps near the fully polarized limit, strictly forbidding the penultimate macroscopic energy levels in both topologies. Using the transfer-matrix formalism, we derive exact closed-form expressions for the critical degeneracies at all energy levels. These results provide a rigorous analytical foundation for extracting exact residual entropies and exposing the intrinsic number-theoretic architecture of quantum critical manifolds.

1. Introduction

The Ising model is among the most enduring paradigms in statistical and condensed-matter physics, providing a straightforward yet profoundly rich framework for studying cooperative phenomena, symmetry breaking, and critical behavior in many-body systems [1,2,3,4,5,6]. Introduced more than a century ago, it has since become a cornerstone of theoretical physics, shaping our understanding of phase transitions, magnetism, and universality classes [7,8,9,10], while simultaneously inspiring developments in areas as diverse as information theory and quantum computation. Beyond its historical relevance, the Ising model continues to unveil novel conceptual and mathematical connections under modern theoretical perspectives. In particular, the interplay between quantum criticality, topology, and degeneracy has gained renewed interest in quantum thermodynamics [11,12,13,14,15,16], as critical manifolds are known to enhance quantum coherence and the efficiency of energy conversion processes. The Ising model has also served as a bridge between quantum and classical descriptions through the Suzuki–Trotter mapping [17] and has been extended to competing-interaction and frustrated systems exhibiting multiparametric quantum criticality [18,19,20,21,22,23]. In these contexts, the emergence of Fibonacci and Lucas-type relations frequently signals underlying recursive or topological symmetries, reflecting the deep link between discrete mathematics and critical phenomena [2,24,25]. In its one-dimensional form, the Ising model remains analytically tractable through integral methods within the canonical ensemble, and its properties continue to be explored using various analytical techniques [26,27,28]. Historically, Kramers and Wannier demonstrated that the partition function of a 2D-lattice model could be expressed as the trace of a product of transfer matrices [29]. Building upon this framework, the two-dimensional Ising model, which had previously resisted a closed-form analytical solution, was exactly solved by Onsager [30] by determining the eigenvalues of the associated transfer matrix to derive the exact thermodynamic quantities. Subsequently, the one-dimensional model has been revisited in multiple modern contexts, ranging from renormalization and scaling theory [8,17] to quantum information and fidelity approaches to phase transitions [31,32,33,34]. Experimentally, Zhang et al. demonstrated the direct observation of quantum criticality in finite Ising spin chains using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) quantum simulators [35], revealing the sequence of level crossings and critical fields associated with the antiferromagnetic-to-paramagnetic transition. From a theoretical standpoint, Silva da Conceição and Maia [36] established a formal connection between the one-dimensional Ising partition function and generalized Lucas polynomials, showing that the canonical partition function obeys a recurrence relation of the Fibonacci–Lucas type. Together, these contributions highlight both the experimental accessibility of critical degeneracies and their algebraic support within the canonical ensemble.

While the partition function of the one-dimensional Ising model has long been exactly solved via canonical transfer-matrix methods, the explicit level-resolved organization of the microcanonical degeneracies at a given critical field remains a useful and nontrivial problem. In particular, the conventional route from the canonical partition function to a discrete density of states through an inverse Laplace transform is not the most transparent way to expose the combinatorial structure of individual energy levels. The transfer-matrix eigenvalues defining Z(β) involve non-analytic radicals and nested exponential structures, which obscure a direct microscopic interpretation of the level-resolved degeneracies.

The density of states of finite one-dimensional Ising systems has also been addressed previously through numerical and combinatorial arguments [37,38], and a recent preprint obtained general closed-form expressions for DN(E,m) across boundary conditions and interaction signs [39]. These provide a general framework; here we instead resolve the exact combinatorial architecture specific to the antiferromagnetic level crossing B/J=2.

For this reason, a complementary methodology rooted in the direct enumeration of microstates is valuable, making analytic combinatorics fundamental to the present formulation. By employing symbolic methods and combinatorial transfer theorems—conceptually distinct from, yet mathematically isomorphic to, physical transfer-matrix derivations [40,41]—microscopic configurations can be rigorously mapped onto exact combinatorial coefficients. For instance, the formal construction of sequences, compositions, and multisets, such as evaluating the number of compositions of an integer n into exactly c positive parts, introduces nontrivial mathematical relations intricately connected to p-fold Fibonacci convolutions [42,43]. Consequently, diverse challenges—ranging from the non-adjacency counting problem in linear arrangements to enumerating the independent vertex subsets of a graph—are fundamentally related to the Fibonacci and Lucas sequences [44,45,46,47]. Furthermore, the requirement that discrete configurations satisfy structural equivalences can be directly modeled via linear Diophantine equations [48].

In this context, understanding the exact combinatorial structure of degenerate states sharing the same zero-temperature energy provides a fundamental bridge between microscopic spin configurations and macroscopic thermodynamic quantities, such as residual entropy [2,3,24,49,50]. Furthermore, establishing the density of states analytically offers a profound advantage over numerical sampling methods. While standard Metropolis Monte Carlo simulations sample individual configurations for a given energy, flat-histogram techniques like the Wang–Landau algorithm [51] reconstruct the energy landscape by computing the macroscopic degeneracies. Deriving these degeneracies exactly provides a thermodynamically richer description, directly yielding the exact total entropy of the system and bypassing numerical approximations entirely [52].

In brief, whereas canonical transfer-matrix methods deliver only the thermally averaged partition function Z(β), here we resolve the exact microcanonical degeneracy of every individual energy level of the antiferromagnetic chain and ring at the critical field B/J=2. Using analytic combinatorics we show that the ground manifolds follow the Fibonacci and Lucas sequences, that the excitations obey a linear Diophantine relation m=b+2k, and that open boundaries act as fractional defects refining the chain spectrum to steps of 2J against the 4J quantization of the ring. Here, “fractional” means only that an excited boundary spin costs one excitation unit versus two for a bulk pair, and does not imply fractional quasiparticles in the many-body sense. The excited-level degeneracies are generated by p-fold Fibonacci convolutions and, equivalently, by a two-state transfer-matrix generating function that yields all levels in closed form. This level-resolved microcanonical decomposition thus extends previous canonical and generalized-density-of-states analyses by exposing the exact number-theoretic architecture that thermal averaging conceals, giving direct access to the residual entropy of each critical level.

2. Model

The working substance consists of N spin particles described by a one-dimensional antiferromagnetic Ising model subjected to a longitudinal magnetic field. The Hamiltonian governing the system is given by(1)H^=J∑i=1N−1σizσi+1z+B∑i=1Nσiz, where J denotes the antiferromagnetic exchange coupling between nearest-neighbor spins (J>0) and σiz represents the Pauli operator along the z direction acting on the ith spin, whose eigenvalues are σi=±1. As illustrated in Figure 1a, the system forms a linear chain of interacting spins. In this configuration, both translational symmetry σi↔σi+δ and spin-reflection symmetry σi⇌−σi are simultaneously broken [4]. The longitudinal magnetic field B is applied along the positive z axis and, in physical units, is expressed as B=μBgzH, where μB is the Bohr magneton and gz the Landé g-factor. For notational simplicity, all field values are expressed in units of the exchange constant throughout this work. By extending the summation from (N−1)→N in the exchange term and imposing periodic boundary conditions, σN+1z=σ1z, the open-chain topology is transformed into a closed ring, as shown in Figure 1b, while the magnetic-field contribution remains unchanged.

Figure 2 illustrates the distribution of the 2N energy levels of the one-dimensional Ising model as a function of the magnetic field. Depending on the parity of N, the Ising chain exhibits three ground-state level crossings (GLCs) for even system sizes and two for odd system sizes [18]. Specifically, an additional parity-dependent critical point emerges at B/J=1 (see Appendix A). In all cases, however, a nontrivial LC occurs at Bcrit=B/J=2, where multiple energy levels cross for N≥2. At this LC, the system undergoes a transition from an antiferromagnetic to a paramagnetic phase as the spins align with the external magnetic field, leading to a nontrivial degeneracy involving several energetically equivalent configurations. In particular, the energy contributions of these degenerate states are primarily determined by the relative orientation of the spins composing the chain (ring). From Figure 2, one can infer that several spin configurations σi share the same total energy, as schematically illustrated in Figure 1. The distinct energy configurations depend solely on the number of up and down spins, N↑ and N↓, the number of parallel nearest-neighbor pairs N↑↑ and N↓↓, and the number of domain walls N↑↓ [28]. These quantities follow N↑+N↓=N. Defining the coordination number q=2 for both topologies [3], the 2N↑ total bonds originating from up spins must either connect to another up spin (sharing two bonds per ↑↑ pair) or to a down spin (one bond per ↑↓ pair). By symmetry, the same logic applies to down spins, yielding:(2)2N↑=2N↑↑+N↑↓,2N↓=2N↓↓+N↑↓.Thus, the total number of spins can be written in terms of nearest-neighbor combinations:(3)N↑↑+N↓↓+N↑↓=N.

Substituting these relations into the Ising Hamiltonian Equation (1) yields an equivalent representation in terms of spin populations:(4)H=JN↑↑+N↓↓−N↑↓+BN↑−N↓.

Aligned spins contribute +1 and anti-aligned spins −1 to the exchange interaction energy. Similarly, the magnetic-field term minimizes (maximizes) the energy when the spins point downward (upward), contributing −1 and +1, respectively. Expressing the Hamiltonian Equation (4) in terms of the number of up spins, domain walls, and total sites yields(5)H=JN−2N↑↓+B2N↑−N.

Focusing on the ground state at the LC with Bcrit=B/J=2 in Figure 2, the minimum energies of the open chain and the closed ring are given by(6)Hchain=−(N+1)+4N↑−2N↑↓,Hring=−N+4N↑−2N↑↓.

A particular case of minimum energy arises when no spins point up, and hence no domain walls are present:(7)Hgroundchain=−(N+1),Hgroundring=−N.

Consequently, the open-chain configuration exhibits a slightly lower ground-state energy than the closed ring for the same number of sites N. The next step is to determine the number of distinct microstates leading to the same ground-state energy, i.e., to find the number of distinct ways in which N spins in the chain or ring can be arranged to yield the same ground energy of Equation (7).

3. Fibonacci–Lucas Ground Degeneracies

To determine the number of possible microstates corresponding to spin-up configurations in the lowest-energy state, the ground-state energies in Equation (7) are equated to the spin-based Hamiltonian in Equation (6). This leads to the same condition for both the open chain and the closed ring:(8)2N↑=N↑↓⇒N↑↑=0, which indicates that no two spin-up sites can be adjacent when this condition is imposed on Equation (2) implies that spin-up sites must be separated by at least one spin-down site. For the open chain, the number of admissible up-spins ranges from zero up to the integer part of (N−1)/2, strictly excluding the boundary sites to maintain the condition in Equation (8). Since the edge spins have only a single neighbor, an up-spin at either boundary can form only one up-down bond, contributing a single domain wall. To satisfy the constraint 2N↑=N↑↓, each up-spin must generate exactly two domain walls, which physically prohibits up-spin occupation at the boundaries. Consequently, the valid configurations correspond to the number of ways to arrange the spins so that no two up-sites are adjacent, which is structurally analogous to the combinatorial problem of distributing non-consecutive elements on a discrete linear chain. The resulting combinatorial expression in the microcanonical ensemble is(9)Ωgroundchain=∑N↑=0N−12N−1−N↑N↑=FN. where the sum in Equation (9) defines the N-th Fibonacci number (FN) [42]. In the case of the closed ring, applying the same non-adjacency condition given in Equation (8) connects the boundary sites, introducing circular symmetry. This symmetry allows additional spin-up placements that are forbidden in the open chain. The number of admissible configurations, ranging from zero up to the integer part of half the system size N/2, is therefore analogous to counting the number of ways to arrange heads on a closed ring of N coins such that no two heads are adjacent:(10)Ωgroundring=∑N↑=0N2NN−N↑N−N↑N↑=LNThe combinatorial factor N/(N−N↑) accounts for the periodic boundary and rotational symmetry of the ring. By definition, this expression represents the N-th Lucas number (LN) [53]. The number of ground-state microstates in the ring, given by Equation (10), exceeds that of the open chain, described by Equation (9).

4. Higher-Order Excitations

4.1. Combinatorial Structure of the Excited States

The procedure to determine the exact combinatorial structure of the excited states relies on evaluating the discrete energy gap generated by perturbing the topological constraints of the ground state. At the Level Crossing (LC) with Bcrit=2, the energy of any arbitrary configuration relative to the absolute minimum can be analytically deduced. For the open chain, the total number of up spins decomposes into interior (N↑,int) and boundary (N↑,b) contributions, such that(11)N↑=N↑,int+N↑,b.The total number of domain walls is related to the internal and boundary up spins. Inserting this decomposition into Equation (2), we obtain(12)N↑↓=2N↑,int+N↑,b−2N↑↑.Substituting these relations into the Hamiltonian in Equation (6) evaluated at B/J=2, the energy shifts relative to the ground state in Equation (7) are given by(13)ΔEchain=J(2N↑,b+4N↑↑),(14)ΔEring=4JN↑↑.

Equation (13) dictates that the open-chain ground state (ΔEchain=0) is strictly achieved when both boundary spins are pointing down (N↑,b=0) and no adjacent up spins exist (N↑↑=0), recovering the results from Equation (9). For the closed ring, periodic boundary conditions naturally forbid boundary spins, rendering the energy gap dependent exclusively on the number of bulk adjacent pairs, as shown in Equation (14).

Spectral Pattern of and Change in Variables: The energy gap equations directly reveal the quantization of the excitation spectrum through the available topological defects. To systematize the combinatorial analysis, we introduce a change in variables, defining the topological indices b≡N↑,b and k≡N↑↑. For the open chain, the discrete excitation index m is determined by the linear Diophantine equation [48]:(15)m=b+2k.Since the number of excited boundaries is strictly bounded as b∈{0,1,2} and the number of bulk adjacent pairs is k∈N0, the integer solutions to this Diophantine equation classify all permissible macroscopic configurations for each excitation level m∈N0. Conversely, for the closed ring, the excitation index is governed solely by bulk defects, defined directly as k∈N0. Expressing the energy gaps in terms of these new indices, the excitation spectrum of the one-dimensional Ising model at the LC B/J=2 rigorously follows:(16)ΔEmchain=2mJ,m∈N0,(17)ΔEkring=4kJ,k∈N0.

Equations (16) and (17) demonstrate that the closed ring spectrum is rigorously quantized in discrete units of 4J, representing the minimum energy cost to violate the bulk non-adjacency constraint. The open chain, however, exhibits a strictly denser spectrum quantized in half-steps of 2J. This discrepancy indicates that the open boundaries function as fractional topological defects; a boundary spin flip costs exactly half the energy of an adjacent bulk pair, permanently altering the macroscopic state counting and the thermodynamic limit of the critical manifold. Furthermore, the Diophantine relation in Equation (15) intrinsically bounds the excitation spectrum. For an open chain of length N, the accessible macroscopic excitation indices are(18)m∈{0,1,…,2N−3}∪{2N}, so that the spectrum contains exactly 2N−1 accessible macroscopic energy levels. The indices m=2N−2 and m=2N−1 are topologically forbidden, while the isolated maximum level m=2N corresponds to the unique fully polarized configuration.

4.2. First-Excited State

The degeneracy of this first excited state, Ω1chain, requires calculating the number of valid configurations where exactly one boundary is up, forcing its immediate neighbor to be down. For a chain of size N, fixing one end to ↑↓ and the opposite end strictly to ↓ leaves N−3 interior sites to be populated by non-adjacent up spins. The number of such configurations is given by the Fibonacci number FN−1. Accounting for the two symmetric boundaries, the total macroscopic degeneracy of the first excited state in the open chain is(19)Ω1chain=2FN−1

To determine the degeneracy Ω1ring, we must place a single ↑↑ block in a ring of size N. To prevent further adjacencies (N↑↑>1), this block must be flanked by down spins, effectively forming a ↓↑↑↓ sequence that occupies 4 sites. This sequence can be anchored in N distinct starting positions around the ring. The remaining N−4 sites must be filled with non-adjacent up spins, generating FN−2 possible arrangements. The total degeneracy for the first excited state of the ring is therefore strictly proportional to the Fibonacci sequence:(20)Ω1ring=NFN−2

5.5. Generalization to the m-th State of the Open Chain

Building upon the mechanisms of defect isolation, boundary collapses, and topological clustering identified in the preceding specific excitations, we employ the symbolic methods of [40] to formalize a unifying combinatorial architecture that yields a closed-form expression for an arbitrary state number m.

5.5.1. Excitation Classes and the Diophantine Constraint

For a macroscopic state at a total excitation energy ΔEmchain=2mJ above the ground state, the excitation quantum numbers (b,k)—where b counts excited boundaries and k counts bulk adjacent pairs—must satisfy the linear Diophantine constraint:(30)b+2k=m,b∈{0,1,2},k∈N0.The admissible integer solutions depend entirely on the parity of m. For even m, the solutions are (b,k)=(0,m/2) and, provided m≥2, (b,k)=(2,(m−2)/2). For odd m, the unique solution is (b,k)=(1,(m−1)/2). Each valid solution defines a distinct topological class of configurations that contributes independently to the total macroscopic degeneracy.

5.5.2. Boundary Symmetry Factor

When b=1, the single excited boundary can be located at either the left or the right end of the chain. Because these spatial configurations are related by reflection symmetry but correspond to distinct spin microstates, the b=1 class carries a global symmetry factor γ1=2. Conversely, classes characterized by b=0 or b=2 are inherently symmetric under reflection, yielding γ0=γ2=1.

5.5.3. Cluster Decomposition of Bulk Defects

For configurations with k≥1 bulk adjacent pairs, these defects can merge into c contiguous clusters, where 1≤c≤k. A topological cluster of ℓ adjacent pairs occupies exactly ℓ+1 consecutive up spins. Consequently, the number of distinct ways to partition k defects into exactly c ordered clusters is dictated by the standard composition count [40]:(31)k−1c−1.

These c clusters, along with the b excited boundary sites, effectively partition the chain into p=c+1 independent non-adjacent segments. Each segment of length ji contributes Fji valid configurations, according to the fundamental Fibonacci counting established for the ground state in Equation (9). To preserve the total system size, the lengths of these segments must sum to the effective volume:(32)V=N−b−k−c, where the subtracted terms explicitly account for the fixed spatial footprint of the boundary sites, the excited pair sites, and the cluster-boundary sites.

5.5.4. The Fibonacci Convolution and Its Boundary Extension

The total number of ways to distribute this effective volume among p segments, with each ji≥1, is governed by the p-fold Fibonacci convolution:(33)Cp(V)=∑j1+⋯+jp=Vji≥1∏i=1pFji.This standard convolution captures the behavior of purely internal defect clusters. However, when a cluster lies adjacent to an excited boundary (b≥1), the corresponding edge segment can collapse to length zero or even −1. Physically, this indicates the spatial merging of the boundary excitation and a bulk cluster, eliminating the non-adjacent segment between them. Utilizing the analytic continuation of the Fibonacci sequence (F−1=1, F0=0) [47], we define the boundary-extended convolution as(34)C˜p(b)(V)=∑j1+⋯+jp=Vji(edge)≥−1,ji(int)≥1∏i=1pFji, where ji(edge) denotes the segments adjacent to excited boundaries (the first segment for b≥1, and additionally the last segment for b=2), while ji(int) denotes all strictly interior segments. To ascertain the rigorous upper bound for j1, all other segments must be minimized: the p−2 interior segments contribute a minimum value of 1, while the final edge segment jp contributes a minimum of −1. Neglecting this correction systematically undercounts configurations where both boundary clusters fully merge with their respective excited boundaries, an effect that becomes dominant for higher excitations (m≥6).

The general pattern dictates that the chain spectrum contains exactly 2N−1 accessible macroscopic levels distributed across the indices m∈{0,1,…,2N−3}∪{2N}, resulting in a strict spectral gap spanning m=2N−2 and m=2N−1. This exclusion is physically intuitive: the fully polarized state (all spins pointing up) inherently possesses b=2 boundary excitations and k=N−1 bulk adjacent pairs. According to the Diophantine relation, this yields an absolute maximum excitation index of m=b+2k=2+2(N−1)=2N. It is topologically impossible to arrange excitations to achieve exactly m=2N−2 or m=2N−1, as introducing a single down-spin into the fully polarized state necessarily breaks multiple adjacent bonds simultaneously, dropping the index directly to a maximum of m=2N−3. Consequently, this establishes the fundamental property that Ω2Nchain(N)=1, corresponding to the unique configuration where all spins align upwards. This unified formulation successfully reproduces the derived low-energy states, providing systematic consistency checks. For the ground state (m=0), the sole class is (0,0), cleanly returning Ω0=FN. For the first excited state (m=1), the unique class (1,0) correctly recovers Ω1=2FN−1. For m=2, evaluating the classes (0,1) and (2,0) precisely reproduces Ω2=C˜2(0)(N−1)+FN−2. Nevertheless, the explicit evaluation of these nested convolutions C˜p(b) for large m becomes combinatorially complex, effectively motivating the algebraic transfer-matrix approach developed in the subsequent section.

6. Exact Algebraic Generalization via Transfer Matrix

6.1. Construction of the Transfer Matrix

While the explicit summation of topological clusters provides deep physical insight into the fractionalization of the critical manifold, expanding the nested discrete convolutions for an arbitrary m-th excited state becomes combinatorially intractable. To achieve a strictly programmable and universally exact generalization, we map the one-dimensional topological defect onto an algebraic generating function using the transfer matrix formalism [2,40]. It is useful to distinguish the role of the transfer matrix in the present work from recent transfer-matrix studies of finite antiferromagnetic Ising chains. For example, Karlová and Strečka [41] used the transfer-matrix method to derive and compare thermodynamic properties of finite open and periodic antiferromagnetic Ising chains. Here, by contrast, the transfer matrix is used as a generating function in a formal variable y, whose coefficients directly extract the microcanonical, level-resolved degeneracies at the critical field B/J=2. Thus, the two approaches are conceptually related but address different objects: canonical thermodynamic response in the former case and exact critical density-of-states coefficients in the present case.

The energy gap relative to the ground state at B/J=2 is governed by the local topological score m=b+2k. We introduce a formal generating-function variable y to track these excitation units. In the basis {|↓〉,|↑〉}, the transfer matrix T encodes the local Boltzmann weights between adjacent sites i and i+1:(42)T=111y2.

Here, the entries T↓↑=T↑↓=T↓↓=1 reflect that these pairs create no additional bulk frustration. Conversely, T↑↑=y2 accounts for the two excitation units contributed by a bulk adjacent pair. Fractional excitations at the boundaries must be treated independently. An up-spin at either boundary contributes exactly 1 unit to the excitation score, carrying a weight of y. Down-spins at the boundaries carry a weight of 1. This establishes the left and right boundary state vectors as vL=1y and vR=1yT. The canonical generating polynomial for an open chain of N sites is the exact contraction of the boundary vectors with the (N−1)-th power of the transfer matrix:(43)ZNchain(y)=vLTN−1vR.The macroscopic degeneracy of the m-th excited state is then mathematically extracted as the exact coefficient of the ym term:(44)Ωmchain(N)=ymZNchain(y).

To extract closed-form analytical expressions, the matrix power is resolved via diagonalization, T=PΛP−1. The eigenvalues are determined by the characteristic equation det(T−λI)=0, which yields(45)λ±=1+y2±Δ(y)2,Δ(y)≡y4−2y2+5.The corresponding right eigenvectors are v±=(1,λ±−1)T. The diagonalization renders the matrix power trivial (TN−1=PΛN−1P−1). Substituting this back into Equation (43), the chain generating function becomes a sum of two geometric terms:(46)ZNchain(y)=A+(y)λ+N−1+A−(y)λ−N−1, where the boundary-dependent amplitudes are given by A±(y)=(vL·v±)(v±−1·vR), with v±−1 denoting the corresponding row of P−1. Substituting this closed-form expression into Equation (44) yields the exact analytical degeneracies of the open chain. For the closed-ring topology with periodic boundary conditions, the generating function is simply the trace of the transfer matrix:(47)ZNring(y)=Tr(TN)=λ+N+λ−N, and the corresponding macroscopic degeneracy is extracted as(48)Ωkring(N)=[y2k](λ+N+λ−N).Ultimately, this algebraic formulation intrinsically absorbs all discrete Fibonacci convolutions, automatically computing every valid spatial partition without requiring piecewise cases. For arbitrary polynomial extraction, this method resolves the entire critical spectrum in logarithmic computational time, proving the exact isomorphism between the macroscopic critical degeneracies and the generating polynomial of local fractional defects. Furthermore, leveraging these exact analytical degeneracies and energies, the specific heat and entropy for both topologies are plotted in the Appendix B. As observed, both systems exhibit a zero-temperature entropy proportional to kBln[Ω0(N)], governed by the Fibonacci sequence for the open chain and the Lucas sequence for the closed ring. We emphasize that this generating-function inversion is complementary to the thermal inverse-Laplace route: by tracking excitations through a formal variable y rather than only through the Boltzmann factor e−β, the formalism reduces the level-resolved counting problem to the extraction of polynomial coefficients. In this precise sense, the transfer matrix acts here as an exact microcanonical generating function for the critical density-of-states coefficients at B/J=2.

7. Exact Degeneracies for the Ring Topology

The combinatorial analysis for the one-dimensional Ising ring fundamentally differs from the open chain due to periodic boundary conditions (σN+1≡σ1), which inherently eliminate fractional boundary excitations. Consequently, the excitation spectrum is governed exclusively by bulk adjacent pairs incurring an energy cost of ΔE=4J. For the m-th excited state, the m bulk defects organize into c contiguous clusters, partitioning the ring into exactly c antiferromagnetic arcs over an effective volume Veff=N−m−c. Factoring in the composition of these clusters, m−1c−1, and the circular rotational symmetry, N/c, the exact macroscopic degeneracy condenses into a single closed-form piecewise expression. Similar to the open chain, the finite dimension of the Hilbert space strictly bounds the spectrum, isolating the absolute maximum excitation of the fully polarized ring (m=N) as a unique configuration:(49)Ωmring(N)=LN,m=0,∑c=1mm−1c−1NcCc(N−m−c),1≤m<N,1,m=NThis structural formulation demonstrates that the absence of boundary defects rigorously simplifies the partition sum. It eliminates the need for boundary-extended convolutions (C˜p(b)), ensuring the convolution order precisely matches the cluster count. Explicit low-energy cases naturally emerge from this expression—such as Ω1ring(N)=NFN−2—and strictly agree with the algebraic trace evaluated via the transfer matrix formalism, Ωmring(N)=[y2m]Tr(TN). A consequence derived from Equation (49) is that the penultimate excitation level of the closed ring exhibits exactly zero degeneracy (ΩN−1ring=0). Physically, this implies that the system is strictly forbidden from occupying this specific state, resulting in a non-trivial spectral gap of 8J just below the highest accessible energy level. This topological restriction arises because achieving the penultimate excitation requires exactly k=N↑↑=N−1 adjacent up-spin pairs. However, introducing a single down-spin into the fully polarized ferromagnetic state (k=N) necessarily breaks two adjacent bonds, dropping the number of pairs directly to k=N−2. As a result, the geometric constraint of the closed ring makes the condition k=N−1 topologically impossible. Therefore, no physical microstate exists at this energy level, an exclusion strictly predicted by the combinatorial formula yielding zero degeneracy. Ultimately, this establishes the fundamental property that ΩNring(N)=1, corresponding to the unique configuration where all spins align upwards. Notably, the distinct ground-state degeneracies inherent to each topology manifest as clear differences in the zero-temperature residual entropy. Such pronounced macroscopic degeneracies hold significant potential for the design of discrete quantum heat machines, as the entropic shifts associated with traversing these critical manifolds can be strategically leveraged to substantially enhance heat jumps. 8. Conclusions

In this manuscript, we have established exact combinatorial expressions governing the complete excitation spectrum of the one-dimensional antiferromagnetic Ising model at the level-crossing (LC) point B/J=2. By systematically analyzing topological frustration and non-adjacency constraints, we demonstrated that the macroscopic degeneracies of both open chains and periodic rings share a unifying mathematical architecture. To the best of our knowledge, this provides the first explicit level-resolved microcanonical combinatorial construction of the critical excitation spectrum of the antiferromagnetic one-dimensional Ising model at B/J=2, including both open-chain and periodic-ring topologies. This statement should be understood in this specific critical-field and microcanonical sense, complementing previous general density-of-states and transfer-matrix treatments of the one-dimensional Ising model [37,38,39,41].

Specifically, the density of states is constructed through integer compositions of topological defects weighted by Fibonacci convolutions. The closed ring strictly follows standard convolutions rooted in the Lucas sequence, whereas the open chain incorporates boundary-extended convolutions to account for fractional edge excitations. Both structural frameworks have been independently verified through algebraic extraction via the transfer-matrix formalism and exact computational enumeration. Furthermore, this rigorous state counting exposes nontrivial spectral gaps near the fully polarized limit. Dictated by the topological constraints of breaking adjacent bonds, our analytical formulas strictly predict vanishing degeneracies for the two macroscopic levels immediately below the fully polarized state in the open chain and the single penultimate level in the closed ring, physically forbidding the system from occupying these specific states.

Beyond the exact counting itself, the present density of states has several direct physical uses. First, it allows the residual and finite-temperature entropies of the critical manifold to be computed without numerical reconstruction. Second, it provides exact benchmark data for enumeration algorithms, Wang–Landau sampling, flat-histogram methods, and transfer-matrix implementations. Third, the boundary-sensitive distinction between open and periodic topologies suggests a route for extending this microcanonical construction to related one-dimensional spin models, frustrated chains, and lattice geometries in which boundary defects and local constraints reorganize the excitation spectrum.

Ultimately, these results reveal that the entire critical manifold inherits the recursive Fibonacci structure of the ground state. The derived exact formulas bypass combinatorial intractability, providing a rigorous analytical foundation for exploring finite-size scaling and the exact thermodynamic limits of discrete spin systems at quantum criticality. By analytically reconstructing the density of states, the exact critical entropy for every accessible energy level can be directly obtained. Future work should aim to investigate the excited states associated with even degeneracies and explore the extension of this combinatorial formalism to other spin models and lattice topologies.
