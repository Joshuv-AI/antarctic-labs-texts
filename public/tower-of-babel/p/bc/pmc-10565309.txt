Community intervention to improve defibrillation before ambulance arrival in residential neighbourhoods with a high risk of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest: study protocol of a cluster-randomised trial (the CARAMBA trial)
Authors: Anne Juul Grabmayr, Carolina Malta Hansen, Nanna Bo, Annam Pervez Sheikh, Christian Hassager, Annette Ersbøll, Jesper Kjaergaard, Freddy Lippert, Tine Tjørnhøj-Thomsen, Gunnar Gislason, Christian Torp-Pedersen, Fredrik Folke
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
IntroductionIn Denmark, multiple national initiatives have been associated with improved bystander defibrillation and survival following out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) in public places. However, OHCAs in residential neighbourhoods continue to have poor outcomes. The Cardiac Arrest in Residential Areas with MoBile volunteer responder Activation trial aims to improve bystander defibrillation and survival following OHCA in residential neighbourhoods with a high risk of OHCA. The intervention consists of: (1) strategically deployed automated external defibrillators accessible at all hours, (2) cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) training of residents and (3) recruitment of residents for a volunteer responder programme.Methods and analysisThis is a prospective, pair-matched, cluster-randomised, superiority trial with clusters of 26 residential neighbourhoods, testing the effectiveness of the intervention in a real-world setting. The areas are randomised for intervention or control. Intervention and control areas will receive the standard OHCA emergency response, including volunteer responder activation. However, targeted automated external defibrillator deployment, CPR training and volunteer responder recruitment will only be provided in the intervention areas. The primary outcome is bystander defibrillation, and the secondary outcome is 30-day survival. Data on patients who had an OHCA will be collected through the Danish Cardiac Arrest Registry.Ethics and disseminationApproval to store OHCA data has been granted from the Legal Office, Capital Region of Denmark (j.nr: 2012-58-0004, VD-2018-28, I-Suite no: 6222, and P-2021-670). In Denmark, formal approval from the ethics committee is only obtainable when the study regards testing medicine or medical equipment on humans or using genome or diagnostic imagine as data source. The Ethics Committee of the Capital Region of Denmark has evaluated the trial and waived formal approval unnecessary (H-19037170). Results will be published in peer-reviewed papers and shared with funders, stakeholders, and housing organisations through summaries and presentations.Trial registration numberClinicalTrials.gov Registry (NCT04446585).

Introduction

Early defibrillation and cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) are pivotal for high survival rates following out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA).1 2 In Denmark, ≈5000 OHCAs occur annually.3 The majority of OHCAs occur in residential neighbourhoods, with 75% of OHCAs occurring in private homes and 50% of all OHCAs in public occurring in residential neighbourhoods.4 5 Multiple initiatives have been implemented to improve survival, such as mandatory CPR training when obtaining a driver’s licence and in elementary school; dispatcher-assisted CPR; the Danish automated external defibrillator (AED) network linked to all emergency medical dispatch centres; and a nationwide volunteer responder programme for presumed OHCAs.6–11 In Denmark, bystander defibrillation has improved to ≈20% when OHCA occurs in public, but bystander defibrillation in private home arrests is still rare at ≈5%.3 Among those who receive bystander defibrillation, survival rates are approximately 58% for public locations and 27% in private homes, respectively.12 13 The European Resuscitation Council (ERC), the American Heart Association (AHA) and the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR) have recently highlighted the importance of developing innovative strategies to target OHCA in high-risk residential settings.14–16

Prior studies

The Public Access Defibrillation Trial from 2004 compared survival following OHCA in private homes and public locations with text message-activated volunteer responders trained in CPR only or CPR and AED use. The trial found improved survival in public locations but no changes in private homes.17 At-home AEDs assigned to patients with prior acute myocardial infarction and not eligible for an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator have also shown no effect on survival.18 Since these trials, smartphone applications for activating and guiding volunteer responders to the scene of the OHCA have been thoroughly implemented in Denmark and many other countries. The association between volunteer responders and improved outcomes has been reported, but randomised trials reporting a causal survival benefit are lacking.11 19–21

Study aim

The CARAMBA (Cardiac Arrest in Residential Areas with MoBile volunteer responder Activation) trial targets residential neighbourhoods with a high incidence of OHCA. It aims to determine if an intervention consisting of (1) strategic deployment of 24-hour accessible AEDs, (2) training residents in CPR and AED use, and (3) recruiting volunteer responders is superior to the standard emergency medical response (described under the Study setting section).

Study setting

The study takes place in residential neighbourhoods within the Capital Region of Denmark, covering approximately 2600 km2 (1000 square miles) and with a resident population of ≈1.8 million inhabitants.25 The Capital Region of Denmark has one emergency medical dispatch centre. When suspecting an OHCA, the dispatcher alerts a two-tiered system consisting of an ambulance and a physician-staffed mobile emergency care unit. Using the national Danish AED network, the dispatcher can guide bystanders to collect the nearest accessible AED, if feasible.10 The dispatcher also instructs bystanders in CPR by voice or smartphone-assisted video transmission.26 In case of suspected OHCA, the dispatcher can activate registered volunteer responders through an app-based alarm system, though not in traumatic OHCA, suicide or unsafe surroundings.11 Within a radius of 5000 m, the 20 nearest volunteer responders receive an alarm and, once accepted, one of two assignments: (1) fetch the nearest AED or (2) go straight to the scene and perform CPR. The app will show a map of the shortest route to fulfil the assignment.11 The above-described emergency response is from hereon referred to as standard care.

Study design

The CARAMBA trial is an investigator-initiated, prospective, cluster-randomised, superiority trial that will compare the proportion of patients who receive bystander defibrillation in geographically defined intervention and control areas. The secondary outcome will be 30-day survival. The study has been designed to investigate the effectiveness of community intervention in a real-world setting.27 28 Twenty-six residential neighbourhoods are pair matched and allocated 1:1 to intervention or control. The intervention serves as a supplement to standard care.

Trial population

The trial population is patients with bystander-witnessed OHCA within 26 geographically defined areas. The exclusion criteria are (1) <8 years of age; (2) trauma, intoxication or suicide; and (3) not treated by emergency medical services (EMS) due to ethical reasons or obvious signs of death. The age limit corresponds to the ERC’s guidelines on when to use adult pads for defibrillating children.29

Data sources used for planning this study

Population density and demographics

Population density and the average age of inhabitants were obtained as grid cells (100×100 m) with geolocations from Statistics Denmark.30

The Danish Cardiac Arrest Registry

The Danish Cardiac Arrest Registry collects data on OHCA cases, including patient characteristics, OHCA location, intervention before and after ambulance arrival, and survival. In the Capital Region of Denmark, all OHCA cases are validated by medical doctors. To identify study sites and conduct a power calculation, we included OHCA data from the Capital Region of Copenhagen from 2016 to 2018 and OHCA data from the Copenhagen Municipality from 1994 to 2015.

The Danish AED network

Information regarding existing AEDs was obtained from the Danish AED network. All AEDs with 24-hour access on 14 April 2020 were included.

Selection of study sites

We initially deployed existing methods for identifying high-risk areas based on historical OHCAs, population density and demographic characteristics as recommended by ILCOR.4 15 This method identified areas on a map with a high risk of OHCA but provided no clear point of entry for the intervention as to who should approve and maintain the AEDs, and how to recruit residents for CPR training and the volunteer responder programme. As implementation is a core element when creating an impactful community intervention, we added a criterion for the study sites; the areas must consist of a housing unit with employees and organisational structures for residents.27 This approach provided a clear intervention point and minimised the number of collaborators while reaching large communities. The intervention became anchored in existing structures and communication channels.

Thus, the study sites have been selected based on the following criteria: (1) high population density of people above 60 years, (2) shared social infrastructure and (3) large housing units.

Using QGIS V.3 (QGIS Development Team, 2020, Geographic Information System, Open Source Geospatial Foundation: http://qgis.org), each study site was marked as a polygon with coordinates, including all communal facilities such as parks and shared buildings. Further, Google Search was used to identify nursing homes within the areas to be excluded from the final polygon.

Description of study sites

A total of 26 areas have been selected, each covering 600–5000 inhabitants, ranging in size from 0.04 to 0.77 km2 and encompassing 2–7 OHCAs per year (figure 1). The mean OHCA incidence rate in the 26 selected residential neighbourhoods is 64 OHCA/100 000 inhabitants/year. Twenty-five out of 26 areas are social housing units; the last is privately owned rental apartments. High-rise buildings, high population density, low socioeconomic status and a high proportion of immigrants characterise the areas included.

Contamination, pair matching and randomisation

To decrease the risk that volunteer responders from intervention areas responded to OHCAs in control areas, we chose a minimum distance of 1000 m between intervention and control areas. Danish volunteer responders travel on average 543 m and rarely more than 1000 m to the OHCA location in straight-line distances.11 A greater distance was explored but deemed not feasible since too many study sites would overlap.

To improve power and balance baseline characteristics, the clusters were matched in pairs.31 Within the pairs, the clusters were randomised to either intervention or control. Variables used for pair matching were average age, number of inhabitants and pre-existing AED/km2.31 32 Areas closer than 1000 m to each other were restricted from becoming a pair as they should receive the same randomisation to prevent contamination. By using distance matrices for each selected variable, pairs were created with the lowest possible sum of differences.33 The pairs were reviewed manually to quality control the matching.

In three cases, two areas were closer than 1000 m to each other. The R program used for the randomisation ensured that these areas received the same randomisation.

Due to the nature of the intervention, it is not possible to blind the investigators to intervention or control areas. The researchers will be blinded to whether a patient belongs to an intervention or control area during the statistical analysis.

Data collection

To improve power and shorten the study time, 250-metre buffer zones were added to the clusters where volunteer responders will be activated, and OHCA cases included. Data collection began once all AEDs were deployed and registered with the Danish AED network. There will be ongoing CPR courses and recruitment of volunteer responders throughout the study period in the intervention areas. Data on bystander defibrillation and 30-day survival will be collected through the Danish Cardiac Arrest Registry, and cases with missing outcome variables will be excluded. This registry is a quality improvement registry with observational data, and by Danish laws, no ethical approval or informed patient consent is required for retrospective studies. All involved researchers will be blinded to the validation of data. Data will be stored at an encrypted server and in accordance with Danish data safety regulations.

Data from this study are structured in three levels, with patients suffering OHCA as level 1. All patients belong to a cluster (housing unit), which is data level 2. All clusters belong to a pair of clusters. The pairs are level 3 (figure 2).

Description of intervention

This complex community intervention is designed to enable an AED to reach the patient within a few minutes.

Strategic deployment of AEDs

AEDs were deployed as part of the intervention to meet the following criteria: accessible 24 hours a day, easily located and most households should have an AED within ≈100 m.10 34 35 The study sites already had 77 AEDs with 24-hour access before our intervention. When considering existing AEDs, 75 additional AEDs were needed to cover all areas within 100 m. Once a year, the research team will assess whether pre-existing AEDs and CARAMBA AEDs are still active on the Danish AED network. If not, the housing organisation will be contacted, and a new AED will be deployed if needed. All AEDs are linked to the EMS dispatch centre through the Danish AED network. OHCA occurrence on ≥3rd floor has been associated with a worse outcome compared with <3rd floor.36 It was practically impossible to place AEDs inside elevators due to: (1) elevators being so small that an AED would interfere with other safety requirements; and (2) in case the elevator was out of order or occupied, the AED would be inaccessible too. Instead, AEDs were placed at the ground level entrance of buildings with >5 floors.

Training residents in CPR and AED use

All residents and employees in intervention areas will be invited to participate in 30-minute hands-on ‘Save a Life’ courses in CPR and AED use conducted by the Danish Heart Foundation and approved by the Danish Resuscitation Council.8 37 Existing instructors from the Danish Heart Foundation will conduct the initial courses. Participating residents will be recruited as instructors during these courses. They will be offered to participate in a 2-day instructor course provided by the Danish Heart Foundation and be certified to conduct 30-minute hands-on ‘Save a Life’ courses. Further, they will be instructed to recruit volunteer responders and new instructors in relation to the courses they conduct in the residential neighbourhoods. By having locally anchored and voluntary instructors, we nurture the longevity and feasibility of the project as the instructors understand the context they are working within and have easy access to social structures and communication channels in the areas.27 38 Further, trained lay people can provide high-quality CPR training.39 We aim to recruit instructors with language skills that reflect the languages spoken in the different housing organisations. If an intervention area fails to recruit instructors or an instructor is unavailable, instructors from other areas will be offered those courses.

Recruitment of volunteer responders

At the end of each 30-minute course, participants will be introduced to the volunteer responder programme and encouraged to sign up. Some potential volunteer responders, such as healthcare professionals, may not have time or find participating in a CPR course relevant. To reach a broader audience, we will also run campaigns for the volunteer responder programme through newsletters, bulletin boards, websites and social media.

Patient and public involvement and sustainability

For the intervention to be feasible and long-term sustainable, residents must take ownership of the project and participate in the planning, implementation and execution of the intervention.27 38 Before implementation, we planned to recruit residents through social media to join a resident board to guide and inform the intervention. We planned to discuss the intervention, communication and recruitment strategies, and problem arising as we implemented them. This strategy proved not feasible since it would have bypassed the existing decisional structures in the housing associations and led to extra work for the stakeholders. Instead, we stayed within the existing structures of the housing associations.

When we contacted the housing associations through email, we got in contact with either (a) the leader of the administrative staff; (b) the chairman of the resident board; or (c) a social worker employed by the housing associations to enable support groups, courses and events in the areas. These people accepted to take ownership of the project, helped us navigate each area, and got in contact with residents and relevant employees. The intervention and the implementation are evaluated with stakeholders, instructors and residents in each area ad hoc. When needed, the intervention is adapted to the current local context. Maintaining a certain level of flexibility in the intervention allows for a realistic implementation in a real-world setting and enables the transferability of the intervention to other settings.27 When challenges in one area occur, problems and solutions are discussed with stakeholders and instructors, and inspiration may be found from other programmes in the housing unit or experiences with CARAMBA from other intervention sites. All adjustments are made while keeping the central elements of the intervention intact.27 28 40

As this study investigates if this intervention can improve outcomes in a real-world setting, it is also crucial to consider scalability and financial cost as they may hinder broader dissemination after the trial has ended. To keep costs at a minimum, we include residents as voluntary instructors and housing association employees who distribute flyers, posters and social media campaigns free of charge. The social housing associations also supply electricity for the AED cabinets and, when needed, catering for course activities. AEDs and cabinets were granted by Zoll Foundation.

We intend that the intervention over time will be conducted by the housing association and its residents with minimal effort from the research team. One year after implementation, 5 out of 13 housing associations arrange courses by their initiative or after an inspirational email from the research team. The remaining housing associations get assistance with communication material and arranging courses. To help broaden the interpretation of the results of this trial, the research team wrote a research logbook describing all steps in the intervention, interactions with local stakeholders and decisions made along the way. The aim of the logbook is to understand potential differences between study sites and identify barriers and facilitators before disseminating the intervention beyond the trial setting. Further, to get an insight into other effects, the trial may have involved neighbourhoods.27

Implementation in the communities

Community involvement

The outreach to gatekeepers varied across sites. Generally, the leader of administrative staff helped with deploying AEDs, but in some areas, they also helped with planning and conducting CPR courses. In other areas, the chairman of the resident’s board helped with all steps of the AED and CPR course implementation. Social workers helped implement course activities in some areas but not AED deployment.

By educating CPR instructors living within the areas, the instructors become active in the intervention by adapting the intervention to the local context, recruiting more residents and arranging CPR courses.

The implementation follows the theory of change (see online supplemental file A), which describes every step necessary for the intervention to succeed, every assumption made and the key uncertainties. It serves to identify challenges and explain the findings of the study. It is a dynamic tool that will be developed with input from stakeholders and residents on an ongoing basis during the implementation, recruitment and trial phase.27 41

Statistics

Clustering effect

Observations within a cluster in a cluster-randomised trial may be correlated. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) quantifies the degree of correlation of observations within clusters compared with the correlation of observations between clusters. It has values between 0 and 1. The higher the ICC, the more correlated the observations within the clusters, and more observations are needed to achieve the sought-after power.32 42 No prior trials could provide an ICC for our intervention, and a pilot study was not feasible due to the rarity of OHCA. We estimated ICC based on historical OHCA data using the proportion of bystander defibrillation and variables relevant to the outcome (age and bystander-witnessed status). ICC was calculated in R.43 The ICC for defibrillation was <0.001, for bystander-witnessed status 0.001, and for age 0.026, suggesting that the observations within clusters are correlated only to a limited degree.44 We chose a conservative ICC of 0.10 for the CARAMBA trial to avoid underestimating the power.

Varying cluster sizes

Due to the rarity of OHCA, the clusters are expected to have varying sizes. Prior studies suggest considering varying cluster sizes when the coefficient of variability (CV), being the variance of mean cluster size, is greater than 0.23.42 45

In this study, the CV of the expected cluster sizes is 0.58. After pair matching, CV is replaced with variances of cluster means within the pairs and ranges from 0.00 to 0.24, allowing us not to consider varying cluster sizes when conducting power calculations.32 45

Sample size

From 2016 to 2018, 167 witnessed OHCAs occurred within the areas in which one received bystander defibrillation. Based on prior evidence and assessed clinical relevance, we will strive to improve the proportion of patients with bystander-witnessed OHCA who receive bystander defibrillation from 1% to 10%.46 With the desired power of 80% and a significance level of 5%, power calculations were conducted with varying ICCs and effect sizes to determine a conservative sample size estimation (see online supplemental material B). We estimate study duration of 5 years which should ensure 260 OHCAs.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis for this trial will be conducted as a stratified (matched) logistic regression with an exact score test of the intervention taking the cluster design into consideration. The data structure is multilevel, with the three levels being pairs of areas, areas randomised to intervention group, and finally, patients with OHCA (figure 2). Data will be aggregated at cluster level, as number of patients with OHCA and number of patients with outcome events. In the stratified logistic regression, the number of outcome events out of the number of OHCAs within an area is an event/trial outcome. The pair of clusters is the strata variable. Intervention is a fixed effect. An exact score test of the effect of the intervention on the outcome will be used.

For the primary outcome, we will conduct the above-described stratified logistic regression with an exact score test of intervention. The event/trial outcome is the number of primary events out of the number of OHCAs. The results will be presented as ORs.

The secondary and exploratory categorical outcomes will be analysed using the same statistical procedure described for the primary outcome. The event/trial outcome is the number of secondary or exploratory events out of the number of OHCAs. Time to defibrillation will be subject to a time-to-event analysis using Poisson regression of incidence rates including clusters as a fixed effect.

The observations are assumed to be independent as we expect no relation between OHCA cases. Aggregation of data at cluster level and using a stratified analysis account for the hierarchical data structure.

Trial status

All 26 study sites have been recruited, the AEDs have been deployed and registered at the Danish AED network, and resuscitation training is ongoing in all housing units. Recruitment of patients with OHCA began in December 2021, and all areas were active by February 2022. Patient recruitment is expected to finish in January 2027.

Discussion

The CARAMBA trial is a pragmatic cluster-randomised trial investigating community interventions targeted at high-risk residential neighbourhoods to improve bystander defibrillation and survival after OHCA. Patient follow-up is conducted through the existing Danish Cardiac Arrest Registry.

The ERC, AHA and ILCOR have warranted knowledge on identifying high-risk residential areas and developing targeted interventions to improve OHCA survival in residential areas.14–16 Prior studies have proven that it is possible to identify high-risk residential areas using historical OHCA and demographic data, and these models are also recommended by ILCOR.4 15 47–49 When planning this study, we found that these models offer no framework for the implementation of interventions. Pragmatic implementation is crucial when designing a community intervention cost-effectively and feasibly.27 Therefore, we used large housing units as a criterion as they offer existing organisational structures needed for implementation. The AHA has further warranted knowledge on how to develop targeted interventions for socioeconomically disadvantaged populations.14 50 Twenty-five out of 26 of the identified housing units proved to be social housing. By using social housing units, we, by design, target a population with a low income, low educational levels and a high proportion of immigrants compared with the average population.51 These factors are all predictors for an increased incidence of OHCA, low levels of bystander interventions and low survival levels compared with the average population.52–57 More research is needed to investigate if targeting social housing units could be a pragmatic way to identify high-risk residential neighbourhoods beyond the context of the CARAMBA trial.

By developing a community intervention and testing its effect using cluster randomisation, we can investigate the intervention in a real-world setting.27 32 The complex community intervention design allows for flexibility which is crucial when implementing an intervention in the real world and not in a controlled environment. By collaborating with the housing organisations and using volunteers as resuscitation instructors, we minimise economic costs, which is crucial for the transferability of the study.27

Bystander defibrillation in the study areas was <1% when planning this study. Denmark’s overall bystander defibrillation before ambulance arrival was 8.7% in 2020.3 Thus, reaching defibrillation at 10% among witnessed OHCA in residential neighbourhoods (in public and private homes) is optimistic but not unrealistic.

Limitations

OHCA is a rare event making randomised trials time-consuming. We have attempted to shorten the patient recruitment time by using residential neighbourhoods with a high incidence of OHCA compared with the remaining housing areas. Further, using bystander defibrillation before ambulance arrival as a proxy for survival enables us to reach the necessary power with fewer cases.

This intervention is implemented in a real-world setting, and a control area may deploy AEDs and arrange courses, diluting the measured effect of our intervention. A recent randomised controlled trial from Sweden allocated volunteer responders to perform CPR only or retrieve a nearby AED.58 The study was insignificant, and the authors suspected crossover and a high baseline of AED attachment to be the cause. By using cluster randomisation instead of individual randomisation, we limit the risk of crossover. Further, the included areas have a very low baseline use of AEDs in contrast to the study in Sweden. The cluster randomisation of community interventions is the best way to test an intervention in a real-world setting. We will gain crucial knowledge on creating interventions for high-risk residential neighbourhoods through planning and conducting the trial, irrespective of the results.

The recruitment of study areas took place during the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 and 2021. The recruitment process had to be on hold for several periods, and the areas had to be re-recruited after periods of COVID-19-related lockdowns. Even in periods without official COVID-19-related restrictions, we had challenges conducting courses because all activities in the housing associations had been cancelled. Currently, the trial is not affected by COVID-19, but it may change again. Extended periods without course activities may dilute the effect of the intervention and lead to prolonged patient recruitment.
