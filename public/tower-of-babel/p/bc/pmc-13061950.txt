Rock layer deformation analysis and mitigation at fault-crossing mining sites in RCRM operations
Authors: Yang Dongshan, Liang Bo, Kuai Xiaohui, Wang Haohao, Yin Songyang, Zhang Shukun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
When dealing with cross-fault mining operations, geological faults pose significant challenges to by unsettling the existing stress conditions, causing lopsided deformation of the surrounding rock, sudden shifts in displacement, and creating hazards such as fault slippage that really hinder the application of the application of Roof Cutting and Retaining by Mining technology. Drawing upon the 11,101 working face at Qipanjing Coal Mine as its foundation, this research brings together theoretical analysis, finite element modelling, and field verification to get to the bottom of the complex dynamics at play. Based on key stratum and voussoir beam theories, a three-stage mechanical model for fault-crossing mining was established. Drawing upon fundamental stratum mechanics and voussoir beam theory, researchers formulated a three-stage mechanical framework for mining across fault lines. This model demonstrates that advanced stress initially plummets as a result of the "fault stress barrier effect" before gradually rebounding once the working face withdraws from the immediate vicinity. Using the double-yield model to simulate caved gangue characteristics, and introducing deviatoric stress to compare three slotting heights, the study found that higher slotting heights reduce surrounding rock distortion energy and compressive deformation intensity, with 10 m achieving optimal economic efficiency (peak deviatoric stress invariant reduced by 24.8 MPa2, overlying strata displacement by 0.11 m, fault displacement by 0.04 m). A “slotting + loose blasting” scheme was proposed. On-site verification showed it cuts hydraulic support force by 15.3% and gangue bulking coefficient by 4.3%, providing technical support for RCRM application in fault-developed areas.

Introduction

The Roof Cutting and Retaining by Mining (RCRM) technology utilizes caved gangue for automatic filling to form roadways, and it boasts advantages such as improving resource recovery rate, reducing surrounding rock stress concentration, and lowering roadway maintenance costs1–4. However, with the continuous exploitation of resources, faults, among the most ubiquitous geological features encountered in mining operations, now present a formidable obstacle to both the safety and efficacy of this excavation method5,6.

Faults tend to have natural weak planes, which creates a stark contrast in stress field characteristics between fault zones and typical geological settings. On one hand, fault stress concentration may induce uneven rock deformation and dramatic displacement increases7–10; On the other hand, The contrasting mechanical characteristics observed in the hanging wall and footwall of a geological fault can serve as a catalyst for fault slippage, ultimately unleashing devastating high-energy catastrophes including rock bursts, intense ground pressure events, and widespread structural collapse of underground excavations11,12.

To investigate the disaster-causing mechanism of faults, relevant researchers have conducted extensive studies. Li et al.7 Developed systematic mechanical and numerical frameworks to pinpoint the correlation between fault slip motion, fault inclination, and frictional characteristics of fault planes, and subsequently deduced the essential prerequisites for fault sliding. Shan et al.13 developed a mechanical model for mining at working faces adjacent to fault-related roadways, findings indicated that the gap between the fault and the roadway is the chief determinant of stress concentration and the resulting plastic deformation in the surrounding rock. Zhang et al.14 obtained the occurrence mechanisms of three types of pillar-type impact ground pressure through the establishment of mechanical models. Onur Vardar et al.15 used UDEC (Universal Distinct Element Code) to investigate the influencing factors of rock bursts in fault-proximal roadways; the results showed that as mining depth increases, the total seismic energy and peak value generated near the roadway increase rapidly, raising the probability of rock burst occurrence. Sun et al.16 researched the mechanism by which different fault dip angles affect roadway instability under the condition of 1000 m deep mines, arguing that the uneven stress field formed in the shallow surrounding rock is a crucial factor leading to roadway instability.

Unlike traditional mining approaches, RCRM technology banks on the expansive characteristics of gangue to bear the weight of overlaying rock layers and create the tunnel necessary for the next extraction area. The system’s integrity hinges directly on this gangue, which in turn is affected by the pressure from the strata above. Given the intricate stress conditions typical of fault zones, it’s imperative to implement robust strategies for managing the deformation of surrounding rock. Current research approaches to surrounding rock control in faulted areas can be broadly categorized into three distinct tracks. The first is improving support strength. To tackle the significant deformation of surrounding rock triggered by intense stress disturbances from faults, engineers implement a range of countermeasures. These include beefing up the anchor cables’ support capacity and effectiveness, applying grouting and shotcrete techniques, and installing supplementary temporary supports to stabilize the area13,17–21; The second is reducing the energy generated and transmitted during fault slippage through technologies such as hydraulic pressure and slotting blasting, thereby lessening the interactional pressure exerted on the surrounding rock20; This final approach integrates enhancing rock mass support while mitigating fault displacement effects. For instance, Sun et al.16 implemented the control technique of “roof cutting + CRAC cable”, Fu et al.22 used the combined control scheme of “portal support + roof pre-cracking”, and Liu et al.23 proposed the supporting scheme of “asymmetric reinforcement support + roof directional blasting”. Although existing achievements have provided many useful explorations on surrounding rock control under fault structural conditions, research on the special control technologies for gob-side entry retaining in fault zones remains relatively limited, and the relevant theoretical and technical systems need to be further improved.

Although existing studies have initially explored the impact of faults on stope ground pressure and proposed corresponding control technology schemes, under the cross-fault mining scenario of RCRM technology, the changes in surrounding rock support stress and instability characteristics during the working face crossing the fault remain unclear, and effective control schemes are also lacking. This study aims to analyze the variation characteristics of surrounding rock support pressure and the evolution law of displacement during the working face crossing a fault by combining theoretical analysis, numerical simulation, and on-site experiments. A targeted control scheme is proposed based on the research on the bulking characteristics of gangue. The results of this study provide an effective technical scheme for the safe and efficient mining of RCRM working faces crossing faults.

Project overview

Currently, the main coal seam mined at the East Area of Qipanjing Coal Mine is the 9# coal seam. To enhance the recovery efficiency of resources and decrease the extent of roadway excavation, the RCRM technology is applied in the 11,101 working face, and the transportation roadway is used as the return airway for the next adjacent mining operation. As shown in Fig. 1, the roadway roof is reinforced with a combination of NPR (Negative Poisson’s Ratio) anchor cables and conventional anchor cables. At the same time, blasting holes for cutting cracks are arranged in the direction of the working face at the angle between the coal face support and the roof of the roadway. The average depth of the coal seam from the surface is 510 m, and its thickness ranges from 2.8 to 3.5 m. The direct roof and direct floor are respectively 8 m and 3.2 m thick sandy mudstone.Fig. 1Location of the working face fault.

Location of the working face fault.

Running parallel to the 11,101 working face’s mining direction, fault ZF44 exhibits a normal displacement of 4400 mm, striking at a 75° relative to the horizontal plane of excavation. Due to the existence of a gap between the faults, there is a slope at the intersection of the 11,101 transportation roadway and the fault; the clear height of the roadway in this section can reach 4.5 m, the temporary support effect is poor, and there is a risk of roadway collapse. The positional relationship between the working face and the fault is shown in Fig. 1.

Mining characteristics

Behavior of the RCRM working face passing through faults

During the fault-crossing mining process, the RCRM technology demonstrates unique advantages. When compared to the traditional longwall mining methods that grapple with stress concentration and rock deformation issues, this new technology cleverly severs the structural tie between the roadway and working face roofs by employing a pre-roof cutting strategy. At the same time, it capitalizes on the load-bearing capacity and bulk properties of gangue in the goaf, which substantially lessens the intensity of stress concentration.

This pioneering method not only reduces the plastic area around the roadway, but also minimizes the cantilever span of the upper structure, thereby enhancing the stability and safety of the roadway. Therefore, this technology enables the implementation of pillarless mining in geologically abnormal areas, improves coal recovery efficiency, and reduces mining costs.

As shown in Fig. 2a, the strike of the ZF44 fault forms an angle of approximately 75° with working face trend. Therefore, the process crossing the fault during mining is simplified as illustrated in Fig. 2b–d. According to the spatial separation from the fault to the operational area, the fault-crossing process of the RCRM working face can be divided into three key stages. Mining stage Ⅰ: The working face is mined on the upper part of the fault, and at the same time, it does not come into contact with the fault plane. Mining stage Ⅱ: The hydraulic support of the working face is located at the fault plane. Mining stage Ⅲ: The working face is mined beneath the fault and gradually moves away from it.Fig. 2Decomposition diagram of the RCRM working face over the fault.

With the ongoing advancement of mining operations, the breadth of the fault coal pillar steadily narrows, yet all other variables stay put. As a result, the cumulative pressure on the working face’s hydraulic supports and the fault coal pillar intensifies. Once this pressure surpasses the bearing capacity, issues like crushed hydraulic supports or the fault coal pillar succumbing to plastic deformation can arise. In the end, this excess pressure shifts onto the surrounding rock on either side of the mining passageways, thereby heightening the stress build-up in the rocks immediately in front and behind the working face.

Surrounding rock evaluation indicators

In underground engineering, if only the vertical stress distribution is focused on and the geotechnical mass is simplified as a uniaxially loaded material, the actual situation where the geotechnical mass is subjected to multi-directional stresses will be ignored. It is difficult to accurately determine the elastic deformation degree, strain state, and stability of the rock based on the vertical stress distribution29. In accordance with elastoplastic mechanics theory, the deformation degree and state of a material mainly depend on the stress deviator, whose magnitude can reflect the elastoplastic state of the engineering rock mass30. The surrounding rock stress results from combined in-situ and deviatoric stress components. The plastic failure exhibited by rock masses is fundamentally governed by deviatoric stress, which primarily dictates the resulting plastic deformation patterns. Consequently, a thorough investigation of the deviatoric stress tensor proves crucial for comprehending the evolution and expansion of plastic zones within various geotechnical formations.

Equation (17) clearly demonstrates that the first invariant of the stress deviator consistently equals zero, rendering it from a research perspective. In contrast, the second invariant of the stress deviator not only goes hand in hand with the distortional energy experienced by the material under stress but also shows a direct parallel with shear stress levels29. The stress and elastoplastic behavior of the geotechnical mass can be analyzed by examining the second invariant of the stress deviator. Similarly, in the field of mining engineering, rocks typically exhibit the characteristic of “being prone to compression but not tension”. When J3 is greater than zero, the element behaves as being in a tensile state; When J3 is equal to zero, the unit exhibits a planar state; and When J3 is less than zero, the unit exhibits a compressive state. The stress and elastoplastic behavior of the geotechnical mass can be analyzed by examining the second invariant of the stress deviator.

To get a handle on how the J2 and J3 are distributed backfilled goaf rock and in the advance stress influence zone under various bulking coefficients, while also examining the shear stress levels, distortional energy density, and strain patterns in the surrounding rock at different points as the hanging wall working face progresses, researchers must set up a stress monitoring line straight down the middle of the coal seam along the mining direction. This allows them to keep tabs on the maximum, minimum, and intermediate principal stresses affecting the surrounding rock. Once they’ve gathered these readings, they can plug the principal stress values into Eq. (17) to work out the corresponding J2 and J3.

Rock control scheme

Control mechanism

When the working face sits in close proximity to a fault, the support force applied to the fault’s hanging wall is a complex interplay of several elements, with the primary contributors being the gangue, the coal body within the hanging wall, and the rock mass in the footwall. The combined effect and load-bearing capacity of these three components work in concert to keep the fault’s hanging wall stable. As depicted in Fig. 16a, with a slot height of 8 m and the roof capable of filling the goaf upon collapse, the gangue’s bulking factor is substantial, indicating wide spaces between individual particles and minimal compaction in the accumulated material. Under these circumstances, the gangue pile’s ability to bear the weight of the overlying strata is considerably diminished. Consequently, as illustrated in Fig. 17, when the overlying strata come under pressure, a greater share of stress gets transferred to the coal mass in the fault’s hanging wall and the fault plane itself, leading to heightened stress concentrations in the surrounding rock on both flanks of the working face. As shown in Fig. 16b, to improve this situation, increasing the collapse height of the gangue can enhance the compactness of the gangue accumulation and reduce the porosity. At the same time, reducing the particle size of the gangue can further improve the compactness of the accumulation. These measures can effectively reduce the porosity of the gangue accumulation and enhance its load-bearing capacity. When the load-bearing capacity of the gangue accumulation is improved, the stress transmitted from the overlying strata to the surrounding rock ahead of the working face decreases. At the same time, the stress transfer from the fault’s hanging wall to the footwall formation and the disruption to the roadway passage are mitigated, which in turn lessens the extent of stress buildup and deformation in the surrounding rock of the underground passage.Fig. 16Distribution of abutment pressure in fault hanging wall rock mass.Fig. 17Stress map of fault surface.

Control scheme

Numerical simulation findings indicate that fault displacements measure 0.182 m, 0.143 m, and 0.142 m for slot heights of 8 m, 10 m, and 12 m respectively. The data clearly shows that increasing the slot height from 8 to 10 m yields the most substantial reduction in fault sliding displacement. Considering the factors of fault control effect and slotting cost comprehensively, the slot height in the fault structural zone is optimized to 10 m (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_{b\max } = 1.32$$\end{document}). Combined with the previous similar engineering geological conditions, the slot angle is finally determined to be 15°. Based on the angular calculations, a slot height of 10 m results in a slot hole length of 10.35 m. To streamline construction operations, the depth of the slot hole has been standardized at 10 m.

Based on the results of theoretical analysis and previous laboratory experiments33, under normal circumstances, the smaller the particle size of gangue formed after crushing, the smaller its bulking coefficient, and the higher its load-bearing capacity. To ensure the full collapse of gangue within the slot height range and reduce the particle size of the collapsed gangue (thereby decreasing the bulking coefficient of the collapsed gangue), loose blasting should be carried out within the slot height range. As shown in Fig. 18, on the basis of slot blasting, three loose blasting holes are arranged downward along the roadway shoulder angle. The vertical distances from the roadway shoulder angle are 1000 mm, 1500 mm, and 2000 mm respectively; the angles with the vertical line are 35°, 45°, and 55° respectively; and the lengths are 13 me, 16 m, and 21 m respectively. Engineering application

It’s clear from Fig. 19 that as the gap between the working face and the fault narrows, the hydraulic supports at the working face begin to provide more support. After the working face hydraulic supports cross the ZF44 fault, their supporting force decreases rapidly, showing an obvious stress barrier effect, which is consistent with the analysis results mentioned earlier. As the mining face advances into the coal deposit within the hanging wall, the barrier effect causes the vertical stress on both the supports at the working face and the coal ahead of these supports to be primarily derived from the vertical load transferred downward from the rock strata above the accumulated waste material in the hanging wall to the footwall. Therefore, the supporting force of the working face hydraulic supports decreases to a certain extent. During the entire fault-crossing period, the average supporting force of the 140# hydraulic support on the side where no slotting holes or loosening holes are applied is 30.1 MPa, and the average supporting force of the 5# hydraulic support on the side close to the retained roadway is 25.5 MPa. It can be seen that after adopting optimization and adjustment measures such as slotting and loosening holes, the supporting force of the working face hydraulic supports has decreased by 15.3%.Fig. 19Supporting force of the working face hydraulic support.

Supporting force of the working face hydraulic support.

As illustrated in Fig. 20, the gangue piled up on the fractured gangue wall side experiences progressive mutual compression under overlying strata pressure as the roadway–working face distance increases. Compressive failure initially occurs in the skeletal supporting sections of large gangue fragments, resulting in a reduction in the void ratio of the gangue accumulation and a corresponding gradual decrease in the tested bulking coefficient. As overlying strata pressure keeps increasing, the voids between gangue blocks are gradually compressed; consequently, the compactness of the gangue accumulation increases, and its load-bearing capacity is enhanced step by step. Upon the roadway at the fault zone being 200 m away from the working face, the impact of mining activities on the roadway diminishes progressively. Correspondingly, the load-bearing capacity of the broken rock rib stops varying and attains a steady state eventually, while the tested bulking coefficient exhibits no more fluctuations. Composed of the fragmented gangue wall, coal body, roof, and floor, the roadway exhibits stable and reliable performance, meeting the safety operation requirements of the mine. Conclusions

Aiming at the surrounding rock control problem of Roof Cutting and Retaining by Mining (RCRM) technology in fault structural zones, this study combines theoretical analysis, numerical simulation and field verification to conduct in-depth research on the strata deformation law and control technology of RCRM working face in cross-fault mining, and the key conclusions with theoretical innovation and engineering application value are as follows:Based on the key stratum theory and voussoir beam theory, a three-stage mechanical model for RCRM cross-fault mining was innovatively established, which for the first time clarifies the dynamic evolution law of advance stress under the action of the fault stress barrier effect. The model reveals that the advance stress drops sharply after the working face mines out the coal seam in the fault hanging wall, and gradually rebounds to the normal geological stress state with the increase of the immediate roof load when the working face advances to the fault footwall and moves away from the fault.FLAC3D and the Double-Yield model were adopted to simulate the compaction characteristics of caved gangue, and deviatoric stress was introduced as the evaluation index of surrounding rock stability. A comparative study of three slotting heights (8 m, 10 m, 12 m) showed that the larger the slotting height, the smaller the surrounding rock distortion energy and compressive deformation intensity; 10 m is the economically optimal slotting height. Compared with the 8 m slotting height, the peak deviatoric stress invariant of the coal mass in front of the working face is reduced by a maximum of 24.8 MPa2, the overlying strata displacement is reduced by 0.11 m, and the fault displacement is reduced by 0.04 m, while the control effect of the 12 m slotting height is limitedly improved.A targeted surrounding rock control scheme of “10 m slotting + loose blasting” was proposed for the fault structural zone. Field verification results show that this scheme effectively improves the bearing capacity of gangue in the goaf, reduces the stress concentration of surrounding rock and the slip displacement of fault plane, and achieves remarkable control effects: the average support force of hydraulic supports is reduced by 15.3%, the gangue bulking coefficient is reduced by 4.3%, and the actual control effect reaches 75% of the expected value, which provides reliable technical support for the safe and efficient application of RCRM technology in fault-developed areas.
