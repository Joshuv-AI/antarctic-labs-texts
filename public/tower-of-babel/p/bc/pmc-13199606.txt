Peri-Operative Surgical and Anaesthetic Predictors of Autogenous Arteriovenous Fistula (AVF) Maturation: A Retrospective Cohort Analysis
Authors: Paulina Bruessel, Denna Fryer, Ché Marfleet, Theresia Klarissa, Samantha Khoo, Gert Frahm-Jensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe maturation of autogenous arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs) remains unpredictable despite being the preferred vascular access for haemodialysis. Early identification of at-risk AVFs could improve outcomes and reduce unnecessary interventions. The 2019 Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDOQI) recommends a volume flow (Qa) threshold of >500 mL/min, replacing the 2006 >600 mL/min benchmark, as a marker of fistula health. This study evaluates whether perioperative and follow-up Qa measurements are associated with AVF maturation, with particular focus on pre-operative and immediate post-operative Qa, which remain underexplored. The influence of anaesthetic and demographic factors was also assessed.MethodsThis single-centre retrospective cohort study included 31 patients undergoing AVF creation. Inflow artery Qa was measured pre-operatively, immediately post-operatively, and at follow-up. Demographics, comorbidities, anaesthetic parameters, and AVF characteristics were obtained from clinical records. Successful maturation was defined as Qa ≥500 mL/min on duplex ultrasound at six weeks and the ability to sustain dialysis (two-needle access for ≥ two-thirds of sessions in the first month without functional concerns).ResultsOf 31 AVFs created, 22 (71%) matured by follow-up, either directly (n = 15, median Qa 1050 mL/min) or after fistuloplasty (n = 7, median Qa 800 mL/min). Nine (29%) failed to mature of which six did not undergo fistuloplasty (median Qa 270 mL/min), and three failed despite fistuloplasty (median Qa 332 mL/min). Successful AVFs demonstrated higher median Qa pre-operatively (91 vs 70 mL/min, p = 0.08), post-operatively (495 vs 234 mL/min, p = 0.02), and at follow-up (925 vs 280 mL/min, p < 0.001). The majority of AVF with Qa >700 mL/min at follow-up matured (OR 22.17, 95% CI 3.25–151.3, p = 0.002). No significant associations were found with age, smoking, diabetes, or anaesthetic factors.ConclusionPerioperative Qa measurement is a practical, low-cost predictor of AVF maturation. The >500 mL/min Qa threshold supports current KDOQI guidance. Pre-operative Qa differences represent a promising area for future research, while demographic and anaesthetic factors showed limited predictive value.

Introduction

Arteriovenous Fistulas (AVF) present a lifeline to end stage kidney disease (ESKD) patients, providing venous access with flow rates (Qa) high enough to conduct long-term haemodialysis with lower rates of stenosis, thrombosis, and infection compared to arteriovenous grafts and central venous catheters.1,2 Ideally, an AVF is established prior to commencing haemodialysis; however, this occurs in only 10–20% of patients largely due to delayed or failed fistula maturation.3–5 The National Kidney Foundation’s Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDOQI) “Guidelines for Vascular Access”, as well as the 2018 European Society for Vascular Surgery guidelines, define successful maturation of an AVF as having a Qa ≥500mL/min, a diameter of 4–5mm, and a depth of ≤6mm from the skin surface.6 Other hallmarks of successful maturation include the ability to sustain 2-needle cannulation for two-thirds or more dialysis sessions within a month, as well as progressive vein enlargement, audible bruit, and palpable thrill on serial examination by experienced clinical staff.6 Achieving successful AVF maturation can be difficult and as many as one-third of AVFs fail to mature and no robust or reproducible predictors of successful AVF maturation currently exist.7

Surgical, haemodynamic, anaesthetic and demographic factors are key in influencing successful AVF maturation. Qa measurement using duplex ultrasonography (dUSS) presents a means to understand some of the surgical and haemodynamic predictors of success, as it is accessible, non-invasive and provides both anatomical and dynamic information to the user.8 It is particularly valuable because the association between Qa and mature AVF dysfunction is well established, with low flows indicating the possibility of a high-risk fistula that requires prompt intervention.8–10

Anaesthetic modality is also recognised as a potential determinant of successful AVF maturation; however, its impact remains a subject of ongoing debate. A regional block (RB) involves administration of anaesthetic to a targeted group of nerves. It is a popular technique used for AVF creation due to its ability to induce peripheral vasodilation, which may enhance intraoperative blood flow.11,12 Nevertheless, general anaesthesia (GA) is commonly employed but carries notable cardiorespiratory risks, particularly in patients with ESKD, where the physiological burden of GA may outweigh its potential intraoperative benefits.13

Demographic factors such as age, smoking, and diabetes have all been hypothesised to influence AVF maturation, but the significance and predictive power of these associations remain uncertain. Evidence for the role of patient age in AVF maturation remains inconsistent, with some studies reporting no effect on maturation time, while others describe poorer success rates amongst older patients.14,15 Smoking has been linked to increased rates of non-maturation, while the influence of diabetes on AVF outcomes is mixed.16–18

Objectives

This study aims to identify key haemodynamic, anaesthetic, and demographic factors influencing AVF maturation and to evaluate the utility of perioperative Qa, alongside the 2019 KDOQI guideline of >500 mL/min immediately post-operatively, as a tool for predicting fistula outcomes.

Methods

Context

This study is a retrospective audit conducted from 09 January 2023 to 23 May 2025 at The Canberra Hospital and The North Canberra Hospital, two tertiary metropolitan Australian hospitals. It contributes to the growing literature regarding whether peri-operative Qa, demographic data, fistula type, or anaesthetic technique used can influence successful AVF maturation.

Included in the study were adults over 18 years old booked for a native upper-limb AVF formation during the study period. Patients were excluded if they were lost to follow up or if they had a prosthetic graft implanted. Data was compiled from operation reports using the Digital Health Record (DHR) and flow measurements were conducted by the surgical team at the time of surgery, or a specialist vascular imaging service. Data was collected by an unaccredited Vascular Registrar, two Resident Medical Officers and a Medical Student under the supervision of two consultant Vascular Surgeons. This revealed 51 operations, 4 of which were excluded because a loop fistula was formed, 8 operations for not having any form of initial flow measurement recorded, and 8 with incomplete data at time of writing, leaving a total of 31 operations for analysis.

Measures

Data were collected from all eligible participants as per the inclusion criteria outlined above. Pre and post-operative brachial Qa values were compared to the AVF flow rate at follow-up and analysed under two categories: less than (unsuccessful) or greater than (successful) 500 mL/min. Patients were then surveilled to see which fistulas were subsequently successfully used for dialysis at first successful canulation.19 At six week post-op follow-up fistula’s failing or slow to mature were reviewed and, if required, booked for re-intervention. Demographic data including age, smoking status, and comorbidities were collected. Anaesthetic data were identified from anaesthetic records and listed by type (general, regional, or local), drug name (ropivacaine, lidocaine, rocuronium, or propofol) and block used (brachial plexus and, if applicable, specific type of brachial plexus block, or suprascapular). Details of the operation were analysed for type of fistula created (brachiobasilic, brachiocephalic, radiocephalic or axillarycephalic). Flow data categories (successful or unsuccessful) were then compared to identify demographic, anatomical or anaesthetic trends that predict success or failure of AVF formation.

Qa Measurements

All patients included in the study had undergone preoperative venous mapping for surgical planning. Radiocephalic, brachiocephalic and brachiobasilic fistulas created in an end-to-side fashion were included in the data. All fistulas were created by consultant vascular surgeons or their advanced trainee. We recognise that inter-operator variability is a potential limitation in observational studies. To mitigate this, all operators were trained in a standardised protocol for intraoperative measurements. A video demonstrating the measurement technique was also developed and circulated to reinforce consistency across operators. Furthermore, all procedures were performed by consultant vascular surgeons or advanced trainees within a single institution, to reduce variability in both surgical and measurement techniques. Brachial artery Qa measurements were taken in all patients immediately pre- and post-operatively, and at their 6-week follow-up ultrasound scan. An average of three separate readings were used. Pre-operative readings were obtained in the anaesthetic bay or on the operating table, prior to the induction of anaesthesia. Immediate post-operative Qa measurements were defined as measurements taken during the interval between end of surgery and end of stay in the post-anaesthesia care unit. At the 6 week follow-up appointment brachial artery Qa was measured by credentialled vascular ultrasonographers.

Analysis

Data were recorded in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA) and analysed using Jamovi version 2.6 (The Jamovi Project, Sydney, Australia). The normality assumption was checked using descriptive statistics, normality tests and plots. Flow rates are non-normally distributed and are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR), and analysed using non-parametric tests, including the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired comparisons and the Mann–Whitney U-test for independent samples. Categorical variables are summarised as frequencies and percentages; compared using Fisher’s exact test as subgroup sizes were small. Contingency tables are used to evaluate threshold values for predictive Qa. A Bonferroni correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons, with the adjusted significance threshold set at p < 0.017.

Ethics Statement

This study protocol and patient consent process were reviewed and approved by the Australian Capital Territory Human Research Ethics Committee, Canberra, Australia (2024.IRE.00001) (REGIS reference 2023/ETH02850), and conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was gained from all participants as required by the Australian Capital Territory Human Research Ethics Committee and conducted in accordance with local governance requirements.

Results

AVF Maturation

Of the 31 AVFs included in this study, twenty-two (71%) matured successfully. Fifteen AVFs were mature at six week follow-up (48%, median Qa 1050 mL/min) while seven were identified at 6-week follow-up as requiring further intervention (n = 7, 23%, median Qa 800 mL/min). All seven subsequently matured following fistuloplasty. Nine AVFs (29%) failed to mature, including six (19%) that did not undergo fistuloplasty (median Qa 270 mL/min), and three (10%) that failed despite fistuloplasty (median Qa 332 mL/min). Among the successfully matured AVFs, 17 (77%) were used for dialysis at first cannulation, while five (23%) remained pre-dialysis at time of follow-up. These results are outlined in Figure 1. Flow Rate (Qa)

Among the 22 successful AVFs, median Qa increased from 91 mL/min (IQR 122–294) pre-operatively to 495 mL/min (IQR 352–697) post-operatively and 925 mL/min (IQR 857–1371) at follow-up. These increases were statistically significant when using Wilcoxon signed-rank test; pre-operative to follow-up (p < 0.001), post-operative to follow-up (p < 0.001), and pre-operative to post-operative (p = 0.004).

In contrast, the nine failed AVFs demonstrated a smaller increase in flow over time, with median Qa rising from 70 mL/min (IQR 57–151) pre-operatively to 234 mL/min (IQR 219–373) post-operatively and 280 mL/min (IQR 213–409) at follow-up. These increases were not statistically significant when using Wilcoxon signed-rank test and adjusted Bonferroni threshold; pre-operative to follow-up (p = 0.048), post-operative to follow-up (p = 0.18) pre-operative to post-operative (p = 0.042).

When comparing successful and failed AVF, median Qa was consistently higher in successful AVFs than in failed AVFs at all time points using Mann–Whitney U-test. Pre-operative flow was 91 mL/min in successful AVFs compared to 70 mL/min in failed AVFs (p = 0.08). Post-operative flow was significantly higher in successful AVFs (495 mL/min vs 234 mL/min, p = 0.02), and this difference was more pronounced at follow-up (925 mL/min vs 280 mL/min, p < 0.001).

A threshold of 500 mL/min was used for analysis of the association between post-operative Qa and AVF maturation (Table 1). Post-operatively, 10 of the 22 successful AVFs had Qa ≥500 mL/min which is only 46% sensitive, while among the nine failed AVFs, six had a Qa <500 mL/min, yielding a specificity of 67%. Positive predictive value is 77%, indicating that AVFs with Qa ≥500 mL/min are more likely to mature; however, the negative predictive value is low at 33%, suggesting that Qa <500 mL/min is a poor predictor of failure. Similarly, follow up Qa ≥700 mL/min was associated with AVF maturation in multiple studies as outlined in the discussion below and was used for threshold analysis for follow-up Qa (Table 1). Nineteen (86%) AVF with a Qa ≥700 mL/min matured, compared with three (14%) AVF with Qa <700 mL/min. AVFs with Qa ≥700 mL/min had higher odds of maturation (OR = 22.17, 95% 3.25–151.3, p = 0.002) which was statistically significant using Fisher’s exact test, compared with those below this threshold. Using Qa ≥700 mL/min as a predictor of maturation yielded a sensitivity of 86%, specificity of 78%, positive predictive value of 91%, and negative predictive value of 70%. As this was a small cohort study, positive predictive value may be skewed.

Radiocephalic fistulas predominated among successful AVFs, but the difference compared to brachiocephalic fistulas was negligible (p = 0.994). Discussion

Post-Operative and Follow-up Qa Is Associated with Successful AVF Maturation

Most of the existing literature utilises Qa values to predict fistula maturation after creation, with significant variation in follow-up dUSS interval. Our pilot study was designed to test if pre-operative as well as immediate post-operative Qa measurements correlated with maturation success at follow-up as this finding has the potential to change surgical planning, surveillance and follow-up imaging.

Our findings suggest that Qa is indeed a useful parameter for predicting fistula maturation. In our cohort of successfully matured fistulas, an approximately ten-fold increase in median Qa from preoperative to follow-up was observed. This compares to only an approximate four-fold increase in Qa in our failed fistula group. Comparing successful versus unsuccessful fistulas revealed that at each point of measurement (pre-operative, post-operative and follow-up), Qa was lower in the failed group, and the difference in Qa between these cohorts increased over time.

The results of our research echo those of existing literature. In a study of 18 patients having native fistulas created, Lomonte et al (2005) reported a stepwise increase in brachial artery Qa in 17 patients at 1, 7 and 28 days post creation, with the largest increase noted on day 1.20 One patient was found to have declining Qa readings over this period, corresponding with a failure to mature. From this data Lomonte et al (2005) extrapolated that fistulas were more likely to mature successfully if Qa values of 250–500mL/min on day 1 and 500–900mL/min on day 28 were achieved.20 Our study was able to demonstrate that immediate pre-operative, post-operative and six week Qa values were useful in predicting fistula success, reducing the need for patients to attend serial outpatient follow-up appointments, increasing efficiency and cost-effectiveness.

Harris et al (2012) evaluated 233 fistulas with a Qa value of 500mL/min at 8–12 weeks post creation.21 They found that 500 mL/min was a poor predictor of maturation, with most successfully matured fistulas having Qa measurements of >700 mL/min. This aligns with the findings of our study, which predict that flows of >700 mL/min at follow up were more likely to be successful and flows less than 500 mL/min post-operatively were less likely to mature. Harris et al (2012) subsequently reviewed all successful fistulas, and all sustained puncture and dialysis on first successful cannulation21 Ko et al (2015) reported similar results in a study of 148 primary and revised AVFs, where Qa of <600 mL/min was associated with a significantly higher rate of maturation failure when compared with moderate (600–800 mL/min) and high >800mL/min Qa values at 1–2 weeks post operation.22 In our study, the majority of failed AVF had a Qa <500mL/min post-operatively, supporting current evidence that Qa values of <500 mL/min are associated with higher rates of fistula thrombosis, stenosis, and ultimately failure.22–24

Our study identified that pre-operative flow was 21 mL/min higher in successful AVFs compared to failed AVFs. As such, we suggest pre-operative Qa values of <70 mL/min may prompt exploration into more suitable sites for fistula creation, or consideration of pre-operative optimisation including angioplasty of the inflow vessel, or ligation of perforators to increase vessel diameter and flow. Likewise, low post-operative values of <234 mL/min may warrant consideration of early fistuloplasty. A further application may be for monitoring the success and progress of balloon-assisted maturation (BAM), a reasonably new and controversial approach to facilitating fistula maturation.25

These findings hold exciting promise for application to clinical practice, as the ability to identify fistulas with low pre-operative and post-operative Qa could influence the decision to proceed with AVF creation, the anatomical configuration and intra-operative approach chosen, as well as the frequency of post-operative monitoring. At present, in our unit, patients have an initial post-operative follow-up with an ultrasound scan at 6 weeks. In fistulas at high risk of failure, as determined by pre- and immediate post-operative Qa, the interval between operation and follow-up could be reduce and earlier surveillance dUSS may be considered, allowing for more timely intervention and reducing the number of fistulas that are beyond salvage at follow-up. In a five-year controlled cohort study, Tessitore et al (2008) showed that integration of Qa measurements into fistula surveillance post-creation significantly reduced thrombosis rates and led to higher elective stenosis repair rates Our results correlate this using the new KDOQI Qa guideline of >500 mL/min and suggest that Qa monitoring is a fast, relatively low-cost, and convenient way to monitor AVF success with the potential to alter management.19

This said, it is important to be aware of the limitations of using Qa as a predictor of AVF maturation. dUSS is vulnerable to inter-user variability, and measurement of vessel diameter and cross-sectional area is influenced by factors such as the angle of insonation and the relationship of the sample volume to the direction of blood flow.8 Anatomical site of measurement, vessel tortuosity, and complexity of the vascular network may further contribute to variation in data collection.8 Furthermore, some studies have found that optimum and critical Qa is variable in different fistula configurations. Yii et al (2023) report a difference in Qa between forearm and arm fistulas, suggesting that the rate of fistula failure in mature fistulas, is partially influenced by the site9 While not formally assessed, this may also be relevant when using Qa as a predictive marker for fistula maturation and represents an area that requires further investigation.

We recognise that haemodynamic conditions under GA are dynamic, and factors such as anaesthetic depth, vasodilation, and vasoactive support may affect flow volume measurements. As such, these readings may not fully reflect post-operative physiological conditions. Future studies could address this by incorporating standardised haemodynamic targets at the time of measurement or by comparing intraoperative flow volumes with post-operative measurements obtained under more physiologically stable conditions.

In addition, we recognise that the relatively small sample size limits statistical power and the ability to identify novel associations; larger, multi-centre studies are required to validate these findings and further explore peri-operative predictors of AVF maturation.

Patient Factors Did Not Significantly Influence AVF Maturation

While demographic factors are important for surgical planning generally, this study does not identify demographic factors such as age, smoking and diabetes as reliable predictors for AVF maturation.14 This is in contrast to Gasparin et al (2023) that found that demographic factors lowered the chance of maturation.16 This may be because of the limited size of our study compared to Gasparin. These results align with previous reports showing inconsistent effects of demographic and anaesthetic factors and reinforce that perioperative flow dynamics play a more important role in predicting AVF outcomes.

Age is not a significant determinant of AVF maturation in this study as the median ages of patients with successful and unsuccessful fistulas were similar. Dunn et al (2015) concluded age had no effect on AVF maturation time and that age alone should not be the basis for excluding older dialysis patients from AVF creation.15,26

The relationship between current smoking status and AVF maturation was not significant. This was surprising, given chronic tobacco use is known to impair endothelial function and promote intimal hyperplasia—factors that could hinder the remodelling and arterialisation required for AVF maturation. Despite this study’s findings, Gasparin et al also identified smoking as a predictor of AVF failure.16

While there are known microvascular and macrovascular complications associated with diabetes, the presence, or absence, of diabetes was not significantly associated with AVF success nor failure in this cohort. The majority of patients with successful and failed AVFs had diabetes. Similarly, Bahrami-Ahmadi et al (2022) reported no statistically significant association between diabetes and early AVF failure.17 However, a recent large cohort study found that type 2 diabetes significantly increased the risk of AVF non-maturation with an odds ratio of 1.72, which they found was primarily mediated by impaired venous remodelling.17 In practice, diabetic vasculopathy can certainly contribute to poor vessel quality, yet our findings suggest any such effect may be modest compared to the dominant influence of flow dynamics.

Anaesthetic Modality Did Not Significantly Influence AVF Maturation

Several studies have shown that AVFs created under RA, compared with GA, were more successful, with RA being associated with higher maturation and patency rates at various time points, and GA being associated with decreased AVF utilisation and increased occlusion rates.27–31 Nonetheless, a randomised control trial (RCT32) found no statistically significant difference in AVF outcomes at 12 months when created under either RA, LA, and GA.33–35 This aligns with the variety of observed choices of anaesthesia in our study.

Interestingly, a systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated the superiority of RA over LA in achieving AVF patency.12,36 This follows that Aitken et al (2016) demonstrated in an RCT of 126 patients that brachial plexus block (BPB) improve short and long-term AVF patency rates compared to LA.35 Out of the total cases, the majority of successful AVFs were created using BPBs, but this association did not reach statistical significance. As such, our study has identified that GA and LA continue to be used despite evidence that BPBs are associated with AVF success.

In fact, several LAs are used in RBs for AVF creation and studies have investigated whether their pharmacological profiles can influence both block quality and vascular outcomes.37,38 Ropivacaine is associated with significantly greater arterial and venous diameters and increased blood flow, and produces a greater increase in arterial diameter when compared to other types of LA.32,39 This study did not show a statistically significant association between LA type and AVF maturation.

The lack of association between anaesthetic type and AVF maturation found in this paper likely reflects several factors. Established predictors of AVF maturation—such as preoperative vessel quality, arterial inflow, vein diameter, and surgical technique—are likely to exert a stronger influence and may confound any effect attributable to anaesthesia in this paper. In addition, haemodynamic variability and heterogeneity within anaesthetic techniques (eg. variation in regional block quality or vasodilatory effects due to GA) may dilute measurable differences. Selection bias in anaesthetic choice, often driven by patient comorbidities, may further obscure true associations. Overall, given the multifactorial nature of AVF maturation, any independent effect of anaesthesia is difficult to detect without larger sample sizes.

Future Work

While this study represents one of the first contemporary Australian analyses in the context of updated KDOQI guidelines, it is limited by its small cohort. Nonetheless, it provides findings that are relevant to the Australian population and may inform future research in larger cohorts; using pre-operative Qa to determine viability, and immediately post-operative Qa as a fast, low-cost and convenient predictor of success. Future research should also incorporate predicted costs saved by early intervention, reducing unnecessary follow-up appointments and interventions. This research should also further develop our consideration of demographic and anaesthetic factors, which did not find any significant relationship with AVF maturation, which could be due socio-cultural differences or due to the size of our cohort.

Finally, other possible predictors of long-term functional patency, such as intraoperative use of systemic anticoagulation, preoperative vein diameter and depth as well as Qa immediately after anaesthetic administration are areas for future research.33

Conclusion

This study evaluates the surgical, haemodynamic, anaesthetic and demographic factors that influence AVF maturation, with a particular focus on the predictive utility of brachial artery Qa measurements. While anaesthetics (type and method) and patient demographics (age, smoking, and diabetes) showed inconsistent effects on AVF maturation, our findings demonstrate that Qa is a clinically useful marker for AVF maturation. Pre-operative, post-operative, and follow-up Qa values were significantly higher in successfully matured AVFs compared to failed AVFs. Qa values <500 mL/min post-operatively is associated with failure, while Qa values ≥700 mL/min at follow-up were predictive of successful maturation. These results align with existing literature and the current KDOQI guidelines. In addition, early identification of low-flow AVFs prior to follow-up flow studies could guide clinical decisions—such as altering surgical strategy, choosing alternative access sites, or intensifying surveillance.
