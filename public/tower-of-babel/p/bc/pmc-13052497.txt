Impact of Limited English Proficiency on the Presentation and Management of Vestibular Schwannomas
Authors: Christian Jung, Carly Y Yang, Keshav V Shah, Douglas C Bigelow, Michael J Ruckenstein, Tiffany Peng‐Hwa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the impact of limited English proficiency (LEP) on the presentation, diagnosis, and management of vestibular schwannoma (VS).Study DesignRetrospective matched cohort study.SettingSingle tertiary academic center.MethodsA retrospective review of patients diagnosed with VS at a tertiary academic center from 2018 to 2023 identified 29 LEP and 1350 non‐LEP patients. LEP patients were matched 1:1 to non‐LEP patients using propensity scores based on age, sex, insurance, and zip code–based income. Clinical variables included audiometric data, tumor characteristics, time to diagnosis and treatment, and management outcomes.ResultsNo significant differences were found in presenting symptoms, tumor characteristics, or treatment recommendations. LEP patients were significantly less likely to undergo word recognition testing (41% vs 97%, P < .001). Among those tested, bilingual LEP patients performed worse than non‐LEP counterparts in word recognition testing (44.5% vs 61.9%, P = .1). LEP patients trended toward larger tumor volumes at diagnosis (6072.5 mm³ vs 2114 mm³, P = .059) and longer delays from symptom onset to diagnosis (4949 vs 2609 days), even after outlier removal. LEP patients also had higher loss to follow‐up rates (34% vs 14%, P = .123).ConclusionThough not statistically significant, several trends suggest LEP patients may face potential barriers to timely diagnosis and follow‐up for VS. The relative underrepresentation of LEP patients may also reflect underlying disparities. These findings underscore the need for targeted efforts to improve access and outcomes for LEP patients in neurotologic care.Keywords: audiological evaluation, health disparities, Limited English Proficiency (LEP), vestibular schwannoma

Vestibular schwannoma (VS) is a benign tumor arising from the Schwann cells of the eighth cranial nerve that can progress to severe neurological deficits due to its proximity to critical neurovascular structures. 1 Advances in audiometric evaluation, diagnostic imaging, and management options (such as surgical innovations and stereotactic radiation) have significantly improved outcomes for patients with VS over the past few decades. 2 , 3 However, the benefits of these advances may not be distributed evenly across all patients, as prior studies have demonstrated disparities in healthcare access, diagnosis, and treatment outcomes across the field of otolaryngology. 4 , 5 , 6 Recent studies have shown that patients of color and those with public insurance were less likely to undergo treatment at high‐quality hospitals, receive dual‐modality therapy, and had higher 30‐day postoperative readmission rates. 7 , 8 Similarly, healthcare disparities have been shown in skull base and intracranial tumors specifically, with minority groups and patients without private insurance having higher rates of postoperative infection, longer hospital stays, and increased mortality. 9 , 10 , 11

Furthermore, limited English proficiency (LEP) specifically has been identified as an independent predictor of adverse health outcomes within otolaryngology. 12 , 13 Previous research has established that LEP patients have decreased healthcare utilization and are less likely to undergo comprehensive audiometric evaluation. 12 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 These delays lead to reduced access to advanced treatment modalities, such as cochlear implantation and other audiologic rehabilitative services, resulting in worse health outcomes than their English‐proficient counterparts. 12 , 14 Moreover, prior studies have demonstrated disparities specifically in the presentation and management of VS. 18 , 19 , 20 Most recently, a 2023 study by Ellsperman et al demonstrated that patients with higher levels of socioeconomic disadvantage presented with higher tumor grades and were more likely to be recommended radiation and surgery. 21 However, these studies did not specifically investigate the effects of native language on the presentation and management of VS. Thus, the impact of LEP on the presentation and management of VS remains poorly characterized. To that end, we report a single‐institution retrospective review of patients with VS and evaluate differences in diagnosis and management based on the patient's primary language.

Methods

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Pennsylvania. A retrospective chart review was performed on patients diagnosed with VS between January 2018 and December 2023. Patients were identified using ICD‐10 codes and data were extracted from the Penn Medicine electronic health record.

LEP status was initially determined based on the primary language recorded in the electronic medical record. This was then independently validated by confirming documentation of interpreter use during clinical encounters. This ultimately yielded 29 LEP patients and 1350 non‐LEP patients.

Propensity score matching (PSM) was employed to reduce confounding, matching LEP patients 1:1 with non‐LEP patients based on sex, age at diagnosis, insurance status (Medicaid, Medicare, managed Medicare, private insurance, and military insurance), and median income of residential zip codes. Matching was performed using the MatchIt package in R with nearest neighbor matching and a standardized mean difference threshold of <0.1 indicating adequate covariate balance. 22

Variables compared tumor characteristics, audiometric data, time between symptom onset and diagnosis, initial treatment plan (as mutually agreed upon by provider and patient at initial consultation), time between diagnosis and treatment, and current status. Time from symptom onset to diagnosis was defined as the interval between the estimated date of patient‐reported symptom onset (based on the first clinic visit and reported duration of symptoms such as hearing loss, tinnitus, or dizziness) and the date of MRI confirming VS. Pure‐tone averages (PTA) were calculated using 500, 1000, 2000, and 3000 Hz. Outliers were identified using interquartile ranges. Comparative analyses were conducted using Mann‐Whitney U tests, Chi‐squared tests, and Fisher's exact tests. Additional analyses were also conducted with median imputation; this had no impact on results.

Results

PSM

Our search yielded 29 LEP patients and 1350 non‐LEP patients, thus LEP patients represent 2.3% of this cohort. Within the LEP group, Spanish and Russian were the most common primary languages, with 7 each. There were 5 Korean speakers, 4 Chinese speakers, and 2 Vietnamese speakers. The remaining 4 LEP patients spoke Albanian, Farsi, Malayalam, and Greek. The LEP and non‐LEP patients were matched based on sex, age at diagnosis, insurance status, and median income of residential zip codes. PSM for age at diagnosis, median income of residential income, and all but 1 insurance types were balanced between LEP and non‐LEP patients, with standardized mean differences of less than 0.1. However, PSM was unable to balance groups for proportions of males and females and Medicaid insurance status (Figure 1), likely due to inadequate sample size.

Propensity score matching reduced standardized mean difference (SMD) in all categories. The SMD is a measure of balance between the 2 groups. A positive SMD indicates a higher proportion or mean in the LEP group, while a negative SMD indicates a higher proportion or mean in the non‐LEP group. The target SMD of ±0.5 was reached for all categories except sex (male and female) and Medicaid insurance. MNG Medicare, Managed Medicare.

There were no differences in demographic variables between the 2 groups (Table 1). Mean age for all patients was 62.1 years (SD 12.8) and there was a total of 34 females and 24 males (N = 58). Median income was $88,250 for LEP patients and $87,154 for non‐LEP patients. Lastly, the distribution of insurance types was similar, with over 60% of both groups having Medicare insurance. LEP Versus Non‐LEP Comparative Analyses

There were no differences in symptoms at presentation, audiometric status, tumor characteristics, or initial management recommendation based on LEP status. Symptoms at presentation included hearing loss, tinnitus, dizziness, otalgia, headache/vision concerns, facial numbness, and facial paralysis. There were no significant differences between LEP and non‐LEP patients in reported symptoms. At presentation, there were no significant differences in air and bone PTA for LEP and non‐LEP patients (Figure 2). As expected, LEP patients were significantly less likely to undergo word recognition testing (41% of LEP vs 97% of non‐LEP patients; X 2 = 20.6, P < .001). LEP patients who identified as bilingual and did undergo testing had lower mean word recognition scores (WRS) than non‐LEP patients (44.5% vs 61.9%; Fisher's exact test P = .1), though notably these individuals were assessed in English and not their native language (Figure 2).

LEP patients had lower speech reception thresholds (SRT) and word recognition scores (WRS). LEP patients were significantly less likely to undergo word recognition testing (X 2 = 20.62, P < .0001). Mean time lapsed from symptom onset to diagnosis was notably greater in LEP patients than non‐LEP patients, but this finding did not reach statistical significance (mean 4949 days vs 2609 days; W = 441, P = .582). Interestingly, time elapsed between diagnosis and treatment was greater in non‐LEP patients than LEP patients but also did not reach statistical significance (846 days vs 389 days; W = 42, P = .148) (Figure 3). These analyses were also conducted after removing outliers identified by interquartile ranges, but this did not alter the results.

Time elapsed from symptom onset to diagnosis was 1.9 times greater for LEP patients. Time elapsed from diagnosis to treatment was greater in non‐LEP patients. LEP, limited English proficiency.

Regarding tumor characteristics, LEP patients had a greater longest tumor dimension (15.8 mm LEP vs 11.2 mm non‐LEP; W = 531.5, P = .089) and a mean tumor volume that was 2.9 times greater than that of non‐LEP patients (6072.5 mm3 for LEP and 2114 mm3 for non‐LEP; W = 542.5, P = .059) (Figure 4). However, neither of these findings reached statistical significance. Once again, removing outliers had no effect on results for tumor volume.

All patients received an initial management recommendation of observation, surgery, or gamma knife radiation (GKR) (Table 2). 66% of LEP patients and 76% of non‐LEP patients were recommended observation. 21% of both LEP patients and non‐LEP patients were recommended surgery. Lastly, 14% of LEP patients and 3% of non‐LEP patients were recommended GKR. There were no significant differences in initial treatment plan between LEP and non‐LEP patients (Fisher's exact test P = .483). There was also no significant difference between groups regarding whether there was a delay of ≥3 months after initial clinic visit (14% of LEP patients vs 7% of non‐LEP patients; Fisher's exact test P = .423).

Regarding current status, all patients fell into 1 of 4 categories: continued observation, received surgery, received GKR, or lost to follow up (Table 3). 24% of LEP patients and 48% of non‐LEP patients opted for continued observation. 17% of LEP patients and 24% of non‐LEP patients were post‐operative. 24% of LEP patients and 14% of non‐LEP patients had undergone GKR. Discussion

While there were no statistically significant differences in presentation and clinical management between LEP and non‐LEP patients, alarming trends were noted. LEP patients had a greater longest tumor dimension and greater mean tumor volume, although neither of these findings reached significance. Nonetheless, mean tumor volume for LEP patients was 2.9 times greater than that of non‐LEP patients, highlighting a notable disparity. This is concerning, as larger tumors are associated with higher rates of facial nerve palsy, postoperative complications (eg, cerebrospinal fluid leaks, lower cranial nerve palsies, and feeding tube requirements). 23 Thus, LEP patients may be at greater risk of increased disease morbidity, both preoperatively and postoperatively.

LEP patients were also significantly less likely to undergo word recognition testing, which may in turn impact timely diagnosis if signs of retrocochlear pathology go undetected. Those who identified as bilingual and did undergo testing performed worse than non‐LEP patients. However, due to the lack of language‐concordant materials, it remains unknown whether LEP patients' WRS are truly lower, or if it is attributable to a function of their bilingual status, as they were tested in English. Additionally, our query identified 29 LEP patients out of a total of 1379, representing 2.1% of the cohort. This is comparable to the percentage of LEP patients among completed ENT visits at our institution. However, based on estimates that LEP patients represent approximately 10% of the working population in the United States, 24 we would expect roughly 150 LEP patients among the 1379 total cases. Yet only 29 LEP patients were identified—nearly 5 times fewer than expected. Even under conservative estimations, this is a substantial underrepresentation. Together, these findings corroborate existing literature that reports language‐related barriers contribute to disparities in access to timely diagnosis and comprehensive care. 12 , 25 , 26 , 27 This is consistent with our findings, with LEP patients experiencing greater time elapsed between symptom onset and diagnosis, which may reflect the barriers that LEP patients face in initially accessing care.

LEP patients not only experience challenges accessing healthcare, but they also have difficulties with interpersonal interactions within the system, which is reflected in difficulty comprehending provider instructions, low‐quality informed consents, and challenges with follow‐up care. 28 , 29 , 30 These difficulties may contribute to LEP patients having a lost to follow up rate 2.4 times greater than that of non‐LEP patients, as consistent challenges with interpersonal communication can discourage patients from continuing to seek care. The barriers faced by LEP patients are only heightened in the setting of untreated hearing loss, which is the most common presenting symptom of VS. Hearing loss has been shown to be independent risk factors for adverse health outcomes, which amplifies existing challenges faced by LEP patients. 31 In a recent study, Halagur and Megwalu found that LEP patients were significantly less likely to have gone audiometric testing. 14 Once again, our findings corroborate this, as LEP patients were significantly less likely to undergo word recognition testing than non‐LEP patients. Therefore, LEP patients diagnosed with VS are positioned at a precarious intersection. 32

Despite these potential barriers, it appears that LEP patients delay care less than their non‐LEP counterparts. Although LEP patients had a greater mean time between symptom onset and diagnosis, they had a shorter mean time between diagnosis and treatment. One potential explanation for this is that LEP patients’ difficulties in accessing specialty care drives them to minimize further delays and seek out definitive treatment as quickly as possible. This is illustrated by a 2022 study by Gulati and Hur, in which LEP patients were significantly less likely to access, but also significantly less likely to delay medical care than their English‐proficient counterparts. In their study, delayed medical care was based on patient‐reported responses to survey questions (eg, “During the past 12 months, did you delay or not get any other medical care you felt you needed?), rather than objective time intervals. 33 In contrast, our study defines delay as the time from symptom onset to MRI‐confirmed diagnosis, providing a measurable timeline. However, a 2024 systematic review by Ruiz Colón et al demonstrated that LEP neurosurgical patients were less likely to receive timely neurosurgical intervention than English‐proficient counterparts. 34 These conflicting reports reflect the often labyrinthine landscape of our healthcare system for LEP patients, which may contribute to LEP patients' increased lost to follow up rate. Additionally, this mirrors the heterogeneity of the LEP population. Represented languages including Spanish, Russian, Chinese, and Farsi, amongst others. Each language group has its own unique culture and thus its own potential barriers to care that may impact outcomes.

While these findings reflect disparities in presentation and management of VS between LEP and non‐LEP patients, the degree of underrepresentation observed in our cohort (29 LEP patients out of 1379 total patients) suggests substantial barriers may prevent LEP patients from being diagnosed, let alone undergoing treatment. Even under conservative assumptions, this gap is striking and warrants further investigation. Furthermore, there are currently no national or large‐scale registry data that specifically report on primary language among VS patients to allow for a comparative population‐level assessment of our findings. Our findings, which suggest a marked underrepresentation of LEP patients within our cohort, highlight the need to consider language for inclusion as a variable in national registries for VS and other pathologies. Further investigation using population‐level datasets would help determine whether this trend reflects broader disparities in access to specialty care or diagnosis.

A strength of our study lies in the rigor of LEP classification. Prior studies often rely solely on EMR‐recorded primary language, which is inconsistently reported and prone to misclassification. To enhance accuracy, we cross‐validated LEP status using interpreter documentation in the clinical record. This dual verification approach minimizes bias and strengthens the reliability of our comparisons. Nonetheless, we once again acknowledge that the limited sample size and lack of statistical significance indicates that these differences may reflect random variation and not true associations. As such, this represents a limitation of this study, and the results should be interpreted cautiously. Larger, multi‐institutional studies will be essential to further characterize these disparities and inform interventions. Additionally, qualitative studies with LEP patients who successfully accessed care may help clinicians better understand their experiences.

Conclusion

Although our findings did not reach statistical significance, several concerning trends emerged that suggest impactful disparities in the presentation and management of VS among LEP patients. Compared to non‐LEP patients, LEP patients presented with larger tumors, had longer intervals from symptom onset to diagnosis, and were more likely to be lost to follow‐up—all of which may indicate delayed or disrupted care trajectories. These patterns are particularly troubling given that larger tumor size is associated with increased surgical complexity and higher rates of postoperative complications. Moreover, the degree of underrepresentation of LEP patients in our cohort was substantial and may reflect systemic barriers that prevent LEP patients from receiving diagnosis and specialty care for VS all together. To fully capture how LEP status affects the presentation and management of VS, larger, multi‐institutional studies will be needed. Understanding the role of LEP status in VS care is essential to ensuring early detection and maximizing hearing preservation. By elucidating these disparities, we hope to improve access to timely and effective care for LEP patients.
