Ultrasonic-driven force-electric nanodomains orchestrate lysosome-to-mitochondria functional cascade to accelerate bone regeneration
Authors: Linbo Zhang, Pandong Lin, Dandan Wang, Xue Wang, Kangqing Zuo, Taixing Zhang, Wen Luo, Jing Li, Yinchuan Wang, Ke Ma, Yilin Zhang, Shengjun Sun, Guiyong Xiao, Yupeng Lu, Dongsheng Zhang, Shengyun Huang, Bing Han, Ningbo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Piezoelectric materials offer a promising bioinspired direction for bone regeneration by mimicking the native electromechanical environment of bone. However, existing studies mainly focus on extracellular piezoelectric cues perceived by cell membrane, while whether force–electric signals can be precisely regulated at subcellular functional units governing osteogenesis remains insufficiently understood. Here, we report an ultrasound-activated force–electric regulation strategy enabled by ultrafine LiNbO3 nanoparticles (∼15 nm). Owing to their ultrafine size, these nanoparticles are efficiently internalized by stem cells and form intracellular force–electric nanodomains that generate localized electrical signals upon ultrasound stimulation, thereby avoiding nonspecific extracellular inflammatory activation and leading to markedly enhanced osteogenic activity. Mechanistically, these nanodomains perturb lysosomal membranes, enhance mitochondrial activity and establish a lysosome-to-mitochondria functional cascade. Transcriptomic analysis further identifies the PI3K–AKT–Ibsp axis as a key pathway mediating the osteoinductive effect. In a rabbit calvarial defect model, this force–electric regulation significantly accelerates bone regeneration. This work demonstrates that ultrasonic-driven ultrafine piezoelectric nanodomains enable spatially precise force–electric regulation by acting closer to subcellular functional units, establishing a materials-based strategy for bone tissue regeneration.

Introduction

Severe bone defects remain a major clinical challenge in regenerative medicine [[1], [2], [3]], currently bone graft substitutes including autografts, allografts, and synthetic implants, are limited by donor scarcity, immune rejection, and poor osseointegration. Native bone exhibits intrinsic piezoelectricity, generating localized electrical potentials in response to mechanical loading [4], which regulate stem cell behavior [5], matrix deposition, and mineralization during bone formation and remodeling [6]. Recent advances in regenerative biomaterials, such as enzyme-programmable DNA–PEG hydrogels, trabeculae-like biomimetic bone-filling materials, and hydrogel microspheres, have collectively demonstrated that spatiotemporal regulation, structural reconstruction, and controlled delivery are key strategies for modulating the bone regeneration microenvironment [[7], [8], [9]]. Mimicking these physiologically relevant electromechanical cues has motivated the development of piezoelectric biomaterials for bone repair [10]. These materials have been incorporated into scaffolds, coatings, and composites [11,12], where mechanically induced electrical signals activate mechanosensitive ion channels, modulate intracellular calcium dynamics [13], and promote osteogenic differentiation [14]. Although considerable progress has been made in this research direction within bone tissue engineering, existing studies mainly generate signals sensed indirectly at the cell membrane, positioning force–electric regulation primarily as an external or upstream stimulus, rather than enabling precise regulation of intracellular functional units. Importantly, in such membrane-centric paradigms, piezoelectric-generated signals must traverse multiple intracellular barriers before reaching organelles that ultimately determine cell fate decisions, which may lead to signal attenuation, reduced spatial specificity, and limited subcellular control precision.

Osteogenic differentiation is governed by tightly coordinated intracellular and subcellular processes, including energy metabolism, signal transduction, and organelle communication [15]. Force–electric cues generated outside the cell may suffer from a spatial mismatch with the intracellular functional units that directly drive osteogenesis. This mismatch can limit regulatory precision and biological specificity, and may occasionally elicit nonspecific extracellular responses [16]. Therefore, achieving spatially confined delivery of force–electric cues to intracellular functional units may represent a fundamentally different regulatory paradigm, in which electromechanical signals directly interface with organelle-level processes governing cellular fate. Osteogenic differentiation is a protracted and highly coordinated biological process that depends on sustained biophysical cues. Accordingly, achieving effective and persistent intracellular piezoelectric stimulation is crucial for modulating subcellular events that govern differentiation. To this end, we reviewed commonly used piezoelectric material classes to assess their suitability for intracellular force–electric regulation. Although organic piezoelectric polymers offer favorable biocompatibility, their piezoelectric performance is highly sensitive to molecular ordering and crystalline stability, which are easily disrupted under physiological conditions [17]. In contrast, inorganic piezoelectric ceramics possess robust crystal lattices and strong electromechanical coupling, making them more suitable for stable force–electric conversion.

The size, morphology, and crystallinity of inorganic piezoelectric nanoparticles critically influence their biological behavior [16]. Larger nanoparticles often exhibit limited cellular uptake and induce elevated oxidative stress, ROS generation, and inflammatory responses [18], which may compromise cell viability [19] and counteract beneficial piezoelectric signaling effects. Conversely, ultrafine nanoparticles can act closer to subcellular functional units [20], enabling cascade signaling and achieving more efficient osteogenic differentiation with minimal electrical input [21]. However, their high cellular accessibility may also raise potential concerns regarding intracellular accumulation and cytotoxicity [22]. Careful regulation of particle concentration is therefore essential to harness the advantages of ultrafine nanoparticles while maintaining a favorable biosafety profile. These considerations highlight that balancing piezoelectric performance and biological adaptability remains a central challenge in material design. Furthermore, variations in nanoparticle size may significantly influence cellular internalization behavior and subsequent subcellular responses, which is critical for achieving precise intracellular piezoelectric regulation.

At the subcellular scale, lysosomes and mitochondria serve as key functional units for both nanoparticle processing and osteogenic regulation. Lysosomes and mitochondria are not merely passive intracellular compartments but active regulators of bioenergetics, ionic homeostasis, and stress-responsive signaling pathways. Lysosomes act as the primary site for nanoparticle uptake, controlling intracellular trafficking, membrane integrity, and metabolic signaling [23], whereas mitochondria function as central energy hubs that drive osteogenic differentiation [24] and are highly sensitive to electrical and metabolic cues [25]. Importantly, lysosome–mitochondria crosstalk constitutes a functional axis that integrates material processing with metabolic reprogramming, thereby directly influencing stem cell fate decisions [26,27]. The endoplasmic reticulum serves as a major intracellular calcium reservoir and plays a crucial role throughout the osteogenic differentiation process [28]. Taken together, these observations suggest that subcellular organelles represent critical functional nodes where force–electric cues must be precisely decoded into biological outputs. Direct delivery of piezoelectric signals to these intracellular hubs may therefore overcome the spatial limitations of extracellular stimulation. Accordingly, intracellularly localized piezoelectric activation may provide a fundamentally distinct regulatory mode compared to conventional extracellular stimulation by enabling force–electric cues to act directly at subcellular functional units.

In this study, we report a strategy in which ultrasonic-driven ultrafine (∼15 nm) LiNbO3 nanoparticles, are efficiently internalized by stem cells to form intracellular force–electric nanodomains, enabling spatially precise regulation at subcellular functional units. Compared with BaTiO3, LiNbO3 was selected as the representative platform due to its superior and more consistent osteogenic performance in our comparative system under identical conditions. By modulating lysosomal integrity and proton homeostasis, enhancing mitochondrial activity, and activating downstream osteogenic signaling, this materials-based strategy establishes a direct link between intracellular force–electric regulation and bone tissue regeneration, offering new insight into how physical cues can be harnessed at the subcellular level to promote osteogenesis and accelerate bone regeneration (Scheme 1). Experimental section

LiNbO3 nanoparticle preparation

Bulk LiNbO3 particles (Cat# 254290, Sigma) were placed in a porcelain boat and subjected to gas-phase pulsed laser ablation under an argon atmosphere using rapid laser micro-area vaporization technology (30 kHz, 25% power). The laser irradiation induced vaporization and fragmentation of the raw material, leading to nanoparticle formation and deposition on the opposite collection zone. The collected powder was subsequently harvested by gradient centrifugation and vacuum-dried to obtain ultrafine LiNbO3 nanoparticles.

Material characterization

The particle size distribution of the ultrafine LiNbO3 nanoparticles was measured using a Zetasizer Pro (Malvern). The morphology and lattice structure were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (Regulus 8100, Hitachi) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (Talos F200i, Thermo Fisher). The crystalline structure was analyzed by X-ray diffraction (Smartlab SE, Rigaku) and laser Raman spectroscopy (inVia, Renishaw). Elemental mapping was performed using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) integrated into either SEM or TEM platforms. The elemental composition and chemical states were further investigated using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (Axis Supra, Kratos).

Hydrodynamic size distribution of ultrafine LN nanoparticles in serum-containing medium

Ultrafine LiNbO3 nanoparticles were dispersed in DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and incubated at 37 °C. The hydrodynamic size distribution was subsequently measured using a dynamic light scattering analyzer (Zetasizer Nano ZS, Malvern Panalytical, UK) to evaluate the colloidal stability of the nanoparticles under physiologically relevant conditions.

FEM simulations of the LiNO3 nanoparticles−ultrasonic wave interaction

FEM analyses were carried out using COMSOL Multiphysics (V6.2), running on AMD 9B14 processor (2), with 384 GB RAM. The COMSOL “MEMS”, “Acoustics”, and “Structural Mechanics” modules were chosen to include the relevant physics of the acoustic pressure wave and the piezoelectric and dielectric response of the LiNbO3 nanoparticles. Below lists the detailed methods. All these modules presented a fully coupled steady-state solution. Multiphysics environment of COMSOL and the steady-state solutions (frequency domain solver) for electric field, stress and strain were solved as a function of pressure and LiNO3 nanoparticles. The radially poled configuration was used for LiNO3 analyses, adopting a spherical coordinate system. Linear piezo response and linear elastic media assumptions were adopted in the study. Piezoelectric strain-charge formulation was used to solve the piezoelectric response to stresses and strains induced by the acoustic field. The input parameters for the FEM simulations were the following: density: 4700 kg/m3, elastic matrix (202.9, 52.9, 202.9, 74.9, 74.9, 243.1, 9.0, −9.0, 0, 59.9, 0, 0, 0, 0, 59.9, 0, 0, 0, 0, 9.0, 74.9) GPa, relative permittivity matrix (43.6, 43.6, 29.16), semidiameter: 7.5, 15, 50, 100, 200, 1000 nm. The external pressure was set as an acoustic plane wave of amplitude 55-141 kPa; The FEM model is shown in Fig. 1m.

Measurement of piezoelectric voltage output

Ultrafine LiNbO3 nanoparticles and large LiNbO3 particles were compacted into a pellet using a tablet press under a pressure of 20 MPa for 10 min. The pellet was then wrapped with copper foil and connected to electrical wires. The assembly was encapsulated with polyimide tape to form a sandwich-like structure. This structure was placed on the surface of an ultrasound probe pre-coated with coupling agent. The output voltage was recorded in real time using a digital oscilloscope (UTD2202CEX+, UNI-T).

Cell membrane potential measurement

Cell membrane potential was evaluated using the membrane potential-sensitive fluorescent probe DiBAC4(3) (Y239271-5 mg, Beyotime Biotechnology, China). A working solution was prepared in DMSO according to the manufacturer's instructions and applied to cells at a final concentration of 2 μM. Cells were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark, washed gently with PBS, and subsequently imaged using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Celldiscoverer 7, ZEISS). Representative fluorescence images were collected for analysis.

Evaluation of cavitation-associated hydroxyl radical generation by ultrafine LN and ultrafine BTO nanoparticles

To evaluate cavitation-associated reactive oxygen species generation under ultrasound stimulation, hydroxyl radical (·OH) production was quantified using a terephthalic acid (TA) fluorescence probe assay. The TA working solution consisted of 2 mM terephthalic acid, 5 mM NaOH, and PBS buffer (pH 7.4). Ultrafine LN nanoparticles or ultrafine BTO nanoparticles were dispersed in the TA solution and subjected to ultrasound treatment under the same conditions used in the in vitro experiments. Immediately after sonication, fluorescence generated by 2-hydroxyterephthalic acid was measured using a multifunctional microplate reader at an excitation wavelength of 310 nm and an emission wavelength of 425 nm. The resulting fluorescence intensity was used to quantify ultrasound-induced hydroxyl radical generation and indirectly reflect cavitation-associated effects involving the nanoparticles.

Synthesis of composite hydrogel scaffolds

2g of gelatin (Cat# V900863, Sigma) was dissolved in 20 mL of deionized water and heated with stirring on a magnetic stirrer at 50 °C and 380 rpm for 30 min. Subsequently, 4 mL of methacrylic anhydride (Cat# 276685, Sigma) was added dropwise to the gelatin solution using a micro-infusion pump at a rate of 0.5 mL/min, and the mixture was stirred continuously at 50 °C and 600 rpm for 3 h. After the reaction, 60 mL of preheated distilled water (50 °C) was added to dilute the mixture, which was then transferred into a dialysis bag (Cat# YA1051, Solarbio) and dialyzed in a 40 °C water bath for 7 days. The dialyzed product was lyophilized, redissolved in deionized water, and mixed with ultrafine LiNbO3 nanoparticles and hydroxyapatite nanoparticles at various concentrations. LAP (Cat# 1173099, LeYan) was then added, and the solution was cast into a mold and crosslinked under UV light to form the composite hydrogel scaffolds.

Composition analysis, mechanical testing, and degradation evaluation of composite hydrogel scaffolds

Composition analysis: Hydrogel samples were compressed into thin films and analyzed using Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) (INVENIO, Bruker) to obtain their infrared absorption spectra. Mechanical testing: Compressive stress-strain curves were recorded by subjecting the composite hydrogel scaffolds to compression at a rate of 0.5 mm/min using a universal testing machine. Degradation evaluation: Composite hydrogel scaffolds were lyophilized and weighed, then immersed in PBS and incubated at 37 °C. Ultrasound treatment conditions were set identically to those used in cell experiments. At predetermined time points, scaffolds were retrieved, lyophilized, and weighed again. Their surface morphology was observed by scanning electron microscopy. The immersion supernatants were subjected to acid digestion and heated at 180 °C for 8 h, after which the concentration of released ions was quantified by ICP-MS.

Culture of rat BMSCs

Rat bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Cat# A3160801, Thermo Fisher) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Cat# P1400, Solarbio) under standard culture conditions. The culture medium was refreshed every 2-3 days, and cells were passaged using 0.25% trypsin (Cat# 25200072, Thermo Fisher), followed by centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 5 min. Cells at passages 3 to 5 were used for all subsequent experiments.

Ultrasound treatment

In vitro: Cells were stimulated using an ultrasound probe (KSJ, China) operating at 40 kHz and 6 W. Sterile water was used as the coupling medium, and the ultrasonic bath was disinfected daily with 75% ethanol. After 24 h of co-culture with nanoparticles, ultrasound stimulation was applied for 10 min per day.

In vivo: Intermittent ultrasound stimulation was initiated one week after surgery and applied every other day until the end of the experimental period. Ultrasound parameters (40 kHz, 6 W, 10 min per session) were identical to those used in vitro. During stimulation, the probe position and coupling conditions were standardized to ensure uniform exposure across the defect region.

Biocompatibility evaluation

In vitro: Rat BMSCs were co-cultured with LiNbO3 nanoparticles for 1, 3, and 5 days. Cell viability and survival were assessed using the CCK-8 assay kit (Cat# BS350A, Biosharp) and Live/Dead staining kit (Cat# 40747 ES, Yeasen), respectively. In vivo: At the end of the animal experiment, heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney tissues were harvested and subjected to H&E staining. Histological analysis was performed using a whole-slide scanner (VS200, Olympus) to assess systemic toxicity and tissue compatibility.

Hemolysis assay

Fresh rabbit blood was collected using vacuum blood collection tubes (Cat# 367886, BD Vacutainer), followed by centrifugation at 1200g, 4 °C for 10 min. After discarding the plasma, red blood cells were gently washed three times with PBS and resuspended to prepare a 2% erythrocyte suspension. The experimental group (LiNbO3 nanoparticle suspension), positive control (double-distilled water), and negative control (PBS) were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by centrifugation at 1200g, 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was collected, and absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader.

Preparation and characterization of large LiNbO3 particles and piezoelectric/non-piezoelectric BaTiO3 nanoparticles

To investigate the synergistic effects of piezoelectricity and ultrafine particle size on, three types of raw powders were selected: (1) cubic-phase BaTiO3 (RHAWN, China), (2) tetragonal-phase BaTiO3 (Yumu, China), and (3) trigonal-phase LiNbO3 (large size). The first two raw powders were processed using a near-infrared high-energy pulsed laser system (30 kHz, 25% power) to obtain ultrafine cubic-phase BaTiO3 nanoparticles (non-piezoelectric) and ultrafine tetragonal-phase BaTiO3 nanoparticles (piezoelectric), respectively. The morphology and lattice structure of the three types of particles were evaluated using high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM, Talos F200i, Thermo Fisher), while their crystal structures were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD, Smartlab SE, Rigaku). Elemental distributions were analyzed by TEM-EDS mapping.

Cytoskeletal staining

A total of 5 × 104 cells were seeded into confocal culture dishes and co-cultured with LiNbO3 nanoparticles for 48 h, with daily ultrasound stimulation. After washing three times with PBS, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 10 min and washed again three times. Permeabilization was performed using 0.5% Triton X-100 (Cat# T8200, Solarbio) for 5 min, followed by PBS washes. Cells were then incubated with phalloidin staining solution (Cat# CA1680, Solarbio) for 30 min in the dark at room temperature. Nuclear staining was performed with DAPI (Cat# C0065, Solarbio) for 10 min at room temperature. After final PBS washes, cells were imaged using a confocal microscope (Celldiscoverer 7, ZEISS).

ROS detection

A total of 5 × 104 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and co-cultured with LiNbO3 nanoparticles for 3 days, with daily ultrasound stimulation. ROS levels were evaluated using a commercial detection kit (Cat# S0033S, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer's instructions. After addition of the working solution, cells were incubated at 37 °C for 20 min, followed by three PBS washes. Fluorescence signals corresponding to intracellular ROS were observed using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Celldiscoverer 7, ZEISS).

Endocytosis inhibition assay

To investigate the internalization pathways of LiNbO3 nanoparticles, the particles were fluorescently labeled with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC). Specifically, 100 mg of pre-synthesized LiNbO3 NPs were ultrasonically dispersed in 25 mL of anhydrous ethanol, followed by the addition of a pre-dissolved FITC solution (2 mg/mL, Sigma). The mixture was stirred at 500 rpm under light-protected conditions at room temperature for 12 h. The suspension was then centrifuged at 9000 rpm for 20 min. The resulting pellet was sequentially washed three times with deionized water and anhydrous ethanol, then collected and vacuum-dried for 24 h. For the endocytosis inhibition assay, three inhibition conditions were applied: low temperature (4 °C), 200 μM genistein (Cat# G6649, Sigma), and 50 μM chlorpromazine (Cat# HY-12708, MCE). A total of 1 × 105 cells were seeded into confocal dishes and allowed to adhere. Cells were then treated with inhibitors or incubated at 4 °C for 2 h, followed by the addition of FITC-labeled LiNbO3 nanoparticles in the same inhibitor-containing medium. After a 5 h incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS, stained with Hoechst 33342 (Cat# C0031, Solarbio) for 10 min at room temperature, and imaged using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Celldiscoverer 7, ZEISS) to assess nanoparticle uptake.

Ultrathin section imaging

Cells co-cultured with LiNbO3 nanoparticles were mildly digested with trypsin, centrifuged, and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution. After standard embedding and ultrathin sectioning procedures, samples were examined using a 200 kV transmission electron microscope (Talos F200i, Thermo Fisher) to visualize intracellular microstructures.

Live-cell subcellular imaging

Organelle labeling: A total of 1 × 105 cells were seeded onto confocal dishes and co-cultured with LiNbO3 nanoparticles for 2 or 3 days. Cells were then incubated with 0.1 μM LysoTracker™ Deep Red (Cat# L12492, Thermo Fisher), MitoTracker™ Deep Red (Cat# A66440, Thermo Fisher), or MitoTracker™ Green (Cat# A66441, Thermo Fisher) for 30 min at 37 °C. After incubation, cells were washed three times with prewarmed PBS and maintained at 37 °C during imaging. LAMP1-mCherry transfection: After 2 days of co-culture with LiNbO3 nanoparticles, cells were subjected to plasmid extraction and transfection according to the manufacturer's protocol to visualize lysosomal membrane dynamics. Mitochondrial membrane potential assessment: Following 3 days of co-culture, cells were rinsed three times with PBS and incubated with 1 mL JC-1 staining solution (Cat# C2003S, Beyotime) for 20 min at 37 °C. After two washes with the supplied buffer and addition of fresh culture medium, JC-1 fluorescence was imaged. Super-resolution imaging: All subcellular structures were imaged using a super-resolution microscope (Elyra 7, SIM2 mode, ZEISS) equipped with a 63× oil-immersion objective (numerical aperture = 1.4), under 37 °C and 5% CO2 conditions. Image acquisition and processing were performed using ZEN software (v3.5). JC-1 fluorescence images were captured using a confocal microscope (Celldiscoverer 7, ZEISS) with a 20× air objective (numerical aperture = 0.95).

Lysosomal Membrane Permeabilization (LMP) assay

L-leucyl-L-leucine methyl ester hydrobromide (LLOMe, T7739, TargetMol, China) was used as a positive control for lysosomal membrane permeabilization. Based on previously established lysosome damage protocols, cells were treated with 1 mM LLOMe for 2 h to induce acute lysosomal membrane disruption prior to fixation [29]. Experimental groups included Control, LN, US, US + LN, and LLOMe-treated groups.

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized using 0.1% Triton X-100. After blocking with 5% BSA, samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-Galectin-3 antibody (60207-1-Ig, Proteintech, USA) and anti-LAMP1 antibody (32435-1-AP, Proteintech, USA). Following PBS washing, the corresponding fluorescent secondary antibodies were applied for 1 h at room temperature in the dark, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI.

Images were acquired using a super-resolution structured illumination microscope (Elyra 7, ZEISS, Germany) operating in SIM2 mode. Recruitment of Galectin-3 to LAMP1-positive lysosomes was used as an indicator of lysosomal membrane permeabilization (LMP). Acquired images were analyzed using ZEN software (v3.5).

Lysosomal pH measurement

Intracellular lysosomal acidification was assessed using the LysoSensor™ Green DND-189 probe (40767ES50, Yeasen Biotechnology, China). The working solution was prepared according to the manufacturer's instructions (1:1000 dilution in culture medium) and added to the cells, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark. After incubation, cells were gently washed with PBS and imaged using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Celldiscoverer 7, ZEISS). Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software to evaluate dynamic changes in lysosomal pH following ultrasound-assisted ultrafine LN nanoparticle treatment at different time points.

Lysosomal function inhibition assay

Bafilomycin A1 (BafA1, HY-100558, MCE, USA), a vacuolar H + -ATPase inhibitor, was used to suppress lysosomal acidification and lysosomal function. Based on previous studies demonstrating effective lysosomal inhibition at nanomolar concentrations while minimizing overt cytotoxicity, BafA1 was added to the culture medium at a final concentration of 5 nM [30]. Cells were incubated with BafA1 for 48 h at 37 °C prior to ultrasound stimulation and subsequent analyses. This treatment was employed to evaluate the contribution of lysosomal function to the regulation of mitochondrial activity and downstream cellular responses.

Calcium ion probe staining

A total of 5 × 104 cells were seeded into confocal culture dishes and co-cultured with LiNbO3 nanoparticles for 72 h, with daily ultrasound stimulation. After three washes with PBS, cells were incubated with 0.5 μM calcium ion fluorescent probe (S1060, Beyotime) at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by three additional PBS washes. Fluorescence imaging was performed using a confocal microscope (Celldiscoverer 7, ZEISS).

Osteogenic differentiation induction and evaluation of osteogenic potential

Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP) Staining: After co-culture with nanoparticles for 3 or 7 days, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature and washed three times with PBS. ALP staining solution (Cat# C3206, Beyotime) was applied for 30 min, followed by rinsing with double-distilled water. Bright-field images were captured under a 10×objective (Olympus). Alizarin Red S Staining (ARS) and Quantification: After 21 days of culture, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and gently washed with PBS. ARS staining solution (Cat# ALIR-10001, OriCell) was added for 5-10 min. After washing with PBS, images were taken using a stereomicroscope (M205FA, Leica). For quantification, calcium nodules were dissolved with 10% cetylpyridinium chloride (Cat# C129534, Aladdin), and the absorbance at 570 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Spark, Tecan).

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction and Western blotting

Total RNA was extracted after 7 or 14 days of co-culture using a commercial kit (Cat# AG21022, Accurate Biotechnology). cDNA synthesis was performed with a reverse transcription kit (Cat# AG11728, Accurate Biotechnology), followed by amplification on the Roche Light Cycler 480 platform. The target genes included GAPDH, Alpl, Col1a1, Runx2, PI3K, AKT, Ibsp, Bmp2, and Spp1 (primer sequences listed in Supplementary Table 2).

Proteins were extracted from cells after 7 days of co-culture and quantified using the BCA assay. Samples were boiled, separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred to membranes. After blocking with rapid blocking buffer (Cat# P0228, Beyotime), membranes were incubated with primary antibodies (RUNX2, BMP2) followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using an imaging system (Bio-Rad).

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were fixed, permeabilized, and blocked, then incubated sequentially with primary and secondary antibodies. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Celldiscoverer 7, ZEISS). The detailed information of all antibodies used in this study, including antibody names, sources, and catalog numbers, is provided in Supplementary Table 5.

RNA sequencing

After 7 days of co-culture with LiNbO3 nanoparticles, total RNA was extracted from BMSCs using TRIzol reagent (Cat# 15596018CN, Thermo Fisher). Transcriptome sequencing and differential gene expression analysis were performed by Shenzhen Chengqi Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

PI3K/Akt signaling pathway inhibition assay

The PI3K inhibitor LY294002 (Proteintech, USA) was used to suppress PI3K/Akt signaling. According to the experimental design, LY294002 was added to the culture medium at a final concentration of 10 μM. The inhibitor-containing medium was refreshed every two days to maintain its activity throughout the 7-day osteogenic induction period. This experiment was conducted to investigate the involvement of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway in ultrafine LiNbO3 nanoparticle-mediated osteogenic differentiation.

Animal study design and construction of cranial defect model

A total of 30 male New Zealand white rabbits (2.5-3.0 kg) were randomly divided into three groups: blank control, non-ultrasound, and ultrasound groups, each with 4-week and 12-week treatment subgroups. Animal allocation was performed using a computer-generated random number table to ensure unbiased group assignment. Following anesthesia induction (30 mg/kg pentobarbital, administered via auricular vein) and preoperative analgesia (meloxicam, 0.1 mg/kg, subcutaneous injection), a midline incision was made to expose the bilateral parietal bones. Bilateral critical-sized calvarial defects (10 mm in diameter, 5 mm apart) were created using a dental high-speed turbine drill fitted with a trephine bur. Hydrogel scaffolds with or without LiNbO3 nanoparticles were implanted into the defect sites, and the allocation of different scaffolds to the left and right calvarial defects was also randomized to avoid positional bias. The surgical area was closed with absorbable sutures. Rabbits were returned to their housing environment and received subcutaneous penicillin injections for 3 consecutive days to prevent infection. Postoperative ultrasound stimulation was subsequently performed as described in Section 2.8.

Micro-CT imaging analysis

At 4- or 12- weeks post-treatment, animals were euthanized, and calvarial specimens were harvested. Samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 48 h, followed by scanning using a Micro-CT imaging system (QuantumGX2, PerkinElmer) under consistent imaging parameters. Image reconstruction and quantitative analyses were performed using the accompanying software to evaluate new bone formation and defect repair. All Micro-CT quantitative analyses were performed by investigators blinded to group allocation to minimize assessment bias.

Histological and staining analysis

Fixed specimens were decalcified in EDTA solution (Cat# G1105, Servicebio) for 6 weeks, then processed for routine paraffin embedding and sectioning. H&E staining and Masson's trichrome staining were conducted to assess new bone formation and tissue remodeling. Immunohistochemical staining was performed to assess the expression of osteogenesis-related proteins, and all staining procedures were conducted by Servicebio (Wuhan, China). Sections were imaged and analyzed with high resolution using a whole-slide scanning system (VS200, Olympus). Histological evaluation and image-based quantification were also conducted in a blinded manner by independent investigators unaware of experimental grouping.

Ultrastructural observation and elemental analysis of liver and kidney tissues

At 12 weeks after implantation, major organs including the liver and kidneys were harvested. Tissue samples (∼1 mm3) were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde, followed by graded ethanol dehydration, epoxy resin embedding, and ultrathin sectioning. Ultrastructural changes in hepatocytes and renal proximal tubular epithelial cells were examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). In addition, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping analysis was performed in selected regions to evaluate the distribution of LiNbO3 nanoparticles within the tissues.

RNA-seq data

The data generated in this study have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under accession number PRJNA1370686. These data will be made publicly available upon publication.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were independently repeated at least three times. Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v.9.5).

Prior to parametric statistical analysis, data distribution and homogeneity of variance were assessed to ensure the appropriateness of parametric tests. For comparisons between two groups, a two-tailed paired or unpaired Student's t-test was applied depending on the experimental design. In particular, paired t-tests were used for analyses involving bilateral calvarial defect samples within the same animal, while unpaired t-tests were used for independent group comparisons. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) followed by Tukey's multiple-comparison post hoc test was performed. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Fabrication and characterization of ultrafine LiNbO3 nanoparticles

To enable efficient intracellular internalization and subcellular force–electric modulation, it is essential to construct piezoelectric nanomaterials with ultrafine dimensions and structural integrity. Conventional approaches, including hydrothermal synthesis and ball milling, have been widely applied for inorganic piezoelectric materials; however, they often involve complex procedures and yield particles with relatively large sizes or residual contaminants, which may limit intracellular applications [31]. Here we employed rapid laser micro-area vaporization technology to ablate commercially available bulk LiNbO3 crystal, obtaining the ultrafine LN NPs (Scheme 1a). This method offers advantages including low energy consumption, environmental friendliness, and uniform nanoparticle size distribution.

Such an ultrafine size regime is particularly favorable for minimizing endocytic stress and enabling close proximity to intracellular organelles, which is a prerequisite for subcellular force–electric regulation. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements showed hydrodynamic diameters averaging 60 nm for the ultrafine nanoparticles and 1182.9 nm for the commercial large particles (Supplementary Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 3), which are larger than their corresponding sizes observed by electron microscopy. This difference is primarily attributed to the hydration layer and interfacial effects in aqueous media, rather than reflecting the actual core particle size. To comprehensively evaluate the size discrepancy between TEM and DLS measurements and to assess the colloidal stability of ultrafine LiNbO3 nanoparticles under biologically relevant conditions, dynamic light scattering (DLS) was further performed in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The results demonstrated that the nanoparticles maintained a relatively stable hydrodynamic size distribution without the emergence of pronounced aggregation peaks in serum-containing medium (Supplementary Fig. 2). Together with TEM observations showing a primary particle size of approximately 15 nm, these results indicate that the ultrafine LiNbO3 nanoparticles remain well dispersed under physiological conditions.

The apparent discrepancy between TEM and DLS measurements arises from their fundamentally different measurement principles, where TEM reflects the dry-state primary particle size, whereas DLS provides an intensity-weighted hydrodynamic diameter that is influenced by solvation layers, protein adsorption, and transient particle–protein interactions in solution [32]. Therefore, the hydrodynamic size does not directly represent irreversible aggregation but rather the effective colloidal state of nanoparticles in biological media. In this context, the ultrafine nanoparticles (∼15 nm) are considered the primary functional units responsible for cellular uptake and intracellular interactions.

Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS, Fig. 1e) confirmed Nb and O elemental presentence. Fig. 1c showed clear lattice fringes (∼0.121 nm spacing) matching the (312) plane of the trigonal LiNbO3 (Supplementary Fig. 4). FFT pattern along the [1 0 0] zone axis shows (0 1 −1), (0 2 4), and (0 1 5) spots, consistent with the theoretical crystal structure (Fig. 1d). X-ray diffraction (XRD, Fig. 1g) confirmed preservation of the trigonal crystal structure, consistent with the large particles. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Fig. 1h–j) detected Li 1s, Nb 3d, and O 1s peaks, verifying the presence of Li+ and Nb5+ oxidation states, indicating a chemically stable surface without significant impurities. Raman spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 5) confirmed trigonal lattice symmetry, with slight peak broadening at 632 cm−1 attributed to size reduction effects [33]. Preservation of the trigonal crystal symmetry and stable surface chemistry proves that size miniaturization does not compromise the intrinsic piezoelectric functionality of LN NPs.

Piezoelectric force microscopy (PFM) measurements demonstrated phase hysteresis loops and butterfly curves (Fig. 1k), indicating strong piezoelectric responses. Ultrafine LN NPs exhibited significant voltage potential changes under alternating electric fields, confirming the presence of piezoelectric nanodomains (Fig. 1l). Finite element simulations were first performed by constructing a meshed LiNbO3 nanoparticle model with a R = 7.5 nm resolution (Fig. 1m). We then applied a series of mechanical stresses to this model to emulate different ultrasound power levels, which generated three-dimensional electric-potential distributions (Fig. 1n, Supplementary Fig. 6: 50 nm) as well as quantitative surface-potential outputs under each pressure condition (Fig. 1o). Notably, the generated electrical potentials fall within a biologically relevant millivolt range, comparable to endogenous intracellular electrical fluctuations, supporting their feasibility for subcellular bioelectrical modulation. Under ultrasound activation, nanoparticles produced a potential gradient along the piezoelectric axis, leading to charge accumulation at opposing poles (Fig. 1p).

Embedded in flexible polyimide tape and stimulated at 3 W and 6 W ultrasound power, the ultrafine nanoparticles generated output voltages of ∼20 mV and ∼65 mV, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 7, Fig. 1q, and Supplementary Videos 1-2). Amplified output was sufficient to power an LED (Fig. 1r).

Notably, although large-sized LiNbO3 particles exhibited a higher macroscopic piezoelectric output under ultrasound excitation (Supplementary Fig. 8), this signal does not directly translate into intracellular force–electric efficacy due to their limited cellular internalization and restricted access to subcellular compartments.

To conceptually connect the intrinsic piezoelectric properties of ultrafine nanoparticles with their biological performance, we propose the term “force–electric nanodomains” as a conceptual framework describing spatially localized electromechanical interactions generated by ultrafine LiNbO3 nanoparticles under ultrasound stimulation in confined biological environments. This term does not represent directly measurable nanoscale electric fields, but rather serves as a descriptive framework for localized and transient piezoelectric interaction regimes arising from discrete nanoparticle–environment interfaces.

To further provide a functional indication of cellular electrophysiological responses, membrane potential changes were assessed using the voltage-sensitive probe DiBAC4(3). Compared with the control, ultrasound-only, and nanoparticle-only groups, the US + LN group exhibited increased fluorescence intensity, suggesting altered cellular membrane potential under combined stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 9). This response reflects global cellular electrophysiological behavior, which may originate from spatially confined electromechanical interactions induced by intracellular piezoelectric nanoparticles.

Collectively, these results establish ultrafine LiNbO3 nanoparticles as a structurally stable and piezoelectrically active platform capable of intracellular activation and provide a physical basis for subsequent subcellular force–electric regulation studies.

Physical prerequisites for intracellular piezoelectric modulation

After establishing that laser-fabricated LiNbO3 nanoparticles are ultrafine and exhibit stable piezoelectric responses, we next sought to define the physical prerequisites required for effective intracellular piezoelectric modulation, rather than downstream biological outcomes. Specifically, this section aims to clarify whether particle size and intrinsic piezoelectricity are both indispensable for enabling force–electric activation in close proximity to intracellular functional units, thereby establishing a justified physical basis for subsequent subcellular mechanistic investigations.

Because LiNbO3 crystallizes exclusively in a trigonal (R3c) piezoelectric phase at ambient temperature and cannot be rendered non-piezoelectric under laboratory conditions (Curie temperature >1100 °C) [34], BaTiO3 was selected as a phase-controllable reference to isolate the contribution of piezoelectricity, with particle size effects examined independently using LiNbO3 particles of different sizes. Using this design, we performed systematic single-factor controls to demonstrate that only nanoparticles combining ultrafine dimensions with piezoelectric character are physically qualified to operate within intracellular environments and thus serve as a valid platform for subsequent subcellular modulation studies (Fig. 2a).

The selected particles are detailed in the corresponding section of the Methods. Tetragonal (piezoelectric) and cubic (non-piezoelectric) BaTiO3 nanoparticles were prepared under identical laser-vaporization conditions and matched in morphology and size to the ultrafine LN NPs (Fig. 2b, d). X-ray diffraction revealed the characteristic peak splitting of tetragonal BaTiO3 (JCPDS No. 05-0626) at ∼2θ = 45°, whereas this feature was absent in the cubic phase (JCPDS No. 31-0174) (Fig. 2h) [35]. High-resolution TEM and elemental mapping confirmed the expected lattice fringes and compositions of cubic BaTiO3, tetragonal BaTiO3 and trigonal LiNbO3 (Fig. 2c, e, f, g, Supplementary Fig. 10). Under identical ultrasonic excitation, oscilloscope measurements detected ∼60 mV output from tetragonal BaTiO3, whereas no measurable signal was observed from the cubic phase (Supplementary Fig. 11), demonstrating that the non-centrosymmetric crystal structure is a necessary physical condition for ultrasound-induced electric output at the nanoscale. These results confirm that piezoelectricity governs force–electric signal generation, providing a critical physical criterion for intracellular activation.

Finite-element simulations of LiNbO3 nanoparticles under 67.48 kPa stress further revealed that, although absolute electric potential increased with particle volume (Supplementary Fig. 12), the force–electric conversion efficiency per unit volume (Φ′ = Φ/V) increased markedly as particle diameter decreased(Fig. 2i–j) [36]. This size-dependent amplification of local piezoelectric responses provides a physical explanation for why ultrafine piezoelectric nanoparticles are uniquely suited for intracellular environments, where available mechanical inputs are inherently limited and spatial confinement necessitates efficient force–electric transduction.

To isolate the contribution of particle size to intracellular accessibility, all biological comparisons were conducted under unified and gradient-validated working parameters (6 W ultrasound, 25 μg · mL−1; Supplementary Figs. 13 and 14). CCK-8 and live/dead assays confirmed acceptable cytocompatibility for all particle types after 72 h (Fig. 2k, l). Cell-section TEM at 5 h revealed substantial intracellular accumulation of ultrafine LN nanoparticles compared with large LN particles (Fig. 2m, n), while time-lapse imaging captured efficient transmembrane entry of nanoscale particles and indicated that large particles were predominantly internalized via macropinocytosis (Supplementary Fig. 15 and Supplementary Video 3, 4) [37]. These observations indicate that ultrafine size is not merely advantageous for uptake efficiency, but represents a necessary physical prerequisite for nanoparticles to access intracellular compartments that function as units for downstream regulation.

With the size prerequisite established, we next examined whether intrinsic piezoelectricity remains functionally relevant once intracellular access is achieved. Under identical ultrasonic stimulation, RT-qPCR analysis showed that tetragonal BaTiO3 (US + P-BTO) induced higher expression of early osteogenic markers Alpl, Runx2 and Col1a1 compared with cubic BaTiO3 (US + N-P-BTO) (Fig. 2p, q). By contrast, large LiNbO3 particles failed to elicit comparable transcriptional responses under the same conditions (Fig. 2o), consistent with their limited intracellular accessibility. Notably, under identical ultrasound conditions, nanoparticles with different piezoelectric properties elicited distinct biological responses, suggesting that ultrasound-induced mechanical stimulation alone cannot fully account for the observed effects.

Importantly, these transcriptional changes are interpreted here not as an endpoint of osteogenic differentiation, but as an early functional readout confirming that piezoelectric activation remains effective only when particles are both intracellularly accessible and intrinsically piezoelectric. To further evaluate whether differences in cavitation-associated effects between ultrafine LN nanoparticles and ultrafine tetragonal BTO nanoparticles contribute to their distinct biological responses, hydroxyl radical (·OH) generation was quantified using a terephthalic acid (TA) assay under identical ultrasound conditions (Supplementary Fig. 16). No significant differences were observed among the nanoparticle groups, suggesting that cavitation-associated effects make only a minor contribution to the biological outcomes observed under the ultrasound parameters used in this study.

Together, these results establish particle size and intrinsic piezoelectricity as the two indispensable physical prerequisites for intracellular piezoelectric activation. By defining these prerequisites independently of downstream biological complexity, this section provides a necessary physical framework for subsequent investigations of ultrafine LiNbO3 nanoparticles acting at intracellular and subcellular functional units, which form the central focus of the following mechanistic sections. Having established the physical prerequisites, we next evaluated the cellular uptake and biosafety of ultrafine LN nanoparticles.

Cellular uptake and endocytic pathway of ultrafine LiNbO3 nanoparticles

Ensuring safety is a fundamental prerequisite for the biomedical application of nanoparticles [38]. For intracellular piezoelectric modulation to be meaningfully interpreted, ultrafine LiNbO3 nanoparticles must satisfy two fundamental prerequisites: biological compatibility under ultrasound stimulation and efficient, non-disruptive transmembrane entry. This section therefore focuses on evaluating the biosafety profile of ultrafine LN nanoparticles and elucidating their cellular uptake behavior, with the specific aim of excluding stress-induced artifacts and establishing a reliable basis for subsequent intracellular and subcellular investigations.

In this study, bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) were divided into four groups: untreated cells (Control group), cells with ultrafine LiNbO3 nanoparticles (LN group), cells exposed to ultrasound alone (US group) and cells treated with ultrafine LN NPs under ultrasound stimulation (US + LN group). A schematic of the ultrasound exposure protocol is shown in Fig. 3a. Ultrasound parameters (6 W, 40 kHz) were selected based on a preliminary dose-dependent evaluation of cell viability under different acoustic power levels (6–24 W), identifying 6 W as the optimal condition balancing cytocompatibility and nanoparticle activation efficiency (Supplementary Figs. 13 and 14). Under the optimized nanoparticle concentration and ultrasound parameters defined previously, ultrafine LN nanoparticles exhibited excellent cytocompatibility. Live/dead staining showed excellent cell viability (Fig. 3d), and CCK-8 assays showed no significant reduction in metabolic activity after exposure (Fig. 3b). Hemolysis assays further demonstrated a low hemolysis rate of 0.45% at a concentration of 1 mg · mL−1, well below the generally accepted safety threshold of 5% (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 18) [39]. Together, these results indicate that the ultrafine LN system operates within a biologically safe window under the applied ultrasound conditions.

To further distinguish functional cellular responses from nonspecific stress effects, intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were quantified. No significant ROS elevation was observed in the LN or US + LN groups, whereas a modest increase was detected in the US-only group (Fig. 3j). The ROS elevation observed in the US group is likely attributable to ultrasound-induced mechanical effects, whereas the absence of further ROS increase in the US + LN group suggests that LN activation does not additionally amplify oxidative stress under the current conditions. These findings suggest that the biological responses induced by US-activated LN nanoparticles are unlikely to be driven primarily by ROS-mediated oxidative stress. The absence of excessive ROS generation in the US + LN group is particularly important, as abnormal oxidative stress is a common hallmark of mechanically induced cellular damage. To further assess potential inflammatory responses, we additionally evaluated macrophage polarization using RAW264.7 cells. Immunofluorescence analysis of iNOS and CD206 showed no evident shift toward a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype under US + LN treatment, suggesting the absence of obvious inflammatory immune activation under the present conditions (Supplementary Fig. 19). These findings suggest that the introduction of ultrafine LN nanoparticles does not amplify ultrasound-induced stress, but instead maintains cells in a relatively stable physiological state, thereby supporting a piezoelectric-mediated rather than purely mechanical mode of stimulation.

Consistent with this interpretation, phalloidin staining revealed markedly enhanced cell spreading in the US + LN group, with an approximately threefold increase in projected cell area compared with other groups (Fig. 3e, i). Notably, this morphological response occurred without concurrent decreases in cell viability or increases in ROS levels, indicating a functional reorganization of the cytoskeleton rather than a stress-associated morphological alteration. Given that cell spreading and cytoskeletal organization are closely linked to mechanosensing and downstream cellular responsiveness, this observation suggests that intracellular piezoelectric activation may modulate early physical interactions at the cell–substrate or membrane–cytoskeleton interface [40].

Having established biosafety, we next examined whether ultrafine LN nanoparticles could efficiently enter cells through defined endocytic pathways. Nanoparticles were fluorescently labeled with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), with successful labeling confirmed by zeta potential measurements and fluorescence imaging (Fig. 3f, g). Time-dependent fluorescence microscopy showed progressive intracellular accumulation of ultrafine LN nanoparticles within 5 h (Fig. 3h). Endocytosis inhibition experiments revealed that nanoparticle uptake was strongly suppressed by low temperature (4 °C) and genistein (GEN), whereas chlorpromazine (CPZ) exerted a comparatively minor effect (Fig. 3k) [41]. These results indicate that uptake is energy-dependent and predominantly mediated by caveolae-associated pathways.

Ultrathin-section TEM further confirmed abundant intracellular localization of ultrafine LN nanoparticles at 3 and 5 h post-incubation (Fig. 3l and m). In contrast, large LiNbO3 particles remained largely extracellular and were internalized only sporadically through macropinocytosis, forming vesicles exceeding 200 nm in diameter (Fig. 3n) [37]. Caveolae-mediated internalization of ultrafine nanoparticles, characterized by vesicular encapsulation and membrane fusion, is generally associated with reduced lysosomal burden and improved preservation of nanoparticle integrity, thereby favoring subsequent intracellular functionality.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that ultrafine LiNbO3 nanoparticles combine favorable biosafety with efficient and well-defined transmembrane entry under ultrasound stimulation. By excluding overt cytotoxicity, oxidative stress, and nonspecific mechanical damage, and by establishing caveolae-mediated intracellular access, this section provides a necessary experimental foundation for interpreting subsequent intracellular and subcellular piezoelectric modulation at defined functional execution units.

Subcellular modulation cascade by ultrafine force-electric domains

Building on the confirmed endocytic internalization of ultrafine LN NPs, we next investigated whether the intracellular piezoelectric signals generated under ultrasound stimulation could directly modulate subcellular functional units. In particular, we focused on lysosomes and mitochondria, two organelles increasingly recognized as central regulators of osteogenic competence through lysosomal signaling integration [42,43], as well as mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming [44]. Nanoparticles are generally trafficked to lysosomes as the first intracellular station after endocytosis [45]; however, the intracellular fate and functional impact of piezoelectric nanoparticles remain poorly understood. To address this, ultrathin sections of BMSCs after two days of co-culture were examined. Transmission electron microscopy combined with EDS analysis revealed vesicular structures enriched in Nb, O, P, and S (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 20). The detected phosphorus signal likely originated from the phospholipid bilayer of lysosomal membranes, whereas sulfur may be associated with lysosomal proteins or enzymes. Consistently, co-localization of LN@FITC with LysoTracker Deep Red confirmed these vesicles as lysosomes (Supplementary Fig. 25c and d). Quantitative co-localization analysis further demonstrated a Pearson's correlation coefficient (PCC) of 0.726, indicating a strong spatial association between ultrafine LN nanoparticles and lysosomal compartments (Supplementary Fig. 25e). Notably, lysosomes in the US + LN group exhibited pronounced yet localized ultrastructural remodeling, including membrane discontinuities and reduced electron-dense contents. Detailed morphological analysis identified three representative lysosomal phenotypes: (1) lysosomes with discontinuous membranes, (2) intact membranes with low-density or vacuolated regions, and (3) hybrid structures exhibiting both features (Fig. 4c–d and Supplementary Figs. 21, 22, 23). Together, these observations indicate that localized ultrafine force–electric nanodomains are sufficient to remodel lysosomal membrane structures. To further validate lysosomal membrane integrity, lysosomes were labeled using a LAMP1–mCherry plasmid. Super-resolution microscopy revealed continuous ring-like membrane staining under control conditions, consistent with previous reports, whereas discontinuous and fragmented fluorescence signals were frequently observed in the US + LN group (Supplementary Fig. 25a and b), providing direct evidence of lysosomal membrane remodeling.

However, whether such remodeling represents classical destructive lysosomal membrane permeabilization (LMP) or a more regulated lysosomal response remains unclear. To further characterize the nature of the lysosomal response induced by US-activated ultrafine nanoparticles, we sought to determine whether the observed lysosomal alterations resembled classical lysosomal membrane permeabilization (LMP), which is typically associated with extensive membrane rupture, oxidative stress, and cell death. Therefore, L-leucyl-L-leucine methyl ester (LLOMe), a well-established inducer of catastrophic lysosomal membrane damage, was employed as a positive control.

Galectin-3 is rapidly recruited to exposed luminal glycans upon lysosomal membrane damage and is widely used as a sensitive marker for LMP. As expected, LLOMe treatment induced pronounced Galectin-3 puncta formation and extensive co-localization with LAMP1-positive structures, indicating severe lysosomal membrane disruption (Fig. 4e–g). In contrast, the US + LN group exhibited only limited and spatially restricted Galectin-3 recruitment, while the overall lysosomal architecture remained largely preserved (Fig. 4h–j). In the Control, LN, and US groups, no evident Galectin-3 aggregation or lysosomal membrane disruption was observed (Supplementary Fig. 24). These observations suggest that the lysosomal response induced by US + LN is substantially milder than that caused by classical lysosome-disrupting agents.

To further define whether this lysosomal remodeling is accompanied by alterations in lysosomal functional homeostasis, we quantitatively assessed lysosomal acidification using LysoSensor-based measurements. Across different conditions (Control, LN, US, US + LN, Bafilomycin A1, and US + LN + BafA1), lysosomal pH exhibited a progressive increase, with the most pronounced elevation observed in the US + LN group (Supplementary Fig. 26). Notably, the US + LN-induced elevation in lysosomal pH was dynamically reversible, gradually returning toward baseline levels over time (Fig. 4k and l).

Given that lysosomes maintain a tightly regulated acidic lumen under physiological conditions, this reversible pH fluctuation indicates a functional perturbation of lysosomal proton homeostasis rather than irreversible membrane rupture. Consistently, neither ultrasound alone nor nanoparticle treatment alone induced comparable changes in lysosomal pH, suggesting that this effect arises from the synergistic piezoelectric activation of ultrafine LN nanoparticles under ultrasound stimulation. Furthermore, pharmacological inhibition of V-ATPase using Bafilomycin A1 recapitulated lysosomal deacidification, supporting a key role of proton pump–mediated regulation in maintaining lysosomal acidification dynamics in this process. These findings collectively indicate that lysosomal proton homeostasis is closely coupled to the intracellular electro-mechanical responses generated by piezoelectric activation of ultrafine LN nanoparticles under ultrasound stimulation.

Furthermore, LLOMe treatment resulted in marked intracellular ROS accumulation, consistent with the cytotoxic consequences of catastrophic LMP (Supplementary Fig. 31). Notably, neither excessive ROS generation nor significant loss of cell viability was observed in the US + LN group. Together, these findings indicate that US-activated ultrafine LN nanoparticles do not trigger classical destructive LMP. Instead, they induce a mild and regulated lysosomal remodeling process that preserves cellular viability and avoids overt oxidative stress.

Given the central role of lysosomes in coordinating cellular metabolism and inter-organelle signaling, we next examined whether lysosomal remodeling was accompanied by functional changes in mitochondria. After three days of co-culture, mitochondria in the US + LN group displayed shorter and more rod-like morphologies compared with elongated networks in control groups (Fig. 4m). Quantitative analysis revealed a significant increase in mitochondria with roundness values between 1.0 and 1.5 (Supplementary Fig. 28), indicative of enhanced mitochondrial fission and metabolic activation. Consistently, mitochondrial membrane potential was markedly elevated in the US + LN group, as evidenced by increased red fluorescence intensity and quantitative analysis (Fig. 4n and Supplementary Fig. 29). Collectively, these morphological and functional features indicate a metabolically activated mitochondrial state induced by intracellular piezoelectric signals.

To determine whether intracellular force–electric nanodomains also influence inter-organelle interactions, Z-axis super-resolution three-dimensional imaging was performed after three days of co-culture. In the US + LN group, mitochondria exhibited closer spatial proximity to lysosomes, accompanied by the characteristic shorter and thicker mitochondrial morphology (Fig. 4o). Fluorescence intensity profiling revealed overlapping and intersecting signals between lysosomal and mitochondrial markers, suggesting an increased frequency and intimacy of lysosome–mitochondria contacts. Together with the elevated mitochondrial membrane potential, these results indicate that ultrafine force–electric nanodomains promote functional coupling between lysosomes and mitochondria, thereby facilitating metabolic and signaling crosstalk [46]. Importantly, intracellular ROS levels remained low at this time point (Fig. 3j), indicating that enhanced organelle contacts were not associated with damage-induced mitophagy. In addition, calcium ion fluorescence staining revealed increased intracellular Ca2+ signals in the US + LN group (Supplementary Fig. 32), suggesting that intracellular piezoelectric stimulation may trigger Ca2+ release from internal calcium stores [47].

Considering the extensive functional crosstalk between lysosomes and mitochondria, we next investigated whether lysosomal remodeling contributes to the mitochondrial response induced by US + LN treatment. Pharmacological inhibition of lysosomal acidification using the V-ATPase inhibitor Bafilomycin A1 (BafA1) markedly attenuated the increase in mitochondrial membrane potential observed in the US + LN group (Supplementary Fig. 30). These findings suggest that intact lysosomal function is involved in the regulation of downstream mitochondrial activation and support the existence of a lysosome–mitochondria signaling axis in response to intracellular piezoelectric stimulation.

Notably, BafA1 treatment also resulted in a significant increase in intracellular ROS levels compared with the US + LN group (Supplementary Fig. 31). Combined with the concomitant reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential, these findings suggest that disruption of lysosomal function compromises mitochondrial homeostasis and redox balance. Together, these results further support a functional lysosome–mitochondria signaling axis underlying the cellular response to intracellular piezoelectric stimulation. Although direct measurement of transient electrical signals generated by intracellular piezoelectric nanoparticles remains technically challenging, the combined evidence from material characterization, piezoelectric measurements, computational simulations, intracellular localization analyses, and functional perturbation experiments collectively supports the existence of localized force–electric nanodomains that contribute to the observed biological responses.

Extracellular piezoelectric biomaterials have been reported to modulate mitochondrial activity through membrane-associated mechanotransduction pathways; however, whether such effects persist or are altered when piezoelectric activation occurs within intracellular compartments remains unclear. To address this spatial dependence, LiNbO3 nanoparticles with different sizes were introduced as structural controls and consistently incorporated into all subcellular analyses. Under identical culture and ultrasound conditions, compared with ultrafine LiNbO3 nanoparticles, large-sized particles exhibited markedly reduced cellular uptake efficiency, as evidenced by cell section analysis, and were predominantly localized outside lysosome-like vesicular structures. Functionally, the large-sized LiNbO3 + US group failed to induce a significant increase in mitochondrial membrane potential, in contrast to the pronounced mitochondrial activation observed in the ultrafine particle group (Supplementary Fig. 27).

These results collectively indicate that mitochondrial activation is closely dependent on the intracellular accessibility of piezoelectric nanoparticles. In contrast, extracellular or poorly internalized large-sized LiNbO3 systems mainly elicit limited membrane-level mechanical responses and are insufficient to establish a spatially confined intracellular force–electric microenvironment required for lysosome–mitochondria coupling.

In summary, ultrasound-activated ultrafine LN nanoparticles induce a regulated form of lysosomal remodeling characterized by mild and reversible alterations in lysosomal structure and proton homeostasis, rather than catastrophic lysosomal damage as observed in LLOMe-treated cells. This process is functionally distinct from classical destructive lysosomal membrane permeabilization, as it occurs without excessive ROS accumulation or cytotoxicity.

Importantly, pharmacological inhibition of lysosomal acidification using Bafilomycin A1 significantly attenuated the downstream mitochondrial activation, indicating that intact lysosomal function is required for the propagation of the lysosome–mitochondria signaling cascade. In addition, large-sized LN nanoparticles lacking efficient intracellular access failed to trigger comparable lysosomal or mitochondrial responses, further highlighting the necessity of spatially confined intracellular piezoelectric activation.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that ultrafine LN nanoparticles establish a spatially and functionally regulated lysosome–mitochondria axis through intracellular piezoelectric activation, forming subcellular functional units that translate mechanical–electrical inputs into coordinated organelle responses.

Quantitative RT–PCR analysis revealed that, at Day 7 of osteogenic induction, the US + LN group exhibited significant upregulation of multiple osteogenic marker genes, including Alpl, Col1a1, Runx2, Bmp2, and Spp1, compared with control and single-factor groups (Fig. 5e–g and Supplementary Fig. 33a). Notably, Col1a1 expression increased by more than sixfold in the US + LN group, indicating a pronounced enhancement of extracellular matrix synthesis associated with osteogenic commitment. When compared with the US + L-LN group, the US + LN group showed substantially higher expression levels of Alpl, Runx2, and Col1a1 (4.26-, 4.82-, and 1.81-fold increases, respectively; Fig. 2o), underscoring the critical role of ultrafine particle size in enabling effective intracellular force–electric activation. These trends persisted at Day 14, with key osteogenic genes such as Col1a1 and Runx2 maintaining elevated expression levels (Supplementary Fig. 33b), suggesting sustained osteogenic signaling rather than a transient response.

Furthermore, to verify the unique superiority of LiNbO3, we utilized the same fabrication process to prepare ultrafine tetragonal-phase BaTiO3 nanoparticles as a parallel inorganic piezoelectric control. Under identical concentrations, we evaluated the expression of key osteogenic differentiation genes, including Alpl, Runx2, and Col1a1. Remarkably, the results demonstrated that our ultrafine LN nanoparticles exhibited significantly superior osteogenic induction effects compared to the BaTiO3 counterparts (Fig. 2p). These results indicate that, although both piezoelectric materials are capable of promoting osteogenesis under ultrasound stimulation, LiNbO3 exhibits a more pronounced effect in our system, which may be attributed to differences in their intracellular force–electric transduction behavior at the ultrafine nanoscale. Consistent with the qRT–PCR results, Western blot analysis confirmed increased protein expression of RUNX2 and BMP2 in the US + LN group (Fig. 5h), validating that intracellular force–electric stimulation promotes osteogenic signaling at both gene and protein levels. Early-stage osteogenic differentiation was further assessed by alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining. As early as Day 3, the US + LN group exhibited visible ALP-positive staining, which intensified markedly by Day 7 and exceeded that of all other groups (Supplementary Fig. 34 and Fig. 5c), indicating accelerated early osteogenic commitment.

Immunofluorescence staining provided spatial confirmation of these molecular changes. Strong nuclear localization of RUNX2 and enhanced cytoplasmic expression of OPN were observed in the US + LN group (Fig. 5i and j), supporting the notion that intracellular force–electric nanodomains promote both transcriptional activation and matrix-associated osteogenic functions. At later stages, Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining at Day 21 revealed markedly increased calcium nodule formation in the US + LN group, with quantitative analysis confirming significantly enhanced mineral deposition compared with all control groups (Fig. 5b, d).

These results demonstrate that early subcellular activation is translated into robust late-stage mineralization. Although long-term intracellular retention of inorganic nanoparticles remains a general concern regarding potential effects on cell viability and function [48], the ultrafine size and stimulus-responsive behavior of this system are expected to mitigate such biological burden, while further long-term evaluation is warranted.

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that ultrafine LN nanoparticles, once internalized, convert external ultrasound stimulation into intracellular piezoelectric signals that precondition subcellular functional units and ultimately enhance osteogenic differentiation. Rather than acting through nonspecific mechanical stimulation, this effect arises from a defined physical cascade linking ultrasound activation, intracellular force–electric nanodomains, subcellular organelle modulation, and coordinated osteogenic gene programs. This establishes intracellular piezoelectric activation by ultrafine LN nanoparticles as an effective and controllable physical strategy for directing stem cell osteogenic fate.

Calvarial defect repair accelerated by force-electric stimulation

To translate the intracellular force–electric mechanism into an in vivo regenerative context, a composite hydrogel scaffold was designed to ensure both mechanical integrity and sustained activation of ultrafine LN nanoparticles. Gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA), selected for its biocompatibility and cell-adhesive properties [49], was reinforced with 12 wt% hydroxyapatite (HA) to enhance mechanical robustness and co-loaded with ultrafine LN NPs. Stress–strain measurements confirmed an increase in compressive strength compared with pure GelMA scaffolds (Supplementary Fig. 35), while FTIR spectra verified the successful incorporation of GelMA, HA, and LN components (Supplementary Fig. 36). Although characteristic LN peaks were less pronounced due to their low loading content, SEM imaging revealed increased surface roughness and enlarged pore structures upon HA and LN incorporation (Supplementary Fig. 37), features favorable for cell attachment and bone regeneration.

In vitro degradation analyses further indicated that ultrasound stimulation accelerated scaffold surface remodeling and nanoparticle release. SEM observations at Days 7 and 14 showed roughened surfaces and partial disruption of the hydrogel mesh in ultrasound-treated scaffolds (Supplementary Fig. 38), accompanied by reduced Ca and P signals detected by EDS (Supplementary Fig. 39 and Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). Quantitative degradation analysis demonstrated that, after 28 days, ultrasound-treated GelMA + HA + LN scaffolds exhibited a 6.2% higher degradation ratio than their non-ultrasound counterparts (Supplementary Fig. 40). Consistently, ICP–MS measurements confirmed sustained Li+ release (Supplementary Fig. 41), supporting the suitability of the scaffold system for prolonged in vivo piezoelectric activation. Li + release from the GelMA/LN hydrogel exhibited a sustained release profile, reaching a peak at approximately day 21 followed by a slight decrease at later time points. This late-stage reduction likely reflects a diffusion–equilibrium process within the hydrogel network, where the readily releasable fraction of LN nanoparticles becomes gradually depleted and the system approaches a dynamic balance between internal retention and external diffusion. Overall, the release behavior indicates a prolonged and controlled ionic release from the hydrogel platform over the experimental period.

A bilateral critical-size calvarial defect model (10 mm diameter) was established in New Zealand rabbits to evaluate bone regeneration at 4- and 12-weeks post-implantation (Fig. 6a). Notably, a pre-stimulation period was introduced after hydrogel implantation to allow gradual nanoparticle release and cellular uptake, thereby reducing the contribution of early extracellular nanoparticle populations during subsequent ultrasound activation. Surgical procedures, including defect creation, scaffold implantation, and ultrasound stimulation, are illustrated in Fig. 6b and Supplementary Fig. 42. No visible erythema or swelling was observed in ultrasound-treated regions throughout the experimental period (Supplementary Fig. 43a). Rabbits underwent adaptive training and did not require anesthesia during ultrasound sessions, enabling consistent and minimally invasive stimulation (Supplementary Video 6). In addition to these regenerative outcomes, H&E staining of major organs at 12 weeks revealed no pathological abnormalities (Supplementary Fig. 44), indicating a favorable biosafety profile of the ultrafine LN nanoparticles. The potential tissue accumulation of non-degradable nanoparticles also warrants attention.

To further evaluate the long-term biosafety and in vivo fate of ultrafine LiNbO3 nanoparticles, we examined major metabolic organs including the liver and kidney at 12 weeks post-implantation. Transmission electron microscopy combined with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (TEM-EDS) analysis was performed on hepatocytes and renal proximal tubular epithelial cells to assess potential nanoparticle retention (Supplementary Fig. 45). No electron-dense nanoparticle-like aggregates were observed in either tissue, and no detectable Nb-related elemental signals were identified at the cellular level. Meanwhile, the ultrastructural integrity of mitochondria, lysosomes, and cellular membranes remained well preserved without evidence of pathological alteration. These findings collectively indicate that, under the present localized delivery and ultrasound-triggered release system, ultrafine LiNbO3 nanoparticles do not exhibit detectable accumulation in major metabolic organs within the experimental timeframe.

Although no pathological abnormalities were observed, and no evidence of nanoparticle retention was detected in the examined organs, we acknowledge that quantitative biodistribution analysis (e.g., ICP-MS), long-term clearance kinetics, and evaluation across additional organs remain necessary to fully define their systemic fate and chronic biosafety. Therefore, prolonged retention in specific tissues cannot be completely excluded, and further systematic studies will be required for future translational development.

Intracellular ultrafine force-electric response reprograms osteogenic transcriptional networks

Building on the observation that ultrasound-activated ultrafine LN nanoparticles robustly enhance osteogenic differentiation, and that such effects critically depend on efficient intracellular entry and subcellular modulation, we next sought to elucidate the downstream molecular programs associated with intracellular piezoelectric activation, particularly those constituting the intracellular osteogenic units that translate subcellular physical cues into osteogenic transcriptional outputs (Fig. 8a). To this end, transcriptomic sequencing was performed across Control, LN, US, and US + LN groups. High Pearson correlation coefficients (>0.97) confirmed the robustness and reproducibility of the datasets (Supplementary Fig. 46). Differential expression analysis revealed 1822 upregulated genes in the US + LN group compared with the US group (Supplementary Fig. 47). Heatmaps and volcano plots highlighted extensive transcriptional remodeling, including numerous genes associated with osteogenic differentiation and matrix regulation (Fig. 8b and Supplementary Fig. 48a–d). Among the most prominently upregulated genes, Ibsp exhibited an approximately eightfold increase relative to the US group and a twofold increase relative to the Control group. Ibsp has been reported as a downstream target of the PI3K–AKT signaling pathway [52], which was also significantly enriched in our KEGG pathway analysis (Fig. 8d and Supplementary Fig. 48f). Consistently, qPCR validation confirmed marked upregulation of PI3K–AKT pathway components together with Ibsp in the US + LN group (Fig. 8e–g), supporting the involvement of this signaling axis in force–electric–mediated osteogenic regulation. To validate whether PI3K–AKT signaling is functionally required for this process, pharmacological inhibition was performed using the PI3K-specific inhibitor LY294002 during osteogenic induction. Inhibition of PI3K signaling markedly attenuated the expression of key osteogenic markers, including Runx2, Col1a1, and Alpl, in the US + LN-treated group (Supplementary Fig. 49). These results indicate that blockade of PI3K–AKT signaling significantly compromises the pro-osteogenic effects induced by US-activated LN nanoparticles.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that activation of the PI3K–AKT pathway is not merely correlative but functionally required for the osteogenic response elicited by the US + LN system, providing mechanistic evidence linking transcriptomic alterations to functional outcomes.

Given that lysosomal remodeling was identified as an upstream subcellular event following intracellular piezoelectric activation, we next examined whether lysosomal function is also required for the downstream osteogenic response. Pharmacological inhibition of lysosomal acidification using Bafilomycin A1 (BafA1) significantly attenuated the expression of osteogenic markers induced by US + LN treatment (Supplementary Fig. 50). These findings indicate that lysosomal remodeling is not merely associated with, but functionally required for, the pro-osteogenic effects triggered by intracellular piezoelectric stimulation, further supporting a lysosome-dependent signaling cascade linking subcellular activation to osteogenic differentiation.

To further connect transcriptional changes with the previously observed subcellular responses, we analyzed transcriptomes from cells after seven days of nanoparticle co-culture. Notably, the transcriptional changes observed in the US + LN group converged on multiple functional modules that collectively constitute intracellular osteogenic units, including lysosomal processing, mitochondrial quality control, antioxidant defense, and extracellular matrix regulation (Fig. 8h). These included lysosomal proteases (CTSS, CTSH, CTSL), the lysosomal membrane protein LAMP2 [53], the mitochondrial damage-sensing kinase PINK1 [54], antioxidant enzymes HMOX1/GSTA2 [55], as well as extracellular matrix regulators CCN3 and CCN5 [56] and the growth factor IGF1 [57]. Intracellular ROS levels remained low in the US + LN group, consistent with HMOX1/GSTA2 upregulation, suggesting that active antioxidant defenses limit oxidative stress.

Integrating these molecular signatures with earlier ultrastructural and functional observations, we propose that intracellular force–electric nanodomains initiate a cascade of subcellular responses involving lysosomal membrane remodeling and altered proteolytic activity, accompanied by mitochondrial functional adaptation and quality control engagement. Concurrent activation of antioxidant programs appears to constrain oxidative stress, while IGF1/PI3K–AKT signaling and extracellular matrix–associated factors may amplify and stabilize osteogenic transcriptional outputs. In vivo immunohistochemical analyses further supported activation of this signaling pattern at the tissue level (Fig. 8i and j).

In summary, intracellular piezoelectric activation by ultrafine LN nanoparticles orchestrates lysosomal, mitochondrial, antioxidant, and signaling modules as integrated osteogenic units. This coordinated intracellular response reprograms transcriptional networks, linking subcellular physical cues to gene expression changes and providing a mechanistic framework for how intracellular force–electric signals drive osteogenic fate decisions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study establishes a strategy for the intracellular application of piezoelectric nanomaterials to regulate osteogenic differentiation, extending force–electric stimulation from conventional extracellular or membrane-level mechanosensing toward intracellular functional units. Using ultrafine (∼15 nm) LiNbO3 nanoparticles, we show that piezoelectric materials can be efficiently internalized by stem cells while retaining ultrasound-responsive force–electric conversion capability, thereby providing a basis for localized intracellular force–electric responses within subcellular environments.

Unlike conventional piezoelectric biomaterials that primarily act through membrane-associated mechanotransduction, ultrafine LN nanoparticles preferentially operate within intracellular organelle compartments. Mechanistically, the system induces regulated lysosomal remodeling, enhances lysosome–mitochondria coupling, and promotes mitochondrial metabolic activation, collectively forming a coordinated intracellular cascade that supports osteogenic gene programs.

Importantly, pharmacological inhibition of lysosomal acidification significantly attenuated both mitochondrial activation and osteogenic outcomes, demonstrating that lysosomal functionality is required for the downstream biological response and supporting a lysosome-dependent signaling axis in this system.

Functionally, this intracellular force–electric strategy enables robust osteogenic differentiation in vitro and sustained bone regeneration in vivo. The confined intracellular localization of ultrafine LN nanoparticles enables efficient coupling between force–electric conversion and subcellular regulatory machinery while avoiding excessive oxidative stress, thereby providing a controlled and biocompatible activation profile.

Although direct measurement of transient intracellular electrical signals generated by piezoelectric nanoparticles remains technically challenging, the combined evidence from material characterization, piezoelectric measurements, computational simulations, intracellular localization analyses, and functional perturbation experiments collectively supports a mechanistic model in which localized force–electric nanodomains contribute to the observed biological responses.

Beyond bone regeneration, this work provides a generalizable framework for intracellular physical modulation using ultrasound-responsive piezoelectric nanomaterials, offering opportunities for regulating organelle-level processes such as intracellular trafficking, energy metabolism, and organelle communication in a spatiotemporally controlled manner.
