From prediction to action: a retrospective observational study on the real-world implementation of Critical Interventions (CrIs), an AI-based clinical decision support system changing clinical behavior in the emergency department
Authors: Hansol Chang, Jae Yong Yu, Hyunjung Park, Yee Jun Song, Sejin Heo, Jong Eun Park, Gun Tak Lee, Se Uk Lee, Taerim Kim, Hee Yoon, Sung Yeon Hwang, Won Chul Cha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOptimal emergency department (ED) care for critically ill patients relies on prompt recognition and initiation of necessary interventions. The Critical Interventions (CrIs) system was designed to predict the need for urgent procedures, including arterial line insertion, oxygen therapy, high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC), intubation, and inotropes or vasopressors, based solely on triage data. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of the CrIs system in enhancing clinical decision-making and reducing time to intervention, as well as to assess user satisfaction post-implementation.MethodA retrospective observational study was conducted at a tertiary hospital in South Korea, comparing time to interventions and outcomes between pre- and post-implementation phases of CrIs. The system’s predictive performance was analyzed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). Additionally, user feedback was collected through surveys.ResultsThe study included 37,632 ED visits, with notable predictive performance for CrIs across various interventions (AUROC: 0.879 for arterial lines, 0.906 for oxygen therapy, 0.95 for HFNC, 0.928 for intubation, and 0.88 for inotropes/vasopressors). Post-implementation, significant reductions were observed in the median time to intervention for A-lines, oxygen therapy, and HFNC. Survey responses highlighted overall satisfaction with CrIs, though suggestions for interface improvements were noted.ConclusionThe CrIs system demonstrated robust predictive accuracy and significantly reduced time to critical interventions, enhancing ED operational efficiency. Future enhancements should focus on improving user interface and communication features to maximize system utility.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-025-03234-x.Keywords: Emergency department, Critical care, Artificial intelligence, Clinical decision support systems, Implementation science

Introduction

For good care in the emergency department (ED), especially for critically ill patients, quick recognition of the need for intervention and subsequent action is essential [1–5]. A triage system, quick sequential organ failure assessment (qSOFA), and modified early warning score (MEWS) are implemented, which may be calculated as soon as the patient arrives at the ED for quick identification of patients who are critically ill in the early stages [6–9]. Following the emergence of artificial intelligence (AI), clinical decision support systems (CDSS) based on AI have been developed in several formal research [10–13].

One of the CDSSs for identifying patients who require interventions at the triage stage is the Critical Interventions (CrIs), which predict the need for six interventions, including the insertion of an arterial line, massive transfusion, inotropes, and vasopressors, high flow nasal cannula (HFNC), oxygen support, and intubation. CrIs were integrated into a tertiary hospital’s electronic medical record system [14]. The CrIs are helpful for more than just providing an expected patient prognosis. They also guide specific action by forecasting the requirement for the intervention itself [15].

Many studies have been conducted on developing or validating AI-based CDSS [11, 16–18]. however, only a few studies have evaluated their performance, assessed their impact, or examined user experience in real-world clinical practice after CDSS implementation Real-world integration of predictive models into electronic health record (EHR) systems remains challenging, and previous reviews have emphasized that continuous re-evaluation and upgrading are essential to ensure performance and usability [22–24].

This study aimed to assess the effectiveness of CrIs, changes in decisions, and timing of interventions following CrIs deployment. Moreover, we used a survey to examine the users’ perceptions of CrIs.

Methods

This was a retrospective, uncontrolled before–after cohort study conducted at a single tertiary hospital in South Korea. The study evaluated the effects of implementing Critical Interventions (CrIs) in the electronic medical record (EMR) system by comparing pre- and post-implementation performance and by analyzing user experiences through surveys of emergency department (ED) medical professionals. To facilitate comparison, CrIs outcomes were retroactively calculated for the pre-implementation phase, allowing the analysis of intervention times and outcomes across different Korea Triage and Acuity Scale (KTAS) categories. The study followed the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guideline for observational research to improve transparency in reporting.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Samsung Medical Center, which waived the requirement for individual informed consent due to the retrospective, observational, and anonymous nature of the study (IRB No. 2022-04-116-001). The user survey was approved separately with informed consent (IRB No. 2022-05-155-001). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Development of Critical Interventions

Utilizing data from patient visits to the emergency department of a Korean emergency center spanning the years 2016 to 2018, researchers developed CrIs. These scores are designed to predict the necessity for six distinct interventions, relying exclusively on information obtained at triage, without recourse to laboratory or imaging test results. The methodology employed for this predictive modeling was the Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) technique [14]. A-line insertion, inotrope and vasopressor use, massive transfusion, oxygen supply (O2), a high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC), and intubation were the six interventions that CrIs predicted in previous research. However, massive transfusion was excluded in the final version of CrIs due to its low frequency of occurrence. Except for the massive transfusion protocol, the CrIs included five interventions when deployed [14]. The variable obtained immediately after a patient visits the ED for triage in the final version of the CrIs for implementation, including six vital sign data, two demographic data, and two patient arrival data, were used as input variables for prediction. Vital sign data, including body temperature (BT), heart rate (HR), respiration rate (RR), Oxygen Saturation (SpO2), systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP), were used. Age and sex were included in demographic data analyses. Both the method of arrival, which reveals the mode of transportation used to reach the hospital, and the route of arrival, which reveals whether the patient was referred from somewhere else, were included in the arrival information.

Researchers re-trained the CrIs utilizing data from patient visits to the emergency department of a Korean emergency center spanning the years 2016 to 2020, and temporal validation was done with data from 2021.

Implementation to EMR and definition of exposure

After implementation, CrIs outputs were fully integrated into the emergency department EMR and were displayed to all physicians on the “ED patient selection screen list,” the main working page of the ED workflow. The CrIs icon indicated the number of predicted interventions, together with the probability and recommendation results for each of the five interventions in a single window (Fig. S3). Thresholds for intervention alerts were determined using the Youden index during model development.

For the study design, the post-CrIs period was defined as the time after October 28, 2021, when CrIs had been implemented and its outputs were automatically visible to all ED physicians, meaning that clinicians were consistently exposed to CrIs recommendations during their routine workflow. In contrast, the pre-CrIs period referred to the time before this system integration, when CrIs predictions were retrospectively generated for analysis but were not available to treating clinicians.

Primary outcomes: predictive performance of CrIs and time to intervention for arterial line insertion, oxygen therapy, high-flow nasal cannula, intubation, and vasopressor use.

Model performance was assessed using AUROC, sensitivity, and specificity in both the pre- and post-implementation groups. Analyses of time to intervention focused primarily on CrIs-positive cases, where specific actions were recommended by the system, with adjustments for age, sex, vital signs, and level of consciousness. Comparisons were also made across CrIs-negative groups and between pre- and post-implementation groups, regardless of CrIs results, to evaluate potential confirmation bias and secular trends.

The relationship between CrIs predictions and patient severity was examined by comparing the number of CrIs alerts with the KTAS, MEWS, NEWS and qSOFA, which are routinely used to assess acuity at triage. KTAS is a five-level triage system adapted from the Canadian Triage Acuity Scale, incorporating both the chief complaint and symptom characteristics [19, 20]. This analysis allowed us to explore whether CrIs alerts were aligned with established measures of acuity during triage.

Survey-based exploratory analyses evaluated emergency physicians’ experiences with CrIs implementation, including perceived usability, workflow integration, and areas for improvement. These results provided additional context on clinical acceptance and system integration.

Data preparation

Age and sex were categorical variables considered during the study period. Time information was also obtained, including ED arrival, entry, and discharge. The KTAS score, qSOFA score, and final disposition information, including discharge, admission, ICU admission and ED mortality, were also collected for each patient. We examined the details of the five interventions, such as whether and when they were carried out, as temporal and categorical variables. Information regarding interventions was collected at each visit.

Data preprocessing and missing data handling

We excluded interventions conducted more than 24 h after admission to the ED. CrIs prediction results were also collected. There were no CrIs prediction result data in the pre-CrIs group, as this group visits ED before the CrIs were implemented, CrIs result was calculated newly and added for outcome measure.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive Statistics were used for demographic characteristics and for comparing the time to intervention between the pre-CrIs and post-CrIs groups. Categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages, and continuous variables as means and standard deviations. Because most intervention times were skewed and not normally distributed, medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) were reported, and comparisons were made using Wilcoxon tests at a 5% significance level.

Descriptive statistics were used for demographic characteristics and for comparing time to intervention between the pre-CrIs and post-CrIs groups. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages, and continuous variables as means and standard deviations. Because most intervention times were skewed and not normally distributed, medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) were reported, and comparisons were made using Wilcoxon tests at a 5% significance level.

For adjusted median values, we applied quantile regression (50th percentile; median regression), which estimates conditional quantiles and is more robust to outliers and non-normal data. Covariates including age, sex, vital signs, and AVPU scores were adjusted for in the regression. While these variables are commonly used in ED risk modeling, they also overlap with those used to train CrIs, which may introduce overadjustment bias. This limitation has been noted explicitly in the Discussion. AUROC values were compared using DeLong’s test.

Survey

A survey of clinicians was conducted to evaluate their user experience with the CrIs system. Participants were clinicians who had worked in the emergency department (ED) of the study site for at least one year prior to the implementation of CrIs, allowing for meaningful comparisons. The survey consisted of three main sections: implementation, content, and system. The implementation and content sections each included three subsections, while the system section included two. Each subsection contained multiple structured questionnaire items. In addition, a set of open-ended, free-text questions was provided to gather detailed feedback (Table S9). (Table S9) [21, 22].

Results

During the study period, a total of 53,484 adult ED visits were recorded: 24,742 in the pre-CrIs phase and 28,742 in the post-CrIs phase.

After applying exclusion criteria—patients under 18 years of age, trauma cases, cardiac arrests, and dead on arrival (DOA)—6,490 patients were excluded from the pre-CrIs group and 9,362 from the post-CrIs group, leaving 18,252 and 19,380 patients, respectively. The cohort selection process is summarized in Supplementary Figure S1. Demographic characteristics are summarized in Table 1. ED hospital mortality was higher in the post-CrIs period (Table 1). This difference likely reflects patient-level factors, as the post-CrIs cohort included a higher proportion of critically ill patients (KTAS level 1–2) and those with unstable vital signs. Given the observational design, these differences cannot be attributed solely to CrIs implementation.

Table S1 further demonstrates a reduction in median intervention times post-CrIs implementation for all procedures except HFNC. Notable reductions include a 20.7% decrease in A-line administration time and a 60.6% decrease in intubation time. Table S2 shows that CrIs-positive results led to a significant reduction in intervention durations, with a minimum 65% decrease in time to completion, affirming CrIs’ efficacy in optimizing intervention speed. Predictive efficacy of CrIs

Supplementary Table S5 compares the performance of CrIs, KTAS, MEWS, NEWS, and qSOFA in predicting ICU admission and ED mortality. CrIs maintained comparable or superior predictive performance relative to these early warning scores. Specifically, CrIs outperformed qSOFA, MEWS, and NEWS and was comparable to KTAS for ICU admission prediction (AUROC 0.747). For ED mortality prediction, CrIs demonstrated the high performance (AUROC 0.927), surpassing KTAS, MEWS, and qSOFA.

Survey results

A survey of 18 ED physicians (median experience of 40.5 months, Table S6) revealed generally positive feedback on CrIs implementation and reliability (Fig. 2, Tables S7, S8, and S10). Most participants agreed that the system was operational and trustworthy. However, some negative feedback was received regarding the pop-up window system, with a few respondents expressing concerns about its appropriateness (disagree n = 6, strongly disagree n = 2). Additionally, free-text responses highlighted suggestions for improving CrIs functionality, display, and feedback methods (Tables S7 and S8).

Discussion

This study evaluated the real-world clinical implementation of an AI-based Clinical Decision Support System (CDSS), CrIs, in the emergency department (ED). While most prior studies remained limited to model development or retrospective validation, our study provides observational evidence on the real-world implementation of an AI-CDSS in routine ED practiceSpecifically, we assessed its clinical impact on intervention timeliness, alignment with actual decisions, and user acceptance. These findings are meaningful in demonstrating how AI-CDSS can be operationalized to influence clinical behavior and support emergency care.

Compared to earlier studies that limited evaluation to model performance in silico or under controlled settings our research contributes to the growing body of literature on real-world AI-CDSS integration [18, 23–25] our research contributes to the growing body of literature on real-world AI-CDSS integration. In particular, the sustained AUROC above 0.8 across interventions indicates that CrIs retained acceptable predictive performance in practice, consistent with results from its original development phase. This supports the feasibility of generalizing ML-based support tools to clinical workflows, though periodic retraining remains essential to accommodate evolving hospital environments. In fact, CrIs has already been retrained with recent data since the conclusion of this study.

In our study, CrIs implementation was associated with reduced time-to-intervention, even though we did not observe improvement in ED mortality or ICU admission. This demonstrates that AI-driven alerts can reduce delays in time-sensitive clinical actions, which is a crucial aspect of improving ED throughput and potentially patient outcomes. The higher mortality observed in the post-CrIs period is more plausibly explained by differences in patient acuity and demographics, and residual confounding is inevitable. Nevertheless, prior research supports the clinical relevance of timely interventions. For instance, delayed vasopressor initiation—beyond several hours after hypotension onset—has been independently associated with increased mortality in septic shock patients [26] More recent analyses suggest that early-high vasopressor use within the first 6 h can be associated with lower mortality [27] Moreover, coordinated and timely emergency interventions have been linked to improved survival in critical care settings [28] (Chang et al., 2023). Taken together, while our observational design does not allow causal inference, these findings suggest that shortening time-to-intervention may plausibly contribute to improved outcomes. Future prospective studies are warranted to confirm these effects.

Additionally, the post-implementation group showed a higher rate of intervention consistency with CrIs recommendations, reflecting improved alignment between model suggestions and clinician judgment. As shown in Table 3, intervention rates were consistently higher for CrIs-positive than CrIs-negative encounters in both periods, with a greater separation after implementation when CrIs outputs were visible to clinicians. Increases were most evident for interventions requiring early decisions (e.g., arterial line insertion, oxygen therapy). Taken together, these patterns might suggest that access to CrIs recommendations contributed to prompting clinical actions—potentially by reinforcing clinician confidence or serving as a reminder—particularly in high-acuity cases. Subgroup analyses among critically ill patients showed similar tendencies, although sample sizes were limited and further validation in controlled settings is warranted.This study observed significant reductions in action durations for arterial lines (A-lines), oxygen therapy (O2), and High-Flow Nasal Cannula (HFNC) after implementingCrIs, with HFNC showing the most notable improvement in the KTAS subgroup analysis. The authors suggest that procedures with lower decision-making barriers, such as non-invasive interventions like O2 and HFNC, were more influenced by CrIs. These procedures are easy to administer, even for patients with limited treatment options due to severe illnesses like cancer. Conversely, more invasive procedures, such as intubation and the use of inotropes, require additional steps like central line insertion and involve long-term planning (e.g., ICU admission), making them less susceptible to CrIs’ influence.

In the CrIs-negative group, intervention times generally increased, potentially because clinicians hesitated after seeing a negative CrIs result. Alternatively, the similarity between clinicians’ decisions and CrIs predictions could explain this, as CrIs are developed through supervised learning. However, whether this is a direct effect of CrIs remains uncertain. Furthermore, when we specifically analyzed CrIs-negative patients (Supplementary Table S3), no significant reductions in intervention times were observed between the pre- and post-implementation periods, and some interventions even showed longer times after CrIs integration. This finding might suggest that the improvements in timeliness were more concentrated in CrIs-positive patients, and that the system’s recommendations may have contributed to these changes, rather than the results being solely explained by a general temporal trend.

In subgroup analyses based on the Korean Triage and Acuity Scale (KTAS), only the use of High-Flow Nasal Cannula (HFNC) in KTAS levels 1 and 2 showed significant reductions in intervention times. A trend toward faster actions was observed in KTAS levels 1, 2, and 3, excluding interventions involving inotropes and vasopressors in the KTAS 3 group. For high-risk patients, CrIs might prompt earlier actions, but the study population was too small to confirm statistical significance. Further in-depth studies and interviews are needed to determine whether CrIs significantly affect decision-making in critically ill patients. No significant improvements were noted in KTAS levels 4 and 5, though these groups had fewer interventions, making comparisons less meaningful.

Additionally, the number of projected CrIs correlated with patient severity, as seen in comparisons with KTAS, MEWS, NEWS and qSOFA scores. CrIs could serve as early severity prediction scores, complementing KTAS, MEWS, NEWS andqSOFA, especially during the initial triage stage in the Emergency Department (ED). This suggests that CrIs may be valuable in assessing patient severity early in the clinical process [29–31].

Finally, survey results indicated that most ED physicians found the system acceptable and useful, though they also highlighted areas for refinement, such as alert fatigue and interface design. Most participants agreed on the clinical significance of CrIs-recommended actions, but opinions differed on whether CrIs fully met their information needs. Some respondents requested more detailed information and additional interventions, highlighting areas for improvement.

Overall, the feedback underscored the importance of continuous system refinement. Although user feedback was incorporated during CrIs development, participants suggested further improvements, particularly in the interface and content, demonstrating the need for ongoing interaction between developers and users to ensure the system meets evolving needs in practice [17, 32].

Some users concur with the additional functions of CrIs. A user-feedback system was also requested. This feedback could not be obtained through data analysis. In-depth interviews should be considered in future studies after prolonged use of the CDSS.

For future studies, CrIs long term effect should be analyzed and an update for CrIs would be needed, and it would be based on this study result. CrIs performance should be maintained while considering adding prediction of other interventions or removing original intervention prediction. The interface should be updated based on user opinion. Experience with implementation and evaluating CDSS functions after implementation might be helpful for other CDSS implementations. These findings may inform future observational and interventional studies of CDSS implementation in diverse clinical settings.

Limitation

Due to the retrospective nature of this study, controlling the composition of the control and test groups was not feasible. This also complicated the evaluation of CrIs’ independent effects in the clinical setting. However, efforts were made to compare data from patients before and after CrIs implementation using the same predictive model. Comparative analyses were conducted based on CrIs outcomes and the KTAS, MEWS, NEWS and qSOFA.

While we reported ED mortality and ICU admission as patient outcomes, the higher mortality observed in the post-CrIs period is more plausibly explained by differences in patient demographics and acuity rather than by the effect of CrIs itself. Because this study was not a randomized controlled trial, residual confounding is inevitable. Accordingly, we did not interpret outcome differences as causal effects. Instead, we focused on time to intervention as the primary evaluative measure, as it is a direct and process-oriented endpoint less susceptible to case-mix variation.

Also, this study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, which could have biased the results. In Korea, ED visit volumes markedly decreased in 2020 but rebounded in 2021–2022, and operational challenges such as PPE and isolation protocols generally increased delays in care [33, 34]. Therefore, if any bias was introduced, it likely underestimated rather than exaggerated the effect of CrIs.

As our primary analysis focused on CrIs-positive patients, which may introduce confirmation bias, we additionally examined CrIs-negative cases (Table S3) but found no significant improvements.Additionally, ED staff received training on CrIs for proper interpretation. Future randomized controlled trials (RCTs) may be needed to clarify CrIs effects. Being a single-center study, the results may not generalize to other settings. Finally, because our adjustment set (age, sex, vital signs, and level of consciousness [AVPU]) overlaps with variables used to train CrIs, the analyses are susceptible to overadjustment bias and residual confounding, with the direction and magnitude of any bias uncertain. The study emphasizes the importance of external validation for broader use. While surveys were conducted, in-depth interviews were not, leaving some aspects of human-computer interaction unexplored. Future studies should include qualitative research, such as interviews, to better understand clinicians’ use of CrIs in decision-making.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the real-world clinical impact of CrIs, an AI-based clinical decision support system that translates predictive recommendations into clinical actions. After implementation, CrIs was associated with more timely execution of critical interventions—particularly arterial line insertion, oxygen therapy, and high-flow nasal cannula. These findings suggest that CrIs may support earlier decision-making and improve care processes in emergency settings. Feedback from ED clinicians further highlighted the need for ongoing interface and workflow refinements to optimize usability and adoption.
