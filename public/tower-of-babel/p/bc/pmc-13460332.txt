Dose-dependent Effects of Propofol on GABAergic Neurotransmission in the Pre-Bötzinger Complex and Its Consequences on Respiratory Rhythm
Authors: Junli Jiang, Shiqing Ai, Ya Chen, Zhiyuan You, Feng Shen, Chengdong Yuan, Qiuping Cheng, Chunchun Tang, Liang Zhou, Shouyang Yu, Lin Zhang, Haiying Wang, Wei L. Shen, Weifeng Yu, Tian Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Propofol acts primarily through γ‐aminobutyric acid type A receptor–dependent central inhibition, while local inhibitory microcircuits in the pre-Bötzinger complex (pre-BötC) exert bidirectional control over respiratory rhythm. Although propofol is widely recognized as a simple respiratory depressant, it remains unclear whether propofol mimics endogenous inhibitory signaling to disrupt pre-BötC circuit function in a complex, dose-related manner. This study aimed to test the hypothesis that propofol modulates pre-BötC activity via dose-dependent reshaping of local inhibitory microcircuits rather than producing only monotonic respiratory suppression.Methods:Using a mouse model, the authors combined whole-body plethysmography, optogenetic manipulation of pre-BötC γ-aminobutyric acid–mediated (GABAergic) neurons, conditional VGAT knockout, and patch clamp electrophysiology in acute medullary slices to examine how propofol modulates respiratory rhythm and inhibitory microcircuit activity.Results:Propofol induced dose-dependent, bidirectional respiratory effects ranging from mild excitation to marked suppression with increasing anesthetic depth. These changes correlated with concordant, dose-dependent excitatory and inhibitory effects of the drug on glutamatergic neurons in the pre-BötC. Light intensity–dependent, sustained optogenetic activation of GABAergic neurons in the pre-BötC recapitulates the dose-dependent, bidirectional effects of propofol on respiratory activity, while concurrent propofol administration and GABAergic pre-BötC activation act synergistically to induce respiratory arrest. Selective ablation of GABAergic neurons in the pre-BötC increased respiratory rate in awake animals, facilitated respiratory recovery after a single propofol bolus but increased the incidence of apnea during continuous propofol infusion.Conclusions:These results suggest a dose-dependent bidirectional effect of propofol on respiratory drive via the modulation of GABAergic neurons in the pre-Bötzinger complex.

General anesthesia is indispensable for clinical procedures, and propofol is one of the most widely used intravenous anesthetics because of its rapid onset and recovery. In the field of anesthesiology research, focusing on the mechanisms underlying general anesthesia–induced unconsciousness and the effects of general anesthesia on fundamental vital functions is necessary. Among these critical issues, alterations in respiratory function triggered by general anesthesia represent a key scientific question with profound clinical relevance. However, the mechanism underlying respiratory changes induced by propofol remains unclear. The pre-Bötzinger complex (pre-BötC), a compact medullary nucleus located in the ventrolateral medulla, acts as the central kernel for respiratory rhythm generation.1 Perturbations in pre-BötC activity induced by anesthetic agents are closely associated with respiratory depression, a life-threatening clinical complication during anesthesia,2 which further underscores the urgency of elucidating the precise modulatory effects of propofol on this pivotal respiratory circuit.

Propofol exerts its core pharmacologic effects predominantly by potentiating the function of γ‐aminobutyric acid type A (GABAA) receptors, the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter receptors that mediate neural inhibition throughout the central nervous system.3 This γ-aminobutyric acid–mediated (GABAergic) inhibitory signaling is not only the foundation of the anesthetic action of propofol but also plays a vital physiologic role in the pre-BötC.1 The pre-BötC harbors a dedicated local inhibitory microcircuit governed by GABAergic neurotransmission; inhibitory interneurons within this microcircuit release GABA to act on inspiratory glutamatergic neurons, preventing the excessive excitation of inspiratory neurons, shortening the refractory period of these neurons, and accelerating the inspiratory–expiratory transition, which ultimately presents as an increase in respiratory frequency at the behavioral level.4 Experimental manipulation of this circuit strongly influences the effect of inhibitory transmission on respiratory rhythm: low-intensity photostimulation of pre-BötC inhibitory neurons (20 Hz, 0.5 to 2.0 mW) increases the respiratory frequency, whereas higher intensities (greater than 3.0 mW) suppress rhythmic output, and very strong activation (greater than or equal to 5.0 mW) can completely abolish respiratory oscillations.5 These findings indicate that the respiratory rhythm is exquisitely sensitive to changes in the inhibitory drive. Considering the pharmacologic target of propofol and the inherent physiologic function of this pre-BötC inhibitory microcircuit, a pivotal unresolved mechanistic question emerges: does the propofol-induced respiratory disturbance mimic the modulatory effects exerted by inhibitory interneurons within this local microcircuit on inspiratory glutamatergic neurons?

However, the bulk of previous research merely characterized propofol as a simple monotonic inhibitor of the pre-BötC, suggesting that increasing propofol concentrations merely elicit progressively stronger neural inhibition. This narrow research perspective fails to account for the nuanced regulatory effects of propofol, ignoring the potential linkage between anesthetic-induced respiratory changes and the physiologic modulation of the pre-BötC local inhibitory microcircuit. Even more crucially, whether propofol disturbs respiratory function by replicating the regulatory mode of endogenous inhibitory interneurons rather than exerting unidirectional, blanket suppression of pre-BötC activity remains elusive.

Given these research gaps, this study aimed to clarify how propofol modulates pre-BötC respiratory microcircuits. To address this scientific question, we explored the dose-dependent regulatory characteristics of propofol on respiratory activity, by combining optogenetics and conditional knockout to further investigate the role of GABAergic neurotransmission in the pre-BötC on respiratory rhythm.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Adult male and female C57BL/6 mice (Shanghai Jiesijie Experimental Animal Co., Ltd., China), Slc32a1-Cre mice (Jackson Stock No. 028862; West Grove, USA), Slc17a6-Cre mice (Jackson Stock No. 016963; West Grove), VgatloxP/loxP mice (Jackson Stock No. 012897; West Grove), and Rosa26R-tdTomato mice (Jackson Stock No. 007908; West Grove) were housed on a 12-h light/dark cycle with unrestricted food and water. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with national and institutional guidelines, and standard precautions were implemented to minimize animal stress and the number of animals used in each experiment. The protocol used in the current study was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Zunyi Medical University.

Plethysmography and Respiratory Analysis

Respiratory activity was measured using a whole-body plethysmography (WBP) system (TOW-Int Tech Inc., China) with an animal chamber that was maintained at room temperature and ventilated with air. Every 3 days before the start of the measurements, the mice were individually placed into the chamber and allowed to acclimate for 0.5 h.6 The chambers were connected to the sensor probe. Breathing traces were collected using TOW-Int software and exported to MATLAB (MathWorks, USA) for analysis. Each breath was automatically segmented based on airflow crossing zero, as well as quality control metrics. Respiratory parameters (e.g., peak inspiratory flow, instantaneous frequency, pause length, and tidal volume) for each breath, as well as averages across states, were then calculated. The instantaneous frequency was defined as the inverse of breath duration. The pause length was defined as the expiratory period after the airflow decreased less than 0.1 ml/s.7 The pause period was initially a prolonged airflow around or just above 0 ml/s, terminating with an increase in expiratory airflow. Note that the airflow in milliliters per second and tidal volume in milliliters are approximations of the true volumes.

Electrophysiology

Before recording resting membrane potential (RMP), adult mice (6 to 8 weeks old) were deeply anesthetized with a ketamine/xylazine cocktail (ketamine, 90 mg kg−1; xylazine, 10 mg kg−1). Afterward, the animals were perfused with 30 ml of ice-cold (4°C) N‐methyl‐d‐glucamine (NMDG) slicing solution (93 mM NMDG, 2.5 mM KCl, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 30 mM NaHCO3, 20 mM HEPES, 25 mM dextrose, 5 mM sodium ascorbic acid, 2 mM thiourea, 3 mM sodium pyruvate, 10 mM MgSO4, and 0.5 mM CaCl2; the pH was adjusted to 7.3 to 7.4 with HCl). The brain was submerged in oxygenated (95% O2 and 5% CO2) NMDG slicing solution, and the medulla region was glued to agar. Sections of the medulla (260 µm) were obtained using a vibratome. The slices were allowed to recover in a chamber containing 37°C oxygenated NMDG slicing solution for 10 min and then transferred to a chamber containing room-temperature oxygenated holding solution (92 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM KCl, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 30 mM NaHCO3, 20 mM HEPES, 25 mM dextrose, 5 mM sodium ascorbic acid, 2 mM thiourea, 3 mM sodium pyruvate, 2 mM MgSO4, and 2 mM CaCl2) and incubated for 1 h. Slice electrophysiology experiments were conducted with an axon amplifier. Patch clamp recordings were performed in a submerged chamber perfused with oxygenated artificial cerebrospinal fluid (124 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM KCl, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 24 mM NaHCO3, 12.5 mM dextrose, 1 mM MgCl2, and 2 mM CaCl2) at 2 ml min−1. For RMP measurements, patch pipettes (3 to 7 Ω) were filled with an intracellular solution (135 mM potassium gluconate, 5 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM KCl, 5 mM EGTA, 10 mM HEPES, 2 mM Mg-ATP, 0.1 mM Li-GTP, and 0.5 mM CaCl2 · 2H2O, the pH was adjusted to 7.3 using Tris base). Recordings were obtained from only one cell per brain slice. The signals were amplified with a Multiclamp 700B amplifier and Digidata 1550 A and pClamp10.7 software (Molecular Devices, USA). The signals were filtered at 2 kHz and digitized at 10 kHz. The data were analyzed with Clampfit 10.7, Mini Analysis 6.0.1 (Synaptosoft, USA), and Prism 8 software (GraphPad Software, USA).

Stereotactic Injection

The mice were anesthetized via the continuous inhalation of isoflurane. Standard surgery was performed to expose the surface of the brain above the brainstem. The coordinates used for pre-BötC injection were −6.96 mm anterior to the bregma, ±1.2 mm lateral to the bregma, and −5.5 mm from the dura. The adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) were stereotactically injected with a glass pipette connected to a microsyringe pump (Legato R130; KD Scientific, USA) at a flow rate of 30 nl/min to avoid local damage to the brain tissue. The pipette remained in place for at least 20 min after the virus injection. For the fiber photometry experiments, AAV injections were unilateral and were followed by ipsilateral optical fiber implantation (see “Optical Fiber Implantation”). For the Vgat conditional knockout experiments, bilateral AAV injections were administered, followed by unilateral optical fiber implantation.

AAV Vectors

rAAV-Ef1α-DIO-hChR2-eGFP-WPRE-pA, rAAV-CaMKIIa-GCaMP6s-WPRE-hGH-pA, rAAV-hSyn-CRE-WPRE-hGH-pA, and rAAV-hSyn-WPRE-hGH-pA (BrainVTA, China) were used.

Optical Fiber Implantation

Thirty minutes after the AAV injection, a ceramic ferrule with an optical fiber (230 μm in diameter; N.A. 0.37) was implanted such that the fiber tip was located above the pre-BötC (unilateral: −6.96 mm anterior and +1.2 mm lateral to the bregma and −5.4 mm from the skull). The ferrule was then secured to the skull with dental cement. After implantation, the skin was sutured, and antibiotics were applied to the surgical wound. Optogenetic and fiber photometry experiments were conducted at least 3 weeks after optical fiber implantation.

For optogenetic manipulation, the output of the laser was measured and adjusted to 2, 5, or 10 mW before each experiment. The pulse onset, duration, and frequency of light stimulation were controlled by a programmable pulse generator attached to the laser system. After AAV injection and fiber implantation, the mice were housed individually for 3 weeks before the behavioral tests.

Optogenetic Stimulation In Vivo

Three weeks after the virus injection, the mice were transferred to experimental cages for 1 week of habituation. At least 2 days before the experiments were performed, a patch cord was connected to the optical fiber to allow acclimation. On the day of the experiment, the patch cord was replaced with a new cord, and the laser was turned on. Blue light was delivered with several protocols. The laser power used to stimulate GABAergic neurons in the pre-BötC ranged from 220 mW. Before the response to laser stimulation was measured, the mice were allowed to acclimate to the recording box for WBP for at least 3 days for 30 min every day. On the day of recording, tail vein catheterization was performed, and a small amount of heparin was applied to the catheter tip to prevent coagulation. After catheterization, the mouse was placed in the WBP recording system. Before the injection of propofol, the mice were placed in the recording box for at least 10 min to obtain baseline data. Afterward, propofol was continuously infused via the tail of the mice using a micropump at a rate of 5 mg · kg−1 · min−1 for 1 h. Laser stimulation was conducted once stable respiratory activity was achieved after the propofol infusion or during the awake stage. Among the 13 mice that received the viral injection, 4 were excluded because of incorrect viral expression verified by histology; thus, 9 mice were included in the statistical analysis.

The same animals were used for both awake optogenetic stimulation and optogenetic stimulation under propofol anesthesia. The mice were randomly assigned to one of two groups to minimize potential confounding effects and reduce animal use: one group underwent awake stimulation first and then anesthetized stimulation 5 days later, whereas the other group underwent anesthetized stimulation first and then awake stimulation 5 days later. The data obtained under awake and anesthetized conditions were subsequently pooled and analyzed separately. This design controlled for interanimal variability while minimizing the total number of animals used.

Calcium Fiber Photometry Recordings

GCaMP fluorescence was measured using a multichannel fiber photometry system; the data were filtered at 40 Hz, digitized at 500 Hz, and then recorded using recording software (Thinker Tech Nanjing Bioscience, Inc., China). The mice were connected to the system using an optical fiber cable. The optical power at the fiber tip was set to 20 to 30 μW to minimize bleaching. After the baseline recording, the animals were anesthetized with a single dose of propofol (twice the median effective dose [2ED50] = 61.2 mg/kg greater than 3 min), and the recording was continued until the animal recovered. Finally, the data were imported into MATLAB 2023a (MathWorks) for analysis. ΔF/F0, which represents the change in fluorescence intensity, was calculated as (F – F0)/F0 (where F is the test fluorescence signal, and F0 is the mean of the basal value).

RNAscope

Adult male C57BL/6 mice (6 to 8 weeks) were anesthetized with a continuous infusion of isoflurane or propofol and then immediately transcardially perfused with saline and 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The control mice were quickly perfused after the induction of anesthesia with isoflurane. The brains were removed and postfixed with 4% PFA for 24 h and then incubated with 30% sucrose in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) two times until the tissue sank to the bottom of the tube. The tissue was then embedded in optimal cutting temperature medium, cut with a cryomicrotome at 15 μm, and mounted directly onto Superfrost Plus slides, which were later used for RNA in situ hybridization (RNAscope) or immunofluorescence microscopy. After slice preparation and mounting on slides, the brain tissues were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions (catalog No. 302535; Advanced Cell Diagnostics [ACD], USA). Briefly, RNAscope was applied to the sections using a multiplex fluorescent version 1 reagent kit (catalog No. 323100; ACD) and an RNAscope kit (catalog No. 320293; ACD). The following probes were used: Mm-Gabrb3 (catalog No. 492441; ACD), Mm-Fos (catalog No. 316921; ACD), Mm-slc32a1-C3 (catalog No. 319191-C3; ACD), and Mm-slc17a6-C2 (catalog No. 319171-C2; ACD).

Neurobiocytin Staining

For a subset of whole-cell recordings, the recording solution additionally contained 1% biocytin. After the electrophysiologic recordings were complete, slices containing cells filled with biocytin were immediately fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in 4% sucrose in PBS at 4°C overnight. The slices were then washed with PBS (Gibco), permeabilized with H2O2 (0.3% in 100% methanol) for 30 min, rinsed three times with 0.1% PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 (PBST), and then blocked with 3% nonfat milk and 10% normal goat serum for 1 h each. The slices were incubated for 2 h with fluorescein isothiocyanate (catalog No. S3762; Sigma–Aldrich, USA; 1:600), blocked, and incubated overnight at 4°C to visualize the biocytin-filled neurons. The sections were imaged using a confocal microscope (SP8; Leica, Germany).

Validation of VGAT Knockout

For the VGAT knockout experiments, vgatloxp/loxp mice were divided into two groups and injected with either AAV-hSyn-Cre-eGFP or AAV-hSyn-eGFP. Each group initially consisted of nine animals. After the viral expression sites were histologically verified, six animals per group were included in the final statistical analysis. VGAT deletion within the pre-BötC was validated using in situ hybridization, which allows region- and cell-specific assessments of Vgat transcript loss in this anatomically small nucleus. Because the pre-BötC is located in a very restricted region of the ventrolateral medulla, bulk quantitative polymerase chain reaction or Western blot analysis would inevitably include surrounding tissue and therefore cannot provide pre-BötC–specific quantification. After the stereotaxic injection of AAV-hSyn-Cre-eGFP, we confirmed both an effective reduction in the Vgat signal and accurate viral localization to the pre-BötC. Animals lacking detectable expression in the target region or exhibiting viral spread beyond the pre-BötC were excluded from the analysis.

RNA Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization

An RNA probe for Vgat was constructed according to the description provided by the Allen Brain Atlas (https://www.brain-map.org). The primers used to clone the Vgat probe were as follows: forward, GCCATTCAGGGCATGTTC; and reverse, AGCAGCGTGAAGACCACC. Similar protocols were used as previously described. For fluorescence in situ hybridization, the mice were transcardially perfused with diethyl procarbonate–treated PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. The brains were then postfixed overnight at 4°C and sectioned using a vibratome at a thickness of 25 μm. Free-floating sections were sequentially washed with PBS and 0.1% PBST for 10 min and quenched with 0.5% H2O2 (vol/vol) in PBS for 30 min, washed with 0.1% PBST for 10 min, and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in 2× saline-sodium citrate (SSC) for 20 min, followed by another round of washing with 0.1% PBST for 10 min. The sections were then acetylated with 0.25% acetic anhydride (vol/vol) in 0.1 M triethanolamine (pH 8.0) for 10 min, followed by two rounds of washing with PBS for 10 min. Then the sections were incubated with the prehybridization solution for 2 h in a 60°C water bath and incubated with the hybridization solution (catalog No. C10919; Ribo Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) containing mouse antisense cRNA probes at 60°C for 20 h. Subsequently, the sections were washed with 4× SSC for 5 min three times in a 42°C water bath, followed by washes with 2× SSC for 10 min and 1× SSC for 10 min. Finally, the sections were imaged using a confocal microscope (SP8; Leica).

Immunofluorescence Staining

The animals were deeply anesthetized with a ketamine cocktail (ketamine, 90 mg kg−1; xylazine, 10 mg kg−1), and the mice were then immediately transcardially perfused with saline followed by 4% ice-cold PFA dissolved in 0.1 mol/l PBS (pH 7.4). The brains were removed and postfixed with 0.1 mol/l PBS supplemented with 4% PFA overnight at 4°C. The brain tissues were transferred to 30% sucrose and incubated for 24 h, followed by another incubation in 30% sucrose in PBS at 4°C for 2 to 3 days. The brain tissues were frozen and cut into sections (25 µm thick) on a cryomicrotome.

For immunostaining, the sections were washed with PBS three times and treated with PBS supplemented with 1% Triton X-100 for 30 min. Subsequently, the sections were washed with 0.3% Triton X-100 and then incubated with an immunofluorescence blocking solution at room temperature for 1 h to block nonspecific staining. Next, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies against somatostatin (rabbit, 1:500, catalog No. T-4103; Peninsula Laboratories International, Inc., USA) and choline acetyltransferase (mouse, 1:500, catalog No. AMAB91130; Sigma) overnight at 4°C, washed with 0.3% PBST three times, and incubated with secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor 594 (donkey anti-rabbit, 1:1,000, catalog No. ab150073; Abcam, United Kingdom) and Alexa Fluor 405 (goat anti-mouse, 1:1,000, catalog No. ab150113; Abcam). The sections were subsequently imaged under a confocal microscope (SP8; Leica).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 8 (GraphPad Software). The experiments examining the effects of a single induction dose of propofol on respiration and the optogenetic stimulation experiments used a within-subject design, in which the same animals were analyzed before and after different treatments. In contrast, experiments involving VGAT knockout mice and experiments with different continuous infusion rates employed a between-subject design in which the animals were randomly assigned to independent experimental groups. The experimental unit was a single animal. No a priori sample size calculation was performed for this experiment. The sample size was determined based on previous studies using similar experimental paradigms and physiologic measurements in mice and was increased relative to that in those studies to improve the statistical robustness.8 The inclusion criteria were correct viral expression at the target site and stable respiratory recordings. Exclusion criteria included incorrect viral expression verified by post hoc histology or unstable respiratory signals that prevented a reliable analysis.

Randomization was performed using a computer-generated random sequence. Animals were assigned to the experimental groups using random numbers generated with the RAND function in Microsoft Excel. Group allocation and data analysis were performed by one investigator, while a separate investigator conducted the experiments and performed the outcome assessment. Therefore, the outcome assessment was performed independently of the group allocation and data analysis. Histologically verified animals in which virus expression was not detected in the target area were excluded from the statistical analysis.

Respiratory outcome measures included the inspiratory time (Ti), expiratory time (Te), pause duration, instantaneous breathing frequency, peak inspiratory airflow, tidal volume (VT), and minute volume (MV). For experiments using a within-subject design, baseline respiratory parameters obtained before drug administration or without optogenetic stimulation were used as control values, and measurements obtained during different experimental conditions (e.g., the excitation phase, inhibition phase, or photostimulation) were normalized to the corresponding baseline values for the same animal. For experiments involving sustained optogenetic laser illumination, repeated-measure analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used because multiple time points (before, during, and after stimulation) were compared within the same animals.

The normality of each data set was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For paired or repeated-measures comparisons, repeated-measures one-way ANOVA was used for normally distributed data, and the Friedman test was used for nonnormally distributed data. When appropriate, post hoc multiple-comparison tests were performed using the Holm–Sidak correction for parametric analyses and Dunn’s test for nonparametric analyses. For comparisons between independent groups, unpaired t tests were used for normally distributed data, and Mann–Whitney tests were used for nonnormally distributed data. For experiments involving more than two independent groups (e.g., different infusion rates), ordinary one-way ANOVA was applied. In whole-cell patch clamp experiments examining the effects of propofol on glutamatergic neurons, paired t tests were used.

The data are presented as the means ± SD, unless stated otherwise. A percent change greater than 15% relative to the baseline was defined as physiologically significant in the normalized analyses. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Propofol-induced Respiratory Perturbations

We monitored the breathing of awake and anesthetized mice using WBP to quantitatively characterize propofol-induced alterations in respiratory behavior. Using the up-and-down method, we first determined the median effective dose (ED50) that induced the loss of the righting reflex as 30.62 mg/kg (supplemental fig. 1, https://links.lww.com/ALN/E581). A dose of 2 × ED50 (61.2 mg/kg) was subsequently selected as the effective anesthetic dose for inducing unconsciousness; under this condition, propofol produced biphasic respiratory effects, with breathing initially exhibiting excitatory changes followed by pronounced inhibition compared with the baseline (fig. 1, B and C). A within-subject design was used, in which the baseline value of each mouse served as its own control, and postdrug excitatory and inhibitory phases were separately normalized to the baseline (n = 7).

Under awake conditions, each respiratory cycle consisted of two phases—inspiration and expiration—which accounted for approximately two fifths and three fifths of the total breath duration, respectively (fig. 1D). After the propofol injection, the inspiratory phase (measured by Ti) and expiratory phase (measured by Te) were markedly shortened during the initial excitatory stage. In contrast, during the subsequent inhibitory stage, both the inspiratory phase and the expiratory phase became substantially longer. Notably, the inhibitory stage showed a third phase, an initial expiration period characterized by minimal or absent airflow (less than 0.1 ml/s). Such expiratory pauses lasted up to several hundred milliseconds, accounting for approximately one third of the average breath length (fig. 1, D and E).

As described previously, Ti is shaped by a stretch-evoked feedback signal from the lungs that terminates inspiration, a mechanism reflected in the positive correlation between Ti and peak inspiratory airflow.7 After propofol administration, this correlation was preserved and even strengthened during the excitatory stage: although Ti was shortened, peak inspiratory airflow increased, resulting in a VT comparable to that of the baseline breaths. While this correlation was not observed for the breath in the inhibitory stage, despite a longer Ti, it did not compensate for the decrease in VT induced by the lower peak inspiratory airflow (fig. 1F). In summary, the Ti and Te during the propofol-induced excitatory stage significantly decreased (propofol-induced excitation/baseline; a percent change greater than 15% was defined as significant), and the instant frequency and peak inspiratory airflow increased. In contrast, the Ti and Te during the inhibitory stage increased (propofol-induced inhibition/baseline; a percent change greater than 15% was defined as significant), and the instant frequency and peak inspiratory airflow decreased significantly. In addition, a noticeable pause occurred during this period (pause length/breath length; fig. 1, I to L). We determined whether sex differences in the bidirectional excitatory–inhibitory effects induced by propofol existed by examining the effect of propofol on respiratory activity in female mice. The results showed that female mice displayed the same phenomena, indicating no detectable sex-specific differences in this response (supplemental fig. 3, https://links.lww.com/ALN/E583). These findings indicate that propofol induces a bidirectional, dose-dependent modulation of respiratory timing and airflow patterning. The structured nature of these changes suggests regulation by the respiratory central pattern generator, particularly circuits within the pre-BötC.

Both vesicular glutamate transporter 2 (VGlut2) and developing brain homeobox 1 (Dbx1) are markers for the same pre-Bötzinger population and are responsible mainly for the generation of the respiratory rhythm.9,10 We recorded Ca2+ signals in glutamatergic neurons by microinjecting AAV-CaMKII-GCamps and implanting optical fibers above the pre-BötC to verify whether the excitatory/inhibitory effects were caused by propofol acting on glutamatergic neurons. We found that the calcium signal significantly increased during the first 60 s after the injection of a single dose of propofol, after which the calcium signal decreased significantly, consistent with the results of the in vivo experiments (supplemental fig. 2, A to C, https://links.lww.com/ALN/E582).

Bath application during patch clamp recordings provides a means to examine the immediate neuronal responses to a drug. Previous work has shown that propofol suppresses the frequency of inspiratory bursts recorded from the C4 phrenic nerve root, an effect that is reversible with GABAA receptor antagonists.11 However, these experiments are usually conducted in neonatal animals because the GABAergic system undergoes a developmental shift driven by changes in chloride transporter expression from being depolarizing (“excitatory”) to hyperpolarizing (“inhibitory”). Therefore, excluding the influence of this developmental timeline on the observed effects of propofol is crucial. We prepared medullary slices from VGlut2::Ai14 mice (both male and female) and recorded the RMP of glutamatergic neurons to assess the effects of different propofol concentrations on neuronal excitability. To avoid potential confounding effects of altered neuronal conditions within each slice, only one neuron per slice was recorded after exposure to either 5 or 10 µM propofol. The results revealed that a 5 μM propofol dose significantly increased the RMP from −60.57 ± 2.359 mV to −57.86 ± 2.385 mV (n = 7, P = 0.0156, paired t test), and an action potential was induced in two of the seven neurons by 5 μM propofol. However, a 10 μM propofol dose significantly decreased the RMP from −55.5 ± 1.628 mV to −58 ± 1.291 mV (n = 6, P = 0.0021, paired t test; supplemental fig. 2, D to F, https://links.lww.com/ALN/E582). Taken together, our results confirmed that propofol-induced a bidirectional excitatory–inhibitory effect in a dose-dependent manner.

Effects of the Photostimulation of GABAergic Neurons on Respiratory Activity in Awake Mice

Glutamatergic neurons in the pre-BötC express both GABAA and γ‐aminobutyric acid type B receptors, and inhibitory regulation within local microcircuits plays a crucial role in stabilizing the respiratory rhythm.12,13 The sustained activation of GABAergic neurons in the pre-BötC of medullary slice preparations modulates respiratory activity in a light intensity–dependent manner.5 In addition, glutamic acid decarboxylase 1 (GAD1+) pre-BötC neuronal activity during inspiration modulates the balance between excitation and inhibition in pattern-generating neurons to increase or decrease the burst duration and amplitude.14 Given that propofol acts primarily through the GABAA receptor–mediated increase in inhibition, these observations suggest that the bidirectional excitatory and inhibitory effects induced by propofol may arise from a disturbance in inhibitory microcircuit regulation within the pre-BötC. In the current study, AAV-DIO-ChR2 was selectively expressed in GABAergic neurons in the pre-BötC (supplemental fig. 4, https://links.lww.com/ALN/E584), and experiments involving short-duration or sustained photostimulation under awake conditions were conducted to elucidate the effect of a local inhibitory imbalance on breathing.

We divided each breathing cycle into four phases: phase 1 (inspiratory acceleration), during which the airflow increases from zero to its peak as inspiration intensifies; phase 2 (inspiratory deceleration), during which the airflow progressively decreases until the end of inspiration; phase 3 (expiratory acceleration), during which the airflow increases from zero to its peak in the opposite direction as expiration begins; and phase 4 (expiratory deceleration), during which the airflow gradually decreases until the completion of expiration. A within-subject design was used. Baseline respiratory parameters were obtained from breathing cycles without optogenetic stimulation in each animal. Respiratory parameters measured during optogenetic stimulation delivered at phase 1, phase 2, phase 3, and phase 4 of the respiratory cycle in the same animal were separately normalized to their corresponding baseline values. Statistical analyses were performed on these normalized data (n = 9; 13 mice received a viral injection for optogenetic manipulation; 4 were excluded because of incorrect viral expression, leaving 9 mice for the analysis).

After the mice acclimated to the environment and their breathing activity stabilized, we delivered short-term pulse stimulation while the mice were awake (100 ms, 0.5 Hz, 5 mW, 30 pulses). Stimulation delivered during phase 1 produced marked reductions in Ti, Te, and peak inspiratory airflow (stimulus/baseline; percent change > 15%), accompanied by an increase in the instantaneous breathing frequency. When the stimulus was applied during phase 2, Ti was slightly lower than that of breaths without stimulation, whereas the Te and pause length increased, after which the instant frequency slightly decreased. When the stimulus was applied during phase 3, Te and the pause length increased significantly, and the instant frequency decreased. When the stimulus was applied during phase 4, Te and the pause length increased significantly, after which the instant frequency clearly decreased. Increasing the laser power of single pulses (100 ms, 0.5 Hz, 10 mW, 30 pulses; data not shown) produced qualitatively similar but more pronounced effects. Thus, the respiratory disturbances induced by photostimulation were both phase dependent and intensity dependent (fig. 2). These findings indicate that GABAergic inhibition exerts phase-specific modulation of the respiratory frequency.

We investigated how sustained stimulation of pre-BötC inhibitory neurons affects respiratory activity by applying epochs (duration, 10 s) of sustained laser illumination (20 Hz, 20-ms pulse trains, laser power of 2 to 10 mW), with recovery intervals of more than 5 min between epochs. Unilateral laser illumination of pre-BötC regions caused rapid and reversible perturbations in the respiratory frequency under awake conditions. Weak stimulation (2.0 mW) did not significantly affect the breathing parameters (fig. 3A). Increasing the power to 5 mW significantly shortened Te without affecting Ti, leading to an increase in the instantaneous breathing frequency (fig. 3B). After 10-mW stimulation, both Ti and Te were markedly reduced, resulting in a pronounced increase in the instantaneous frequency and a reduction in the peak inspiratory airflow (fig. 3C). These findings demonstrate that the sustained activation of GABAergic neurons modulates respiratory activity in a light intensity–dependent manner under awake conditions.

Effects of the Photostimulation of GABAergic Neurons on the Respiratory Activity of Mice under Anesthesia

We further performed single pulses and sustained photostimulation in mice under anesthesia to clarify the function of GABAergic neurons within the pre-BötC under the state of the inhibitory imbalance mediated by propofol. After the mice acclimated to the chamber and their breathing activity stabilized, a dose of 2 × ED50 (61.2 mg/kg) was administered, followed by a continuous infusion at 2 mg · kg−1 · min−1 to maintain an appropriate anesthetic depth (n = 8; 13 mice received a viral injection for optogenetic manipulation; 4 were excluded because of incorrect viral expression, and 1 animal was lost to recording, leaving 8 mice for the analysis).

Under these anesthetized conditions, a short-term pulse stimulation was delivered (100 ms, 0.5 Hz, 5 mW, 30 pulses). Compared with unstimulated breaths, photostimulation delivered during phase 1 significantly reduced Ti, Te, and peak inspiratory airflow, resulting in an increase in the instantaneous breathing frequency. In contrast, stimulation during phase 3 markedly prolonged Te and the pause duration, leading to a subsequent decrease in the instantaneous frequency. Similarly, stimulation during phase 4 resulted in significant increases in Te and the pause length, followed by a clear reduction in the instantaneous frequency (fig. 4). These results indicate that under propofol anesthesia, the effects of short-term stimulation on breathing are similar to those observed in the awake state.

Respiratory Recovery after a Single Propofol Injection Is Accelerated by Conditional VGAT Knockout in the Pre-BötC

We further investigated the role of GABAergic neurons in regulating the respiration of mice under anesthesia by stereotactically injecting an AAV-hSyn-Cre vector into VgatloxP/loxP mice to knock out Vgat within the pre-BötC. Vgat transcription was substantially suppressed after knockout (fig. 6A). Despite the loss of VGAT—the vesicular transporter required for GABA and glycine release—basic respiratory function remained intact, consistent with previous reports indicating that GABAergic transmission is not essential for generating the core respiratory rhythm. However, VGAT deletion resulted in a significant increase in the respiratory frequency, accompanied by pronounced decreases in both Ti and Te (fig. 6C). These findings suggest that while GABAergic signaling is not required for rhythmogenesis per se, it plays a critical role in fine-tuning respiratory patterning, including inspiratory–expiratory transitions and the overall regulation of the breathing rate.

After a single dose of propofol (2ED50, 61.2 mg/kg) was injected, both the loxpcon and loxpcre groups showed a significant suppression of respiratory activity, but the loxpcre group recovered earlier than the loxpcon group did (fig. 6E). We further analyzed the breath changes at 4 min after the propofol injection, which represents the stage at which changes in breathing are most obvious. Although propofol significantly decreased the instant frequency in both the loxpcon and loxpcre groups, the peak inspiratory flow and pause did not change significantly in the loxpcre group compared with those at baseline (fig. 6, F and G). The duration of propofol-induced respiratory depression was much shorter in the loxpcre group than in the loxpcon group.

We further assessed how VGAT knockout influences the activity of excitatory neurons—the primary drivers of respiratory rhythm—by performing real-time calcium imaging to monitor their calcium dynamics in mice with or without VGAT deletion. We compared changes in excitatory neuronal activity before and after propofol administration in both groups. Propofol-induced inhibition of excitatory neurons in loxpcon mice for much longer than in loxpcre mice, and the minimum ΔF/F of loxpcon mice was significantly lower than that of loxpcre mice (supplemental fig. 6, https://links.lww.com/ALN/E586).

Continuous Moderate Sedation with Propofol Leads to the Activation of Pre-BötC GABAergic Neurons

Pre-BötC GABAergic neurons play a crucial role in maintaining respiratory stability under anesthesia, and we hypothesized that during the continuous propofol infusion, the respiratory network may reach a new equilibrium that supports a stable respiratory pattern at a given anesthetic depth. We tested this hypothesis by maintaining propofol anesthesia using an induction dose of 2 × ED50 (61.2 mg/kg) followed by a continuous infusion of either 2 or 5 mg · kg−1 · min−1 for 1 h. Respiratory activity throughout the infusion period was continuously monitored using WBP. After the continuous infusion of propofol, the respiratory frequency and MV of the propofol infusion groups (2 and 5 mg · kg−1 · min−1) were significantly increased during the first 2 min compared with the baseline value in each group and the value at the same time point in the control group. As the drug was further infused through the tail vein, the mouse entered an anesthetized state, accompanied by a decrease in the respiratory rate. Five minutes after drug infusion, significant differences in the respiratory rate were observed among these three groups. The MV value in the 5 mg · kg−1 · min−1 group further decreased 10 min after the propofol infusion. No significant change in the VT was detected during the first 2 min after the propofol infusion, and the VT significantly decreased after 6 min of propofol infusion. The VT in the 5 mg · kg−1 · min−1 group further decreased 10 min after the propofol infusion (fig. 7, A to C).

We further compared the details of the breathing patterns among these groups during the last 10 min of the continuous propofol infusion. Compared with the awake group, both the 2 and 5 mg · kg−1 · min−1 groups had significantly higher Ti and Te values and lower peak inspiratory flow, instant frequency, and MV values. Compared with the awake and 2 mg · kg−1 · min−1 groups, the 5 mg · kg−1 · min−1 group experienced a longer pause duration. In addition, the VT in the 5 mg · kg−1 · min−1 group was significantly lower than that in the awake group. These results indicate that propofol inhibits respiratory activity in a dose-dependent manner. When deep sedation is initiated by applying a higher infusion rate, the decrease in the respiratory rate is primarily due to the increase in the expiratory time and the significant increase in the pause length (fig. 7, D to O).

We subsequently tested the blood gas levels of the mice after the continuous propofol infusion at 2 and 5 mg · kg−1 · min−1 for 1 h. Compared with the normal ranges, the mice receiving a continuous infusion of 2 mg propofol exhibited mild hypoxia at the end of the infusion, which was characterized by a slight decrease in the partial pressure of oxygen (Pao2) in some animals, a slight increase in the partial pressure of carbon dioxide (Paco2), a marked reduction in the arterial oxygen saturation (Sao2), a slight decrease in the pH, and a slight decrease in the bicarbonate (HCO$3−$) level. In contrast, the continuous infusion of 5 mg propofol led to pronounced hypoxia and acidosis; significant decreases in Pao2, Sao2, pH, and HCO$3−$; and a significant increase in Paco2 (supplemental fig. 7, https://links.lww.com/ALN/E587).

Next, we evaluated the expression of c-Fos in GABAergic and glutamatergic neurons in the pre-BötC after the propofol infusion at different rates for 1 h using RNAscope. The number of c-Fos+ glutamatergic neurons was significantly lower in the 5 mg · kg−1 · min−1 group than in the control and 2 mg · kg−1 · min−1 groups. In addition, the number of c-Fos+ GABAergic neurons was significantly greater in the 2 mg · kg−1 · min−1 propofol infusion group than in the control and 5 mg · kg−1 · min−1 propofol groups. The number of these neurons did not differ significantly between the control group and the 5 mg · kg−1 · min−1 group (fig. 7, P to R). These results show that GABAergic neurons in the pre-BötC were significantly activated after the propofol infusion at 2 mg · kg−1 · min−1 for 1 h but inhibited at higher infusion rates of propofol, which indicates that propofol might play a crucial role in the modulation of the respiratory rhythm and adaptive adjustments (such as under conditions of high carbon dioxide concentrations or hypoxia).

Conditional Knockout of VGAT in the Pre-BötC Leads to Apnea after a Continuous Propofol Infusion

We performed WBP recordings greater than 1 h of propofol infusion in loxpcon and loxpcre mice to clarify the role of GABAergic neurons in respiratory activity under continuous propofol infusion. After 10 min of baseline recording, the mice were infused with propofol at 5 mg · kg−1 · min−1 for 60 min. We detected a significant increase in the baseline respiratory frequency between the conditional pre-BötC vgat knockout mice and the control mice. After the continuous propofol infusion for approximately 30 min, the loxpcre mice tended to experience apnea, whereas the mice in the loxpcon group maintained decreased but regular respiratory activity until the end of the propofol infusion (continuous infusion for 1 h; fig. 8, B to D).

Discussion

The results of the current study delineate the critical role of pre-BötC GABAergic transmission in mediating propofol-induced respiratory changes, and our core findings challenge the conventional view that propofol acts as a simple unidirectional suppressor of respiratory neural circuits. We observed that propofol elicits dose-dependent, bidirectional respiratory alterations characterized by a shift from respiratory excitation to profound inhibition as the anesthetic depth increases, which is consistent with the bidirectional regulatory properties of GABAergic signaling within the pre-BötC. Notably, the optogenetic activation of pre-BötC GABAergic neurons drives distinct respiratory responses in a state-dependent manner: increasing the respiratory frequency in awake mice but precipitating apnea in mice under propofol anesthesia, a discrepancy mediated by the intensity of local inhibitory microcircuit transmission. Additionally, the ablation of pre-BötC GABAergic neurons accelerates respiratory recovery after the administration of a single bolus of propofol but predisposes mice to lethal respiratory failure during a continuous propofol infusion, confirming that these GABAergic neurons exhibit dual functionality and are indispensable for maintaining respiratory stability under sustained propofol anesthesia. Collectively, these data indicate that propofol modulates respiration by recapitulating the endogenous inhibitory regulation of pre-BötC GABAergic microcircuits rather than exerting monotonic neural suppression (supplemental fig. 8, https://links.lww.com/ALN/E588).

A single injection of propofol leads to respiratory perturbation, resulting in a bidirectional excitatory–inhibitory effect. In the early stage after the propofol injection (mainly between the start and the end of the second min of propofol injection), respiratory activity increased significantly, and the instant frequency also increased, accompanied by increased peak inspiration airflow, which resulted in the animals maintaining the pulmonary stretch reflex; subsequently, respiratory activity tended to decrease, characterized by decreased inspiratory airflow and delayed breath initiation, which we refer to as the pause, representing the primary changes that resulted in propofol-induced respiratory suppression. In addition, the pulmonary stretch reflex was lost in this stage. As the excitatory neurons in the pre-BötC are primarily responsible for the breathing rhythm, the perturbation of propofol-induced neuronal activity in this area was confirmed by whole-cell patch clamp recordings and histologic evaluations.

The pre-BötC, which is crucial for inspiratory rhythmogenesis, critically involves interactions of inhibitory neuronal circuits. In the pre-BötC, at least half of the neurons are inhibitory; by performing site-specific photostimulation of inhibitory neurons within the pre-BötC, Ausborn et al.5 reported that relatively weak sustained stimulation of pre-BötC GABAergic neurons increased the respiratory frequency, whereas a further increase in stimulus intensity reduced the frequency and ultimately terminated respiratory oscillations. This phenomenon is similar to the observations in the current study, as propofol is a GABAA receptor agonist; sustained stimulation of GABAergic neurons within the pre-BötC may induce the release of large amounts of GABA, and different amounts of GABA/propofol produce different effects on excitatory rhythmic neurons. In addition, Baertsch et al.15 reported that unlike phasic stimulation during inspiration, the activation of inhibitory neurons during the expiratory phase slowed pre-BötC bursting. We confirmed the findings of both of these studies in vitro by further testing the role of GABAergic neurons in respiratory regulation in vivo using optogenetic experiments on vgat-cre animals expressing channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2) in the pre-BötC. A single pulse of stimulation in phase 1 decreased Ti and the peak inspiration airflow; stimulation in phases 3 and 4 tended to increase Te and induce a significant pause, resulting in an obviously decreased instant frequency (5 mW/10 mW). These results are consistent with those of previous studies. The changes in respiratory activity induced by a single-pulse stimulation were similar in awake and anesthetized animals. The role of glycinergic pre-BötC neurons in respiratory rhythmogenesis in mice has been reported by Sherman et al.16 In ChR2-transfected mice, brief inspiratory-phase bilateral photostimulation targeting the pre-BötC prematurely terminated inspiration, whereas expiratory-phase photostimulation delayed the onset of the next inspiration, consistent with our observations. In addition, the authors reported that prolonged photostimulation produced apnea in both awake and anesthetized animals, which differs from the findings of our study. We found that the activation of GABAergic neurons under awake conditions increased the respiratory frequency and decreased the peak inspiratory airflow (5 mW/10 mW), whereas prolonged photostimulation produced apnea in mice under anesthesia (5 mW/10 mW). This difference could be due to the following reasons: (1) they used only “strong” light stimulation and (2) they performed bilateral optogenetic stimulation of glycinergic neurons. While Ausborn et al.5 reported biphasic responses to optical stimulation depending on the laser intensity, relatively weak, sustained stimulation (20 Hz, 0.5 ± 2.0 mW) of pre-BötC inhibitory neurons increased the respiratory frequency, consistent with our observations in the awake state. However, although we increased the laser intensity to 20 mW, we still failed to induce the termination of respiratory oscillations. The reasons could be that (1) our optogenetic stimulation was performed in vivo, and other regulatory circuits outside the central pattern generator are still active, and (2) our optogenetic stimulation was unilateral, resulting in an insufficient stimulation intensity. Interestingly, we found that relatively weak, sustained stimulation (2 mW) significantly decreased the instant frequency and peak inspiratory airflow, whereas a higher intensity of stimulation (5 mW/10 mW) produced apnea in anesthetized animals. These results support the concept that the activation of inhibitory neurons mediates phase switching but only within a certain range of inhibition. Within this range, anesthesia with propofol and inhibitory neuron activation act synergistically, leading to excessive inhibition that triggers apnea.

In the pre-BötC, a recurrently connected network of predominantly glutamatergic Dbx1-derived (Dbx1+) neurons generates a rhythmic inspiratory drive to inspiratory premotoneurons and motoneurons and sends inspiration-related signals throughout the brain.17 Glycine transporter 2 (GlyT2)+ and GAD1/2+ neurons are functionally and anatomically intercalated among Dbx1+ neurons, with many, but not all, showing inspiratory-modulated activity.18 Pharmacologic blockade of GABAA receptors increased the amplitude and modestly increased the duration of inspiration,15,19,20 and optogenetic GlyT2+ and VGAT+ neuron stimulation in vivo decreased the duration of inspiration or terminated inspiration.15,16,21 In the current study, conditional knockout of Vgat within the pre-BötC did not terminate respiration under awake conditions, which confirmed that GABAergic neurons are not responsible for the generation of the respiratory rhythm. In contrast, apnea was induced in animals with local Vgat knockout suffering from hypoxia under continuous propofol-induced anesthesia, which suggests that GABA plays an important role in the maintenance of breathing under anesthesia.

Rhythm generation at the network level always involves the dynamic interactions of intrinsic cellular, synaptic, and circuit mechanisms. The pre-BötC also receives inhibitory afferent inputs from the brainstem and suprapontine sites, such as the Kölliker–Fuse nuclei, which may modify or alter central pattern generator function.22 In addition, inhibitory neurons and processes, including rhythmic GABAergic and glycinergic inhibitory neurons, are concentrated in the pre-BötC and BötC regions. In knockout mouse models lacking glycinergic inhibition, the rhythm is disrupted,23 which in some cases causes death by respiratory failure after birth.24 Inhibitory–excitatory neuronal interactions at the level of the rostral ventral respiratory group,25 retrotrapezoid nucleus/parafacial respiratory group,26,27 and nucleus tractus solitarius15 also control neuronal population activity and motor output patterns. A previous study by Smith et al.28 tested the mechanistic hypothesis that pre-BötC circuits are dynamically regulated by inhibitory and excitatory circuits when the pre-BötC is embedded in the intact system and endogenous rhythmogenic properties of the pre-BötC are generally not expressed. These properties are expressed only when these circuits are isolated from more rostral brainstem compartments. Our previous study suggested that the inhibitory effect of propofol varies among different nuclei,29 and further studies are needed to clarify the effect of afferent inhibitory inputs on respiratory perturbation in response to propofol-induced anesthesia.

Conclusions

Our findings indicate that propofol produces a dose-dependent biphasic modulation of respiration, with transient excitation at lower concentrations and pronounced suppression at higher concentrations. This transition reflects a concentration-dependent shift in GABAergic transmission within the pre-BötC. During continuous propofol anesthesia, GABAergic neurons exhibit adaptive activation that helps maintain the respiratory rhythm. However, when GABAergic transmission is selectively disrupted, the mice are unable to sustain breathing under continuous propofol anesthesia and develop fatal respiratory failure. Together, these results reveal a specific brainstem microcircuit mechanism that governs respiratory stability during anesthesia and provide mechanistic insights into how propofol produces life-threatening respiratory depression.
