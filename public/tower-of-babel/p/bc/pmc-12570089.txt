Percutaneous transesophageal gastrostomy in gynecologic oncology patients with symptomatic malignant bowel obstruction: A case series
Authors: Leslie Panella, Megan Gorman, Divya Gowthaman, Gary L Goldberg, Marina Frimer, Daniel Putterman, Jill S Whyte
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Malignant bowel obstruction (MBO) is a frequent complication in patients with advanced gynecologic malignancies.•Traditional percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy is sometimes technically infeasible in these patients.•Percutaneous Transesophageal Gastrostomy (PTEG) provides a new option for palliative gastric decompression.Keywords: Ovarian cancer, Malignant bowel obstruction, Gastrostomy tube, Interventional radiology

Introduction

Malignant bowel obstruction (MBO) is a frequent complication observed in patients with advanced or terminal malignancies. In gynecologic oncology, malignant bowel obstructions are particularly common in women with recurrent ovarian cancer and uterine serous carcinomas, with incidence rates as high as 51 % (Lee et al., 2018). Nasogastric tube (NGT) decompression with concomitant medical management is usually the first line therapy for these patients. Percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) tubes or surgical gastrostomies are often employed in patients with inoperable, persistent MBO which necessitates gastric decompression as a palliative measure (Shaw et al., 2013). Clinical features of advanced gynecologic cancer such as ascites and extensive upper abdominal or peritoneal carcinomatosis may introduce technical limitations for the performance of transabdominal decompression procedures. The morbidity and mortality associated with PEG tube placement are notably elevated in patients with malignant ascites (Zera et al., 1993). When PEG placement is technically challenging or contraindicated, ongoing NGT placement has traditionally been the preferred alternative for gastric decompression. However, the long-term use of a NGT is discouraged because of potential complications and patient discomfort (Motta et al., 2021). Few clinical alternatives have been developed for patients who are unable to undergo PEG placement due to relative or absolute contraindications. Percutaneous transesophageal gastrostomy (PTEG) appears to be a safe and effective alternative to PEG in these patients. In our case series, we review three women with MBO who underwent successful placement of PTEG for gastric decompression and symptom control.

Materials and methods

The procedure was performed using the techniques described by Rotellini-Coltvet et al in 2023. All patients considered for PTEG placement had a CT Neck with IV contrast as part of their evaluation. Pre procedure laboratory tests included a CBC and a coagulation panel to ensure platelets >50,000/μL and INR < 1.5.

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia. If one was not already present, an NG tube was inserted under fluoroscopy. The NGT was then exchanged over a 0.035 Axis guidewire (Teleflex, Morrisville, NC) for a 24 mm x 4.5 cm valvuloplasty balloon (BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ) (Fig. 1). The balloon was positioned at the level of C5-C6 and inflated with saline and contrast (Fig. 2A). The left neck was then prepped and draped. Using ultrasound and fluoroscopic guidance, an 18-gauge INRAD needle (INRAD, Kentwood, MI) was advanced into the balloon above the clavicle. In select cases, hydrodissection was utilized to increase the space between the left thyroid lobe and carotid artery prior to puncturing the balloon. A 180 cm 0.035 axis wire was advanced through the needle into the balloon (Fig. 2B). The wire and balloon were both advanced into the stomach under fluoroscopic guidance. The wire was partially withdrawn from the balloon and a 5 Fr 65 cm Berenstein catheter (Merit Medical OEM, South Jordan, UT) was advanced over the wire into the stomach. The axis wire was removed and contrast was injected through the catheter confirming the positioning within the stomach. A 0.035 180 cm Amplatz wire (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA) was advanced through the Berenstein and coiled within the stomach (Fig. 2C). The Berenstein catheter and balloon were removed and following serial dilation, a 14 Fr 45 cm multipurpose drainage catheter (Cook, Bloomington, IN) was advanced into the stomach. The Amplatz wire was removed and contrast was injected through the catheter under fluoroscopy to confirm appropriate positioning in the stomach (Fig. 2D). The catheter was then secured to the skin with sutures, connected to suction, and a sterile dressing was placed.

The catheters were connected to drainage bags, and the patients were instructed to flush the catheters with 10 cc sterile NS twice a day to ensure patency. After placement, diet was slowly advanced as tolerated to clear liquids or soft solids. Patients were instructed to cap the catheter for short durations if needed for showers or other activities. Medications were able to be delivered through the catheters, however all patients were instructed not to use them for tube feeding.

Clinical case series

Case 1

A 68-year-old woman with stage IVA high-grade serous carcinoma of the fallopian tube refractory to multiple chemotherapy and immunotherapy regimens. She had undergone primary debulking surgery two years prior. She presented with vomiting and constipation due to malignant bowel obstruction (MBO). On CT of the abdomen and pelvis, she was found to have a transition point at the proximal jejunum and much of the small bowel contained in a capsule of thickened enhancing peritoneum. She had no resolution of the MBO with NGT placement and conservative medical management. She was evaluated by the gastroenterology and general surgery teams, and both endoscopic and surgical PEG placement were deferred due to her history of gastric sleeve surgery and the presence of interposed liver and bowel overlying the gastric wall. She underwent placement of a PTEG in the left neck, draining to gravity. The patient experienced no postoperative complications and was subsequently discharged home with hospice care. The PTEG remained in place, and the patient reported resolution of emesis and only minimal nausea with a clear liquid diet. She died of her disease 25 days after the PTEG placement.

Case 2

A 63-year-old woman with stage IIIC clear cell and serous ovarian cancer refractory to multiple chemotherapy agents. She had primary debulking surgery one year prior, and had multiple admissions to the hospital for vomiting and abdominal pain with recurrent MBO secondary to a distal ileum stricture. PEG placement was deferred due to overlying dilated loops of bowel and elevated risks associated with the underlying ascites. She underwent insertion of a PTEG in the left neck, draining to gravity. There were no postoperative complications. She was advanced slowly to a clear liquid and then soft solid diet. She was discharged home with hospice care. She was readmitted two days later with abdominal pain and vomiting. PTEG was noted to be in place and draining adequately. It was placed to low intermittent suction and an abdominal PleurX catheter was placed for ascites drainage with significant improvement in her symptoms. PTEG was returned to gravity suction and clear liquid diet was resumed without difficulty. The patient was discharged home on hospice care and ultimately died of her disease 42 days after initial PTEG placement.

Case 3

A 55-year-old woman with stage IIIC primary peritoneal cancer refractory to chemotherapy and immunotherapy, who had primary debulking surgery four years prior and multiple admissions within the last month with nausea, vomiting, and constipation. CT revealed a high-grade MBO with a transition point in the mid-ileum. She had no resolution of the MBO with NGT and medical management. The patient was scheduled for a percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) placement by gastroenterology; however, the procedure was aborted due to the absence of a clear window due to gastric serosal involvement and potential intramural tumor implantation. She underwent insertion of a PTEG in the left neck, draining to gravity. There were no postoperative complications. She tolerated sips of clear liquids without nausea or vomiting and was discharged home with home hospice two days later. She died of her disease 12 days later.

Discussion

In this case series, we highlight the potential advantages of PTEG as a safe and effective alternative for patients with MBO who are not candidates for PEG placement. PTEG was first introduced in Japan in 1994 as an alternative to PEG tubes (Oishi et al., 2003). Unlike PEG, which involves direct percutaneous access to the stomach or the intestines, PTEG involves the creation of an esophagostomy (Oishi et al., 2003). Several small-scale case studies and randomized controlled trials have explored the use of PTEG for enteral feeding and as a palliative management option for patients with MBO (Udomsawaengsup et al., 2008, Singal et al., 2010). In our patient cohort, PTEG has primarily been employed for symptom relief and gastric venting in individuals with inoperable malignant bowel obstruction with absolute or relative contra-indications to PEG or surgical gastrostomy tube placement.

PTEG is most often performed under general anesthesia, however, successful placement under moderate sedation and local anesthesia has been reported. The technical success of placement of PTEG was 92–100 %, with difficult neck anatomy and inadequate visualization on imaging as a primary reason for technical failure (Rotellini-Coltvet et al., 2023, Aramaki et al., 2013, Aramaki et al., 2019). The procedure can be completed in 15–40 min, is usually performed in an Interventional Radiology suite, and can be done under ultrasound and fluoroscopic guidance, reducing the need for endoscopy (Mackey et al., 2005). In the three cases described in this series, technical success was achieved in every patient, and all patients reported decreased symptoms related to their obstructions. No significant periprocedural complications were noted.

Risks associated with traditional means of gastric decompression are well-documented. Percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) and surgical gastrostomy have been associated with technical failure, aspiration, sepsis, pneumo-peritonitis, gastric leakage, entero-cutaneous fistula, tube migration, bleeding, wound infection, and obstruction. Although rare, death has been reported as a complication (Thampy et al., 2019). Risk of morbidity and mortality may be increased in those patients requiring PEG tubes with the presence of ascites, a common symptom of advanced gynecologic cancer (Zera et al., 1993). Long-term NGT use also carries risk of complications such as misplacement, dislodgement, respiratory issues, esophageal or pharyngeal trauma, gastric irritation, tube obstruction, and patient discomfort (Motta et al., 2021).

PTEG placement is a relatively novel procedure in the USA, and its associated complications and adverse events are not yet fully understood. However, the overall complication rate is generally low (Litwin et al., 2021). Of the documented complications, intra-operative issues include bleeding, injury to adjacent structures, and technical failure. Postoperative complications include tube dislodgement, obstruction, need for tube exchange, subcutaneous emphysema, leakage, pain, and infection (Oishi et al., 2003, Rotellini-Coltvet et al., 2023, Toh Yoon and Nishihara, 2017). A major complication in one study was tracheoesophageal fistula with aspiration pneumonia (Aramaki et al., 2013), while another reported procedure-related mortality due to aspiration pneumonitis after bolus tube feeding (Rotellini-Coltvet et al., 2023). Although PTEG complications tend to be less severe than those associated with PEG, the incidence of minor complications is higher compared to NGT decompression. Despite this, patient satisfaction with PTEG is significantly higher than with long-term NGT use, with most patients achieving significant symptom improvement (Aramaki et al., 2019, Nakama et al., 2024). There was no difference in overall survival, with most patients succumbing to progressive malignancy within 60 days of PTEG insertion, although one patient had the tube in place for over a year (Rotellini-Coltvet et al., 2023).

PTEG was developed as a safe and effective alternative for gastric decompression for patients who cannot safely undergo transabdominal decompression procedures. Although a relatively new procedure in the United States, PTEG has demonstrated efficacy as an alternative to PEG, providing patients with symptomatic relief with low rate of complications. While it has not yet gained widespread adoption in clinical practice, PTEG holds the potential to become a valuable tool for palliative relief of MBO secondary to end-stage gynecologic malignancy. Our case series demonstrates the utility of PTEG in patients with MBO where NGT, PEG, and surgical gastrostomy are contraindicated.
