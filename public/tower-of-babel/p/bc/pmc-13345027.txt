Development of an End-to-end Phantom and Procedure for Computed Tomography and Cone-beam Computed Tomography-guided Online Adaptive Radiotherapy
Authors: Yoshifumi Kamikubo, Masato Tsuneda, Shouji Kutsuki, Takashi Uno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:The purpose of this study was to develop an end-to-end (E2E) testing procedure to verify in-room computed tomography (CT)-on-rails and cone-beam CT (CBCT)-guided online adaptive radiation therapy (OART) workflows.Materials and Methods:A water tank-type lung phantom was developed to mimic the lung tissue and tumors, to evaluate deformable image registration (DIR) and dosimetry. Tumor shrinkage was mimicked by shape, position, and material changes. DIR was performed from the ellipsoidal tumor contoured on the initial CT image to multiple tumor cases on the CT and CBCT images. DIR accuracy was evaluated using the dice similarity coefficient (DSC) for manually delineated contours and those propagated by the adaptation anatomy algorithm. Dosimetric accuracy was evaluated by comparing the calculated and measured doses. An ionization chamber was used for point dose measurement, and a GafChromic film was used for gamma analysis using a 3%/2 mm criterion.Results:The averaged DSC values for the CT-guided and CBCT-guided OART workflows were 0.988 and 0.982, respectively. We confirmed high DSCs across different tumor conditions. The relative point dose differences between the measured and calculated doses for the CT-guided and CBCT-guided OART workflows were 0.02% and 0.86%, respectively. In the sagittal planes, the gamma passing rates were 97.8% and 95.6% for the CT-guided and CBCT-guided OART workflows, respectively, indicating good agreement between the measurements and calculations.Conclusion:We developed a novel lung phantom for E2E testing and established workflows for both CT-guided and CBCT-guided OART.

INTRODUCTION

Current commercially available online adaptive radiation therapy (OART) systems include magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-guided systems such as Unity (Elekta, Stockholm, Sweden) and MRIdian (Viewray, Oakwood Village, OH, USA) as well as cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT)-guided systems such as Ethos (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA). CBCT images are generally lower quality, owing to scattered radiation, ghosting, and beam hardening.[12345] Acquiring accurate relative electron density for dose calculation using CBCT is challenging; therefore, the Ethos system performs dose calculations by deforming the planning CT images.[6] The Hypersight (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA) system has been reported to improve image quality and CT value quantification.[7] The CBCT images acquired by HyperSight were directly used for dose calculation. The accuracy of image quality and dose calculation has been reported for several anatomical sites.[8910] The CBCT imaging system is often selected for its availability, lower costs, and compact size, making it globally accessible. However, some facilities install an in-room CT scanner for “near-treatment-position” imaging. The in-room CT-on-rails scanner represents an image-guided radiation therapy system installed within the treatment room.[1112] The in-room CT offers several advantages, including superior image quality compared to CBCT,[13] and it facilitates the comparison of daily and reference planning CT images, minimizing uncertainties. Clinically, rapid replanning based on daily CT scans using an in-room CT scanner has been reported for photon-based treatments for prostate cancer.[1415]

An end-to-end (E2E) test is a method that tests whether all processes in radiation therapy function correctly, to ensure that the appropriate dose is delivered precisely to the correct spatial location. There are few reports related to E2E in OART. Although several phantoms are specifically designed for MR-guided OART,[1617181920] reports on CT-guided or CBCT-guided OART are limited. The OART process involves multiple complex steps; therefore, an E2E test procedure is necessary to verify OART processes, including image acquisition, delineation, relative electron density mapping, treatment planning, and irradiation. Previous research has documented an E2E test for the Ethos OART system using standard quality assurance (QA) phantoms,[21] and commercial adaptive QA inserts are also available.[22]

In this study, we developed a novel water tank-type lung phantom to perform E2E testing for both CT-guided and CBCT-guided OART workflows. Using this phantom, we further evaluated deformable image registration (DIR)-based contour propagation and assessed the dosimetric accuracy of OART plans for commissioning purposes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Phantom design

The in-room CT scanner (Aquilion LB; Canon Medical Systems, Tochigi, Japan) and linear accelerator (linac, Synergy; Elekta, Stockholm, Sweden) were installed in the treatment room. The treatment table is shared by the in-room CT and linac and is equipped with a mechanism that allows it to rotate 180 degrees. The CT images were acquired by the in-room CT scanner, and the CBCT images were obtained with the onboard X-ray volume imaging (XVI) system. The treatment room is depicted in Figure 1a.

The cylindrical cork includes both ellipsoid and sphere-shaped tumors (d-f). The film and chamber allow for insertion into the sphere tumor (e and f). Figure 1b shows a photograph of the water tank-type lung phantom. The water-tank component of this phantom was previously reported.[23] In this study, the lung and tumor components inside the water tank were newly designed for the E2E test of the CT-guided and CBCT-guided OART workflows. The shape, position, and material of the tumor can be modified. A cork was used to simulate the lung regions. A cork block [Figure 1c] was inserted into the area outlined by the dark blue line [Figure 1b]. This block was divided into a cylindrical cork and the remaining parts. The cylindrical parts could be rotated with a 15° pitch (range, 0°–45°). A tumor with an ellipsoid or sphere shape could be placed inside a cylindrical cork [Figure 1d and e]. The ellipsoid-shaped tumors consisted of tough water (TW; Kyoto Kagaku, Kyoto, Japan) and polyethylene to simulate decreases in CT values. The spherical tumor phantom was designed to accommodate film and ionization chamber dosimeters, enabling the measurement of two-dimensional dose distributions and point doses [Figure 1e and f]. It was also possible to translate the tumor position along the SI axis by changing the order of the cork blocks. The average CT values for the materials were 9 Hounsfield units (HU) for TW, −56 HU for polyethylene, and −770 HU for cork. By changing the tumor shape from an ellipsoid to a sphere, the volume decreased from 132.0 to 113.1 cm3, corresponding to the actual volumes of the ellipsoid and sphere tumors.

End-to-end test procedure

In the initial treatment process, our phantom was placed on the couch of the CT simulator. The phantom was scanned using the CT simulator, and the acquired images were exported to the Monaco treatment planning system version 6.1.4.0 (Elekta, Stockholm, Sweden). The ellipsoid-shaped tumor, consisting of TW material, was delineated to represent the gross tumor volume (GTV), and a 5 mm margin was added in all directions to create the planning target volume (PTV). The GTV delineation was performed with a window width of 1600, a window level of −400, and edge detection sensitivity at 50%. The lung contours were delineated manually. An initial plan was created for this tumor. The prescribed dose for the PTV was 60 Gy/30 Fr, and 50% of the PTV received the prescribed dose. A dual full-arc volumetric modulated arc radiotherapy (VMAT) technique was used, and the XVMC algorithm was used with a grid size of 3 mm and a dose uncertainty of 1% per calculation.

Propagation accuracy

The DIR-based contour propagation was performed using the DIR tool adapt anatomy (AA) in Monaco.[24] DIR accuracy was evaluated using the dice similarity coefficient (DSC), which compares the contours propagated by AA with the manual contours. The initial CT images (an ellipsoid-shaped tumor made from TW) were used as the reference, and images with different shapes, positions, and materials were used as the secondary CT and CBCT images. These CT and CBCT images were acquired by the in-room CT and XVI system, respectively. The manual contours were delineated using thresholding and then manually corrected. This process was performed by one medical physicist and independently reviewed by another. The tumor and lung contours were evaluated. All of the cases used are listed in Table 1. The volumes of the DIR-based contours were compared with the actual object volumes (132.0 and 113.1 cm3).

Dose measurement for the online adaptive radiation therapy plan

The phantom with a sphere-shaped tumor was scanned using the in-room CT and XVI, and the acquired CT and CBCT images were exported to Monaco. Re-optimization and re-calculation were performed on these images. In the CBCT-guided OART procedure, the bulk density assignment was required because the CBCT images do not have an electron density information. Predetermined relative electron density values were assigned to each contoured structure. Therefore, plan generation was allowed with the same dose constraints as in the initial plan. After replanning, the OART plan was approved. The VMAT beams were irradiated onto the sphere-shaped tumors. An EBT4 GafChromic film (Ashland Inc., Wayne, NJ, USA) was sandwiched between the phantoms to measure the 2D dose distribution in the sagittal and coronal planes. An ionization chamber (CC04; IBA dosimetry, Schwarzenbruck, Germany) was inserted to measure the point dose at the center of the tumor. After irradiation, the films were kept in a dark box for 24 h to protect them from fluorescent light and were scanned using a flat-bed scanner (Epson Expression DS-G20000; Epson Tokyo, Japan) in 48-bit RGB mode with a resolution of 75 dots/inch. The pixel intensity of the scanned film was converted to the irradiated dose using a dose conversion table. Dose analysis was performed using the red channel of the scanned image, and the calculated and measured dose distributions were compared using DD system software version 6.8 (R-TECH. INC., Tokyo, Japan). Gamma evaluation was performed with an absolute dose using the criteria of 3%/2 mm and a threshold of 10%. The point dose measured for the chamber was compared with the dose calculated by the treatment planning system (TPS) at the center of the tumor.

RESULTS

Figure 2 shows the workflow of OART that we established. In the initial planning process, the CT images are acquired using the CT simulator [Figure 2a] and transferred to the Monaco TPS. The initial treatment plan was created and approved [Figure 2b and c]. In the OART process, the images are first acquired using in-room CT or XVI [Figure 2d] and transferred to the TPS. DIR-based contour propagation is then performed using the AA algorithm [Figure 2e], followed by template-based dose calculation [Figure 2f]. The DIR was performed from the initial CT to the daily CT/CBCT images. The OART plan is subsequently approved and delivered to the phantom [Figure 2g]. The times required for image acquisition, contouring, re-planning, and dose delivery were approximately 2, 5, 5, and 3 min, respectively.

Propagation accuracy

The averaged DSC values for the CT and CBCT cases were 0.988 and 0.982, respectively. The DSC of the lung was 0.987 for the CT-guided OART. For the CBCT-guided OART, the lung volume could not be evaluated because the whole lung was not visualized in the field of view. Differences between the manual and propagated contours were observed at the edge between the tumor and lung regions in the SI direction due to the partial volume effect. For the CT-guided OART workflow, the volume differences were within −1.38% of the actual volumes. In contrast, for the CBCT-guided OART workflow, volume differences ranged from −4.12% to −2.99%.

Dose measurement

Figure 3 shows the dose distributions and gamma index distributions in the coronal and sagittal planes for CT-guided OART. The gamma passing rates for CT were 94.1% and 97.8% in the coronal and sagittal planes, respectively, whereas those for CBCT were 95.1% and 95.6%, respectively. The point dose differences were 0.02% and 0.87% for CT-guided OART and CBCT-guided OART, respectively. Both point dose measurements and gamma analyses showed good agreement.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we developed an E2E phantom that simulates lung tumor shrinkage and established an E2E testing for CT-guided and CBCT-guided OART workflows. Locally advanced lung cancer (LALC) requires offline replanning because the tumors often shrink during chemoradiotherapy.[2526] A previous report has demonstrated the superiority of CBCT-guided OART for LALC.[27] In the planning CT simulation, tumors were detected on CT images including a maximum intensity projection, an average, and 4DCT images. CBCT is a relatively slow acquisition; therefore, the target delineation on CBCT images is generally sufficient for matching 4DCT-based internal target volumes. For the CT-guided OART workflow, the contouring can be performed using the same imaging modality. This may improve the delineation accuracy of the tumor and whole lungs, thereby reducing dosimetric errors. Moreover, the exposure dose of CT is lower than that of CBCT.[28] However, CBCT technology is rapidly advancing, and the doses can vary significantly, potentially matching those of fan-beam CT, depending on the specific scanner and protocol.

We performed an E2E test using our phantom to verify the CT-guided and CBCT-guided OART workflows such as daily image acquisition, DIR-based contouring, replanning, and irradiation. The OART process was feasible within approximately 15 min under the present experimental conditions. However, because these results were derived from a phantom with simple geometry, clinical implementation is likely to involve more extensive contour modifications, which may result in longer treatment times. For DIR-based propagation accuracy, the American Association of Physicists in Medicine Task Group (AAPM TG)-132 specifies that the accuracy of DIR should be reflected in a DSC value of 0.8–0.9.[29] In our study, the DSCs exceeded 0.90, indicating high agreement in contour propagation. Compared with CT images, CBCT images demonstrated a greater volumetric underestimation of the GTV relative to the actual object volumes. The limitations of this study include the clear contrast between the tumor and surrounding lung tissue and the simple geometric phantom. In addition, the rigid and static design of the phantom does not account for clinically relevant factors such as inter- and intra-fraction variability and respiratory motion. Therefore, the DSC showed high values. To verify the accuracy with respect to heterogeneous internal structures, 3D-printed tumors made of acrylonitrile butadiene styrene plastic with varying percentages of honeycomb fill should be established.[30] In AAPM TG-218, it is recommended that the absolute point dose should be within 2% and the gamma analysis should be > 95% with 3%/2 mm and a 10% dose threshold.[31] All of our measurement results fell within these tolerance limits. However, a gamma analysis for the film measurements could be performed in only high-dose region because of the limited film size. Tumor shrinkage was mimicked by changes in the shape and HU value of the tumor. The volume reduction was ~ 14%, which has been observed early in the treatment course in a previous report.[26]

Daily tests and routine QA and quality control for the latest OART technologies and systems, as well as commissioning tests for TPS and treatment machines, should be performed.[3233] Practical guidelines recommend that the E2E test be performed not only during the commissioning period but also regularly after clinical implementation to detect any abnormalities in the process. To ensure safe OART, it is recommended to conduct E2E tests, independent dosimetry audit, and facility-specific training.[34] Using our phantom, the technical aspects of the OART workflow related to imaging, contouring, treatment planning, and dose delivery can be verified. However, our current procedure does not include the evaluation of the human elements, such as human error and clinical decision-making. Nevertheless, the use of this phantom may support institutional discussions on workflow design, including staff allocation and the establishment of verification steps within the OART process. We believe that the phantom we developed is useful for E2E testing of the CT-guided and CBCT-guided OART procedure.

CONCLUSION

We developed a novel water tank-type lung phantom for the E2E testing and established CT-guided and CBCT-guided OART workflows. Using this phantom, we evaluated the accuracy of DIR-based contour propagation and dose calculation. The phantom demonstrated the feasibility for verification of both OART workflows.
