Body mass index and depressive symptoms among Chinese nurses: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Yanan Wang, Xuedan Cui, Yanmin Lu, Chang Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Depressive symptoms are common in the nursing profession. A normal body mass index (BMI) is an important indicator of individuals’ health. However, few studies have examined the relationship between depressive symptoms and BMI in nurses. The aim of the present study was to investigate the association between them in Chinese nurses. A total of 1866 nurses from 12 tertiary hospitals participated in a survey conducted in Shandong, China. BMI was calculated by measuring participants’ height and weight. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale. The relationship between BMI and depressive symptoms was analyzed using a multivariate logistic regression analysis. The depressive symptom prevalence in Chinese nurses was 47.8%. The results of the multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that compared with participants whose BMI was in the normal range, those who were underweight (odds ratio = 1.670, 95% confidence interval: 1.161–2.403) or obese (odds ratio = 1.540, 95% confidence interval: 1.003–2.365) were positively associated with depressive symptoms, and those who were overweight were not significantly associated with depressive symptoms. Our findings indicated a high depressive symptom prevalence in Chinese nurses. Nurses who were obese or underweight were more likely to have depressive symptoms than those with a normal BMI. Age, educational background, professional title, monthly income, and department were factors that influenced depressive symptoms. Hospital administrators should take effective measures to encourage nurses to maintain a normal BMI status or be overweight and focus on the role of influencing factors that are conducive to improving nurses’ depressive symptoms.

1. Introduction

Depression is a state of psychological distress[1] that has become a common mental disorder in the nursing profession.[2] Many studies have confirmed that nurses, who play an important role in hospitals, face increasingly serious depressive problems. The prevalence of depression among nurses in Australia is >30%.[3] A systematic review and meta-analysis indicated that 22% of Iranian nurses had depression.[4] Another systematic review and meta-analysis showed that 43.83% of Chinese nurses had depressive symptoms and that the prevalence of depressive symptoms, which was already relatively high, showed an increasing trend.[5] Heavy workload, medical disputes, and professional status place nurses at high risk of experiencing depressive symptoms.[6] Depressive symptoms can negatively affect nurses’ sense of achievement and happiness, reduce work efficiency, and affect the long-term development of human resources for the nursing profession as well as medical and health quality.[7] Therefore, it is necessary to explore influencing factors related to nurses’ depressive symptoms and to develop relevant intervention strategies to reduce depressive symptom prevalence.

Body mass index (BMI) is an important indicator of physical health that can predict health outcomes.[8] Nutritional status as calculated using BMI is divided into the following classifications: underweight, normal weight, overweight, and obesity. Previous studies have confirmed that different BMI classifications have different effects on physical and mental health. Being underweight is a risk factor for fracture,[9] tuberculosis,[10] and cardiovascular disease.[11] It may indicate malnutrition or an underlying health condition that has adverse effects on mental health. Being overweight or obese are risk factors for many noncommunicable diseases, for example, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and cancer,[12] and these physical health issues can contribute to a decreased quality of life, leading to psychological distress.

The influence mechanism of BMI on depressive symptoms is a complex and multidimensional process involving physiological,[13,14] psychological,[15,16] and sociocultural.[17,18] Previous studies had reported that BMI is associated with depressive symptoms among middle-aged adults/older adults[17] and children/adolescents,[19] but the results of these studies were inconsistent. A study showed that the depressive symptom prevalence in overweight/obese children/adolescents was significantly higher than their peers in the normal classification.[19] Another study found that there is a U-shaped relationship between BMI and depressive symptoms in a specific population, such as Mexican Americans and non-Hispanic whites.[20] In addition, previous studies have pointed out that because of the “fat and jolly” hypothesis and Chinese traditional culture, obese people were not prone to depressive symptoms.[17,21]

However, few studies have examined the relationship between BMI and depressive symptoms in nurses. Although a previous study has described the association between BMI and depression among nurses,[22] the connection between different categories of BMI (underweight, normal weight, overweight, and obesity) and depressive symptoms is ignored. Due to their job characteristics (such as irregular scheduling and high-intensity work pressure), nurses are susceptible to BMI change.[23] Therefore, it is essential to pay attention to the impact of different BMI levels on their health. Furthermore, another distinctive aspect of nurses’ work is the necessity for continual patient interaction. During patient contact, patients can evaluate a nurse’s professional identity, attitude, emotions, and personality traits within seconds based on their external appearance and other nonverbal cues.[24] Such evaluations may significantly influence the patient–nurse relationship. As a result, the nursing profession imposes certain requirements on external appearance,[25] and excessively high or low BMI indices may increase psychological burdens on nurses, thereby affecting their mental health.

Therefore, the aim of the present study was to explore the association between BMI and depressive symptoms, to identify other influencing factors, and to provide suggestions for improving their mental health from the perspective of BMI in Chinese nurses. Based on the aforementioned literature, the following hypothesis is proposed for this study: both obesity and underweight are positively associated with depressive symptoms among nurses; there is no association between being overweight and depressive symptoms.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and sampling

This study utilized an online-based cross-sectional survey. To reduce sampling bias, a multistage random sampling method was adopted in the survey.[26] First, based on per capita gross domestic product, the 16 prefecture-level cities were divided into 3 categories: high, medium, and low. Two cities were selected randomly from each category. Two tertiary hospitals were randomly selected from each city. Finally, two-thirds of the departments, including internal medicine, surgery, obstetrics, gynecology, and others, were chosen in selected hospitals.[27] All nurses in the selected departments participated in the survey (The department head distributed the survey link to the internal workgroup chat of their respective department.). Registered nurses employed by the hospital who voluntarily participated in the survey were included. Nurses who were absent due to vacation or participation in continuing education or who suffered from serious mental or physical illnesses that prevented their participation were excluded.

In the current research, we employed a single population proportion formula to ascertain the minimal sample size required, which is expressed as n = Zα/2²p(1 − p)/d². Our premise rested on the estimated prevalence of depressive symptoms among nurses in China at 31.12%,[5] allowing for a margin of error of 5% within a 95% confidence interval (CI), corresponding to α = 0.05. Applying these values, the initial calculation yielded n = 1.962 × 0.3112 × (1 − 0.3112)/0.052 ≈ 330. To account for the complex sampling design, we incorporated a design effect of 2 due to the adoption of a multistage sampling technique. Furthermore, to mitigate the potential impact of nonresponse, we factored in an additional 20% nonresponse rate. Consequently, the minimum required sample size for this study was determined to be 792 nurses, calculated as 330 × 2 × (1 + 0.2).

The research was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of the School of Nursing and Rehabilitation, Shandong University (Approval number: 2020-R-50). All subjects gave their informed consent for inclusion before participating in the survey.

2.2. Data collection

The present cross-sectional survey was performed from July 30 to September 30, 2020. The questionnaire survey was distributed through a secure online platform (Wen Juan Wang, https://www.wenjuan.com/). Each IP address or device was restricted to a single submission of the questionnaire to prevent duplication. Before collecting data, we contacted the directors of nursing departments from the selected 12 hospitals to explain the purpose of this study. To reduce self-selection bias, this study emphasizes the anonymity and confidentiality of responses. We sent the link to an online anonymous questionnaire via WeChat (a social media platform) to the directors of nursing departments at each hospital. The directors then forwarded the link to the head nurses of the selected departments. The head nurses subsequently shared the questionnaire link in their respective departments’ work groups. In total, 1933 nurses joined the survey. Data from Wen Juan Wang was imported into Excel for screening. After excluding 67 questionnaires with completion times <5 minutes, logically inconsistent answers, and missing values, 1866 nurses were included in the analysis, with an effective response rate of 96.53%.

2.3. Measurement

2.3.1. Body mass index

BMI is a common indicator that uses weight and height to calculate the nutritional status of adults over 18 years of age and reflects the degree of fat and thinness. BMI was calculated using the equation {BMI = weight (kg)/height (m2)}. The cutoff values for judging the degree of underweight to obesity in Chinese adults are as follows: BMI < 18.5 kg/m2 is underweight, 18.5 to 24.9 kg/m2 is the normal weight range, 25.0–29.9 kg/m2 is overweight, and ≥30.0 kg/m2 is obese.[28]

2.3.2. Depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10).[29] The CESD-10 has been widely used, and its validity and reliability were confirmed for the measurement of depressive symptoms in Chinese populations.[30,31] The CESD-10 assesses depressive symptoms experienced by individuals over the past week. Each item of the CESD-10 has 4 options from 0 to 3, where 0 means rarely, 1 represents some days (1–2 days), 2 means occasionally (3–4 days), and 3 means most of the time (5–7 days). Items 5 and 8 were positive items and therefore reverse coded (from 3 to 0); the other items, as negative questions, were coded from 0 to 3.[31] The scores for the 10 items were summed to obtain a total score, which ranged from 0 to 30. A score of 10 was set as the cutoff point, indicating that a person whose total score ≥10 had high depressive symptoms.[32] In the survey, Cronbach alpha for the CES-D 10 scale was 0.68, which showed that the reliability was minimally acceptable.[26]

2.3.3. Covariate

In this study, the potential confounding factors were chosen based on previous studies.[33–36] Demographic information included sex, age, marital status, educational background, department, professional title, employment status, and monthly income. Marital status was classified as either married or single. Educational background was categorized as junior college or below, bachelor’s degree, and master’s degree or above. The departments, for example, internal medicine, surgery, obstetrics and gynecology, pediatrics, emergency department, and others, were included in the survey. Professional titles were categorized as primary, intermediate, or senior. Employment status was divided into formal or contract. Monthly income was classified into 4 levels: <5000 Chinese Yuan (CNY), 5000–8000 CNY, 8001 to 10,000 CNY, and <10,000 CNY.

2.4. Data analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS Version 20.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). Demographic characteristics were reported as numbers and percentages. The depressive symptom prevalence in different BMI types was analyzed by the χ2 test. Multivariate logistic regression enables the concurrent examination of the effects of multiple predictor variables on a binary outcome. Given that the dependent variable in our study, depressive symptoms, is a binary variable, and our research encompasses numerous predictor factors, including BMI as well as other variables such as age and gender, we employed a multivariate logistic regression model to analyze the association between depressive symptoms and BMI while also exploring the influencing factors of depressive symptoms. Potential confounding factors were identified based on the literature review. To select the relevant confounders, we utilized the change-in-estimate method.[37] These variables were then incorporated as covariates in our multivariate logistic regression model. The variance inflation factor of all independent variables in our study was <5, which indicates no multicollinearity.[38] A Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was used to assess the model’s fitness, with a P-level above .05 indicating an acceptable fit.[39] Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

As shown in Table 3, the multivariate logistic regression analysis indicated that depressive symptoms were positively associated with being underweight (odds ratio [OR] = 1.670, 95% CI: 1.161–2.403), being obese (OR = 1.540, 95% CI: 1.003–2.365), working in the departments of surgery (OR = 1.570, 95% CI: 1.133–2.175), obstetrics and gynecology (OR = 1.777, 95% CI: 1.067–2.958), or pediatrics (OR = 1.797, 95% CI: 1.140–2.834), and having a monthly income of between 5000 and 8000 CNY (OR = 1.364, 95% CI: 1.018–1.826). Depressive symptoms were negatively associated with being 50 years of age and older (OR = 0.319, 95% CI: 0.146–0.697), having a bachelor’s degree (OR = 0.651, 95% CI: 0.448–0.945) or a master’s degree or above (OR = 0.446, 95% CI: 0.242–0.821), and having a senior professional title (OR = 0.484, 95% CI: 0.245–0.957).

4. Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that explored the relationship between BMI and depressive symptoms in Chinese nurses. Our findings provide suggestions for hospital administrators to implement prompt intervention measures to reduce depressive symptom prevalence in nurses.

According to our results, the prevalence of depressive symptoms among Chinese nurses was 47.8%, which is higher than the prevalence among the Chinese labor force (17.3%)[40] and older adults (20.0%).[41] Because of differences in countries, sample sizes, analytical methods, and tools, the results of previous studies were different, but most studies have consistently concluded that nurses are at a high risk for depressive symptoms. Compared with previous studies,[42,43] the prevalence of depressive symptoms in the present study was higher. This can be explained as follows: first, an increase in social medical demands may cause psychological distress in nurses. With the improvement in public awareness of health and the growth in the demand for health services, coupled with the aggravation of the aging population in China, the social requirements for nursing levels and service quality are also gradually increasing, which increases nurses’ pressure.[44] According to the Job-Demands-Resources model, the demands of nursing can put extreme stress on nurses, deplete their physical and mental resources, and lead to depressive symptoms.[45] Second, high job responsibility and low social status have negative psychological effects on nurses. Nurses are required to provide not only medical care but also psychological comfort and support to patients.[46] However, a study conducted by Li et al[47] found that the nursing profession and clinical duty were the highest stressors because of low social status and lack of recognition by others. Chinese nurses are under pressure from both physical and emotional labor.[48] Third, excessive workload is a predominant factor influencing nurses’ work pressure.[49] Because of the shortage of nurses in China, nurses’ workload and working hours are higher than those of other professions, which causes mental pressure and leads to depressive symptoms.[50] Finally, nurses inevitably experience occupational injuries. Nurses often face threats from various factors, including biological, chemical, and physical.[51] Nurses are a high-risk population for occupational diseases such as insomnia, varicose veins, and musculoskeletal disorders, which may have negative effects on mental health.[50]

Our results showed that nurses with obesity were more likely to have depressive symptoms. The previous study found that obesity can lead to depressive symptoms, and obese people face obesity-related diseases and malfunctions, which result in adverse emotional experiences.[52] Nurses are at an increased risk of obesity because of their occupational environment and demands,[53] and obesity reduces their psychological qualities such as self-esteem and negative and positive feelings.[54] Because the majority of clinical nurses are female, and they are disproportionately affected by weight-based stigma and discrimination if they are obese.[55] It is plausible that societal biases against obesity might influence patients’ perceptions of nurses’ competence and trustworthiness.[18] This could potentially lead to communication barriers and challenges in patient care. Furthermore, obese nurses may experience heightened fatigue during physical labor, such as moving heavy equipment or assisting patients in transferring. This fatigue can also impact their work efficiency and quality of care.[28] Both appearance anxiety and limited mobility associated with obesity increase the likelihood of depressive symptoms.

The data also revealed that nurses who were underweight were more likely to experience depressive symptoms, which is consistent with previous studies. Studies have indicated that the association between BMI and depressive symptoms is U-shaped,[56,57] indicating that underweight and obese people are the focus of depressive symptoms. Considering the physical demands of nursing, BMI plays a crucial role in determining physical stamina. Nurses with excessively low BMI (underweight) may struggle with tasks that require significant physical strength, such as lifting patients. This physical limitation can exacerbate workplace stress and increase the risk of musculoskeletal injuries, ultimately undermining their capacity to execute their duties effectively.[50] Consequently, this can lead to a heightened workload, potentially contributing to the development of depressive symptoms.[58,59] Therefore, hospital administrators should pay more attention to underweight nurses’ nutrition and mental health status.

Our data showed that there is no association between being overweight and depressive symptoms among nurses, which is consistent with our hypothesis. One possible explanation could be that the physical demands of nursing might not be as challenging for overweight nurses as they are for underweight or obese nurses. This could mean that overweight nurses are less likely to experience physical stress and related mental health issues compared to their underweight or obese counterparts. Another possible explanation could be that, because of the “jolly fat” hypothesis and traditional Chinese culture, suggesting that overweight individuals may enjoy better psychological well-being.[60] This could potentially mitigate the adverse effects of being overweight on their mental health.

In addition, this study found that age, educational level, professional title, monthly income, and working in certain departments were factors influencing nurses’ depressive symptoms. First, our data showed that nurses who were 50 years and older, had a bachelor’s degree or above, and had a senior professional title were not likely to have depressive symptoms, meaning that low-qualified nurses were prone to depressive symptoms. Cheung and Yip[2] found that younger nurses may experience more frequent symptoms of stress. Conversely, older nurses may have developed coping strategies over time, allowing them to better manage stress and maintain their mental health. Nurses with lower educational levels may face challenges in advancing their careers or accessing professional development opportunities, which could contribute to feelings of great psychological pressure in clinical nursing work and further lead to depressive symptoms. Nurses with senior professional titles may experience greater job satisfaction and autonomy,[61] which can positively impact their mental health. Second, our results also showed that, compared with nurses with a monthly income of <5000 CNY, those with a monthly income between 5000 CNY and 8000 CNY were more prone to having depressive symptoms, which was inconsistent with previous studies.[62,63] The possible explanations for this research result may involve multiple factors, and the specific causes require further investigation to determine. One potential explanation is the tendency for individuals to engage in social comparisons regarding their life circumstances.[64] For nurses earning between 5000 and 8000 CNY per month, they may perceive themselves as neither as financially secure as those in higher-income brackets nor sufficiently differentiated from lower-income peers in terms of income and quality of life. This scenario may exacerbate their psychological disequilibrium. Third, our results revealed that nurses working in the departments of surgery, obstetrics and gynecology, and pediatrics were more likely to have depressive symptoms. According to previous studies,[65–67] compared to nurses working in other departments, nurses working in surgery, obstetrics and gynecology, and pediatrics departments often face complex and diverse patient diseases, a heavy daily nursing workload, high risks, and a high level of burnout, which lead them to experience more depressive symptoms.[68]

5. Limitation and strength

This study has some limitations. First, as this study adopted a cross-sectional design, it lacks the ability to establish a causal link between BMI and depressive symptoms. Second, the utilization of self-report methodology for data collection in this study poses the risk of biases. Third, some variables that may affect both BMI and depressive symptoms were not included in the analysis of this study, such as genetics, physical illness, physical activity, nutrition, sleep quality, and work burden. Fourth, although the present study was conducted in the context of China’s progress in containing COVID-19 and subsequent relaxation of COVID-19 restrictions, it is crucial to consider the enduring effects of the pandemic on the mental health status of nurses. Fifth, because a level of depressive symptoms measured by a self-reporting scale that on its own cannot lead to the diagnosis of depression, the result of this study is a preliminary finding. For further research, a more rigorous approach involving clinical diagnosis can be employed to meticulously investigate and elucidate the potential associations between BMI and depression. Lastly, although it is indeed a prevalent phenomenon for Chinese nurses to be subjected to high stress and high-intensity workloads,[44,47] the lack of measurement within the target population may introduce a certain degree of bias in our interpretations. Therefore, it is crucial to acknowledge this limitation and strive for more comprehensive and accurate data collection in future studies to mitigate any potential biases. Despite the aforementioned limitations, this study presents several strengths. First, it identifies a novel relationship between BMI and depressive symptoms among nurses, addressing a critical gap in the existing literature. These findings provide valuable insights for developing targeted interventions to improve nurses’ mental health. Second, the study sample demonstrates strong representativeness, as Shandong Province exhibits demographic, social, and cultural characteristics typical of China.[69] This geographical selection enhances the generalizability of our results. In addition, this study had a large sample size and a high response rate.

6. Conclusion

In conclusion, the depressive symptom prevalence in nurses was high, suggesting that depressive symptoms are common among Chinese nurses. Compared with nurses within the normal weight range, nurses with underweight or obesity were more likely to have depressive symptoms. In addition, younger nurses, nurses with a low educational background, professional title, and income, and nurses working in departments of surgery, obstetrics and gynecology, or pediatrics were more likely to have depressive symptoms. Hospital administrators should take effective measures to encourage nurses to maintain a normal or slightly overweight BMI status and focus on the role of various risk factors to improve nurses’ depressive symptoms. However, as a cross-sectional design, this study cannot establish causal relationships between the identified risk factors and depressive symptoms. The observed associations may reflect bidirectional influences or be confounded by unmeasured variables. Hospital administrators should therefore implement holistic interventions (including BMI monitoring programs) while acknowledging that longitudinal studies are needed to clarify causal pathways and inform evidence-based preventive strategies.
