Intestinal Ischemia Unveiling Takayasu Arteritis: A Rare Encounter
Authors: Dhamodhara Kannan Shivarajan, Bhupendra Mehra, Soumya Ghoshal, Siddharth P. Dubhashi
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

Takayasu arteritis (TA) is a rare large-vessel vasculitis primarily affecting adolescent girls and young women,[1] with a higher prevalence among the Asian population.[2] This idiopathic condition predominantly involves the aorta and its branches,[3] leading to vascular stenosis and various clinical manifestations such as claudication, headache, neck pain, and hypertension. Intestinal and mesenteric involvement, though uncommon, can occur in 11%–28% of cases.[4] We present a young male who attended the emergency ward with intestinal obstruction, leading to the discovery of TA during diagnostic evaluation.

A 16-year-old male presented with acute abdominal pain, vomiting, nausea, and distension. He had a similar episode 6 years prior managed conservatively. Physical examination revealed tachycardia, hypertension, and fever. Abdominal X-ray indicated small-bowel obstruction. A contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan confirmed bowel obstruction and revealed a narrowing of the aorta. The patient responded well to conservative management.

Persistent hypertension and CT findings of mesenteric ischemia contemplated further investigation for vasculitis syndromes. The patient lacked typical vasculitis symptoms, but showed a notable blood pressure difference between the upper and lower limbs. CT angiogram [Figure 1] demonstrated significant aortic wall thickening with complete luminal narrowing of both the celiac and superior mesenteric arteries. The formation of collateral vessels from the inferior mesenteric artery played an essential role in restoring blood flow, contributing to the reformation of the celiac and superior mesenteric arteries. Treatment involved oral prednisolone, methotrexate, and antihypertensives. Follow-up showed improvement without new vascular involvement.

TA causes arterial inflammation, leading to stenosis and ischemic complications such as mesenteric ischemia.[5] The disease course varies, from nonspecific systemic symptoms to vascular insufficiency. Our case aligns with the criteria for pediatric-onset TA, characterized by persistent hypertension and a notable blood pressure difference between the upper and lower limbs (>10 mmHg) with characteristic CT angiography.[6]

Collateral circulation from the inferior mesenteric artery likely contributed to our patient’s favorable outcome. Treatment involves immunosuppression and, in some cases, vascular surgery or endovascular therapy.

Intestinal ischemia is a rare but severe complication of TA. Timely recognition and appropriate management are crucial to prevent morbidity and mortality associated with intestinal involvement in TA patients.
