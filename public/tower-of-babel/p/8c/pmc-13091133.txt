Muscle-fiber-inspired nanofibrillar microbundles induce myogenic differentiation in human adipose-derived stem cells
Authors: Camilla Mussoni, Corinna Heinze, Matthias Ryma, Indong Jun, Zan Lamberger, Kristina Andelovic, Juliane C Kade, Philipp Stahlhut, Gregor Lang, Taufiq Ahmad, Jürgen Groll
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSkeletal muscle function relies on uniaxially organized myofibers, whose aligned extracellular matrix provides instructive topographical cues that regulate myogenic behavior. Here, we introduce a melt electrofibrillation strategy, melt electrowriting (MEW) of poly (ε-caprolactone)/poly (vinyl acetate) blends, followed by selective polyvinyl acetate removal, to fabricate highly aligned nanofibrillar microbundle scaffolds that present collagen-like nanotopography. We first verified biocompatibility and alignment guidance using primary human skeletal muscle cells compared with 2D tissue culture polystyrene controls. We then assessed the myogenic response of human adipose-derived stem cells (hASCs) on nanofibrillar scaffolds relative to conventional MEW-printed microfibers and 2D controls. Nanofibrils supported sustained viability over 35 days and promoted pronounced cell alignment, aligned collagen type-I deposition, and enhanced myogenic differentiation. hASCs on the scaffolds formed myosin-positive, multinucleated myotube-like structures by days 28–35 and exhibited increased MYOG and MYF6 expression by qPCR. Bulk RNA sequencing (day 21) further showed that nanofibrils induce a distinct transcriptional state enriched for muscle development/differentiation and contraction-associated programs, accompanied by activation of mechanosensitive signaling and adhesion–cytoskeleton remodeling pathways (PI3K-Akt, MAPK, Wnt, Hippo, and TGF-β). Titin immunostaining revealed early sarcomere assembly, indicating progression toward maturation. Collectively, nanofibrillar scaffolds establish a programmable, muscle-mimetic fibrillar niche that strengthens topography-driven myogenesis of hASCs and supports prolonged culture, providing a versatile platform for in vitro muscle tissue engineering.Keywords: Nanofibrillar microbundles, Melt electrowriting, Melt electrofibrillation, Muscle fiber, Extracellular matrix, Myogenesis, Muscle tissue engineering

Introduction

Skeletal muscle is one of the most abundant tissues in the human body and plays a crucial role in enabling body movement, maintaining posture, and driving locomotion [[1], [2], [3]]. However, due to their significant involvement in body activities, skeletal muscles are prone to injury caused by strain, trauma, contusions, exercise, or surgical intervention, which could disrupt muscle functions and lead to tissue fibrosis and chronic disability [4]. Although the damaged muscle has an intrinsic capacity to regenerate via activation, proliferation, and differentiation of muscle resident stem cells [5], it fails to restore volumetric muscle loss (VML) during critical injury, advanced age, and chronic inflammation [6,7]. The current gold standard treatment options involve the transplantation of an autologous muscle flap to injured muscle tissue [8]. However, the limited availability of donor site, morbidity, and loss of function of donor tissue limit the use of muscle flaps for clinical applications [9]. These limitations motivate the development of biomaterial platforms that can recreate key structural cues of native muscle to guide organized regeneration.

Skeletal muscles are highly organized tissues composed of densely packed and highly uniaxially oriented microstructures called myofibers [3,10]. The proper functioning of skeletal muscles relies heavily on the hierarchically arranged myofibers, which generate contractile forces [11]. The myofiber's pivotal role extends beyond mere locomotion; it supports resident muscle stem cells, called satellite cells, and serves as a template to guide them into elongated, multinucleated myotube formation [12] during acute muscle injury [13]. Primary muscle-derived stem cells held a central role in studying myogenesis [14]. However, in tissue engineering and biofabrication, reliance often shifted toward murine myoblasts (C2C12) due to their robustness and ease of handling compared to primary skeletal muscle cells [15,16]. Another promising avenue emerged with multipotent mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), which exhibit differentiation potential into osteogenic, chondrogenic, adipogenic, and myogenic lineages [17]. These cells, sourced from various sites such as bone marrow, adipose tissue, and skeletal muscle, opened up new possibilities for tissue regeneration [18,19]. Especially, the MSCs from adipose tissues, also called adipose-derived stem cells (ASCs), are readily obtainable from discarded adipose tissue without limitations on cell numbers, offering a more accessible and abundant cell source than other sources [19]. Transplantation of ASCs promoted anal sphincter muscle repair in a rat injury model by secreting immunomodulatory cytokines and also contributed to new muscle fiber formation in immunocompetent mdx mice [20,21]. Moreover, in vitro experiments have revealed ASCs' ability to merge with C2C12 cells to form myotubes [22]. Nevertheless, robust and efficient myogenic induction of ASCs remains challenging, typically requiring extensive biochemical and/or biophysical stimulation. [20,23,24]. Accordingly, ASCs provide a stringent and clinically relevant cell source to test whether defined scaffold topography can bias lineage commitment.

Therefore, biomaterial scaffolds that precisely replicate myofiber micro-nanoarchitecture, enabling control over three-dimensional (3D) cellular distribution and alignment, hold immense potential for advancing strategies in muscle regeneration and addressing critical tissue loss scenarios. Conventionally, the cellular arrangement and anisotropic alignment of myoblasts are achieved on two-dimensional (2D) substrates via defined microarchitecture grooves or through anisotropically aligned cell adhesive proteins that are micropatterned [[25], [26], [27]]. These biomaterials promoted elongation and aligned myotube formation. Alternatively, electrospun nanofibers closely resemble the structural arrangement of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins down to the nanoscale and could be functionalized with bioactive signals [28]. Previous research has shown a significant effect of nanofiber alignment on adhesion, elongation, and myogenic differentiation [[29], [30], [31], [32]]. Recent reports indicated that aligned nanofibrous scaffolds stimulated the elongation of human-induced pluripotent stem cells and human embryonic stem cells, which further fused and formed myotubes [33,34]. Despite promising in vitro results, electrospun nanofiber scaffolds are densely packed nanofiber sheets with low porosity, limiting cell infiltration and primarily providing a 2D surface for cell growth and differentiation, whereas muscle fibers arranged in microbundles allow cell growth in the 3D microenvironment. Conversely, melt electrowriting (MEW) enables precise, programmable deposition of fibers into reproducible architectures, but its fibers are commonly in the micrometer range, limiting direct recapitulation of ECM-relevant nanotopography [[35], [36]]. Thus, a key unmet need is a fabrication strategy that combines ECM-like nanotopography with the geometric controllability and reproducibility of MEW.

In native skeletal muscle tissue, the satellite cells are surrounded by highly ordered collagen fibers, and their nano-topographical cues greatly influence the growth and differentiation of satellite cells into myoblasts [3]. Besides, the remnant ECM fiber following injury was reported to guide satellite cells alignment, proliferation, and myotube formation to ensure muscle regeneration [37]. Therefore, biomaterial scaffolds composed of nanofibrils arranged in microbundles could mimic the 3D microarchitecture of muscle tissue, and their nano-topographical aspect of microbundles could modulate the myogenic induction in stem cells. Our group recently introduced a novel approach called melt electrofibrillation (MEF) to create nanofibrillar microbundles to tune nano-topography assisted macrophage polarization to modulate immune response [38]. We hypothesized MEF of highly aligned nanofibrillar microbundle scaffolds (nFMBs) could mimic the muscle fiber nano-topography to support myogenic induction in primary human skeletal muscle cells (HSkMCs) and human adipose-derived stem cells (hASCs). In this study, we evaluated the fabrication of aligned nFMBs, and characterized them using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and performed Raman spectroscopy, as well as X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis. Finally, we examined the effect of nFMBs on HSkMCs and hASCs morphology and myogenic induction using immunofluorescence staining and qPCR analysis.

Materials and methods

Melt electrofibrillation of nanofibrillar microbundles

Three blends of poly (vinyl acetate) (PVAc; Kremer Pigmente GmbH & Co. KG, Aichstetten, Germany) and poly (ε-caprolactone) (PCL; PC12, Purasorb, Corbion, The Netherland) in different ratios (70/30, 75/25, 80/20) were produced by dissolving PVAc and PCL accordingly in dichloromethane (DCM). The solutions were poured on a sheet of Teflon. After the solvent evaporated overnight, the blends were stored in centrifuge tubes at room temperature in a vacuum desiccator. Scaffolds were printed with a custom-built MEW device [38,39]. To print scaffolds, the syringe was preheated to 175 °C and the nozzle at 160 °C. The needle tip-to-collector distance is kept constant at about 3 mm, and the aligned fibers were generated with an applied voltage of 3 kV and a pressure of 1 bar. The collector speed was set to 1500 mm/min, but it is to be noted that speed and temperature varied slightly between printing sessions to maintain a stable jet. The movement of the grounded collector plate was programmed in G-code and controlled by the software Mach3Mill. The scaffolds were printed to build aligned microfibers (1 cm × 1 cm) with a spacing of 0.25 mm. The printed scaffolds were stamped on the edges to prevent defibration with a metal punch heated to 120 °C. To achieve the nFMBs, selective dissolution of PVAc was carried out by washing scaffolds in 70% ethanol twice for 30 min, followed by a third wash for 1 h in 70% ethanol to expose PCL nanofibrillar microbundles. Then, scaffolds were washed thrice in distilled water for 5 min each step to remove ethanol before further characterization.

Characterization of scaffolds

The scaffold characterization process began with macroscopic imaging using a stereomicroscope (Discovery V20, Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Göttingen, Germany) equipped with a CCD camera (AxioCam ICc 5, Carl Zeiss) to preliminarily confirm nanofibrillar microbundle formation in nFMBs, with PCL-microfiber scaffolds (mFiBs) imaged for structural comparison. Subsequently, high-resolution images of the nanofibrillar microbundles were acquired after sputter-coating the scaffolds with an 8 nm platinum layer (Leica EM ACE600, Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany). The coated scaffolds (n = 3 scaffolds per group) were then imaged by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) using a Crossbeam 340 system equipped with a Zeiss Gemini column (Zeiss Microscopy, Göttingen, Germany). Image analysis using ImageJ software facilitated the determination of fiber diameter; 50 randomly selected fibers from SEM images were used to calculate mean values.

Following this, Raman spectroscopy analysis was conducted on the scaffolds before and after washing to verify the absence of PVAc. Employing a Raman Microscope DXR (Thermo Scientific) equipped with a 780 nm laser at a power level of 24 mW, measurements were taken at 10x magnification, exposing the samples to three 60-s exposures.

The thermal degradation behavior of the scaffolds was assessed through Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA) using a TG 209F1 Iris instrument (NETZSCH, Selb, Germany). Samples weighing 5-10 mg were placed in aluminum oxide crucibles (NETZSCH, Selb, Germany) and subjected to heating from 30 °C to 900 °C under synthetic air at a 10 °C/min heating rate.

Furthermore, X-ray Diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded using a diffractometer (D5005; Siemens, Karlsruhe, Germany) with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 18 nm) under specified parameters: voltage of 40 kV, current of 40 mA, 2ϴ range from 10° to 60°, step size of 0.02°, and a scan rate of 8 s per step. The mechanical performance of the scaffolds was assessed by tensile testing. For tensile mechanical tests, scaffolds for each condition (n = 3) were mounted on a universal testing machine (Z010, ZwickRoell, Ulm, Germany) equipped with a 100 N load cell and stretched at 1 mm/s until 150% of deformation. The area on which stress is applied was evaluated as the sum of the area of the fiber cross-section.

Cell culture

HSkMCs (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) were cultured according to the manufacturer's recommendations in ATCC Mesenchymal Stem Cell Basal Medium (PCS-500-030, ATCC) supplemented with the Primary Skeletal Muscle Growth Kit (PCS-950-040, ATCC) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (P/S), under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2). In parallel, hASCs (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) were cultured in DMEM/F-12 (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS) and 1% P/S under the same conditions. Both cell types were commercially obtained as de-identified research-use materials; therefore, no donor-identifying information was available to the authors. Both cell types were used up to passage 7. HSkMC experiments were performed as an initial benchmark validation of scaffold biocompatibility/alignment (n = 3 independent samples), whereas hASC experiments represent the primary study focus and were conducted with increased replication (n = 4 independent samples).

For cellular experiments, HSkMCs (and hASCs where applicable) were cultured either on scaffolds (nFMBs or mFiBs) or on standard tissue culture-treated polystyrene plates (TCP) as the 2D control. Scaffolds were sterilized in 70% ethanol, exposed to UV light for 30 min, and washed five times with DPBS without calcium and magnesium (Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany). Prior to seeding, scaffolds were pre-wetted in the corresponding complete growth medium for at least 30 min. Scaffolds were then transferred to ultra-low attachment (ULA)-coated 6-well plates and secured with pre-sterilized metal rings to prevent floating. For the 2D control, cells were seeded directly into standard tissue culture-treated 6-well plates.

At ∼90% confluence, cells were detached using trypsin-EDTA and collected by centrifugation. Subsequently, 5 × 105 cells were seeded per scaffold (and per well for the 2D control unless stated otherwise). Following seeding, constructs were maintained for 5 days in complete growth medium (HSkMC growth medium or hASC growth medium, as defined above) to allow attachment and achieve scaffold coverage prior to downstream analyses.

Live and dead assay

The viability of cells on scaffolds was evaluated using the Live and Dead staining kit (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Waltham, USA) on the 7th, 21st, and 35th day of culture (n = 3 samples per group). Briefly, cells were treated with a working solution of Calcein AM (1:1000) and ethidium-homodimer (1:500) in DPBS for 30 min under standard culture conditions. After that, images were captured using a confocal microscope (Leica LSM SP8, Germany).

Immunofluorescence staining

The F-actin staining was performed to analyze the cell morphology and alignment on day 7 as the cells were confluent at this time point. Briefly, scaffolds seeded with cells (n = 3) collected on day seven were washed with PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4 °C. Then, samples were washed thrice in PBS and incubated in blocking buffers for 1 h at room temperature. Then, a working solution of Phalloidin-iFluor 555 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) was applied for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Afterward, samples were washed with PBS 5 times and 5 min for each washing step. Finally, samples were mounted with DAPI (Dianova, Hamburg, Germany) and analyzed using a confocal microscope. The cell alignment was evaluated using ImageJ.

Focal adhesion and ECM staining

Focal adhesion and ECM staining were performed using paxillin and collagen type-1 antibody staining. Briefly, the samples (n = 3) were fixed and blocked, as described earlier. Then we incubated the scaffolds with anti-paxillin (Invitrogen, Thermo Fischer Scientific, Waltham, USA) and anti-collagen type-1 (Millipore, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) working solution 1:100 overnight at 4 °C. Next, we washed the samples thrice in PBS and incubated them with Alexa fluor 488 labeled secondary antibody (1:200) for 2 h at room temperature under the dark. We then washed the samples with PBS 3 times and mounted them with DAPI. Images were taken using a confocal microscope.

SEM imaging of cells on the scaffolds

Preparation was conducted on ice following fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde and 6% glutaraldehyde for 15 min each. The samples (n = 3) were then dehydrated using a graded ethanol series up to 100% and dried with hexamethyldisilane (Sigma-Aldrich, Steinheim, Germany), as previously described [40,41]. A 4 nm layer of platinum was sputter-coated onto the scaffolds using a Leica EM ACE600 sputter coater. SEM images were acquired with a Zeiss Crossbeam 340 Field-Emission Electron Microscope (Zeiss Microscopy, Oberkochen, Germany). Focused ion beam (FIB) was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions, and protocols were established to ensure precise sectioning, and SEM micrographs were taken to analyze the cell infiltration.

Myogenic differentiation

After cell seeding, scaffolds were maintained in growth medium for 5 days to achieve full cell coverage. The medium was then replaced with differentiation medium, and this time point was defined as Day 1.

For HSkMCs, myogenic differentiation was induced using Skeletal Muscle Differentiation Medium (ATCC, Manassas, USA). HSkMC control conditions were maintained in the corresponding HSkMC growth medium.

For hASCs, myogenic induction medium consisted of high-glucose DMEM (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) supplemented with 2% horse serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 50 ng/mL IGF, 5 μg/mL L-ascorbic acid 2-phosphate, and 1% P/S. The corresponding hASC control group during the experimental phase was cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FCS and 1% P/S. For myogenic marker staining, samples were immunostained using anti-myosin (Invitrogen), anti-myogenin (DSHB, Iowa City, USA), or anti-titin (DSHB, Iowa City, USA) following the same procedure described infocal adhesion and ECM staining.

Myogenic gene expression

Firstly, we collected samples (n = 3) of HSkMCs on 1, 7, and 14 days of culture under differentiation media conditions. At the same time, samples for hASCs (n = 4) were collected on 7, 14, 21, 28, and 35 days. The samples were homogenized in Trizol (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Waltham, USA) and stored at −80 °C until further use. Then, samples were thawed on ice, added chloroform, and centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 10 min to achieve phase separation. The separated phases were collected and precipitated with isopropanol on ice, followed by 12000 rpm centrifugation. Then, supernatants were removed, and RNA pellets were washed with 75% chilled ethanol. After that, the extracted RNA was dried and solubilized in DEPC water. The RNA concentration was measured with the TECAN reader (Tecan Trading AG, Männedorf, Switzerland), and RNA was stored at −20 °C. The cDNA was synthesized using a high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) was used to synthesize. About 500 ng RNA was diluted in 10 μl DEPC-H2O and mixed with 10 μl Master mix. The reaction was performed with the PCR-Cycler at 25 °C (10 min), 37 °C (120 min), 85 °C (5 min) and four °C. Further, qPCR was performed to analyze the expression of the myogenic markers by keeping hEF1α as the housekeeping gene. About 2 μl of Synthesized cDNA was mixed with the master mix and with specified primer sequences for hEF1α (For GCCCATGTGTGTTGAGAGC and Rev: CCGCAACTGTCTGTCTCATATC), hMYOG (For CGAATGCAGCTCTCACAGCG and Rev: CCGTGAGCAGATGATCCCC), and hMYF6 (For GATAACGGCTAAGGAAGGAGGAG and Rev: TGTCCACGATGGAAGAAAGGC). Then, mixed solutions and centrifuge qPCR were performed for 40 cycles with melting at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing and extension at 60 °C for 60 s, and then at 62 °C. The relative expression of each gene was normalized with 2D control in growth media.

Whole transcriptome sequencing (RNA-Seq) analysis: Transcriptome sequencing analysis was performed using hASCs at 21 days. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions, and RNA quality and integrity were assessed prior to sequencing. Purified RNA samples were then submitted to Novogene (Munich, Germany) for library preparation and high-throughput sequencing. Following quality control, mRNA was enriched using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads, and strand-specific libraries were constructed using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Sample Preparation Kit. Paired-end sequencing (150 bp) was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw sequencing data were subjected to quality assessment using FastQC to evaluate read quality, and adapter sequences and poly-A stretches were removed using Trimmomatic. Cleaned reads were then aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using HISAT2 (version 2.2.1). The resulting BAM files were processed with StringTie (version 2.2.1) for transcript assembly and quantification, and gene expression levels were calculated as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Differentially expressed genes were identified using DESeq2 with a fold change threshold of at least 1.5 and a p-value below 0.05. Principal component analysis, Venn diagrams, heatmaps, and volcano plots were generated using R (version 4.2.2) with the ggplot2, VennDiagram, pheatmap, and EnhancedVolcano packages. Functional enrichment analyses were performed using an over-representation analysis approach, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment was conducted using the clusterProfiler package.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance was assessed using Student's t-test, one-way ANOVA (single-factor comparisons), and two-way ANOVA (two-factor comparisons) followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests, as appropriate. The significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Melt electrofibrillation to fabricate aligned nanofibrillar microbundles

To fabricate muscle fiber-inspired nanofibrillar microbundles, we utilized the MEF approach, which our group introduced to fabricate scaffolds while maintaining the nano-topography of collagen fibrillar microbundles [[38], [42], [43]]. Here, we deploy MEF to create highly aligned collagen-sized nano fibrillar microbundles to mimic the micro-nanoarchitecture of muscle fibers. As shown in Fig. 1, we first prepared aligned mFiBs of poly (ε-caprolactone) (PCL) blended with a high shared volume of polyvinyl acetate (PVAc) through melt electrowriting (MEW). After that, PVAc dissolution by washing mFiBs in 70% ethanol resulted in the formation of highly ordered nFMBs, whose nano-topography was further evaluated for the myogenic induction of HSkMC and hASCs.

Schematic representation illustrating the melt electrofibrillation process of nFMBs and their influence on cell myogenic induction.

To make stable microbundles of nFMBs, we screened different blend ratios of PCL and PVAc, including 30% PCL +70% PVAc, 25% PCL +75% PVAc, 20% PCL +80% PVAc, and we confirmed all the blends formed aligned fibers. However, only the 30% PCL +70% PVAc blended mFiBs resulted in highly ordered and stably aligned nanofibrils microbundles (Fig. 2a and b). Meanwhile, with the remaining two blends, the nanofibril microbundles were loosely formed, unstable, and collapsed during the washing steps. Due to its excellent miscibility with polyesters [44,45], the PVAc has been reported to form blends with various polymers [46]. Both PVAc and PCL can interact with each other through hydrogen bonding, which provides a homogenous distribution of polymers in blends [45]. Besides, the electric field during the MEW process may influence the alignment of respective molecular chains by stretching individual polymer chains into the direction of the field [47]. Therefore, the selected phase separation resulted in the distribution of fine nanoscale-aligned fibrils in microbundles [48].

Nanofibrillar bundles fabrication: a) Stereomicroscope images displaying the formation of nanofibrillar bundles using various PVAc-PCL blends. b) SEM images revealing nanofibrillar bundles formed after the removal of different PVAc blends, indicating stable formation in the 30% PCL +70% PVAc blend. Surface chemistry analysis c-d) of nanofibrillar bundles using Raman spectroscopy, with PVAc representative peaks marked with grey backgrounds.

Following the fabrication of various blends of PCL + PVAc mFiBs and subsequent washing steps, we conducted Raman spectroscopy on PCL-mFiBs, the different blends, and the washed nanofibrillar microbundles (Fig. 2c, d, and 2e). The Raman analysis confirmed characteristic peaks of PVAc within the ranges of 558-665 cm−1 (attributed to C=O bending), 761-835 cm−1 (attributed to C-C stretching), 1336-1404 cm−1 (attributed to C-H stretching), and 1718-1790 cm−1 (attributed to C=O stretching) [49,50] in all blends before washing. However, post-washing, the absence of PVAc-related peaks in all washed samples suggested the efficacy of 70% ethanol in the triple-washing process in removing residual PVAc from the blends. Conversely, distinctive peaks representative of PCL were detected in PCL-mFiBs and the thrice-washed nanofibrillar microbundles, at 913 cm−1 (attributed to C-COO stretching), 1008-1115 cm−1 (attributed to skeletal stretching), 1268-1324 cm−1 (attributed to CH2 wagging), 1412-1485 cm−1 (attributed to CH2 scissoring), and 1725-1770 cm−1 (associated with C=O stretching) [51,52].

Further, we characterized the 30% PCL + 70% PVAc blended mFiBs before and after each washing step. SEM images of PCL-PVA blends reveal distinct stages: initially, aligned microfibers are observed in scaffolds. After the first wash, PVAc dissolution occurs, forming a surface film. Subsequent washings unveil nanofibril microbundles, yet remnants of PVAc persist. The third washing exhibits clear nanofibrillar microbundles, indicating the complete removal of PVAc (Fig. 3a).

Characterization of 30% PCL +70% PVAc scaffold: a) SEM images before and after each washing cycle showing complete PVAc removal after washing three times. b) XRD analysis before and after washing with comparison to pure PVAc and PCL, displaying enhanced PCL characteristic peaks after each washing step. c) TGA analysis of 30% PCL +70% PVAc. d) Fiber diameter comparison between PCL-microfibers (PCL-mFiBs) and nanofibrillar bundles (nFMBs). e) Alignment analysis of PCL-mFiBs and nFMBs.

These observations highlight the transformation from microfibers to nanofibrils, marked by PVAc dissolution and eventual removal through successive washings. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis revealed two broad peaks in PVAc at 14.5° and 22.4°, signifying its amorphous nature. Prior to washing, these characteristic broad peaks of PVAc were evident in the blended mFiBs, coexisting with the distinct sharp peaks of PCL at 21.4° and 23.9°. However, following the first wash, the intensity of the PVAc peaks noticeably reduced, and by the third wash, they were almost completely absent. Simultaneously, we observed the emergence of sharper peaks representative of PCL (Fig. 3b). In the washed samples, the peak at 23.8°, a characteristic PCL peak, was enhanced compared to the PCL-mFiBs. This enhancement suggests a potential increase in PCL's crystallinity or molecular ordering within the washed scaffold.

Then, we conducted a comparative analysis of their thermal degradation behavior to prove that blending PCL with PVAc doesn't affect the scaffold's stability. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) revealed no mass loss up to 250 °C for all the polymers and blends, confirming suitability for MEW processing. Pure PVAc exhibited degradation in two distinct stages, commencing with deacetylation at 300 °C and followed by structural backbone degradation at 415 °C [45,53]. On the other hand, PCL polymer granules underwent degradation in a single step, initiating gradually from 270 °C. The thermal degradation of PCL-PVAc blended mFiBs also occurred in two steps (300 °C and 354 °C), positioned between the degradation patterns of PVAc and PCL. Our results were consistent to previous report indicating the changes in thermal degradation behavior in PCL-PVAc blends [45]. Remarkably, we observed an enhancement in the thermal stability of the scaffolds with each washing step; PCL-mFiBs displayed a slightly improved thermal resistance compared to pure PCL granules, and this resistance was further augmented in nanofibrillar microbundle scaffolds (Fig. 3c). We assumed that the larger surface-to-volume ratio of PCL granules and PCL-mFiBs facilitated more efficient heat transfer during combustion [54]. Additionally, the heightened crystallinity observed in XRD, along with the reduced chain mobility due to the stretching and alignment process within the nanofibrillar microbundles, could have contributed to the overall improvement in thermal stability [48,55]. Importantly, TGA revealed no measurable mass loss up to 250 °C for PCL, PVAc, and the PCL/PVAc blends, indicating that thermal decomposition is not expected within the melt processing window used for MEW in this study. However, TGA identifies decomposition onsets and does not capture subtle, time-dependent chain scission that may occur during prolonged melt residence; thus, the present thermal analysis supports process suitability but does not define long-term dwell-time limits for repeated heating. Thermal history during MEW is an important parameter that can influence polymer molecular weight and, consequently, printability and scaffold mechanics, because the polymer reservoir may be held at elevated temperature for extended periods during low-throughput printing. Böhm et al. reported that continuous heating with daily printing over 25 days is feasible for medical-grade PCL, with temperature- and time-dependent effects on process stability and polymer properties. [56]. Likewise, Paxton et al. showed that cyclic heating over several days did not measurably compromise PCL molecular weight or scaffold mechanics under their tested conditions, whereas plasma treatment produced more pronounced degradation effects [57]. In the present study, a dedicated multi-day dwell-time study (defined residence times, number of reheating cycles, and molecular-weight tracking) was outside the scope. Instead, melt residence time was minimized by loading only the material required for planned printing runs; within this time window, no obvious changes in printing behavior were observed. Future work will quantify the effects of prolonged melt residence and repeated reheating of PCL/PVAc blends at our processing temperatures using SEC/GPC and rheology to establish evidence-based limits for syringe reuse across printing sessions. Based on our findings, we proceeded with three-time-washed nanofibrillar microbundles scaffolds (nFMBs) for subsequent studies. Image analysis revealed that both PCL-mFiBs and nFMBs exhibited uniaxial alignment (Fig. 3e). However, the distribution of nanofibrils within nFMBs appeared slightly broader than in PCL-mFiBs. This difference is attributed to the somewhat looser packing of nanofibrils within the microbundles. Notably, the diameter of fibrils within nFMBs was measured at 0.347 ± 0.11 μm (≈350 nm), contrasting with the recorded diameter of 67 ± 11.8 μm for PCL-mFiBs (Fig. 3d).

We compared the mechanical properties of nFMB scaffolds (70/30) and just PCL-mFiB. Tensile testing showed similar behavior of all the scaffolds, with PCL-mFiB showing a higher ultimate tensile strength (Fig. 3f). Young Modulus was evaluated for the PCL-mFiB and the unwashed and washed nFMBs as, respectively, 859.63 MPa, 876.85 MPa and 643.42 MPa.

Considerable focus has been placed on developing scalable nanostructured biomaterials that emulate muscle ECM characteristics, particularly nanoscale fibrous topography [58]. In this context, solution electrospinning (including aligned electrospinning) has been widely adopted because it can generate nanofibers within an ECM-relevant size range [34,58]. However, electrospun constructs are most commonly obtained as membrane-like fiber mats, and even aligned electrospun membranes typically provide a predominantly two-dimensional growth environment with limited deterministic control over three-dimensional microarchitecture and pore interconnectivity, which can restrict cellular infiltration unless additional post-processing strategies are employed [59]. Moreover, electrospinning frequently results in relatively broad fiber diameter distributions; for example, in our previous report, aligned electrospun nanofibers exhibited an average diameter of ∼600 nm with a wide distribution [40,59]. Alternatively, melt electrowriting (MEW) has transformed scaffold fabrication by enabling precise, G-code–guided control over microarchitecture geometry [60,61]. Nonetheless, MEW-generated fibers are typically in the micrometer range and therefore remain larger than the nanoscale features of native ECM [61]. In addition, sacrificial materials combined with biomaterials have conventionally been employed to induce nano- or microstructural pores within scaffolds, diversifying their architecture [62]. To bridge this gap, we integrated a sacrificial polymer into PCL blends and processed them via MEW to establish melt electrofibrillation (MEF) as a strategy to generate precisely aligned nanofibrillar microbundles within a well-defined scaffold geometry. MEF combines collagen-like nanotopography with an ordered, deterministic layout defined by G-code (Fig. S1), thereby uniting nanoscale cues with programmable architecture [40,63]. In our previous work, this nanofibrillar microbundle architecture promoted macrophage elongation and biased polarization toward an M2-like phenotype associated with pro-healing, anti-inflammatory responses [16,63] Leveraging this design control, we printed straight-line scaffolds to fabricate aligned nanofibrillar microbundles that replicate the micro–nanotopography observed in muscle fibers (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). We incorporated PVAc into our blends to closely match nanofibril diameters to collagen-fibril length scales, given its exceptional compatibility with PCL [38,44,45]. The chain arrangement and stretching alignment during printing facilitated the formation of PCL nanofibrils within microbundles in the submicron range, which is relevant because collagen is hierarchically organized from nanoscale fibrillar features into micron-scale fibers in native tissues [38,64]. Therefore, the nanofibrillar microbundles, with their closer resemblance to natural collagen fibers, may offer a closer mimicry of the natural ECM structure compared to the larger diameter microfibers present in PCL-mFiBs. This similarity in diameter might influence various aspects, including cellular interactions, tissue integration, and mechanical properties, thereby affecting the scaffold's overall performance in tissue engineering applications.

Myogenic induction of primary human skeletal muscle cells on nanofibrillar microbundles

To evaluate biocompatibility and the impact of nFMBs on myogenic induction, we cultured human skeletal muscle cells (HSkMCs) on nFMB scaffolds and compared their response to a 2D control group consisting of HSkMCs seeded on standard tissue culture plates (TCP). In both groups, cells were maintained in growth medium for five days to allow attachment and achieve full coverage of cells on scaffolds. Subsequently, myogenic induction medium was applied, and myogenic differentiation was assessed by immunofluorescence staining and quantitative PCR (qPCR).

We observed that while nFMBs facilitated excellent adhesion and alignment of these sensitive primary cells, the control PCL-mFiBs did not promote sufficient attachment, which is a known challenge for culturing HSkMCs without surface treatment. This initial study confirmed the superior topographical guidance of our nFMBs, leading us to focus the remainder of our study on this scaffold. Immunofluorescence analysis indicated that nFMBs facilitated the adhesion of HSkMCs, with the fibrillar topography notably influencing cell alignment. It's worth noting that we refrained from applying any additional treatments to the surface of nFMBs to enhance cell adhesion. The presence of nanofibrils within the nFMBs notably contributed to augmenting surface roughness, thereby fostering enhanced cell adhesion. Moreover, on aligned nFMBs, HSkMCs initiated the formation of multinucleated myotubes by day 7, and a subsequent increase in both myotube length and width was evident by day 14, as depicted in Fig. 4a. Besides, we observed multinucleated myotubes with nuclei clustered in the center of the cell body. We assume this observed structure reflects an early stage of myotube formation as part of the natural myotube formation process. This is further supported by observations in other studies, where nuclear clustering during early differentiation is common before the full maturation of myotubes [65]. This transient stage of nuclear arrangement is well documented in muscle development, particularly in the transition from nascent to mature myotubes, as noted in research on myoblast fusion and nuclear migration [[65], [66], [67]].

(mean ± SD, n = 3). Statistical significance: ns = not significant; ∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001.

The qPCR analysis revealed a significant increase in MYOG expression in nFMBs group from 4.35 ± 3.1, 12.51 ± 1.4, and 31.11 ± 1.5-fold on day 1, 7, and 14, respectively, compared to 2D control. Similarly, the MYF6 expression indicated 4.85 ± 2.64, 27.72 ± 1.83, and 35.2 ± 0.71 folds higher than 2D control on day 1, 7, and 14, respectively (Fig. 4 b and 4c). Notably, the expression levels of both genes were significantly higher in the nFMBs group compared to the 2D control across all time intervals studied. MYOG and MYF6 are predominantly expressed during the intermediate phase of myogenic differentiation and are essential for the formation and maintenance of myotubes [68,69]. In line with our myosin staining results, we observed higher expression of these factors in cells cultured on nFMBs. These findings underscore the nanofibrillar microbundle topography's influence in fostering cell alignment and adhesion and the enhanced expression of genes associated with muscle differentiation and maturation. Human primary muscle cells are ideal for studying muscle in vitro but are challenging to maintain due to limitations in passage number and availability [12,14]. Consequently, C2C12 cells are often used due to their ease of handling but may not accurately represent human physiology [15]. Besides, in the natural muscle environment, the guidance of muscle resident stem cells involves a highly intricate process, where the ECM's topographical features significantly influence cell morphology and the formation of myotubes [37]. As a result, biomaterial substrates that control cell alignment have been extensively investigated in muscle tissue engineering [70]. However, these biomaterials often necessitate additional modifications with cell adhesive signals to support cell adhesion effectively [70,71]. In contrast, nFMBs offer a distinct advantage by presenting a nanofibrillar arrangement organized within microbundles. This unique structure not only refines surface roughness but also plays a pivotal role in the interaction between cells and the material. Hence, nFMBs supported cell adhesion without requiring added cues, guiding cell morphology and ultimately leading myogenic differentiation of cells. This inherent capability of nFMBs showcases their potential as biomaterials that promote crucial cellular behaviors necessary for muscle tissue engineering. For the main part of this study, we shifted our focus to hASCs to provide a more rigorous test of our scaffold, as hASCs are a more challenging model for myogenic induction due to their limited growth and differentiation potential.

Myogenic induction of human adipose-derived stem cells through nanofibrillar microbundles

Then, we established hASCs cultures on nFMBs and compared their effects on hASCs with those of PCL-mFiBs over a 35-day culture. We assessed the cells' viability, alignment, ECM secretion, and myogenic induction. The myogenic induction was studied in both growth and differentiation media.

We initially evaluated the viability of hASCs when cultured on PCL-mFiBs and nFMBs, as illustrated in Fig. S2. Based on our seeding protocol, constructs typically reached full surface coverage by ∼ day 5; therefore, we performed cytoskeletal, focal adhesion, and ECM staining at day 7 (i.e., ∼48 h post-confluence) to capture a stabilized early cell layer and enable robust quantification of alignment and cell-material interactions. The live and dead cell staining revealed distinct behaviors in cell adhesion and viability between these substrates. On day 7, the hASCs fully covered the surface of the nFMBs, indicating excellent cell adhesion without significant changes in cell density over the culture period. Additionally, there were notably minimal signals of cell death observed in hASCs grown on the nFMBs throughout the culture time. In contrast, on the PCL-mFiBs, cells adhered around the fibers by day 7 but did not entirely cover the surface. These sparsely adhered cells exhibited a considerable number of signals indicating cell death. However, with extended culture duration (specifically, on days 21 and 35), the adhesion of cells improved on the PCL-mFiBs, yet there were still indications of dead cell signals.

Then, we examined the alignment and morphology of hASCs cultured on the scaffolds. Actin staining provided insights into the cellular arrangement on the substrates (Fig. 5a), revealing a highly ordered alignment of hASCs on the scaffolds, closely resembling the natural alignment seen in muscle fibers. Interestingly, cells cultured on the PCL-mFiBs displayed a tendency to form aggregates, potentially influenced by the larger diameter of the fibers, resulting in compromised cell alignment compared to the more organized alignment observed on nFMBs. Quantitative image analysis further supported these observations, indicating a higher degree of alignment among cells on nFMBs in contrast to a broader distribution of alignment observed on PCL-mFiBs (Fig. 5b). Additionally, our examination revealed a significant impact of nFMBs on reducing nuclear circularity to 0.5, while cells on PCL-mFiBs displayed a higher circularity value of 0.8 (Fig. 5d). This reduction in nuclear circularity suggests a more elongated and aligned cell morphology facilitated by the nanofibrillar microbundle substrates compared to the less organized and more rounded cellular morphology seen on the PCL-mFiBs.

Adhesion and morphology of hASCs on the scaffold: a) F-actin staining of hASCs cultured on PCL-mFiBs and nFMBs, showing elongated aligned cells on nFMBs compared to PCL-mFiBs. b) Alignment of hASCs on PCL-mFiBs and nFMBs. c) Nuclear circularity of hASCs on both scaffolds.

We examined the focal adhesions of hASCs in relation to the fibers within the substrates. Paxillin staining indicated a distinct pattern of focal adhesion signals distributed prominently alongside the nanofibrils within the nFMBs (Fig. 6a). Conversely, the intensity of paxillin signals appeared comparatively lower on the PCL-mFiBs, suggesting a difference in the extent of cell-material interactions between the two substrates. This variation in paxillin signals indicated that cells adhering to the nanofibrils within the nFMBs could actively modulate their morphology and cellular responses [72,73]. Further SEM imaging provided additional insights. On the PCL-mFiBs, cells seemed to encase or wrap around the fibers, while a highly aligned orientation of cells along the fibrils within the nFMBs was notably evident (Fig. 6b). This observation reinforced the notion of distinct cell behaviors on these substrates, emphasizing the influence of nanofibrillar microbundle structures in guiding cell alignment and morphology. Beyond cell morphology and scaffold interactions, we confirmed cell infiltration into the nFMB scaffolds. The z-stack confocal images, particularly from DAPI staining, revealed cells at multiple layers, demonstrating their presence within the scaffolds (Fig. S3a). However, we also observed a monolayer of cells covering the fiber periphery in PCL-mFibs. To further validate these results, we performed focused ion beam scanning electron microscopy (FIB-SEM), which confirmed cell infiltration into the nFMB scaffolds (Fig. S3b), underscoring the potential of these scaffolds for fabricating 3D in vitro tissue and aiding in the recovery of volumetric muscle loss.

Moreover, we evaluated the collagen type-1 secretion through immunostaining. The results unveiled a remarkable difference in the deposition pattern of collagen type-1 between the two substrates. There was a notably aligned collagen deposition on nanofibrils in nFMBs, contrasting with the less organized distribution observed on the PCL-mFiBs (Fig. 6c–d). These findings underscore the capacity of nFMBs to not only support cell adhesion but also stimulate the secretion of collagen type-1, a key component of skeletal muscle ECM, in an aligned manner. This highlights the potential of nFMBs in facilitating a cellular environment conducive to tissue engineering applications by promoting specific ECM deposition and cell-material interactions. Collectively, the focal adhesion organization together with scaffold-guided, aligned collagen type-I deposition indicates that nFMB topography modulates cell-matrix adhesion architecture and ECM remodeling in an anisotropic manner-two upstream features commonly linked to mechanotransduction in aligned tissues. These phenotypic observations are consistent with transcriptome-level enrichment of focal adhesion/ECM-receptor interaction and cytoskeletal remodeling pathways, as described below (bulk RNA-seq; Fig. 9).

Finally, we evaluated the myogenic induction in hASCs cultured on nFMBs in comparison to PCL-mFiBs. Myogenic differentiation was assessed through MYOG staining, where cells cultured on nFMBs demonstrated significantly higher MYOG + cell populations compared to those on PCL-mFiBs (Fig. 7a and 7b). A clear increase in MYOG + cells was observed as the culture time progressed from day 21 to day 35. The evaluation of myogenic marker expression, MYOG, and MYF6, in hASCs under various conditions provided intriguing insights into the influence of scaffold types. The qPCR analysis demonstrated a consistent increase in MYOG and MYF6 expression over time on nFMBs scaffolds, both in growth and myogenic media conditions (Fig. 7 c and 7d). Notably, a synergistic rise in myogenic marker expression was observed in differentiation media conditions on nFMBs, emphasizing the scaffold's ability to enhance the myogenic differentiation process further. Similarly, PCL-mFiBs exhibited a significant expression of myogenic markers in differentiation media compared to 2D control and nFMBs in growth media. However, compared to nFMBs in differentiation, the expression on PCL-mFiBs appeared comparatively lower. Overall, nFMBs consistently showcased a significant increase in myogenic marker expression across all groups and time intervals. Particularly intriguing was the observation that nFMBs exhibited substantial MYF6 and MYOG expression even under growth media conditions compared to 2D control in all conditions and PCL-mFiBs in growth media, indicating that the nano-topographical features of the scaffolds could influence differentiation pathways independent of specific myogenic induction media. This underscores the profound impact of the nano-topography of nFMBs in guiding and stimulating myogenic differentiation pathways in hASCs, potentially offering a more conducive environment for muscle tissue engineering applications compared to conventional scaffolds like PCL-mFiBs. During myogenic differentiation of stem cells, the myogenic regulatory factors (MRFs) are upregulated, starting with early markers like MYOD and MYF5, followed by MYOG and MYF6, which signal the differentiation into myoblasts [68,[74], [75], [76]]. In this study, we evaluated the expression levels of key myogenic markers, including MYOG and MYF6, as these genes play crucial roles in the myogenic differentiation process. MYOG is essential for the terminal differentiation of muscle precursor cells, while MYF6 contributes to the maturation and maintenance of muscle fibers [68,69]. By analyzing the expression of these genes, we aim to confirm the successful induction of myogenesis in hASCs, underscoring the robustness and effectiveness of our differentiation approach.

Immunofluorescence staining for myosin, a key marker for muscle-specific proteins [77], revealed notable differences in expression between the substrates, with robust staining observed on nFMBs compared to the sparser staining on PCL-mFiBs. On nFMBs, higher myosin expression in hASCs was evident by day 21, indicating the initiation of myotube formation. Remarkably, by day 28 and day 35, a substantial number of well-formed myotubes were observed on the nFMBs, as depicted in Fig. 8a–b. Additionally, the presence of numerous cells with two nuclei, characteristic of developing myotubes, further supported the myogenic differentiation observed on the nanofibrillar microbundle substrates. Conversely, the myosin signals on PCL-mFiBs were notably weaker, indicating significantly lower levels of myotube formation compared to the myogenic induction observed on the nFMBs.

To further analyze the structural characteristics of the myotubes, we examined the distribution of myotube length and width (Fig. 8c–d). The data revealed an increase in myotube length from day 21 to day 35, with a higher frequency of longer myotubes on day 35 compared to day 21. On the other hand, the distribution of myotube width showed less variation over time. The width remained relatively consistent, suggesting that while the myotubes elongated along the fibers, the cell-material interactions and alignment with the nanofibers may have constrained changes in width. This indicates that the scaffolds may play a key role in directing myotube elongation without significantly affecting their width, which could be beneficial for achieving well-aligned, functional muscle fibers.

The multipotent nature of hASCs sourced from accessible fat tissue enables their differentiation into diverse cell lineages, including myogenic cells [19]. The co-culture experiments involving hASCs and C2C12 cells have shown the integration of ASCs in myotube formation, suggesting their potential for contributing to muscle tissue regeneration [22]. The extracellular biochemical and biophysical cues play an important role in determining the hASCs differentiation fate [78]. Studies, such as the work by Engler et al., have demonstrated that the stiffness of substrates can induce myogenesis in MSCs [79]. Similarly, nanotopographical cues modulate myogenic outcomes; while both random and aligned nanostructures can influence myogenic programs relative to flat controls, aligned nanotopographies are typically more effective because they promote contact-guided cell elongation and alignment that support myogenic commitment and organized myotube formation. In particular, aligned nanostructures have been shown to increase myogenesis compared with random topographies and to upregulate early myogenic/progenitor markers (e.g., PAX7 and MYF5), consistent with a satellite-cell–like progenitor bias, whereas random topographies generally yield more isotropic cell shapes and comparatively weaker alignment-dependent myogenic maturation (while still modulating specific markers such as PAX7) [80]. ASC myogenesis has also been achieved through a combination of biochemical and biophysical stimulations. For instance, a cocktail comprising 5-azacytidine and horse serum, along with dynamic culture via uniaxial cyclic strain, has led to the formation of multinucleated cells expressing myogenic markers like MyoD and myosin heavy chain [81].

While other studies have successfully differentiated more committed primary muscle cells on scaffolds, our study highlights the robust influence of our nFMBs on a more challenging cell type, hASCs. Unlike muscle-resident stem cells, hASCs typically require extensive biochemical cues to achieve myogenic differentiation. The success of our scaffolds in guiding this process with minimal external cues underscores the profound impact of our muscle-mimetic nano-topography in directing cell behavior. This approach provides a stronger proof of concept and showcases the potential for our technology to be used with a readily available and less-committed stem cell source. Consistently, our nFMB scaffold exhibited highly aligned nanofibrillar arrangements, offering an environment conducive to cell adhesion and influencing cellular morphology. hASCs cultured on nFMBs showed increased expression of intermediate myogenic markers, which likely contributed to the upregulation of mature muscle fiber markers such as desmin and titin [82]. During differentiation, we observed a significant increase in myotube length from day 21 to day 35, along with enhanced myosin staining, indicating ongoing muscle fiber maturation. The expression of myogenin, confirmed by both PCR and immunostaining, increased over this period, highlighting its role in promoting myotube formation and differentiation. Similarly, PCR analysis showed a rise in MYF6 expression [68,69,75,76], further supporting sarcomere development.

To examine how scaffold topology shapes myogenic differentiation, we performed bulk RNA-seq on hASCs cultured under myogenic differentiation conditions in three culture platforms, 2D control, PCL-mFiBs and nFMBs, and the bulk RNA transcriptome indicates marked differences (Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6, Fig. 7, Fig. 8, Fig. 9). In the principal component analysis (Fig. 9a), the nFMBs form a distinct cluster, separated from 2D control and PCL-mFiBs, supporting the idea that the fibrillar architecture induces a unique transcriptional state rather than a minor shift within the same program. Consistent with this, the Venn diagram (Fig. 9b) shows a large, shared core of detected genes across all groups (24,393) while also revealing group-specific sets and pairwise overlaps (for example, 2D-only 1259; PCL-mFiBs-only 2426; nFMBs-only 576), reinforcing that each culture condition produces both common and distinct transcript features. To contextualize what changes are simply attributable to moving from 2D to a fibrous substrate, we first analyzed PCL-mFiBs relative to 2D, which showed pronounced enrichment of microtubule-based motility, cytokinesis, mitotic spindle assembly and organization, and organelle fission, indicating a proliferation-centric profile dominated by cell-cycle and mitotic processes; notably, KEGG analysis in this comparison yielded significant enrichment mainly in ECM-receptor interaction, without direct activation of muscle maturation programs. We then asked whether the nFMBs architecture provides an additional, muscle-specific differentiation advantage beyond generic fibers, and the answer was clear in functional enrichment: GO biological process ORA revealed that 2D versus nFMBs is characterized by strong upregulation of muscle organ development, muscle tissue development, striated and skeletal muscle tissue development, muscle cell differentiation, and stem cell differentiation, while extracellular matrix organization and cell-matrix adhesion terms are inversely regulated, suggesting a transition from an adhesion-dominated state toward a differentiation-focused state under the fibrillar microenvironment. Importantly, PCL-mFiBs versus nFMBs further strengthens muscle contraction, muscle system processes, muscle cell differentiation, and skeletal muscle tissue development while downregulating focal adhesion assembly, indicating that generic fibers like PCL-mFiBs do not, by themselves, engage muscle-specific functional programs that are uniquely promoted by the nFMBs topology. KEGG pathway analysis supports the same interpretation: compared with PCL-mFiBs, nFMBs enriches PI3K-Akt, MAPK, Wnt, Hippo, and TGF-beta signaling together with Focal adhesion, Adherens junction, ECM-receptor interaction, and Cytoskeleton in muscle cells, consistent with a model in which fibrillar physical cues couple adhesion and cytoskeletal remodeling to fate-determining mechanosignaling networks, and these pathway patterns reappear in PCL-mFiBs versus nFMBs, supporting reproducibility of the fibril-specific state. Finally, CNP gene-function networks place the muscle cell differentiation node in nFMBs within an interconnected framework of actin filament organization, actin bundle assembly, extracellular matrix regulation, and integrin-mediated adhesion, incorporating muscle-associated genes such as ACTC1, MYH3, DMD, MEF2A, SIX1, STAC3, and TNNC1, which aligns with coordinated cytoskeletal reprogramming and lineage commitment; in contrast, the PCL-mFiBs network remains centered on chromosome segregation and mitotic regulation, with CDK1, MKI67, and TOP2A as key hubs. Collectively, these transcriptomic and pathway data provide mechanistic support that nFMB topography promotes an alignment-dependent adhesion-cytoskeleton-mechanosignaling axis (focal adhesion/ECM interaction with actin–microtubule remodeling), thereby biasing hASCs toward muscle differentiation and contractile program acquisition beyond what is observed on 2D TCP or generic microfibers. Importantly, this transcriptomic signature aligns with our earlier phenotypic observations of altered focal adhesion organization and aligned ECM deposition on nFMBs (Fig. 6). Notably, enrichment of Hippo signaling together with focal adhesion/ECM–cytoskeleton terms is consistent with involvement of canonical mechanotransduction circuitry frequently linked to integrin-FAK signaling and YAP/TAZ-regulated transcription; while YAP/TAZ localization and phospho-FAK were not directly quantified here, the combined adhesion/ECM phenotypes (Fig. 6) and pathway-level enrichment (Fig. 9) support a topology-driven mechanosignaling framework for the observed myogenic bias.

To evaluate sarcomere formation, titin staining was performed on day 35. The results revealed early signs of sarcomere assembly, although the diffuse titin signal suggests that the muscle fibers were still in the process of maturation. These findings indicate that while myogenic differentiation is advancing, full sarcomere organization and muscle fiber maturation have not yet been achieved (Fig. S9b). While hASCs are more challenging to differentiate into myoblasts compared to induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived myogenic progenitors [83,84], which benefit from well-established and standardized protocols, our study highlights the strong influence of nFMBs topography in promoting myogenic induction. Using hASCs, we demonstrate that the muscle-mimetic nano topography of the nFMB scaffolds provides sufficient biophysical cues to stimulate myogenic differentiation. Our findings reveal significant cellular alignment, multinucleated myotube formation, and the expression of key myogenic markers under general growth media conditions. These results underscore the potential of nanofibrillar microbundles to direct myogenic behavior in challenging cell populations effectively. Similar outcomes in muscle fiber formation have been reported with iPSC-derived myogenic progenitors and engineered microenvironments, where organized topographical and mechanical cues facilitated muscle tissue alignment, maturation, and contractile functionality [85,86]. Despite these promising findings, further research is required to optimize muscle fiber maturation and functional performance. Strategies such as surface modifications, including the incorporation of bioactive cues, may enhance myogenic marker expression and accelerate differentiation timelines. In addition, while our culture period (up to 35 days) demonstrates sustained progression of myogenic markers, this study did not directly assess functional contractility (e.g., force generation), which represents an important next step. Addressing these limitations through dynamic culture conditions, such as electrical or mechanical stimulation, could provide critical insights into muscle fiber contractility and scaffold integration and would advance our understanding of mature, functional muscle tissue development processes. Overall, the structured nature of the nFMBs holds significant promise for guiding and supporting the myogenic differentiation of hASCs, offering potential for applications in muscle tissue engineering and regeneration.

While this work primarily establishes an in vitro proof-of-concept for topography-guided myogenic responses, we recognize that clinical translation requires scalable and reproducible manufacturing, comprehensive biocompatibility testing, and validation in physiological environments. A key strength of MEF is its deterministic, G-code-guided fabrication, which supports reproducible scaffold geometry and enables standardization by using defined printing parameters and post-processing steps. In this study, scaffold quality was supported by morphological and compositional characterization (including confirmation of effective sacrificial polymer removal after washing), providing a practical basis for batch-to-batch control. From a materials perspective, we selected medical-grade PCL to support future translational development, and our in vitro analyses demonstrate sustained viability and robust cell-material interactions over extended culture. Importantly, in vivo performance was not assessed in the current study, which represents a limitation. Future work will evaluate MEF-fabricated scaffolds in relevant muscle injury/VML animal models to assess host immune response, vascularization, degradation behavior, integration with native tissue, and functional outcomes (e.g., force generation and recovery). Such studies will be essential to bridge the present mechanistic in vitro findings toward regenerative and reconstructive applications. Overall, these findings support nFMBs as a programmable, muscle-mimetic fibrillar platform that promotes alignment- and mechanosignaling-associated myogenic programs in hASCs, while highlighting clear next steps toward functional maturation and translational validation.

Conclusion

In this study, we engineered muscle-inspired aligned nanofibrillar microbundles to serve as structural support, guiding the morphology of hASCs and their induction towards the myogenic lineage. Employing MEF, we fabricated meticulously arranged, collagen-like aligned nanofibrillar microbundles of PCL. The nano-topography of these nanofibrillar microbundles enhanced hASC adhesion and alignment and maintained high cell viability throughout a 35-day culture period. Immunostaining assays revealed a high alignment among cells and the secretion of an equally aligned collagen type-1, a key component of the muscle ECM. This led to an increased tendency for myotube formation over time compared to cultures on PCL microfibers. These findings underscore the importance of using tissue-like microarchitectures to drive lineage-specific differentiation, thus highlighting the significant potential of this technology for applications in muscle tissue engineering and regeneration. This approach also holds promise for future studies, particularly in achieving advanced muscle tissue maturation using iPSCs, building on the robust outcomes demonstrated here with hASCs.
