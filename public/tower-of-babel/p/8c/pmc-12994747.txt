Factors Influencing End-of-Life Care Performance Among Community Care Workers Prior to the Implementation of the Integrated Care Support Act: Focusing on Home Care Aides
Authors: Jae Eun Yu, Ju Young Park, Young Gil Jeong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to identify the factors influencing end-of-life care performance among community-based care workers prior to the implementation of the Integrated Care Support Act in South Korea.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 153 community care workers who provided home-based services. Data were collected using structured questionnaires that assessed perceptions of a good death, attitudes toward end-of-life care, end-of-life care stress, and end-of-life care performance. Data analysis included descriptive statistics, independent t tests, Pearson correlation coefficients, and hierarchical multiple regression using SPSS version 29.0.ResultsEnd-of-life care performance demonstrated a significant positive correlation with end-of-life care stress (r=0.43, P<0.001). Hierarchical regression analysis identified end-of-life care stress (β=0.32, P< 0.001), gender (female; β=–0.20, P=0.01), and intention to participate in end-of-life care education (β=–0.20, P=0.01) as significant predictors. The final model explained 25% of the variance in end-of-life care performance.ConclusionEnd-of-life care stress was the strongest predictor of performance among community-based care workers, along with gender and intention to participate in end-of-life care education. These findings suggest that, when appropriately managed and supported, end-of-life care stress may function as a motivating factor rather than solely a burden. Therefore, structured education and emotional support interventions—such as debriefing and peer-based supervision—are recommended. Additionally, the implementation of the Integrated Care Support Act should be accompanied by institutional systems that facilitate effective end-of-life care practices.Keywords: Community health services, Terminal care, Caregivers, Attitude, Psychological stress

INTRODUCTION

1. Background

In 2024, older adults living alone accounted for 37.8% of all older adult households in South Korea, and a substantial proportion of this population was vulnerable in terms of health management and activities of daily living [1]. This demographic shift, combined with the continuing growth of the aging population, indicates the potential emergence of diverse social challenges, including the rising number of older adults living alone and increasing health-related vulnerabilities. In response, the government has promoted community-based care policies designed to integrate and coordinate care services at the municipal level, thereby enabling individuals facing difficulties in daily life to remain in their own homes while maintaining their health and independence [2]. Furthermore, the Act on Integrated Support for Community Care Including Medical and Long-term Care, enacted in March 2024 and scheduled to take effect in March 2026, provides a legal framework for delivering integrated care that encompasses healthcare services. This Act intends to establish a comprehensive support system tailored to individuals’ characteristics and needs, covering the entire continuum of care—from preventive health management to end-of-life care [3].

According to the 2023 National Survey of Older Adults, most older adults in South Korea perceived a good death as one occurring without pain (85.4%), in the presence of family members (76.8%), and at home (53.9%) [4]. However, only 14% of deaths actually occur at home, while 69.9% occur in medical institutions—a proportion substantially higher than the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development average [5,6]. This hospital-centered pattern of death demonstrates a clear discrepancy between older adults’ preferred and actual places of death. Accordingly, under the Integrated Care Support Act, the government seeks to strengthen the role of community care workers in supporting individuals receiving home-based care and to establish a community-based end-of-life care system [3,7]. Previous studies in South Korea have primarily focused on nurses working in long-term care hospitals or general hospitals [8-11], whereas relatively little research has examined community care workers who provide primary care services in home-based settings. As the community-based integrated care system is scheduled for full implementation in March 2026 under the Integrated Care Support Act [3], it is particularly important to identify the factors that influence end-of-life care performance among community care workers, especially home care aides. Among community care workers, this study focuses specifically on home care aides, who maintain the most consistent and direct contact with care recipients and deliver daily care services.

Community-based home care aides are essential frontline providers who assist older adults with activities of daily living and offer both physical and emotional care under the Long-Term Care Insurance system [12]. They play a pivotal role in delivering continuous care in close proximity to care recipients. In home-based settings, they often face situations in which care recipients approach the end of life under conditions of limited resources and inadequate professional support [13,14]. Despite these environmental constraints, home care aides perform a critical role in ensuring a dignified death for care recipients [15,16].

Previous studies have identified perception of a good death as a significant factor influencing end-of-life care performance among nurses working in long-term care hospitals [9]. Similarly, research involving home care aides in community-based elderly welfare facilities found that perceptions of a good death represented a core concept explaining the quality of care services [17]. These findings suggest that the values care providers hold regarding death may substantially influence their end-of-life care performance.

Attitudes toward end-of-life care have also been reported as significant predictors of performance among nurses in long-term care and university hospital settings [8,11]. However, studies involving general hospital nurses have not consistently demonstrated this relationship [10]. Such discrepancies imply that the association between attitudes toward end-of-life care and performance may vary according to the work environment and the specific characteristics of care roles, underscoring the need for empirical verification among community care workers.

End-of-life care stress—defined as the physical and emotional burden experienced while caring for terminally ill patients—has been found to exceed general occupational stress and may contribute to burnout [18-20]. Previous research has shown that end-of-life care stress is significantly associated with end-of-life care performance, with higher stress levels potentially serving as barriers to effective care provision [11,21,22]. In community-based home settings, where professional support in end-of-life situations is often limited, the stress experienced by community care workers may be further intensified [18,19,23]. Although prior studies indicate that perception of a good death, attitudes toward end-of-life care, and end-of-life care stress are interrelated and significantly affect performance, most existing research has been conducted among healthcare professionals working in medical institutions. Consequently, these studies have not adequately reflected the unique characteristics and work contexts of community care workers. Therefore, a comprehensive investigation of the effects of these variables on end-of-life care performance among community care workers is warranted.

Accordingly, this study aimed to explore key factors associated with end-of-life care performance among community-based home care aides who support terminally ill individuals in accepting and preparing for death peacefully. Furthermore, based on the study findings, it sought to provide foundational evidence for developing systematic interventions and educational programs to enhance end-of-life care performance among community care workers, thereby contributing to improving the overall quality of community-based end-of-life care.

2. Purpose

The purpose of this study was to identify the factors influencing end-of-life care performance among community-based home care aides prior to the implementation of the Integrated Care Support Act.

The specific objectives of this study were as follows:

1) To examine differences in study variables according to participants’ general characteristics and end-of-life care–related characteristics.

2) To assess levels of perception of a good death, attitudes toward end-of-life care, end-of-life care stress, and end-of-life care performance among participants.

3) To examine correlations among perception of a good death, attitudes toward end-of-life care, end-of-life care stress, and end-of-life care performance.

4) To identify the factors influencing end-of-life care performance among participants.

METHODS

1. Study design

This descriptive correlational study was conducted to examine the factors influencing end-of-life care performance among community-based home care workers.

2. Participants

Participants were recruited from a home care aide training institute (G Training Institute) and home-based elderly welfare centers in G City, Chungcheongnam-do Province, South Korea. Eligible participants were community care workers with at least six months of work experience in home-based care settings who understood the study purpose and voluntarily agreed to participate. The required sample size was determined using G*Power version 3.1.9.7. Based on a multiple regression analysis with a significance level (α) of 0.05, statistical power (1–β) of 0.80, a medium effect size (f2) of 0.15, and 18 predictors (9 general characteristics, 6 end-of-life care–related characteristics, and 3 major independent variables), the minimum required sample size was 150. To account for an anticipated 10% dropout rate, a total of 165 participants were recruited. Of these, eight were excluded due to insincere responses, and four due to having less than six months of work experience. Consequently, data from 153 participants were included in the final analysis.

3. Instruments

The instruments used in this study included 17 items measuring perception of a good death, 30 items assessing attitudes toward end-of-life care, 40 items evaluating end-of-life care stress, and 22 items measuring end-of-life care performance. Additionally, the questionnaire comprised 9 items on general characteristics and 6 items on end-of-life care–related characteristics, resulting in a total of 124 items.

4. Data collection and ethical considerations

This study was conducted after obtaining approval from the Institutional Review Board of K University (IRB No. 2025-05-033). Data were collected using a paper-based, self-administered questionnaire between July 5 and August 30, 2025, at a home care aide training institute (G Training Institute) and home-based elderly welfare centers in G City, Chungcheongnam-do Province, South Korea. Before data collection, participants received a detailed explanation of the study purpose and procedures, the voluntary nature of participation, their right to decline or withdraw at any time without penalty, and the measures taken to ensure privacy and confidentiality. Written informed consent was obtained from participants who voluntarily agreed to participate prior to questionnaire distribution. Participants completed the questionnaires independently, and the completed forms were sealed and collected directly by the researcher. The data collected were used solely for research purposes, and strict measures were implemented to maintain anonymity and confidentiality. Participants were informed that refusal to participate or withdrawal from the study would involve no disadvantage. They were also informed that, in the event of withdrawal, any collected data would be immediately discarded. The survey was conducted anonymously. All research data, including personal information such as age, gender, and education level, were encrypted and securely stored in a locked location accessible only to the researcher. The collected data will be retained for three years following the completion of the study and then permanently destroyed in accordance with institutional and regulatory guidelines.

5. Statistical analysis

The collected data were analyzed using SPSS/WIN version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Participants’ general and end-of-life care–related characteristics were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Levels of perception of a good death, attitudes toward end-of-life care, end-of-life care stress, and end-of-life care performance were analyzed using means and standard deviations.

The assumption of normality was tested by examining skewness (±2) and kurtosis (±7) [29]; all variables satisfied these criteria. Differences in major variables according to participants’ general and end-of-life care–related characteristics were analyzed using independent t-tests. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to examine relationships among perception of a good death, attitudes toward end-of-life care, end-of-life care stress, and end-of-life care performance. Hierarchical multiple regression analysis was conducted to identify factors influencing end-of-life care performance.

RESULTS

1. Differences in study variables according to general and end-of-life care–related characteristics

A total of 153 participants were included in the analysis. The mean age was 66.22±8.59 years, and the majority were female (n=147, 96.1%). Regarding education level, most participants had a high school education or below (n=133, 87.5%). Direct care workers accounted for 138 participants (90.2%), and most were employed in private institutions (n=132, 86.3%). With respect to the residential area of home care service provision, the majority worked in myeon (township) areas (n=74, 48.4%) and dong (urban neighborhood) areas (n=62, 40.5%), whereas 17 participants (11.1%) worked in eup (rural town) areas. For end-of-life care–related characteristics, the mean score for confidence in end-of-life care was 6.22±2.29 (range=0~10), willingness to provide end-of-life care was 6.10±2.71 (range=0~10), and preparedness for end-of-life care was 3.02±0.98 (range=1~5). Most participants perceived the preferred place of death to be at home (n=145, 94.8%). A total of 142 participants (92.8%) reported a need for end-of-life care education, and 139 participants (90.8%) expressed an intention to apply for end-of-life care education. Attitudes toward end-of-life care were significantly higher among participants with a college-level education or above (3.11±0.19) than among those with a high school education or below (2.88±0.31) (t=–4.24, P<0.001).

End-of-life care performance was significantly higher among female participants (3.31±0.65) than among male participants (2.78±0.52) (t=2.34, P=0.02). In addition, participants who expressed an intention to apply for end-of-life care education (3.31±0.52) demonstrated significantly higher end-of-life care performance than those without such intention (2.98±0.65) (t=2.34, P=0.02). No statistically significant differences were found in perception of a good death or end-of-life care stress according to participants’ general characteristics (Table 1).

2. Levels of perception of a good death, attitudes toward end-of-life care, end-of-life care stress, and end-of-life care performance

The mean score for perception of a good death was 3.38±0.43 out of 4. Among its subdomains, the clinical domain had the highest mean score (3.44±0.48), whereas the personal control domain had the lowest mean score (3.33±0.66). The mean score for attitudes toward end-of-life care was 2.91±0.30 out of 4. Among its subdomains, attitudes toward family members scored 3.04±0.46, while attitudes toward individual patients scored 2.78±0.29. The mean score for end-of-life care stress was 4.08±0.57 out of 5. Among the subdomains, interpersonal conflict with terminally ill patients had the highest mean score (4.25±0.69), followed by conflict related to limitations of medical care (4.22±0.65). The mean score for end-of-life care performance was 3.28±0.53 out of 4. Among its domains, the physical domain had the highest mean score (3.45±0.54), whereas the spiritual domain had the lowest mean score (3.01±0.84) (Table 2).

3. Correlations among perception of a good death, attitudes toward end-of-life care, end-of-life care stress, and end-of-life care performance

Pearson’s correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationships among perception of a good death, attitudes toward end-of-life care, end-of-life care stress, and end-of-life care performance. Perception of a good death showed a significant positive correlation with attitudes toward end-of-life care (r=0.36, P<0.001) and end-of-life care stress (r=0.42, P<0.001). In addition, attitudes toward end-of-life care were significantly positively correlated with end-of-life care stress (r=0.29, P<0.001). End-of-life care performance demonstrated significant positive correlations with perception of a good death (r=0.32, P<0.001), attitudes toward end-of-life care (r=0.30, P<0.001), and end-of-life care stress (r=0.43, P<0.001) (Table 3).

4. Factors influencing end-of-life care performance

Hierarchical multiple regression analysis was performed to identify factors influencing end-of-life care performance. Prior to the regression analysis, multicollinearity and model fit were assessed. The tolerance values ranged from 0.76 to 0.99 (all >0.10), and the variance inflation factor (VIF) values ranged from 1.00 to 1.31 (all <10), indicating no multicollinearity. The Durbin–Watson statistic was 2.03, which is close to 2, confirming independence of residuals and supporting the adequacy of the regression model.

In Model 1, gender and intention to apply for end-of-life care education, which were statistically significant in the univariate analysis, were entered. Male gender (β=–0.20, P=0.01) and absence of intention to apply for end-of-life care education (β=–0.20, P=0.01) were identified as significant predictors of end-of-life care performance. The explanatory power of Model 1 was 6% (F=5.92, P<0.001). In Model 2, perception of a good death, attitudes toward end-of-life care, and end-of-life care stress were additionally entered. End-of-life care stress emerged as a significant predictor of end-of-life care performance (β=0.32, P<0.001). The explanatory power of the overall model increased to 25% (F=11.14, P<0.001) (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

This study is significant because it comprehensively examined the characteristics and determinants of end-of-life care performance among home care aides working in community-based home care settings prior to the implementation of the Integrated Care Support Act. Unlike previous research that primarily focused on healthcare professionals in institutional medical environments, this study investigated end-of-life care performance within the distinctive context of home-based care. It conceptualized end-of-life care performance as a multifaceted construct that encompasses individual perceptions, attitudes, emotional burden, and educational preparedness. This perspective underscores that end-of-life care performance in home settings is not simply a technical function but rather a complex practice shaped by the dynamic interaction among care workers’ internal perceptions, emotional competence, and institutional support.

The finding that female care workers exhibited significantly higher end-of-life care performance than male care workers suggests that the gendered nature of care labor continues to exert an influence. Prior studies indicate that female care workers often perceive emotional labor and affective support as central components of end-of-life care, which may contribute to more responsive interactions with patients and their families [14]. However, these differences should not be interpreted as inherent disparities in competence. Instead, they likely reflect variations in socially constructed caregiving roles, accumulated experience, and access to educational opportunities. Consequently, ongoing efforts should aim to foster a gender-neutral caregiving culture and ensure equitable training opportunities that enhance end-of-life care competencies regardless of gender.

Participants who expressed an intention to participate in end-of-life care education demonstrated significantly higher levels of end-of-life care performance. This finding implies that education functions not merely as a form of knowledge transmission but also as a psychological and motivational resource that enhances attitudes and self-efficacy in care provision [11]. Therefore, the establishment of a standardized end-of-life care education system within the home care sector is warranted. Such a system should enable community care workers to perceive end-of-life situations not as sources of anxiety but as integral aspects of professional caregiving practice.

The overall mean score for end-of-life care performance was relatively high (3.28 out of 4). This finding may reflect the characteristics of home-based care, in which home care aides maintain continuous relationships with care recipients and routinely provide both physical assistance and emotional support [13,14]. Notably, the physical and psychological domains demonstrated relatively higher scores, consistent with the nature of home-based care that emphasizes daily living support and interpersonal interaction [12,17]. In contrast, the spiritual domain exhibited comparatively lower scores, which may indicate an insufficient systematization of discussions and education regarding death and spirituality in home care settings [13,14]. Kim [15] emphasized that meaning in life and human dignity are central components of a good death. From this perspective, strengthening spiritual care should be considered an essential component of quality improvement within the integrated care system. Therefore, spiritual care should be systematically incorporated into home-based care services as a formal domain of practice.

Perception of a good death and attitudes toward end-of-life care were positively correlated with end-of-life care performance. These findings are consistent with prior studies conducted among long-term care providers and nurses [9,11], suggesting that more positive and accepting attitudes toward death are associated with care practices that preserve patients’ dignity at the end of life. Perception of a good death may serve as a cognitive framework that enables care workers to view death not merely as the cessation of life but as a meaningful conclusion of human existence [17]. Considering that caregiving entails both emotional labor and ethical practice, understanding and respecting the meaning of death may be more fundamental to care quality than technical competence alone.

However, the perception of a good death did not emerge as a direct predictor of end-of-life care performance in the regression model. This finding suggests that individual-level awareness alone may be insufficient to translate into actual care practices without supportive organizational and structural environments. Previous studies have reported that structural constraints—such as workforce shortages, unclear role boundaries, and administrative limitations—can restrict consistent end-of-life care practices in long-term and integrated care settings [11,30]. Thus, structural conditions may limit practical implementation regardless of individual perception levels.

End-of-life care stress was identified as a significant predictor of end-of-life care performance. This finding contrasts with some previous studies that reported stress as a negative factor impairing performance [18,22]. This discrepancy may be interpreted through Lazarus and Folkman’s stress–coping theory [28], which posits that the impact of stress depends on how individuals cognitively appraise and respond to it. When stress is perceived as a challenge rather than a threat, it may facilitate proactive coping and improved performance. In home-based settings, where care is delivered within close relationships with patients and families, care workers may interpret end-of-life situations as extensions of ongoing care, thereby transforming stress into purposeful action [14,23]. Accordingly, stress management interventions for home care aides should consider not only stress levels but also cognitive appraisals and coping resources. Nevertheless, excessive or prolonged stress may still lead to burnout and decreased performance. Therefore, educational and training programs should assist care workers in meaningfully interpreting the emotional burdens associated with end-of-life care [28]. Regular debriefing programs, peer-based supervision systems, and emotional labor management training are recommended. These strategies may help operationalize the philosophy of the Integrated Care Support Act, particularly its principle of “care for caregivers.”

The regression model explained 25% of the variance in end-of-life care performance. This result indicates that psychological factors—including perception, attitudes, and stress—play meaningful roles; however, end-of-life care performance remains a multidimensional construct shaped by diverse individual and environmental influences [11,30]. Previous studies have also highlighted the influence of work environment, role scope, caregiving experience, education level, and organizational support [11,30]. Future research should incorporate expanded models that include workplace characteristics, organizational culture, team-based collaboration, and educational experiences to more comprehensively explain end-of-life care performance.

This study is significant in that it empirically identified the factors influencing end-of-life care performance among home care aides working in community-based home care settings prior to the implementation of the Integrated Care Support Act. The findings indicate that end-of-life care performance in home-based settings is closely associated with care workers’ perception of a good death, attitudes toward end-of-life care, end-of-life care–related stress, and educational experience concerning end-of-life care. These results suggest that efforts to enhance end-of-life care performance among community care workers should address not only the educational content but also the emotional burden experienced in end-of-life situations. In particular, end-of-life care education should move beyond one-time training sessions and adopt staged, case-based approaches. Furthermore, supportive strategies—such as structured debriefing programs and peer-based supervision following end-of-life experiences—may improve end-of-life care performance by helping care workers manage emotional stress effectively.

However, this study has several limitations. First, participants were recruited from a single region, which limits the generalizability of the findings to the national level. Second, reliance on self-administered questionnaires may have introduced social desirability bias. Third, the internal consistency reliability of attitudes toward end-of-life care was slightly lower than that reported in previous studies [24,25], possibly reflecting variations in end-of-life experiences and role scope between home care aides and healthcare professionals working in medical institutions. Finally, because this was a cross-sectional quantitative study, causal relationships among variables could not be conclusively established. Future research should employ qualitative methods or longitudinal designs to explore changes in end-of-life care performance and its determinants following the implementation of the Integrated Care Support Act.
