Malignant carotid body paraganglioma with nodal metastasis: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Seyedhamid Madani, Donya Rahmati, Payam Nikjo, Seyedehmahsa Madani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCarotid body tumors are rare neuroendocrine tumors that typically present as benign masses. However, they can exhibit malignant transformation, especially when metastasis occurs, making them a significant challenge in diagnosis and management. This case report highlights the paradoxical nature of malignant carotid body tumors, where benign histological features can coexist with confirmed metastatic spread, emphasizing the critical need for careful evaluation and long-term follow-up.Case presentationWe report the case of a 67-year-old Iranian woman who presented with a right-sided neck mass and vague, dull pain for 1 year. Imaging studies, including Doppler ultrasound, computed tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging, revealed a hypervascular mass at the right carotid bifurcation with the classic “salt-and-pepper” appearance. Surgical resection with selective neck lymphadenectomy was performed. Histopathological examination showed the classic Zellballen pattern with low mitotic activity. Immunohistochemistry confirmed the neuroendocrine origin, with strong positivity for chromogranin A, synaptophysin, and S100, and a low Ki-67 index. Importantly, one out of ten resected lymph nodes showed metastatic involvement, confirming the diagnosis of a malignant carotid body tumor.ConclusionThis case underscores that malignancy in carotid body tumors is defined by the presence of metastases rather than histological appearance alone. It highlights the importance of maintaining high clinical suspicion, utilizing comprehensive diagnostic evaluations including imaging and thorough lymph node assessment, and implementing long-term follow-up for patients with carotid body tumors, even when initial histological findings seem benign. Such a multidisciplinary approach is crucial for improving treatment strategies and patient outcomes for these rare and unpredictable tumors.

Introduction

Carotid body tumors (CBTs) are rare neoplasms that arise from the paraganglionic tissue at the bifurcation of the carotid artery [1]. These tumors are usually benign, but malignant transformations are possible, especially when there is evidence of metastatic spread. Most CBTs are asymptomatic and typically present as a neck mass, which may or may not be associated with symptoms [2]. Management of these tumors involves surgical resection, and long-term follow-up is necessary in cases where malignancy is detected [3].

These tumors are of significant importance owing to their unique characteristics in diagnosis and treatment [4]. Previous studies and reports indicate that while carotid body tumors are rare, they can present challenging diagnostic scenarios for clinicians [5]. In cases where early diagnosis and appropriate treatment are not provided, serious risks to the patient can arise. Therefore, accurate assessment and continuous monitoring are crucial for patients with these tumors [6].

The reporting of this particular case is important as it provides an opportunity to explore the thorough evaluation and management of a rare carotid body tumor, which could contribute to increased awareness and improved treatment strategies for these uncommon tumors.

Case presentation

A 67-year-old Iranian woman presented with a 1-year history of a right-sided neck mass accompanied by vague, dull pain. She denied symptoms such as hoarseness, dysphagia, or constitutional complaints. Her medical history included well-controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus and hypertension, and a prior cholecystectomy. She was on metformin and glibenclamide. There was no history of familial paraganglioma syndromes.

On examination, a firm, non-tender mass was palpated in the right neck, approximately 4 cm in diameter. Vital signs were stable, and there was no evidence of systemic illness.

Imaging studies, including Doppler ultrasound, contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), showed a hypervascular, well-circumscribed mass at the right carotid bifurcation. The MRI demonstrated the classic “salt-and-pepper” appearance, suggesting a paraganglioma. Surgical resection with selective neck lymphadenectomy was performed owing to suspected nodal involvement.

Macroscopic findings: The tumor was a 4 cm firm mass with a well-defined border. Its appearance was typical of a paraganglioma, with a reddish-brown color and a smooth surface. The tumor was excised with clear margins, and the surrounding tissue appeared unaffected by the neoplasm.

Microscopic findings: Histopathological examination revealed a classic Zellballen arrangement of tumor cells, which is characteristic of paragangliomas. The tumor cells were arranged in nests or alveolar patterns with a prominent vascular network (Figs. 1, 2). The cells exhibited mild nuclear pleomorphism, and mitotic activity was low, with only one to two mitoses per ten high-power fields (HPF). Discussion

Carotid body tumors (CBTs) are uncommon neuroendocrine neoplasms originating from paraganglionic tissue located at the bifurcation of the common carotid artery [7]. Despite their generally indolent course, these tumors can occasionally exhibit aggressive behavior, most notably in the form of metastatic spread. Malignancy in CBTs is not defined by cytological atypia or local invasiveness, but strictly by the presence of metastases, typically to regional lymph nodes, bone, liver, or lungs [8, 9]. The current case contributes valuable insight into the unpredictable biological nature of CBTs, presenting a paradoxical scenario where histologically bland morphology is accompanied by confirmed lymph node metastasis—raising critical implications for both diagnosis and clinical management.

Although CBTs typically manifest as painless neck masses, the clinical presentation can be subtle and nonspecific, leading to diagnostic delays [10]. In the present case, the patient’s only complaint was vague, dull neck pain over a year-long course, with no cranial nerve involvement or compressive symptoms. This emphasizes the importance of clinical vigilance in evaluating lateral neck masses, particularly in older individuals, even in the absence of alarming symptoms.

The classic radiological findings observed in this case were instrumental in guiding diagnosis. The hypervascular mass at the carotid bifurcation demonstrating splaying of the internal and external carotid arteries—the so-called “lyre sign”—in conjunction with the salt-and-pepper appearance on MRI, is virtually pathognomonic for paragangliomas. These radiological hallmarks aid in distinguishing CBTs from other differential diagnoses such as schwannomas, branchial cleft cysts, or metastatic lymphadenopathy [11].

Histologically, the tumor exhibited hallmark features of a paraganglioma, including the Zellballen architecture—nests of uniform chief cells surrounded by delicate vascular septa and sustentacular cells [12]. The low mitotic rate and lack of necrosis might traditionally suggest a benign course; however, one of the ten excised lymph nodes demonstrated metastatic involvement, shifting the diagnostic categorization to malignant CBT. This case starkly illustrates that histological appearance alone is insufficient for prognostication and should never replace thorough lymph node assessment and long-term follow-up.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) profiling was integral in confirming the diagnosis and excluding other mimickers. Diffuse positivity for neuroendocrine markers chromogranin A and synaptophysin confirmed the neuroendocrine nature of the neoplasm. S100 protein positivity in sustentacular cells reinforced the diagnosis of paraganglioma, and the low Ki-67 proliferation index (< 2%) might suggest indolence; however, as this case demonstrates, Ki-67 alone is not a reliable predictor of metastatic potential. GATA3 expression, though not traditionally used in the diagnosis of CBTs, was noted in this case and may suggest broader expression profiles in extra-adrenal paragangliomas. The absence of staining for cytokeratin and HMB-45 effectively ruled out epithelial and melanocytic neoplasms.

From a genetic standpoint, increasing evidence supports the role of hereditary mutations in the pathogenesis and clinical behavior of paragangliomas [13]. Mutations in the succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) gene complex—particularly SDHB—have been closely linked to aggressive tumor behavior, multifocality, and recurrence [14]. SDHB mutations impair mitochondrial complex II function and lead to accumulation of succinate, which stabilizes HIF-1α and promotes pseudo-hypoxic signaling pathways conducive to tumorigenesis [15]. SDHB-related tumors are notably more prone to malignancy compared with mutations in other SDH subunits (for example, SDHD and SDHC) [16]. Although genetic testing was not performed in the present case owing to resource limitations, the potential presence of an SDHB mutation cannot be excluded, especially considering the confirmed nodal metastasis. Guidelines increasingly recommend SDH genetic testing in all patients with paragangliomas, regardless of family history, owing to the possibility of de novo mutations and the implications for familial screening [17].

In reviewing the literature, malignant CBTs remain rare, comprising approximately 6–10% of all cases [18]. A comprehensive review by T Su that analyzed CBTs found that while histological markers such as the mitotic index or necrosis lacked predictive value, the presence of SDHB mutations correlated strongly with poor prognosis [19, 20]. Similarly, recent studies underscore the potential for integrating molecular diagnostics into routine clinical practice to better risk-stratify patients [21].

Treatment for CBTs remains primarily surgical [22]. In this case, the patient underwent complete surgical resection with selective lymphadenectomy. This approach aligns with current best practices, particularly when nodal involvement is suspected or intraoperatively identified. Although there is no established role for adjuvant radiotherapy or chemotherapy in the absence of unresectable disease or distant metastasis [23], patients with malignant CBTs require prolonged surveillance owing to the risk of recurrence or delayed metastasis [24]. The utility of serial imaging and biochemical markers (for example, plasma metanephrines and chromogranin A) remains under investigation but may aid in early detection of recurrence [25].

Ultimately, this case illustrates how reliance on histologic or proliferative markers alone may be misleading in predicting the clinical trajectory of CBTs. Multidisciplinary evaluation—including careful imaging, comprehensive histopathological analysis, and genetic consideration—is essential for accurate diagnosis and risk assessment. Given the malignant potential observed despite bland histologic features, extended follow-up and heightened clinical vigilance are imperative to optimize patient outcomes.

Conclusion

Paragangliomas of the carotid body are rare neuroendocrine tumors that usually exhibit indolent clinical behaviour [26]. However, this case emphasizes that histologically bland-appearing tumors may still carry malignant potential, particularly when lymph node metastasis is present—as seen in our 67-year-old patient. The diagnosis was established through a combination of imaging findings (including the classic “lyre sign” and “salt-and-pepper” appearance), characteristic histopathology (Zellballen pattern), and supportive immunohistochemical markers.

This case underlines the necessity for high clinical suspicion, even in the absence of overt atypia, and demonstrates the value of comprehensive diagnostic evaluation. Surgical resection remains the mainstay of treatment, with lymphadenectomy indicated when nodal involvement is suspected.

Given the unpredictable nature of CBTs, long-term follow-up is essential. Moreover, this case suggests the need for future clinical trials focused on the prognostic utility of biomarkers such as SDHB mutation status and Ki-67 index, which may help stratify patients based on their risk of recurrence or metastasis. Such studies could pave the way for personalized surveillance strategies and potential adjuvant therapies in malignant paragangliomas.

This case not only contributes to the limited literature on malignant CBTs but also provides valuable insights for clinicians and researchers, reinforcing the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in managing these uncommon yet clinically significant tumors.
