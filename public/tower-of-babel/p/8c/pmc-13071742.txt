Baseline bicarbonate level is associated with apnea of prematurity in preterm neonates with neonatal jaundice: insights from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III (MIMIC-III) database
Authors: Dachun Wang, Weixiao Hu, Donglian Xiong, Jin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundApnea of prematurity (AOP) and neonatal jaundice are among the most common diagnoses in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). This study aims to evaluate the association between baseline serum bicarbonate levels and the risk of AOP in preterm neonates with neonatal jaundice.MethodsUsing data from the MIMIC-III database (2001–2012), we identified 2,170 preterm neonates diagnosed with neonatal jaundice. Baseline bicarbonate was defined as the initial serum level measured within 24 hours of admission and categorized into quartiles (Q1–Q4). Multivariate logistic regression, mediation analysis, propensity score matching (PSM), subgroup analyses, and restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis were performed.ResultsBaseline bicarbonate levels showed a significant inverse association with AOP risk when analyzed as a continuous variable {Model 1: odds ratio (OR) =0.964 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.937–0.992], P=0.01; Model 2: OR =0.919 (95% CI: 0.895–0.944), P<0.001; Model 3: OR =0.912 (95% CI: 0.888–0.936), P<0.001}. When bicarbonate levels were divided into quartiles, individuals with baseline levels in the Q2 range exhibited the lowest risk of AOP [Model 1: OR =0.545 (95% CI: 0.423–0.702), P<0.001; Model 2: OR =0.383 (95% CI: 0.300–0.488), P<0.001; Model 3: OR =0.363 (95% CI: 0.285–0.461), P<0.001; Model 4: OR =0.758 (95% CI: 0.579–0.992), P=0.044]. Further evidence from mediation analysis and subgroup analysis following PSM consistently supported the mediating role of caffeine treatment (indirect effect: β=−0.059, P<0.001; direct effect: β=0.048, P=0.97). RCS analysis revealed a non-linear relationship between bicarbonate levels and AOP risk (P for nonlinear <0.05).ConclusionsThis study found that lower baseline bicarbonate levels were linked to higher AOP risk in preterm neonates with jaundice. Caffeine treatment fully mediated this nonlinear association, and those with bicarbonate levels in Q2 (16–19 mEq/L) had the lowest AOP risk.Keywords: Apnea of prematurity (AOP), neonatal jaundice, bicarbonate, caffeine treatment, Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III (MIMIC-III)

Highlight box

Key findings

• Baseline serum bicarbonate levels are significantly and inversely associated with the risk of apnea of prematurity (AOP) in preterm neonates with neonatal jaundice.

• When bicarbonate levels were divided into quartiles, individuals with baseline levels in the Q2 range exhibited the lowest risk of AOP.

• The association between bicarbonate levels and AOP is significantly mediated by caffeine treatment.

What is known, and what is new?

• It is known that AOP is a common and significant clinical problem in preterm neonates managed in the neonatal intensive care unit.

• This study newly identifies baseline bicarbonate as a potential marker for AOP risk specifically in jaundiced preterm neonates, revealing a complex, non-linear association. Furthermore, it uncovers the novel insight that caffeine treatment acts as a mediator, partially explaining the mechanism behind this association.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Baseline bicarbonate levels could be used as an early, non-invasive clinical marker to stratify AOP risk in this vulnerable population.

• Clinicians should be aware that both low and high bicarbonate levels may be associated with increased AOP risk.

• Further prospective research is now warranted to validate these findings and to investigate whether interventions targeting bicarbonate levels could modulate AOP risk.

Introduction

Preterm neonates are defined as those born before 37 weeks of gestation (1). Compared with term newborns, they exhibit physiological and metabolic immaturity, which contributes to higher rates of morbidity and mortality (2,3). Apnea of prematurity (AOP), defined as a cessation of breathing lasting more than 20 seconds or 15 seconds if accompanied by hypoxia or bradycardia, is one of the most common diagnoses in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) (4,5). AOP is generally in an inverse relationship with gestational age (GA) (6). Nearly all infants born at or before 28 weeks of GA experience at least one occurrence of AOP, whereas approximately 20% of those born at 34 weeks GA are affected by this condition, and beyond 40 weeks GA, 98% of preterm babies no longer experience AOP (7-9). AOP reflects the immature regulation of cardiorespiratory control and can lead to hypoxemia and hypercapnia, thereby increasing the risk of brain injury and neurodevelopmental disorders (10).

Neonatal jaundice is also highly prevalent among preterm neonates, affecting approximately 80% of this population (11). It results from the accumulation of bilirubin in the skin and mucous membranes, and excessive bilirubin levels can induce neurotoxic effects (12). Due to the underdeveloped central nervous system and the presence of concurrent clinical complications, preterm neonates are particularly vulnerable to bilirubin-induced toxicity, which may be further exacerbated by these comorbidities (13). Acute bilirubin encephalopathy in preterm neonates is often characterized by recurrent episodes of symptomatic apnea (14), suggesting that jaundiced preterm neonates may have an increased susceptibility to AOP.

Preterm neonates admitted to the NICU frequently present with multiple comorbidities, such as sepsis (15). Although continuous respiratory monitoring is available in the NICU, clinical attention is often diverted by numerous concurrent alarms and clinical demands (10). Therefore, early identification of preterm neonates at high risk for AOP is crucial for the rational allocation of NICU resources and timely preventive interventions.

Respiratory control is regulated by the central respiratory center, and an increase in hydrogen ion ([H+]) concentration in body fluids stimulates central chemoreceptors, leading to enhanced respiratory drive (16). Bicarbonate is a key component of the body’s buffering systems, working alongside protein and phosphate buffers to maintain acid-base homeostasis (17). Low bicarbonate levels indicate metabolic acidosis, which theoretically stimulates the respiratory center and may enhance respiratory drive, potentially reducing the risk of AOP. However, due to the developmental immaturity of the central nervous system in preterm neonates, the relationship between bicarbonate levels and AOP risk remains unclear in this vulnerable population, indicating a gap in current research.

Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the association between baseline serum bicarbonate levels and the risk of AOP in preterm neonates with neonatal jaundice. We hypothesize that lower bicarbonate levels may be associated with an increased risk of AOP in this specific patient group. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2025-1-863/rc).

Methods

Data source and study participants

The data for this study were obtained from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III (MIMIC-III) database, a comprehensive and publicly available resource containing de-identified health information from over 40,000 patients, including 7,870 neonates treated at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2001 and 2012. This dataset includes demographic characteristics, vital signs, medication records, laboratory test results, length of hospital stay, and diagnoses according to the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revisions (ICD-9). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. All patient identifiers were removed in the data used for this study; therefore, ethical approval by an institutional review board was not required.

A total of 7,870 neonates from the MIMIC-III database were selected for this retrospective cohort study. Exclusion criteria: (I) absence of a diagnosed neonatal jaundice; (II) length of stay in the NICU less than 24 hours; (III) neonatal jaundice not associated with preterm delivery. Finally, 2,170 preterm neonates diagnosed with neonatal jaundice were included in the final analysis. The detailed patient selection process is illustrated in Figure 1.

Exposure and Clinical outcome

The primary exposure variable in this study was the initial bicarbonate levels measured within 24 hours of neonatal admission to the NICU. Bicarbonate levels were categorized into four groups based on quartiles: quartile 1 (<16 mEq/L), quartile 2 (16–19 mEq/L), quartile 3 (20–21 mEq/L), and quartile 4 (≥22 mEq/L). The primary endpoint was the occurrence of AOP in neonatal jaundice patients during hospitalization. AOP and neonatal jaundice were diagnosed according to the ICD codes (18,19).

Data collection

Moreover, we collected the following data: (I) basic demographic information, including age, gender, race, height, birth weight, and GA; (II) comorbidities, including acute kidney injury (AKI), acidosis, anemia, pneumonia, intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), congenital malformation, and sepsis; (III) treatment, including phototherapy, acetaminophen, vancomycin, dopamine, and mechanical ventilation; (IV) laboratory indicators, including hematocrit, lymphocytes, neutrophils, eosinophils, red blood cell (RBC), red blood cell distribution width (RDW), pH, partial pressure of oxygen (PO2), partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PCO2), total CO2, urine output, platelets, white blood cell (WBC), anion gap (AG), hemoglobin, base excess, bilirubin, potassium, sodium, and chloride. All data were collected within 24 h following admission to the NICU, and only the initial measurements were analyzed.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are summarized using median and interquartile range (IQR), while categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages (%). Group differences for continuous measures were evaluated with the Mann-Whitney U test, and categorical comparisons employed the Chi-squared test. SPSS (version 26.0) and R (version 4.2.1) were utilized for all statistical analyses. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value less than 0.05 throughout the study.

Variables with over 50% missing data were excluded, while those with less than 50% missingness underwent median imputation (20). Variables showing significant baseline inter-group differences underwent collinearity analysis. To mitigate multicollinearity, variables with a variance inflation factor (VIF) greater than 3 were excluded. The remaining variables were retained as covariates for subsequent model construction. Distinct multivariate logistic regression models were established to investigate the association between the baseline bicarbonate levels and the risk of AOP, as well as to identify potential effect modifiers or mediators. Model 1 was adjusted for demographic characteristics, including gender, birth weight, and GA; Model 2 was adjusted for comorbidities, including congenital malformation, anemia, sepsis, acidosis, and IVH; Model 3 adjusted for laboratory indicators, including hemoglobin, bilirubin, neutrophils, WBC, RDW, and chloride; Model 4 adjusted for treatment, including phototherapy, mechanical ventilation, and caffeine. Results were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). Additionally, mediation analyses were performed to assess the potential mediating effects of the identified modifier. Prior to conducting subgroup analyses stratified by the identified effect modifier to evaluate potential confounding effects, we first performed propensity score matching (PSM) analyses between the two subgroups to balance baseline differences and enhance comparability. Finally, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses were employed to explore potential nonlinear relationships in both the overall population and subgroups.

Results

Descriptive results

A total of 2170 preterm neonates with jaundice were included, of whom 988 developed AOP and 1182 did not (Table 1). The median age was 13 hours, and 51.4% were girls. Preterm neonates diagnosed with AOP had significantly lower bicarbonate levels compared to those without AOP (P<0.001). The AOP group exhibited lower birth weight, a higher proportion of boys, and a greater proportion of GA < 28 weeks. Regarding comorbidities, the AOP group was more likely to have acidosis, anemia, IVH, congenital malformations, and sepsis. Furthermore, preterm neonates with AOP were more frequently treated with phototherapy, caffeine, and mechanical ventilation. In terms of laboratory parameters, the AOP group had lower levels of RDW, hemoglobin, bilirubin, hematocrit, neutrophils, RBC, and WBC, as well as higher serum sodium and chloride levels (all P<0.05). No significant differences were observed between the AOP and non-AOP groups in several baseline characteristics, including age, height, AKI, pneumonia, acetaminophen use, vancomycin use, dopamine use, lymphocyte count, eosinophil count, pH, PO2, PCO2, total CO2, urine output, platelet count, AG, base excess, and potassium levels (all P>0.05).

Associations between baseline bicarbonate levels and the risk of AOP in preterm neonates with jaundice

Four multivariate logistic regression models were constructed to examine the association between bicarbonate levels and AOP (Figure 2). Model 1 adjusted for demographic characteristics, Model 2 for comorbidities, and Model 3 for laboratory indicators. In these models, bicarbonate levels, when treated as a continuous variable (Figure 2A), were significantly associated with a reduced risk of AOP among preterm neonates with jaundice [Model 1: OR =0.964 (95% CI: 0.937–0.992), P=0.01; Model 2: OR =0.919 (95% CI: 0.895–0.944), P<0.001; Model 3: OR =0.912 (95% CI: 0.888–0.936), P<0.001]. However, after adjusting for treatment in Model 4, the association was no longer significant (OR =1.004, 95% CI: 0.975–1.033, P=0.80). When bicarbonate levels were categorized into quartiles (Figure 2B) with quartile 1 as the reference group, quartile 2 showed a significant inverse association with AOP risk across all models, exhibiting the lowest ORs compared to other quartiles [Model 1: OR =0.545 (95% CI: 0.423–0.702), P<0.001; Model 2: OR =0.383 (95% CI: 0.300–0.488), P<0.001; Model 3: OR =0.363 (95% CI: 0.285–0.461), P<0.001; Model 4: OR =0.758 (95% CI: 0.579–0.992), P=0.044].

Given that the association was no longer significant after adjusting for treatment when bicarbonate was analyzed as a continuous variable, we conducted a leave-one-out analysis based on Model 4 to identify a potential confounding factor (Figure 2C). After excluding caffeine treatment, the significant association between bicarbonate levels and AOP was reinstated (OR =0.919, 95% CI: 0.896–0.943, P<0.001). In contrast, when the other two treatment factors were excluded individually, no significant association between bicarbonate levels and AOP remained [phototherapy: OR =1.002 (95% CI: 0.974–1.032), P=0.87; mechanical ventilation: OR =1.003 (95% CI: 0.974–1.032), P=0.86]. Furthermore, the multivariate logistic regression analysis (Table S2) revealed a positive association between caffeine treatment and the risk of AOP. These findings suggest that caffeine treatment may be a potential effect modifier or mediator.

Mediation analysis

To investigate the mediating effect of caffeine treatment, we established a mediation model of “bicarbonate levels -> caffeine treatment -> AOP”. Figure 3A,3B showed the results of the mediation analysis unadjusted and adjusted for all covariates, respectively. Caffeine treatment fully mediated the association between bicarbonate and AOP [indirect effect (IE): β=−0.161, P<0.001; direct effect (DE): β=0.134, P=0.93]. Notably, this significant full mediation persisted even after adjusting for potential confounding variables (IE: β=−0.059, P<0.001; DE: β=0.048, P=0.97). In summary, the results of mediation analysis supported that caffeine treatment acted as a mediator in the relationship between bicarbonate levels and the risk of AOP.

Subgroup analyses

Due to the vital role of caffeine treatment in AOP risk, we further performed PSM and subgroup analysis to examine the association between bicarbonate levels and AOP across caffeine treatment groups. First, a 1:1 PSM analysis was conducted to minimize baseline differences between the caffeine and non-caffeine groups and enhance comparability. The following variables associated with both caffeine treatment and AOP were adjusted: phototherapy, WBC, RDW, neutrophil, birth weight, and anemia. Baseline characteristics grouped by caffeine treatment status were summarized in Table S3 (before PSM) and Table S4 (after PSM).

Subsequent subgroup analyses (Table 3) revealed that when bicarbonate was analyzed as a continuous variable, no significant association with AOP was observed in either the caffeine or non-caffeine group (all P>0.05). When bicarbonate was analyzed as a categorical variable, a significant association with AOP risk was detected in the non-caffeine group, with quartile 2 showing the lowest OR [0.609 (95% CI: 0.399–0.928)], consistent with prior findings. This pattern suggests that there may be a non-linear relationship between bicarbonate levels and the risk of AOP.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that baseline bicarbonate levels were significantly inversely associated with the risk of AOP among preterm neonates with jaundice during NICU hospitalization. However, this association was no longer significant after adjusting for caffeine treatment. Further evidence from mediation analysis and subgroup analysis following PSM consistently supported the mediating role of caffeine treatment. RCS analysis revealed a non-linear relationship between bicarbonate levels and AOP risk. Among preterm neonates with jaundice, those with baseline bicarbonate levels in the Q2 range (16–19 mEq/L) exhibited the lowest risk of AOP in all adjusted models.

This study found that when bicarbonate levels were analyzed as a continuous variable, the association between baseline bicarbonate levels and AOP became non-significant after adjusting for caffeine treatment. Subsequent mediation analysis confirmed that caffeine treatment fully mediated this relationship. As illustrated in Figure 3, the proposed pathway is as follows: “low bicarbonate levels → prompts physicians to initiate caffeine treatment → observed increase in AOP risk”. However, clinical experience and existing literature indicate that caffeine therapy is a well-established intervention for the prevention and treatment of AOP in preterm infants (22,23), which contradicts the mediation model’s implication that caffeine treatment leads to increased AOP risk. This discrepancy represents a classic case of indication confounding, wherein the treatment is preferentially administered to the highest-risk population (24,25). Clinicians are more likely to prescribe caffeine therapy to preterm infants who already exhibit signs of elevated AOP risk (26), and low bicarbonate levels may serve as one such clinical risk marker.

This study also found that preterm neonates with baseline bicarbonate levels in the Q2 range (P25–P50: 16–19 mEq/L) had the lowest risk of AOP. Moreover, this significantly lower risk of AOP (Q1 as reference) persisted in all the adjusted models. The RCS analysis indicated a non-linear association between bicarbonate and AOP. Low bicarbonate levels are a manifestation of metabolic acidosis, suggesting a pathological state in the body (27). Acidosis may affect the sensitivity of the respiratory center, and metabolic acidosis may stimulate the respiratory center to increase the respiratory rate to expel CO2 (28,29). However, if acidosis is severe or accompanied by other complications (such as hyperbilirubinemia), it may induce neurotoxicity, suppress the respiratory center, and increase the risk of AOP (14,30). This well explains why preterm neonates with moderately low bicarbonate levels (i.e., mild to moderate acidosis) have the lowest risk of AOP.

In the subgroup analysis, we found that in the caffeine-treated group, bicarbonate showed no significant association with AOP, regardless of whether it was analyzed as a continuous or categorical variable. This result further confirmed the mediation result that “the association between bicarbonate levels and AOP is fully mediated by caffeine treatment”. In the non-caffeine-treated group, only when bicarbonate was analyzed as a categorical variable, individuals in the Q2 range showed significantly lower AOP risk compared to those in Q1, which aligns with the results of Model 4. This suggests that in the absence of caffeine intervention, bicarbonate levels are directly associated with AOP risk and may serve as a potential clinical marker for the early identification of preterm neonates at high risk for AOP.

We also considered the potential confounding effect of physiological immaturity on bicarbonate levels. It is well established that preterm neonates exhibit a lower renal threshold for bicarbonate reabsorption due to the functional immaturity of the proximal tubules, often resulting in lower baseline bicarbonate levels in the most premature infants (31). Consequently, there is a concern that low bicarbonate might merely serve as a proxy for lower GA, which is the primary risk factor for AOP. However, our multivariate logistic regression models were strictly adjusted for GA and birth weight. The persistence of a significant association between baseline bicarbonate and AOP after this adjustment suggests that acid-base status contributes to respiratory instability independently of renal immaturity. Furthermore, the observed non-linear pattern—where neonates in the Q2 range (16–19 mEq/L) exhibited the lowest risk compared to both lower (Q1) and higher (Q4) quartiles—argues against a simple linear correlation driven solely by the degree of prematurity, suggesting a complex physiological optimum for respiratory control.

This study provides clinically actionable insights: baseline bicarbonate levels have the potential to serve as a clinical marker for the early identification of preterm neonates at high risk for AOP, offering clinicians an early warning signal. This may facilitate the earlier initiation of preventive measures, such as closer monitoring or timely consideration of caffeine therapy, particularly for preterm neonates with low bicarbonate levels.

This study also has several limitations that warrant discussion. First, it is based on retrospective data, and due to the constraints of the public database, the exact timing of AOP events, laboratory tests, and caffeine treatment could not be determined. Therefore, future prospective studies are needed to validate these findings. However, the study population consisted of preterm infants with neonatal jaundice, who are typically admitted to the NICU immediately after birth and undergo routine heel-stick blood testing at birth (10). The exposure variable in this study—bicarbonate level—was defined as the first measurement obtained after NICU admission. Thus, it can be reasonably inferred that both AOP occurrence and caffeine treatment took place after baseline bicarbonate levels were recorded. Although the precise temporal relationship between AOP and caffeine treatment remains uncertain—given that caffeine may be administered both prophylactically and therapeutically for AOP in preterm infants—it is a common clinical practice in the NICU to provide caffeine therapy to high-risk preterm infants for the prevention of AOP (2). Therefore, the assumed temporal sequence in this mediation model is clinically plausible. Second, the identification of neonatal jaundice was based on ICD-9 codes, which typically capture clinically significant cases requiring medical evaluation or intervention, rather than encompassing all instances of physiological jaundice. This approach may explain why the prevalence of neonatal jaundice in our study is lower than the commonly reported range of 60–80%, but it ensures that the analysis focuses on a population with clinical relevance. Additionally, the generalizability of our findings is limited by the fact that the database is derived from a single-center cohort in the United States. Future research should aim to incorporate multi-center datasets and conduct prospective studies in diverse populations, including those in regions such as Asia.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that among preterm neonates with jaundice, lower baseline bicarbonate levels were associated with a higher risk of AOP. Caffeine treatment fully mediated this significant nonlinear association, and preterm neonates with baseline bicarbonate levels in the Q2 range (16–19 mEq/L) exhibited the lowest risk of AOP. In conclusion, baseline bicarbonate levels may serve as a potential clinical marker for the early identification of preterm neonates at high risk for AOP. Further research with rigorously designed prospective studies is warranted to validate these findings.
