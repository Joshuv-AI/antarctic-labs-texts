Thromboembolism and bleeding in newly diagnosed multiple myeloma: rates, risk profile, and patterns of thromboprophylaxis
Authors: Lea Vospernik, Hermine Agis, Cihan Ay, Lina Rüsing, Heinz Gisslinger, Christina Brunbauer, Clara Sophie Michel, Ingrid Simonitsch-Klupp, Alexandra Kaider, Maria-Theresa Krauth, Julia Riedl
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGuidelines suggest pharmacological thromboprophylaxis for patients with multiple myeloma (MM) treated with immunomodulatory drugs due to a high risk of venous thromboembolisms (VTE).ObjectivesTo analyze rates of VTE and bleeding, risk profiles, and patterns of thromboprophylaxis in MM patients.MethodsFor this cohort study, all consecutive patients with newly diagnosed MM between January 2012 and January 2022 who received induction treatment at our center were included. Characteristics at time of diagnosis, antimyeloma treatment, thromboprophylaxis, and thrombotic and bleeding events during 2-year follow-up were recorded.ResultsOf 293 patients screened, 208 were included. During follow-up, VTE occurred in 19 (9.1%) patients, any bleeding in 35 (16.8%), and major bleeding in 14 (6.7%). IMPEDE-VTE score predicted VTE, but SAVED score did not. Two-year cumulative incidences of VTE by type of thromboprophylaxis prescribed after diagnosis (1) and at landmark 3 months (2) for aspirin versus standard anticoagulation (low molecular weight heparin or vitamin K antagonist) versus direct oral anticoagulants versus no antithrombotic therapy were (1) 18.73% (95% CI: 7.43-33.97) versus 2.78% (95% CI: 0.21-12.6) versus 4.00% (95% CI: 0.27-17.36) versus 9.88% (95% CI: 5.03-16.67) and (2) 12.46% (95% CI: 4.48-24.74) versus 5.56% (95% CI: 0.34-23.06) versus 0.00% versus 10.51% (95% CI: 4.88-18.64), respectively. Major bleeding rates did not significantly differ between antithrombotic strategies.ConclusionWe observed a high rate of VTE and bleeding in patients with newly diagnosed MM. Use of anticoagulant therapy including direct oral anticoagulants was associated with a trend toward reduced risk of VTE compared with aspirin or no antithrombotic therapy. Bleeding risk was high irrespective of antithrombotic strategy.Keywords: Anticoagulants, clinical decision rules, hemorrhage, multiple myeloma, venous thromboembolism

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a hematological malignancy characterized by the clonal expansion of plasma cells in the bone marrow, the production of monoclonal immunoglobulin and associated end-organ damage [1]. Venous thromboembolism (VTE) is a common complication in MM patients. Two large population-based studies in the United States and Sweden reported a relative risk of 11.6 and a hazard ratio of 7.5, respectively, compared with the general population during the first year after diagnosis [2,3]. Various factors relating to disease biology, patient characteristics, and treatment choices play into this elevated risk [4]. The use of immunomodulatory drugs (IMiDs) for antimyeloma treatment is associated with a particularly high risk of developing VTE, especially in combination with multiagent therapy or high-dose glucocorticoids. VTE rates as high as 36% in patients treated with thalidomide and anthracyclines and 26% in patients receiving lenalidomide and 160 mg weekly of dexamethasone have been reported [5,6].

While it is unclear whether the occurrence of VTEs is linked to an inferior overall survival in MM patients [[7], [8], [9], [10]], thrombotic events are associated with increased morbidity and may interrupt or delay antimyeloma treatment. VTE risk remains substantial in patients treated with IMiDs despite prophylaxis [7,11,12]. At the same time, some MM patients are at a high bleeding risk due to MM-associated hemostatic dysfunction [13]. An unmet need for safe and effective thromboprophylaxis remains.

Several MM-specific VTE risk assessment models have been published. The IMPEDE-VTE score accounts for a number of individual patient-related risk factors as well as medical interventions and divides patients into low-risk, intermediate-risk, and high-risk groups [14]. The SAVED score was developed specifically for patients with an IMiD-based therapy and differentiates between those at a low risk versus high risk of developing VTE based on patient characteristics and history as well as dexamethasone dosage [15]. The components of these scoring systems are summarized in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

Current guidelines recommend primary thromboprophylaxis for all patients receiving IMiD-based regimens. There is, however, a scarcity of formal recommendation for those who are not treated with IMiDs. The use of aspirin is suggested as thromboprophylaxis in IMiD-treated patients with a low risk of VTE, whereas prophylactic dose low molecular weight heparins (LMWHs) or full anticoagulation with vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) are recommended for those with higher risk. The updated guidelines of the International Initiative on Thrombosis and Cancer as well as the National Comprehensive Cancer Network and the European Society of Medical Oncology now also include direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), specifically apixaban, or alternatively rivaroxaban for the latter two, at a prophylactic dose. Updates of the International Myeloma Working Group, European Myeloma Network, and American Society of Hematology guidelines are awaited [[16], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21]].

DOACs were established for thromboprophylaxis in ambulatory cancer patients through randomized clinical trials; however, very few patients with MM were included [22,23]. Data on efficacy and safety in this setting are mostly derived from predominantly retrospective studies with small numbers of patients. Nevertheless, the available studies [[24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29]] show encouraging results regarding the use of DOACs as thromboprophylaxis in MM, which could be a more convenient alternative to LMWHs and VKAs, especially when administered over a long period of time, as they are given orally and do not normally require blood monitoring.

At our center, DOACs have been offered to patients with MM as an alternative option for thromboprophylaxis since 2012. The aim of this study was to analyze VTE rates and bleeding complications in newly diagnosed patients receiving different prophylactic strategies as well as to comprehensively describe this large and diverse patient population to gain insight into demographic, disease, and treatment characteristics in the real-world setting and investigate whether existing risk assessment models are applicable to these patients.

Methods

Study population

For this cohort study, we screened all consecutive patients that presented to our center between January 2012 and January 2022 with MM or related plasma cell disorders. Inclusion criteria were newly diagnosed, biopsy-proven MM within this time range and systemic induction treatment at our center within the observational period. A detailed breakdown of the inclusion and exclusion criteria as well as reasons for exclusion are shown in Supplementary Table 3. Data were collected through review of electronic and paper medical records at our center and electronic patient charts from other hospitals for patients who presented with relevant acute events.

Baseline characteristics of disease as well as risk factors for VTE according to the IMPEDE-VTE and SAVED scores were assessed at time of diagnosis. Antimyeloma treatment, including the use of IMiDs (defined as having ≥2 prescriptions or documentation of IMiD use at a minimum of 2 separate visits) and thromboprophylaxis (defined as having ≥2 prescriptions or documentation of a certain type of anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy at a minimum of 2 separate visits and grouped into antiplatelet therapy, heparins, VKAs, and DOACs regardless of the specific substance or dosage) were recorded continuously during a 2-year observational period, starting with the time of first diagnosis of MM by bone marrow biopsy. For statistical analysis of the association between type of thromboprophylaxis and outcome events in our real-world patient cohort, we additionally recorded the current type of thromboprophylaxis with landmarks at time of diagnosis and 3 months of follow-up to consider treatment changes within the first 3 months as most patients had completed diagnostic workup and started specific treatment including IMiDs at this point (with a margin of tolerance of 2 weeks after the landmark). We also recorded thromboprophylaxis at 3 more landmarks (1 month, 6 months, and 1 year after diagnosis of MM) to analyze changes in thromboprophylaxis over time and compare thromboprophylaxis in those with and without IMiD-based therapy.

Patients with missing data were excluded for the relevant analyses. Patients lost to follow-up were censored at last contact.

Outcome events

Venous and arterial thromboembolisms diagnosed objectively by standard imaging as well as major, clinically relevant nonmajor, and minor bleeding events and survival were documented in the same time frame and reviewed by an adjudication committee. In those that had multiple VTE or bleeding events, only the first event was counted.

For VTE, standard imaging refers to deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism (PE) diagnosed by compression ultrasonography or computed tomography pulmonary angiography or ventilation/perfusion lung scintigraphy, respectively. Bleeding events were classified according to the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) definition of major bleeding as (1) fatal bleeding, and/or (2) symptomatic bleeding in a critical area or organ, such as intracranial, intraspinal, intraocular, retroperitoneal, intra-articular or pericardial, or intramuscular with compartment syndrome, and/or (3) bleeding causing a decrease in hemoglobin level of 20 g/L (1.24 mmol/L) or more or leading to transfusion of ≥2 units of whole blood or red cells [30]. Clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding (any sign or symptom of hemorrhage, eg, more bleeding than would be expected for a clinical circumstance, including bleeding found by imaging alone) that did not fit the criteria for the ISTH definition of major bleeding was defined by ≥1 of the following criteria: (1) requiring medical intervention by a healthcare professional, and/or (2) leading to hospitalization or increased level of care and/or (3) prompting a face to face evaluation (ie, not just a telephone or electronic communication) [31].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are described by the median values (IQRs) and categorical variables by absolute and relative frequencies.

To quantify the probability of thrombotic or bleeding events, cumulative incidence functions (CIFs) were calculated considering death as a competing event. The CIF was estimated for the total cohort, as well as stratified by the type of thromboprophylaxis at time of diagnosis and at 3 months of follow-up, respectively. To evaluate the effect of the type of thromboprophylaxis at 3 months of follow-up on the probability of thrombotic or bleeding events, landmark analyses were performed. Calculations in these landmark analyses were based on patients still at risk at 3 months, and 3 months after diagnosis was considered the starting time point (time 0). Gray’s test was calculated to compare CIFs between treatment groups. Due to the exploratory nature of this study, no multiplicity correction was applied.

To evaluate the association of the IMPEDE-VTE score and the SAVED score with the risk of VTE, univariate Cox proportional cause-specific hazards regression models were conducted.

SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute Inc) was used for statistical calculations.

According to the IMPEDE-VTE score, 38.9% of patients had low baseline risk of VTE, 48.1% had intermediate risk, and 13% had high risk. The SAVED score was assessed for 172 patients receiving IMiD-based therapy, of which 69.8% had low baseline risk of VTE and 30.2% had high risk.

In our cohort, 86% of patients were treated with an IMiD-based regimen during the first 2 years after diagnosis. Most patients received thromboprophylaxis at some point during follow-up, with only 12 patients never receiving any anticoagulation or antiplatelet therapy. In total, 47% of patients received a DOAC at some point. Median age at diagnosis and VTE risk scores stratified by type of thromboprophylaxis at baseline and at 3 months of follow-up are shown in Supplementary Tables 4 and 5.

Switching of thromboprophylactic strategies was common, with a trend toward more frequent treatment with DOACs at later points during follow-up and a higher prevalence of antithrombotic therapy of any type at later landmarks. Over half of patients (52%) were not using any thromboprophylaxis at the time of diagnosis, but the vast majority (86%) used pharmacological thromboprophylaxis at 12 months, 54% of which were prescribed a DOAC. For aspirin and LMWH, the numbers remained relatively stable, with a slightly higher number of patients receiving LMWHs at diagnosis due to orthopedic complications and a modest increase in aspirin use over time. The distribution of thromboprophylaxis at different points during follow-up is shown in Figure 1. Thromboprophylaxis also varied between patients that were treated with IMiDs at any point during the observation period and those that were not. In total, 90% of patients who received IMiD-based therapy had thromboprophylaxis after the first year of follow-up, and over half of cases with DOACs, compared with 61.5% in those without IMiD treatment. Outcome events

Events that occurred during follow-up are listed in Table 2. In total, 19 patients (9.1%) had a VTE, 10 of which were PEs. One patient died after a PE. The majority, 84.2%, of VTE events occurred during the first year after diagnosis, and 52.6% of patients were being treated with an IMiD-based regimen at the time of VTE. Three patients had multiple VTEs during follow-up, but only the first was counted in our analysis. Arterial thromboembolisms occurred in 4 (1.9%) patients, 3 of which were being treated with IMiDs, including 2 myocardial infarctions, 1 ischemic stroke, and 1 central artery thrombosis of the eye.

Altogether, 35 (16.8%) patients had ≥1 bleeding event, 14 of which were major bleedings and 11 of which were clinically relevant nonmajor bleedings. Of the major bleedings, 2 were intracranial hemorrhages and 3 were gastrointestinal bleedings. Four major bleedings (28.6%) occurred after renal biopsy. One patient presented with an acquired bleeding disorder, which led to the eventual diagnosis of MM, and 3 patients had tumor bleeding. Four (28.6%) patients died after a major bleeding. Similarly to VTE, the majority (74.3%) of bleedings occurred within the first year, and almost a quarter (22.8%) occurred within the first month after diagnosis.

A total of 20 (9.6%) patients died during follow-up.

Predictive potential of VTE risk scores

The number of VTEs in each risk group of the SAVED and IMPEDE-VTE scores are shown in Table 3. The 2-year VTE rate was 5.45% (95% CI: 1.74-12.37) in the IMPEDE-VTE low risk group, 10.43% (95% CI: 5.3-17.55) in the intermediate risk group, and 18.52% (95% CI: 6.57-35.19) in the high risk group. A high IMPEDE-VTE score was associated with risk for VTE (hazard ratio [HR] per 1 point increase: 1.19 [95% CI: 1.04-1.37], P = .01; HR for high (≥8 points) vs low (≤3 points) IMPEDE-VTE: 3.99 [95% CI: 1.07-14.88], P = .04; HR for intermediate vs low: 2.13 [95% CI: 0.67-6.81], P = .2; overall for low vs intermediate vs high: P = .12). The SAVED score did not predict VTE in our cohort (HR for high vs low SAVED: 0.97 [95% CI 0.34-2.75], P = .95).

Antithrombotic therapy and risk of VTE

Of the 19 VTE events in our cohort, 8 patients were receiving any type of thromboprophylaxis at the time of event, 9 received thromboprophylaxis with aspirin and 2 with LMWH. No patient had a VTE while on oral anticoagulation with VKA or DOACs.

Estimated 2-year cumulative incidences of VTE stratified by type of thromboprophylaxis prescribed after diagnosis were 18.73% (95% CI: 7.43-33.97) with aspirin, 2.78% (95% CI: 0.21-12.6) with standard anticoagulation with LMWH or VKA, 4.00% (95% CI: 0.27-17.36) with DOACs, and 9.88% (95% CI: 5.03-16.67) without thromboprophylaxis. For type of thromboprophylaxis prescribed at 3 months of follow-up, cumulative incidences for the following 21 months were 12.46% (95% CI: 4.48-24.74) with aspirin, 5.56% (95% CI: 0.34-23.06) with LMWH or VKA, 0.00% with DOACs, and 10.51% (95% CI: 4.88-18.64) without thromboprophylaxis. Antithrombotic therapy and risk of bleeding

Of the 35 bleeding events, 9 patients were receiving no thromboprophylaxis at the time of event, 5 received aspirin, 7 LMWH, and 13 DOACs. No patient had a bleeding event while on VKA. Of the 14 major bleeding events, 6 patients received no thromboprophylaxis at the time of event, 1 received aspirin, 3 LMWH, and 4 DOACs. Two patients had a lethal bleeding event without any antithrombotic agent, and 1 patient had a lethal bleeding event while taking LMWH.

Estimated 2-year cumulative incidences of bleeding of any degree and major bleeding stratified by type of thromboprophylaxis prescribed after diagnosis were 12.68% (95% CI: 3.89-26.91) and 3.57% (95% CI: 2.48-15.72), respectively, with aspirin 22.09% (95% CI: 10.2-36.83) and 11.09% (95% CI: 3.41-23.9) with standard anticoagulation with LMWH or VKA, 16.00% (95% CI: 4.86-32.94) and 0.00% with DOACs, and 15.52% (95% CI: 9.29-23.21) and 6.82% (95% CI: 2.99-12.82) without thromboprophylaxis. For type of thromboprophylaxis prescribed at 3 months of follow-up, cumulative incidences for the following 21 months were 12.86% (95% CI: 4.62-25.46) and 2.86% (95% CI: 0.209-12.89) with aspirin, 31.58% (95% CI: 12.42-52.89) and 0.00% with LMWH or VKA, 8.28% (95% CI: 2.55-18.41) and 5.92% (95% CI: 1.49-14.99) with DOACs, and 8.22% (95% CI: 3.33-15.98) and 4.07% (95% CI: 1.07-10.45) without thromboprophylaxis. Discussion

Here, we describe a large cohort of newly diagnosed MM patients using different thromboprophylactic medications in a real-life setting. Our data emphasize the high risk of VTE faced by MM patients in the era of new antimyeloma therapies. While the introduction of IMiDs has added significantly to the improved survival seen over the past years [32], the associated VTE risk remains a challenge. Like in our cohort, most patients now receive IMiD-based therapy in early treatment lines, and established thromboprophylactic strategies such as LMWH or VKA may add to the therapeutic burden, especially when prescribed over an extended period. While DOACs also have limitations, in particular for patients with severe renal insufficiency, they provide multiple advantages due to their oral administration at a fixed dose without need for monitoring and with less drug-drug and food interactions than VKAs and higher patient satisfaction [[33], [34], [35]], but there are limited data regarding efficacy and safety in this setting [[24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29]].

Our results suggest that DOACs could be effective in preventing VTE in MM patients, especially in the context of IMiD-based therapy, as there were no VTEs in patients who were receiving a DOAC, which was the most common thromboprophylaxis in IMiD-treated patients in our cohort. Furthermore, our observations indicate that VTE occurs more frequently with aspirin thromboprophylaxis than with anticoagulation, as shown in previous studies [36,37]. In our cohort, those with aspirin thromboprophylaxis had higher cumulative incidence rates of VTE than those without any pharmacological thromboprophylaxis at both landmarks, even if statistical significance was not reached due to the low number of events. This might be explained by a higher baseline VTE risk, which prompted the prescription of thromboprophylaxis, although this is not evident in the VTE risk score assessments (Supplementary Tables 4 and 5). However, aspirin does not seem to be adequately effective in mitigating the elevated VTE risk. Similar results were reported for a recent study [38] that analyzed 250 newly diagnosed MM patients almost exclusively treated with aspirin thromboprophylaxis and found VTE rates of 8% within 6 months and 14.8% within 19 months. Considering these findings, the common clinical practice of prescribing aspirin for VTE prophylaxis should be questioned.

Interestingly, we did not find any association between the SAVED score and risk of VTE in MM, while the IMPEDE-VTE score did show a similar association with VTE risk in our cohort as in previous studies [14,39]. However, the sample size of our study was smaller than the derivation and validation cohorts of the original studies [14,15]. Of note, there were no Asian patients in our cohort, and only 3 patients received high-dose dexamethasone, which might have decreased applicability. The IMPEDE-VTE score accounts for a higher number of risk factors and might therefore be more robust and better suited to assess VTE risk in various populations of MM patients. Another difference between our cohort and the population on which the SAVED score was based is the number of patients with thromboprophylaxis as only 13% of patients in the derivation cohort for the SAVED score received concurrent anticoagulation [15]. This could also have impacted the predictive potential in our cohort; however, if this is the case, it calls into question the usefulness of this score designed specifically for IMiD-treated patients who, in accordance with all major guidelines, should receive thromboprophylaxis in any case.

We decided on a 2-year observation period to reflect the improved survival in MM. With patients surviving and continuing treatment for extended periods, a new need to closely evaluate the years following first-line treatment and characterize risks in this population arises. Our results are in line with previous studies that indicate that the risk of VTE is highest during the first year after diagnosis [2,3]. The VTE rate during the second year of follow-up was comparatively low in our cohort despite frequent maintenance therapy with IMiDs. This should prompt further research into whether thromboprophylaxis is necessary during maintenance therapy.

While the increased VTE risk in newly diagnosed MM patients has been a focus of discussion, less research exists on the bleeding risk in these patients. In our cohort, bleeding rates were high in all thromboprophylaxis groups, including those without any antithrombotic medication, suggesting other underlying causes for bleeding such as thrombocytopenia, bleeding after interventions, or tumor bleeding. There were more lethal bleeding events than fatal thrombotic events, highlighting the importance of this adverse event. Interestingly, a high proportion of bleeding events occurred soon after diagnosis, which might be due to diagnostic interventions or uncontrolled disease activity interfering with hemostatic function.

Cumulative incidences of bleeding in patients on thromboprophylaxis with DOACs did not exceed those of patients treated with LMWH but were in fact significantly lower at the 3-month landmark; however, the difference between thromboprophylaxis groups was only statistically significant at this one landmark. There was no significant difference in rates of major bleeding between thromboprophylaxis groups, and there was no lethal bleeding while on DOACs. Our results suggest that DOACs are at least as safe as other anticoagulants in this indication.

Some limitations need to be considered when interpreting our results. Due to the observational nature of our study, there is a risk of selection bias, and patients did not receive a predefined standardized antithrombotic strategy and often switched medication. We aimed to provide a complete picture by analyzing cumulative incidences of events both based on the type of thromboprophylaxis prescribed at the time of myeloma diagnosis to represent the entire cohort before any patients were censored and ensure no events are missed and at the 3-month landmark, which more accurately depicts the thromboprophylaxis prescribed during most of the observation period (although this introduces a survivor bias as switching of thromboprophylaxis cannot be considered in those with very high VTE risk that were already censored due to an early event). However, for statistical analysis, the total number of adverse events was not high enough to perform time-varying analyses or adjust for multiple possible confounders. We did not systematically record response to treatment or correct for disease activity as a possible influence on VTE risk. Furthermore, due to the small number of events, the power to detect clinically relevant differences is rather low. Therefore, large randomized controlled trials should be conducted to confirm our findings.

In conclusion, most patients received antithrombotic therapy in our cohort (only 12 never used any form of antithrombotic medication). Compared with antiplatelet agents or no antithrombotic therapy, use of anticoagulants including DOACs was associated with a trend toward reduced risk of VTE. No VTE occurred in patients that were treated with DOACs. Bleeding rates were generally high, but significantly lower with DOACs than with standard anticoagulation at the 3-month landmark.
