Real-time deep learning interpretation of echocardiographic video for automated detection of anatomical features associated with tetralogy of fallot in pediatric patients : a feasibility study
Authors: Mi Jin Kim, Jeong Jin Yu, Seulgi Cha, Jae Suk Baek, Dongha Yang, Yeon Jin Jang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) is a complex congenital heart defect requiring precise diagnosis. Echocardiography is essential for detection, but a global shortage of pediatric cardiologists can hinder timely diagnosis. Our study aims to develop an Artificial intelligence (AI) model using deep learning to automate TOF diagnosis from echocardiograms, serving as a screening tool in regions lacking specialists. We trained an AI model with echocardiograms from 174 pediatric patients at Seoul Asan Medical Center (2018–2023). Utilizing Detectron2 and Mask R-CNN, we processed, labeled, and trained the model to detect four characteristic defects of TOF in images and videos. Model performance was assessed via PR curves, accuracy, and F1 scores. The developed model achieved an AUC of approximately 1.00 and F1 score of 96.8%, demonstrating high sensitivity and precision. It reliably distinguished TOF with over 97% accuracy across multiple videos, showing promise for clinical application. Deep learning-based automated feature level detection of TOF offers a promising way to improve diagnosis, increase accessibility, and reduce the workload of pediatric cardiologists globally. Future research should focus on expanding datasets and refining models to apply to other congenital heart disease, enhancing patient care overall.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45943-x.

Background and objectives

Congenital heart disease (CHD) is the most common birth defect worldwide, accounting for one-third of all congenital anomalies,1 and is a leading cause of mortality in newborns and children. Among CHDs, Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) is one of the most common cyanotic CHD, with many cases diagnosed after the neonatal period. Early and accurate diagnosis of TOF is crucial for improving prognosis, as delayed diagnosis can lead to prolonged cyanosis, impaired growth of the pulmonary arteries and loss of the optimal timing for surgery, thereby adversely affecting the child’s future quality of life.2.

Echocardiography is the primary, non-invasive, cost-effective, and repeatable imaging modality for diagnosing CHD, including TOF.3 Currently, accurate interpretation relies on physicians or licensed sonographers, but the required specialized knowledge poses a challenge, especially in centers with limited expertise.4 Misdiagnosis can delay treatment and worsen outcomes. Therefore, developing diagnostic support tools powered by Artificial intelligence (AI) to assist less experienced practitioners holds significant promise.

Recently, AI applications in cardiology have gained momentum, with numerous studies exploring its role in interpreting echocardiography.5–8 However, in the context of pediatric CHD diagnosis, research remains in the early stages, with few studies focusing specifically on automated diagnosis. Some efforts have used deep learning to interpret individual conditions such as atrial septal defect (ASD),9,10 ventricular septal defect (VSD) and coarctation of the aorta.11 Additionally, there are studies introducing algorithms aimed at distinguishing between ASD and VSD.12 While promising, there studies highlight the potential for AI to improve diagnostic accuracy in pediatric echocardiography and facilitate clinical translation.

Recognizing this need, our teams at Seoul Asan Medical Center and MAILab® collaborated to develop a deep learning algorithm capable of identifying TOF, aiming to aid less experienced physicians and serve as screening tool, particularly in regions lacking CHD specialists.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective study included pediatric patients under 18 years of age diagnosed with TOF at Seoul Asan Medical Center from July 2018 to March 2023. The presence of TOF was confirmed based on diagnoses presented in the electronic medical record (EMR) and echocardiographic clinical reports, with final confirmation by an experienced cardiologist. A total of 411 patients were diagnosed with TOF. Among them, 109 patients with Fallot type double outlet right ventricle (DORV) and 105 patients with other complicated cardiac malformations were excluded. These included absent pulmonary valve (8), pulmonary atresia (37), atrioventricular (AV) canal defect (13), major aortopulmonary collateral arteries (MAPCAs) (17), dextrocardia (3), partial anomalous pulmonary venous return (PAPVR) (5), left isomerism (3), and other branch pulmonary arteries anomalies (19). Additionally, 16 patients were excluded because they were adults aged 18 years or older at the time of imaging, and 47 patients were excluded due to insufficient or poor echocardiographic images. However, patients with minor cardiac abnormalities that did not significantly affect hemodynamics, such as small patent foramen ovale, atrial septal defect, patent ductus arteriosus, or persistent left superior vena cava, were included. During the study period, echocardiography images were obtained from 40 patients with good image quality who visited our hospital due to complaints of cardiac murmur or chest pain and underwent echocardiography for evaluation. This group included 20 patients with normal cardiac structure and 20 patients with isolated VSD and/or pulmonary stenosis. In total, 174 patients were enrolled (134 patients with TOF and 40 patients without TOF), and their high quality echocardiography images were retrospectively collected and randomly divided into a training, validation and testing datasets for analysis. This retrospective study used anonymized videos obtained before IRB approval, with a waiver of informed consent due to the large volume. The study was performed according to the declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional Medical Ethics Committee.

Echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiographic (TTE) video sequences were obtained in accordance with the 2006 American Society of Echocardiography (ASE) guidelines13,14. All patient data were anonymized. These echocardiograms were acquired using a diverse range of echocardiography machines and models, including Phillips iE-elite with transducer S8-1 and S5-1 (Phillips, Andover, MA, USA), Vivid E95 (General Electric, Fairfield, CT, USA), and the Siemens SC2000 with transducer 4V1c (siemens, Munich, Germany). Images were stored in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format for subsequent processing.

Collection and annotation of input data for model development

Current guidelines for diagnosis of TOF recommend identifying four characteristic components using standardized echocardiographic protocols4,14: (1) aortic override (AO) over the muscular septum, (2) ventricular septal defect (VSD), (3) right ventricular outflow tract obstruction (RVOTO) with varying degrees of subvalvular, valvular, and supravalvular pulmonary stenosis, and (4) right ventricular hypertrophy (RVH) accompanied by RV infundibular narrowing. To evaluate these hallmark defects, pediatric cardiologists primarily rely on six echocardiographic views4,14,15: parasternal long-axis, parasternal short-axis, apical four-chamber, apical five-chamber, subcostal coronal, and subcostal right anterior oblique views. The key features of TOF observable in each view are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.16 For subsequent AI analysis, echocardiogram videos were exported in DICOM format and converted into individual frames. Frames with clearly visible TOF-associated features were selected from multiple standard transthoracic views. Preprocessing included intensity normalization (rescaling pixel values to a 0–1 range) and mild de‑noising using a Gaussian filter to reduce speckle noise while preserving anatomical boundaries. All frames were resized to a fixed resolution of 600 × 1400 pixels to ensure consistent input dimensions for Mask R‑CNN. Each frame was then annotated for the presence or absence of AO, VSD, RVOTO, and RVH using ImageLab. Because multiple abnormalities can co‑occur in a single image, the dataset was structured for multi‑label classification, and the annotation files were formatted as COCO (common objects in context) JSON files. Examples of annotations are provided in Supplementary Fig. 1. The selection and annotation processes were independently performed by two pediatric cardiologists with over 10 years of experience (JJY, MJK), and discrepancies in lesion labels were resolved by joint review and consensus; only consensus annotations were used for model development and evaluation.

Model development and training using detectron2

For model development, we utilized the Detectron2 platform, developed by Facebook AI Research (FAIR)17, leveraging a dataset of annotated echocardiographic images. The process began with the registration of a custom multi-label dataset, followed by implementation of a data mapper to apply image transformations, enhancing the model’s generalization. A custom script was created to split the dataset into training, validation, and test sets, enabling a robust and reproducible evaluation framework. Each image was labeled with one or more of the following conditions: AO, VSD, RVH, PS, and no-CHD. The dataset included 903 AO, 2,033 VSD, 833 RVH, 1,158 PS, and 311 no-CHD samples (Fig. 1B). In total, 5,238 image samples were used: 4,268 for training, 554 for validation, and 416 for testing. These distributions across the five categories are also illustrated in Fig. 1B. A data loader was implemented to efficiently batch and deliver images during model training. The model was trained using data augmentation techniques to improve robustness and reduce overfitting. Upon completion of training, final model weights were saved for deployment. During inference, the trained model accurately performed multi-label classification, detecting one or more of the five cardiac conditions simultaneously in previously unseen echocardiographic images.

Model development and training configuration

Model training was conducted on a Windows AI Server equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 6000 GPU (22 GB VRAM), Intel CPU, and 128 GB RAM. The software environment included Python 3.8.1, Torch 1.10.0 + cu113, Detectron2 version 0.6, and TensorBoard 2.12. The training was performed over 5,000 iterations using a learning rate of 0.01 with a warmup schedule and step decay. A batch size of 6 images per iteration was used, with 4 data loading workers to parallelize input processing. Automatic Mixed Precision (AMP) was enabled to enhance computational efficiency. Model evaluations were conducted every 500 iterations, and both training logs and model weights were saved to an output directory to ensure reproducibility.

A hybrid model for TOF detection and classification using deep feature learning

This study presents a hybrid model for detecting and classifying TOF-associated cardiac defects in echocardiograms, employing the Detectron2 platform with Mask R-CNN for object detection, classification, and instance segmentation. The model architecture, illustrated in Fig. 2, was developed using a PyTorch-based Detectron2 framework, incorporating a pretrained model from the Detectron2 Model Zoo. The model utilized a ResNet-101 backbone with a Feature Pyramid Network (FPN) for multi-scale feature extraction. The Region Proposal Network (RPN) generated bounding box proposals using anchor boxes, and features were extracted via ROI Align. Predictions were processed through three heads: bounding box regression, classification, and mask segmentation. The Adam optimizer was employed to minimize the total loss function and mitigate overfitting, and non-maximum suppression (NMS) was applied to eliminate redundant detections.

The codebase seamlessly processed both images and videos, integrating training, inference, and classification into a unified system. For video analysis, the code reads .avi files from a specified directory frame-by-frame using OpenCV, tracking TOF-associated classes—AO, VSD, RVH, and PS. A video is classified as TOF if all four classes (or three, excluding RVH) are detected; otherwise, it is labeled “No TOF” due to missing classes. RVH, a secondary finding due to chronic systemic pressure, was excluded from the diagnostic criteria because it may or may not be present depending on when the echocardiography was performed.

Model evaluation for TOF detection in echocardiogram videos

The model was evaluated using echocardiogram videos from 46 pediatric patients with congenital heart disease: 21 with isolated VSD, 7 with VSD and PS, 8 with truncus arteriosus, and 10 with TOF. Each patient contributed 3 to 16 video sequences. These videos were used to assess the model’s accuracy in detecting TOF-associated cardiac abnormalities in real-world clinical settings. The pretrained model was applied to these videos without additional fine-tuning. Model outputs were aggregated on a per-patient basis, and we computed performance metrics for distinguishing TOF from non-TOF (accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1 score) with 95% confidence intervals estimated via bootstrap resampling.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.8.1 (Scikit-learn), GraphPad Prism 9.0, SPSS v21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), or R software (version 3.6.3; www.r-project.org). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Model performance was evaluated using standard multi-label metrics: accuracy, sensitivity, precision, and F1 score. Each metric was calculated per label using one-vs-all comparisons and macro-averaged across all classes. Sequence-level accuracy was derived from majority-voted frame predictions. Confidence scores were derived from softmax output probabilities. Additionally, mean average precision (mAP) across Intersection over Union (IoU) thresholds and precision–recall (PR) AUC were used to assess detection quality and classification reliability.

Model training and validation performance

The training and validation accuracy curves across 50 epochs are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. During the early phase of training, model accuracy increased rapidly, surpassing 98% by approximately the 10th epoch. Thereafter, both training and validation curves plateaued, indicating convergence and model stability. The close alignment of these curves suggests minimal overfitting and good generalization, confirming that the model effectively learned relevant features from pediatric echocardiographic data.

Evaluation of model performance

The model’s performance was evaluated using an independent test dataset. As summarized in Table 2, it achieved a high average accuracy of 99.5%, with a sensitivity of 91.9%, precision of 95.8%, and an F1 score of 96.9%. These metrics indicate strong feature-level detection performance, effectively balancing high true positive rates with a low incidence of false positives—an essential requirement in clinical settings. As a multi-label classifier, the model was capable of detecting multiple coexisting cardiac defects per frame, including TOF-associated lesions such as VSD, PS, AO, and RVH.

Frame-Level deep learning detection of tof-associated heart defects in pediatric echocardiography

Detectron2, the object detection and instance segmentation framework used in this study, generates bounding boxes with class labels and associated confidence scores (Fig. 4). The confidence score represents the model’s estimated probability that a detected structure is correctly classified and is expressed as a percentage. These scores are calculated using mAP across various IoU thresholds. The accuracy of each detection was evaluated using these confidence metrics. Figure 4 presents representative outputs from the model. In Fig. 4A, an AO was accurately identified with a 93% confidence score, as highlighted by a orange bounding box. This prediction closely matched expert annotations, validating the model’s accuracy for detecting isolated defects. Figure 4B demonstrates the model’s capability for multi-label detection, identifying both a VSD (96% confidence) and pulmonary stenosis (98% confidence) in a color Doppler image, each marked with distinct bounding boxes. These predictions aligned with the clinical diagnosis, indicating the model’s utility for both spatial localization and diagnostic inference.

Real-time deep learning interpretation of pediatric echocardiograms for TOF detection

We further evaluated the model’s performance on real-time echocardiographic video streams to determine its clinical applicability. The model processed each video frame sequentially, overlaying detected features onto the live feed with accompanying confidence scores and timestamps. Inference speeds varied from 1.45 to 5.98 frames per second (fps), depending on frame complexity, demonstrating feasibility for near real-time use. Representative frames and console outputs are shown in Fig. 5. In the upper pane, the model displays image frames with high-confidence bounding boxes; the lower console summarizes model simulation accuracy for each video sequence. In Fig. 5A, the model detected a VSD but did not identify additional anomalies required to diagnose TOF. Conversely, Fig. 5B shows a case where the model identified all key components of TOF, fulfilling diagnostic criteria. These examples underscore the model’s capacity to differentiate between isolated and complex congenital heart defects with high reliability, supporting its integration into clinical workflows for decision-making.

Model performance for TOF detection across CHD Subtypes in echocardiogram video sequences

To assess diagnostic consistency, we analyzed the model’s performance across different CHD subtypes using real-time echocardiogram videos from 46 children, each contributing 3 to 16 real time echocardiogram video sequences (Table 3) by including an additional conotruncal cohort. In all 46 cases, the model provided correct diagnoses consistent with the ground truth. The model achieved high mean classification accuracy across all groups: 0.92 (95% confidence intervals (CI) : 0.78–0.97) in VSD-only cases, 0.93 (95% CI : 0.85–0.98) in VSD with PS, and 0.92 (95% CI : 0.79–0.96) in TOF cases. No significant differences in accuracy were observed among the subgroups (one-way ANOVA, p = 0.55), confirming the model’s robustness and generalizability across diverse patient populations and CHD presentations. In the newly added cohort, the model classified all 8 truncus arteriosus pateints as “not TOF” (specificity for excluding TOF among truncus cases 100%, 95% CI 0.93-1.00 by exact methods), while correctly identifying typical TOF cases in this subcohort. We note that the model was not trained to recognize truncus arteriosus specifically; therefore, these results support the model’s ability to exclude TOF anatomy in truncus cases but do not establish positive diagnostic performance for truncus arteriosus itself.

Discussion

TOF is one of the CHD characterized by four associated defects and has a very morphologically distinctive presentation. In this study, we developed and validated a deep learning-based diagnostic model capable of identifying TOF using pediatric echocardiographic video sequences. Our model was designed as a multi-label classifier, enabling the simultaneous detection of multiple coexisting cardiac anomalies within each image frame, including AO, VSD, PS, and RVH. It achieved high diagnostic performance, with a test accuracy of 99.5%, sensitivity of 91.9%, precision of 95.8%, and an F1 score of 96.9%. These results demonstrate the feasibility of applying artificial intelligence (AI) to support TOF diagnosis in real-world pediatric settings, particularly where specialized expertise may be limited.

Accurate diagnosis of TOF remains critically important, given its prevalence among congenital heart diseases and the significant implications of delayed treatment. Traditional echocardiographic interpretation requires specialized training, and misdiagnosis may delay timely intervention. Our AI model addresses this challenge by providing automated, frame-level detection of anatomical features associated with TOF (namely AO, VSD, PS, and RVH) with spatial and probabilistic precision.

Notably, the model performed well even in real-time scenarios, achieving inference speeds of up to 5.98 frames per second (fps), which supports its potential use in point-of-care settings. Its ability to accurately distinguish between TOF and other CHD subtypes, including isolated VSD and PS, further highlights its diagnostic specificity.

While previous studies have explored AI applications in echocardiography for adult cardiovascular diseases and isolated lesions such as atrial or ventricular septal defects, few have focused specifically on TOF or applied real-time video analysis in pediatric populations. Our study builds on this foundational work by integrating object detection and instance segmentation using the Detectron2 and Mask R-CNN frameworks, thereby enabling both classification and spatial localization of cardiac anomalies. To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to apply a deep learning model to perform automated, frame-by-frame TOF detection using pediatric echocardiographic videos.

Importantly, to address the concern that our initial control cohort lacked adequate representation of conotruncal pathology, we performed an additional analysis in a separate cohort of truncus arteriosus patients. The model showed reasonable performance in distinguishing TOF from truncus arteriosus, a diagnosis that shares several anatomic features and represents a key differential consideration in clinical practice. Although the sample size of this conotruncal cohort remains small, these findings provide initial evidence that the feature-based approach can extend beyond simple controls to more challenging conotruncal differentials.

The clinical utility of the model is further enhanced by its interpretability. Bounding boxes and class confidence scores offer visual cues that may assist clinicians—particularly those with limited cardiology training—in making more accurate and timely diagnoses.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study was conducted at a single tertiary center, and the overall sample size—particularly in the external test set—was relatively small. Accordingly, the external performance metrics should be interpreted as preliminary, and further validation in larger, independent cohorts will be necessary. Second, our non-TOF cohort primarily consisted of patients with isolated VSD, VSD with pulmonary stenosis, and structurally normal hearts, and did not systematically include other conotruncal anomalies such as truncus arteriosus or DORV. This may limit the current assessment of the model in more challenging differential diagnostic scenarios, although we partially addressed this by performing an additional analysis in a small cohort of truncus arteriosus patients. Nevertheless, the number of truncus arteriosus and TOF patients in this additional conotruncal analysis was still limited, and the confidence intervals around performance metrics remain wide. Larger, multi-center conotruncal cohorts including truncus arteriosus and other TOF-related phenotypes (e.g., TOF-type DORV) will be essential to fully establish diagnostic generalizability. Third, we did not perform a formal head‑to‑head comparison with experienced pediatric echocardiographers, nor multi‑center external validation, so this work should be regarded as a feasibility study of automated detection of TOF‑associated anatomical features rather than a fully validated diagnostic tool. Finally, as with other deep learning approaches, issues of interpretability and potential biases remain and warrant further investigation. In resource‑limited settings or in laboratories with limited pediatric echocardiography expertise, consistent acquisition of all six standard TOF views may be challenging and could represent a rate‑limiting step for deploying our approach. Future studies should address these limitations by incorporating multi-center and multi-vendor data, including a broader spectrum of conotruncal anomalies, and performing reader studies that compare model performance with that of expert pediatric echocardiographers. In addition, it will be important to (1) identify a minimal, high‑yield subset of views sufficient for robust feature detection, and (2) evaluate whether targeted training of sonographers to obtain these key views could facilitate the practical use of AI‑assisted TOF screening in underserved regions. Such efforts will be essential to move from this proof‑of‑concept stage toward robust clinical validation and broader implementation.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that a deep learning model trained on echocardiographic video data can reliably detect TOF in pediatric patients, achieving high diagnostic accuracy and real-time performance. With further validation, such AI-driven tools could provide valuable decision support for clinicians, reduce diagnostic variability, and improve early detection of complex congenital heart defects—particularly in underserved areas with limited access to pediatric cardiology expertise. Furthermore, it is hoped that this will significantly contribute to reducing the workload of pediatric cardiologists worldwide, who are currently facing shortages. Future efforts should focus on collecting larger and more diverse datasets and the refining deep learning models to extend their application to other congenital heart diseases, ultimately enhancing patient care and outcomes.
