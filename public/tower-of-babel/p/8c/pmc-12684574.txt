Endothelial Cell‐Related Proteins in Plasma Predict Major Adverse Cardiovascular Events and Worsening Heart Failure in Patients With Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy
Authors: Shogo Tamura, Keitaro Akita, Michael A. Fifer, Albree Tower‐Rader, Mathew S. Maurer, Yuichi J. Shimada
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) often causes major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and worsening heart failure (HF). Endothelial cell (EC) dysfunction is known to be involved in the pathogenesis of HCM. However, the prognostic value of proteins related to EC function (EC‐related proteins) in HCM is unknown.MethodsIn this prospective cohort study of patients with HCM, we measured plasma levels of 90 EC‐related proteins upon enrollment. The primary outcome measure was MACE. The secondary outcome measure was worsening HF. We developed machine learning models based on EC‐related proteins to predict MACE or worsening HF using data from one institution (training set). We tested the predictive ability in independent samples from the other institution (test set) and performed time‐to‐event analyses.ResultsThe study included 722 patients (n=458 in the training set and n=264 in the test set). Using our EC‐related protein‐based model, the area under the receiver‐operating‐characteristic curve to predict MACE was 0.71 (95% CI, 0.64–0.77) and that for worsening HF was 0.71 (95% CI, 0.63–0.79). When we divided the test set into low‐ and high‐risk groups according to the predicted probabilities derived from the training set, the high‐risk groups had significantly higher risks of developing MACE and worsening HF compared with the low‐risk groups (both P logrank<0.001).ConclusionsThe present prospective study demonstrated that EC‐related proteins in plasma predict MACE and worsening HF in patients with HCM. These EC‐related proteins have a potential to become novel biomarkers for risk stratification in HCM to improve current prediction models.

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is one of the most common genetic cardiac diseases, affecting 1 in 200 to 500 people around the world. 1 Whereas some patients with HCM lead a normal life expectancy without developing any symptoms, others experience major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) such as atrial and ventricular arrhythmias, stroke, worsening heart failure (HF), and sudden cardiac death (SCD). Indeed, a recent study reported that, among patients diagnosed with HCM at <40 years of age (about one third of the population with HCM), 90% experience MACE and 65% experience worsening HF during their lifetime. 2 The identification of high‐risk patients with HCM who are more likely to experience MACE or worsening HF is clinically important as it allows for more frequent monitoring and treatment of modifiable factors, such as atrial fibrillation (AF) and obesity, thereby eventually preventing MACE. However, models to predict MACE or worsening HF in patients with HCM focusing on proteins associated with a specific cell type have never been reported.

Endothelial cells (ECs) constitute the vascular endothelium and play a critical role in hemostasis, gas/nutrient exchange, and regulation of blood flow. 3 Therefore, cardiac EC dysfunction is closely associated with heart disease. For instance, cardiac EC dysfunction is an independent predictor of MACE in patients with chronic HF. 4 An association between cardiac EC dysfunction and increased HF severity has been reported in conditions of ventricular dysfunction as well. 5 Furthermore, coronary microvascular dysfunction has been shown to increase MACE in patients with HCM, 6 suggesting the association of cardiac EC dysfunction with worse prognosis in patients with HCM. Despite these studies connecting EC dysfunction with MACE and worsening HF in patient populations with non‐HCM heart disease, the prognostic value of EC‐related proteins in plasma in patients with HCM remains unknown.

Thus, in this multicenter study, we prospectively tested whether plasma circulating EC‐related protein levels at enrollment can predict subsequent MACE or worsening HF in patients with HCM.

METHODS

Because of the sensitive nature of the data collected for this study, requests to access the data set from qualified researchers trained in human subject confidentiality protocols may be sent to Dr. Yuichi J. Shimada at Columbia University Irving Medical Center (CUIMC).

Study Design and Sample

In this prospective cohort study, adults (aged ≥18 years) with an attending physician diagnosis of HCM were included. Participants were enrolled from the HCM programs at CUIMC, New York, NY and Massachusetts General Hospital (Boston, MA) between April 2008 and October 2023 and were consecutively included in this study if phlebotomy for EC‐related proteins measurement was performed. The diagnosis of HCM was established by echocardiographic evidence of left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy (maximum LV wall thickness ≥15 mm) that was out of proportion to systemic loading conditions. For patients with a family history of HCM, LV wall thickness ≥13 mm was considered diagnostic of HCM. 7 Patients with HCM phenocopies such as Fabry disease or cardiac amyloidosis were excluded by performing additional testing (eg, genetic testing, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, technetium‐99 m pyrophosphate scintigraphy imaging, and heart biopsy) when necessary. The EC‐related proteins did not have any missing values. For clinical variables, median imputation was used for missing values.

We collected baseline characteristics at the time of phlebotomy to determine plasma EC‐related protein levels. We obtained echocardiographic and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging parameters from these tests performed closest to the time of the phlebotomy. We obtained echocardiographic images using commercially available ultrasound systems (iE33, Philips Healthcare, Amsterdam, The Netherlands). We performed genetic testing using commercial gene panels for HCM from Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments approved laboratories whose use has previously been validated in HCM. 8 We considered variants categorized as “definitely pathogenic” or “likely pathogenic” to be genotype positive and those categorized as “variant of uncertain significance,” “likely benign,” and “benign” to be genotype negative. 2 The training set to derive the prediction model consisted of patients from Massachusetts General Hospital. The independent test set for validation was based on patient data from CUIMC. The Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board (2015P001953) and that of CUIMC (AAAR5873) approved the study protocol and all participants provided written informed consent.

Blood Sample Processing and Plasma Endothelial Cell‐Related Protein Level Measurement

We drew venous blood specimens at the time of an outpatient clinic visit. We collected samples in K2EDTA‐treated tubes and centrifuged for 10 minutes at 3100 rpm. Immediately after aliquoting, we froze the supernatant plasma at −80 °C. 9

We took 2 steps to identify EC‐related proteins that are included in the SomaScan assay (SomaLogic, Inc., Boulder, CO). 9 , 10 First, we specified 234 EC‐related proteins based on published literature. 11 Second, we found that 90 out of the 234 proteins were included in the SomaScan assay. This assay is highly multiplexed, sensitive, quantitative, and reproducible, with the lower limit of quantification of 100 fM and median coefficient of variance of <5% for human proteins. 12 Table S1 shows the list of these 90 EC‐related proteins analyzed in the present study.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome of MACE was defined as the composite of SCD with or without successful resuscitation, sustained ventricular tachycardia or ventricular fibrillation, new‐onset AF, stroke, and worsening HF as defined later. 13 Successful resuscitation refers to return of spontaneous circulation with external shocks or internal shocks from implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator. The secondary outcome measure was worsening HF, which was included in MACE and defined as death due to HF, heart transplant, HF hospitalization, and any increase in the New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class or HF hospitalization. 14 These definitions for MACE and worsening HF have been used in prior studies as well. 13 , 14

The event rate was expressed as number of events per 100 patient‐years. Two attending cardiologists independently adjudicated events. The first cardiologist reviewed the medical record, and if necessary, performed direct interviews with the treating physicians and patients. The second cardiologist also reviewed the medical record and resolved any disagreements through discussion. There were no disagreements between the cardiologists in terms of event adjudication.

Statistical Analysis

We presented continuous variables as mean±SD if normally distributed and as median (25th–75th percentile) if not normally distributed or ordinal. We tested the normality of each continuous variable using the Shapiro–Wilk test. We presented categorical variables as numbers and percentages. To compare clinical characteristics between patients with and without MACE, we used the unpaired Student’s t test for normally distributed continuous variables and the Mann–Whitney–Wilcoxon test for other continuous and ordinal variables. We used Pearson’s χ2 test for categorical variables. We declared statistical significance if the 2‐sided P value was <0.05.

Development of EC‐Related Protein‐Based Model to Predict MACE or Worsening HF

Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) classification models were used to establish the prediction model for MACE using the aforementioned 90 EC‐related proteins. Data of proteins related to 1 cell type often exhibit sparsity, with only a small fraction of the analyzed proteins significantly contributing to outcome prediction. Moreover, such data typically have high collinearity, with multiple proteins displaying intercorrelations. The LASSO algorithm performs feature selection and regularization simultaneously, offering the capacity to effectively handle both sparse and collinear data. 15 LASSO selects only the most relevant features and outputs relative importance, offering full interpretability which is well‐suited for biomedical studies. 15 Furthermore, LASSO has a substantially lower number of hyperparameters compared with decision tree‐based models, thereby minimizing the risk of model overfitting and ensuring that the model is generalizable and robust. 15 The LASSO regularization technique also reduces the need for bootstrapping or nested cross‐validation. 15 Five‐fold cross‐validation method was applied for the training set using the R caret package. The predictive ability of the model in the independent test set was measured to test the performance of the model developed from the training set. After generating the receiver‐operating‐characteristic (ROC) curve in the test set, the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated as an indicator of the model’s predictive ability. Using an optimal cutoff value of the predicted probability for MACE determined by the nearest point to the top‐left corner of the ROC curve, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value were calculated. The same approach was performed to establish the prediction model for the secondary outcome measure of worsening HF using the 90 EC‐related proteins in the training set, and to test the model performance in the test set. The clinical applicability of both models in the test set was examined via Brier scores and calibration plots. Brier scores range from 0 to 1 and values closer to 0 are considered better calibration whereas scores closer to 1 are considered worse calibration. 16 , 17

Several subgroup and sensitivity analyses were performed. First, the EC‐related protein‐based model for predicting MACE was applied to the test set stratifying by HCM subtypes: obstructive HCM and nonobstructive HCM. Second, the EC‐related protein‐based model to predict MACE or worsening HF was developed after removing EC‐related proteins that were significantly associated with any of the 9 medications listed in Table 1. Third, in the prediction of MACE in the test set, a combined model of the EC‐related protein‐based model plus the HCM‐risk SCD score was developed and compared with the HCM‐risk SCD score alone. Decision curve analysis was further conducted to compare the net benefits of predicting MACE onset with the combined model of the EC‐related protein‐based model plus the HCM‐risk SCD score, the HCM‐risk SCD score alone, and treating all/none. Fourth, in the prediction of MACE, a combined model of the EC‐related protein‐based model plus NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide), an established cardiac biomarker, was developed and compared with the predictive capability of NT‐proBNP alone. Similarly, a combined model of the EC‐related protein‐based model plus troponin I, another established cardiac biomarker, was developed and compared with troponin I alone. Finally, to address the issue of multiple comparisons, another LASSO regression model was developed using only EC‐related proteins that had a significant association with each outcome. The significance was defined by corrected P value <0.05 after the false discovery rate correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure.

Survival Analysis to Identify Patients at High Risk of MACE and Worsening HF

Using the optimal cutoff value of the predicted probability of MACE, we divided the test set into a low‐risk group and a high‐risk group. In this analysis, we used the log‐rank test to compare the Kaplan–Meier survival estimates for patients in the low‐ and high‐risk groups. We developed a Cox proportional hazards model in the test set, to examine the association of the high‐ and low‐risk group for MACE classified at enrollment. We confirmed that the Cox proportional hazards assumption holds via the Schoenfeld residual test. We also developed a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model adjusting for clinical factors that were included in a previously reported prediction model from a large‐scale meta‐analysis. 18 , 19 The clinical factors included were age, NYHA functional class, family history of SCD, syncope, AF, nonsustained ventricular tachycardia, maximum LV wall thickness, and LV outflow tract gradient at rest. 18 , 19 For maximum LV wall thickness, data from the thicker of echocardiography or cardiac magnetic resonance were used. We applied the same survival analysis method to the secondary outcome of worsening HF.

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics and Event Rate

The present study included 722 patients with HCM. Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. Demographically, patients with MACE were more likely to be Hispanic. There was no significant difference in time after HCM diagnosis between patients with MACE and those without. Clinically, patients with MACE were more likely to have higher body mass index, lower diastolic blood pressure, prior sustained ventricular tachycardia or ventricular fibrillation, or AF. They were also likely to be taking β‐blockers, loop diuretics, potassium‐sparing diuretics, or amiodarone. In echocardiographic evaluation, patients with MACE had smaller LV cavity, thicker LV walls, larger left atrium, and higher LV outflow tract gradients, as well as a greater degree of mitral regurgitation. With exercise stress tests, patients with MACE achieved lower metabolic equivalents and peak heart rate during stress. With cardiac magnetic resonance, patients with MACE had larger LV mass. Other demographic and clinical characteristics were similar between the 2 groups.

As shown in Table 2, the primary outcome event of MACE was observed in 192 patients (27%) during the study period (1.3 [0.5–4.0] years). The incidence rate per patient‐year was 14%. This number is in agreement with a previous report on MACE in patients with HCM reporting that the annual incidence rate was ≈10%. 13

Candidate Protein Selection and Model Performance on MACE

The training set to develop the prediction model for MACE comprised 458 patients from Massachusetts General Hospital, of whom 115 patients (25%) subsequently developed MACE. The independent test set for validation consisted of 264 patients from CUIMC, including 77 patients (29%) who subsequently developed MACE. The EC‐related protein‐based model to predict MACE in the training set, comprised of 33 EC‐related proteins selected by the LASSO algorithm, exhibited an AUC of 0.71 in the test set (95% CI, 0.64–0.77; Figure 1A). The cutoff value of the predicted probability for MACE at the nearest point to the top‐left corner of the ROC curve was 21.5%. With this cutoff value, the sensitivity was 0.74 (95% CI, 0.63–0.83) and the specificity was 0.60 (95% CI, 0.52–0.67) (Table 3A). When stratified by HCM subtypes, the AUCs of the test set were 0.65 (95% CI, 0.56–0.75) for obstructive HCM (Figure S1A) and 0.76 (95% CI, 0.66–0.86) for nonobstructive HCM (Figure S1B). Figure 2 displays the relative importance of the top 20 (Figure 2A) among the 33 EC‐related proteins in the prediction model for MACE. Fibulin‐3, lactadherin, and perlecan were the first, second, and third most important proteins in the model to predict MACE.

Sensitivity Analyses

Patients in the test set were categorized to low‐risk group (n=132) or high‐risk group (n=132) according to the cutoff value of the predicted probability. Figure 3 shows the time to the first outcome event in the test set stratified by the group allocation. Survival analysis showed a significant difference in the rate of MACE (Figure 3A) between the low‐risk group and the high‐risk group (P logrank<0.001). Compared with the low‐risk group, the Cox‐proportional hazards model demonstrated that the high‐risk group for MACE had a significantly higher rate of developing MACE, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 2.5 (95% CI, 1.5–4.1, P<0.001; Table 4A), even after adjusting for the 8 clinical parameters (adjusted HR, 1.8 [95% CI, 1.1–3.1], P=0.03; Table 4A). The Schoenfeld residual test yielded a P value of 0.23, confirming that the Cox proportional hazards assumption holds. Calibration assessment resulted in a strong Brier score of 0.19 (Figure S2A).

A total of 53 EC‐related proteins were significantly associated with at least 1 of the 9 medications listed in Table 1. A new machine learning model was developed using the remaining 37 EC‐related proteins. Survival analysis showed a significant difference in the rate of MACE between the low‐risk group and the high‐risk group (P logrank<0.001) (Figure S3A).

Furthermore, the combined model of the EC‐related protein‐based model plus the HCM‐risk SCD score significantly outperformed the HCM‐risk SCD score (combined model, AUC, 0.68 [95% CI, 0.60–0.76]; HCM‐risk SCD score, AUC, 0.58 [95% CI, 0.49–0.67]; DeLong’s P=0.04, Figure S4) in predicting MACE onset in the test set. Decision curve analysis showed that the combined model of the EC‐related protein‐based model plus the HCM‐risk SCD score outperformed the HCM‐risk SCD score alone (Figure S5).

The combined model of the EC‐related protein‐based model plus NT‐proBNP exhibited an AUC of 0.71 [95% CI, 0.65–0.78], significantly outperforming the predictive capability of NT‐proBNP alone with an AUC of 0.66 [95% CI, 0.58–0.73] (DeLong’s P=0.04, Figure S6A). Similarly, the combined model with troponin I exhibited an AUC of 0.71 [95% CI, 0.65–0.78], significantly outperforming the predictive capability of troponin I alone with an AUC of 0.62 [95% CI, 0.55–0.69] (DeLong’s P=0.01, Figure S6B).

A total of 23 EC‐related proteins were significantly associated with MACE after the false discovery rate correction, and a new machine learning model was developed using these 23 EC‐related proteins. Survival analysis showed a significant difference in the rate of MACE between the low‐risk group and the high‐risk group (P logrank<0.001) (Figure S7A).

Candidate Protein Selection, Model Performance, and Sensitivity Analyses on Worsening HF

The same training and test sets were used to develop and validate the prediction model for worsening HF, where a total of 79 patients (17%) within the training set and 60 patients (23%) within the test set subsequently developed worsening HF. The AUC to predict worsening HF was 0.71 (95% CI, 0.63–0.79; Figure 1B). The cutoff value of the predicted probability for worsening HF at the nearest point to the top‐left corner of the ROC curve was 16.0%. With this cutoff value, the sensitivity was 0.63 (95% CI, 0.50–0.75) and the specificity was 0.72 (95% CI, 0.65–0.78) (Table 3B). Figure 2B shows the relative importance of the prediction model for worsening HF, which is composed of 8 proteins selected from the LASSO algorithm. Scavenger receptor class F member 2, growth arrest‐specific 6 protein, and sushi, von Willebrand factor type A, EGF (epidermal growth factor), and SVEP1 (pentraxin domain‐containing protein 1) were the first, second, and third most important proteins in the prediction model for worsening HF.

Patients in the test set were categorized to low‐risk group (n=169) or high‐risk group (n=95) according to the cutoff value of the predicted probability. Survival analysis showed a significant difference in the rate of worsening HF (Figure 3B) between the low‐risk group and the high‐risk group (P logrank<0.001). Compared with the low‐risk group, the Cox‐proportional hazards model demonstrated that the high‐risk group for worsening HF also had a significantly higher rate of developing worsening HF, with a HR of 3.0 (95% CI, 1.8–5.2, P<0.001; Table 4B) and an adjusted HR of 2.6 (95% CI, 1.4–4.8, P=0.003; Table 4B). The Schoenfeld residual test yielded a P value of 0.61, confirming that the Cox proportional hazards assumption holds. Calibration assessment resulted in a strong Brier score of 0.17 (Figure S2B).

Survival analysis showed a significant difference in the rate of worsening HF between the low‐risk group and the high‐risk group after removing proteins associated with medications (P logrank<0.001) (Figure S3B). A total of 27 EC‐related proteins were significantly associated with worsening HF after the false discovery rate correction. A new machine learning model using these 27 EC‐related proteins showed a significant difference in the rate of worsening HF between the low‐risk group and the high‐risk group (P logrank<0.001) (Figure S7B).

DISCUSSION

Summary of Findings

In the present multicenter prospective cohort study of 722 patients with HCM, the EC‐related protein‐based model derived from the training set exhibited reasonable predictive ability to predict MACE or worsening HF in the independent test set for validation. After dividing the test set into low‐ and high‐risk groups according to the prediction model, the high‐risk group had a significantly higher rate of developing MACE or worsening HF. The present study serves as the first to use plasma circulating EC‐related proteins to predict MACE or worsening HF in patients with HCM.

Results in Context

ECs constitute a large portion of noncardiomyocyte cells in the heart, 3 and the dysfunction of EC‐related proteins would result in failure to support the extensive metabolic demands of the myocardium. Indeed, prior studies have linked EC‐related proteins and MACE 4 or worsening HF5 in a patient population of non‐HCM heart disease. However, to date, no studies have been carried out to connect EC‐related proteins and MACE or worsening HF in patients with HCM.

Our models successfully identified EC‐related proteins predictive of MACE and worsening HF. The MACE model revealed that extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins, which support EC function and integrity, play an important role in MACE onset. This finding is in agreement with animal studies reporting ECM dysregulation in severe HCM 20 and ECM from patients with HCM being sufficient to impair naïve myocardium function. 21 Among the highly predictive ECM proteins in our study, as shown in Figure 2A, is fibulin‐3, which promotes the development of a profibrotic microenvironment in the progression of myocardial fibrosis. 22 Although there are no studies explicitly reporting an association between fibulin‐3 and HCM, fibulin‐2, which belongs to the same fibulin family of ECM proteins as fibulin‐3, has been shown to be upregulated in patients with HCM. 23 Lactadherin, or milk fat globule‐EGF factor 8, also emerged as highly predictive of prognosis in the present study. Animal studies suggest lactadherin to play a protective role in endothelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition, leading to myocardial fibrosis. 24 Perlecan, which has been shown to promote hypertrophic growth of cardiomyocytes, 25 is also included.

The worsening HF model highlights several EC‐related proteins as important to predict worsening HF. Among the top 3 proteins shown in Figure 2B is scavenger receptor class F member 2, which has recently been discovered to exhibit scavenging functions such as endocytosis. 26 Growth arrest‐specific 6 protein, known to worsen pressure overload‐induced cardiac hypertrophy, 27 follows on the list. SVEP1 is also included. SVEP1 is an ECM and cell adhesion protein involved in calcium and chromatin binding and has been linked to SCD risk factors including coronary heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, and AF. 28 Recent work from our laboratory has shown that adding plasma SVEP1 levels improves the performance of conventional models in predicting HCM prognosis including MACE. 29

This study serves as the first to report the relevance of many of the aforementioned key predictive proteins, such as fibulin‐3 and lactadherin, in the context of HCM. The prior reports in a non‐HCM context lend significant support to the biological plausibility that these proteins may serve as novel circulating biomarkers to predict future events in HCM.

Clinical Significance of Identifying High‐Risk Patients With HCM

A recent study reported that, among patients diagnosed with HCM at <40 years of age (about one third of the population with HCM), 90% experience MACE and 65% experience worsening HF during their lifetime, 2 highlighting its high cumulative prevalence. The identification of high‐risk patients with HCM who are more likely to experience MACE or worsening HF is clinically important as it allows for the communication with patients and families on the possibility of “any major event” happening in the future. Furthermore, the identification of high‐risk patients with HCM allows for more frequent monitoring and intervention against modifiable factors, eventually preventing MACE or worsening HF, and enhancing the patients’ quality of life.

Examples of available treatments to prevent MACE include implantable cardioverter‐defibrillators that can effectively and reliably abort potentially lethal ventricular tachycardia/ventricular fibrillation, thereby reducing the incidence of SCD and extending longevity in the population with HCM. 30 Also, initiation of anticoagulation based on early detection of AF (which increases the risk of stroke by ≈18‐fold in HCM 31 ) is known to prevent stroke. 7 , 32 In terms of available treatment to prevent worsening HF, bariatric surgery in obese patients is known to prevent HF exacerbation in the general population and specifically in patients with HCM. 33 For symptomatic patients with obstructive HCM, the newest class of medications, cardiac myosin inhibitors, may be effective in preventing LV remodeling and slowing disease progression. 34 Indeed, the cardiac myosin inhibitor mavacamten has been shown to be associated with improvement in exercise capacity, LV outflow tract obstruction, NYHA functional class, and health status.34 Finally, for patients with the highest risk of HF progression, early referral for transplant evaluation has been shown to improve prognosis. 35

As such, identification of high‐risk patients with HCM who are more likely to develop MACE or worsening HF using biomarkers that can be noninvasively obtained with low cost (eg, blood) is critically needed. However, there are currently no established models to predict MACE or worsening HF in patients with HCM. Our study would serve as the first step toward the establishment of prediction models using a small set of circulating EC‐related proteins, with high potential for application in clinical settings. Furthermore, EC‐related proteins are associated with myocardial fibrosis 36 , 37 and LV hypertrophy, 38 , 39 which leads to atrial fibrillation, stroke, HF, and sudden cardiac death, 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 making prediction of MACE based on EC‐related proteins biologically plausible.

Strengths of the Present Study

In the present prospective, multicenter study, the largest number of patients with HCM who underwent measurement of plasma EC‐related proteins was enrolled. The quality of outcome adjudication is ensured by the prospective study design, and generalizability is enhanced given the multicenter nature. Generalizability of our model was further demonstrated by reproducing its predictive ability in the independent, external test set for validation. The predictive ability of the EC‐related protein‐based model was preserved irrespective of HCM subtypes. Predictability was retained when using only the EC‐related proteins with a significant false discovery rate ‐corrected association with each outcome as well. The combination of the EC‐related protein‐based model and the HCM‐risk SCD score outperformed the HCM‐risk SCD score on the decision curve analysis, highlighting its clinical relevance. The combination of the EC‐related protein‐based model and an established cardiac biomarker also outperformed the established cardiac biomarker in predicting MACE. Finally, the present study uses the SomaScan assay to measure plasma levels of the 90 EC‐related proteins. However, a quick and low‐cost measurement of plasma EC‐related proteins can be achieved with the sandwich enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay. Therefore, our prediction models with a small panel of plasma EC‐related proteins have a high potential for application in clinical settings, such as shared decision‐making with patients about the necessity of more frequent monitoring and referral to specialists.

Potential Limitations

Several potential limitations in the current study should be kept in mind. First, the study was conducted at 2 high‐volume tertiary care centers, with some patients having advanced HCM. Therefore, our findings may not be generalizable to patients with less advanced HCM or those treated at community hospitals or nonspecialized centers. Second, misclassifications of outcomes were possible although we minimized misclassification by adopting a prospective study design and conducting rigorous event adjudication with multiple attending cardiologists. Third, the possibility of informative censoring could not be excluded. Fourth, plasma samples were collected only at a single time point. Examination of the association between measurements at different time points and prognosis could be informative. Fifth, data on the time of the first symptom onset were not collected. Time between symptom onset and diagnosis may affect the biomarker levels and prognosis. Sixth, the present prediction models do not provide cutoff values of each EC‐related protein for stratifying patients at high risk. Seventh, although the risk of MACE was higher in Hispanic patients, subgroup analysis could not be performed due to the small number of Hispanic patients in the test set (n=35). Eighth, although our study had sufficient statistical power, our sample had a median follow‐up of 2.3 years, which was relatively short for a disease like HCM with slow progression. Ninth, the NYHA functional class is a subjective measure. Nonetheless, we believe that subjective symptoms matter most to the patients as they determine their quality of life, making an increase in the NYHA class a meaningful criterion to detect worsening HF. Finally, the EC‐related proteins in plasma do not necessarily come from the heart. Nonetheless, the plasma circulating EC‐related proteins reflect the status of the cardiovascular system as a whole, and we find it to be relevant for the purpose of our study—prognostication in HCM.

CONCLUSIONS

The present study serves as the first to demonstrate the ability of plasma EC‐related proteins to predict MACE or worsening HF in patients with HCM. The prediction models have the potential to help physicians identify patients with HCM who are at high risk of MACE or worsening HF and enable necessary interventions to prevent its onset. Additionally, our study would prompt further mechanistic research on individual EC‐related proteins predictive of MACE and worsening HF in HCM. This could in turn contribute to the development of targeted therapeutics.
