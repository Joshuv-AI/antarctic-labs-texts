The Endothelial Glycocalyx: The First Line of Defense in the Prevention and Treatment of Kidney Diseases
Authors: Xiaoyu Yuan, Mengzhen Jia, Li Zhou, Gailing Hou, Bing Zhang, Xinshou Ouyang, Yanjie Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe endothelial glycocalyx (EG) is widely located on the surface of endothelial cell membrane. It serves as an important component of the endothelial cell barrier and plays a crucial role in maintaining charge balance within the cell. The shedding, excessive thickening and deposition of the EG located on the surface of endothelial cells within the renal capillary network is widely observed in acute kidney injury and chronic kidney disease. Based on recent research advancements, this article focuses on how the EG in the renal microvasculature participates in pathological processes such as renal tubulointerstitial fibrosis, cross-talk between endothelial cells and epithelial cells, and immune system dysregulation. Preserving and restoring the integrity of the EG in the renal microvasculature has emerged as an innovative strategy for the early prevention and treatment of kidney diseases. This includes supplementation of EG components and inhibition of EG shedding ingredients to prevent EG damage.Index Words: Acute kidney injury (AKI), chronic kidney disease (CKD), cross-talk, endothelial glycocalyx (EG), fibrosis

As a glycoprotein located on the surface of endothelial cells, the endothelial glycocalyx (EG) is composed of proteoglycans, glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), glycoproteins, and soluble components (Fig 1A).1 EG in the kidney is classified into 6 types based on their location: EG within the glomerular capillaries, EG around the peritubular capillaries, EG in the extracellular matrix, EG in the lymphatic capillaries, EG in large arteries and veins, and EG in the medulla. This article focuses primarily on the EG within glomerular capillaries, with occasional reference to EG around the peritubular capillaries and in the matrix.2 EG of glomerular capillaries, as the first layer of the filtration barrier, play a significant role in early filtration function and participate in the formation of proteinuria.3,4 The shedding of the EG is significantly associated with kidney diseases,5 cardiovascular diseases,6 liver diseases,7 and sepsis-associated kidney injury,8 representing an early pathological change in kidney diseases. Ramnath et al9 discovered that in animal models of diabetic nephropathy, injury to EG of glomerular capillaries occurred prior to other ultrastructural changes, such as thickening of the glomerular basement membrane, increased podocyte hiatus membrane and foot process width, and increased permeability of individual glomerular capillaries. In immortalized renal endothelial cells, the removal of EG using heparinase reduces endothelial cells’ transendothelial resistance by 59%, increases albumin flux by 207%, and enhances glomerular filtration barrier permeability.10 Hence, restoring EG is considered the first line of defense in preventing various kidney diseases, but currently, there is a lack of evidence from studies concerning the structure, physiological and pathological effects or knockout of EG genes. This review aims to summarize the potential therapeutic targets for maintaining and restoring EG integrity based on the pathological processes of EG injury involved in renal tubulointerstitial fibrosis, cross-talk between glomerular endothelial cells and epithelial cells, and immune system dysfunction, to enable exploration of new strategies for the prevention and treatment of kidney diseases.

The Complex Structure and Function of the EG

Proteoglycans

Proteoglycans serve as the structural backbone of EG, comprising protein cores that undergo extensive glycosylation modifications. There are 2 types of membrane proteins: syndecans (SDCs) and glypicans. SDCs can be further divided into 4 subtypes, whereas the latter comprises 6 subtypes.11 SDC is a transmembrane protein composed of intracellular, transmembrane, and extracellular domains. The intracellular domain of SDC consists of 3 parts, located at the proximal (C1), central (V), and distal (C2) regions. Although the C1 and C2 regions are highly conserved, the V region exhibits variability. The C1 domain connects to the actin cytoskeleton and regulates cell adhesion and migration. The C2 domain has the capability to interact with proteins containing PSD95-Discs large-ZO1 domains, such as syntenin, participating in exosome formation. The serine-glycine sequences in the extracellular domain of SDC are covalently linked to GAG chains and may also be linked to GAGs via threonine and proline residues. Glypicans are non-transmembrane proteins that anchor to the cell membrane via glycosylphosphatidylinositol anchors. Their extracellular domains are exclusively linked to heparan sulfate (HS) chains. Glypican-1 is the sole glypican expressed on endothelial cells (Fig 1A).12 These functionally distinct domains work together to maintain the physiological roles of EG.

Glycosaminoglycans

GAGs consist of 5 forms: HS, hyaluronic acid (HA), chondroitin sulfate, keratan sulfate, and dermatan sulfate. HS and HA are the most extensively studied components. HS is composed of repeating disaccharide units of glucuronic acid and N-acetylglucosamine, with chain lengths ranging from 50-200 disaccharide units, constituting 50%-90% of GAGs. HA is the longest sugar chain among GAGs, composed of repeating units of glucuronic acid and N-acetylglucosamine, forming a hexagonal mesh structure. HS is situated at the intersections of the HA mesh structure.13 HA is connected to the cell membrane via CD44, and no binding sites between HA and SDC have been identified,14,15 but a study conducted in 2019 demonstrated that knockout of hyaluronan synthase 2 (HAS2) leads to the loss of EG on the surface of glomerular endothelial cells, indicating that HA plays a critical role in maintaining the stability of EG.16 Inflammation and glucose metabolism can influence HA synthesis via HAS2.17 Inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, TNF-α, and TGF-β upregulate HAS2 expression, thereby increasing HA synthesis.18,19 Conversely, genetic knockout of glucose dehydrogenase or inhibition of the NF-κB pathway destabilizes HAS2. Additionally, glycosylation at serine 221 of HAS2 enhances its stability, whereas phosphorylation at threonine 110 or threonine 328 reduces its stability (Fig 2).17 GAGs undergo self-modification through N-deacetylation, N-sulfation, epimerization, and O-sulfation.20 The sulfate groups on the disaccharide units of GAGs possess negative charges, which not only repels negatively charged molecules, preventing their leakage from endothelial cells, but also inhibits interactions between leukocytes and platelets with the endothelium, thereby protecting against endothelial cell injury (Fig 1A).21

The Shedding of EG

EG Enzymes Degrading EG

Enzymes that degrade EG include matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), heparanase, and hyaluronidase. MMPs, zinc-dependent endopeptidases, are capable of cleaving SDC.22 Similarly, in streptozotocin-induced type 1 diabetic mice, MMP-2 and MMP-9 can induce early shedding of SDC-4, thereby enhancing the permeability of glomerular capillaries.9 In conditionally immortalized human glomerular endothelial cells, inhibition of MMP-9 using inhibitors or silencing MMP-9 expression with siRNA can suppress TNF-α-induced shedding of SDC-4.23 Heparanase can cleave HS into fragments of 4-7 kDa.24 A significant correlation exists between heparanase and HS shedding in renal tissues of patients with diabetic nephropathy, as well as in the kidneys of streptozotocin-induced diabetic rats and mice. Overexpression of heparanase in diabetic mice can result in complete shedding of HS.25 Inhibition of heparanase using inhibitors or genetic knockout of heparanase can reduce EG shedding in diabetic nephropathy mice. This can alleviate renal damage in these mice.26 In mouse models of glomerulonephritis induced by lipopolysaccharides or anti-mouse glomerular basement membrane IgG serum, genetic knockout of heparanase prevented the reduction in glomerular HS expression and preserved renal function.27 In a rat model of Heymann nephritis, Levidiotis et al28 demonstrated that anti-heparanase antibodies significantly reduce HS shedding and decreases proteinuria. Similarly, in the same rat model, the heparanase inhibitor PI-88 also markedly diminished HS shedding and proteinuria.29 Hyaluronidases degrade HA, and there are 6 types present in the human body: HYAL1, HYAL2, HYAL3, HYAL4, SPAM1, and HYAL6. HYAL1 and HYAL2 are found in most tissues such as cardiovascular, pulmonary, and renal tissues. HYAL1 is predominantly located on the cell membrane and degrades extracellular HA polymers. After degradation, small HA fragments are endocytosed into the cells and further degraded into smaller molecules by intracellular HYAL2.30 Harada et al31 demonstrated using HEK293 cells that HA could only be cleaved by HYAL1 and HYAL2 in the presence of CD44. In streptozotocin-induced diabetic mice, genetic knockout of HYAL1 better preserved the thickness of the EG, prevented HA shedding, maintained vascular endothelial function, and protected the glomerular filtration barrier.32 Therefore, inhibiting the expression levels of the aforementioned shedding enzymes to protect the EG from degradation may be a key strategy in preventing and treating kidney endothelial cell injury (Table 1).

Cytokines Promoting the Shedding of EG by Upregulating Heparanase Expression

In mice with streptozotocin-induced diabetic nephropathy, inhibition of monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 reduced the secretion of tissue cathepsin L by macrophages and decreased heparanase secretion. This intervention facilitated the restoration of EG and reduced proteinuria.34 In the human umbilical vein endothelial cell line EA.hy926, the addition of Angpt-2 increased heparanase release via the ROCK signaling pathway and reduced the immunofluorescence intensity of EG on the endothelial cell surface. Injection of Angpt-2 into the backs of C57BL/6J mice revealed increased vascular permeability, leading to back edema and an increase in neutrophil infiltration due to EG shedding.35 In diabetic nephropathy mice, endothelin-1 produced by endothelial cells stimulated podocytes to secrete heparanase, leading to the shedding of EG and an increase in urinary protein. Podocyte-specific knockout of endothelin receptor A reduced podocyte heparanase expression, alleviating EG damage and urinary protein.36 In patients with diabetic nephropathy, the use of endothelin receptor A antagonists, a novel therapeutic agent for kidney disease, can restore the EG and reduce proteinuria.37 In primary podocyte diseases or adriamycin-induced nephropathy mouse models, the use of endothelin receptor A antagonists or clearance of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species can prevent pathological cross-talk between podocytes and endothelial cells. This reduces EG shedding and improves focal segmental glomerulosclerosis.38 Therefore, atrasentan, an endothelin-A receptor antagonist, is expected to treat kidney diseases by preventing EG shedding in clinical practice. Exploring drugs that can inhibit the expression of monocyte chemoattractant protein-1, Angpt-2, and other cytokines or antagonize their receptors will help alleviate urinary protein and other kidney damage symptoms caused by EG shedding (Fig 3).

Metabolites Damaging EG

The shedding of EG is also associated with high concentrations of structurally and functionally altered high-density lipoprotein, high glucose levels, and aldosterone in the internal environment. In hemodialysis patients, structurally and functionally altered high-density lipoprotein caused significant damage to EG in vitro in a dose-dependent manner.39 In diabetic nephropathy mice, high glucose can induce an accumulation of advanced glycation end products. These products bind to the receptor for advanced glycation end products, activate the NF-κB signaling pathway, induce an increase in podocyte heparanase expression, and lead to endothelial cell HS shedding.40 In a study conducted by Butler et al,41 it was found that EG increased aldosterone levels in mice blood, which enhanced the activity of renal glomerular MMP-2, promoted EG shedding, reduced SDC-4 expression, and impaired renal function. Furthermore, the addition of aldosterone to human renal glomerular endothelial cells can also induce EG shedding. A study conducted in 2022 demonstrated that in human umbilical vein endothelial cells cultured with uremic serum from patients with chronic kidney disease, the use of aldosterone receptor antagonists could alleviate EG damage and mitigate endothelial dysfunction.42 In rat endothelial cells, albumin-conjugated sphingosine-1-phosphate binds to the receptor S1PR1, thereby inhibiting MMP activity, preserving EG integrity, and maintaining vascular permeability.43 In summary, high-density lipoprotein, high blood sugar, aldosterone, and other metabolic products can promote EG shedding through various pathways, thereby accelerating renal damage.

Other Factors Inducing EG Shedding

Research has found that nuclear proteins, protein kinases, transcription factors, and microRNAs within cells are also closely associated with EG shedding. The inhibition of high mobility group box 1 protein expression or blockade of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB/p65 signaling pathway in primary endothelial cells isolated from human kidneys and cultured with plasma from patients with lupus nephritis, as well as in lupus nephritis mice, reduced EG shedding, significantly decreased endothelial cell permeability, and alleviated glomerular endothelial cell injury and proteinuria.44 In studies utilizing the same animal and cell models, researchers found that the protein kinase B pathway could induce enhanced activation of the FoxO1 transcription factor, expression of Tripartite Motif 27, and promotion of EG shedding.45 Endothelial progenitor cells from septic mice released extracellular vesicles containing microRNA-21-5p. These vesicles silenced the RUNX transcription factor 1, reduced shedding of SDC-1, and alleviated acute kidney injury (AKI) in mice.46

Shedding and Overexpression of EG Located in Renal Capillary Endothelial Cells Contributing to Tubulointerstitial Fibrosis

Renal interstitial fibrosis represents a terminal consequence of kidney diseases. Inflammation is a key pathological feature of renal tubulointerstitial fibrosis. The TGF-β/NF-κB pathway is a crucial pathway for inflammation activation. SDC-4, as a downstream target of this pathway, plays a significant role in the fibrotic process, thereby linking inflammation to fibrosis.47 The deposition of the extracellular domain of SDC-4 in the tubulointerstitial and glomerular basement membrane regions can directly induce the differentiation of myofibroblasts.48 Additionally, SDC-4 can function as a cell surface transporter, facilitating the translocation of transglutaminase 2 (TG2) to the extracellular space, where TG2 promotes fibrosis through its non-enzymatic functions. TG2, a member of the transglutaminase enzyme family, catalyzes the acyl transfer reaction between γ-glutamylamide and the ε-amino group of lysine. This results in stable ε-(γ-glutamylamide)-lysine isopeptide cross-links,49,50 thereby enhancing arginine-glycine-aspartate-independent cell adhesion.51 In SIRT1endo−/− mice, the activation of TGF-β/NF-κB in kidney becomes linked with the increase of SDC-4 expression. SIRT1endo−/− mice exhibit characteristics of endothelial dysfunction and kidney fibrosis, rendering them a widely utilized model for studying defects in endothelial cells. Sirtuin 1, encoded by SIRT1, is a nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide-dependent histone deacetylase that regulates suppress the transcriptional activity of NF-κB.52,53 Lipphardt et al.48 stimulated TGF-β in SIRT1endo−/− mice and conducted mass spectrometry analysis on histone secretion from renal capillary endothelial cells. They found a notable enrichment of the extracellular domain of SDC-4. Additionally, in vitro experiments demonstrated that the addition of the extracellular domain of SDC-4 to the culture medium promoted the transformation of renal fibroblasts into myofibroblasts. Thus, shedding of the SDC-4 extracellular domain may be a major driver of tubulointerstitial fibrosis. In the unilateral ureteral obstruction mouse model, NF-κB activation increased SDC-4 mRNA expression and surface immunofluorescence intensity on capillary endothelial cells, and Scarpellini et al54 confirmed that high SDC-4 expression contributed to tubulointerstitial fibrosis. Furthermore, immunofluorescence colocalization studies in the unilateral ureteral obstruction mouse model and aristolochic acid nephropathy mouse model revealed colocalization of SDC-4 and HS with TG2 in the tubulointerstitium and glomerular basement membrane.54 In SDC-4 knockout mice, renal immunofluorescence analysis showed that SDC-4 shedding reduced TG2 activity, increased TG2 accumulation within cells, decreased TG2 levels at the cell membrane, and impaired TG2 transport. This also inhibited TGF-β activation, reduced local inflammatory responses, and suppressed collagen deposition in both the tubulointerstitial and basement membrane regions. However, the source of SDC-4 may not be restricted to endothelial cells of renal capillaries.54 Additionally, Wee et al55 used a nephropathy mouse model induced by aristolochic acid and administered anti-ASGM1 or anti-NK1.1 antibodies to deplete natural killer cells. This approach led to an elevated expression of both SDC-4 and TG2, and significantly exacerbated tubulointerstitial fibrosis (Fig 4).

Therefore, SDC-4 may serve as a key link in the pathogenesis of interstitial inflammation and fibrosis in kidney diseases. Targeting SDC-4 could potentially emerge as a novel therapeutic approach to inhibit interstitial fibrosis.

Thickened EG Promoting Immune System Dysregulation

In certain immune-related kidney diseases, excessive thickening of the EG occurs, which primarily functions as a "molecular concentrator" to amplify immune responses. In lupus nephritis and other immune-related nephritis diseases, the pathogenesis involves the activation of the complement system and the infiltration of immune cells. Complement can bind to HS, while HA interacts with the CD44 receptor on T cells. Both HS and HA play crucial roles in regulating the renal immune environment.56 GAGs can bind to chemokines in a length-dependent manner, thereby increasing the local concentration of inhibitory chemokines and amplifying the inflammatory response.57 Mice with mutations in the binding sites of EG glycan chains and chemokines exhibited a decrease in the aggregation of leukocytes, monocytes, and macrophages.58 In the complement system, complement factor H (CFH) acts as a negative regulator, whereas properdin serves as a positive regulator. Both can bind to HS. The degree of sulfation of HS determines the ratio of CFH and properdin binding to HS, thereby maintaining the balance between CFH and properdin and the homeostasis of the immune system.59,60 The binding sites of CFH to HS are located in CCP6-8 and CCP19-20.59 In the human kidney, CCP19-20 serves as the primary binding site for CFH to HS.61 In atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, mutations in CFH prevent its binding to GAGs, thereby reducing the ability of CFH to regulate the complement system. This leads to complement accumulation and aberrant activation of the immune system.62 Factor H-related proteins (FHR) act as competitive inhibitors of CFH by competing with CFH for binding to GAGs.63 Under normal circumstances, both factors are in a balanced state. CFH/FHR gene rearrangements enhance FHR binding to GAGs, resulting in an excessive activation of the complement system.64, 65, 66 In recent years, there has been a growing acknowledgment of the role of EG in immune cell aggregation.67,68 In mice with podocyte-associated Alport syndrome69,70 and lupus nephritis,41,44,71 the administration of hyaluronidase reduced immune cell homing and alleviated albumin leakage. Evidence from gene knockout studies of enzymes that catalyze the sulfation of HS chains indicates that HS is closely related to the infiltration of inflammatory cells (Fig 2).72 In mice with lupus nephritis, high-resolution in vivo imaging has demonstrated that thickened HA can bind to the T cell surface receptor CD44, resulting in increased T cell aggregation in the glomeruli, leading to local immune disorders. The administration of hyaluronidase and heparinase can block T cell homing (Fig 2).73

In summary, HS and HA are closely associated with immune dysregulation in kidney diseases. Targeting HS synthase and HAS2 represents a crucial therapeutic strategy.

Participation of EG in the Cross-talk Between Endothelial Cells of Renal Capillary and Epithelial Cells

EG Involvement in Cross-talk Between Endothelial Cells of Renal Capillary and Renal Tubular Epithelial Cells

Injury and necrosis of renal tubular epithelial cells are primary pathological features in the plasma and urine of patients with AKI, suggesting a potential association between EG and renal tubular epithelial cell injury.74, 75, 76 Among 201 patients with decompensated heart failure, those who developed AKI exhibited higher serum concentrations of SDC-1 compared to those who did not develop AKI (area under the curve [AUC], 0.741; P < 0.001).77 A study involving 250 patients undergoing cardiac valve surgery revealed that a preoperative serum SDC-1 concentration >90 ng/mL may indicate severe AKI after cardiac valve surgery (AUC, 0.714; P = 0.009).67 In a study involving 289 children undergoing cardiac surgery, postoperative serum SDC-1 levels were found to be significant in diagnosing AKI (AUC, 0.77; P = 0.004).78 A study conducted in 2021 demonstrated that in a cohort involving 721 patients who underwent cardiac surgery, serum and urine concentrations of SDC-1 could be utilized to predict progressive AKI (AUC, 0.847; P < 0.001).79 In patients with AKI, the serum SDC-1 concentration within 2 hours of admission was significantly associated with severe AKI.80 In patients with sepsis, high concentrations of SDC-1 and thrombomodulin predicted the 90-day mortality rate.81 Puskarich et al82 and Uchimido et al8 both noted the identical phenomenon in patients with sepsis. In adult pig experiments conducted in vitro, immunofluorescence staining of renal histological sections revealed significant shedding of GAGs.83 However, there is currently limited research on how EG participates in the mechanism of renal tubular epithelial cell injury. In lipopolysaccharide-induced AKI mice, genetic knockout of TNF receptor 1 inhibited the shedding of EG by suppressing TNF and heparinase. This action blocked the presence of endothelial cell injury during AKI, indicating potential cross-talk between endothelial cells and tubular epithelial cells.84 A study demonstrated that in mouse cardiac allografts, shed HS promoted the infiltration of T cells into local tissues85 (Fig 3).

EG Involvement in Cross-talk Between Endothelial Cells and Podocytes in Glomerulus

Podocytes, the epithelial cells of the renal glomerulus, share common signaling pathways with endothelial cells, forming the basis for cross-talk between them. As one of the major components on the surface of endothelial cells, EG plays a crucial role facilitating this cross-talk. First, injury to the endothelial EG can indirectly lead to damage to podocytes. Second, shed enzymes secreted by podocytes can promote the shedding of EG from endothelial cells, thereby increasing the permeability of the filtration membrane. In diabetic nephropathy mice, the loss of EG was observed prior to podocyte injury and was positively associated with increased albumin permeability.9,34 In mice with podocyte autophagy deficiency, there were more damaged podocytes near the impaired endothelial cells, and podocyte injury became more severe after the disruption of EG by neuraminidase.86 However, there is still a lack of research on how damaged endothelial cells exacerbate podocyte damage. Podocytes can generate vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-A and VEGF-C. In a study conducted by Oltean et al,87 it was found that in patients with diabetic nephropathy and better renal function, the expression of VEGF-A 165b mRNA was upregulated. This upregulation promoted the phosphorylation of VEGF receptor-2 in renal glomerular endothelial cells, thereby reducing EG damage and protecting renal function. In cultured human conditionally immortalized glomerular endothelial cells, both VEGF-A and VEGF-C are capable of promoting the synthesis of HA. Additionally, VEGF-A can increase the anions in EG, thereby protecting the endothelial cells’ charge barrier.88, 89, 90

In summary, further research is needed to elucidate how EG participates in cross-talk between endothelial and epithelial cells. However, targeting TNF receptor 1 and endothelin receptor A can protect EG by inhibiting sheddase activity.

Restoring EG Integrity by Supplementing EG Components

The current mainstream method for treating EG damage involves supplementing EG components. Drugs such as sulodexide have been approved for marketing. Additionally, some hospitals utilize proprietary formulations primarily composed of EG components in clinical practice and have reported positive outcomes. Sulodexide, composed of low molecular weight heparin and dermatan sulfate, was initially used for the antithrombotic treatment of venous ulceration and recurrent venous thromboembolism.91 The compound is currently undergoing clinical trials aimed at the treatment of diabetic nephropathy.92 It can inhibit the expression of TGF-β2 and acetyl heparin sulfate in tubular epithelial cells, thereby preventing the transformation of epithelial cells into mesenchymal cells.93 There was a significant enhancement in the regeneration of EG, which correlated positively with the survival rate of the mice.94 In 2 multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials, sulodexide showed no significant difference from placebo in reducing proteinuria and kidney damage in diabetic nephropathy patients.33,95 In one reported case, a 54-year-old male patient with minimal change disease, unresponsive to steroids and cyclosporine, achieved complete remission without relapse after using a proprietary formula primarily composed of EG.96

Prospects

In conclusion, the strong correlation between EG damage and the onset and progression of various kidney diseases highlights the significance of understanding mechanisms of EG shedding. Experimental evidence suggests that certain enzymes, including heparanase, play a pivotal role in EG shedding. Specifically, studies have shown that the knockout of the heparanase gene can effectively prevent EG shedding and mitigate renal injury in diabetic mice. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting heparanase to preserve EG integrity and prevent renal complications associated with EG damage. Specific knockout or antagonism of endothelin receptor A in podocytes has been demonstrated to reduce podocyte heparanase expression. This reduction alleviates EG damage and proteinuria while simultaneously preventing pathological interactions between podocytes and endothelial cells. Ultimately, this intervention shows promise in improving experimental focal segmental glomerulosclerosis. Evidence from genetic knockout studies targeting SDC-4 indicates that the reduction of extracellular transport of TG may mitigate renal fibrosis. Moreover, the thickening EG appears to play a role in immune responses. There is a notable gap in experimental research concerning the specific mechanisms underlying EG involvement in renal tubular epithelial cell injury. Further studies should be imperative to explore whether nuclear proteins, transcription factors, protein kinases, and microRNAs act as upstream or downstream regulators in EG shedding. Clarifying these regulatory pathways will provide valuable insights into the pathogenesis of renal epithelial cell injury and may uncover therapeutic targets for kidney diseases.
