Disparities in Heart Disease Mortality Among Asian American and Pacific Islander Breast Cancer Survivors, 2000 to 2019
Authors: Jacqueline B Vo, Carolyn Brandt, Kekoa Taparra, Cody Ramin, Amy Berrington de González, Gretchen L Gierach, Wayne R Lawrence, Jongeun Rhee, Meredith S Shiels, Lene HS Veiga, Paloma R Mitra, Jaimie Z Shing, Stella S Yi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical AbstractKey Words: Asian American, disparities, heart disease mortality, Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islander, Pacific Islander, race and ethnicity

Risk for cardiovascular disease (CVD) is an increasing concern among breast cancer survivors because of shared risk factors and treatment-related cardiotoxicity (eg, anthracyclines, trastuzumab, chest radiotherapy).1 There are well-established racial and ethnic disparities in CVD risk among breast cancer survivors due to complex, multidisciplinary cardio-oncology care,2 yet there are limited data for disaggregated Asian and Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander breast cancer survivors, who constitute 2 different racial groups. Pacific Islander women experience a 3-fold higher heart disease mortality rate compared with Asian American women in the general population.3 To understand how these disparities extend to breast cancer survivors and inform clinically relevant survivorship care, the aim of this retrospective cohort study was to compare risks for heart disease mortality among 59,712 Asian American and 4,948 Pacific Islander women diagnosed with invasive, primary breast cancer (18-84 years of age, from 2000 to 2018, survived ≥1 year, known cause of death) by race and ethnogeographic region using data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) 17 registries.

This study was exempt from National Institutes of Health (NIH) Institutional Review Board review. Race and ethnicity were identified by medical record self-report or death certificates. Follow-up time began at 1 year after breast cancer diagnosis until heart disease death, other cause of death, loss to follow-up, or end of SEER follow-up (December 31, 2019). We calculated the age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) per 1,000 person-years standardized to the 2000 U.S. standard population. We used cause-specific Cox proportional hazards models to calculate HRs and 95% CIs comparing risks for heart disease mortality by race and Asian ethnogeographic region: East Asian American (reference; n = 23,844), South Asian American (n = 5,847), Southeast Asian American (n = 21,572), and other or unknown Asian American (n = 8,449). Models used attained age as the time scale and adjusted for radiotherapy, chemotherapy, stage, estrogen receptor [ER] status, and diagnosis year. For absolute risks, we estimated the 10-year cumulative incidence of heart disease mortality, accounting for non–heart disease deaths as competing events. Analyses were conducted using SEER∗Stat version 8.4.3 (National Cancer Institute) and Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp). Two-sided P values <0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance.

Over 7.25 median follow-up years, there were 813 heart disease deaths, and the ASMR was 0.94 per 1,000 person-years among Asian American women (East Asian, 0.98; South Asian, 1.42; Southeast Asian, 1.03; and other or unknown, 0.77) (Figure 1). Over 6.67 median follow-up years, there were 153 heart disease deaths, and the ASMR was 2.59 per 1,000 person-years among Pacific Islander women. Mean age was similar for both races, but Pacific Islander women were more likely to be diagnosed at later stages, with ER+ and progesterone receptor–positive breast cancer, and to receive chemotherapy plus radiotherapy. Among Asian American women, South Asian American women were younger, and South Asian and Southeast Asian American women were more likely to be diagnosed at advanced stages, with ER− or progesterone receptor–negative breast cancers, and receive chemotherapy plus radiotherapy.

Cause-specific HRs for heart disease mortality and 95% CIs comparing Pacific Islander (n = 4,948) with Asian American breast cancer survivors and by Asian American ethnogeographic region: East Asian American (Chinese, Japanese, and Korean; n = 23,844; reference), South Asian American (Asian Indian and Pakistani; n = 5,847), Southeast Asian American (Filipino, Vietnamese, Laotian, Kampuchean, Hmong, and Thai; n = 21,572), and other or unknown Asian American (n = 8,449). Models were calculated using attained age as the time scale and adjusted for radiotherapy receipt (yes, no, or unknown), chemotherapy receipt (yes, no, or unknown), stage at diagnosis (localized, regional, distant, or unknown), estrogen receptor status (positive, negative, unknown, or missing), and calendar year of diagnosis (2000-2005, 2006-2011, or 2012-2018). Data are suppressed if <10 events. HER2 = human epidermal growth factor receptor 2.

By race, heart disease mortality risk among Pacific Islander breast cancer survivors was >2 times higher than among Asian American women (HR: 2.72; 95% CI: 2.28-3.23). Most notable elevated risks were among those diagnosed at <65 years of age, with regional or distant stage, who received chemotherapy alone, and within 1 to 4 years of diagnosis (HR range: 3.31-4.89). By Asian ethnogeographic region, South Asian and Southeast Asian American women were 61% and 25%, respectively, more likely to die of heart disease than East Asian American women. Elevated heart disease mortality risks were prominent for South Asian American women 65 years and older, localized stage, ER+, and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2–negative breast cancer, after surgery only or left-sided radiotherapy, and within 1 to 5 years (HR range: 1.61-2.14) and for Southeast Asian American women diagnosed at 65 years or older, with localized stage, ER+ breast cancer, and received radiotherapy (HR range: 1.23-1.35).

For women <65 years of age, the 10-year cumulative heart disease mortality was 1.86% (95% CI: 1.36%-2.49%) for Pacific Islander women and 0.35% (95% CI: 0.28%-0.42%) for Asian American women (East Asian, 0.33% [95% CI: 0.23%-0.45%]; South Asian, 0.28% [95% CI: 0.14%-0.51%]; Southeast Asian, 0.45% [95% CI: 0.34%-0.60%]). For women 65 years and older, the 10-year cumulative heart disease mortality was 8.32% (95% CI: 6.38%-10.56%) for Pacific Islander women and 4.34% (95% CI: 3.91%-4.79%) for Asian American women (East Asian, 4.25% [95% CI: 3.67%-4.88%]; South Asian, 6.12% [95% CI: 2.35%-8.31%]; Southeast Asian, 4.38% [95% CI: 3.62%-5.22%]).

In conclusion, we found evidence that Pacific Islander women had greater absolute and relative risks for heart disease mortality compared with Asian American women, indicating that racial disparities extend into breast cancer survivorship. We also observed significant heterogeneity in absolute and relative heart disease mortality risks for East Asian, South Asian, and Southeast Asian American breast cancer survivors. Two prior studies compared CVD among Asian American and Pacific Islander with White breast cancer survivors4,5; however, our internal comparison provides a more direct effect of how aggregation can conceal health differences for Pacific Islander patients, who constitute a smaller population. Heart disease disparities for Pacific Islander and South Asian and Southeast Asian American women could be related to differences in socioeconomic status, uninsurance rates, and CVD prevalence and risk factors, which were unavailable in SEER. Other limitations include potential misclassification of cause of death, underreporting of cancer treatments, and lack of detailed cardiotoxic chemotherapy treatment administered. Careful consideration is needed to generalize findings to individuals not in the SEER catchment area because of geographic differences. Given the known heart disease mortality disparities for Pacific Islander female general population, which extended to breast cancer survivors, it is imperative that clinicians personalize treatment decisions to reduce cardiotoxicity and optimize cardiovascular risk factors for high-risk patients in concordance with clinical recommendations.
