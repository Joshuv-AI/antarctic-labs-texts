Association between the Prognostic Nutritional Index and survival outcomes in hepatocellular carcinoma patients receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jiajia Qian, Shang Gao, Yunhao Zhu, Jingxia Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have improved outcomes in advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), but substantial heterogeneity in treatment response and survival remains. The Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI), reflecting both nutritional status and systemic immune function, has been increasingly investigated for its association with survival outcomes in the immunotherapy setting. However, existing studies have reported inconsistent results, possibly due to differences in patient populations, treatment strategies, and methodological factors. Therefore, we conducted an updated systematic review and meta-analysis to clarify the prognostic value of PNI in HCC patients receiving ICIs.MethodsPubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Chinese Science and Technology Periodical Database (VIP), and Wanfang Data databases were systematically searched from inception to April 18, 2026. Studies were included according to a predefined PICOS (Population, Intervention//Exposure, Comparison, Outcome, and Study Design) framework: adult HCC patients receiving ICI-based therapy, baseline PNI assessment before treatment, comparison between high- and low-PNI groups using study-defined cut-offs, and reported multivariable-adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) for overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS). A random-effects model was used to calculate pooled HRs with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for OS and PFS and study quality was evaluated using the Quality in Prognosis Studies (QUIPS) tool.ResultsFourteen studies including 3,341 HCC patients treated with ICIs were included. Pooled analyses showed that higher PNI was significantly associated with improved OS [hazard ratio (HR) =0.60, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.48–0.75] and PFS (HR =0.72, 95% CI: 0.57–0.91). Substantial heterogeneity was observed for OS and moderate heterogeneity for PFS. Subgroup analyses showed that the association remained generally consistent across most studies. Importantly, similar effect estimates were observed in both low-risk and moderate-risk studies, suggesting that study quality did not materially influence the pooled results. Based on the QUIPS assessment, 10 studies were judged to be at low overall risk of bias and 4 at moderate risk, with the main concerns related to confounding, study participation, attrition, and statistical reporting.ConclusionsFor advanced HCC patients receiving ICI therapy, higher pretreatment PNI was associated with improved survival outcomes in HCC patients receiving ICI-based therapy. Because the available evidence was derived mainly from retrospective studies, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and require prospective validation.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is known for its high incidence rate and mortality, and is a major burden of global health (1). The development of HCC is often associated with many diseases, including hepatitis B and C virus infections, alcohol-related liver disease, and metabolic dysfunction associated steatohepatitis (MASH) (2). Although there are currently many monitoring programs targeting high-risk populations, the majority of patients are still diagnosed in the middle to late stages, with limited treatment options and poor prognosis (3).

In recent years, the emergence of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) such as atezolizumab combined with bevacizumab and other programmed cell death protein 1/programmed deathligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) inhibitors has changed the treatment of advanced HCC and become a standard treatment method (4,5). However, substantial heterogeneity remains in treatment response and survival outcomes among patients receiving ICIs (6,7). Therefore, reliable and easily accessible prognostic markers are needed to help identify patients with different risk profiles before treatment initiation.

The Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI), derived from serum albumin (ALB) concentration and peripheral blood total lymphocyte count, can comprehensively reflect a patient’s nutritional status and systemic immune-inflammation levels (8). As an acute-phase protein, serum ALB exhibits both antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities, thereby serving as a significant marker for assessing nutritional status and systemic inflammation levels (9). Lymphocytes are components of cell-mediated immunity and function to suppress tumor proliferation and invasion via cytokine-mediated cytotoxic mechanisms (10). Therefore, low PNI represents a state of nutritional impairment and immune dysfunction. This combination creates conditions that help tumors thrive (11). Its prognostic value has been consistently validated across a spectrum of solid tumors, including those of the digestive tract (12), lung (13), and breast (14).

ICIs are increasingly used for HCC. Early evidence suggests that PNI plays a role here. Liu et al. studied 151 patients treated with ICIs. PNI independently predicted progression-free survival (PFS) (15). Nutritional markers actually performed better than inflammatory markers alone (15). Persano et al. analyzed 773 individuals receiving first-line atezolizumab-bevacizumab across multiple centers. PNI was identified as an independent prognostic factor for both overall survival (OS) and PFS (16). Mei et al. also showed that, among several inflammation-based scores, PNI had favorable prognostic performance in HCC patients treated with anti-PD-1 therapy (17). These findings suggest that PNI may have potential prognostic value in the immunotherapy setting.

Although several recent studies and meta-analyses have explored the prognostic value of the PNI in patients with HCC treated with ICIs, their findings remain inconsistent. These inconsistencies may reflect both clinical and methodological heterogeneity across the available studies (18-20). Clinically, differences in underlying etiologies, such as hepatitis B virus (HBV)-dominant versus hepatitis C virus (HCV)-dominant HCC populations, may influence the prognostic role of PNI because viral background is closely related to tumor biology, liver reserve, and the immune microenvironment (21). However, etiology-specific data were not consistently reported across the included studies, which limited our ability to perform a reliable subgroup analysis based on HBV or HCV status. Methodologically, several measurable study-level factors may also contribute to the variability of reported results. In particular, differences in sample size may affect the precision and stability of effect estimates, especially in retrospective cohorts. In addition, the cut-off values used to define low and high PNI varied across studies, which could influence patient classification and lead to differences in the estimated prognostic effect. Therefore, in the present updated meta-analysis, we aimed not only to synthesize the latest evidence but also to explore potential sources of heterogeneity using available study-level variables, including sample size and PNI cut-off values. This approach may help clarify whether the prognostic association between PNI and survival outcomes remains consistent across different methodological settings.

Our study aimed to address these issues through a systematic review and meta-analysis. By doing so, we comprehensively retrieved and synthesized data from existing studies. We conducted a quantitative pooled analysis to elucidate the relationship between PNI and OS and PFS in HCC patients treated with ICIs. We hope that these findings may provide additional evidence for prognostic assessment and risk stratification in patients with advanced HCC receiving immunotherapy. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1-0262/rc).

Methods

Literature Search

A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Chinese Science and Technology Periodical Database (VIP), and Wanfang Data from database inception to April 18, 2026. No language restrictions were applied at the search stage. Publication status did not affect eligibility. Search terms reflected three domains. These covered target population, therapeutic intervention, plus biomarker. Specific terms encompassed “hepatocellular carcinoma”, “immune checkpoint inhibitors”, “Prognostic Nutritional Index”, plus variant expressions. Boolean operators linked these concepts. This approach ensured comprehensive retrieval. Reference lists from included articles underwent manual screening. Additional eligible publications were identified through this process (shown in Table S1). The detailed literature search strategy is provided in Appendix 1. The protocol was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420261425188). It remains accessible via the Centre for Reviews and Dissemination platform.

Study selection

Study selection followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) recommendations and was structured a priori using the PICOS (Population, Intervention/Exposure, Comparison, Outcome, and Study Design) framework.

Population (P)

Eligible studies enrolled adult patients with a confirmed diagnosis of HCC, irrespective of underlying etiology (e.g., viral hepatitis, alcohol-related liver disease, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis) or tumor stage. Patients had to receive at least one cycle of ICI-based therapy, including programmed cell death protein 1/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) inhibitors or atezolizumab plus bevacizumab, administered either as monotherapy or in combination with other systemic or locoregional modalities [e.g., tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), transarterial chemoembolization (TACE)]. Studies exclusively involving non-HCC malignancies, mixed cohorts without separate data for HCC, or animal/in vitro models were excluded.

Intervention/exposure (I)

The prognostic factor of interest was the pre-treatment PNI, calculated from serum ALB concentration and peripheral blood lymphocyte count according to the definition adopted in each study. PNI had to be measured at baseline, prior to or at the start of ICI therapy. Studies evaluating PNI after treatment initiation only, or using composite scores without the possibility to identify the independent effect of PNI, were not eligible.

Comparison (C)

Included studies were required to compare outcomes between groups categorized by different baseline PNI levels, typically dichotomized into “high” and “low” according to an explicit cut-off value (e.g., ROC-derived threshold, median, or previously published value). Studies that treated PNI purely as a continuous variable without reporting hazard ratios (HRs) for distinct PNI strata, and those lacking a clearly defined comparison between higher and lower PNI groups, were excluded from quantitative synthesis.

Outcomes (O)

The primary outcome of interest was OS. The secondary outcome was PFS, including studies that reported equivalent endpoints such as time to progression, recurrence-free survival (RFS), or disease-free survival (DFS), provided that definitions were clearly stated. For inclusion in the meta-analysis, studies had to report effect estimates for PNI (high vs. low) expressed as HRs with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), or provide sufficient data to derive these statistics. When both univariate and multivariate analyses were available, only multivariable-adjusted HRs were considered for pooling. Studies that did not report time-to-event outcomes or lacked extractable quantitative data were excluded.

Study design (S)

We considered prospective or retrospective observational cohort studies (including registry-based analyses) that evaluated the association between baseline PNI and survival outcomes in HCC patients receiving ICIs. Randomized controlled trials were eligible only if they reported suitable prognostic analyses of PNI within ICI-treated subgroups. Non-original articles (narrative reviews, systematic reviews, meta-analyses), editorials, letters, comments, conference abstracts without full text, case reports or case series with fewer than 10 patients, and studies not published in full-text form were excluded.

Screening process

Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts of all retrieved records against these PICOS-based eligibility criteria. Full texts of potentially relevant articles were then assessed in detail for final inclusion. Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage (e.g., absence of ICI treatment, lack of PNI stratification, missing HRs) were recorded. Any discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion; when disagreement persisted, a third investigator adjudicated. The study selection process and numbers at each stage are summarized in the PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1). Data extraction

Four investigators conducted the data extraction independently. Each followed the same predefined protocol to ensure consistency. Any discrepancies that arose were addressed through group discussion until consensus was achieved. From each included study, the following information was collected: first author, publication year, country, study design, sample size, and details of ICI treatment. We gathered patient demographic details including median age and gender distribution across each group, as well as PNI cut-off values and the number of patients split into high- and low-PNI groups. We also extracted key outcome measures: OS, PFS, RFS and DFS. Additional data points included median follow-up duration, Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage distribution in the study cohorts, the specific methods used for HR estimation, and the type of statistical analysis done, either univariate or multivariate. Only multivariate‑adjusted HRs were extracted from each study. If a study reported both univariate and multivariate HRs, only the multivariate estimate was used for pooling. We also recorded RFS and DFS when reported, but the quantitative synthesis of this study focused on OS and PFS.

Quality assessment

Four reviewers independently assessed the methodological quality and risk of bias for all included studies, using the Quality in Prognosis Studies (QUIPS) tool. The evaluation includes: study attrition, study participation, outcome measurement, study confounding, statistical analysis and prognostic factor measurement.

For each field, we classify the risk of bias as low, medium, or high according to the standards of the QUIPS tool. Researchers sometimes have disagreements when conducting quality ratings. Disagreements were resolved through group discussion until consensus was reached.

To improve transparency, risk-of-bias judgments were reported at the domain level for each individual study rather than being based solely on a numerical summary score. Particular attention was paid to domains considered especially important in prognostic factor research, namely prognostic factor measurement, outcome measurement, study confounding, and statistical analysis and reporting.

Study participation was judged according to the clarity of the eligibility criteria, the representativeness of the study sample, and the adequacy of baseline patient characteristics. Study attrition was assessed on the basis of follow-up completeness, reporting of missing data, and the likelihood that missingness could have affected outcome assessment. Prognostic factor measurement was evaluated according to whether PNI was clearly defined, measured before treatment initiation, and assessed using consistent methods across participants. Outcome measurement was judged according to the clarity and validity of OS and PFS definitions and the adequacy of follow-up procedures. Study confounding was assessed with particular attention to whether important prognostic variables were accounted for, including age, sex, performance status, tumor burden or stage, liver function, treatment line, treatment regimen, and other clinically relevant factors when available. Statistical analysis and reporting were judged according to the appropriateness of the analytical model, adequacy of multivariable adjustment, and completeness of the reported effect estimates.

An overall risk-of-bias judgment was assigned after consideration of all six domains, with greater weight given to the key domains noted above. A study was considered to be at low overall risk of bias only when all key domains were rated as low risk, with at most minor concerns in non-key domains. A study was considered to be at high overall risk of bias if at least one key domain, particularly study confounding or statistical analysis and reporting, was rated as high risk, or if multiple domains showed high risk of bias. All remaining studies were classified as having moderate overall risk of bias. Detailed domain-level assessments for each study are provided in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). HRs with corresponding 95% CIs were used as the common effect measure for time-to-event outcomes, including OS and PFS. For each study, we extracted the multivariable-adjusted HR whenever available; if both univariable and multivariable results were reported, only the multivariable-adjusted estimate was included in the pooled analysis. This approach was adopted to reduce the influence of confounding and to improve comparability across studies.

Between-study heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test and the I2 statistic. A P value <0.10 for the Q test or an I2 value >50% was considered to indicate substantial heterogeneity. Given the expected clinical and methodological variability across studies, including differences in region, treatment regimen, sample size, PNI cut-off value, and baseline patient characteristics, a random-effects model was prespecified and used for the primary pooled analyses.

Subgroup analyses were conducted based on country, sample size, PNI cut-off value and study quality. Sensitivity analyses were performed by sequentially omitting one study at a time to assess the robustness of the pooled estimates. Publication bias was evaluated using Egger’s regression test and visual inspection of funnel plots. All P values were two-sided, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Subgroup analyses were performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity based on the following study-level characteristics: country, PNI (low vs. high), sample size, PNI cut-off value, study quality, follow-up duration, mean age, proportion of BCLC stage C, and proportion of male patients. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by sequentially omitting each study to evaluate the stability and robustness of the pooled results. Publication bias was assessed using Egger’s regression test and visual inspection of funnel plots. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study selection

Database searches yielded a total of 278 records. We first removed duplicate entries, then screened each record by title, abstract and full text. Fourteen studies met all eligibility criteria in the end, and they were included in this meta-analysis (15-17,22-32). These studies involve 3,341 HCC patients who received ICI treatment. The full process of study selection is laid out in Figure 1.

Study characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the main features of the 14 studies we included. These were conducted in China, Japan, and Italy, and all looked at how the PNI relates to prognosis in HCC patients on immunotherapy. Study sample sizes differed quite a bit, and most patients in each group were men. The PNI cut-off values used in the studies fell between about 37.25 and 53, showing some slight differences in how they were applied. Most patients had advanced disease (BCLC stages B through D). Early-stage cases (BCLC 0-A) were rare. All the studies used multivariate Cox regression models to calculate HRs, and only these multivariate-adjusted estimates were pooled in our meta-analysis, which enhances the comparability of results. All included studies reported multivariable Cox regression analyses, and only multivariable-adjusted HRs were used in the pooled analyses to improve comparability across studies. Based on the QUIPS assessment, 10 studies were judged to be at low overall risk of bias and 4 at moderate overall risk; no study was considered to have a high overall risk of bias. Across domains, prognostic factor measurement and outcome measurement were consistently rated as low risk in most studies, whereas moderate concerns were more commonly noted in study participation, study attrition, study confounding, and statistical analysis/reporting. Risk of bias assessment

The results of the QUIPS assessment are summarized in Table S1. Overall, 10 studies were judged to be at low risk of bias and 4 at moderate risk; none was rated as high risk overall. Prognostic factor measurement and outcome measurement were generally robust across the included studies, reflecting the use of routinely collected laboratory parameters for PNI and clearly defined survival endpoints. Moderate concerns were more frequently observed in study participation and study attrition, mainly because several studies were single-center retrospective cohorts with limited information on sample representativeness or incomplete reporting of follow-up and missing data. In addition, some studies were judged to have moderate risk in the confounding domain because adjustment for clinically important prognostic variables was not fully consistent across reports. Moderate concerns in statistical analysis and reporting were mainly related to limited detail regarding model-building strategies or covariate handling. No study was judged to be at high risk in the key domains of outcome measurement or prognostic factor measurement.

OS

As shown in Figure 2, we performed pooled association analyses of the relationship between PNI and survival outcomes, including OS (Figure 2A) and PFS (Figure 2B). Thirteen studies were included in total for the OS analysis. The OS rates of high PNI patients and low PNI patients were compared. As shown in Figure 2A, the analysis indicates that the higher the PNI, the lower the risk of death (pooled HR =0.60, 95% CI: 0.48–0.75). The study showed substantial heterogeneity (I2=86.6%). A random-effects model was used to estimate pooled HRs.

The association between higher PNI and better OS was generally consistent across most subgroups.

In the regional subgroup analysis, studies conducted in China and non-China both showed a favorable OS trend in patients with higher PNI, with no significant difference between subgroups (P=0.78). Similar results were observed when studies were stratified by the PNI low/high ratio, suggesting that the prognostic association of PNI was not substantially affected by differences in the distribution of patients with low and high PNI across studies.

When stratified by PNI cut-off value, using the median cut-off value of 43.37 as the grouping threshold, both studies using a cut-off <43.37 and those using a cut-off ≥43.37 showed that higher PNI was associated with improved OS. The test for subgroup differences was not significant (P=0.85), indicating that variation in the cut-off value did not materially change the overall direction of the association. Consistent findings were also observed in studies with low and moderate risk of bias, suggesting that the survival benefit associated with higher PNI remained present across different study-quality categories.

For the subgroup analysis based on follow-up duration, studies were divided according to the median follow-up duration of 20 months. Three studies were excluded because they did not report follow-up duration in months: Junfeng Ge, Yuan-Hung Kuo, and Kuo YH. Among the remaining studies, the association between higher PNI and improved OS was still observed in the follow-up duration subgroups. For the subgroup analysis based on mean age, studies were stratified using the median mean age of 59 years as the cut-off. Two studies, Junfeng Ge and Kuo YH, were excluded because mean age was not reported. The results of the remaining studies also generally supported the favorable association between higher PNI and OS.

Subgroup analyses based on the proportion of BCLC stage C and the proportion of male patients showed similar trends. These two subgroup thresholds were also determined by the median values across the included studies, with 74.3% used for BCLC stage C and 81.82% used for male proportion. Although heterogeneity remained in several subgroups, the direction of the association between higher PNI and improved OS was largely unchanged.

Notably, sample size appeared to be an important contributor to heterogeneity. The subgroup difference by sample size was statistically significant (P<0.001). Meta-regression further confirmed that sample size was significantly associated with the effect estimate (P=0.004), explaining approximately 49.12% of the between-study heterogeneity (Figure 6).

Meta-regression analyses for potential sources of heterogeneity: (A) meta-regression of sample size for OS; (B) meta-regression of PNI cut-off value for PFS. OS, overall survival; PFS, progression-free survival; PNI, Prognostic Nutritional Index.

Overall, these results suggest that higher PNI is associated with improved OS in patients with HCC receiving ICI-based treatment. However, given the substantial heterogeneity, particularly the influence of sample size, this association should be interpreted with caution.

PFS

Nine studies reported the association between PNI and PFS. As shown in Figure 2B, patients with higher PNI had a significantly lower risk of disease progression than those with lower PNI (pooled HR =0.72, 95% CI: 0.57–0.91). Moderate heterogeneity was observed across studies (I2=61.9%, τ2=0.0762, P=0.007), and therefore a random-effects model was applied.

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were conducted according to country, sample size, PNI cut-off value, study quality, follow-up duration, mean age, proportion of BCLC stage C, and proportion of male patients (Figures 7,8). Subgroup analysis according to the PNI low/high ratio was not performed for PFS because only one included study had a PNI low/high ratio >1, making a meaningful comparison between subgroups inappropriate. For continuous study-level variables, including PNI cut-off value, follow-up duration, mean age, proportion of BCLC stage C, and proportion of male patients, subgroup thresholds were defined according to the median values of the included studies.

In the subgroup analysis by country, both Chinese and non-Chinese studies showed a favorable trend for higher PNI, and no significant subgroup difference was detected (P=0.59). When stratified by sample size, studies with fewer than 100 patients showed a more evident association between higher PNI and improved PFS, whereas larger studies showed greater variability; however, the subgroup difference was not statistically significant (P=0.32).

Because the PNI cut-off values varied across the included studies, ranging from 37.25 to 53, we further examined whether cut-off selection contributed to heterogeneity. Using the median PNI cut-off value of 42.1 as the grouping threshold, studies were divided into cut-off <42.1 and cut-off ≥42.1 groups. Higher PNI was associated with better PFS in the cut-off <42.1 subgroup, while the direction of effect remained generally favorable in the cut-off ≥42.1 subgroup. The test for subgroup differences was not significant (P=0.73), suggesting that the cut-off category did not significantly modify the prognostic effect of PNI.

We also performed meta-regression using PNI cut-off value. In the categorical meta-regression analysis, PNI cut-off group was not significantly associated with the effect estimate (P=0.55). Similarly, when PNI cut-off was treated as a continuous variable, no significant association was observed (P=0.79). These findings indicate that variation in PNI cut-off values did not explain the heterogeneity in the PFS analysis.

When stratified by study quality, higher PNI remained associated with improved PFS in moderate-risk studies, whereas the estimate was less stable in low-risk studies; however, the subgroup difference was not significant (P=0.19).

For follow-up duration and mean age analyses, studies were grouped using the median values of 20 months and 58.25 years, respectively. Junfeng Ge was excluded from both analyses because follow-up duration and mean age were not reported. The favorable association between higher PNI and PFS was generally maintained across these subgroups, with no significant subgroup differences for follow-up duration (P=0.43) or mean age (P=0.68).

Similar trends were observed when studies were stratified by the proportion of BCLC stage C and male patients, using median thresholds of 61.5% and 82.12%, respectively. Neither BCLC stage C proportion nor male proportion significantly modified the association between PNI and PFS (P=0.13 and P=0.91).

In addition to PNI cut-off, meta-regression analyses were also performed for other study-level variables; however, none of these factors significantly explained the remaining heterogeneity.

Overall, higher PNI was associated with improved PFS in patients with HCC receiving ICI-based treatment. Importantly, although PNI cut-off values differed across studies, both subgroup analysis and meta-regression suggested that cut-off variation was unlikely to be the main source of PFS heterogeneity. Therefore, the PFS findings support the prognostic relevance of PNI, but they should still be interpreted cautiously because residual heterogeneity remained.

Publication bias and sensitivity analysis

Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test. Visual inspection of the funnel plots did not suggest obvious asymmetry for either OS or PFS (Figure 9A,9B). Egger’s test also showed no significant evidence of publication bias for OS (P=0.06) or PFS (P=0.54).

Funnel plots for publication bias assessment: (A) funnel plot for OS; (B) funnel plot for PFS. OS, overall survival; PFS, progression-free survival.

Sensitivity analyses were performed by sequentially omitting one study at a time. For OS, the pooled HR remained stable after the exclusion of each individual study, ranging from 0.57 to 0.64, and all estimates remained statistically significant (Figure 10A). Similar results were observed for PFS. The pooled HRs after leave-one-out analyses ranged from 0.68 to 0.76, and the direction of the association was unchanged in all analyses (Figure 10B).

Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses: (A) sensitivity analysis for OS; (B) sensitivity analysis for PFS. CI, confidence interval; HR, hazard ratio; OS, overall survival; PFS, progression-free survival.

In summary, these findings indicate that the pooled results for both OS and PFS were not driven by any single study and were generally robust.

Discussion

The PNI was first proposed by Buzby to predict surgical risk (33). It has become more and more important in cancer treatment because it reflects both a patient’s nutritional and immunological status (34). Due to the continuous emergence of research in this field, it is crucial to summarize and evaluate the accumulated evidence to draw new conclusions. This meta-analysis focuses on analyzing patients with advanced HCC who receive ICIs treatment. The analysis results indicate that high pre-treatment PNI is associated with longer OS and PFS. Our results are consistent with many studies. PNI has been widely reported as a prognostic factor associated with survival outcomes in various cancers, including those treated with different methods. These show the great value of PNI in immunotherapy (35-39). Compared with previous meta-analyses, our study incorporates the most up-to-date evidence through 2026 and includes a larger number of recent studies. In addition, we performed more comprehensive subgroup analyses and meta-regression to explore potential sources of heterogeneity in both OS and PFS. Notably, sample size was identified as a contributor to heterogeneity in the OS analysis. These additional analyses provide a more detailed understanding of the variability across studies and help to clarify the robustness of the observed associations.

Our study found that higher PNI indicates better survival outcomes for HCC patients treated with ICIs. This result can be explained. PNI combines serum ALB and lymphocyte count. They are fundamental parameters of homeostasis (10). ALB not only provides information about the patient’s nutritional status, but also reflects the patient’s liver condition. It is also important for transporting immune cells, hormones, and nutrients (40). In patients with advanced liver cancer, hypoproteinemia often indicates chronic inflammation and cachexia. It may promote tumor progression (41). Lymphocytes are also important for anti-tumor immunity. They prevent tumor recurrence and growth by supporting immune function (42). Thus, PNI is a convenient comprehensive indicator that can predict the host’s ability to withstand treatment and the immune adaptation required for effective response to immunotherapy (43,44). Recent studies on comprehensive scoring systems have confirmed this finding. For example, the nutritional-immune-inflammatory score (NIIS) score includes aspartate aminotransferase-to-lymphocyte ratio index (ALRI) and aspartate aminotransferase-to-platelet ratio index (APRI) (45). The Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII)-PNI score combines the SII with PNI (46). They both performed better than traditional staging system in predicting outcomes across treatments, such as PD-1 monotherapy or TACE combined with targeted and immunotherapy (47,48). By integrating more inflammatory and liver function indicators, they collectively demonstrate the influence of the microenvironment on treatment efficacy (45,46).

PNI has high clinical value (49). We found that PNI holds independent prognostic value across multiple therapy lines and drug combinations. Other studies have reached similar conclusions. One of these analyses focused on second-line treatment for patients who had failed first-line therapy with atezolizumab plus bevacizumab (Atezo/Bev). All these works point to the same consistent conclusion on PNI’s prognostic role. For instance, Majidova et al. conducted a multicenter study. They found that PNI, with a cut-off of 47.6, was an independent factor strongly linked to OS in advanced HCC patients receiving second-line TKIs (50). PNI reflects a patient’s baseline nutritional and immune condition (51). Regardless of the initial treatment chosen, its prognostic value remains consistent in different treatment environments (50). This stability may stem from the patient’s immune adaptation and treatment tolerance reserve (20,52). The variability seen in the PFS analysis may due to several differences across the studies. These include differences in treatment lines, the various combination regimens used, and the different levels of disease aggressiveness in the patients included across the different studies. For example, a small-sample study by Sun shows that PNI is an independent prognostic factor for patients on TKI combined with ICI treatment, while NLR is not (53). In contrast, Wei’s study evaluated more optimized schemes, including TACE, and found that the joint scoring based on SII and PNI provided stronger predictive ability (46). This indicates that different treatment methods have different anti-tumor mechanisms. This may lead to changes in important host factors that affect prognosis. Future studies should determine the best PNI cut-off value for specific treatment groups. They should also explore whether combining PNI with dynamic change indicators can deliver stronger predictive performance.

Substantial heterogeneity was observed in the OS analysis, and moderate heterogeneity was observed in the PFS analysis. Subgroup analyses showed that the association between higher PNI and better OS was generally consistent across different countries, PNI low/high ratios, PNI cut-off values, study quality, follow-up duration, mean age, BCLC stage C proportion, and male proportion (Table 2). For PFS, the direction of the association also generally favored patients with higher PNI, although the estimates were less stable in some subgroups (Table 3).

Among the explored factors, sample size appeared to partly explain OS heterogeneity. Meta-regression showed that sample size was significantly associated with the OS effect estimate and explained approximately 49.12% of the between-study heterogeneity. This finding suggests that smaller studies may have produced less stable or more pronounced estimates. In contrast, subgroup analyses and meta-regression did not identify a definite source of heterogeneity for PFS, including PNI cut-off value, despite the cut-off values varying from 37.25 to 53.0 across studies.

The remaining heterogeneity may be related to differences in ICI types, combined targeted or locoregional therapies, treatment lines, liver function, tumor burden, follow-up schedules, and definitions of disease progression. Unmeasured confounding in retrospective studies may also have contributed to the variability.

Despite this heterogeneity, the overall direction of effect remained consistent, and sensitivity analyses showed that no single study substantially changed the pooled HRs for either OS or PFS. These findings support the robustness of our results, while also highlighting the need for prospective studies with standardized PNI cut-off values, treatment protocols, and progression assessment criteria.

PNI is calculated based on ALB levels and total lymphocyte counts in the blood (10). It reflects both nutritional and immunological status. Thus, it can help clinical doctors make medical decisions. The treatment options for HCC are constantly evolving. There is a need for easily accessible and affordable biomarkers. PNI can help us identify patients who are more likely to benefit from ICI therapy (54). It not only helps clinicians stratify patients’ risks, but also enables the provision of personalized treatment and medical decisions. These interventions can improve the nutritional and immune status of patients before or during treatment with ICIs (37,53,55,56). Our research provides a theoretical basis for practical application. In the future, this simple indicator can be applied to assess the prognosis of patients with advanced HCC before starting immunotherapy.

This meta-analysis has several limitations. Firstly, all included studies were retrospective, so selection bias and unmeasured confounding could not be completely avoided. Although, adjusted HRs were preferentially extracted, the covariates included in multivariable models varied among studies, and residual confounding from factors such as performance status, comorbidities, nutritional intervention, body composition, liver reserve, and systemic inflammatory status may still exist. This also suggested that we should pay attention to prospective validation in future work. Secondly, the PNI cut-off values were not uniform across studies, ranging from 37.25 to 53.0. Although our subgroup analyses and meta-regression did not indicate that PNI cut-off value was a major source of heterogeneity, this variability may still reduce comparability between studies. Future studies should aim to establish a more standardized and clinically applicable PNI threshold. Also, most patients included in this meta-analysis were from East Asian populations, mainly China and Japan. Therefore, the generalizability of our findings to other populations remains uncertain. Future studies should include more geographically diverse cohorts to determine whether the prognostic value of PNI differs across regions and ethnic backgrounds. Third, there were differences in patient characteristics and treatment strategies across the included cohorts, including HCC stage, tumor burden, liver function, type of ICI regimen, treatment line, and the use of combined targeted therapy or locoregional treatment. These factors may influence both survival outcomes and the prognostic effect of PNI. Because individual patient data were unavailable, we could not conduct more detailed stratified analyses according to these variables. Finally, some of the studies we included noted that antiviral therapy for HBV can affect ALB and lymphocyte levels (57,58). We were not able to account for this in our pooled analysis. This is a limitation of our study. Antiviral therapy may improve liver function in these patients. It can independently improve PNI levels and survival outcomes (59).

Conclusions

In conclusion, this updated meta-analysis suggests that a higher pretreatment PNI is associated with better OS and PFS in patients with HCC receiving ICI-based treatment. PNI may provide useful information for pretreatment risk stratification in this population.

However, these findings should be interpreted with caution because the available evidence is mainly based on retrospective cohort studies, and substantial heterogeneity remained, particularly in the OS analysis. Although subgroup analyses and meta-regression partly explored potential sources of heterogeneity, residual confounding could not be fully excluded.

Future prospective studies with larger and more geographically diverse populations are needed to validate the prognostic role of PNI, establish standardized cut-off values, and determine whether changes in nutritional or immune status during treatment can further improve prognostic assessment.
