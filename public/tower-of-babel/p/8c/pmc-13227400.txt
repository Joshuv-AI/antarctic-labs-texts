Bridging the Gender Gap in Cardiovascular Care: A Framework for Women's Heart Care
Authors: Julia Grapsa, Ana G Almeida
Published: 2026

Women's cardiovascular disease (CVD) has lived in the margins of cardiology—acknowledged but understudied and underdiagnosed. While CVD remains the leading cause of death among women globally, still greater focus is needed on the profound sex differences in presentation, risk, diagnosis, and outcomes [12]. Over the last decades the dedicated women's heart programs have contributed substantially in the early diagnosis and treatment of women. The issue we are facing though is more complex: early diagnosis and treatment begin with education [2]. Establishing a dedicated curriculum and formal certification in women's CVD is no longer optional; it is an urgent requirement for modern cardiology.

At the heart of this challenge is the fact that women are not simply “smaller men” with identical disease patterns. Their CVD risks shift across the life course, puberty, pregnancy, perimenopause, menopause, while the interaction between hormones, autoimmune disease, metabolic changes, and social determinants of health create a uniquely complex risk landscape. Women are disproportionately affected by conditions such as myocardial infarction with normal coronary arteries, spontaneous coronary artery dissection, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, microvascular dysfunction, Takotsubo syndrome, and pregnancy-associated cardiomyopathies.

This is where Women's Heart Programs become transformative. Their value extends beyond clinical care; they represent incubators for expertise, training, and standard-setting. If cardiology is to meaningfully close the gender gap, women's heart programs must evolve into training hubs that formalise the knowledge and skills required to care for female patients. Without setting these standards, the field risks remaining fragmented and dependent on self-taught or interest-driven practice.

A comprehensive curriculum in women's CVD should begin during cardiology training and extend into advanced post-fellowship certification [3]. Core elements must include sex-specific CVD physiology, the epidemiology of CVD in women, and the nuances of diagnostic testing, symptom presentation, and imaging interpretation unique to female patients [3]. Trainees must learn to recognise and manage the entire spectrum of conditions disproportionately affecting women, from microvascular disease to SCAD [4], from peripartum cardiomyopathy to the CVD implications of menopause [5].

One of the most critical components of such a curriculum is cardio-obstetrics, a field that demands formalisation across all training programs. As maternal mortality rises, much of it driven by CVD causes, every cardiologist should be equipped with the skills to manage pregnancy in patients with congenital heart disease, cardiomyopathies, valvular disorders, arrhythmias, and hypertensive syndromes. This requires hands-on exposure to pregnancy heart-team meetings, obstetric-cardiac co-management, risk stratification tools, and delivery planning for women with complex CVD.

Cardio-oncology, another rapidly expanding field, also deserves structured training under the umbrella of women's CVD [6]. With breast cancer survival improving, cardiologists must be versed in the cardiac consequences of chemotherapeutic and targeted therapies, long-term surveillance strategies, and collaborative care models with oncology. Similarly, cardio-rheumatology, critical for the many women affected by autoimmune conditions, should be part of the knowledge base of any certified women's heart specialist [7].

Although curriculum alone or the description of Women's Heart Program director role, as recently published by American College of Cardiology [89] is not enough. To ensure competence, the field must move toward formal certification or subspecialty recognition within cardiology. This could take the form of a defined training pathway similar to heart failure, electrophysiology, or interventional cardiology. Certification should require documented clinical experience, participation in multidisciplinary care, exposure to women-specific imaging and diagnostics, and mastery of preventive care tailored to women's unique risk factors and life stages. Examinations, recertification, and continuing medical education requirements would ensure that expertise remains current as the evidence base grows.

Such certification would bring credibility and consistency, making it clear that expertise in women's CVD is a rigorous discipline grounded in science. Crucially, education must extend beyond physicians. Nurses, advanced practice providers, physiotherapists, pharmacists, psychologists, and primary care clinicians all play essential roles in the care of CVD in women. A multi-tiered educational model, including structured courses, simulation training, interprofessional case discussions, and digital learning platforms, would elevate the entire care ecosystem. Women's Heart Programs are uniquely positioned to serve as the coordinating hubs for this broader training mission.

Research training must also be embedded into the curriculum [10]. Historically, women have been underrepresented in clinical trials, resulting in diagnostic thresholds, therapeutic doses, and treatment algorithms that are often extrapolated from male-dominated datasets. Future specialists must learn to design trials that consider sex as a biological variable, recruit across diverse populations, and interpret outcomes through a gender-informed lens.

Creating such a curriculum and certification pathway carries societal implications beyond medicine. It validates women's health as a scientific priority, counters decades of gender bias, and signals to policymakers and funders that women's CVD requires sustained investment. It strengthens prevention, improves early detection, and ensures that clinical expertise is not left to chance or interest but institutionalised across regions and health systems.

As a summary, within CVD in women, the path forward is clear: establish specialised training, embed women's heart disease into core curricula, and create formal certification to guarantee expertise. Without these foundational steps, even the best clinical models will fall short. The time for that transformation is now.
